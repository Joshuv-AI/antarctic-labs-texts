Rosemary essential oil: chemical composition, antiviral, cytotoxicity, and in silico molecular docking analysis against tobacco mosaic virus
Authors: Abdulaziz A Al-Askar, Karrar A Hamzah, Dalia G Aseel, Ali H El-Far, Mohamed M Abdelhamid, Said Behiry, Toufic Elbeaino, Ahmed Abdelkhalek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAromatic plants represent a significant source of bioactive compounds and have recently expanded their potential applications in agriculture. In the current study, the essential oil of Salvia rosmarinus (rosemary) was extracted, and its chemical composition was analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Under greenhouse conditions, the antiviral effectiveness of the essential oil against tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) was evaluated on Nicotiana glutinosa, marking the first comprehensive assessment of its impact on plant viruses. A computational analysis of molecular docking interactions was performed to explore the potential interactions of target compounds with the three protein components of TMV. Additionally, the influence of essential oil on the root tips of Allium cepa was assessed for its genotoxic effects. In comparison to untreated infected plants, the curative treatment demonstrated a peak viral inhibition rate of 76.3% at 300 µg/mL, while the protective and inactivation treatments reached maximum rates of 70.7% and 62.8%, respectively, at the same concentration. At elevated concentrations of essential oil (300 µg/mL), the genotoxicity assay showed a concentration-dependent decrease in the mitotic index and an increase in chromosomal abnormalities. The GC-MS analysis revealed that the primary constituents of the essential oil were α-terpineol (19.3%), isoborneol (14.1%), and camphor (13.2%). Furthermore, an in-silico assessment of the candidate phytoconstituents indicated that the antiviral properties are mainly associated with the isoaromadendrene epoxide (0.35%), cubenol (0.22%), epiglobulol (2.06%), caryophyllene (5.73%), and caryophyllene oxide (2.66%) compounds. Thus, we assert that the synergistic properties of oil components account for their significant antiviral efficacy. Overall, the results of our study suggest that rosemary essential oil demonstrates significant antiviral properties and may serve as a promising antiviral agent for combating plant viral infections. Introduction

Plant viral infections pose a significant threat to agricultural production, resulting in considerable global economic losses1,2. Given the absence of a cure for viral infections in plants, unlike the treatments available for bacteria and fungi3, most defense strategies are focused on preventing their spread4,5. This involves utilizing certified propagation material and eliminating insects that transmit viruses. Engineered transgenic plants offer a promising strategy; nonetheless, their application is restricted to a limited number of plant/virus species6. Moreover, their application is constrained by regulatory prohibitions in the transgenic plant studies and implementations of numerous nations. In situations where alternative measures are not viable, in vitro virus elimination is achieved through methods like meristem culture and micro-grafting. However, these approaches are costly and labor-intensive, and may not apply to every plant-virus pathosystem7. A range of substances has been employed to manage plant virus infections, with numerous studies documenting the antiviral properties of essential oils8,9.

Essential oils are complex mixtures of low-molecular-weight and volatile compounds produced and stored in aromatic plant families, such as Lamiaceae, which contain many of these secondary metabolites10,11. These oils are typically obtained by steam or hydrodistillation12 and comprise various chemical classes, including aliphatic and aromatic compounds. Many of these substances have been reported to exhibit antimicrobial, antiviral, antifungal, and insecticidal properties against plant pathogens13–15. Such treatments aim to reduce symptom expression and limit yield losses due to infection by lowering viral titer or inactivating viral functional genes. The use of natural products is also gaining interest in light of anticipated restrictions on the overuse of synthetic chemicals, which have been shown to cause environmental and human health issues (e.g., Regulation 1107/2009/EC, 2011)16. Consequently, several studies have focused on utilizing natural products for controlling plant viruses. Trials involving tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) were based on counting local lesions. Bishop17 and Lu18 used Nicotiana glutinosa as an experimental host and found that essential oils from Melaleuca alternifolia, ginger, lemon, tea tree, tangerine peel, artemisia, and lemongrass inhibited the local lesion-symptom development. Dunkić et al19. used the essential oil of Satureja montana to treat Chenopodium quinoa and C. amaranticolor hosts infected with TMV and cucumber mosaic virus (CMV). Further tests using the C. quinoa-CMV pathosystem revealed that essential oils from Micromeria croatia and Teucrium species could reduce the number of local lesions20,21. Due to its numerous active ingredients, including phenolic compounds, di- and triterpenes, and essential oils, Salvia rosmarinus, also known as rosemary, has many medical applications22. Rosemary has demonstrated its antiviral potential against several viruses by interacting with the viral envelope, inhibiting viral replication, and protecting against viral infection23–25. The aqueous extract of rosemary aerial parts at a concentration of 30 µg/mL resulted in a 55% inhibition of herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) plaques, while a concentration of 40 µg/mL led to a 65% inhibition of HSV-2 plaques. The extract entirely prevented plaque formation of HSV-1 and HSV-2 at a concentration of 50 µg/mL23. On the other hand, Nasr-Eldin et al26. demonstrated that an aqueous extract of rosemary leaves at 200 µg/mL suppressed HSV-1 by 18.9%, whereas no inhibitory effect against HSV-2 was detected. Furthermore, the antiviral properties of essential oils derived from rosemary leaves have been documented27.

The tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) has been widely recognized as the most suitable model for investigating the characteristics and identification of plant viruses28. The virus was classified as a highly significant phytopathogen affecting several commercially valuable crops and approximately 400 economically important plants, such as Solanaceae plants (tobacco, potato, and pepper), vegetables, and decorative flower species29,30. TMV, also known as plant cancer, causes the distortion of fruits and leaves and stunted growth in plants, leading to decreased seed germination rates31. The genome of TMV contains genetic information for the production of replicase protein, movement protein, and coat protein. Concerning TMV, several in-silico investigations have been conducted32,33. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics are crucial techniques in the design and discovery of therapeutic drugs. They have been effectively employed to assess the specific binding mechanism between a ligand and its enzyme receptor34,35.

Developing novel anti-TMV agents to mitigate the devastating effects of this virus is therefore imperative. In this context, the present study aimed to integrate in vitro and in silico approaches to evaluate the antiviral potential of rosemary essential oil against TMV infection. The cytotoxicity of the essential oil was also assessed to ensure its biosafety. Furthermore, the chemical composition of the essential oil was characterized using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), and molecular docking analyses were performed to evaluate the binding affinity of its major constituents with the three key TMV proteins (replicase, movement, and coat). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to comprehensively investigate the antiviral efficacy of rosemary essential oil against TMV through a combined approach involving in vitro assays, in silico modeling, and genotoxicity assessments.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection

Leaves of Salvia rosmarinus (rosemary) were collected from a private farm with the farm owner’s permission, following local guidelines and regulations at coordinates (Latitude: 31°11’22.0"N; Longitude: 29°57’27.7"E) in the Alexandria governorate, Egypt. The rosemary plant was identified by Prof. Dr. El-Sayed F. El-Halwany, Professor of Plant Ecology, Botany Department, Faculty of Science, Mansoura University, Egypt, and deposited in the Herbarium of Alexandria University, Alexandria, Egypt (Voucher Number: HTS-206511).

Essential oil preparation

The collected leaves were washed with running tap water and then air-dried at room temperature for two weeks to remove moisture. After drying, the leaves were finely powdered and subjected to hydrodistillation for three h using a Clevenger-type apparatus to extract the essential oil11. For this process, 500 g of dried plant material was combined with 2 L of distilled water. The resulting essential oil was then carefully separated from the aqueous phase and dehydrated using anhydrous sodium sulfate. The essential oil yield was calculated36, resulting in 0.57%. The essential oil was stored for one day at 4 °C in airtight dark vials and then subjected to GC-MS analysis37.

Identification of active compounds

A GC-MS analysis was employed to identify the active constituents of rosemary essential oil. This analysis utilized an Agilent 7890 A GC system equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, with a 0.25 μm film thickness). The oven temperature was programmed to increase from 60 °C to 280 °C at a rate of 4 °C per minute, with helium serving as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The mass spectrometer operated in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV, scanning a mass range of 50–550 m/z. The identification of the active compounds was based on their mass spectra and retention times, which were compared against standard compounds and referenced to the NIST library database38,39.

Source of viral isolate

The purified TMV isolate AM (accession number PP133743), previously isolated from infected tomato plants, serves as the viral source40. Before application, the purified virus was diluted to a concentration of 20 µg/mL in a 100 mM phosphate buffer, adjusted to pH 7.0.

Antiviral activities assay

The antiviral efficacy was evaluated on Nicotiana glutinosa, which functioned as a local lesion host for TMV. The virus-free seeds of N. glutinosa were surface sterilized before being planted in 12-cm plastic pots filled with a soil mix (1:1) sand and clay ratio. The seedlings were grown in a greenhouse at 23 °C with a 16-hour light photoperiod, receiving water as needed. Once germinated, the plants were transferred to an insect-proof greenhouse under the same conditions. Two weeks after sowing, plants with two fully expanded cotyledons were selected for experimentation, ensuring uniform size across the experimental group. The essential oil was dissolved in Tween 20 [0.01% (v/v)] and diluted with distilled water to achieve the desired concentrations. A Tween 20 solution of equivalent concentration served as the negative control. The assayed oil concentrations were 100, 200, and 300 µg/mL.

The antiviral efficacy of the essential oils was assessed using the inhibition percentage of local lesions developed on N. glutinosa leaves. The inhibitory percentage was calculated using the formula: IP = [1 - (T/C)] × 100, where IP represents the inhibition percentage, T represents the number of local lesions on oil-treated leaves, and C represents the number of local lesions on virally infected leaves without oil treatment. Three different antiviral assays were performed. The first was a protective assay, in which each N. glutinosa leaf was treated with 50 µL of an essential oil solution 24 h before viral inoculation. The second treatment was a curative, in which each N. glutinosa leaf was treated with 50 µL of an essential oil solution 24 h after viral inoculation. The third was inactivation, in which each N. glutinosa was mechanically inoculated with 100 µL of oil/viral mixture. The oil/viral mixture was prepared by mixing 50 µL of oil and 50 µL of viral solution and incubating for 1 h at 4 °C before inoculation. The mechanical inoculation of TMV was performed as described previously41. The viral inoculum concentration for each leaf was 50 µL of 20 µg/mL. Three different control groups were used. Control 1 was made up of untreated N. glutinosa leaves. Control 2 consisted of N. glutinosa leaves treated with viral inoculation buffer. Control 3 included N. glutinosa leaf treatment for Tween 20 (0.01%). Antiviral efficacy was assayed at the three oil concentrations (100, 200, and 300 µg/mL). Each treatment was biologically replicated three times. The local lesion-symptom development was observed and recorded daily, and leaves were collected at 5 days post-viral inoculation (dpi).

Impact of rosemary essential oil on viral accumulation level

The double-antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (DAS-ELISA) was used to detect and quantify TMV levels in infected tissues. ELISA was done on TMV-challenged inoculated and non-inoculated leaves of N. glutinosa plants. The collected samples at 5 dpi were transferred to the lab and directly evaluated for TMV infection using DAS-ELISA (DSMZ No.: RT-1065), according to the manufacturer’s instructions42. We classified samples as having a viral-positive infection if their optical density readings (OD405 nm) were at least double those of the healthy controls. The average absorbance values for each treatment group, as well as the control, are presented.

Cytotoxicity assay of rosemary oil using onion root tips

Onion bulbs (Allium cepa) were purchased from a local supplier. The bulbs were peeled and soaked in distilled water for 24 h to stimulate root growth. The prepared rosemary oil solutions were applied to onion root tips in the following manner. The control group immersed the onion root tips in water containing 0.01% Tween 20. Treatment Groups: Onion root tips were immersed in rosemary oil solutions at concentrations of 100, 200, and 300 µg/mL for 24 h. After treatment, the root tips were rinsed with distilled water and then fixed in a 3:1 ethanol: acetic acid solution for 24 h. The fixed root tips were then stained with a 2% aceto-orcein solution for 1 h. Following staining, the root tips were squashed onto microscope slides, and coverslips were applied43. Chromosomal analysis was performed under a light microscope. The mitotic index (M%) percentage for each treatment is determined using the following formula: M% = (number of dividing cells/total number of cells) × 100. The number of cells with chromosomal aberrations, including breakages, bridges, and abnormal mitotic figures, was recorded. The chromosomal aberrations were calculated using the method (Ab%) = (number of aberrant cells/total number of dividing cells) × 100. At least 1500 cells from each treatment group were examined to assess the frequency of chromosomal abnormalities44.

Molecular docking assessment

The identified compounds were subjected to molecular docking studies to evaluate their potential inhibitory effects against the three TMV proteins (coat, movement, and replicase). The coat protein structure was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (PDB IDs: 6 × 0R) with UniProt ID of P69687 and GenBank ID of CAA50227.1 (Supplementary File 1). The protein sequences of the replicase large subunit (UniProt ID: P03586 and NCBI Reference Sequence: NP_597747.1) and movement (UniProt ID: P03583 and GenBank: CAA24689.1) of proteins were retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Their three-dimensional structures were generated using the RoseTTAFold (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/) server (Supplementary Files 2 and 3, respectively). The docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina software (version 1.2.0; https://vina.scripps.edu/), with global docking that considered both the ligand’s and protein’s flexibility. The 2D and 3D protein–ligand interaction diagrams were drawn by using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 21.1.0.20298; free academic version available at: https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download). The docking results were analyzed based on binding free energy (kcal/mol) and the number of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions formed with the key residues of target proteins. Compounds with binding affinities below − 5.0 kcal/mol were considered significant and subjected to further interaction analysis45.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using CoStat software. Data from all treatments were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) based on three independent replicates per treatment. Before analysis, datasets were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then performed to evaluate significant differences among treatments. When the ANOVA indicated significance, Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was applied for multiple comparisons, and different letters were assigned to denote statistically distinct groups at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of essential oil on symptom development and viral inhibition percent

Under greenhouse conditions, applying rosemary essential oil significantly reduced the total number of local lesions on treated N. glutinosa leaves, while also delaying the appearance of symptoms (Fig. 1). At 3 dpi, the TMV treatment began to develop local lesions, which were clearly visible at 4 dpi. At 5 dpi, the leaves showed malformations, necrotic cells, and dead cells at the edges (Fig. 1). The N. glutinosa leaves treated with essential oil showed local lesions at 4 dpi, accompanied by a notable decrease in the overall number of lesions across all three concentrations (100, 200, and 300 µg/mL) and the three treatment methods (protective, curative, and inactivation).

Photograph illustrating disease symptoms on Nicotiana glutinosa leaves infected with TMV at 5 dpi, demonstrating rosemary essential oil’s protective, curative, and inactivating effects at 200 µg/mL.

In the protective treatment, the oil at a concentration of 300 µg/mL achieved the highest inhibition rate of 70.7% with a mean lesion count of 52.7 ± 1.25. A concentration of 200 µg/mL resulted in a 68.15% inhibition rate with 57.3 ± 2.05 mean lesions, while the lowest concentration of 100 µg/mL showed a 53.9% inhibition rate with 83 ± 3.74 mean lesions. Statistical analysis indicated significant differences across concentrations, with the highest concentration showing the most substantial antiviral effect. For the curative treatment, the oil at 300 µg/mL had the highest rate of inhibition (76.3%) and the most lesions (42.7 ± 3.68). It was followed by the oil at 200 µg/mL, which had a rate of inhibition of 75.4% and the most lesions (44.3 ± 3.30). The 100 µg/mL concentration had the lowest inhibition rate of 54.6%, with a mean local lesion of 81.7 ± 3.30 (Table 1). Significant differences were made among the treatments, with the higher concentrations showing more effectiveness. In the inactivation treatment, the 300 µg/mL concentration of rosemary oil achieved the highest inhibition rate of 62.8%, with a mean lesion count of 67 ± 3.27, followed by 200 µg/mL, which had an inhibition rate of 59.3% and a mean lesion count of 73.3 ± 6.6. The lowest concentration, 100 µg/mL, had an inhibition rate of 50.37% and a mean lesion count of 89.3 ± 2.49. Statistical differences were observed between these concentrations, indicating varying levels of effectiveness. Control plants infected with TMV had a mean lesion count of 180 ± 5.72, reflecting 100% disease incidence and 0% inhibition rate. In contrast, mock-treated plants had no lesions, indicating a 0% disease and 100% inhibition rates (Table 1).

Effect of essential oil on viral accumulation level

The DAS-ELISA results supported the effect of the essential oil on the development and frequency of local lesions on treated leaves. Essential oil at a 100 µg/mL concentration yielded ELISA values of 1.05 for the protective treatment, 1.03 for the curative treatment, and 1.11 for the inactivation treatment. The protective, curative, and inactivation treatments all had 46.5%, 45.2%, and 49.9% less virus at 100 µg/mL, respectively, than the TMV treatment (Fig. 2). The TMV treatment yielded an ELISA value of 2.02, whereas the mock treatment showed a value of 0.21. Significant changes were observed in the viral accumulation and inhibition levels across both the protective and inactivation treatments at the three essential oil concentrations (Fig. 2). No significant changes were seen in viral accumulation levels and inhibition percentages at concentrations of 200 µg/mL and 300 µg/mL for the curative treatment (Fig. 2).

Chemical composition of rosemary essential oil

The majority of the volatile components in the essential oil were identified and quantified using GC-MS analysis. The GC-MS chromatograph chart of the essential oil is shown in Fig. 3. Table 2 lists the retention time (RT), associated area percentage, molecular formula, and molecular weight for each identified molecule. The most abundant compound identified in the sample is α-terpineol, with the highest area percentage at 19.32%, followed by isoborneol at 14.07% and camphor at 13.20%. These three compounds constitute the primary components of the sample. Other notable compounds present in moderate amounts include eucalyptol (10.15%), bornyl acetate (8.62%), and caryophyllene (5.73%). Additional compounds, such as terpinen-4-ol (4.98%), epiglobulol (2.06%), citronellol (1.90%), and 11-octadecenoic acid, methyl ester (1.91%), also contribute to the composition. Several minor compounds are present in lower concentrations. For instance, cubenol (0.22%), α-copaene (0.28%), isoaromadendrene epoxide (0.35%), and humulene (1.07%) appear in smaller amounts.

Molecular docking analysis

The molecular docking technique was carried out to estimate the binding affinities of 29 compounds identified in the essential oil with the three TMV proteins. The molecular interactions investigation revealed that the active compounds exhibited differing levels of binding affinity with the coat, movement, and replicase proteins. The results presented in Table 3 indicate differing binding affinities for the primary constituents of rosemary essential oil (α-terpineol, isoborneol, camphor, and eucalyptol), alongside the compounds exhibiting the highest binding free energy (caryophyllene oxide, caryophyllene, cubenol, epiglobulol, and isoaromadendrene epoxide) against the three proteins of TMV. Isoaromadendrene epoxide exhibited the highest binding affinities of −5.90 and − 6.90 kcal/mol for the coat and replicase proteins, respectively. Conversely, epiglobulol exhibited the highest binding affinities at −7.10 kcal/mol for the movement, showing no significant variation with isoaromadendrene epoxide (Table 3). In the meantime, the cubenol compound exhibited binding affinities with the coat and movement proteins of −5.70 and − 7.00 kcal/mol, respectively, and with the replicase protein at −6.60 kcal/mol. Other compounds, including caryophyllene and caryophyllene oxide, demonstrated notable binding affinities, especially with the replication and movement proteins. Caryophyllene exhibited binding affinities of −6.60 kcal/mol for the replicase protein and − 6.50 kcal/mol for the movement protein. In contrast, caryophyllene oxide demonstrated affinities of −6.50 and − 6.10 kcal/mol, respectively. The docking analysis indicates that key constituents of rosemary oil, α-terpineol (19.3%), isoborneol (14.1%), camphor (13.2%), and eucalyptol (10.15%), demonstrate moderate binding affinity to viral proteins. Isoborneol exhibits a strong interaction with coat and movement proteins, with no significant changes with cubenol and epiglobulol compounds (Table 3). Conversely, camphor and eucalyptol specifically target the movement and replicase proteins, respectively. Although exhibiting lower binding energies compared to minor compounds, their high concentrations indicate a substantial contribution to antiviral activity, potentially through synergistic effects that influence multiple viral targets.

Molecular docking interactions of isoaromadendrene epoxide and epiglobulol with TMV-proteins

The molecular docking tests of the active compounds showed that isoaromadendrene epoxide had the best affinity for binding to two viral target proteins (the coat and replicase large subunit proteins). On the other hand, epiglobulol had the strongest binding affinity with the movement protein (Fig. 4). The isoaromadendrene epoxide has a strength of −6.90 kcal/mol. It bound to the replicase protein primarily through the hydrophobic residues of LYS287 and TYR289 (Fig. 4A). For the epiglobulol compound, the interactions with the movement protein with three residues in the binding site (ILE11, LYS32, and VAL34), resulted in a binding affinity of −6.90 kcal/mol (Fig. 4B). Regarding coat protein interaction, docking revealed a binding affinity of −5.90 kcal/mol through the hydrophobic residues PRO70, ILE71, and PHE117 (Fig. 4C).

Genotoxicity evaluation of rosemary essential oil

The present study evaluated the impact of rosemary essential oil (at concentrations of 100, 200, and 300 µg/mL) on mitotic division and chromosomal aberrations in the roots of onion plants (Allium cepa L.). Tap water was used as a control. The root tips displayed typical stages of cell division, as detailed in Table 4; Fig. 5. Following 24-hour exposure to rosemary oil, cell counts of 1452, 1425, and 1360 were recorded for the respective concentrations, with 804, 789, and 670 cells undergoing mitosis. The results obtained in mitotic indices of 55.4%, 55.3%, and 49.3%, respectively, were compared to the high mitotic index observed in the control. Significant chromosomal aberrations were detected at the highest concentration (300 µg/mL), including breakages and bridges. The rosemary oil treatments demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between concentration and mitotic index, with an increase in chromosomal abnormalities at higher concentrations (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Plant-derived essential oils serve as a natural solution for phytopathogenic diseases and provide effective pest management without reliance on synthetic chemicals, thereby minimizing environmental damage and safeguarding beneficial insect populations46,47. The present investigation revealed the efficacy of rosemary essential oil as an antiviral agent, as evidenced by a notable reduction in the development of local lesions and the accumulation level of viral particles across all treatment groups. The effectiveness was demonstrated through protective, curative, and inactivation assays targeting TMV in N. glutinosa plants. The curative treatment showed the highest inhibition rate, with 76.3% at 300 µg/mL concentration. In addition, protective and inactivation treatments also revealed substantial inhibition rates, with 70.7% and 62.8% concentration, respectively, at the same concentration. These findings suggest that rosemary essential oil may comprise multiple compounds capable of directly inactivating TMV and potentially disrupting or inhibiting the formation of the coat protein, which is crucial for adsorption or entry into the host18,48. Furthermore, the curative effects of the essential oil may also result from the small molecular compounds it contains, which can penetrate plant cells and directly inactivate TMV particles18. It is noteworthy that the compounds with the highest detection rates, α-terpineol (19.3%), isoborneol (14.1%), camphor (13.2%), and eucalyptol (10.15%), exhibit the lowest molecular weights among those identified.

The GC-MS analysis revealed that the most prevalent compounds detected in the rosemary essential oil were α-terpineol (19.3%), isoborneol (14.1%), camphor (13.2%), and eucalyptol (10.15%). It has been reported that several essential oils rich in these compounds are recognized for their significant biological activities, including antiviral properties. The α-terpineol exhibits notable antiviral properties, likely due to its capacity to disrupt viral envelopes or inhibit proteins associated with viral replication49. It was reported that the application of Melaleuca leucadendron essential oil, which contains α-terpineol (8%) as a secondary main compound after 1,8-cineole (58%), demonstrated anti-TMV activity ranging from 37.28 to 100% when applied at concentrations of 1, 2, and 3 µl/mL50. Furthermore, the use of Cymbopogon citratus essential oil, which contains α-terpineol (9.81%) as a secondary main compound, following terpinolene (10.7%), demonstrated an anti-TMV activity of 77.9% when administered at a concentration of 100 µg/mL18. Moreover, the application of pure α-terpineol compound at a concentration of 320 µg/mL exhibited moderate curative antiviral activity against TMV, achieving approximately 32% efficacy18. Isoborneol is a monoterpenoid compound detected in the essential oils of numerous plant species. The structure shows resemblances to camphor and borneol, and its functions are thought to align with those of other terpenoids. It is highly probable that it interferes with the virus’s structure or diminishes its ability to attach to and infect the host plant’s cells. Previous research indicates that it functions as a protective agent, inactivating virus particles before they can initiate an infection17,18,51. Camphor, another significant component, has been shown to exhibit both antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties, which could contribute to reducing the symptoms and spread of viral infections52. It was observed that the application of Micromeria graeca essential oil, containing camphor (8.1%) as a secondary main compound after α-bisabolol (10.67%), demonstrated anti-CMV activity of 59.3% at a concentration of 500 µg/mL53. Additionally, the utilization of the essential oil of Micromeria croatica (sourced from Stupacinovo, Croatia), which contains camphor (6.8%) as the third primary compound, following caryophyllene oxide (20.2%) and β-caryophyllene (10.2%), reported 34.5% inhibition against TMV when applied at a concentration of 500 µg/mL21. Eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), a prevalent monoterpene present in essential oils, demonstrates antiviral capabilities by directly obstructing virus propagation and modifying the host’s immunological response54. The application of Eucalyptus citriodora essential oils, characterized by eucalyptol as the predominant compound at 83.19%, demonstrated antiviral activity against potato leaf roll virus and exhibited the capacity to inhibit the viral mRNA expression of its capsid protein55. Moreover, the application of Melaleuca leucadendron essential oil, which features eucalyptol (58%) as its primary compound, has shown a notable level of effectiveness against TMV, achieving an inhibition value of up to 100% at a concentration of 3 µL/mL50. Additionally, the application of Artemisia argyi essential oil, characterized by eucalyptol (27.45%) as its primary component, along with bornyl formate (19.88%), exhibited an anti-TMV efficacy of 56.5% at a concentration of 100 µg/mL18. Furthermore, the use of eucalyptol compound (purity ≥ 99%) at a concentration of 320 µg/mL demonstrated moderate antiviral curative activity against TMV, reaching an efficacy of around 45%18. Consequently, the reported antiviral activities in the current study may result from the synergistic effect of these major compounds, whose antiviral properties have been well-reported previously.

Essential oils can exhibit dose-dependent toxicity, which could negatively impact plants, beneficial insects, or soil microbiota if not properly optimized56. The assessment of the genotoxic properties of rosemary essential oil revealed a dose-dependent influence on mitotic indices and chromosomal abnormalities. At 300 µg/mL, the oil demonstrated a notable decrease in the mitotic index and an increase in chromosomal abnormalities, including breakages and bridges. These findings align with previous reports, which indicate that R. officinalis essential oil exhibits low or negligible genotoxicity at moderate doses; however, higher concentrations may induce chromosomal alterations57,58. This suggests that although rosemary essential oil exhibits strong antiviral effects, it may also pose cytotoxic risks when used at higher concentrations. However, the mitotic index remained elevated at lower concentrations (100 and 200 µg/mL), and no chromosomal abnormalities were observed. This suggests that rosemary essential oil may be considered safe for use at these concentrations59–61. The observed genotoxicity at the highest concentration could be attributed to compounds such as camphor and α-terpineol. These compounds can potentially induce oxidative stress and DNA damage at elevated concentrations62,63. However, at low levels, they may either safeguard or damage DNA, membranes, and antioxidants64,65. From an application perspective, the absence of genotoxic effects at 100–200 µg/mL, where antiviral activity remains robust, suggests that rosemary essential oil may be effectively and safely exploited in agriculture if applied at carefully optimized doses. Recent work by Şanlı and Ok66 demonstrated that the foliar application of rosemary essential oil at low concentrations improved potato yield by up to 38%. In contrast, higher doses caused visible leaf damage and a corresponding yield reduction. Such findings underscore the importance of precise dose calibration and comprehensive toxicological evaluation to strike a balance between antiviral efficacy and crop safety, as well as environmental sustainability. For instance, low non-genotoxic concentrations could be integrated into eco-friendly plant protection strategies to mitigate viral and fungal diseases without compromising the genetic stability of the host plant. Nevertheless, actual field performance will depend on formulation stability, environmental factors, and plant physiology; thus, such doses should be validated only through controlled pilot trials. In addition, exceeding the safety threshold could pose cytogenetic risks, emphasizing the necessity of dose standardization, controlled-release formulations, and risk-mitigation practices. These include drift reduction, pollinator protection, buffer zones near aquatic ecosystems, and proper use of personal protective equipment. Incorporating encapsulation or surfactant systems could further enhance oil stability and minimize off-target effects.

The molecular docking studies indicated that isoaromadendrene epoxide (0.35%) demonstrated the highest binding affinity for viral target proteins, implying its potential as the most effective antiviral compound evaluated. epiglobulol (2.06%) and cubenol (0.22%) demonstrated a stronger binding affinity for viral target proteins, showing no significant difference from isoaromadendrene epoxide. The notable binding affinity (−6.90 kcal/mol) with the replicase protein likely arises from interactions between the ligand and the viral protein, potentially inhibiting viral replication and spread67,68. Furthermore, the highest affinity for the movement protein, indicated by a binding energy of −7.10 kcal/mol, likely inhibits the assembly and mobility of viral particles within the host plant. Although these compounds exhibit higher individual binding affinities, their low concentrations in the essential oil may restrict their overall influence relative to the cumulative effect of the major constituents. Consequently, such various minor constituents may contribute to the overall antiviral efficacy of rosemary essential oil, despite their lower concentrations. Previous research has demonstrated that minor components can interact with major compounds to improve antiviral efficacy, a phenomenon frequently observed in essential oils, where complex mixtures function together to produce biological effects49,69–72. Such synergistic interactions align with findings from prior research on plant-virus essential oils, wherein thyme and oregano oils offered timing-dependent protection against CMV and TMV infections19,73,74. The antiviral efficacy and defense activation are likely affected by both the numerous components and the complex interactions among them.

Caryophyllene (5.73%) and caryophyllene oxide (2.66%) are additional compounds identified at moderate concentrations, exhibiting strong binding affinity among three viral proteins. The two compounds demonstrated excellent binding free energy, showing no significant difference compared to cubenol, along with their well-documented antiviral properties48,71,75–78. The application of Teucrium arduini essential oil, which features β-caryophyllene (19.9%) and caryophyllene oxide (14.6%) as its primary compounds, demonstrated anti-TMV and anti-CMV activities of 25.7% and 21.9%, respectively, at a concentration of 500 µg/mL71. Furthermore, the utilization of essential oils from Teucrium polium and Teucrium flavum, characterized by β-caryophyllene as their main component at 52% and 23.1%, respectively, demonstrated anti-CMV activity of 41.4% and 22.9% when administered at a concentration of 500 µg/mL20. Additionally, the essential oils of Veronica arvensis and Veronica persica comprise caryophyllene oxide (14.1% and 10.1%, respectively) and caryophyllene (6.21% and 9.29%, respectively), which are among the five predominant compounds in these oils, demonstrating antiviral efficacy against TMV with inhibition rates of 56% and 69%, respectively79. Furthermore, the essential oil of Micromeria fruticulosa comprised pinocarvone (17.6%), borneol (11.2%), α-bisabolol (10.5%), caryophyllene oxide (4.6%), and linalool (4.5%), demonstrating a 43.6% inhibition of CMV at a concentration of 500 µg/mL80. Moreover, the essential oil of Micromeria croatica, obtained from Bojinac, Croatia, comprises β-caryophyllene (25.2%) and caryophyllene oxide (10.1%) as its primary constituents, demonstrating 33.9% inhibition against TMV21. Vuko et al21. reported that the application of individual β-caryophyllene and caryophyllene oxide at 500 µg/mL exhibited moderate antiviral activity against CMV, showing approximately 38.3% and 33.4% effectiveness, respectively. Conversely, Dunkić et al71. showed reduced antiviral activity against TMV, with inhibition percentages recorded at 7.6% for β-caryophyllene and 5.9% for caryophyllene oxide, both at the same concentration. Additional compounds, including bornyl acetate, identified at moderate levels (8.62%), and citronellal, found in lower concentrations (1.90%), exhibit antioxidant characteristics and demonstrate antiviral curative effects against TMV, achieving an inhibition rate of 57.9%18. The elevated docking scores noted for minor constituents underscore their potential for target engagement; however, the in-planta antiviral efficacy of essential oils is more likely influenced by two primary factors: (a) the abundance and mass-action effect of major compounds and (b) the synergistic or additive interactions characteristic of essential oil mixtures. This suggests that compounds with low concentration and high affinity can work in conjunction with dominant compounds to enhance the overall antiviral effectiveness. As a result, essential rosemary oil shows enhanced effectiveness as a versatile antiviral agent.

It was reported that components of essential oils can directly inactivate viral particles, with their effectiveness frequently associated with the complexity of their composition and the synergistic effects of bioactive compounds73. Consequently, the collective presence of α-terpineol, isoborneol, camphor, and eucalyptol, despite each having moderate binding affinity individually, may together produce a significantly more substantial antiviral effect. The docking results demonstrated that these compounds interact with various TMV proteins. Such a multi-targeted approach exemplifies the essence of synergistic action. The notable binding affinity of isoborneol to the coat protein, alongside the lack of significant changes with cubenol and epiglobulol, indicates its possible involvement in disrupting viral assembly and stability, which is a vital phase in the viral life cycle. The relatively strong binding affinity of camphor to the movement protein, coupled with the absence of significant alterations with caryophyllene oxide, indicates its potential involvement in intercellular movement, an essential process in the cell-to-cell viral transmission within plant cells. Moreover, the robust affinity of eucalyptol for the replicase protein indicates its possible role in hindering viral replication, which is essential for viral proliferation. The allocation of roles among the compounds allows the essential oil to target various stages of the viral life cycle at once, complicating the virus’s ability to evade inhibition or develop resistance73.

Overall, the observed differences in inhibition rates across treatments suggest that rosemary essential oil may have multiple mechanisms of action (Fig. 6), such as interfering with viral particle assembly or enhancing host resistance48. The protective treatment, involving the application of essential oil before viral inoculation, resulted in a significant reduction in the number of lesions on pre-treated leaves compared to the controls. This observation suggests that the oil may establish a physical, hydrophobic barrier on the leaf surface, which could repel the aqueous virus inoculum or modify the receptors on host cell surfaces, thereby hindering the attachment and entry of TMV into host cells72,81. The curative mechanism (inhibition of replication and movement) was the most potent identified, with the docking data providing a clear molecular basis. The in silico docking results indicated that specific molecules, such as isoaromadendrene epoxide and epiglobulol, effectively targeted and inhibited the virus’s essential components, the replicase and movement proteins, thus preventing viral replication and its spread throughout the plant cell. The strong binding likely confines the movement protein to a non-functional conformation, thereby inhibiting its assembly into the tubular structure required for guiding the virus through the plasmodesmata82. Such interactions explain why the curative treatment was the most effective in the study, indicating that the oil or its components interfere with the viral replication machinery post-entry. In the inactivation treatment (direct viral inactivation), where the essential oil and virus were incubated together before inoculation, the efficacy was comparatively lower. This may be due to insufficient exposure time or compound stability issues outside the host tissue; however, the partial inactivation observed still indicates that specific oil components directly interact with and destabilize viral particles, leading to reduced infectivity83. However, the inactivation approach still demonstrated notable inhibition rates, suggesting that rosemary essential oil contains components capable of weakening viral particles, albeit potentially to a lesser extent than those observed with direct curative or preventative application to the plant tissue. Together, these findings suggest that rosemary essential oil exerts antiviral effects through membrane-level interference, replication inhibition via direct protein binding, and destabilization of virions—mechanisms that vary in dominance depending on the treatment mode. The consistent antiviral activity across concentrations and treatment modalities underscores its versatility as a natural antiviral agent with promising potential for integrated plant disease management strategies.

Proposed antiviral actions of rosemary essential oil against tobacco mosaic virus (TMV). This figure illustrates the proposed pathways by which rosemary essential oil inhibits TMV infection, derived from protective, curative, and inactivation assays, as well as in silico molecular docking analysis. The protective pathway indicates that viral entry can be inhibited via a physical barrier or through modulation of host cells. The curative pathway, exhibiting the greatest efficacy, entails the inhibition of two critical intracellular processes: viral replication and viral movement. The inactivation pathway indicates a direct virucidal effect, wherein compounds compromise the integrity of the TMV coat protein. These mechanisms collectively result in a notable decrease in viral load and disease symptoms in Nicotiana glutinosa.

Ultimately, the obtained results validated that rosemary essential oil, rich in bioactive compounds, presents a promising natural option for managing TMV infections. The in silico docking analysis and greenhouse trials collectively highlight its antiviral potential, which is likely attributed to both major constituents and the synergistic interactions with minor compounds that can enhance bioactivity84–86. This complex interaction profile highlights the need for future studies to elucidate the synergistic or additive mechanisms among these constituents, thereby optimizing formulation and application strategies. However, the current findings are derived from greenhouse experiments under controlled conditions, which may not fully replicate the complexities of field environments. In open-field settings, factors such as volatilization, photodegradation, and environmental dilution can substantially reduce the persistence and effectiveness of essential oils87,88. To overcome these limitations, future research should prioritize multi-location field trials to evaluate efficacy across diverse environmental conditions and crop systems while testing advanced formulation approaches. Encapsulation technologies, such as nano- or microencapsulation, emulsions, and biopolymer-based carriers, have shown promise in enhancing the stability, bioavailability, and residual activity of volatile bioactives in agricultural applications89. Equally important are safety and phytotoxicity considerations that must be addressed before large-scale application. By integrating such strategies, understanding constituent interactions, optimizing formulation, evaluating safety, and conducting robust field trials, rosemary essential oil could advance from a promising greenhouse-tested treatment to a practical and sustainable antiviral solution in crop protection systems. Such advancements would also contribute to reducing the agricultural sector’s dependence on synthetic antiviral agents, aligning with global trends toward eco-friendly and integrated plant health management.

Conclusion

The current study highlighted the chemical composition of rosemary essential oil and its potential antiviral properties against TMV, as evidenced by in vivo experiments and molecular docking analyses. The findings demonstrate that rosemary essential oil effectively inhibits TMV, particularly when applied for curative and protective purposes, at concentrations that do not exhibit significant genotoxic effects, making it suitable for integrated pest management strategies. The GC-MS analysis identified significant compounds contributing to combating viruses, including α-terpineol, isoborneol, camphor, epiglobulol, cubenol, and isoaromadendrene epoxide. The evaluation of genotoxicity underscores the need to adjust concentrations to ensure safety while maintaining antiviral efficacy. The molecular docking studies indicate likely mechanisms of action, emphasizing the role of individual compounds in their interaction with and potential inhibition of viral proteins. While these in silico results provide valuable preliminary insights, they remain predictive and require further confirmation through targeted biochemical and molecular assays. Overall, the obtained results suggest that rosemary essential oil could serve as a natural element in IPM strategies. Nonetheless, additional investigation is required to elucidate its mechanisms and examine further field applications in plant protection.
