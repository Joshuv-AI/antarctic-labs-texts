Preparation and characterization of Fe3O4 nanoparticles coated with succinic anhydride-modified polypropylene glycol
Authors: Guobao Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles with universal dispersibility in both polar and non-polar media remains a significant challenge for advanced ferrofluid applications. In this study, polypropylene glycol (PPG) was chemically modified via esterification with succinic anhydride to introduce terminal carboxyl groups, facilitating its robust anchoring onto Fe3O4 nanoparticles through a facile co-precipitation route. The resulting Fe3O4@mPPG nanoparticles maintained a spherical morphology with a primary core size of approximately 9.5 nm. FT-IR spectroscopy and TG-DSC analysis confirmed the successful grafting of the modified PPG (mPPG), with surfactant loadings of 4.2% (mPPG400) and 6.7% (mPPG1000). Magnetization measurements revealed that the particles retain high superparamagnetism (Ms > 63 emu g−1), despite a slight reduction caused by the magnetic dilution effect of the coating layer. Zeta potential analysis demonstrated superior colloidal stability at pH < 4 and pH > 10, where absolute values exceeded 30 mV. This research provides a robust strategy for synthesizing “solvent-agnostic” magnetic nanomaterials, bridging the gap between polar and non-polar dispersion requirements.

Introduction

Superparamagnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles (MNPs) are pivotal in diverse fields such as magnetic fluid seals, targeted drug delivery, and environmental remediation.1–4 However, their high surface-area-to-volume ratio and strong magnetic dipole–dipole interactions often lead to irreversible aggregation. To counteract these forces, surface functionalization with organic surfactants is essential to provide either electrostatic repulsion or steric hindrance.5,6

Traditional stabilizers, such as oleic acid or citric acid, typically offer stability only in media of specific polarities (either hydrophobic or hydrophilic). This limitation restricts the versatility of magnetic fluids in complex industrial environments. Polypropylene glycol (PPG) is an exceptional amphiphilic polymer, exhibiting broad solvent compatibility under appropriate surface functionalization. Nevertheless, raw PPG lacks reactive functional groups capable of forming strong chemical bonds with the iron oxide surface, leading to poor coating stability.7–9

To date, various surface modification strategies have been developed for Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Small-molecule surfactants such as oleic acid and oleylamine are among the most widely used coating agents, offering good dispersibility in non-polar organic solvents; however, they suffer from poor stability in aqueous media and potential cytotoxicity concerns.10–12 Silica encapsulation provides excellent chemical stability and easy functionalization, but the coating process is complex and significantly increases particle size.13 Polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification imparts good biocompatibility and water dispersibility, but PEG-coated particles are generally poorly compatible with non-polar solvents because PEG is highly hydrophilic and often insoluble in non-polar media.14 Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) and other cationic surfactants can stabilize particles in aqueous media through electrostatic stabilization, but their inherent cytotoxicity often limits biological applications.15 Therefore, developing a simple and versatile surface modification strategy that enables Fe3O4 nanoparticles to be stably dispersed in both polar and non-polar media remains a significant challenge.

The novelty of this work lies in the design of a dual-affinity surface modification strategy. By utilizing succinic anhydride as a chemical bridge,8,16 we successfully converted the terminal hydroxyl groups of PPG into carboxyl groups. These carboxyl groups act as high-affinity anchoring sites that form bidentate coordination bonds with the Fe atoms on the nanoparticle surface. This “in situ coating” approach, combining a modified co-precipitation route with simultaneous surface grafting not only ensures a dense and stable organic shell but also achieves a rare dual dispersibility in both polar and non-polar media, allowing the same nanoparticles to be utilized across various carrier liquids without further modification.

Fe3O4@mPPG nanoparticles are attractive for ferrofluid applications because their surface chemistry can be tuned to achieve dispersibility in both polar and non-polar carrier liquids, enabling application-specific selection of the carrier medium for sealing, heat management, and lubrication.17–19

Experimental

Materials and preparation

Succinic anhydride was selected because it can introduce a terminal carboxyl group onto PPG through a mild ring-opening esterification reaction, while preserving the flexible polymer backbone. The resulting carboxyl-functionalized mPPG provides strong coordination sites for anchoring onto Fe3O4 surfaces. In addition, succinic anhydride offers a short and reactive spacer, which is beneficial for obtaining stable surface grafting without excessively increasing the thickness of the coating layer.

PPG (PPG400/PPG1000) was mixed with succinic anhydride at a 2 : 1 molar ratio. At 90 °C, the succinic anhydride underwent a ring-opening reaction, with one end esterifying with the hydroxyl group of PPG and the other end releasing a free carboxyl group. To ensure conversion, the temperature was subsequently increased to 150 °C for dehydration condensation enhancement, ultimately yielding mPPG with a highly active terminal. This step is crucial for determining the density of the subsequent coating.

A modified coprecipitation method was employed to control the Fe3+/Fe2+ molar ratio at 1.75, slightly lower than the theoretical value of 2 to compensate for oxidation losses during the reaction. The Fe3+ and Fe2+ solutions were mixed under vigorous stirring at 450 rpm and heated to 60 °C. Aqueous NH3·H2O was then added at a controlled rate until the desired alkaline condition was reached. Under the strongly alkaline environment, the nanonuclei rapidly nucleated and grew. After 30 min of reaction, mPPG was introduced, and the temperature was further increased to 80 °C to accelerate the chemisorption of carboxyl groups onto the particle surface. The reaction was then maintained for an additional 90 min to allow sufficient in situ surface coordination. These conditions were selected to promote rapid nucleation, limit excessive particle growth, and reduce pre-agglomeration. The schematic of the procedure and chemical reactions are detailed in Fig. 1. Compared with methods in which particles are first prepared and subsequently modified, this in situ coating strategy effectively reduces pre-agglomeration between particles.

(a) Synthetic process of modification of polypropylene glycol and mPPG coated Fe3O4 particles, (b) schematic representation of the procedure of synthesis.

For TEM observation, the dispersion was diluted and ultrasonicated before being dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid and dried naturally at room temperature.

Characterization

Morphology was assessed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) JEOL-2100, while crystal structure was verified by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) Brucker D8. Surface chemistry was analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) Nicolet iS20. Magnetic and thermal properties were evaluated using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) Lakeshore 7404 and thermogravimetric analysis-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) Netzsch STA449 F3, respectively. Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured using an Anton Paar SurPASS analyzer.

Results and discussion

Morphology and structure

The morphology and primary particle size distributions of the various samples are presented in Fig. 2a–c. The observed nanoparticles exhibit a predominantly spherical morphology, with average primary particle sizes for Fe3O4, Fe3O4@mPPG400, and Fe3O4@mPPG1000 measured at 9.51 nm, 9.66 nm, and 9.49 nm, respectively. These results indicate that the introduction of surfactants has a negligible impact on the size distribution of the magnetic cores.

Particle size distribution and TEM images of (a) Fe3O4, (b) Fe3O4@mPPG400 and (c) Fe3O4@mPPG1000, (d) high-resolution (HR) TEM image and (e) the selected area electron diffraction patterns (SAED) of Fe3O4@mPPG400. (f) XRD patterns of Fe3O4, Fe3O4@mPPG400, and Fe3O4@mPPG1000.

The particle size statistics were obtained from multiple TEM images collected from different regions, and only well-separated primary particles were included to minimize the influence of drying-induced aggregation.

A high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of a representative Fe3O4@mPPG400 nanoparticle is shown in Fig. 2d. While the organic surfactant layer is not clearly visible due to its low electron contrast, the core dimensions are consistent with the statistical size distribution analysis.

XRD patterns (Fig. 2f) show that all samples exhibit sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ = 30.1°, 35.5°, and 43.1°, perfectly consistent with the inverse spinel structure (JCPDS 74-0748). The average grain size calculated using the Scherrer formula is 9.5 nm, highly consistent with TEM observations. This indicates that mPPG coating occurs immediately after grain nucleation, and the repulsive force of the molecular chains limits excessive grain growth, thus ensuring good size uniformity.

Furthermore, the well-defined lattice fringes observed in the HRTEM image (Fig. 2d) and the distinct diffraction rings in the Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 2e) confirms the good crystallinity and spinel structure of the particles. The lattice spacings derived from the SAED rings are in excellent agreement with the XRD data, further validating the crystal structure.

Surface and thermal analysis

The FT-IR spectra of Fe3O4, Fe3O4@mPPG400, and Fe3O4@mPPG1000 are presented in Fig. 3a. The spectra exhibit primary absorption peaks within the fingerprint region. The successful esterification of the hydroxyl groups in PPG to carboxyl groups in mPPG400 and mPPG1000 was confirmed by the emergence of a characteristic band at 1728 cm−1, which is absent in the spectrum of raw PPG. The strong absorption peak at 1728 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of the ester bond and the free carboxyl group. The broad band centered at 3401 cm−1 is attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations of bound water. In all spectra, the two peaks at 2851 cm−1 and 2923 cm−1 correspond to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the methylene (–CH2–) groups in the PPG backbone.20,21 Furthermore, the C–O–C ether vibration at 1085 cm−1, characteristic of PPG, is clearly observed in both modified samples. Additional bands at 1374, 1280, 962, and 842 cm−1 are assigned to the –C–C– skeletal stretching and –C–H– deformation vibrations. Finally, the characteristic Fe–O stretching vibration at 566 cm−1 is present in both Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@mPPG samples, confirming the preservation of the magnetic core.22 Notably, the modified Fe–O bond (566 cm−1) exhibited a blue shift, confirming that a bidentate coordination was formed between the carboxyl anion (–COO−) and the Fe atom.23 This chemical bond strength is much higher than that of physical adsorption.

(a) FT-IR spectra of PPG, mPPG400, mPPG1000, Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@mPPG400. (b) TG-DTG, (c) DSC, and (d) VSM curves of Fe3O4, Fe3O4@mPPG400, and Fe3O4@mPPG1000.

To quantify the amount of mPPG anchored to the nanoparticle surfaces, TGA was conducted from 30 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere. Bare Fe3O4 nanoparticles exhibited a mass loss of approximately 5% below 500 °C, primarily due to the evaporation of residual and physically adsorbed water. In contrast, Fe3O4@mPPG400 and Fe3O4@mPPG1000 showed total mass losses of 9.2% and 11.7%, respectively. As indicated by the DTG curves, the peak weight loss temperatures for mPPG400 were 232 °C and 336 °C, while those for mPPG1000 were 245 °C and 367 °C. These peaks are attributed to the stepwise decomposition of free mPPG molecules and those chemically grafted to the nanoparticle surfaces. The minor mass loss observed up to 600 °C is likely due to residual molecular sieves used during synthesis. The DSC curves in Fig. 3c show exothermic behavior across the 30–800 °C range for all samples, corresponding to the oxidative decomposition and desorption of the surfactants. Consequently, the net surfactant content on the Fe3O4 surface was determined to be 4.2% for mPPG400 and 6.7% for mPPG1000.

The TGA curve reveals two stages of mass loss: desorption of physically adsorbed water below 100 °C; and thermal degradation of the mPPG chain between 200 °C and 450 °C. Calculations show that the coating weight of mPPG1000 (6.7%) is significantly higher than that of mPPG400 (4.2%). According to polymer conformation theory, the longer PPG1000 chain has a larger radius of gyration in the solvent, providing a stronger steric hindrance effect.

Magnetic properties

The magnetic hysteresis loops are displayed in Fig. 3d. All samples exhibit superparamagnetic behavior, characterized by negligible remanence and coercivity upon removal of the external magnetic field.24 The saturation magnetization Ms values for Fe3O4, Fe3O4@mPPG400, and Fe3O4@mPPG1000 were measured at 75.4, 65.8, and 63.1 emu g−1, respectively.

The saturation magnetization (Ms) decreased from 75.4 emu g−1 for pure Fe3O4 to 63.1 emu g−1 for Fe3O4@mPPG1000. This decrease is attributed to two factors:

(1) Mass dilution effect: the presence of the nonmagnetic organic shell increases the total mass, thereby reducing the magnetic moment per unit mass.

(2) Surface spin tilt layer (dead layer): incomplete coordination of surface atoms and surface defects lead to a decrease in magnetic order. Although the chemical bonding of mPPG stabilizes the surface, it also forms a magnetic dead layer of approximately 0.5–1.0 nm. Even so, the intensity of 63.1 emu g−1 is still sufficient to ensure a fast response under an external gradient magnetic field.

The present XRD patterns and magnetic results do not show clear evidence of phase transformation to γ-Fe2O3. Nevertheless, because Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3 have very similar crystal structures, minor surface oxidation cannot be completely excluded based only on XRD and VSM analysis. The in situ coating process was therefore designed to minimize post-synthesis oxidation and preserve the magnetite core.

The molecular weights of 400 and 1000 were selected to represent two typical chain-length regimes, enabling a comparison of coating density and steric stabilization. Compared with mPPG400, mPPG1000 provides a longer polymer chain and therefore stronger steric hindrance, which is consistent with the higher coating amount observed in TGA. In contrast, mPPG400 is more compact and may favor a denser interfacial packing. This comparison helps clarify the structure–property relationship of the mPPG coating.

Colloidal stability and size discrepancy

The zeta potential of Fe3O4@mPPG400 was analyzed across a pH range of 3 to 11 (Fig. 4a). The isoelectric point (IEP) occurs near pH 6.5. At pH < 4 or pH > 10, the absolute zeta potential exceeds 30 mV, providing sufficient electrostatic repulsion to maintain a stable dispersion.

(a) Zeta potential values at different pH values, (b) hydrodynamic size distributions of Fe3O4@mPPG400.

According to DLVO theory, the stability of a colloid is determined by the sum of van der Waals attraction and electrostatic repulsion.

Although the experiment did not present a separate zeta potential diagram for mPPG1000, based on the principle of chemical structure consistency, its potential change should be highly synchronized with that of mPPG400. Both rely on the ionization of the terminal –COO− groups. The advantage of long-chain PPG1000 lies not in electrostatic repulsion, but in stronger steric hindrance.

A significant discrepancy was observed between the DLS hydrodynamic diameter (186.9 nm) and the TEM core size (9.66 nm). This ∼20-fold difference arises from two primary factors:

(1) Hydration layer: DLS measures the “hydrodynamic” size, which includes the mPPG polymer shell and the surrounding layer of solvent molecules that move with the particle.

(2) Secondary aggregates (clusters): near pH 7, due to the low Zeta potential (∼−10 mV), electrostatic shielding is insufficient. Under the influence of magnetic dipoles, particles form “primary clusters” composed of multiple original grains. This cluster structure is often more stable than a single particle in practical magnetohydrodynamic applications, and can effectively prevent the sedimentation of extremely fine particles caused by Brownian motion.25

Compared with conventional surface modification strategies reported for Fe3O4 nanoparticles, the present mPPG coating strategy offers a distinct combination of properties that is particularly attractive for ferrofluid applications. As summarized in Table 1, oleic acid and other hydrophobic ligands can provide good dispersibility in non-polar media, but their stability in aqueous systems is limited. In contrast, PEG-based modification generally improves water dispersibility and biocompatibility, while compatibility with non-polar solvents remains poor. Silica encapsulation can enhance chemical stability and facilitate further functionalization, but it often requires multistep processing and may introduce a non-magnetic shell that reduces the effective magnetic response. CTAB and related cationic surfactants are also capable of stabilizing nanoparticles in aqueous media; however, their biological applicability is constrained by cytotoxicity concerns. By comparison, the Fe3O4@mPPG particles prepared in this work exhibit dispersibility in both polar and non-polar carrier liquids, which is achieved through a relatively simple one-step coating process under mild conditions. In addition, the magnetic performance is well preserved after modification, while the particles maintain favorable colloidal stability over a broad pH range. These results suggest that mPPG coating provides a practical balance between dispersibility, magnetic retention, and synthetic simplicity, making it a promising surface modification route for ferrofluid systems operating under varying environmental conditions.

Conclusions

In this work, Fe3O4@mPPG nanoparticles with a highly efficient coating structure were successfully prepared through a simple esterification modification combined with an in situ co-precipitation strategy. The effectiveness of succinic anhydride as a chemical bridge was confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy, which demonstrated that the terminal carboxyl groups of mPPG form stable bidentate chelate coordination bonds with the nanoparticle surface, ensuring robust coating stability. The introduction of mPPG endowed the particles with the unique ability to switch dispersibility between polar and non-polar media, overcoming the fundamental limitation of conventional single-function surfactants. Although the organic coating layer induced a slight reduction in saturation magnetization, the particles retained a high Ms exceeding 63 emu g−1, while exhibiting absolute Zeta potential values greater than 30 mV under extreme pH conditions, reflecting excellent anti-agglomeration performance.

These combined properties—chemical bonding stability, dual-solvent adaptability, and preserved magnetic response—make Fe3O4@mPPG nanoparticles promising candidates for a range of practical applications. In ferrofluid-based dynamic sealing systems, the ability to disperse in both polar and non-polar carrier liquids allows flexible selection of the base fluid according to operating conditions, such as vacuum compatibility or thermal stability requirements. In magnetic lubrication and heat dissipation applications, the preserved high saturation magnetization (Ms > 63 emu g−1) ensures a rapid and controllable response under external magnetic fields. Furthermore, the robust colloidal stability across a wide pH range suggests potential utility in water treatment and environmental remediation, where magnetically recoverable adsorbents are of considerable interest. These results provide a useful basis for the further development of versatile magnetic working fluids for engineering applications.
