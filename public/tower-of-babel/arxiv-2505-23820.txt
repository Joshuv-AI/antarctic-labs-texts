Arbiters of Ambivalence: Challenges of Using LLMs in No-Consensus Tasks
Bhaktipriya Radharapu, Manon Revel, Megan Ung

Abstract
The increasing use of LLMs as substitutes for humans in “aligning” LLMs has raised questions about their ability to replicate human judgments and preferences, especially in ambivalent scenarios where humans disagree. This study examines the biases and limitations of LLMs in three roles: answer generator, judge, and debater. These roles loosely correspond to previously described alignment frameworks: preference alignment (judge) and scalable oversight (debater), with the answer generator reflecting the typical setting with user interactions. We develop a “no-consensus” benchmark by curating examples that encompass a variety of a priori ambivalent scenarios, each presenting two possible stances. Our results show that while LLMs can provide nuanced assessments when generating open-ended answers, they tend to take a stance on no-consensus topics when employed as judges or debaters. These findings underscore the necessity for more sophisticated methods for aligning LLMs without human oversight, highlighting that LLMs cannot fully capture human disagreement even on topics where humans themselves are divided.

3.1
What are No-Consensus Questions?

We derive a taxonomy of no-consensus scenarios in
Table 1
based on a review of the NLP literature on disagreements arising from differing perspectives
Pavlick and Kwiatkowski (2019)
;
Plank (2022)
;
Yin et al. (2023)
;
Amayuelas et al. (2024)
and a survey of LLM real-world applications.

We define no-consensus questions as questions with multiple valid answers where human annotators are likely to disagree.
1
1

1

Let
X
be the binary variable that models the human response distribution of a question
q
with two valid answers
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
have empirical frequencies
p
and
1-p
respectively. The entropy then is
H(X)=-p\log(p)-(1-p)\log(1-p)
. Low-agreement questions have a high entropy; both answers are similarly likely. We define a dataset-specific threshold
t=\max(p,1-p)
to identify questions with a balanced human answer distribution. We generally set
t=0.7
to balance the number of examples with sufficiently high disagreement, i.e., we filter out questions where one answer is selected by more than 70% of annotators.
We curate no-consensus questions from existing datasets covering various sources of disagreement based on this taxonomy. For datasets with a human label distribution, we select questions with a high-entropy distribution. Overall, the No-Consensus Benchmark collect 10 datasets across 7 tasks as shown in
Table 2
. The No-Consensus Benchmark uniquely captures disagreements by focusing on questions with two valid answers, simplifying analysis. In contrast, other datasets on controversial views often elicit multiple correct open-ended answers, making it difficult to assess LLM neutrality across a broad answer space.

4.1
Answer Generator Mode

In the answer generator mode, the model sees two scenarios and is asked to say whether it agrees, disagrees, or finds both options debatable.

We allow the models to engage in chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning while answering as this approach has been shown to improve the model’s ability to identify ambiguity in questions

Impact of explicit ‘neutral’ prompt option

We conducted an initial analysis using GPT-4o, where we requested open-ended responses without any explicit incitation to agree or disagree, as outlined in Appendix
C.1
. In this setting, models often selected an answer from the provided options, and did not naturally adopt a neutral stance. Our initial prompt explorations suggested that models are more inclined to remain neutral when this is provided as an explicit option. As a result, we opt for a more constrained setup—explicitly allowing the option of a neutral answer or tie—across all modes to maximize models’ potential for neutrality and to eliminate the need to annotate responses as neutral or not. This aligns with prior research
Wen et al. (2024)
;
Patel and Anand (2024)
indicating that models stick to given options, but can improve ambiguity handling with conflict-aware prompting.

4.2
Judge Mode

We investigate the performance of LLMs as judges in evaluating pairs of stances. We employ two widely recognized configurations: pointwise and pairwise judges, using prompts from
Zheng et al. (2023)
. In the pointwise setup, judges assign a score (0--10) to each response individually when prompted to pay attention to aspects such as helpfulness, relevance, accuracy, and level of depth. In the pairwise setup, judges compare both stances simultaneously across the same dimensions, with the possibility of a tie. To capture variability, we conduct three rounds of judging with both configurations. Judges evaluate the stances generated by another instance of the same model, which is prompted to produce answers supporting each stance

4.3
Debater Mode

In the debater mode, we utilize three instances of the same model. The first two instances each adopt opposing stances in a debate, engaging in three rounds of discussion. In the initial round, they present their arguments. During the second round, they review their opponent’s arguments and refine their own while providing counterarguments. In the final round, they examine the complete transcript of all rounds and formulate a concluding argument. A third instance of the model serves as a pairwise judge, evaluating the arguments from each debater to determine whether one stance is superior or if the debate results in a tie.

The use of a pairwise judge is motivated by its use in existing studies that claim debate leads to improved reasoning
Du et al. (2023)
, honesty
Khan et al. (2024)
and helps LLMs be better evaluators
Chan et al. (2023)
. These studies also indicate that the one-by-one debate protocol yields the most successful outcomes compared to other methods, such as consultancy or interactive debate. Therefore, we adhere exclusively to this protocol in our study. We use the debate prompts and set up from
Khan et al. (2024)
. To mitigate position bias, the judge assesses arguments from both debaters in both orders, and we report the average score.

4.5
Metrics

Neutrality

We define neutrality as a metric applicable across all three evaluation modes, capturing the propensity of a large language model (LLM) to remain impartial on issues lacking clear consensus. In the
answer generation mode
, neutrality is measured by the percentage of times the model responds with answers such as
Debatable
,
Neutral
, or agrees with
Both
stances, depending on the task, rather than clearly siding with or against a stance. We use regular expressions to extract the model’s answer and compute neutrality as the proportion of such responses. In the
pairwise judge mode
, neutrality is defined as the percentage of times the model judges a tie between two competing stances, as opposed to preferring one over the other, averaged over three inference runs per example. In the
pointwise judge mode
, neutrality corresponds to the percentage of times the model assigns identical scores to both stances, also averaged over three rounds. In the
debate mode
, after reviewing transcripts from both sides, the judge—functioning similarly to a pairwise judge—can either call a tie or choose a winning stance. Neutrality in this mode is computed as the percentage of ties, with scores averaged across both presentation orders to control for bias introduced by answer sequencing.

Converting Ratings to Distributions

For two stances
A
and
B
, we convert ratings to probability distributions as follows. For each round
i
, the count
c_{i}
for each stance is:

c_{i}(\text{stance})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if stance wins}\\
0.5&\text{if tie}\\
0&\text{if stance loses}\end{cases}

In pairwise judging, we say that a stance wins if it is chosen, and if no stance is chosen there is a tie. In pointwise judging, we say that a stance wins, if the score assigned to that stance is strictly greater (respectively, equal) that that of the other stance; if they are equal, it is a tie.
For both methods, with
n
rounds, the probability distribution is:

P(\text{stance})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(\text{stance})/{n}.

pointwise judge configuration
, we observe several key trends. Similar to the pairwise judge, models generally adopt more definitive stances, showing reduced neutrality for subjective topics, controversial issues, and known unknown questions across all models. Conversely, all models show increased neutrality in linguistic inference tasks. Results are heterogeneous across models regarding moral dilemmas, societal and cultural norms, and the causal ambiguity tasks. This pattern relates to two important findings. First, from qualitative analysis of transcripts, models prioritize reasoning quality and presentation over the specific stance taken, often declaring ties when both positions are well-articulated. Second, these findings illustrate the answer generator model’s steerability, showing its ability to produce equally persuasive arguments for contrasting perspectives and receive comparable high scores from the pointwise judge as discussed in
A.4
.

Finally, in the
debate mode
, we observe a near-universal decrease in neutrality across all models when addressing controversial questions, subjective matters, known unknown questions, moral dilemmas, and causal ambiguity questions. Notably, Llama models exhibit an interesting exception, showing increased neutrality post-debate on societal cultural norms (Llama 3.1 8B) and linguistic inference ambiguity (Llama 3.1 8B and 70B), suggesting that debate can potentially enhance these models’ recognition of valid competing perspectives in certain domains.

The neutrality percentage, averaged across all datasets, is fairly stable across the LLM used as judge (x-axis), regardless of the LLM used as debater (y-axis) (see §

for results split by dataset).

5.2
Impact of model choice on debate results

We conduct an ablation study comparing the final decision of the judge in the debate when different models are used as debaters (see Figure
4
). We find that models maintain their initial stance consistently when acting as judges, regardless of the models engaging in debate—suggesting that a model’s stance is likely inherent, regardless of the persuasiveness of the transcripts from both larger and smaller models. We observe this consistent trend across all datasets, independent of debating model size and generic capabilities, indicating that models tend to adhere to their original neutrality scores with minimal variation. These results are compatible with other LLM-as-a-judge findings suggesting LLMs have a preference for their own outputs over those from other LLMs
(
Panickssery et al., 2024
;
Stureborg et al., 2024
;
Wataoka et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
)
.

5.3
Comparison to human answer distribution

For tasks with a known human distribution (Moral, Causal, and Linguistic Inference Ambiguity), we use Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) to measure the difference between human and LLM labels in judge mode (see Figure

).
Figure

compares JSD with the entropy of LLM labels, averaged across datasets. Judges with lower entropy (more consistent stances) align less with the high-entropy human distribution. Pairwise judges are generally more decisive (lower entropy) and less neutral than pointwise judges (see Figures
5
and
1
).

6
Discussion

Our examination of LLMs across the three modes can be compared to the three modes of pluralistic alignment
Sorensen et al. (2024)
: Overton, steerable, and distributional. As answer generators, LLMs demonstrate Overton alignment by providing balanced and varied responses. As debaters, LLMs exhibit steerable alignment by crafting arguments for opposing viewpoints and achieving high neutrality scores with pointwise judges, indicating effective steering (Appendix
A.4
). As judges, LLMs reflect distributional stances, similar to distributional alignment. For example, they often use utilitarian reasoning in moral dilemmas, such as prioritizing saving more lives, and default to “no” in scientific debates with mixed results, like “Does cranberry juice help prevent UTI?”. In political issues of ongoing debate, such as citizenship for children born on US soil to undocumented immigrants or the removal of “In God We Trust” from currency, LLMs rely on historical constitutional precedents. They also take definitive stances on subjective matters, like advocating for watching sports on TV for convenience or promoting vegetarianism based on health and environmental benefits, as shown in Appendix
E
.

7
Conclusion

Our study provides significant insights into how LLM alignment varies across different modes. We find that while models often exhibit extreme neutrality as answer generators, they do not maintain this neutrality when acting as judges. This suggests that current alignment efforts to generate diverse and nuanced answers
Muscato et al. (2024)
;
Bakker et al. (2022)
may create only a facade of neutrality, evident primarily when LLMs are used as answer generators. Furthermore, although previous research indicates that debate can enhance LLMs’ evaluative capabilities and lead to more truthful answers, our findings reveal that LLM debates do not preserve neutrality on topics, even those they were trained to handle neutrally. This work opens up new avenues for exploring the complexities of model alignment and neutrality, highlighting the need for more robust strategies to ensure consistent neutrality and express ambiguity across various LLM modes.

8
Limitations

While we control for prompt variation (coming from e.g., positional biases) and for the ambivalence of the definition of “non-consensus tasks” by sampling examples from a wide range of examples, there remain a few limitations we discuss below.

Task-based designations of “no agreement” may be too coarse-grained

One of the conceptual limits of this work pertains to our decision to deem tasks as “no agreement” instead of examples. In cases like linguistic ambiguity (
is a hotdog a sandwich
) this strikes us as obviously valid, but for other kinds of disagreement (
were Adam and Eve real historical figures?
from
Wan et al. 2024
) there may be meta-disagreement about whether any particular example should count as “no agreement” in the first place. For our practical purposes here (benchmarking LLMs), we have either argued for our own decision to count a task/dataset as “no disagreement”, or we have accepted the designations of the authors of the original datasources, but we acknowledge that meta-disagreement may exist.

Some Limits of Nuanced Answers and Ties

In this work, we have explored whether models can preserve nuance and neutrality across 10 “no agreement” tasks. There are many, real-world content moderation scenarios where one would want models to take such nuanced and neutral stances on tasks such as these. However, we acknowledge that not every “no agreement” task should be handled by the model with nuance and neutrality. Sometimes there are examples that people and/or legal systems disagree on, but for which model creators might want their model to take a stand. For example, across the world, laws differ about whether drinking alcohol is legal or not. Model creators in separate regions will likely want their models to generate a particular stance on questions like these. In short, the fact that some content is “no agreement” is not necessarily a sufficient reason to conclude that the LLM should employ a neutral or nuanced answer. We feel it is reasonable to build a model to take nuanced/neutral stances for the 10 tasks in our benchmark, and we feel that our results for the tested LLMs on these tasks are informative about their suitability to be used as judges or debaters. Alongside this though, we also acknowledge that others may choose to adapt our taxonomy or approach for other “no agreement” tasks, or other situations.

C.2
Judge and stance ablation over GPT-4o

In this study, we investigate two main aspects: First, we examine the impact of shorter responses, or stances, by using simple one-word replies such as “yes," “no," “agree," and “disagree" as inputs to the judges. This is to determine if the judges exhibit any inherent bias towards a particular stance.
Second, we explore various prompt configurations with GPT-4o, as detailed in
Gera et al. (2024)
(see §
D.2.2
). Unlike the prompts from
Zheng et al. (2023)
, which evaluate responses based on presentation, accuracy, helpfulness, and multiple other factors, the prompts from the IBM paper focus solely on assessing how "good" a response is. We also investigate the use of Likert scales alongside pairwise and promptwise judging methods. This is to observe if the model shows a preference for one stance over another when stances are presented without further explanation.

Our findings indicate that regardless of the judge setup employed, judges consistently assign low scores to brief answers like "yes" and "no," citing their lack of helpfulness and poor quality as shown in tables
6
,
8
,
7
. This is evident from the histogram distribution of scores.
The judges demonstrate extreme neutrality, largely disfavoring both stances due to their brevity, without much justification beyond the terseness of the answers.

Sustainable livestock farming practices are mitigating environmental concerns, promoting responsible consumption rather than elimination. Additionally, meat holds significant cultural and traditional value in various societies, making complete transition to vegetarianism impractical for many. Instead of advocating for vegetarianism, encouraging balanced and responsible dietary choices is a more effective approach to achieving health and environmental goals.

This cultural richness and community involvement make traditional weddings a more enriching and memorable experience compared to modern weddings.

Jim, Carol, Bob, and Nancy are all allowed to use their lamps on Thursdays. This Thursday Jim turns on his lamp at 8 PM. Just then, Carol turns on her lamp, and Bob also turns on his lamp. Stance B (Yes)

In a shared electrical system with limited capacity, circuit breaker failures result from collective, simultaneous electricity usage, not individual actions. Jim was one of three researchers who turned on their lamps simultaneously, which triggered the breaker. Therefore, Jim cannot be singularly responsible for the circuit breaker’s failure.

In this remote research scenario, Jim’s lamp activation directly caused the circuit breaker to fail. By turning on his lamp simultaneously with Carol and Bob, Jim was the third person to activate a lamp, which explicitly triggers the breaker failure. The research team’s established rule states that three people turning on lamps at the same time will cause the breaker to fail. Jim’s action was a necessary and sufficient condition for the breaker’s failure.
