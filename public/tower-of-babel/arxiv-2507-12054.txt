Contracting with a Mechanism Designer
Tian Bai, Yiding Feng, Yaohao Liu, Mengfan Ma, Mingyu Xiao

Abstract
This paper explores the economic interactions within modern crowdsourcing markets. In these markets, employers issue requests for tasks, platforms facilitate the recruitment of crowd workers, and workers complete tasks for monetary rewards. Recognizing that these roles serve distinct functions within the ecosystem, we introduce a three-party model that distinguishes among the principal (the requester), the intermediary (the platform), and the pool of agents (the workers). The principal, unable to directly engage with agents, relies on the intermediary to recruit and incentivize them. This interaction unfolds in two stages: first, the principal designs a profit-sharing contract with the intermediary; second, the intermediary implements a mechanism to select an agent to complete the delegated task. We analyze the proposed model as an extensive-form Stackelberg game. Our contributions are fourfold: (1) We fully characterize the subgame perfect equilibrium and show that the intermediary’s optimal mechanism coincides with the Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism parameterized by the principal’s contract. This characterization further reduces the principal’s contract design problem to a novel auction-theoretic formulation we term virtual value pricing , and reveals that linear contracts are optimal even when the task have multiple outcomes and agents’ cost distributions are asymmetric. (2) To quantify the principal’s utility loss from delegation and information asymmetry, we introduce the price of double marginalization (PoDM) and the classical price of anarchy (PoA), and derive tight or nearly tight bounds on both ratios under regular and monotone hazard rate (MHR) distributions. (3) We further examine these two ratios in a natural setting where the intermediary is restricted to anonymous pricing mechanisms, and show that similar qualitative insights continue to hold. (4) Finally, we extend our results on both PoDM and PoA to a robust framework that accommodates scenarios in which the principal lacks precise information about the market size.

Introduction

Since its emergence in
2006

(
Howe et al. 2006
)
, crowdsourcing has become an integral part of the modern economy, enabling companies and institutions to connect with a globally distributed workforce through an open call.
According to a report by
Dataintelo 2023
, the global crowdsourcing market was valued at USD 2.8 billion in 2023 and is projected to reach USD 7.5 billion by 2032, growing at a compound annual growth rate of 11% during the forecast period.
This growth is primarily driven by the increasing adoption of social and digital platforms, which enable more efficient and large-scale crowdsourcing efforts.

Prominent platforms such as Freelancer, 99designs, and Upwork demonstrate how crowdsourcing has expanded across various industries, including product development, marketing, data collection, innovation, and more.
One of these platforms’ most attractive features is their ability to provide flexible and quick access to a diverse talent pool, transcending geographical barriers to enable seamless collaboration.
This capacity is particularly appealing to employers who (i) face high costs when hiring local workers, (ii) have difficulty finding skilled professionals, or (iii) require the flexibility to scale projects up or down based on demand, including one-time hires.
Acting as intermediaries, these platforms allow employers to function as task requesters while relieving them of project management duties, facilitating efficient collaboration with crowd workers worldwide.

To better understand the dynamics of crowdsourcing markets, it is essential to recognize three primary parties: employers (also known as requesters)

The general workflow operates as follows: employers issue requests for tasks or projects, platforms facilitate the recruitment of crowd workers, and crowd workers complete the assigned tasks.
To motivate workers, platforms typically use monetary incentives to compensate for their efforts.

However, in this paper, we focus on monetary incentives and emphasize that the overwhelming majority of crowdsourcing tasks are completed for financial compensation.

In most cases, the types of crowd workers remain private, motivating platforms to adopt mechanisms that extract this information while optimizing the platforms’ profit.
In these mechanisms, crowd workers are incentivized to reveal their types (costs for task completion), after which the platforms allocate tasks and determine payments based on the revealed types.
This platform-worker interaction as a mechanism is well understood and has been extensively studied in the literature

.
However, most existing crowdsourcing models focus solely on this mechanism-based interaction between platforms and workers, overlooking the employers’ role in the three-party ecosystems.
In reality, employers play a crucial role in the whole system, as they are the ones who provide payment to the platform, which then compensates the workers and retains the difference between the two payments as its profit.
Unlike employers, platforms have no intrinsic interest in the task itself but are primarily concerned with profit maximization.
Thus, the way employers compensate the platform directly influences how platforms design their mechanisms.
We provide two concrete motivating examples.

Example 1.1
.

Freelancer

is a leading online crowdsourcing platform that connects clients with freelancers across various industries.
As one of the largest freelance marketplaces globally, it has a network of over 78 million professionals from 247 countries, specializing in fields such as web development, data collection, writing, and marketing.
Clients post job listings with requirements and budgets, while the platform ensures compliance with payment terms and project expectations.
The platform selects freelancers through bidding, algorithmic recommendations, or direct hiring.
Upon completion of the task, clients submit payment to the platform, which takes a service fee before transferring the remainder to freelancers.

Example 1.2
.

The online freelancer platform,
99designs
, connects clients with a global community of professional graphic designers, specializing in custom designs for logos, branding, websites, and packaging.
As one of the largest design marketplaces, it has facilitated over 1 million creative projects, with a network of designers from more than 100 countries.
Clients initiate a design contest or project on the platform by providing a design brief and offering a reward.
The platform recommends suitable designers based on client needs and the designers’ expertise.
Designers submit proposals to be selected for the projects. Upon selection, the platform collects payment from the client, subtracts its commission, and transfers the remaining amount to the winning designer.

Motivated by the applications discussed above, we introduce a
principal-intermediary-agents
model that captures the economic interactions among the employer (the principal), the platform (the intermediary), and the crowd workers (the agents) within crowdsourcing markets.
In this model, the employer interacts with the platform through a
contract
.
Under this contract, the employer delegates a task to the platform and commits to paying a service fee contingent on the task’s realized outcome, which represents the quality level of task completion.
To fulfill this contract, the platform must recruit a worker from a pool of available workers to execute the task.
We model this recruitment process as a Bayesian mechanism game, where the workers’ private types correspond to their costs of completing the task and are assumed to be independently distributed.
Each worker, once recruited, completes the task and induces a probability distribution over outcomes, each providing a fixed reward to the principal.
We assume these outcome distributions are publicly known to both the principal and the intermediary (see
Section

2.2
for further details on this assumption).

To justify our modeling of the employer–platform interaction as a contract, observe that the mechanisms employed by online platforms are typically not disclosed. Even when some details are available, they often consist only of high-level descriptions—such as the auction format—while critical parameters, like reserve prices or ranking algorithms, remain hidden.
3
3

3

For example, the reserve price on sites like
eBay
is hidden, and until the reserve is met, the system displays “Reserve Not Met.”

Consequently, the employer observes only the realized outcome of these mechanisms, namely the quality level at which the task is completed.
This setting aligns with the classic principal–agent model in contract theory
(
Holmström 1979
;
Grossman and Hart 1983
)
, where: (i) the employer acts as the principal and the platform as the agent; (ii) the platform’s choice of mechanism constitutes the agent’s hidden action; and (iii) the payment associated with each mechanism corresponds to the action’s cost.
Naturally, the platform’s use of unrevealed mechanisms reflects the hidden-action assumption of the classical model.
We thus refer to our modeling paradigm as
contracting with a mechanism designer
.

Beyond crowdsourcing platforms, our model naturally aligns with the broader economic framework of
double marginalization

(
Spengler 1950
)
, which describes inefficiencies resulting from sequential pricing decisions made by two monopolistic entities within a supply chain, such as a manufacturer and a retailer.
In our context, the employer acts analogously to the manufacturer by setting a contract (comparable to wholesale pricing), while the platform acts as the retailer by subsequently selecting mechanisms (comparable to retail pricing) to engage workers.
As in classic double marginalization scenarios, this decentralized decision-making by both parties can generate inefficiencies not present under an integrated, centralized decision-maker.

Our proposed three-party model raises several key questions:

How can the principal design a contract with the intermediary when the exact mechanism employed by the intermediary is unknown? How should the intermediary select her mechanism to recruit agents profitably given the contract offered by the principal?
What impact does the intermediary’s presence have on the principal’s utility?

The primary goal of this paper is to address these questions by
(i) characterizing the optimal contract and mechanism for the principal and intermediary, respectively,
and (ii) providing quantitative insights into the impact of the intermediary and the market size (i.e., number of agents).

1.1
Our Contributions and Techniques

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we propose a game-theoretical model that captures the key characteristics of the crowdsourcing markets, including the principal-intermediary-agents interaction, the intermediary’s hidden mechanisms, and workers’ private costs.
In our extensive-form Stackelberg game, the principal issues a contract, the platform selects a mechanism, and workers submit bids.
Second, we characterize the subgame perfect equilibrium of the game and provide a closed-form solution for the intermediary’s optimal mechanism.
This mechanism aligns with the Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism and is parameterized by the principal’s contract.
This, in turn, enables a characterization of the principal’s optimal contract design.
Finally, leveraging our equilibrium characterization, we derive tight or nearly tight bounds on the principal’s utility compared to benchmark scenarios where the principal can directly access agents without the intermediary.
In addition, we extend our analysis to a robust framework that accounts for scenarios in which the principal lacks precise information about the market size.

In the following, we provide a more detailed explanation of the above results and discuss their implications.
To derive the subgame perfect equilibrium of our Stackelberg game, we use backward induction—a widely used method in game theory.
Applying the standard revelation principal
(
Myerson 1981
)
, without loss of generality, we assume that the possible mechanisms of the intermediary are dominant strategy incentive compatible (DSIC), ensuring that the agents always report their true costs (see
Section

2.1
for details).
We first characterize the optimal mechanism that maximizes the intermediary’s utility for any given contract and subsequently derive the optimal contract that maximizes the principal’s utility.
This completes our characterization of the subgame perfect equilibrium.
Our characterization results are elaborated as follows.

Optimal mechanism characterization for the intermediary.

Suppose the principal has issued a contract to the intermediary.
In response, the intermediary’s objective is to design a DSIC mechanism that maximizes her utility, defined as the promised payment under the principal’s contract minus the total payment made to the workers in her mechanism.
We first show that any mechanism between the intermediary and the agents is equivalent to a single-item auction between a seller and buyers.
In this correspondence, the intermediary and the agents correspond to the seller and buyers, respectively, while the task corresponds to the auctioned item.
The intermediary’s utility in the mechanism is equal to the seller’s revenue in the corresponding auction.
Additionally, there exists a bijection between agents’ costs and buyers’ values of the auctioned item (see
Lemma

C.1
for details).
This equivalence enables us to apply the standard auction theory and demonstrate that the optimal mechanism for the intermediary is essentially the
Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism

(
Myerson 1981
)
parameterized by the given contract (see
Theorem

3.1
for the formal result).
Essentially, we establish a one-to-one correspondence between instances of our mechanism problem and single-item auctions.
As we will show later, our model is even more deeply connected to single-item auction theory.
We illustrate the optimal mechanism for the intermediary in the following running example.

Example 1.3
.

The task has a binary outcome (i.e., success and failure).
The reward of the task for success is
4
while the reward for failing is
0
.
There are two agents with identical success probability
0.5
and i.i.d. uniform distributions on
[0,1]
.
Suppose the principal and the intermediary share the reward equally (50-50 split).
Specifically, if the task is successfully completed, the intermediary receives
4\times 0.5=2
.
The optimal mechanism selected by the intermediary (given this 50-50 split contract) works as follows: if both agents’ costs exceed
0.5
, neither agent is assigned the task; otherwise, the task is assigned to the agent with the lower cost and she is paid the maximum value between
0.5
and
1
minus the other agent’s cost, regardless of the task outcome.

Optimal contract characterization for the principal.

The principal’s goal is to determine the optimal contract that maximizes her expected utility.
Leveraging the characterization of the intermediary’s optimal mechanism, we characterize the contract design problem under three scenarios in
Theorem

4.1
, which range from general to special: (i) agents have asymmetric outcome distributions and cost distributions; (ii) agents have identical outcome distributions and asymmetric cost distributions; and (iii) agents have identical outcome distributions and cost distributions.

Optimal contracts are generally complex, and linear contracts are typically suboptimal—achieving optimality only in highly restricted settings
(
Holmstrom and Milgrom 1987
;
Carroll 2015
;
Dütting et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. In contrast, our results establish the optimality of linear contracts even in settings where agents face multiple outcomes with possibly asymmetric cost distributions but identical outcome distributions (i.e., scenarios (ii) and (iii)). This substantially simplifies the contract design space.
Intuitively, when all agents share the same outcome distribution, the principal’s utility depends on whether or not to select an agent (instead of selected agent’s identity). The optimal mechanism, which determines the selection probability, depends only on the expected payment from the contract to intermediary. Therefore, if there exists an optimal (possibly nonlinear) contract that induces a certain expected payment, we can construct a linear contract with the same expected payment. By the reasoning above, this linear contract must also be optimal.

Interestingly, when the cost distributions are also identical (i.e., scenario (iii)), our characterization reveals that the optimal contract design problem can be reformulated as a novel
virtual value pricing problem
in a single-item auction: the seller offers buyers a take-it-or-leave-it price for an item but her received payment is equal to the virtual value of the price rather than the price itself if the item is sold (see
Section

2.3
for the definition of virtual value). Intuitively, the fact that the principal receives the virtual value of the price—rather than the price itself (or equivalently, the virtual value of the winning buyer’s value)—stems directly from the double marginalization effect. (The virtual value of a price is always less than or equal to the price, implying a revenue loss due to the presence of the intermediary.)
In
Theorem

4.1
, we show that the seller’s optimal revenue in the virtual value pricing equals the principal’s optimal utility under a contract.
Additionally, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the seller’s price and the principal’s contract.
This virtual value pricing formulation establishes a close connection between contract design in our model and pricing in the classic single-item auction and may be of independent interest.
We illustrate the optimal contract characterization for the principal using our running example.

Impact of the intermediary.

Understanding the intermediary’s impact on the principal’s utility is crucial for both developing theoretical insights and informing practical decisions in the crowdsourcing markets.
If the principal’s utility when working with the intermediary nearly matches that achieved through direct access to workers, then employing the intermediary is justified as a cost-effective alternative; conversely, a substantial gap would indicate that the platform’s fee is prohibitively high, potentially deterring the principal from using the intermediary.
To quantify the efficiency impact of the intermediary, we introduce two performance measures. The
price of double marginalization
(PoDM) is defined as the ratio of the principal’s utility when she directly engages with agents through a dominant strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) mechanism to her utility in our model with an intermediary. This captures the utility loss due solely to delegation. The
price of anarchy
(PoA) compares the principal’s utility in the ideal full-information setting—where agents’ private costs are observable and allocations can be optimized directly—with her utility in our model, thereby quantifying the total efficiency loss, including that from information asymmetry.
For clarity, we refer to the utility under the DSIC mechanism as the
second-best benchmark
, and the utility under the full-information setting as the
first-best benchmark
.
4
4

4

Our definition of the second-best benchmark slightly deviates from the standard in the auction theory literature
(
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
;
Deng et al. 2022
)
.

In our analysis, we study these two ratios under the assumption that agents have identical success probabilities and cost distributions.
Under this assumption, as we have established, the principal’s maximum utility in our model equals the optimal revenue achieved by the virtual value pricing.
By the correspondence established in our characterization of the intermediary’s optimal mechanism, the first-best benchmark corresponds to the maximum welfare in the single-item auction instances, whereas the second-best benchmark corresponds to the maximum revenue in the single-item auction.
Thus, bounding the two ratios in our model amounts to bounding their analogues in the auction framework—namely, the ratio of optimal revenue under virtual value pricing to the maximum welfare, and to the maximum revenue under a standard auction, respectively.
This connection allows us to leverage established auction theory techniques—such as the revenue curve and hazard rate function—to bound the two ratios.

We observe that the PoA is approximately equal to the square of the PoDM. As discussed above, given the established connection between our model and the single-item auction setting, the first-best benchmark in PoA and the second-best benchmark in PoDM can be interpreted as the expected welfare and expected revenue, respectively. Meanwhile, the principal’s optimal expected utility corresponds to virtual value pricing, i.e., the virtual value of the price times the purchase probability.
Since the expected revenue equals the expected virtual welfare, PoDM effectively measures the ratio between welfare and revenue in a modified instance where the buyer’s value distribution is replaced by the virtual value distribution from the original instance. Notably, this transformation does not preserve key structural properties: for example, a regular (resp. MHR) distribution may induce a virtual value distribution that is not regular (resp. MHR). Nevertheless, our analysis suggests that certain properties (e.g., probability tail bounds) implied by regularity or MHR conditions still (approximately) hold under this transformation.

1.2
Related Work

Our work lies broadly within the fields of contract design and mechanism design, both of which have been extensively studied and are central to the Econ-CS community.
For an overview, we refer interested readers to a recent survey on algorithmic contract design
(
Dütting et al. 2024
)
and a textbook on algorithmic mechanism design
(
Nisan et al. 2007
)
.
More specifically, our work connects to three key areas: market design for crowdsourcing, intermediation in online advertising, and Bayesian auction design.
Below, we outline these connections in detail.

Market design in crowdsourcing.

Designing effective mechanisms is a key challenge in crowdsourcing systems, where requesters seek to purchase services from crowd workers.
This is often studied as a
procurement
problem, focusing on mechanisms that solicit bids, determine worker selection, and set payments based on reported costs.

Singer 2010
,
Chen et al. 2011
, and
Neogi et al. 2024
explore incentive-compatible mechanisms under
budget-feasible mechanism design
, ensuring utility maximization within a budget, while
Shah-Mansouri and Wong 2015
extend this framework to large-scale crowdsourcing.

Singer and Mittal 2013
examines pricing mechanisms where workers accept tasks at pre-specified rates.
Additionally,
Sun et al. 2024
examines how entry restrictions influence effort and revenue in all-pay contests, while
Chawla et al. 2019
analyzes reward structures in crowdsourcing contests to maximize the principal’s utility, providing insights into optimal participation policies and prize allocations.
Additionally,
Chawla et al. 2019
and
Sun et al. 2024
model crowdsourcing as
all-pay auctions
, as some platforms require entrants to exert effort upfront to participate (see
Example

1.2
).
However, posted pricing alone fails to incentivize high-quality work, as workers can exert minimal effort without penalty.
To address this, requesters may use contracts where payments depend on output quality. Since effort levels are unobservable, mechanisms must dynamically adjust contracts as they learn about worker performance.
This motivates studying crowdsourcing under the
repeated principal-agent
framework, where workers arrive online and requesters continuously update quality-contingent payments
(
Ho et al. 2014
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Collina et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.
Previous work typically models crowdsourcing markets as a two-party interaction.
In contrast, our model distinguishes the requester, platform, and workers as three separate entities and examines all of their economic interactions.

Intermediation in online advertising.

In online advertising markets, the
ad exchange
functions as a intermediary, as it has exclusive access to advertisers (i.e., buyers) seeking to purchase impressions (i.e., items) from the publisher (i.e., seller), conceptually aligning with the role of the intermediary in our model.

Feldman et al. 2010
analyzes equilibria in a setting where advertisers compete for a unique impression from a central seller through intermediaries, with the flexibility to select which intermediary to engage with.

Stavrogiannis et al. 2013
;
Stavrogiannis et al. 2014
focus on advertisers who are not liquidity-constrained and bid through intermediaries but restrict attention to variants of the Vickrey auction.

Balseiro and Candogan 2017
examines the optimal contract a intermediary offers to advertisers when their budgets and targeting criteria are private.

Balseiro et al. 2021
explores multistage intermediaries in display advertising under second-price rules and characterizes a subgame perfect equilibrium among intermediaries and the seller.

Zeithammer and Choi 2024
studies multi-layer auction markets under first-price auction rules.
As noted in
Section

1.1
, when agents have identical success probabilities and cost distributions, the optimal contract design problem simplifies to a virtual value pricing problem, closely related to the publisher’s revenue optimization in online advertising.
Our results on PoDM and PoA are also relevant, as they essentially compare publisher revenues in markets with and without ad exchanges.

Bayesian auction design.

To bound the first-best and price of double marginalization in our model, we express the principal’s optimal utility through virtual value pricing, linking it to Bayesian auction design.
Bayesian auction design focuses on revenue-optimal and efficiency-maximizing mechanisms under Bayesian priors on bidders’ valuations.
Myerson 1981
introduces virtual values and characterizes the revenue-optimal auction as a virtual welfare maximizer.

Aggarwal et al. 2009
examines the trade-off between revenue and efficiency, showing that optimal mechanisms often introduce inefficiencies.
Several works explore simple yet near-optimal mechanisms:
Dhangwatnotai et al. 2010
shows how a single value sample can approximate optimal pricing, while
Jin et al. 2019b
analyzes the effectiveness of uniform pricing.
Auctions with MHR distributions have been widely studied:
Giannakopoulos and Zhu 2018
,
Jin et al. 2019a
, and
Giannakopoulos et al. 2021b
evaluates how simple mechanisms perform relative to optimal auctions under MHR assumptions.

Yan 2011
studies the impact of value correlation on auction revenue, while
Feng and Jin 2024
investigates when simple mechanisms can approximate optimal auctions even in irregular settings.

Double marginalization and efficiency loss.

Double marginalization arises when upstream and downstream firms with market power set markups independently, leading to inflated prices and reduced welfare.

Spengler 1950
shows that vertical integration can eliminate this inefficiency by internalizing markups—a structure conceptually similar to our benchmarks without an intermediary.

Rey and Tirole 1986
further demonstrates that vertical contracts, such as two-part tariffs, can replicate the integrated outcome without requiring ownership consolidation.

Perakis and Roels 2007
introduces the
price of anarchy
to quantify efficiency loss in decentralized supply chains under price-only contracts, a setting analogous to our anonymous pricing restriction.
While their metric focuses on total system profit, our measures center on the principal’s utility.

Cachon and Lariviere 2005
shows that revenue-sharing contracts can coordinate decentralized supply chains and flexibly divide surplus, though practical concerns such as administrative burden and incentive distortion may limit their effectiveness.

Bernstein and Federgruen 2005
analyzes decentralized supply chains with competing retailers under demand uncertainty and demonstrates that price-discount sharing and buy-back contracts can restore efficiency, yet neither study quantifies inefficiency through formal ratios.
More recent work by
Choné et al. 2024
investigates vertical relationships under asymmetric information and strategic foreclosure, focusing on welfare impacts without defining explicit efficiency metrics.

Loertscher and Marx 2022
further cautions that eliminating double marginalization through vertical integration may distort bargaining and harm competition.
In contrast to these studies, we define and bound two formal efficiency measures—the
price of anarchy
(PoA) and the
price of double marginalization
(PoDM)—to quantify the intermediary’s impact from the principal’s perspective in environments with informational and contractual frictions.

2.1
The Three-Party Model

We consider a model that involves three parties: the
principal
, the
intermediary
, and
n

agents
.
The principal has a task that can be completed by any one of the
n
agents. However, the principal cannot directly access the agents.
Instead, she can only interact with an intermediary who has access to the agents. Thus, our model can be described as a two-period interaction.
At time
T=1
, the principal delegates the task to the intermediary via a
contract

At time
T=2
, the intermediary recruits an agent via a
mechanism
consisting of an allocation rule that maps the agents’ reported costs to the selected agent and a payment rule that specifies how much agents should be paid.
The selected agent (if any) then completes the task at some cost. Details about the contract and mechanism designed by the principal and intermediary are discussed below.

We assume that the agents’ completion of the task can result in
m

is associated with a value
\lambda_{j}\geq 0

j
occurs.
Each agent

c_{i}
for completing the task, independently drawn from a publicly known distribution
G_{i}
. If selected, agent

\bm{\rho}_{i}=(\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2},\ldots,\rho_{i,m})\in[0,1]^{m}

Both the outcome distribution

Contract design between the principal and intermediary.

The principal cannot directly observe the actions of the intermediary, that is, the mechanism selected by the intermediary or the agent subsequently chosen to complete the task. Instead, the principal observes only the stochastic outcome of the task resulting from this mechanism.

Definition 2.2

(Contribution and contracted contribution of agents)
.

For an agent
i\in[n]
with an expected reward

and cost

1.

The

. We denote its distribution as

2.

The intuition behind these concepts is as follows.
Suppose an agent

is selected to complete the task.
Given the realized cost

.
Since there is a one-to-one mapping between an agent’s cost

and contribution

Moreover, given a contract

Notably, an agent’s contribution

2.2
Model Discussion

In this subsection, we discuss key assumptions regarding modeling choice.

To justify the assumption that the principal initiates the contract, we note that
in real-world crowdsourcing markets, requesters typically hold greater bargaining power because multiple intermediaries compete for their business. For example, both Upwork and Freelancer charge clients a similar fee of around 3
(
Mert Bulut 2024
)
. This competitive dynamic is further illustrated by shifts in the microtask market: on Amazon Mechanical Turk, requesters historically benefited from low fees, but after Amazon raised its commission to 20% in 2015, many migrated to alternatives such as Prolific, which offered better user terms and higher data quality
(
Banakou and Slater 2023
)
. These observations align with standard contract theory, where principals with strong outside options propose contracts that agents accept.

To justify the hidden mechanism assumption, we observe that in many real-world crowdsourcing platforms, it is difficult for requesters to observe the intermediary’s inner workings. Platforms like
Upwork
deliberately design their talent rankings, proposal sorting, and payment structures to be proprietary and opaque. Requesters typically see only surface-level outcomes, such as sorted freelancer lists and basic performance metrics. This opacity motivates our assumption that the principal does not observe the intermediary’s mechanism. Modeling full opacity isolates the core contracting problem and reflects a standard and realistic assumption for such markets.

Contract design with private information has been studied in previous work
(
Alon et al. 2021
;
Guruganesh et al. 2021
;
Castiglioni et al. 2021
;
Alon et al. 2023
)
.
Castiglioni et al. 2021
study a multi-dimensional private-type setting where both the cost and the outcome distribution are private information.
Guruganesh et al. 2021
consider a setting where only the outcome distribution is private.
In contrast, our model assumes that the only uncertainty lies in agents’ skill levels, which determine their costs to complete the task. This single-parameter type is also studied in
Alon et al. 2021
;
Alon et al. 2023
, where an agent’s cost corresponds to the cost per unit of effort in their model, as the outcome space in our model is binary.

To justify the assumption in our model that the principal and platform know workers’ outcome distributions, we note that crowdsourcing platforms systematically track task completion rates and historical outcomes. This enables them to estimate outcome distributions based on accumulated data.
While the principal lacks direct access to this data, they can still infer outcome distributions from publicly available information: Platforms display ratings, completion rates, and certifications, and workers often showcase their past successes in their public profiles to attract employers. These signals provide the principal with a reasonable estimate of success likelihood.
It is worth emphasizing that all our results also hold when the outcome distributions are private (see
Appendix

A
for a detailed discussion of this assumption).

Now we clarify the information structure in our model. The principal and the intermediary know the market size
n
, as well as the success probability
\rho_{i}
and cost distribution
G_{i}
of each agent
i\in[n]
. However, agent
i
’s realized cost
c_{i}
is privately known only to the agent herself.
Furthermore, the mechanism implemented by the intermediary is hidden from the principal, who observes only the task’s stochastic reward
\lambda
, or equivalently, the task’s outcome. The mechanism, however, is known to the agents before their costs are realized.
Finally, in
Section

7
, we consider a robust setting where the exact value of
n
is known only to the intermediary, while the principal knows only a range.

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{\bm{t}}=\left(\left\langle\bm{t},\bm{\gamma}_{1}\right\rangle-c_{1},\left\langle\bm{t},\bm{\gamma}_{2}\right\rangle-c_{2},\ldots,\left\langle\bm{t},\bm{\gamma}_{n}\right\rangle-c_{n}\right)

of the agents’ contracted contributions

.

•

If above virtual values are all negative, no agent is assigned the task; otherwise, assign the task to the agent with the maximum virtual value.

•

Define the payment

Implications of

The first two formulations in

In contrast, the third formulation considers essentially homogeneous agents (i.e., all agents share identical reward and cost distributions), and is expressed as the utility a single agent provides to the principal multiplied by the probability that at least one agent is selected. The first formulation reformulates the principal’s problem by expressing each agent’s winning probability as a function of the contract

The second formulation assumes all agents have the same expected reward profiles

Under this condition, we show the optimality of linear contract, thereby simplifying the principal’s contract design space from the

Since

While the threshold for each agent depends on the contract, her winning probability remains unchanged as long as her contribution meets or exceeds her posted threshold.

The third formulation assumes all agents have identical expected reward profiles and contribution distributions, simplifying the principal’s problem further into a

We outline the main ideas behind the proof of

To establish the third formulation for the identical expected reward profiles and distributions case, we reformulate the sum of winning probabilities to represent the probability that the first-order statistic of the agents’ contribution meets or exceeds the anonymous price set by the contract.

5
Impact of Intermediary and Price of Double Marginalization

In this section, we analyze the intermediary’s impact on the principal’s utility, which is of both theoretical and practical importance in crowdsourcing markets. If the principal’s utility in the presence of an intermediary closely matches that in a setting where she directly engages with agents, then the intermediary can be considered a cost-effective implementation channel. However, a significant utility gap may reflect substantial platform costs, potentially discouraging the principal from using the platform. To quantify this impact, we compare our model to two benchmarks in which the principal interacts directly with agents.

Double marginalization refers to the inefficiency that arises when multiple parties in a marketplace – such as an intermediary and downstream agents – each impose their own markup. It is well established in the literature that the presence of an intermediary typically reduces the principal’s utility due to this sequential distortion. This observation motivates the central goal of this section: to quantify the principal’s utility loss caused by double marginalization. In doing so, we aim to complement qualitative insights with a formal characterization of efficiency loss under various benchmarks.

To this end, we introduce the concept of the
price of double marginalization
(PoDM), which captures the efficiency loss resulting from the intermediary’s presence. Specifically, PoDM compares the principal’s utility under the second-best benchmark—where the intermediary optimizes on her behalf—to the utility achieved in our delegated mechanism design model.

Definition 5.1
.

The
price of double marginalization
(PoDM) is defined as the ratio of the principal’s utility under the second-best benchmark to her optimal utility in our model:

\mathrm{PoDM}\triangleq\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{U^{*}},

where
U^{*}
denotes the principal’s optimal utility in our model, and
U_{\textsc{sb}}
denotes the second-best benchmark, corresponding to the expected revenue of the virtual welfare maximizer mechanism:

U_{\textsc{sb}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}\sim\bm{F}}\!\left[{\max\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\left\{{\left(\phi_{i}(w_{i})\right)^{+}}\right\}}\right].

Intuitively, the PoDM measures the extent to which the principal’s utility deteriorates due to delegation to an intermediary, as compared to the ideal scenario in which the principal can directly implement a mechanism to maximize her expected revenue.

To evaluate the overall efficiency loss relative to the welfare-optimal outcome, we additionally consider the classical
price of anarchy
(PoA). This benchmark compares the principal’s utility under the first-best benchmark—which maximizes total welfare—to that in our model. The two benchmarks correspond, respectively, to the maximum welfare and the maximum revenue in a related single-item auction, as characterized in our earlier analysis.

Definition 5.2
.

The
price of anarchy
(PoA) is defined as the ratio of the principal’s utility under the first-best benchmark to her optimal utility in our model:

\mathrm{PoA}\triangleq\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}}{U^{*}},

where
U^{*}
denotes the principal’s optimal utility in our model, and
U_{\textsc{fb}}
denotes the
first-best benchmark
, corresponding to the maximum expected social welfare achievable when the principal can directly select the agent that maximizes welfare:

U_{\textsc{fb}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}\sim\bm{F}}\!\left[{\max\nolimits_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\right].

Recall that
Lemma

2.1
shows that the virtual welfare maximizer mechanism ensures truthfulness and achieves maximum expected revenue.
Since the contribution distributions
\bm{F}
are regular by
2.4
, we have the optimal mechanism for the principal is the virtual welfare maximizer mechanism.
Therefore, the second-best benchmark is equivalent to the expected revenue of the virtual welfare maximizer mechanism, i.e.,
U_{\textsc{sb}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}\sim\bm{F}}\!\left[{\max\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\left\{{\left(\phi_{i}(w_{i})\right)^{+}}\right\}}\right]
.

Furthermore, the first-best benchmark is equivalent to the maximized utility of the principal in the scenario where the principal has direct access to agents and knows their cost realizations.
Indeed, the assumption in this scenario implies that the principal can assign the task to the one with the lowest cost, thereby obtaining a utility equivalent to her contribution.
Therefore, the first-best benchmark is equivalent to the expectation of the maximum value between
0
and the first order statistic
w_{\max}
from the
n
contribution, i.e.,
U_{\textsc{fb}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}\sim\bm{F}}\!\left[{{\left(w_{\max}\right)^{+}}}\right]
.

In summary, the first-best benchmark yields the highest utility, followed by the second-best benchmark, while the principal’s utility in our model is the lowest.
That is,

U_{\textsc{fb}}\geq U_{\textsc{sb}}\geq U^{*},

which implies

\mathrm{PoA}\geq\mathrm{PoDM}\geq 1.

Remark 5.3

Throughout this section, we define the PoA with respect to the
principal’s utility

In many crowdsourcing settings, tasks are simple and uniform, requiring similar effort from workers and leading to comparable success probabilities. The intermediary also attracts a specific type of workers, where individuals with similar expertise, experience, and availability tend to self-select into the platform, resulting in homogeneous cost distributions. Given this natural homogeneity, we bound the ratios under the following assumption.

for every index
k\in[K]
.
Note that
R^{\prime}(q_{K})=\bar{w}
.
One can verify that this construction holds for any regular distribution
F^{n}
since the virtual value function is non-decreasing.
Consequently, for every
q\in[q_{k},q_{k-1}]
with
k\in[2:K]
, it holds

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})\leq R^{\prime}(q)\leq R^{\prime}(q_{k})=2R^{\prime}(q_{k-1}),

(3)

and for every
q\in[q_{1},q_{0}]
, it holds

\displaystyle 0=R^{\prime}(q_{0})\leq R^{\prime}(q)\leq R^{\prime}(q_{1})=2\underline{w}\cdot 2^{\log\kappa-2\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}\leq\frac{2\underline{w}}{\kappa}.

(4)

Since the support of
F
is
[\underline{w},\bar{w}]
, we know
R(1)\geq\underline{w}
.
Thus, we know that monopoly revenue
R^{*}=R(q_{0})\geq R(1)\geq\underline{w}
.
Moreover, since
R(q_{0})=0
, we have

\displaystyle R^{*}

\displaystyle={}\int_{0}^{q_{0}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle={}\sum_{k\in[K]}\int_{q_{k}}^{q_{k-1}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}{}\sum_{k\in[2:K]}(q_{k-1}-q_{k})\cdot 2R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})+\int_{q_{1}}^{q_{0}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\leq{}2\cdot\sum_{k\in[2:K]}q_{k-1}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})+\int_{q_{1}}^{q_{0}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\leq{}2\cdot\sum_{k\in[2:K]}q_{k-1}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})+\int_{0}^{1}R^{\prime}(q_{1})\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}{}2\cdot\sum_{k\in[2:K]}q_{k-1}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})+\frac{2\underline{w}}{\kappa}.

where inequality (a) holds by
Equation

3
and inequality (b) holds by
Equation

4
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[2:K]}q_{k-1}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k-1})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(R^{*}-\frac{2\underline{w}}{\kappa}\right),

which implies that there exists
k^{{\dagger}}\in[2:K]
such that

q_{k^{{\dagger}}}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k^{{\dagger}}})\geq\frac{1}{2K}\cdot\left(R^{*}-\frac{2\underline{w}}{\kappa}\right)=\frac{1}{4\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}\cdot\left(R^{*}-\frac{2\underline{w}}{\kappa}\right)=\frac{R^{*}}{8\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}+\frac{1}{8\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}\cdot\left(R^{*}-\frac{4\underline{w}}{\kappa}\right).

Notice that
R^{*}\geq\underline{w}
and
\kappa\geq\bar{w}/\underline{w}\geq 4
, we get
R^{*}-4\underline{w}/\kappa\geq 0
.
As a result, we obtain that

\displaystyle q_{k^{{\dagger}}}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k^{{\dagger}}})\geq\frac{R^{*}}{8\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}.

Note that by
Lemma

5.4
, the second-best benchmark satisfies
R^{*}\leq U_{\textsc{sb}}\leq\eta\cdot R^{*}
.
Combine
Equation

2
, we can obtain that

\displaystyle U^{*}\geq\max\limits_{q\in[0,1]}q\cdot R^{\prime}(q)\geq q_{k^{{\dagger}}}\cdot R^{\prime}(q_{k^{{\dagger}}})\geq\frac{R^{*}}{8\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}\geq\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{8\eta\left\lceil\log\kappa\right\rceil}.

Therefore, we have
\mathrm{PoDM}=\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{U^{*}}\leq\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)
if
4\leq\bar{w}/\underline{w}\leq\kappa
.

Finally, we consider the case where
1\leq\bar{w}/\underline{w}\leq 4
.

We let
q^{{\dagger}}
be the largest quantile such that
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq\underline{w}/2
and
q^{*}
be the monopoly quantile such that
R^{*}=R\left(q^{*}\right)
.
Notice
q^{{\dagger}}
is well-defined since
R^{\prime}(0)=\bar{w}
.
By definition, we have
0\leq q^{{\dagger}}\leq q^{*}\leq 1
and
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\leq\underline{w}
.
Besides, if
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq\underline{w}/2
it must hold
q^{{\dagger}}=q^{*}=1
.

On the one hand, if
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})=\underline{w}/2
, by definition, we have

\displaystyle\underline{w}\leq R^{*}=\int_{0}^{q^{*}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q=\int_{0}^{q^{{\dagger}}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q+\int_{q^{{\dagger}}}^{q^{*}}R^{\prime}(q)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}q\overset{(a)}{\leq}q^{{\dagger}}\cdot\bar{w}+R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\cdot\left(1-q^{{\dagger}}\right).

where inequality(a) holds since
R^{\prime}(q)
is non-increasing due the regularity of
F^{n}
.
Moreover, notice that
R^{*}\geq\underline{w}
.
Thus, we can obtain that

\displaystyle q^{{\dagger}}\geq\frac{\underline{w}-R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})}{\bar{w}-R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})}\overset{(a)}{\geq}\frac{\underline{w}-R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})}{\bar{w}}\overset{(b)}{=}\frac{\underline{w}-\frac{\underline{w}}{2}}{\bar{w}}=\frac{\underline{w}}{2\bar{w}}\geq\frac{1}{8}.

where inequality (a) and equality (b) hold since
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})=\underline{w}/2\geq 0
.

Consequently, we can obtain that

\displaystyle U^{*}\geq\max\limits_{q\in[0,1]}q\cdot R^{\prime}(q)\geq q^{{\dagger}}\cdot R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq\frac{R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})}{8}\overset{(a)}{\geq}\frac{\underline{w}}{16}\overset{(b)}{\geq}\frac{R^{*}}{64}\geq\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{64\eta}.

where inequality (a) holds by
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq\underline{w}/2
and inequality (b) holds by
\bar{w}/\underline{w}\leq 4
and
R^{*}\leq\bar{w}
.

On the other hand, if
R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})>\underline{w}/2
, then we know
q^{{\dagger}}=1
.
We finally obtain that

\displaystyle U^{*}\geq\max\limits_{q\in[0,1]}q\cdot R^{\prime}(q)\geq q^{{\dagger}}\cdot R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq R^{\prime}(q^{{\dagger}})\geq\frac{\underline{w}}{2}\geq\frac{R^{*}}{8}\geq\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{8\eta}.

Therefore, we can also obtain that
\mathrm{PoDM}=\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{U^{*}}\leq\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)
if
1\leq\bar{w}/\underline{w}\leq 4
.

Upper bounding PoA.

We next prove the upper bound of PoA.

By
Lemma

5.5
, we have that for any monopoly revenue
R^{*}\geq 0
, the largest first-best benchmark for one agent is achieved by the truncated equal-revenue distribution with monopoly revenue equal to
R^{*}
.
We can obtain that

\displaystyle U_{\textsc{fb}}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}1-F^{n}(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z\leq R^{*}\cdot\left(1+\ln\frac{\bar{w}}{R^{*}}\right)\overset{(a)}{\leq}R^{*}\cdot(1+\ln\kappa),

where inequality (a) holds since
R^{*}\geq\underline{w}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}}{U_{\textsc{sb}}}\leq\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}}{R^{*}}\leq\frac{R^{*}\cdot(1+\ln\kappa)}{R^{*}}=\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right).

As a result, we can obtain that for any regular distribution,

\displaystyle\mathrm{PoA}=\mathrm{PoDM}\cdot\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}}{U_{\textsc{sb}}}\leq\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)\leq\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left((\log\kappa)^{2}\right).

Lower bounding PoDM and PoA.

We now analyze
Example

5.5
, which shows the lower bound part of the two ratios, i.e.,
\mathrm{PoA}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(\left(\log\kappa\right)^{2}\right)
and
\mathrm{PoDM}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)
.

By definition, the virtual value for
z\in[z_{k},z_{k-1})
is

\displaystyle\phi(z)=R^{\prime}(q)=2^{K-k+1},

and the quantile
q_{k}=1-F(z_{k})=q_{k-1}+\frac{2^{k-1}}{K2^{K}}
and
q_{0}=0
, which leads to
q_{k}=\frac{2^{k}-1}{K2^{K}}
for every
k\in[K]
.

As a sanity check,
R(q_{k})=\frac{k}{K}
, and the virtual value
\phi(z)
is negative for
z\in(0,z_{k}]
. Moreover, we can obtain that

\displaystyle U^{*}=\max_{q\in[0,1]}q\cdot R^{\prime}(q)\overset{(a)}{=}\max_{k\in[K]}q_{k}\cdot 2^{K-k+1}=\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{2-2^{1-k}}{K}=\frac{2-2^{1-K}}{K}.

where equality (a) holds since
q\cdot R^{\prime}(q)=q\cdot 2^{K-k+1}\leq q_{k}\cdot 2^{K-k+1}
for any
q\in[q_{k-1},q_{k}]
.

In this example, we have the second-best benchmark
U_{\textsc{sb}}=R(1)=1
.
Consequently, we can obtain that
\mathrm{PoDM}=\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}}{U^{*}}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(K\right)
.

Next, we consider the PoA.
We have

\displaystyle U_{\textsc{fb}}={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z_{0}}1-F(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[2:K]}\int_{z_{k}}^{z_{k-1}}1-F(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[2:K]}\frac{k-2+2^{1-k}}{K}\cdot\ln\left(2+\frac{1}{2^{k-1}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\sum_{2\in[K]}\frac{k-2}{K}\cdot\ln 2

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}{}

\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}-3K+3}{2K}\cdot\ln 2,

where equality (a) and (b) hold by calculations, and inequality (c) holds since
2^{1-k}\geq 0
and
1/\left(2^{k-1}-1\right)\geq 0
.

Therefore, we can obtain that
\mathrm{PoA}=\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}}{U^{*}}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(K^{2}\right)
. Since
\kappa=z_{0}=\frac{2^{K}}{\ln 2}
, we have
\mathrm{PoA}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(\left(\log\kappa\right)^{2}\right)
and
\mathrm{PoDM}=\Omega_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)
.
∎

5.2
Comparison under MHR Distributions

In this subsection, we bound the two ratios under identical expected rewards
r
and MHR contribution distributions. Our first main result in
Theorem

5.6
shows that for MHR contribution distributions, both ratios are constant—a significant improvement over the results obtained for regular distributions. Notably, unlike our findings for regular distributions in
Theorem

7.1
,
Theorem

5.6
does not impose any restrictions on the support of the contribution distribution.

Theorem 5.6
.

For agents with identical expected rewards
r
and MHR contribution distributions
F
, the tight PoDM is in
[{\mathrm{e}},3.033]
and the tight PoA is
{\mathrm{e}}^{2}
. Moreover, if
n=1
, the tight PoDM is
{\mathrm{e}}
.

Our second main result in
Theorem

5.7
provides a market-size–dependent bound for both ratios and shows that for fixed contribution distribution, the two ratios asymptotically approach
1
as the market size tends to infinity. This implies that the impact of the intermediary’s presence and agents’ private information on the principal’s utility diminishes to zero as the market expands.

Theorem 5.7
.

For agents with identical expected rewards
r
and MHR contribution distributions
F
, then PoDM and PoA asymptotically approach
1
as
n
approaches positive infinity.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{H}_{n}=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}
be the
n
th harmonic number.
Then we have

\mathrm{PoDM}\leq\mathrm{PoA}\leq\left(1-\frac{\ln\mathcal{H}_{n}}{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1-F(0)}{{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathcal{H}_{n}-\ln\mathcal{H}_{n}+1}}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1}=1+\mathcal{O}_{n}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right).

Implications of
Theorem

5.7
.
The upper bound in
Theorem

5.7
depends on both the number of agents
n
and the probability
F(0)
.
Viewing the agents as a market of size
n
, the market quality can be measured by
1-F(0)
, the probability that an agent’s expected reward exceeds her cost.
There are two interesting observations.
Firstly, in a market of fixed quality, as the market size grows, the impact of the intermediary’s participation and the presence of agents’ private information on the utility of the principal diminishes to zero.
Secondly, for two markets of the same size, this impact is smaller in the higher-quality market (higher
1-F(0)
) compared to lower-quality one.

Proof overview of
Theorem

5.6
.

Here, we outline the main ideas behind the proof of
Theorem

5.6
, which consists of three parts.
In the first part, we start by scaling down the maximized utility
U^{*}
to ensure that the revenue from anonymous pricing is at most
{\mathrm{e}}\cdot U^{*}
(
Lemma

5.10
).
Using the ratio between the first-best benchmark and the revenue from anonymous pricing (

{\mathrm{e}}^{2}
.

In the second part, we apply another version of
Lemma

5.10
to scale down
U^{*}
and break the upper bound for PoDM into two multiplicative factors, one of which is provided by
Lemma

5.12
.
For the other factor, we use its monotonicity for scaling. We calculate the upper bound for
n\leq 44
, as shown in
Table

3
.
For
n>44
, since both factors decrease, we conclude that the upper bound of PoDM is
3.033
.
In the third part, we provide an example (
Example

5.7
) to illustrate the lower bounds of the two ratios.
The lower bound part of
Theorem

5.6
utilizes
Example

5.7
. The upper bound part utilizes
Lemmas

5.8
,
5.9
,
5.10
,
5.11
and

.

Proof overview of
Theorem

5.7
.

Here we provide a high-level overview of the proof of
Theorem

5.7
.
We first reference a conclusion that provides powerful tail-bounds with respect to the order statistics of the distribution (
Lemma

5.13
).
Through this conclusion, we prove that the ratio of the first-best benchmark to the principal’s utility has an upper bound related to the contract and the expectation of the first-order statistic (
Lemma

5.14
).
Then, we scale the principal’s optimal utility by selecting a specific contract, thereby obtaining an upper bound for PoA in relation to the number of agents
n
.

Example 5.7
.

Consider
n
agents.
Let
K
be a real number larger than
1
and denote a non-positive contribution
\tilde{w}=-\ln(K-(K-1){\mathrm{e}}^{-r})
.
We construct a contribution distribution
w
for the agent as

F_{r}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&&z\in\left(-\infty,\tilde{w}\right);\\
&\frac{1}{K}\cdot\frac{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-z}}{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right),&&z\in\left[\tilde{w},r\right];\\
&1,&&z\in(r,+\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

We know that
F_{r}
is an MHR distribution with
F(\tilde{w})=0
and
F(0)=1-1/K
.

Lemma 5.8

(
Aggarwal et al. 2009
, Lemma 1)
.

For any random variable
v
that follows an MHR distribution
F
with support in
[0,+\infty)
,
1-F(\theta)\geq\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}
, where
\theta
is the monopoly reserve price of the distribution
F
.

It is important to note that
Lemma

5.8
holds only when the support of the distribution is non-negative.
We next extend this conclusion to cases with arbitrary support sets.

Corollary 5.9
.

For any random variable
v
that follows an MHR distribution
F
,
1-F(\theta)\geq\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}\cdot\prb\!\left[{v\geq 0}\right]
where
\theta
is the reserve price for the distribution
F
.

Proof.

Recall that the hazard rate function of distribution
F
is
h(z)
.
By definition, we have
1-F(z)=e^{-\int_{-\infty}^{z}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z}
.
By definition of reserve price, we have
h(\theta)=\frac{1}{\theta}
.
Therefore, we can obtain that

\displaystyle 1-F(\theta)={}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{-\int_{-\infty}^{\theta}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{-\int_{-\infty}^{0}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z-\int_{0}^{\theta}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{-\int_{-\infty}^{0}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z-\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{1}{\theta}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}(1-F(0))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}\prb\!\left[{v\geq 0}\right].

where inequality (a) holds since
F
has MHR, we have
h(z)\leq\frac{1}{\theta}
for any
z\leq\theta
and equality (b) holds since
\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{1}{\theta}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z=1
and
{\mathrm{e}}^{-\int_{-\infty}^{0}h(z)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z}=1-F(0)
.
∎

A consequence of
Corollary

5.9
is that the principal’s utility under a linear contract
\alpha
can be lower-bounded as follows.

Lemma 5.10
.

For agents with identical reward
r
and MHR distribution
F
, given linear contract
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the principal’s utility, denoted by

, satisfies that

\displaystyle U(\alpha)\geq\max\nolimits_{k\in[n]}r(1-\alpha)\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}(1-F^{k}(r(1-\alpha)))\right)^{n/k}\right).

Proof.

In this proof, we specially use
v_{\max}^{(k)}
to denote the first-order statistic out of
k\in\mathbb{N}
i.i.d. variables drawing from a distribution
F_{\alpha}
.
By
Theorem

4.1
, we have

\displaystyle U(\alpha)=\phi(z)\left(1-F^{n}(z)\right)=r(1-\alpha)(1-F_{\alpha}^{n}(\theta_{\alpha})),

where
z=r(1-\alpha)+\theta_{\alpha}
.
Notice that by
Lemma

5.2
, the distribution
F^{k}
is MHR for any

.
We denote the monopoly reserve price of

\theta_{\alpha}^{(k)}

Based on Corollary

\displaystyle U(\alpha)={}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)(1-F_{\alpha}^{n}(\theta_{\alpha}))

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)(1-F_{\alpha}^{n}(\theta_{\alpha}^{(k)})))

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}\prb\!\left[{v_{\max}^{(k)}\geq 0}\right]\right)^{n/k}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}\prb\!\left[{w_{\max}^{(k)}\geq r(1-\alpha)}\right]\right)^{n/k}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}(1-F^{k}(r(1-\alpha)))\right)^{n/k}\right).

Moreover, when

\displaystyle U(\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}r(1-\alpha)(1-F^{n}(r(1-\alpha))).

And when

∎

In this section, we consider the case where the mechanism space of the intermediary is restricted. In particular, we focus on the space of anonymous pricing mechanisms, which are widely studied in the algorithmic mechanism literature and practically prevalent in the crowdsourcing industry.

\displaystyle x_{i}^{{\dagger}}(\bm{c})\triangleq\frac{1}{|W(\bm{c})|}\cdot\mathds{1}\{i\in W(\bm{c})\}.

7
Unknown Market Size

In practice, the principal may lack perfect knowledge of the number of agents or incur significant costs to acquire it.
To address this, we introduce
linear contract design for unknown market size
, where the principal knows only a range for the number of agents, i.e.,
n\in[s:\ell]
, rather than its exact value.
Our goal is to determine how the principal in our three-party model should design linear contracts to safeguard her utility in this robust setting.
To evaluate the principal’s utility under a given linear contract, we continue using the first-best and second-best benchmarks and redefine the price of anarchy and the price of double marginalization as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PoA}(s,\ell)=\min\nolimits_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\max\nolimits_{n\in[s:\ell]}\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}(n)}{U(\alpha,n)},

\displaystyle\mathrm{PoDM}(s,\ell)=\min\nolimits_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\max\nolimits_{n\in[s:\ell]}\frac{U_{\textsc{sb}}(n)}{U(\alpha,n)},

where for the number of agents
n
,
U_{\textsc{fb}}(n)
(resp.
U_{\textsc{sb}}(n)
) is the first-best benchmark (resp. the second-best benchmark) and
U(\alpha,n)
is the principal’s utility under linear contract
\alpha
.

In the above definition, both ratios are defined in the worst case over the range
[s:\ell]
of possible agent numbers.
To ensure that these ratios have upper bounds related to the market size range,
[s:\ell]
, we propose two contract design schemes under regular and MHR contribution distributions, respectively.
Our results show that when the market size is unknown, both ratios suffer a multiplicative loss compared to the bounds established for known market sizes in
Section

5
.
Under regular distributions, the loss factor is
\frac{\ell}{s}
(
Theorem

7.1
), whereas under MHR distributions, it reduces to
\frac{\log\ell}{\log s}
(
Theorem

7.2
). Interestingly, the former bound is achieved by designing the contract assuming the largest possible market size,
\ell
, while the latter is obtained by assuming the smallest possible market size,
s
.
Moreover, we provide an example illustrating that if the principal has no knowledge of the market size—i.e., if the number of agents ranges from
1
to infinity—then, regardless of the contract design, the gap between her utility and these benchmarks cannot be upper bounded (
Theorem

7.3
).

Theorem 7.1
.

For agents with identical expected rewards
r
and regular contribution distributions
F
with support
\mathtt{supp}(F)=[\underline{w},\bar{w}]

(0\leq\underline{w}\leq\bar{w}\leq r)
, if
1\leq\bar{w}/\underline{w}\leq\kappa
and the principal is only aware of the range of the number of agents
n\in[s:\ell]
, then

\mathrm{PoDM}(s,\ell)=\frac{\ell}{s}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left(\log\kappa\right)
, and
\mathrm{PoA}(s,\ell)=\frac{\ell}{s}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\kappa}\left((\log\kappa)^{2}\right)
.

The intuition behind this theorem is that when
n\in[s:\ell]
, if the principal designs the contract as if the market size were
\ell
, then the worst-case upper bounds for the two ratios increase by a multiplicative factor of
\left\lceil\ell/s\right\rceil
compared to the case where the principal knows the exact value of
n
.
Moreover, when
s=\ell
, meaning the principal knows the exact market size, this result aligns with
Theorem

5.1
and
Theorem

5.6
.

Theorem 7.2
.

For agents with identical expected rewards
r
and MHR contribution distributions
F
, if the principal is only aware of the range of the number of agents
n\in[s:\ell]
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{PoDM}(s,\ell)\leq\mathrm{PoA}(s,\ell)\leq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-F(0))\ell+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-F(0))s+1\right)}\cdot{\mathrm{e}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}_{s,\ell}\left(\frac{\log\log s\cdot\log\ell}{(\log s)^{2}}\right).

Under MHR contribution distributions,
Theorem

7.2
provides better results than
Theorem

7.1
.
Meanwhile, when
s=\ell
, the result of this theorem is also consistent with the results of
Theorem

5.6
.

We remark that the ratios in the above two theorems tend to infinity when
\ell
approaches infinity and
s
remains constant.
Intuitively, this captures the scenario where the principal has almost no knowledge of the range of
n
.
In
Theorem

7.3
, we show that in this case, no matter how the principal designs the contract, the two ratios cannot be upper bounded.
The detailed proof of
Theorem

7.3
can be found in
Section

C.7
.

Theorem 7.3
.

For any small constant
\varepsilon>0
and agents with identical expected rewards
r
and MHR contribution distributions
F

n\in[1:\infty]
, there exists no linear contract
\alpha
such that both
\mathrm{PoA}(1,\infty)
and
\mathrm{PoDM}(1,\infty)
have upper bounds.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper presents a three-party model that captures the strategic and contractual interactions in crowdsourcing markets, where a principal delegates task execution through a profit-sharing contract with an intermediary who, in turn, selects an agent via a mechanism.
Modeling this interaction as an extensive-form Stackelberg game, we characterize the subgame perfect equilibrium and show that the intermediary’s optimal mechanism corresponds to a Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism parameterized by the principal’s contract.
This characterization enables the principal’s contract design problem to be reformulated in a more explicit and tractable manner and reveals that linear contracts are optimal even when the task has multiple outcomes and agents have asymmetric
cost distributions, provided their outcome distributions are identical, substantially simplifying the contract design space.
Moreover, when agents also share identical cost distributions, we reduce the principal’s problem to a virtual value pricing formulation and derive tight or nearly tight bounds on the price of double marginalization (PoDM) and the price of anarchy (PoA), thereby quantifying the principal’s utility loss due to delegation and information asymmetry under regular and monotone hazard rate (MHR) assumptions.
We further demonstrate that our results remain robust when the intermediary is restricted to simpler mechanisms such as anonymous pricing, and when the principal has only partial information about market size.

Several promising directions remain for future research.
One natural extension is to study non-myopic intermediaries who seek to maximize cumulative utility over repeated interactions.
Another is to consider a reversal in the Stackelberg order, where the intermediary first selects a mechanism class and the principal subsequently responds with a contract—potentially altering the equilibrium structure.
It would also be valuable to explore more complex market structures involving multiple layers of intermediation, such as hierarchical or networked delegation.
Finally, extending the PoDM and PoA analysis to heterogeneous agent populations—particularly when both reward and cost distributions vary—would broaden the applicability of our framework to more realistic environments.

Appendix A
Unobservable Reward

Our results hold even when the reward profile
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
is unobservable to the intermediary.
For convenience, a contract
\bm{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m})
in our paper is defined as a ratio, implying that the intermediary’s payment is
t_{j}\lambda_{j}
when outcome
j\in[m]
occurs. In standard literature, contracts typically specify the outcome-based payment directly (i.e.,
t_{j}\lambda_{j}
). Thus, given a contract, the intermediary already knows the payment for every possible outcome. This information is sufficient for the intermediary to design the optimal mechanism, and therefore all our results continue to hold without requiring observability of
\bm{\lambda}
.

Appendix B
Two Intermediaries with Different Mechanism Spaces

In our model, the principal’s utility depends critically on the constraints faced by the intermediary in her mechanism design spaces. A natural question arises:
consider two intermediaries with different mechanism design spaces, if one’s mechanism space strictly contains the other’s, does the principal always benefit from the more flexible intermediary?
We show that this is not universally true by offering two examples, illustrating that the inclusion-relation of mechanism design spaces for the two intermediaries does not imply the order of the principal’s optimal utility.

Example B.1
.

Intermediary
A
can choose all mechanisms while intermediary
B
can choose a single mechanism that never assign the task to agents.
In this example, principal has higher utility facing
A
than
B
.

Example B.2
.

Intermediary
A
can choose all mechanisms while intermediary
B
can choose a single mechanism: assigning the task to the agent with the lowest cost and paying her the second lowest cost among the other agents.
In this example, principal has higher utility facing
B
than
A
.

The intuition behind is that the utility of the intermediary depends both on the allocation and payment of the mechanism, but only the allocation directly affects the utility of the principal.
Therefore, the intermediary that optimize her own utility within a smaller mechanism space may design allocation rules that are either more or less favorable to the principal.

\displaystyle U(\alpha,n)={}

\displaystyle r(1-\alpha)\left(1-F_{r}^{n}(r(1-\alpha)+\theta_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle U_{\textsc{sb}}(1)=\max_{z\in[0,r]}z\left(1-\frac{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-z}}{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}}\right)\geq\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}-{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}}{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}}\geq{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}-\varepsilon,

Lemma C.5
.

Define
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=0
and

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-(tz+1-t)^{n}}{1-z}{\mathrm{d}}z

for any positive number

.
Then, for any real

and

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}}\geq\frac{\ln(\frac{1}{2}ts+1)}{\ln(\frac{1}{2}t\ell+1)}.

Proof.

By induction, it is sufficient to show that

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n+1}}{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}}\leq\frac{\ln(\delta t(n+1)+1)}{\ln(\delta tn+1)},

where
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
.
Letting
\Delta_{n}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
, it is sufficient to show that

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\Delta_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{i}}\leq\frac{\ln(\delta t(n+1)+1)}{\ln(\delta tn+1)},

which is equivalent to showing

\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{i}}\leq\frac{\ln(\delta t(n+1)+1)-\ln(\delta tn+1)}{\ln(\delta tn+1)}=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)}{\ln(\delta tn+1)}.

We rewrite the above condition as

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\Delta_{n}}\geq\frac{\ln(\delta tn+1)}{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta ti+1}+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)}.

Therefore, by induction, it suffices to show

\frac{\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_{n}}\geq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta t(n-1)+1}+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)},

for any integer

.

Define real function
\xi\colon z\mapsto z\ln\left(\frac{1}{z}+1\right)
.
We know
\xi(\cdot)
is increasing since

\xi^{\prime}(z)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{z}+1\right)-\frac{1}{z+1}=\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u}{u(u+1)^{2}}>0,

Hence, we further have
\xi\left(\frac{\delta tn+1}{\delta t}\right)\geq\xi\left(\frac{\delta t(n-1)+1}{\delta t}\right)
.
This implies that

\frac{\delta tn+1}{\delta t}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)\geq\frac{\delta t(n-1)+1}{\delta t}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta t(n-1)+1}+1\right),

which is equivalent to the fact that

\frac{\delta tn+1}{\delta t(n-1)+1}\geq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta t(n-1)+1}+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)}.

Finally, according to

C.6
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_{n}}\geq\frac{\delta tn+1}{\delta t(n-1)+1}\geq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta t(n-1)+1}+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{\delta t}{\delta tn+1}+1\right)}.

which gives us the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

Define
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=0
and

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-(tz+1-t)^{n}}{1-z}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z,

for any positive number
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and real
t\in[0,1]
.
Then, we have

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}}{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}tn+1}{\frac{1}{2}t(n-1)+1}.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{n}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
.
It is sufficient to show

\frac{\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_{n}}\geq\frac{\delta tn+1}{\delta t(n-1)+1}.

First, by the definition of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}={}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1-(tz+1-t)^{n+1}}{1-z}-\frac{1-(tz+1-t)^{n}}{1-z}\right)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle t\cdot\int_{0}^{1}(tz+1-t)^{n}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1-(1-t)^{n+1}}{n+1}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\Delta_{n-1}\cdot(\delta t(n-1)+1)-\Delta_{n}\cdot(\delta tn+1)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1-(1-t)^{n})\cdot\left(\delta t+\frac{1-\delta t}{n}\right)-(1-(1-t)^{n+1})\cdot\left(\delta t+\frac{1-\delta t}{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1-\delta t}{n(n+1)}-(1-t)^{n}\cdot\left(\delta t+\frac{1-\delta t}{n}\right)+(1-t)^{n+1}\cdot\left(\delta t+\frac{1-\delta t}{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1-\delta t}{n(n+1)}-(1-t)^{n}\cdot\left(\delta t^{2}+\frac{(nt+1)(1-\delta t)}{n(n+1)}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1-\delta t}{n(n+1)}-(1-t)^{n}\cdot\frac{\delta t^{2}n^{2}+tn+1-\delta t}{n(n+1)}.

This indicates that we only need to show the function

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)={}

\displaystyle(1-\delta t)-(1-t)^{z}\left(\delta t^{2}z^{2}+tz+1-\delta t\right)

is non-negative when
z
is a positive integer.
Since
g_{t}(0)=0
, it is sufficient to show
g_{t}(\cdot)
is weakly increasing for any fixed
t\in[0,1]
.

Next, notice that the first derivation of the function
g_{t}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{t}(z)=-(1-t)^{z}\left(\ln(1-t)(\delta t^{2}z^{2}+tz+1-\delta t)+(2\delta t^{2}z+t)\right).

Clearly, we have
g^{\prime}_{1}(z)=2\delta z+1\geq 0
.
Now, we assume that
t\in[0,1)
.

On the one hand, for any
t\in[0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{-g^{\prime}_{t}(z)}{(1-t)^{z}}\right|_{z=0}=\ln(1-t)\left(1-\frac{1}{2}t\right)+t=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{1-t}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{u}+\ln u-1\right)\cdot{\mathrm{d}}u\leq 0.

On the other hand, since
\ln(1-t)<0
for
t\in[0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}z}}\left(\frac{-g^{\prime}_{t}(z)}{(1-t)^{z}}\right)=t\left(\ln(1-t)(2\delta tz+1)+2\delta t\right)=t\left(\ln(1-t)(tz+1)+t\right)\leq t(\ln(1-t)+t)\leq 0,

which implies that the function
-g^{\prime}_{t}(z)/(1-t)^{z}
is weakly decreasing.
Combining the above two facts, we derive that for any
z\geq 0
, it holds
-g^{\prime}_{t}(z)/(1-t)^{z}\leq 0
, leading that
g^{\prime}_{t}(z)\geq 0
.

As a result, we finally obtain that
g_{t}(\cdot)
is weakly increasing for
t\in[0,1)
.
This completes our proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Lemma

7.5
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.5
.

Let
\bar{F}
be the conditional distribution conditioning on the event that the random value following
F
is non-negative.
Denote by
\bar{\mu}_{n}
the expected value of the first-order statistic out of
n
i.i.d. variables drawing from distribution
\bar{F}
.
Define
t=1-F(0)
and
B(n,k)=\binom{n}{k}t^{k}(1-t)^{n-k}
.
According to
Lemma

C.4
, we know

U_{\textsc{fb}}(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{n-i}\cdot\bar{\mu}_{i}.

For given
s
and
\ell
with
s\leq\ell
, we define a positive real
k=\sqrt{s\ell}t
, so that
st\leq k\leq\ell t
.
Based on
Lemma

C.3
, we can obtain a lower bound of
\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}(s)}{U_{\textsc{fb}}(\ell)}
by replacing
\bar{\mu}_{i}
with
\mathcal{H}_{i}
as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}(s)}{U_{\textsc{fb}}(\ell)}\geq{}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k\rfloor}B(s,i)\bar{\mu}_{i}+\sum_{i=\lfloor k\rfloor}^{s}B(s,i)\bar{\mu}_{k}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil k\rceil}B(\ell,i)\bar{\mu}_{k}+\sum_{i=\lceil k\rceil}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\bar{\mu}_{i}}\geq{}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k\rfloor}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}+\sum_{i=\lfloor k\rfloor}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{k}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil k\rceil}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{k}+\sum_{i=\lceil k\rceil}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}.

In addition, by using the Chernoff inequalities and
Lemma

C.3
, we further obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}(s)}{U_{\textsc{fb}}(\ell)}\geq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k\rfloor}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}+\sum_{i=\lfloor k\rfloor}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{k}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil k\rceil}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{k}+\sum_{i=\lceil k\rceil}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}-(\mathcal{H}_{s}-\mathcal{H}_{k})\sum_{i=\lfloor k\rfloor}^{s}B(s,i)}{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}+\mathcal{H}_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil k\rceil}B(\ell,i)}

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}-(\mathcal{H}_{s}-\mathcal{H}_{k}){\mathrm{e}}^{-2s(1-t-(1-\lfloor k\rfloor/n))^{2})}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}+\mathcal{H}_{k}{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\ell(t-\lceil k\rceil/n)^{2})}}

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}-\mathcal{O}_{s,\ell}\left(\ln\ell\cdot{\mathrm{e}}^{-t\sqrt{s\ell}}\right).

Recall that
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=0
and

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-(tz+1-t)^{n}}{1-z}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}z,

for any positive number
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and real
t\in[0,1]
.
Consequentially, according to
lemmas

C.4
and
C.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{U_{\textsc{fb}}(s)}{U_{\textsc{fb}}(\ell)}\geq\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s}B(s,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}B(\ell,i)\mathcal{H}_{i}}-\mathcal{O}_{s,\ell}\left(\ln\ell\cdot{\mathrm{e}}^{-t\sqrt{s\ell}}\right)=\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}}-o_{s,\ell}\left(1\right)\geq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}ts+1\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}t\ell+1\right)}-o_{s,\ell}\left(1\right),

which completes the proof.
∎
