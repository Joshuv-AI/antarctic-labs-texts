Perceived Importance of Good Death Components Among Patients With Advanced Cancer and Family Caregivers
Authors: Hammoda Abu-Odah, Gulbeyaz Can, Sotirios Plakas, Juanjuan Zhao, Xixi Wang, Alexandros Stergiou, Ourania Govina, Kimya Kilicaslan, Bahar Ozdemir, Matthew J. Allsop, Mitsunori Miyashita, Alex Molassiotis, Janelle Yorke, Frances Kam Yuet Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow do perceived priorities for good death components differ across settings and between patients with advanced cancer and family caregivers?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 1059 participants, Good Death Inventory mean scores differed significantly across 5 settings, with the highest total scores in Gaza, Greece, and mainland China and the lowest in Hong Kong and Turkey. Patient–caregiver differences in total and core domain scores were not significant.MeaningEnd-of-life care and discussions should address shared priorities, while remaining attentive to local clinical, family, cultural, and health-related contexts.

Introduction

Ensuring a good death is central to palliative care and end-of-life policy and practice.1 A good death is generally defined as dying with minimal distress, with respect shown for patients’ values, preferences, and cultural contexts.2 However, the importance and meaning of a good death may vary across cultural, religious, societal, and health care system settings.2,3 Religious and philosophical traditions, including Muslim beliefs about the afterlife,4 Christian faith–informed rituals,5 and Chinese traditions (eg, ancestor worship and Taoist, Confucian, and Buddhist philosophies),6,7 may shape health care behaviors, interpretations of suffering, and family caregivers’ roles and expectations.8 Therefore, understanding how death and dying are conceptualized across settings is vital to providing high-quality, person-centered end-of-life care.9

Patients with advanced cancer are an appropriate population for examining good death priorities because the illness often follows a recognizable trajectory of progressive decline and escalating supportive care needs,10 creating opportunities for timely care planning and end-of-life conversations.11 Family caregivers often serve as decision-makers, emotional supporters, and cultural interpreters during the dying process,12 particularly in family-centered contexts. Comparing patient and family caregiver perspectives is clinically important because differences in priorities may affect advance care planning, shared decision-making, and culturally sensitive palliative care interventions.13,14

Existing research has primarily focused on single countries, such as Belgium,15 Japan,8 and Indonesia,16 and has often used different instruments, eligibility criteria, and analytic approaches, limiting cross-country comparability. Although one multicountry study assessed cross-cultural variation across India, Singapore, Kenya, the UK, and the US using a discrete choice experiment with 13 indicators,17 this design captured a restricted set of preselected attributes and elicited preference tradeoffs rather than comprehensively assessing the multidimensional good death construct. Therefore, evidence remains limited on broader good death domains, patient–caregiver differences, and directly comparable variations across settings.

Pooling existing single-country studies is not a feasible substitute, because they differ in measurement tools and items, response scales, translation and adaptation processes, sampling frames, and timing along the illness trajectory. Moreover, cross-cultural evidence for good death priorities remains scarce in Middle Eastern and Arab Muslim settings,17 where cultural and religious norms shape end-of-life communication, decision-making, and beliefs and priorities regarding dying.18 The evidence gap limits the generalizability of existing frameworks and emphasizes the need for globally inclusive comparative research.

This international comparative cross-sectional study addresses this gap by examining priorities for a good death among patients with advanced cancer and family caregivers across 5 settings: Hong Kong, mainland China, Turkey, Greece, and Gaza. These settings differ in cultural expectations surrounding death and the development of palliative care systems, which may shape interpretations of a good death.19 Using the 54-item Good Death Inventory (GDI),20 we aimed to determine the importance of good death components, compare priorities across settings, and examine patient–caregiver differences and setting-by-role interactions. We hypothesized that good death priorities and patient–caregiver differences would vary by setting.

Methods

This international cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards and/or ethics committees of all participating sites, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki21 (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). All participants were informed that participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential, and written informed consent was obtained before they completed the questionnaire.

Study Settings

This study was conducted in 5 settings: Hong Kong, mainland China, Turkey, Greece, and Gaza. A maximum variation rationale was used to capture differences in cultural and/or religious orientation, end-of-life decision-making norms, and palliative and health care system development. Hong Kong represents a pluralistic and relatively secular East Asian setting in which most adults report no religious affiliation, although Christian and Buddhist minorities exist, and spiritual and ancestral practices remain common.22 In mainland China, Buddhist philosophy continues to influence moral views and family structures.23 Gaza provides a Muslim-majority Middle Eastern perspective,24 while Greece reflects Greek Orthodox Christian traditions, and Turkey reflects Islamic European traditions.25 Together, these settings support comparison across pluralistic and single-tradition contexts, secular and faith-informed societies, and varied palliative care system development.

Participant Characteristics

This study included 2 participant groups: patients diagnosed with advanced cancer (stage III or IV) and family caregivers primarily responsible for supporting these patients. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had a confirmed diagnosis of stage III or IV cancer, and were physically and cognitively capable of completing the questionnaire.

Patients were identified from oncology and palliative care clinic or ward lists at each site. Eligibility was verified by trained local research staff in collaboration with local clinical teams. Cancer stage was abstracted from medical records and reflected the cancer-specific staging assigned by the local treating oncology team. Stage III or IV disease was used to define advanced cancer for this study; stage III generally denoted locally advanced disease without distant metastasis, and stage IV denoted metastatic disease.26 This classification was used for study eligibility and should not be interpreted as indicating that participants were imminently dying. Performance status, clinician-estimated prognosis, and proximity to death were not collected consistently across all sites.

Family caregivers were included if they were aged 18 years or older, were the primary informal caregiver of a patient with advanced cancer during the illness trajectory, and were willing to participate and complete the questionnaire. Family caregiver participants were relatives or other informal caregivers involved in supporting a patient with advanced cancer. Caregiver participants were recruited in Hong Kong, Turkey, Greece, and Gaza. Although caregiver recruitment was undertaken in mainland China in accordance with the study protocol, caregiver data from this site were not included in the locked centralized analytic dataset used for the current analysis; therefore, mainland China was included as a patient-only setting in this study.

Participants were recruited from oncology and/or palliative care services across 6 participating institutions in 5 settings using convenience sampling: Hong Kong (palliative care units), mainland China (medical oncology wards and the nasopharyngeal carcinoma department), Turkey (palliative care and hematology–oncology inpatient services), Greece (general oncology hospital), and Gaza (oncology day care chemotherapy, outpatient, and inpatient services). Oncology and palliative care services were organized differently across settings; therefore, recruitment from these services did not necessarily indicate that all participants were receiving specialist palliative care. Within each institution, trained local research staff identified potentially eligible participants from clinic, ward, or services lists using prespecified criteria and approached them during the recruitment period when they were physically and cognitively able to complete the questionnaire. Clinical staff assisted, when needed, by confirming the appropriateness of approaching patients at that time. Family caregivers were approached either alongside participating patients (where feasible) or via referral by patients, according to site-specific procedures. Additional site-level details are reported in eTable 2 in Supplement 1.

Patient and caregiver participants were not consistently enrolled as linked dyads across sites. Therefore, patient–caregiver comparisons were analyzed as group-level comparisons rather than within-dyad comparisons.

Sample Size Calculation

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not conducted because this was an exploratory, feasibility-based international comparative study. Recruitment targets were set according to the feasibility of obtaining a sufficient number of participants at each site to estimate setting-specific mean GDI scores with acceptable precision and to compare patients and family caregivers in settings where both groups were recruited. In the final analytic sample (N = 1059; range, 143-359 participants per setting), the precision for estimating mean GDI total scores was acceptable, with approximate 95% CI half-widths ranging from 0.04 to 0.14 across settings based on observed SDs. Analyses therefore emphasized effect sizes, CIs, and sensitivity analyses rather than post hoc power.27

Data Collection

Data were collected from March 2023 to February 2025 using self-administered questionnaires distributed to eligible participants in oncology and palliative care settings. Data collection in Gaza was completed before October 2023, ensuring that responses were not influenced by subsequent regional conflict, while data collection at the other sites (Hong Kong, Greece, Turkey, and mainland China) continued until early 2025.

Site research assistants coordinated recruitment and data collection under the supervision of local investigators. Questionnaires were completed in the clinic or returned via sealed envelopes.

GDI

This study used the GDI20 with permission from author M.M., the original developer of the GDI. The GDI was selected because it provides a comprehensive, multidimensional assessment of good death components across physical, psychological, relational, spiritual, autonomy-related, and preparation-related domains. Compared with shorter or preference and/or tradeoff measures, the 54-item GDI allowed assessment of a broad range of good death priorities using a common item set across settings.20 The original structure of 10 primary and 8 optional domains was retained to preserve comparability with the original instrument and previous GDI-based studies. The GDI was originally developed to evaluate a good death from bereaved family members’ perspective.20 Participants were asked to rate the importance of each item on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (absolutely unimportant) to 7 (absolutely important). Item and domain scores are calculated, with higher scores indicating greater importance. This importance framing has been used in prior GDI-based studies.28

Validated Chinese23 and Arabic29 versions were used. The Arabic version had been validated in the Palestinian context by members of the author team (H.A.-O., F.K.Y.W., J.Z., and A.M.).29 For Greek and Turkish versions, the instrument was translated and culturally adapted using forward translation, synthesis, back translation, expert panel review, and pilot testing (30-40 participants) in accordance with recommended procedures.30 Adaptation focused on linguistic clarity, cultural relevance, and semantic equivalence. No domains were removed, and the same 54-item structure and 1 to 7 response scale were retained across settings.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed in SPSS Statistics, version 25 (IBM). All statistical tests were 2 tailed, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Participant characteristics were reported as frequencies (percentages), and GDI scores were reported as means (SDs). Participants with incomplete item responses for a given GDI outcome were excluded from the primary analysis. Missing values were assessed by comparing participants included in the analytic sample with those excluded because of incomplete GDI data.

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach α. Mean GDI scores were compared across settings using analysis of variance. In settings where both patients and family caregivers were recruited (Hong Kong, Turkey, Greece, and Gaza), the main and interaction effect sizes of setting and participant role (patient compared with caregiver) were examined using 2-way analysis of variance. Effect sizes are reported using partial η2 (small, 0.01; medium, 0.06; and large, ≥0.14).31

To assess robustness to compositional differences across settings, prespecified sensitivity analyses (model B) were conducted using general linear models including setting, participant role, and setting by role interaction terms, adjusting for age group, sex, education level, and cancer type. Variables not collected consistently across all settings were not included in adjusted models; the analytic approach prioritized comparability across settings using a common set of covariates, which necessarily limited adjustment for setting-specific variables that were not consistently available. Heteroscedasticity-consistent robust SEs were applied. Nested random-effects models for institution-level or family-unit clustering were not fitted, because the number of participating institutions was small, some settings were represented by a single institution, institutions were strongly nested within their setting, and harmonized patient–caregiver dyad identifiers were not retained consistently across sites. Adjusted marginal means and 95% CIs were estimated and compared using Bonferroni correction.

Results

Overall, 1117 participants completed the questionnaire across settings. Of these, 58 participants were excluded because of incomplete GDI data. The final analysis included 1059 participants: 640 patients (60.4%) and 419 family caregivers (39.6%) (mean [SD] age, 50.8 [18.8] years; 547 of 1056 participants with sex data [51.7% ] were female) (Table 1). Participants included in the analytic sample and those excluded because of incomplete GDI data are compared in eTable 3 in Supplement 1.

Internal Consistency and Comparison of GDI Scores

The GDI demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the overall sample (Cronbach α = 0.94) (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Reliability was similarly high for patients and family caregivers (Cronbach α = 0.94). Across settings, Cronbach α ranged from 0.77 (Greece) to 0.96 (Turkey), all exceeding the accepted threshold for adequate internal reliability (Cronbach α ≥0.70).

The GDI total, core domain, and optional domain values reported in Table 2 are presented as mean item scores on the original 1 to 7 response scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceived importance. The mean (SD) total scores were highest for participants from Gaza (5.93 [0.56]), followed by those from Greece (5.88 [0.30]) and mainland China (5.78 [0.73]), and scores were lowest for participants from Turkey (4.71 [0.87]) and Hong Kong (4.93 [0.71]) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Across settings, core domains were generally rated higher than optional domains, and spiritual and physical comfort showed marked cross-setting variation (Table 2).

A modest but consistent main effect size of setting on GDI total scores was identified (partial η2 = 0.383; P < .001), with total scores highest in Gaza and Greece and lowest in Turkey and Hong Kong (Table 2). Similar setting effect sizes were observed for core domains (partial η2 = 0.446; P < .001) and optional domains (partial η2 = 0.221; P < .001). Adjusted models yielded comparable patterns (eTables 6-8 in Supplement 1).

In sensitivity analyses restricted to the 4 settings with participant groups (model B), adjusted models confirmed between-setting differences for total (partial η2 = 0.271; P < .001), core (partial η2 = 0.336; P < .001), and optional GDI scores (partial η2 = 0.135; P < .001). Setting by role interactions were statistically significant but small for total (partial η2 = 0.012; P = .02) and core scores (partial η2 = 0.021; P < .001) but not for optional scores (partial η2 = 0.004; P = .37) (eTables 9-11 in Supplement 1).

Within-setting analyses showed that among patients, mean (SD) total scores were highest in Gaza (5.92 [0.58]), Greece (5.88 [0.27]), and mainland China (5.78 [0.73]) and lowest in Turkey (4.59 [0.89]). In settings with caregiver data, family caregivers reported modestly higher scores than patients, with the largest difference observed in Turkey (mean [SD], 4.87 [0.83] vs 4.59 [0.89]; P = .03) (Table 3).

Participant and Interaction Effects

Two-way analyses in the 4 settings with both participant groups showed modest but consistent setting effect sizes for GDI total (partial η2 = 0.383; P < .001), core (partial η2 = 0.446; P < .001), and optional scores (partial η2 = 0.221; P < .001) (Table 4). Participant role showed no main effect size on total (partial η2 = 0.001; P = .45) or core scores (partial η2 = 0.000; P = .55) but had a small effect size on optional scores (partial η2 = 0.005; P = .02). Setting by role interactions were small for total (partial η2 = 0.009; P = .02) and core scores (partial η2 = 0.017; P < .001), whereas the interaction for optional scores was not statistically significant (partial η2 = 0.006; P = .05), indicating modest setting-specific differences between patients and caregivers.

Discussion

This international cross-sectional comparative study examined good death priorities among patients with advanced cancer and family caregivers in 5 settings. Although participants across settings reported moderate to high GDI scores, considerable cross-setting variation was observed in how components were valued. Patients and family caregivers generally reported similar overall priorities regarding a good death. Cross-setting differences remained robust after adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, with small setting by role interaction effect sizes.

The GDI total scores were highest among participants from Greece, Gaza, and mainland China and lowest among those from Turkey and Hong Kong. These patterns should not be attributed to religion or culture alone. In Greece, the enduring influence of Orthodox Christianity promotes spiritual acceptance, forgiveness, and relational closure,32 which may help contextualize high domain scores for spiritual comfort and family connection among Greek participants. However, individual religiosity was not measured, and observed differences may also reflect age, illness trajectory, recruitment setting, palliative care access, family role, and response-style differences.

In Gaza, participants reported high GDI total scores. They rated physical and psychological comfort, spiritual comfort, and family relationships highly, despite systemic constraints. This finding supports the notion that Islamic beliefs, emphasizing surrender to God’s will, spiritual tranquility (sakina in Arabic), and strong familial bonds, shape important interpretative frameworks for understanding death.29 A Saudi Arabian study found similar emphasis on faith and family as keys to a good death.33

Confucian traditions in mainland China, particularly their emphasis on filial piety, intergenerational harmony, and reverence for ancestors, reinforce emotional closure and relational continuity, aligning closely with the core domains of the GDI.23 Mainland Chinese participants had high scores for good relationships with family and independence. Despite Hong Kong’s shared Chinese heritage, its lower scores may reflect distinct sociocultural and health care conditions. Hong Kong is a pluralistic and relatively secular East Asian society, although spiritual beliefs and ancestral practices remain common.22 These low scores should not be attributed to palliative care service development alone. Dying well also depends on timely communication, autonomy in treatment decisions, symptom relief, family support, preferred place of care and death, and meaningful cultural or spiritual practices.22,34 The findings for participants in Hong Kong may therefore reflect the combined influence of medicalized end-of-life care, secularized expectations, family burden, service transitions, and incomplete alignment between available services and the wider conditions needed for a good death.

In Turkey, the relatively low GDI total scores may reflect a complex interplay between health care system constraints, timing of palliative care referral, communication practices, and societal attitudes toward death. Turkish participants rated physical comfort and the quality of their relationship with medical staff lower than expected, suggesting that structural and clinical factors35,36 may be as important as religious or cultural frameworks.

While culture was the dominant factor shaping good death priorities, alignment between patients and family caregivers varied across settings. GDI total scores tended to be higher among family caregivers than among patients, aligning with reports from Muslim-majority contexts showing notable discrepancies in end-of-life preferences.37 Such patterns may reflect protective caregiving norms and heightened emphasis on moral duty and family responsibility compared with patients experiencing greater symptom burden. In addition, although between-setting differences in mean scores were numerically modest on the 1 to 7 scale, consistent domain-level patterns suggest potential contextual influences that warrant further investigation. Furthermore, these findings of cross-country comparisons should be interpreted as indicative of cross-context variation rather than precise quantitative comparisons of fully equivalent constructs.

Limitations

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference. Participating institutions were purposively selected, and participants were recruited using convenience sampling; therefore, findings may not be representative of all patients with advanced cancer and family caregivers in each setting. Participant characteristics and available covariates differed across settings, and key variables, including performance status, prognosis, diagnosis disclosure, proximity to death, palliative care exposure, employment status, living arrangements, treatment characteristics, caregiver relationship, and institution-level or dyadic identifiers, were not collected consistently. Residual confounding is therefore likely despite adjustment for selected shared covariates. In mainland China, awareness of the cancer diagnosis was required for participation; therefore, patients who were not fully informed of their diagnosis or prognosis may have been excluded. Caregiver data from mainland China were not included in the locked analytic dataset; therefore, patient–caregiver comparisons and setting-by-role interaction analyses were restricted to Hong Kong, Turkey, Greece, and Gaza.

Although validated Chinese and Arabic versions of the GDI were used and Greek and Turkish versions underwent translation and cultural adaptation, subtle linguistic or conceptual variations may have influenced item interpretation. Formal measurement invariance testing, including configural, metric, or scalar invariance, was not conducted. Therefore, some between-setting differences may reflect variations in how items were interpreted or functioned across cultural contexts rather than true differences in perceived importance. However, structured translation and cultural adaptation procedures, including forward-backward translation, expert panel review, and pilot testing, were undertaken to maximize semantic and conceptual equivalence across settings. Individual religiosity, spiritual practice, and cultural values were not directly measured; therefore, cultural and religious interpretations should be considered contextual rather than causal. Although the number of participants excluded because of incomplete GDI data was small, excluded participants differed from included participants by setting and respondent type, which may have introduced selection bias. Finally, no established minimally clinically important difference exists for GDI importance scores. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted using effect sizes, CIs, and domain-level patterns rather than absolute score differences or clinical thresholds. Taken together, residual confounding, absence of formal measurement invariance testing, and lack of clinical thresholds mean that findings should be interpreted as indicative patterns rather than definitive estimates of cross-cultural differences.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, patients with advanced cancer and family caregivers across 5 settings shared a broad aspiration for a good death, but priorities varied by context. Emotional peace, dignity, and family connection were consistently highly valued. These findings support palliative care models that recognize shared end-of-life priorities while remaining responsive to setting-specific meanings of a good death.
