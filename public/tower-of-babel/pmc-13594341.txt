Mixed-methods study on GenAI Usage, dependence behaviors, and standardized application paths among Chinese medical students
Authors: Liujie Fu, Ruifeng Li, Wuxiang Shi, Rui Quan, Jinyu Wu, Kunyu Zhaoyang, Yanhui Li, Liji Yang, Wenjiao Li, Shujun Liu, Yao Dong, Liujie Yang, Zhiwei Rong, Yinghua Qin, Liangru Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping medical education while fostering technological dependence among students. This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. In the quantitative phase, an empirical analysis was conducted using survey data collected from a sample of 1295 Chinese medical students. The subsequent qualitative phase involved thematic analysis of interview transcripts from 16 medical educators to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Findings reveal that GenAI was deeply integrated into medical students’ daily learning routines. Tool selection favored general-purpose platforms, whereas specialist medical tools exhibited exceptionally low utilization rates. The clinical application possibilities remained below 20% across all situations. With an overall dependency score of 21.91 ± 6.75, over 60% of students reported dependence on GenAI. Multivariate linear regression analysis indicated performance expectancy, academic pressure, and social influence showed significant positive correlations with GenAI dependency. Conversely, critical thinking exhibited a significant negative correlation. Future medical education should strategically reposition GenAI as a “cognitive scaffold” by reinforcing critical thinking and establishing standardized usage guidelines to facilitate high-quality development.Subject terms: Health care, Medical humanities, Medical research

Introduction

The iterative development and application of artificial intelligence technologies have reshaped social production and lifestyles, with the explosive growth of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) being particularly noteworthy. GenAI is defined as a set of machine learning algorithms capable of simulating existing datasets to generate new data samples. It employs advanced algorithms for pattern learning and produces novel content in various formats, including text, images, audio, videos, and code1. The GenAI technology is currently undergoing rapid iterations and holds significant potential for enhancing teaching and learning practices in higher education2,3. It includes delivering automated personalized feedback, reducing the burden of complex tasks, and offering cognitive and emotional support4.

University students are expected to integrate GenAI into their academic learning and professional development1. This is particularly relevant, given that medical knowledge systems are highly specialized, rapidly evolving, and demand rigorous practical applications. Consequently, medical students generally demonstrate high acceptance and positive perceptions of GenAI technologies, extensively applying them across various educational and disciplinary contexts5,6. First, it offers multifaceted support for medical writing7,8. This technology is capable of extracting information from electronic health records, assisting with literature retrieval, and performing text interpretation. Simultaneously, it provides generic templates for documents, guidance on writing style and formatting, helps organize logical ideas, and performs grammar checks9. Moreover, it facilitates multilingual communication in the medical field10, thereby significantly enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of knowledge acquisition. Second, GenAI primarily functions as a virtual teaching assistant, responding to learners’ inquiries and suggesting resources. In clinical settings, it operates as a personalized interactive mentor and ward assistant, facilitating students’ comprehension of intricate clinical scenarios. This technology facilitates clinical reasoning, diagnostics, and treatment skills, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making abilities11–13. Its applications extend to the development of communication scripts for doctor-patient interactions. Using the discipline of pharmacy as an example, GenAI tools are being actively integrated into experiential settings. Recent surveys indicate that pharmacy students widely utilize tools, such as ChatGPT®, when retrieving complex pharmacological information and simulating clinical decision-making14. Notably, up to 72.4% of pharmacy students reported using artificial intelligence (AI) during clinical rotations, including Advanced Pharmacy Practice Experiences (APPEs)15. Finally, GenAI technologies contribute to the systemic optimization of medical education processes; by employing these tools to automatically evaluate student assignments, educators can provide immediate feedback while simultaneously reducing the time spent on manual grading16. Students are encouraged to engage in self-reflection through data analysis and individualized reports that help them identify their strengths and flaws. This, in turn, empowers them to proactively develop learning plans and actively pursue improvement17. Teachers and teaching administrators have the capacity to modify instructional techniques and resource allocation in real-time based on big data reports, thereby driving precision and efficiency in teaching models18.

While GenAI technology holds transformative potential for medical education, it also promotes dependence, which cannot be disregarded. Traditional technological dependence is characterized by physical increases in usage frequency and duration. However, with the evolution of the advanced reasoning capabilities of language models, the educational sector is confronting a more insidious psychological shift19. Media Dependency Theory defines “dependence” as a state in which an individual’s capacity to achieve their goals relies on information resources provided by a media system20. The convenience, efficiency, and personalized features of GenAI foster psychological reliance and seamlessly integrate these tools into daily routines and workflows21. A survey in the United States revealed that 48.9% of respondents had utilized ChatGPT® in their medical studies, with 43.7% doing so weekly or daily; notably, these users exhibited a greater inclination to substitute ChatGPT® for traditional learning resources such as professors, textbooks, and lectures22. Furthermore, research has indicated that some students employ GenAI to complete assignments on their own, thereby circumventing the learning process altogether23. Cognitive Offloading Theory posits that humans naturally leverage external tools to reduce cognitive load, while moderate offloading enhances efficiency; uncalibrated cognitive offloading can weaken internal cognitive processes and degrade long-term thinking abilities24,25. Consequently, this study defined GenAI-dependent behaviors as altered behavioral patterns formed through repeated exposure to and perception of GenAI. This dependence manifests as a pronounced preference for technological assistance, potentially creating barriers to the independent completion of academic tasks when the technology is withdrawn26.

Because of the complexity of medical information and the uncertainty of clinical situations, students must possess autonomous critical thinking and problem-solving skills. However, an overreliance on standardized answers provided by GenAI violates the training requirements for this ability. Consequently, this hinders students’ opportunities to traverse manifold pathways of reasoning, curtails interpersonal interactions and engagement, and ultimately erodes their capacity to interact with patients27. Conversely, GenAI outputs are prone to “hallucination” difficulties, meaning they may generate incorrect or faulty information. This issue is particularly noticeable in extensive deep-learning-based models. Specifically, this pertains to the phenomenon in which models generate inaccurate, unrealistic, surreal, or entirely fabricated content through overgeneralization, unreasonable extrapolation, or creative interpretation of training data in the absence of authentic data or effective constraints28. In clinical education settings that lack structured oversight, students’ uncritical acceptance of GenAI-generated output not only raises profound academic integrity concerns but also threatens to severely undermine their core professional competencies29.

With respect to the aforementioned context, previous research has undertaken preliminary investigations into the perceptions and attitudes of medical students toward GenAI using various methodological approaches. These approaches included qualitative interviews30, quantitative questionnaires31, and mixed methods32. A review of the current literature revealed that although medical students recognized the convenience and efficiency of AI, they also expressed significant concerns regarding information inaccuracies, privacy and ethical risks, and potential overdependence issues33,34. This underscores the urgent need for structured training35. Recent cross-sectional surveys further corroborate that up to 73% of medical students perceive overreliance on AI and the potential degradation of clinical skills as significant concerns36. Although the existing literature suggests that students’ application of GenAI is transitioning from a mere efficiency tool to deep cognitive reliance, prompting calls for critical reflection on how technology alters cognitive habits, ethical boundaries, and educational values37—these studies predominantly remain confined to subjective apprehensions. The underlying mechanisms and influencing factors of GenAI-dependent behaviors are yet to be thoroughly investigated. Consequently, there remains a notable paucity of systematic empirical data to facilitate in-depth research specifically targeting medical student cohorts6.

This study explored the current usage of and dependence on GenAI among the surveyed Chinese medical students from two universities. The specific research questions addressed were as follows: (1) the current landscape of GenAI tools, usage frequency, and application scenarios; (2) the extent of GenAI-dependent behaviors among these students; (3) the positive and negative impacts of GenAI on medical education processes and learning outcomes; and (4) the individual and socio-environmental factors driving students’ dependence behaviors. To answer these questions, this study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-method design. First, a quantitative questionnaire was used to measure the prevalence and determinants of dependence. Qualitative interviews were conducted to elucidate the underlying reasons and mechanisms driving the statistical findings, thereby achieving data complementarity. Ultimately, the findings provide an evidence base for standardized application paths of GenAI among medical students, supporting artificial intelligence-driven transformation in medical education.

Results

Basic characteristics of the sample

The demographic characteristics of the respondents are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Of the 930 valid questionnaires collected, the cohort consisted primarily of females (n = 709, 76.24%), students of rural origin (N = 551, 59.25%), those with lower annual family income (n = 497, 53.44%), and individuals with siblings (N = 701, 75.38%). The surveyed population was proportionally distributed across the two medical colleges, with 55.38% (N = 515) from University A, and 44.62% (N = 415) from University B. In terms of major fields, near-medical programs referred to non-medical majors within medical universities, such as pharmacy, medical law, medical English, and health administration. Pure medical programs confer a bachelor’s degree in medicine upon graduation, including clinical medicine, preventive medicine, and nursing38, covering core specializations in medical education. Near medical students accounted for 59.68% (N = 555), whereas pure medical students accounted for 40.32% (N = 375).

The interviews included 16 medical teachers, who constituted a representative and comprehensive sample. In terms of teaching experience, the interviewed teachers comprised young, mid-career, and senior teaching groups. Of these, 68.75% (N = 11) had fewer than ten years of teaching experience, while 31.25% (N = 5) had more than ten years, with the longest tenure being 41 years. In terms of professional specialization, teachers with pure medical backgrounds accounted for 81.2% (N = 13), covering fields such as clinical medicine, pathology, and medical ethics. Teachers with near medical backgrounds constituted 18.8% (N = 3), involved in interdisciplinary areas such as health management and medical English, accounting for the largest share at 56.25% (N = 9); clinical practice teachers formed 6.25% (N = 1), and blended teachers represented 37.5% (N = 6). Moreover, 75.0% of the teachers (N = 12) had received training in GenAI teaching applications, whereas 25.0% (N = 4) had not taken such training.

Current status of medical students using GenAI

The survey results on the GenAI usage behavior of 930 students are displayed in Fig. 1. Regarding tool selection, general-purpose GenAI tools dominated: Doubao® (78.82%) and DEEPSEEK® (73.33%) had the highest usage rates, followed by ChatGPT® (20.22%) and Kimi® (25.59%). In contrast, the specialized medical tool Med-PaLM ®garnered only 2.26% utilization. Tools such as iFlytek Spark® (3.55%) and Claude® (1.51%) also registered usage rates below 5%, indicating that medical students’ awareness of GenAI remained largely confined to general-purpose platforms, with insufficient access to or trust in specialized medical GenAI tools. Regarding utilization frequency, combined daily (32.37%) and 3–5 times per week (40.11%) usage accounted for 72.48%. Low-frequency use (1–3 times a month: 6.45%; rarely: 5.05%) comprised just 11.50%, demonstrating that GenAI was firmly integrated into medical students’ everyday study as a high-frequency auxiliary tool. Regarding usage scenarios, coursework support (88.28%) greatly surpassed other applications, followed by medical literature research (64.09%) and thesis/report writing (51.83%). Scenarios directly linked to clinical practice—such as simulated case analysis (18.60%), medical documentation assistance (14.19%), and simulated patient consultations (12.80%)—indicated that GenAI’s potential for enhancing medical students’ clinical competency development remained largely untapped.

The current state of medical students’ reliance on GenAI

The current state of GenAI dependence among medical students includes both subjective and objective assessments. First, GenAI dependence was ascertained through subjective reports by students and teachers using peer observation and self-evaluation. According to the results of the subjective self-assessment, more than half the medical students formed a subjective awareness of their reliance on GenAI, with 60.32% (N = 561) directly acknowledging this dependency. Concurrently, the surveyed medical staff commonly observed that student GenAI usage permeates multiple elements of medical education. In contrast to clinical and laboratory practice sessions, the teachers observed greater dependence during theoretical knowledge acquisition. Furthermore, lower-year students displayed stronger GenAI dependency than their older counterparts. The second component analyzed GenAI dependency levels using objective scales. Medical students had an average GenAI dependence level of 21.91 ± 6.75 points on an 8-item dependency scale (total score: 40 points).

As Fig. 2 shows, specific manifestations of GenAI dependence can be observed. Thirty-six percent of students (31% agree + 5% strongly agree) endorsed the statement “I would feel anxious or uncomfortable if I could not use GenAI.” Twenty-six percent of students (22% agree + 4% strongly agree) tended to agree that “Without the use of GenAI, I would find it difficult to accomplish professional-related tasks” while 30% of students (26% agree + 4% strongly agree) reported “I’m spending more and more time on GenAI,” reflecting a rise in usage intensity. Forty-six percent (38% agree + 8% strongly agree) perceived that “For me, life without GenAI is inconvenient,” revealing behavioral dependency inertia. These responses suggested that some students developed emotional dependence.

The impact of GenAI dependency on medical students

The impact of GenAI on medical education was bidirectional. On the positive side, the teachers noted that GenAI could assist students in rapidly arranging medical knowledge frameworks and boost their learning efficiency. This is particularly applicable to fields that require extensive knowledge, such as anatomy and pathology. GenAI was perceived as offering access to medical literature and clinical scenarios, expanding learning materials, and compensating for the outdated nature of traditional textbooks.

On the other hand, knowledge acquisition can become shallow. Students who rely on GenAI for assignments often acquire fragmented memories and poor comprehension, resulting in gaps in the integration of key medical theories. Second, key competency development was limited, as students lacked training in independently evaluating cases. Specifically, among the 561 students who explicitly reported GenAI dependency, 61.32% of the students reported impaired independent literature retrieval skills, 76.83% felt poorly prepared to evaluate GenAI-generated content, and 56.71% committed fundamental errors due to overreliance. Third, threats to academic integrity and data security were identified: 29.95% of students faced scrutiny of academic integrity for directly duplicating GenAI answers, 28.70% expressed concerns about data privacy breaches, and 26.20% mentioned intellectual property difficulties.

We also explored whether GenAI reliance significantly affected academic achievement (see Supplementary Table 1). Logit regression analysis revealed that neither self-reported nor objectively assessed GenAI dependency levels had a significant effect on students’ academic outcomes.

Analysis of factors influencing medical students’ dependence on GenAI

This study detailed the variable assignment protocol employed in the analysis of GenAI dependency among the sampled Chinese medical students, specifying the categorical recoding of control variables and the use of original values for independent and dependent variables (see Supplementary Table 2). The ANOVA results of the GenAI dependency levels across different demographic characteristics showed that only the source of the university yielded a significant difference (P < 0.001) (see Supplementary Table 3).

The score distribution features of the seven independent variables associated with GenAI dependency among medical students at Universities A and B are shown in Fig. 3 along with the P-value findings from the independent samples t-tests. The findings showed that there were statistically significant variations in scores between the two groups across all core independent variables, with P‑values for all seven variables being less than 0.05. In terms of score levels, University A (red group) demonstrated higher median scores across categories than University B (blue group). Regarding dispersion, the students from University B had a more scattered distribution of scores on certain factors, whereas students from University A showed a relatively more concentrated distribution.

Multiple linear regression analysis (see Table 1) revealed the mechanisms of the factors influencing GenAI dependence among medical students and identified variations across universities. In the total sample (Model 1), the three core factors exhibited significant positive correlations with the degree of dependence. Performance expectancy (β = 0.588, P < 0.001) indicated that higher subjective expectations regarding GenAI’s ability to enhance learning efficiency and academic performance are associated with a greater tendency toward dependence. Academic pressure (β = 0.746, P < 0.001) identified a specific profile among medical students: those reporting heavy course loads also exhibited more frequent use of GenAI for efficiency and showed higher levels of tool dependence. Social influence (β = 0.804, P < 0.001) demonstrated that positive media coverage, endorsement from field experts, and peer recommendations are significantly and positively correlated with the extent of dependence. Conversely, critical thinking (β = −0.724, P < 0.001) emerged as the only factor with a significant negative correlation. Students characterized by stronger critical thinking skills also tended to exhibit more rigorous verification habits and reported less reliance on AI outputs. Factors such as effort expectancy (P = 0.241), self-efficacy (P = 0.397), and facilitating conditions (P = 0.294) yielded no significant effects.

Discussion

The most prominent finding of this study was that GenAI has become a universally adopted learning tool for medical students. Medical students’ willingness to integrate GenAI into their daily studies and clinical practice is consistent with the findings of several previous studies6,39. While GenAI can alleviate the cognitive load of medical students, its use for cognitive offloading in complex tasks—such as clinical reasoning and decision-making—may lead to automation bias, “skill atrophy,” “skill never-acquired,” and “skill mis-acquisition”40. The current high-frequency interaction between medical students and GenAI is evolving into an uncalibrated form of cognitive offloading, characterized by an overreliance on GenAI technology. This has become a significant concern for both students and faculty41. Without intentional design and critical engagement, GenAI may erode the essential attributes of a qualified physician, including clinical judgment, ethical reasoning, and intellectual autonomy42.

Specifically, medical students show a marked preference for general-purpose Large Language Models, whereas the use of domain-specific medical AI tools remains notably low. Comparative studies indicate that domain-specialized models, such as Med-PaLM®—which are fine-tuned on vast medical corpora and clinical QA datasets—have demonstrated encouraging performance43. However, students’ usage preferences suggest that they currently treat GenAI more as a shortcut for information retrieval and text generation rather than as a sophisticated engine for clinical decision support. Consistent with the existing literature, the adoption rate of GenAI as a clinical learning tool is lower than its use in broader educational settings39. This further reinforces the findings of this study: students in near-medical majors exhibited a higher degree of GenAI dependence than those in pure medical majors. This disparity arises because pure medical curricula demand extreme precision in medical knowledge and high proficiency in clinical skills, positioning the GenAI as a strict supplementary reference. In contrast, coursework for near-medical majors focuses more on general tasks such as information integration, document drafting, and data organization. This task profile fosters a higher reliance on GenAI, which can easily trigger “automation bias”—a hazardous mindset where users bypass verification and preemptively assume the AI’s accuracy due to excessive trust44. This risk is particularly acute for lower-year students with nascent foundational knowledge; lacking sufficient clinical context to critically audit GenAI outputs, they face severe risks of internalizing medical inaccuracies or hallucinations. These observations align closely with a national cross-sectional survey that found that most medical students prefer mainstream tools for daily tasks: undergraduates primarily use AI for exam preparation, whereas postgraduates focus on research-oriented tasks, such as study design and data analysis6.

The multiple regression models used in this study provide a robust theoretical framework for understanding GenAI dependence. First, individual factors, specifically performance expectancy45 and academic pressure46,47—exhibited significant positive correlations with the degree of GenAI dependence among medical students. This aligns with multicountry research that identifies these two factors as the primary drivers of AI reliance. The surveyed Chinese medical students face multifaceted pressures, including heavy course loads, intensive assessment cycles, and fierce competition in national medical licensing examinations and postgraduate admissions, which inevitably amplify the demand for learning efficiency. Consequently, the more a student expects GenAI to enhance academic performance, the more likely their usage behavior is to solidify into an entrenched habit of dependence45. Furthermore, students experiencing higher stress levels show a greater propensity to rely on AI technologies48. This phenomenon is consistent with the Cognitive Offloading Theory. When individuals face high cognitive load and psychological stress, they are more inclined to utilize external tools to reduce their cognitive expenditure. However, this form of uncalibrated cognitive offloading presents a critical trade-off, as it may lead to the long-term attenuation of intrinsic cognitive capabilities.

Medical students’ attitudes toward and dependence on GenAI are often not formed solely through individual experience but are profoundly shaped by the perceptions of their social environment. Research in India indicates that university students’ behavioral intentions are molded by social influence49, suggesting that the social context and opinions of peers, friends, or experts are pivotal factors in GenAI adoption50. Similarly, a study involving 933 Italian students identified “social influence” as a significant predictor of AI habit formation and sustained usage intentions45. Within the medical student peer group, GenAI usage has become ubiquitous. However, in the absence of unified and standardized usage guidelines, students are highly susceptible to peer behaviors. This often leads to uninformed conformity in GenAI adoption, which evolves into a state of dependence.

Notably, critical thinking exhibited a significant negative correlation with GenAI dependence among medical students. Although AI reduces cognitive load and offers immediate convenience by automating tasks and providing rapid information51, this benefit entails substantial hidden cognitive costs25. Extensive evidence suggests that overreliance on AI triggers “cognitive offloading,” a process where individuals “outsource” mental tasks to external tools, thereby drastically reducing deep information processing, memory retention, and reflective engagement24,52. Empirical research further confirmed that this increase in cognitive offloading behaviors mediates the negative association between high-frequency AI usage and the erosion of critical thinking skills53. Consequently, while GenAI may enhance task efficiency in the short term, chronic overdependence deprives students of opportunities for deep contemplation, ultimately undermining their core competencies in critical inquiry and autonomous decision-making53.

The divergent effects of effort expectancy, self-efficacy, and facilitating conditions across universities are associated with the heterogeneity of individual student characteristics and GenAI application environments in different institutions. Under traditional hypotheses, self-efficacy is typically expected to have a negative correlation with dependent behavior, suggesting that students—driven by a lack of confidence—view AI as a shortcut to compensate for perceived inadequacies54, thereby fostering “passive substitutive dependence.” However, the data from University A in this study revealed a positive relationship between self-efficacy and the degree of GenAI dependence. This suggests that high-self-efficacy groups do not perceive GenAI as a replacement for their own competence; rather, they proactively leverage the tool to achieve maximum task efficiency, a phenomenon we define as “active augmentative dependence.” Facilitating conditions at University A showed a marginally significant negative correlation with GenAI dependence, a finding consistent with prior studies55, which suggests that specific institutional environments may serve as buffers against excessive reliance on GenAI. By contrast, at University B, effort expectancy exhibited a significant positive correlation with dependent behavior. This indicates that while perceived ease of use (the hallmark of effort expectancy) facilitates academic performance, it may concurrently solidify into deep-seated cognitive reliance. This aligns with the findings of an online survey involving 772 students in Peru, which demonstrated that effort expectancy exerts a positive influence on the actual usage of ChatGPT®56.

Future medical education should not marginalize GenAI; instead, it should be precisely positioned as “cognitive scaffolding” to facilitate reflection57, while the irreplaceable leading roles of both educators and students must be maintained. To counter potential inaccuracies in AI outputs, foundational thinking training should be strengthened for lower-year students, whereas the information discernment capabilities of senior students must be enhanced. Medical students should prioritize critical thinking as a core cognitive tool to safeguard their independence in clinical judgment. Educators can implement instructions that focus on information verification and critical inquiries. In theoretical teaching, students should be guided to independently deconstruct and analyze complex medical problems or be instructed to validate GenAI outputs using authoritative medical databases. “Process-oriented assessments” should be integrated into theoretical assignments, for instance, requiring students to submit handwritten knowledge maps or logical deduction processes rather than merely the final answers. Institutions must establish clear guidelines for GenAI use. Course syllabi should explicitly define the boundaries of permissible GenAI use and citation requirements, complemented by AI content-detection mechanisms to uphold academic integrity. Critically, data privacy protection must be incorporated into routine safety education, with a particular emphasis on alerting students to the risk of leaking patient information during AI-assisted diagnosis or medical record processing. At the societal level, media outlets and medical experts should maintain an objective and neutral stance by balancing reports on GenAI’s technical advantages with the potential risks. Avoiding one-sided, positive portrayals fosters a rational and rigorous technological environment for medical students.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, as an observational study, the associations identified between GenAI dependence and its antecedents cannot be construed as causal. Second, the assessment of dependence levels and academic impact relied primarily on cross-sectional self-reported data and subjective observations from educators who lacked longitudinal measurements of students’ actual clinical reasoning skills or long-term objective academic performance. This limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions. Future research should employ longitudinal intervention designs to elucidate causal mechanisms between GenAI dependence and academic outcomes.Third, contrary to some previous studies46, academic self-efficacy did not show a robust, direct correlation with AI dependence in our overall sample. Notably, a study on 300 South Korean university students suggested that the relationship between academic self-efficacy and AI dependence is mediated by academic pressure and performance expectancy58. Future studies should explore the potential mediating effects of these variables. Fourth, although the sample was drawn from two specific medical universities, caution is warranted when generalizing these results to other educational systems or healthcare contexts with different cultural backgrounds. Subsequent research should aim to expand both the scope and size of the sample to enhance the external validity (generalizability) of the findings. Furthermore, developing and validating bespoke scales tailored to the specific needs of medical education combined with more precise measurement methodologies would significantly improve data quality.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods approach (see Fig. 4), integrating both quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis methodologies. First, a quantitative phase was conducted using standardized questionnaires administered to a sample of medical university students to identify current usage patterns, overall characteristics of dependence behaviors, and influencing factors. Subsequently, the qualitative phase involved semi-structured interviews with medical faculty members with experience observing or implementing GenAI in educational settings. A qualitative analysis was performed on the interview transcripts to extract core insights. At the data integration level, in-depth observations gained from the qualitative interviews were utilized to meticulously deconstruct the mechanisms of dependence revealed by the quantitative data. By ensuring that the quantitative and qualitative findings complement and triangulate each other, this study aimed to elucidate the phenomena and underlying mechanisms of GenAI dependence among medical students, thereby proposing targeted intervention strategies.

Investigation tools

In the introduction, we provided questionnaire instructions to medical school students, emphasizing participant identity and data privacy. The formal survey comprised four sections. The first section covered sociodemographic characteristics, including basic information such as gender, origin of the student, family status, and current academic situation. The second section examined usage patterns and self-assessments related to GenAI, including the frequency of use, tools employed, specific application scenarios, academic assistance received, and perceived negative impacts. The third section quantified medical students' dependency on GenAI through the use of eight items. The items were drawn from the Artificial Intelligence Chatbot Dependence Scale developed and validated by Chinese scholars59. The initial pool consisted of 17 items derived from extensive literature review and in-depth interviews. Following item and exploratory factor analyses (EFA), a unified model was established with eight items accounting for 58.42% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated that our AI Chatbot Dependence Scale possessed acceptable model fit indices, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.50 to 0.76. Furthermore, the scale exhibited robust reliability and validity, reflecting its superior psychometric properties. Consequently, specific items were adapted for the educational context—for instance, “I prefer spending time on GenAI rather than engaging with other instructors or textbooks”—to accurately capture GenAI dependence behaviors among medical university students. The total score ranges up to 40, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of dependence. The fourth section of the questionnaire examined the factors that influenced students’ dependence on GenAI. Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, self-efficacy, critical thinking, and academic pressure were representative of internal individual factors, whereas facilitating conditions and social influence constituted external societal factors. Specific items within these categories were adapted to the medical context. Subsequently, descriptive statistics, significance comparisons, and multiple linear regressions were used for empirical analysis of the questionnaire data to support the research findings. The survey questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Information 1.

A qualitative interview outline was designed based on preliminary quantitative survey results. This outline is included in Supplementary Information 2. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with frontline medical educators, structured around the following five modules: direct observations of students’ GenAI usage and dependence behaviors; the actual impact of dependence on teaching processes and learning outcomes; existing or envisioned coping strategies; and insights into the rational integration of GenAI into future medical education. All interviews were audio- or video-recorded with the participants’ informed consent, and subsequently transcribed into text for qualitative analysis. This integration process ensured that educators’ narratives and case studies provided in-depth information to complement the preceding quantitative findings, thereby offering a rich contextual grounding for quantitative models through qualitative evidence.

Variable measurement

Focusing on the factors influencing medical students’ dependence on GenAI, covering both individual and societal dimensions, this research indicates that seven variables exert significant effects on GenAI dependency levels (see Fig. 5). Individual factors included performance expectancy, effort expectancy, self-efficacy, critical thinking, and academic pressure. Performance expectations refer to the users’ subjective assumptions that utilizing GenAI enhances learning, work efficiency, and overall performance. Excessively high-performance expectancy may lead to students’ overdependence on GenAI. Effort expectancy pertains to students’ perceived ease of using GenAI methods, with ease directly proportional to reliance. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s confidence in their ability to perform or master a specific task. Individuals with higher self-efficacy demonstrate greater enthusiasm for learning and exert greater effort. Students with low self-efficacy may rely heavily on GenAI. Critical thinking60 refers to an individual’s capacity for rational, objective, and systematic reflection when confronting problems and making decisions, and higher levels of critical thinking are hypothesized to be negatively correlated with the degree of GenAI dependence. A case study illustrated this dynamic by examining the experience of a student named Lina, who initially adopted it as a productivity tool; it gradually became an indispensable part of her daily learning routine. Although it enhanced her immediate efficiency, it simultaneously compromised her critical thinking, creativity, and academic self-confidence, ultimately fostering a state of cognitive dependence37. Medical students experience considerable pressure during their education61, and stress has been demonstrated to have a significant impact on learning62, either enhancing or inhibiting the educational process. Social environmental factors encompass both the direct facilitating conditions individuals experience and the societal implications arising from others’ use of GenAI. Positive social settings support the development of acceptable GenAI behavioral intentions49,63.

Quality control

This study implemented the following quality control measures: (1) All items were designated as required fields to prevent data gaps; (2) A response pattern monitoring mechanism was enforced to trigger manual review if consecutive questions yielded identical answers; (3) Geographical consistency and prevented duplicate submissions was enforced through IP address verification; (4) Unreliable responses were excluded based on pre-set attention check questions in the questionnaire (e.g., Please select “Strongly Disagree”); (5) Interview duration was limited to approximately 20 min; (6) Audio or video recording of all interviews was required.

Reliability and validity test

A pilot study was conducted from August 25 to 29, 2025, with 132 valid questionnaires recovered for preliminary reliability and validity testing. To ensure that the instrument was precisely tailored to the medical education context, a panel of five experts with extensive backgrounds in medical education was invited to evaluate the content validity of the questionnaire. This expert group comprised diverse practitioners and administrators: two medical university deans with experience in educational management, two frontline faculty members with expertise in theoretical instruction, and one dedicated counselor specialized in student affairs management. Based on a rigorous review by the expert panel and feedback from pilot study participants, the questionnaire’s items, phrasing, and structural design were optimized, and the reliability and validity analysis of the final formal questionnaire yielded the following results: Cronbach’s alpha for each research variable exceeded 0.7, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.909, indicating high internal consistency and reliability (see Supplementary Table 4). Regarding validity, the KMO value was 0.917 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded an approximate chi-square value of 9919.476 (P < 0.001), confirming that the data were highly suitable for factor analysis (see Supplementary Table 5). The rotated component matrix showed that the factor loadings for all items on their respective components were above 0.6, with robust communality, thus demonstrating excellent structural validity (see Supplementary Table 6).

Sample size calculation

The minimum sample size was estimated using the population proportion formula for cross-sectional surveys70. The confidence level was set at 95% (Z = 1.96) with a margin of error (E) of 5%. To ensure a conservative estimate and maximize the statistical power of the study, the expected prevalence (p) was set to 0.5. Based on this formula, the theoretical minimum effective sample size required was 385.

Data collection

The target population for the survey was restricted to full-time students currently enrolled in medical universities in China, encompassing all academic levels from undergraduate to postgraduate. The sampling process primarily drew participants from University A and University B, with strategic expansions based on institutional type, academic tier, and geographical distribution to increase the demographic diversity of our cohort, including filters for response duration, attention checks, and logical consistency, a final set of 930 valid questionnaires was included, yielding an effective response rate of 71.8%. The final effective sample size (N = 930) significantly exceeded the theoretical threshold required for cross-sectional surveys, ensuring statistical power and population representativeness of the findings.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted simultaneously with the survey to collect qualitative data. The interviewees were primarily faculty members teaching at the sampled universities, all of whom had observational or practical experience with the GenAI in educational settings. Sixteen faculty members were sampled through online and offline interviews, and a total of 16 faculty samples were collected. These interviews provided detailed narratives supported by specific cases, with an average duration of approximately 20 min.

Ethical considerations

This study used a non-interventional, anonymous questionnaire survey. It did not involve the collection of biological samples or the risk of personal privacy exposure, qualifying it as a low-risk epidemiological investigation. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University. As it falls under the low-risk simplified exemption review, no formal ethics approval number was assigned. All research participants provided informed consent, and the study strictly adhered to the relevant ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
