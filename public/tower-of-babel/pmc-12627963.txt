Incidence and associated factors of delirium after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: A retrospective study
Authors: Koji Otsuki, Yusuke Okada, Tsutomu Takahashi, Kouhei Sato, Michiharu Nagahama, Ritsuro Suzuki, Masatoshi Inagaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIn general, delirium is reported to have a higher incidence in hospitalized patients and is associated with a higher risk of mortality. However, there is insufficient knowledge of the incidence and prognostic impact of delirium following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Therefore, we investigated the incidence of delirium after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and to determine the relationship between delirium and associated factors.MethodsThe study was a retrospective examination of the electronic medical records of 140 patients who underwent initial hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for hematologic cancers. The incidence of delirium occurring within 30 days of transplantation was assessed. We also examined its prognostic impact, pretransplantation risk factors, and association with graft‐versus‐host disease.ResultsThe incidence of delirium was 14.6%. Delirium was significantly associated with lower survival at 6 months posttransplant, but there was no significant effect of delirium on survival at 1 year or overall. A high or very high Disease Risk Index score was significantly associated with lower survival. Older age, a high or very high Disease Risk Index score, allogeneic transplantation, and lower oxygen saturation were identified as pretransplantation risk factors for delirium. A significant association was found between delirium and graft‐versus‐host disease.ConclusionThe incidence of delirium following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is relatively high, and delirium is associated with short‐term survival. When pretransplantation risk factors are present, thorough prevention and early detection of delirium are essential.Keywords: allogeneic transplantation, cancer, delirium, graft‐versus‐host disease, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

INTRODUCTION

Delirium occurs in 11%–42% of hospitalized patients and has a high incidence in postoperative and intensive care unit settings. 1 , 2 , 3 However, few studies have been conducted on delirium following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), which is primarily performed to treat hematologic cancers. Delirium after HSCT has been examined within 30 days of HSCT in four prospective 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 and two retrospective studies 8 , 9 (Table S1), with estimates ranging widely across studies from 24% to 50%. Thus, further investigation is warranted.

Regarding the prognostic impact of delirium, it has been suggested that delirium increases the risk of death. A meta‐analysis showed that this risk is 1.95 times higher at an average follow‐up of 22.7 months. 10 To date, only three reports have examined the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium 5 , 8 , 9 (Table S1). Amonoo et al. found that post‐HSCT delirium is associated with lower survival at 6 months posttransplantation. 9 Furthermore, Beglinger et al. reported that post‐HSCT delirium is associated with a 14‐fold higher risk of death at 1 year post‐surgery. 5 However, Smith et al. reported that post‐HSCT delirium does not significantly affect overall survival (median of 1.81 years of observation). 8 Currently, relevant studies are limited, and thus the short‐ and long‐term prognostic impact of delirium remains unclear.

Although there are numerous reviews and meta‐analyses of risk factors for delirium, 11 , 12 only four studies have reported on pretransplantation risk factors for post‐HSCT delirium. 5 , 6 , 8 , 13 Older age, lower oxygen saturation, various laboratory data, and myeloablative treatment have been identified as pretransplantation risk factors for delirium 5 , 6 , 8 , 13 (Table S1). However, many of these studies involved small numbers of patients, 5 , 6 , 13 the timing and methods used to evaluate laboratory data varied, and the risk factors were not consistent across studies.

To date, only one study has investigated the association between post‐HSCT delirium and graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), which found no association between delirium and the development of GVHD 9 (Table S1). Therefore, further study is crucial to understanding the association between post‐HSCT delirium and GVHD.

The aim of our study was to investigate the incidence of post‐HSCT delirium and determine the prognostic impact of delirium, the pretransplantation risk factors for delirium, and the association between delirium and GVHD.

METHODS

Study design and participants

A total of 140 patients aged 20 years or older who underwent their first HSCT for hematologic cancer at our hospital between January 2007 and June 2021 were included in the study. Patients were excluded if they had been diagnosed with dementia or delirium at the time of HSCT. Of the 140 patients, three patients with delirium at the time of HSCT were excluded, which resulted in a final sample of 137 cases. The study was a retrospective medical record study, and subjects were given the opportunity to opt out of the study on the website. The primary outcome was (1) the incidence of post‐HSCT delirium, and the secondary outcomes were (2) the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium, (3) the pretransplantation risk factors for post‐HSCT delirium, and (4) the association between post‐HSCT delirium and GVHD.

Demographic and clinical data

We obtained the following demographic and clinical data from medical records: age, sex, body mass index, diagnosis of hematologic cancer, Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation Comorbidity Index score, Disease Risk Index (DRI) score, donor type, stem cell type, treatment regimen, medication taken at the time of HSCT, delirium, acute GVHD (aGVHD), chronic GVHD (cGVHD), period of hospitalization (days), and length of stay in a sterile room before transplantation (days). The DRI is determined according to disease type and status at the time of transplantation and is stratified into four groups: low, intermediate, high, and very high. 14 A higher rating indicates a poorer prognosis. We divided our patients into two groups: low/intermediate and high/very high. GVHD is a significant complication that can arise following allogeneic HSCT. This condition stems from a pathological immune response in which donor‐derived immune cells recognize and attack the recipient's cells as foreign cells. Historically, GVHD has been classified as either acute or chronic according to the time of onset: aGVHD is defined as occurring within 100 days posttransplant, whereas cGVHD is considered to occur after 100 days. However, in recent clinical practice, the two are differentiated according to their distinct pathophysiologies and clinical manifestations, moving away from a strict time‐based criterion. 15 , 16 To identify the most recent pretransplantation risk factors, we extracted laboratory data 24 h before HSCT and oximetry values on the day of HSCT from medical records, similar to the methods of Weckmann et al. 6

Diagnosis of post‐HSCT delirium

We defined post‐HSCT delirium as delirium that occurred within 30 days of HSCT, in line with previous reports. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 We used the chart‐based method by Inouye et al. for the identification of delirium. 17 Specifically, a psychiatrist with sufficient psychiatric practice experience retrospectively searched the patient's medical chart for keywords indicating acute confusion (e.g., delirium, mental status change, inattention, disorientation, hallucinations, agitation, and inappropriate behavior). If appropriate, delirium was diagnosed. In addition, the source of information, the time of onset, a detailed description of each episode, duration, and reversibility were confirmed. 17 The sensitivity and specificity of this method have been reported to be 74% and 83%, respectively. 17

Prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium

We examined the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium at 6 months and 1 year as well as overall survival. This time period was set following previous reports. 5 , 8 , 9

Statistical analysis

spss version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. For comparisons between two groups for demographic and clinical data, we used a chi‐square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. A univariate Cox regression analysis was used to assess the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium and related factors. A multivariate analysis was performed using age, sex, and variables that were significantly different in a univariate analysis as covariates. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify pretransplantation risk factors for post‐HSCT delirium. Logistic regression analysis was also used to examine the association between post‐HSCT delirium and aGVHD and cGVHD. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Regarding the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium, the results of the univariate analysis for each time period are shown in Tables S2–S4. The multivariate analysis showed that delirium significantly increased the risk of death at 6 months (hazard ratio [HR], 3.363 [1.400, 8.078], p = 0.01) (Table 2). At 1 year, there was no significant effect of delirium, but the DRI score (≥high) was significantly associated with survival (Table S5). For overall survival (mean observation period of 3.28 years), the multivariate analysis showed no significant effect of delirium, with only the DRI score (≥high) reaching significance (Table 3).

For the pretransplantation risk factors for post‐HSCT delirium, the univariate analysis revealed that older age, DRI score (≥high), allogeneic transplantation, and lower oxygen saturation were significantly associated with an increased risk of delirium (Table 4). In allogeneic transplantation, the proportion of the DRI score (≥high) was significantly higher than in autologous transplantation (47.4% vs. 11.5%, p < 0.001). Myeloablative treatment was significantly associated with a decreased risk of delirium (Table 4). Exploratory multivariate analysis showed that older age and lower oxygen saturation were associated with an increased risk of delirium (Table S6).

Allogeneic transplantation was shown to be a pretransplantation risk factor for post‐HSCT delirium, and therefore, we performed subgroup analyses in the allogeneic transplantation group. For prognostic impact, the multivariate analysis revealed that none of the factors were significant at 6 months (Table S7). At 1 year, there was no significant effect of delirium, but the DRI score (≥high) was significant (Table S8). For overall survival (mean observation period of 2.95 years), the multivariate analysis showed no significant effect of delirium, and only the DRI score (≥high) was significant (Table S9). Regarding the pretransplantation risk factors for delirium, the univariate analysis found that older age and lower oxygen saturation were significantly associated with an increased risk of delirium (Table S10). Exploratory multivariate analysis showed that older age and lower oxygen saturation were associated with an increased risk of delirium (Table S11).

The logistic regression analysis examining the association between post‐HSCT delirium and aGVHD and cGVHD showed that delirium was significantly associated with a decreased risk of aGVHD (odds ratio, 0.179 [0.051, 0.626], p = 0.01). In addition, delirium was significantly associated with a decreased risk of cGVHD (odds ratio, 0.100 [0.012, 0.808], p = 0.03). In all four cases of delirium and aGVHD and one case of delirium and cGVHD, delirium occurred first.

DISCUSSION

In our retrospective study, we determined the incidence of post‐HSCT delirium and investigated the prognostic impact of post‐HSCT delirium, pretransplantation risk factors, and the association between post‐HSCT delirium and GVHD. We found that allogeneic transplantation is a pretransplantation risk factor for post‐HSCT delirium and that post‐HSCT delirium is significantly associated with GVHD.

Post‐HSCT delirium occurred in 14.6% of all patients, 6.6% of those who underwent autologous transplantation, and 21.1% of those who underwent allogeneic transplantation. The incidence of delirium in allogeneic transplant patients was comparable to the 24% reported in a previous prospective study. 7 However, the incidence of delirium in autologous transplantation patients was lower than previously reported incidences of 33.3%–70.6%. 4 , 5 , 6 , 9 Such a discrepancy in the incidence of delirium in autologous transplantation patients may be attributed to differences in HSCT protocols, such as the drugs used, regions, and facilities.

In the current study, we found that post‐HSCT delirium significantly increased the risk of death at 6 months; however, this was not observed at 1 year or overall. Previously, Amonoo et al. suggested that delirium increases the risk of mortality at 6 months, 9 whereas Smith et al. reported that delirium has no prognostic impact on overall survival 8 (Table S1), which is consistent with our results. By contrast, Beglinger et al. found that the delirium group had a higher risk of death than the non‐delirium group 1 year after transplantation. 5 This discrepancy may be due to our adjustment of hematologic cancer severity using the DRI. Our results suggest that post‐HSCT delirium is associated with poorer prognosis in the short term but not long term.

We revealed that older age, a high or very high DRI score, allogeneic transplantation, and lower oxygen saturation were pretransplantation risk factors for delirium. To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify allogeneic transplantation as a risk factor for delirium. This finding may have been influenced by the higher proportion of the DRI score (≥high) with allogeneic compared with autologous transplantation. Therefore, when pretransplant risk factors are present, delirium prevention and early detection are essential.

We found that post‐HSCT delirium was significantly associated with a lower occurrence of aGVHD or cGVHD. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a significant association between post‐HSCT delirium and GVHD. Based on the neuroinflammation hypothesis, GVHD is thought to promote the onset of delirium; however, we observed an opposing pattern. Previously, Amonoo et al. reported no association between post‐HSCT delirium and the development of aGVHD and cGVHD. 9 We found that delirium was significantly associated with lower survival rates during the 6‐month posttransplant period, which may have contributed to this discrepancy. That is, in cases where delirium occurred, it is conceivable that GVHD was less frequent as a result of death occurring before GVHD developed. Further studies are needed to determine the association between post‐HSCT delirium and GVHD.

The current study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single institution and involved a small number of subjects. Therefore, future studies should be conducted at multiple institutions with a larger sample. Second, our study was conducted retrospectively, and thus we may have missed cases of hypoactive delirium, in which hallucinations and agitation are not prominent. Therefore, prospective studies are required in the future to include cases of hypoactive delirium. Third, age is considered the most significant risk factor for delirium, and we found a significant difference in age between the delirium and non‐delirium groups. Therefore, even after adjusting for other risk factors in the multivariate analysis, caution is required when interpreting risk factors other than age. Fourth, we did not examine the dosage of opioids, corticosteroids, or benzodiazepines, which should be considered in future studies. Fifth, we had missing laboratory data, which requires systematic evaluation in a prospective study. Sixth, although lower oxygen saturation was identified as a risk factor, we note that levels were within the normal range even in the non‐delirium group. Finally, the concomitant occurrence of post‐HSCT delirium and aGVHD and cGVHD was small (i.e., four patients and one patient, respectively). Therefore, this association should be investigated further in a larger patient sample.

The incidence of post‐HSCT delirium was relatively high. We identified several risk factors for delirium, which included allogeneic transplantation. We also revealed a significant association between delirium and GVHD. Our findings suggested that delirium is associated with a lower rate of short‐term survival. Therefore, when pretransplantation risk factors are present, it is essential that delirium is detected early and the recommended non‐pharmacological and pharmacological interventions for delirium prevention are thoroughly implemented. Future studies on the relationship between delirium and GVHD and preventive interventions for delirium are warranted.
