High-risk human papillomavirus positivity and associated factors among women living with HIV in public health facilities of Mekelle, Northern Ethiopia

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorldwide, it is believed that high-risk human papillomavirus (hr-HPV) strains are the root cause of many types of cancers, including cervical cancer, anorectal cancers, vaginal cancers, and penile cancers, with an estimated 922, 728 females diagnosed with HPV-related cancer in 2022. Particularly, in sub-Saharan Africa, the majority of women (55.13%) were affected by hr-HPV, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive women have had double-fold higher risk of hr-HPV infection. Evidence is scarce in the study area; therefore, the current study aimed to assess the prevalence of hr-HPV and associated factors among HIV-positive women in public health facilities of Mekelle, Tigray, northern Ethiopia.MethodsA health facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in five conveniently selected public health facilities of Mekelle, northern Ethiopia, from December 10, 2024, to July 10, 2025. The study included 390 consecutively recruited HIV-positive women, from whom questionnaire-based interviews were made, and provider-collected cervical brush specimens were processed using the Abbot cervi-collect kit in accordance with standard procedures. Following automated deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction using the Abbot m2000sp, the presence of 14 hr-HPV genotypes was detected using the m2000rt real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assay. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the statistical association between the potential factors and hr-HPV status. Finally, statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval.ResultThe mean ± SD age of the respondents was 42 years (42.82 ± 9.55). The majority, 231/390 (59.2%) of the respondents, were of a normal weight, and with an overall mean Body Mass Index (BMI) of 21.77 ± 3.86 kg/m2. According to the RT-PCR, the overall prevalence of hr-HPV DNA positivity was 39.7% (155/390); [95% CI: 34.9–44.9%]. Specifically, the prevalence of HPV-16, HPV-18, and collectively the other hr-HPV types was 10.8% [95% CI: 7.7–13.8%], 3.6% [95% CI: 1.8–5.6%], and 32.7% [95% CI: 27.9–37.2%], respectively. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, condom use was assocatied with lower odds of hr-HPV infection [AOR = 0.23; 95%CI: 0.06–0.89; p = 0.034], whereas oral contraceptive use [AOR = 4.47; 95%CI: 1.39–14.28; p = 0.012], history of vaginal discharge [AOR = 2.61; 95%CI: 1.01–6.70; p = 0.046], genital ulcer disease [AOR = 4.09; 95%CI: 1.12–14.59; p = 0.030], history of unprotected sex before marriage [AOR = 2.20; 95%CI: 1.14–4.24; p = 0.017], lack of awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer [AOR = 2.23; 95%CI: 1.18–4.24; p = 0.013], duration ( less than or equal to11 years) since ART initiation [AOR = 2.05; 95%CI: 1.13–3.74; p = 0.018], a history of 3 or more documented ART interruption events [AOR = 4.37; 95CI: 1.06–17.99; p = 0.041], and having detectable recent HIV viral load (40 copies/mL or greater) [AOR = 6.89; 95%CI: 2.75–17.28; p < 0.001] were independently associated with higher odds of hr-HPV infection.ConclusionThe findings of the current study indicate a high burden (39.7%) of oncogenic HPV strains among HIV-positive women, though lower than the pooled estimates in sub-Saharan Africa. Notably, HPV-16 and the non-HPV-16/18 high-risk genotypes collectively accounted for the majority of infections. Factors that influenced the presence of high-risk HPV included a history of unprotected sex before marriage, oral contraceptive use, vaginal discharge, genital ulcer disease, lack of awareness regarding HPV and cervical cancer, a shorter duration since ART initiation, frequent ART interruptions, and detectable recent HIV viral loads.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-026-00769-0.Keywords: High-risk human papillomavirus, HIV-positive women, Risk factors, Mekelle, Ethiopia

Background

Worldwide, 1,505,394 new cancer cases (922, 728 females and 582,666 males) were attributable to human papillomavirus (HPV) in 2022. Africa takes the highest share of incidence and death rates, which is mainly driven by cervical cancer [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), recent pooled estimates show that the prevalence of high-risk HPV (hr-HPV) strains is as high as 55.13% [2]. In Ethiopia, existing literature indicates that the pooled prevalence of hr-HPV types 18 and 16 was around 53.6% among women with gynecologic complaints [3]. Additionally, a study conducted in Shashemene, south Ethiopia, reported a high prevalence of hr-HPV among women living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (35.2%) [4]. However, evidence across different regions is lacking, considering that the country is highly affected by cervical cancer (the second deadliest cancer in women of Ethiopia) [5].

Most HPV infections could be asymptomatic, but persistent infections have cancerous effects on the squamous epithelium of different anatomical sites [6, 7]. Hence, the global focus on HPV arose from the fact that hr-HPV strains are responsible for carcinogenesis [8–10]. Hr-HPV strains are associated with 96% of cervical cancer, 87.6% of anorectal cancers, 64% of vaginal cancers, 36% of penile cancers [11], and 94% of adenocarcinomas of cervical type [12]. Hr-HPV’s early proteins, called oncoproteins, cause host-cell transformation and carcinogenesis by inactivating tumour-suppressor genes [13]. Therefore, HPV is among the type 1 carcinogens, with a huge research and policy focus [14]. Moreover, due to shared modes of transmission and mucosal lesions, HPV infection can predispose to other tumor viruses, including HIV and hepatitis B virus, which further increase the development of malignant cancers [15]. Compared to HIV negative women, women living with HIV have a double-edged risk of HPV acquisition and a low rate of HPV clearance [16]. On the other side, HPV infection changes the profile of vaginal cytokines, thereby increasing the risk of HIV infection by more than double [17].

To date, the existing literature on HPV prevalence has not adequately represented most geographic regions of Ethiopia, and the determinants of hr-HPV have not been exhaustively assessed in context. In the context of Tigray Regional State, there is a lack of research evidence on the prevalence of HPV and its determinants, which can be useful for the implementation of diagnostics, treatment, vaccination and other targeted prevention strategies. Dealing with hr-HPV prevalence and its determinants can bridge clinical care and global health strategies by identifying high-risk populations, magnifying vaccine demand, and informing resource allocation, targeted screening of HPV and related malignancies, and trend monitoring. Furthermore, it serves as baseline evidence for advanced research on viral characterization, pathogenesis and vaccine escape mechanisms. Notably, compared to other regions of Ethiopia, Tigray has only recently joined the national HPV vaccination campaign since August 15, 2023, due to the onset of conflict in 2020–2022. Additionally, there is a low coverage of vaccine intake [18]. Even though the WHO recommends the use of HPV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) as a primary screening test both for women in the general population and HIV survivors, HPV screening is not widely available in the region, which has limited the information on the burden of HPV [19]. Therefore, considering the public health importance of HPV and the existing gaps in knowledge stated, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of hr-HPV and its associated factors among HIV-positive women in public health facilities of Mekelle, Tigray, northern Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted from December 10, 2024, to July 10, 2025, in five selected governmental health facilities of Mekelle city and sub-cities, Tigray, northern Ethiopia. Mekelle, the capital city of the regional state of Tigray, is located approximately 783.18 km north of Addis Ababa by road. According to the World Population Review’s estimate, the city has an estimated population of 611,574 in 2024 [20]. There are 15 public health facilities in Mekelle city (one Comprehensive Specialized Teaching Hospital, 2 General Hospitals, 2 Primary Hospitals and 10 Health Centers). Considering the availability of ART center/clinic, a dedicated room and materials (examination coach, speculum, sterilization center) for cervical brush collection, five health facilities were conveniently selected: Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, College of Health Sciences, Mekelle General Hospital, Hewo Primary Hospital, Mekelle Health Center, and Semien Health Center. All of the selected health centers and hospitals provide HIV testing and ART service, which were necessary to conduct this research.

Study design

A health facility-based cross-sectional study design was conducted.

Source and study population

The source population were those HIV positive women and /or receiving ART services in the study sites, and those HIV-positive women attending ART services in the selected study sites during the study period were the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion: All women 18 years old, living with HIV, not currently pregnant, were included in the study. Whereas, those mothers who were within 45 days of postpartum, those unable to undergo cervical sample collection for valid reasons (example: active menstruation, virgins, post-hysterectomy or cervical stenosis), comatose patients, currently pregnant, previously confirmed to have HPV DNA and under follow-up, or undergoing ablative or surgical intervention were excluded from the study.

Data collection procedures

Sociodemographic and risk factor assessment

The study participants presented at the selected study sites and, after fulfilling the eligibility criteria, consented to provide information and a clinical specimen. Trained nurses and midwives interviewed all eligible participants who consented to participate in the study. An interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire, customized from different literature [4, 21–24], and designed for this research, was used to collect sociodemographic and associated risk factor-related data, including behavioral, sexual and reproductive factors from each respondent. The body mass index (BMI) of each respondent was computed by measuring the respondent’s height and weight using an adult height-weight scale. Additionally, information about the date of HIV disclosure, date of ART initiation, current WHO acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) stage, ART regimen, recent HIV viral load, documented history of sexually transmitted infection (STI) syndromes, history of opportunistic infections, and loss-to-follow-up/ART interruption events was assessed from the client’s medical records.

Sample collection, storage and transportation

The WHO recommends the use of self-collected or healthcare provider-collected cervical samples in the study population [19]. Hence, a trained nurse/midwife collected cervical brush specimens from each study participant following preparing her in lithotomy position, pelvic examination, and after introducing a bivalve speculum through the vagina as per the WHO recommended procedures [25, 26]. Then, using an Abbott cervicollect specimen collection kit, the sample was collected from the squamocolumnar junction of the cervix with a cervical brush, as per the manufacturer’s directions and as recommended previously [27]. By removing the cervical brush from the kit, it was inserted into the cervical os, giving that only the bottom bristles remained exposed, the brush was rotated in a similar direction for three full turns. Then, the brush was withdrawn gently, and without external contamination, the brush was inserted into the uncapped transport tube. The brush was rinsed into the buffer by rotating up to 10 times, plunging against the wall in between the rotations and swirling briskly. Finally, the brush was discarded, and the transport tube was stored with proper labelling. The kit enabled the collection of cervical brush and safe transportation by keeping the integrity of the HPV DNA in its 2.4 mL of specimen transport buffer, which contains guanidine thiocyanate in Tris buffer. Then, the specimen was stored at refrigerated temperature for a maximum of 24 h, transported, and stored at -80 until analysis [28] in a molecular laboratory of Tigray Health and Research Institute (THRI), Mekelle, Northern Ethiopia.

Molecular laboratory investigations (DNA extraction, amplification and detection)

Extraction of the viral genetic material (DNA) was performed using the Abbot m2000sp automated nucleic acid extractor, which uses the magnetic bead system to isolate HPV DNA. The system enabled an automated method for transferring the eluted DNA and creating a reaction assembly in the 96-well optical reaction plate, Abbot. The system (m2000sp) mixes approximately 50 µL of the pure extract with 50 µL of amplification reagents containing oligonucleotides (primers &dNTPs), the polymerase enzyme (AmpliTaq Gold enzyme), and activation reagents (38 mM MgCl2 in a buffered solution). The reaction plate was then carefully sealed using an optical seal manually and loaded into the m2000rt real-time PCR, Abbot. The presence of DNA for14 hr-HPV types including 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68 types was detected using the Abbot RealTime high-risk HPV assay (Abbot, Abbot Park, Illinois, USA), which WHO approved for invitro diagnostics and one of the VALGENT approved molecular assays for the detection of hr-HPV DNA [29–31]. The assay enabled amplification and qualitative identification of the hr-HPV genotypes by targeting the major capsular protein’s (L1) consensus region (150 bases) using three forward primers and two reverse primers. In the assay, AmpliTaq Gold enzyme was pre-activated (at 92 ℃ for 10 min), and with the help of the thermocycler, amplification of target DNA was performed through the denaturation, annealing and polymerization process for up to 38 thermal cycles.

Lastly, an additional reading step after the 38 thermal cycles, the temperature is lowered to allow probe annealing to the target DNA (hr-HPV) and detection of fluorescent signals in a real-time fashion. The detection was based on the principle of fluorescent-quencher technology. This means, the system employs type specific fluorescent labeled probes (ss-DNA oligonucleotides) for the genotypes (HPV-16, HPV-18, non 18/16 other hr-HPVs), and for the endogenous human beta globin gene (136 bases) (internal control (IC)) specific probe to distinguish their fluorescent signals in four different channels (VIC, NED, FAM, &Cy5) and as described in other studies [28, 32, 33]. Hence, the RT-PCR specifically identifies hr-HPV-16 &hr-HPV-18, and collectively (pool) reports the remaining twelve strains as “other hr-HPVs” while the IC is used as a means of controlling sample validity (cell adequacy, and efficiency of the extraction and amplification process) [33]. The interpretation of results was made based on the cycle number (CN) in relation to the assay’s cycle threshold (CT) cutoff values: CN values below the CT cutoff values [32] were considered as positive (“detected”), samples with no CN generated or a CN value of 32 were considered as negative (“not detected”). Additionally, the results were considered as invalid (repeat) if the CN of the IC were above 35 [34]. After recording, the results of all HR-HPV positive women were procedurally linked to their medical record for further management (visual inspection acetic acid (VIA) screening and or histopathology) as per the WHO’s testing algorithm [19].

The current RT-PCR modality displays the results of HPV-16 and HPV-18 independently, whereas it is capable of detecting and reporting the remaining 12 high-risk types collectively as “other hr-HPVs”.

Data analysis

After coding and entering the data into Epi Info version 7.2.7, the data were exported to a Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 for analysis. The frequency and percentages of hr-HPV positivity, sociodemographic characteristics, and other characteristics of the study participants were computed using descriptive statistics; cross-tabulations were made to generate two-by-two tables with chi-square (X2) values. A bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to determine a statistical association between the potential risk factors and a dichotomous variable (hr-HPV status). The Model fitness was determined using a Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and the model was considered fit at p > 0.05. All variables that showed statistical significance at p < 0.05 were selected for a multivariate analysis. Additionally, before the final multivariate analysis, multicollinearity was assessed simultaneously in the regression model. Final judgment of multicollinearity was made by carefully visualizing the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) in the correlation matrix and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Then, only two variables were found to be with a potential issue of multicollinearity: (1) “years since HIV disclosure” (VIF = 3.874) versus “years since HAART initiation” (VIF = 3.926) and (r=-0.85); (2) “history of ART regimen change (VIF = 1.210) versus “Number of ART interruption events” (VIF = 1.422) and (r=-0.344). Based on the correlation coefficients, the history of ART regimen change and years since HIV disclosure were excluded from the multivariate analysis due to collinearity with the above variables of similar context. Finally, reanalysing the data in the multivariate analysis yielded a VIF of < 2 for all variables (minimum VIF value = 1.106 and maximum VIF value = 1.426). This shows the overall model’s coefficients are stable because VIF values between ‘1’ and ‘5’ are generally acceptable in a multivariate analysis (the details of the correlation matrix and VIF values can be found in Supplementary file 2).

Furthermore, the events per variable rule of thumb (10 EPV) was applied to avoid the overfitting effect; in this case, the total sample size (N) was 390 with 155 positives, which gives 10 EPV = 155/10 = 15.5. Therefore, 15 variables were included in the multivariate analysis. The “enter” method was used to analyze all variables simultaneously, control confounders and obtain the adjusted odds ratios. Finally, the 95% confidence interval and p < 0.05 were considered to declare statistically significant associations between the dependent variable and independent variables. The strength and direction of the associations were determined by the adjusted odds ratios (AOR).

Data quality assurance

To ensure the quality of the research, all efforts were invested from the pre-analytical to the post-analytical phase. Five per cent of the questionnaire was pretested in a similar population, and corrective measures were taken for any inconsistencies and terminologies. All data collectors and supervisors were healthcare providers in the field, and after a one-week formal training, they received on-the-job coaching by a team of trainers (a General Practitioner, senior nurse and medical microbiologist). The cervical sample was collected aseptically according to standard protocols in the field (refer to the sample collection section) and the standardised directions of the Abbot cervi-collect kit. During the molecular investigation, external positive (< 0.01% noninfectious DNA with HPV and Beta Globin sequences in a buffered solution with carrier DNA) and negative controls (< 0.01% noninfectious DNA with Beta Globin sequence in a buffered solution with carrier DNA) were tested in each run to rule out contamination, false positivity and ensure equipment functionality, sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, the Alinity m2000sp system software enabled maintaining the quality of the extraction and amplification process (cell adequacy, DNA extraction, and amplification efficiency) by detecting the endogenous sequence of β–globin as a cellular control signal or internal control (IC) [32]. The use of these external and system controls was in compliance with the recommended quality assurance measures for automated HPV testing [35]. Additionally, molecular testing (extraction and RT-PCR-based amplification) was performed according to the manufacturer’s (Abbot) directions and under the direct supervision of two senior molecular experts.

Substance use, reproductive, behavioural, clinical, ART-related and other characteristics of the respondents

According to the descriptive statistical assessments on the substance-related behaviors, most of the HIV-positive women were free of smoking (97.4%), “shisha” (98.7%), “khat” chewing (98.7%), and alcohol addiction (94.1%). Almost half (50.8%) of the respondents had early sexual coitus history in the early ages (11–18 years of age), and the majority were non-contraceptive users (64.4%). While the history of rape (3.1%) and commercial sex work (3.3%) were rare, a significant fraction of the respondents had experienced STI syndromes, a history of multiple sexual partnerships, a history of bare sex before marriage, and a partner history of STI. Given that the majority were unvaccinated for HPV, they also lack awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer.

Regarding the ART-related characteristics, the mean ± SD number of years since HIV disclosure and ART initiation was 11.42 ± 5.84 years and 10.95 ± 5.61 years, respectively. All of the respondents have initiated their ART (100%), and most of them were on a first-line ART regimen (90.3%), on WHO AIDS stage one (72.8%), and with undetectable (< 40 copies/mL) HIV viral load levels (80.5%) (Table 2).

Prevalence of high-risk HPV among HIV-positive women

In the current study, cervical brush specimens from the 390 respondents were subjected to RT-PCR for high-risk HPV DNA positivity as detailed in the methods section. According to the RT-PCR findings, the overall prevalence of hr-HPV DNA positivity was 39.7% (155/390) [95% CI: 34.9–44.9%]. Specifically, and given that the denominator is the total population (390), the prevalence of HPV-16, HPV-18, and collectively the other hr-HPV types were, 10.8% (42/390) [95% CI: 7.7–13.8%], 3.6% (14/390) [95% CI: 1.8–5.6%], and 32.7% (127/390) [95% CI: 27.9–37.2%], respectively. Proportionally, hr-HPV types other than types 16 &18 were detected in 82% (127/155) of the hr-HPV positive respondents, and mixed infection of HPV-16/18 with other hr-HPVs were 16.8% (26/155), while no HPV-16&18 co-infection (without the involvement of other Hr-HPVs) was detected (Fig. 2).

Descriptively, hr-HPV positivity was high in the age group of 35–49 years (43%), those overweight in BMI (49.3%) (Fig. 3), those who read and write (55.1%) and elementary (47.1%) in educational level, not married (70.8%) in marital status, daily laborers (56.5%) in employment status and urban (41.7%) in residence. Regarding the ART-related characteristics, the distribution of hr-HPV appears to be almost comparable between the categories of the WHO AIDS stages (1–4) and ART regimens (first–line to third–line). However, clients with a high frequency of ART interruption events (82.6%) were found to be highly affected by hr-HPV. Additionally, hr-HPV positivity was high among those with detectable HIV viral load (86.6%), years since ART initiation (52%), and those with a history of vaginal discharge (63.6%), pelvic inflammatory disease (77.3%), and genital ulcer disease (80.6%) (Fig. 3, & S Table 1).

Determinants of HR-HPV among HIV-positive women

Bivariate and multivariable analysis of factors associated with high-risk HPV DNA positivity

In this study, different sociodemographic, substance use, sexual, reproductive, and clinical and ART-related variables were subjected to bivariate logistic regression analysis (supplementary file 1). In the bivariate analysis, variables including age, BMI, self-reported alcohol addiction, cigarette smoking, substance use (“khat” & “shisha”), history of abortion, history of commercial sex work, history of rape, HPV vaccination status, WHO AIDS stage, ART regimen, history of opportunistic infections, and history of underlying chronic disease were not significantly associated with hr-HPV in the bivariate analysis (p values for these vaiables were > 0.05); refere the details of the bivariate logistic regeression analysis and all p values in supplementary file 1. Whereas, being urban in residence, widowed in marital status, daily labourers in employment status, educational level (read and write, and elementary), contraceptive use, age at first sexual coitus (11–18 years), history of STI syndrome, partner history of STI, history of bare sex, and lifetime number of sexual partners were significantly associated variables. Furthermore, lack of awareness on STI causes, and HPV-linked cervical cancer, years since HIV disclosure &ART initiation, history of ART interruption &regimen change, and detectable levels of HIV viral load were found to be significantly associated with hr-HPV at p < 0.05. The variables in the bivariate analysis, along with their crude odds ratios (COR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), are summarized in Supplementary file 1 (supplementary file 1).

In the multivariate analysis, all variables that showed statistical significance at p in th bivariate analysis were included to identify the final significant predictors of hr-HPV, along with their adjusted odds ratios (AOR). After screening for multi-collinearity and model fitness, 15 variables were fiited in to the multivariate analysis. Finally, the multivariate analysis indicated that condom use [AOR = 0.23; 95%CI: 06-0.89, p = 0.034], oral contraceptive use [AOR=4.47; 95%CI: 1.39–14.28, p = 0.012], history of vaginal discharge [AOR=2.61; 95%CI:1.01–6.70, p = 0.046], genital ulcer disease [AOR=4.09; 95%CI: 1.12–14.59, p = 0.030], history of bare sex before marriage [AOR=2.20; 95%CI: 1.14–4.24, p = 0.017], lack of awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer [AOR=2.23; 95%CI:1.18–4.24, p = 0.013], years since ART initiation [AOR=2.05; 95%CI: 1.13–3.74, p = 0.018], 3 times of history of ART interruption events [AOR=4.37; 95CI: 1.06–17.99, p = 0.041], and recent HIV viral load of 40 copies/mL [AOR=6.89; 95%CI: 2.75–17.28, p < 0.01] were the significant predictors of hr-HPV positivity (Table 3).

Discussion

The current study determined the prevalence of hr-HPV and its associated risk factors among HIV-positive women, which can potentially fill the evidence gap in Tigray, northern Ethiopia. The findings indicate that the prevalence of hr-HPV among HIV-positive women was 39.7% [95% CI: 34.9–44.9%]. Specifically, the non-HPV-16/18 pool of other hr-HPV types (32.7% was more prevalent than HPV-16 (10.8%) and HPV-18 (3.6%). In comparison to the same study population, the overall prevalence of hr-HPV in the current study is higher than the findings of northeast Ethiopia (28.8%) [36], Togo (22.2%) [37], and Indonesia (24%) [38], but lower than the findings in Kenya at 47% [39] and India (66.6%) [40]. Nearly comparable findings were also reported in the studies conducted in western Oromia, Ethiopia (30.4%) [21], in Shashemene, south Ethiopia (35.2%) [4], Adama, Ethiopia (46%) [41], south Africa (32.2–40.6%) [42, 43], Ghana (42.7–44.4%) [24, 44], Mali (42.6%) [45], Cameron (43.38) [46], and Gabon (41.37%) [47]. The variations across studies could be attributable to study period, sample collection method (self-collected versus provider collected), diagnostic modalities, and the socio-cultural and clinical characteristics of the study populations [48].

The current prevalence of hr-HPV is slightly lower than the pooled estimates in Africa (50.9-75.51%) [2, 23, 49]; however, the abundant pool of other hr-HPVs underscores the need for further genotyping to uncover vaccine-uncovered genotypes and challenge the vaccine coverage in the region and Africa at large [42, 47, 50]. Furthermore, evidence in Africa, such as Cameroon, Zimbabwe and Kenya, indicates that there was a high burden of hr-HPV strains among HIV-positive women, and with severe cervical abnormalities [39, 46, 51]. The non-16/18 hr-HPV strains, including HPV-45 and HPV-58, were also associated with persistent infections among cohorts of HIV-positive women [52]. Hence, the high burden of oncogenic HPV strains in the current study has many implications in exploring HPV-linked cervical cancer in the region. The current findings further highlight the substantial and often underrecognized burden of HPV- associated cervical abnormalities in the region, underscoring the need to integrate the HPV testing modalities with existing cervical cancer screening programs for resource-limited settings as previously recommended [39, 53]. This approach aligns with the WHO’s “screen-triage-treat” strategy and has the potential to enhance the efficiency of visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) and cytological examinations in the early detection and longitudinal monitoring of cervical abnormalities.

According to the risk factor analysis, the current study revealed that condom and oral contraceptive use, history of vaginal discharge, history of genital ulcer disease, history of unprotected sex before marriage, lack of awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer, years since ART initiation, history of ART interruption events, and detectable HIV viral load were the significantly associated risk factors of hr-HPV infection. In the context of condom use, the odds of hr-HPV positivity were 77% lower among condom users compared to non-condom users. On the other hand, those HIV-positive women with a history of bare sex before marriage had 2.2 times higher odds of hr-HPV infection compared to their counterparts. This finding strengthens the role of condom use for reducing HPV infections, and is in line with other studies that reported unprotected sex or lack of condom use as a risk factor for hr-HPV in Ethiopia and elsewhere [21, 54, 55]. On the other hand, according to a study conducted in Kenya, HIV-positive women were more frequently condom users but with multiple hr-HPV types compared to HIV-negative women, which is an indication for the non-eliminating role of condom use for HPV infection, especially in the context of multiple sexual partnerships [56]. In connection to this, oral contraceptive users have had 4.47 times higher odds of hr-HPV compared to non-contraceptive users. The existing literature indicates that non-protective contraceptive users, including oral contraceptives, have been significantly associated with HPV infection among women in other studies of eastern and southern Africa [55, 57]. Long-term use of oral contraceptives may promote HPV persistence due to various reasons, including hormonal changes, immune modulation, depletion of nutrients required to mount an immune response, and increasing cervical ectropion [58–61].

Even though the association of sexually transmitted infections and hr-HPV had been established in many other studies [4, 21, 62], in the current study, specifically, those with a history of vaginal discharge and genital ulcer diseases have had 2.61 and 4.09 times higher risk of hr-HPV infection. Vaginal discharge syndrome as a risk factor for HR-HPV is in line with the study conducted in Oromia, Ethiopia (AOR = 3.31) [21], and South Africa (AOR = 2.13) [43]. These STI syndromes may be indications for immunosuppression, superinfections, and may favour lesion-induced HPV infection and low degree of clearance. For instance, a study conducted in Kenya indicated that those HIV-positive women with bacterial vaginosis, cervicitis and immunosuppression were heavily affected by multiple hr-HPV strains, including HPV-16, HPV-18 and HPV-53 [63]. Additionally, through alterations on the mucosa and local immunologic responses, etiologies of genital ulcer disease, such as type 2 herpes simplex virus, were suggested to increase the risk and persistence of hr-HPV infection [64, 65].

The current findings indicate a significant correlation between ART-related factors and high-risk human papillomavirus (hr-HPV) infection among HIV-positive women. Specifically, those with a history of frequent interruptions in ART (≥ 3 times) have 4.37 times higher odds of hr-HPV infection, while women with shorter ART durations (≤ 11 years) face 2.05 times higher odds, and those with detectable HIV viral loads exhibit a 6.89 times higher likelihood of testing positive for hr-HPV compared to their counterparts. These findings align with previous studies conducted in four African nations, suggesting that shorter ART duration and increased viral load contribute to greater susceptibility to hr-HPV due to compromised immunity [23]. Furthermore, evidence from Tanzania shows a notable increase in hr-HPV persistence linked to lowered CD4 levels among both HIV-positive and HIV-negative women [52]. Additional studies in Ethiopia, Ghana, and Nigeria support the higher odds (3–5 times higher) of hr-HPV infection associated with elevated HIV viral loads and decreased CD4+ cell counts [4, 53, 66]. Conversely, prolonged ART usage is associated with a reduced risk of hr-HPV [44, 67], highlighting the potential benefits of long-term ART in minimizing cervical neoplasia risk in sub-Saharan Africa by enhancing immune response and lowering hr-HPV prevalence [67, 68].

The other important risk factor was a lack of awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer. HIV-positive women who have no awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer were found to be 2.23 times more likely to have acquired hr-HPV. In Ethiopia, it was indicated that there is low community awareness of cervical cancer and HPV infection, which can increase the risk of HPV infection, thereby reducing testing/screening, low vaccine intake and limited risk reduction measures [69]. This indicates that awareness creation programs are critically important in reducing HPV infection and associated abnormalities, as suggested in Ethiopia and other countries [69, 70].

By implication, the recent findings reveal a substantial prevalence of HPV and other sexually transmitted infections in the study population, indicating a considerable risk for HPV-related health complications. This elevated incidence, often fueled by vaccine-type and non-vaccine type high-risk HPV strains, greatly heightens the likelihood of developing cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and various cancers, especially when combined with risk factors such as early initiation of sexual activity, multiple sexual partnership, weak ART monitoring and insufficient vaccination. The region demands implementation of benchmarks for an accelerated coverage of the HPV vaccination among women living with HIV, possibly through catch-up strategies including targeted campaigns, combining HPV vaccination with other services such as HIV testing and monitoring, an opportunistic vaccination approach, and through outreach activities to breach barriers to vaccination.

Overall, the current study has included a large volume of study participants, determined the prevalence of hr-HPV, and explored a broader range of determinants that have high public health and clinical implications. The study addressed an area of low-resource settings in which evidence is also scarce. However, the study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which restricts the ability to establish causality and temporality. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling limits generalizability, particularly to the rural/displaced populations, while the reliance on a single cervical sample makes it impossible to distinguish between incident and persistent infections. The absence of CD4 count data and the potential for self-report bias regarding sexual behaviors further compromise the findings. The genotyping was also limited, categorizing 12 types as “other hr-HPVs.”

Conclusion

The findings of the current study indicate a high burden (39.7%) of oncogenic HPV strains among HIV-positive women, though lower than the pooled estimates in sub-Saharan Africa. Notably, HPV-16 and the non-HPV-16/18 high-risk genotypes collectively accounted for the majority of infections. Condom use was protective, history of unprotected sex before marriage, oral contraceptive use, history of vaginal discharge, history of genital ulcer disease, lack of awareness about HPV-linked cervical cancer, lower length of years since ART initiation, frequent history of ART interruption events, and detectable recent HIV viral load were the determinants of hr-HPV. Therefore, routine HPV testing should be integrated into HIV care. Additionally, enhanced protocols for STI screening, targeted health education campaigns, vaccine catch-up programs for women living with HIV, and expanded genotyping studies are recommended.
