Virome characterization of wild small mammals provides new insight into zoonotic pathogens in West Africa
Authors: Xiao-Ai Zhang, Mei-Qi Zhang, Ya-Wen Liu, Lei Lin, Jing-Tao Zhang, Thomoshire George, Mohamed Boie Jalloh, Stephen Sevalie, Kandeh Bassie Kargbo, Bao-Gui Jiang, Zhi-Qiang Mi, Shu-Chao Wang, Guang-Qian Si, Lei Zhang, Li-Qun Fang, Wei-Wei Chen, Gang Dong, Wei-Jin Huang, Wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA significant number of infectious diseases affecting humans have been associated with zoonotic viruses. Wild small mammals, such as bats, rodents, and shrews, serve as natural reservoirs for a multitude of zoonotic viruses, particularly in Africa, where zoonosis is prevalent. Nevertheless, our knowledge of the virome composition within these hosts remains limited, impeding a more profound understanding of spillover events into human populations.ResultsWe employed a viral metagenomics approach to characterize the virome in 846 wild small mammals sampled from Sierra Leone. Based on the complete RNA-dependent RNA polymerase genome, a total of 39 RNA viruses infecting mammals were identified, comprising 13 known viruses and 26 novel viruses. Notably, the Paramyxoviridae family exhibited the highest diversity of viral species across all three orders of wild mammal. The animal species Hipposideros jonesi and Lophuromys chrysopus were found to harbor the highest viral richness. Among these viral species, 15 were identified as cross-species transmitted viruses shared among different animal species, 3 were classified as zoonotic (Encephalomyocarditis virus, Rocahepevirus sp., and Lassa virus), while 3 others posed a potential risk for spillover (melian virus, Rodent hepacivirus, Hunnivirus A). Cross-species transmission analysis revealed that rodents played central roles in virus sharing, while cross-order viral transmission was less likely to occur in bats. Among 26 newly identified viruses, four viruses (Bat ledantevirus 2, Rattus rattus jeilongvirus, Miniopterus inflatus ribovirus, and Rat mamastrovirus) were predicted to have high zoonotic potential. Among them, Bat ledantevirus 2 exhibited the highest zoonotic potential and phylogenetic relatedness to the known human-infecting virus (Le Dantec virus). Further seroepidemiological analysis in patients, using single-round infectious virus particles as antigens, revealed the presence of neutralizing antibodies against Bat ledantevirus 2, a novel virus belonging to the Rhabdoviridae family.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the critical need for enhanced surveillance at the human-animal interface in order to identify viruses with cross-species transmission potential prior to their spillover into human population. Introduction

Over the past decade, the majority of emerging human infectious diseases have originated from animals via cross-species transmission. The persistent spillover of zoonotic viral pathogens imposes a substantial burden on global public health and social security. Wild small mammals are recognized as primary natural reservoirs for zoonotic viruses, and the cross-species transmission within specific mammalian taxonomic groups, such as bats, shrews, and rodents, has been intensively described [1, 2]. Rodents, representing ~ 42% of mammalian species, constitute the most abundant and diverse mammalian order. They harbor numerous zoonotic viruses, including human coronaviruses OC43 and HKU1, which significantly impacted public health [3]. Shrews, the fourth-largest mammalian group, carry notable human pathogens like hantavirus, Rotavirus A, Wenzhou mammarenavirus, and the recently discovered Langya henipavirus (LayV) [4–9]. Apart from rodents and shrews, bats are known to harbor a wide variety of viruses, including Ebola virus, Marburg virus, Nipah virus, Hendra virus, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East respiratory coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and SARS-CoV-2, leading to severe epidemic or endemic diseases in humans [10, 11]. These wild small mammals often inhabit areas in close proximity to human settlements and domestic animals, facilitating both zoonotic transmissions to humans and reverse-zoonotic transmission back to animal populations. Characterizing viral diversity in these wild mammals and identifying potential cross-species transmission or spillover events are crucial for the early detection of emerging infectious diseases.

Africa is a recognized “geographic hotspot” for the emergence of zoonotic diseases. Sierra Leone, among the world’s least developed nations [12], exemplifies this risk. The country has a population size of 8.79 million, ~ 50% living below US $1.25 per day, and a low life expectancy at birth of 60.41 years [13]. Limited access to basic infrastructure exacerbates the threats from infectious diseases, such as HIV, tuberculosis, malaria, Lassa fever, Ebola virus disease (EVD), and monkeypox [14, 15]. During the 2014–2016 West Africa EVD outbreak, Sierra Leone suffered the most severe impact [16], reporting over 28,000 cases and more than 11,000 fatalities [17]. As a consequence of this unprecedented epidemic, Sierra Leone continues to receive international support for the surveillance and control of local infectious diseases. Zoonoses remain the primary cause of infectious diseases in this region, given that a significant portion of the population engages in agricultural activities. However, there is limited knowledge regarding viral diversity and abundance within wild small mammals in this hotspot country.

In this study, we employed transcriptomic sequencing to investigate the virome and cross-species viral transmission among small mammals (bats, rodents, and shrews) in Sierra Leone. Using pseudotyped virus neutralization assays, we further detected specific antibodies against the novel rhabdovirus Bat ledantevirus 2. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of viral diversity and identifies putative zoonotic viruses in wild small mammals.

Materials and methods

Sample collections

During the 2018–2023 period, as an integral part of a long-term project dedicated to assessing rodent-borne pathogens, wild small mammals were captured and sampled at 17 geographic sites across 3 districts in Sierra Leone: Freetown, Kenema, and Moyamba. Rodents and shrews were captured using snap traps, while bats were captured using mist nets strategically placed near caves or suitable habitats with natural flyways and corridors. Species identification was initially conducted based on morphological features and subsequently confirmed through sequencing of the mitochondrial cytochrome b (mt-cyt b) gene [18, 19]. Animal dissections were performed within biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory setting at the African Tropical Infectious Disease Prevention and Control Center (Freetown, Sierra Leone). Organ samples including heart, lung, liver, kidney, and spleen were collected from each captured animal. Concurrently, an epidemiological investigation was performed on local residents living in close proximity to the animal capture sites. A structured questionnaire was administered to identify individuals who had exhibited symptoms of fever within one year prior to the interview. Serum samples were collected from these individuals for serological testing. Additionally, serum samples from healthy individuals were obtained and used as controls for the serological test. All procedures adhered to ethical standards established by the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee and followed guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians.

RNA extraction, library construction and next-generation sequencing (NGS)

The metagenomic NGS analysis was conducted using previously established metagenomic NGS methods (Supplementary Table 1) [4, 20]. Tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from each individual animal were pooled by animal species and locations. Equal amounts of these tissues were mixed and homogenized (Supplementary Table 2). Subsequently, RNA extraction was performed using the TIANamp Virus RNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer’s introductions. The concentration of RNA was quantified using Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and the quality of RNA was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2200 (Agilent, USA). Ribosomal RNA was removed using the Nucleic Acid Microbes Purification Kit (Macro & Micro Test Co., Ltd., China), and the Pathogen Metagenomic Assay Kit (RNA-Illumina Edition) (Macro & Micro Test Co., Ltd.) was utilized to prepare the sequencing library. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), generating paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp.

Data processing and virus identification

The sequencing reads were trimmed for adaptors and quality using the Trimmomatic program (v0.39) with the following parameters: LEADING:5 TRAILING:5 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36. After filtering, trimming, and error removal, high-quality reads were obtained and mapped to host sequences using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) to remove host-associated reads. De novo assembly of the clean reads was performed using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) software. To identify viral contigs, all assembled contigs were compared against the nonredundant (nr) protein database from GenBank (as of July 2023) using DIAMOND BLASTx (v2.0.14), with an e-value threshold of 1 × 10−5. Contigs showing significant similarity to viral proteins within Riboviria (NCBI Taxonomy ID 2559587) through top blast hits were initially identified as potential virus sequences. To further eliminate host sequences, all potential virus contigs were compared against the NCBI nucleotide sequence database (nt, 2023 July 31 version) using BLASTn (v2.15.0) and the database of China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB). Virus contigs assigned to Retroviridae were excluded from further analysis. Unclassified viruses were labeled as such when no specific family could be assigned based on the best match for a given viral contig. Potential host associations for virus contigs were preliminarily identified based on taxonomic information obtained from DIAMOND BLASTx and BLASTn results, which were further confirmed by their phylogenetic relationships with known host-associated viruses. Viral contigs falling into known vertebrate-associated virus groups were retained, while those clustering with bacterial, fungal, plant, or invertebrate-associated virus groups were excluded for further analysis. To quantify virus abundance, ribosomal RNA reads in each library were subtracted by aligning them to the SILVA rRNA database (https://www.arb-silva.de/) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5). Then non-rRNA reads were aligned using the “end-to-end” parameter. The resulting alignments were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.5) to obtain read counts for each contig. The virus abundance in each sample was quantified as the number of mapped reads per million non-rRNA reads (RPM) in each library. Only viral contigs with an RPM ≥ 1 were retained to reduce false positives. Geneious Prime (v2021.1.1) was used to merge contigs with overlapping sequences that had not been previously assembled, thereby generating longer viral contigs. During this process, viral contigs containing RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) for RNA viruses or replicase for DNA viruses were retained for further analysis. Species assignment of resulting viral contigs followed the species demarcation criteria established by the ICTV (https://ictv.global/) (Supplementary Table 3). For genera that lacked clear species demarcation criteria, a stricter threshold of 80% amino acid identity to known virus species for RdRp or replicase was used. These criteria also facilitated the identification of both novel and identical virus species across sequencing libraries and mammal species. The schematic of the workflow for data processing is provided in Fig. S1.

Phylogenetic and recombination analyses

To determine the evolutionary history of the known and newly discovered viruses, we estimated viral phylogenies using the amino acid sequences of hallmark proteins of virus. Viral amino acid sequences were aligned with their respective virus families or genera based on DIAMOND BLASTx results using MAFFT (v7.505). Ambiguously aligned regions in multiple sequence alignments were removed using trimAl (v1.4.rev15). Amino acid sequences were used to construct phylogenetic trees with IQ-TREE (v1.6.12) by using LG + G as the best-fit substitution model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. Potential recombination events within viral genomes and possible recombination breakpoints were detected through Simplot (v3.5.1) [21] and RDP (v4.97) [22]. To accurately identify recombination breakpoints, we utilized multiple algorithms integrated within RDP, including BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and GENECONV. A breakpoint was deemed valid only when supported by multiple methods and further verified by similarity plots in Simplot. For each identified virus, a set of reference sequences from the top 10 closely related viruses, which were identified through BLASTx searches against the GenBank database, was employed for the initial recombination screening. Subsequently, the nucleotide sequences of two viruses that exhibited putative recombination signals, Hunnivirus A and Lophuromys chrysopus hunnivirus, were analyzed using corresponding reference strains obtained from GenBank. Specifically, for our Hunnivirus A strain X-rodent-19, the reference strains were Rattus tanezumi hunnivirus (Wencheng-Rt38-2; NCBI accession: MF352430) and Hunnivirus A (22,084 × 8–856; MZ544200). For our Lophuromys chrysopus hunnivirus strain X-rodent-5, the reference strains were Hunnivirus A9 (RtRrs-PicoV/YN2014, KY432925) and Hunnivirus A6 (rodent/Rn/PicoV/SX2015_2, KX156157). Due to the smoothing algorithm used in Simplot, certain genomic regions with very low similarity may display slightly negative values in the plot. These negative scores are computational artifacts and were not considered biologically meaningful in our analysis.

Virus confirmation and detection

The presence of the identified viruses was confirmed through real-time RT-PCR assays using primers and probes specifically designed based on the assembled viral contigs (Supplementary Table 4). For a comprehensive characterization of the genomes of significant and newly discovered viruses, 5′ and 3′ RACE Kit (Invitrogen) were employed where appropriate.

Cross-species transmission of viruses

Cross-species transmission analysis of viruses was conducted based on host information. A total of 39 viruses (13 known viruses and 26 novel viruses) identified in the current study along with the host information of 13 known viruses obtained from the NCBI/GenBank database (specifically the version released on Sep 17, 2024 (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank) were utilized.

We integrated host data from two sources:(i)Direct detection in this study: In our metagenomic survey, the mammalian host species of each novel and known virus were identified.(ii)Historical records: Host information was derived from the NCBI GenBank nucleotide database (version released September 17, 2024).

For each known virus identified in our study, relevant viral sequences were retrieved from the NCBI nucleotide database, and host information was extracted from the corresponding GenBank records. Host names were standardized using the NCBI taxonomy database. Entries with ambiguous or nonanimal hosts (e.g., environmental sample and plants) were manually curated and removed. Duplicate host information per virus species was merged. Ultimately, the host data of 39 viruses from our study were integrated with those from NCBI/GenBank, as elaborated in Supplementary Table 5. The Cytoscape software (v3.10.2) was employed for the analysis of the cross-species transmission network using the prefuse force-directed layout algorithm [23]. The “Analyze Network” function was employed to conduct a topological analysis of cross-species transmission network. Node shapes were used to differentiate virus species (circles) and animal species (squares), while node colors were used to differentiate between the different risk categories of viruses.

Viruses identified in our study were classified into distinct risk categories based on the integrated host dataset [24]. Zoonotic virus was defined as virus species with documented detection in human samples. Spillover-risk virus was defined as those demonstrating infection in hosts belonging to more than one animal order and with no documented detection in humans. High-risk virus was defined as those classified as either zoonotic or spillover risk [25]. Cross-species virus was defined as those species detected in more than one host species (within the same order or across orders). Species-specific virus was defined as those detected in only one host species within the integrated dataset.

To investigate the influence of phylogenetic or geographic distance between host species on the number of shared virus species, we conducted Poisson regression analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0).

Zoonotic potential assessment of viruses

To characterize the zoonotic potential of the 36 identified viral species which have not been detected in humans, the best expression model [26] was used with complete genome or near-complete genome sequence. Zoonotic risk model was trained on a range of viral genome features (i.e., codon usage biases, amino acid biases, and dinucleotide biases) and was calculated directly from viral genome or based on the similarity of viral genome composition to human gene transcripts. Viruses were categorized into four priority tiers based on the relationship between the model’s predicted probability (with 95% confidence intervals, CIs) and the established cutoff value of 0.293 [26], i.e., low zoonotic potential: entire 95% CI or ≤ 0.293; medium zoonotic potential: mean prediction ≤ 0.293 but CI crosses it; high zoonotic potential: mean prediction > 0.293 but CI crosses it; and very high zoonotic potential: entire CI > 0.293.

Pseudovirus-based neutralization assay (PBNA)

The Bat·ledantevirus·2 pseudovirus was generated and utilized to measure neutralization antibody titers by the reduction in GFP expression [27, 28]. To produce the pseudovirus, the G-Bat·ledantevirus·2 plasmid was transiently transfected into 293 T cells using lipofectamine 3000 for 4–6 h, followed by infection with prVSV-ΔG-GFP pseudovirus (MOI = 0.2) for 1–2 h. The supernatant containing the pseudovirus was harvested, subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min, and stored at − 80 °C until further use. For the neutralization assay, serum samples were serially diluted and incubated with prVSV-Bat·ledantevirus·2-GFP pseudovirus before being added to Vero cells. GFP-positive cells were subsequently quantified to determine the neutralizing antibody titers. The ID50 values were then calculated using the Reed-Muench method [29, 30].

These animals were classified into 22 species from 12 genera, comprising 387 rodents (12 species, 9 genera), 448 bats (7 species, 2 genera), and 11 shrews (3 species, 1 genus). Virome analysis of wild small mammals

The samples were grouped into 49 pools according to species and sampling locations and subjected to next-generation sequencing across 49 libraries (Supplementary Table 2). Following rigorous quality control, over 12.9 billion sequence reads were acquired which were de novo assembled into 9113 viral contigs (totally 0.2 billion viral reads). Contigs associated with bacterial, fungal, and plant-associated viruses, amounting to approximately 1877 in total, were identified and subsequently excluded from further analyses. Through blast alignment and homology analysis of RdRp for RNA viruses, a total of 39 RT-PCR-verified vertebrate-associated RNA viruses were identified. These viruses belong to three phyla (Kitrinoviricota, Negarnaviricota, and Pisuviricota) as well as unclassified Riboviria. Negative-sense ssRNA viruses

Among the 13 negative-sense ssRNA viruses, 9 were newly identified and 4 had been previously characterized, spanning 10 viral families (Fig. 4). Based on the number of viral counts, the predominant families were Paramyxoviridae, Arenaviridae, and Rhabdoviridae.

Within the Paramyxoviridae family, 11 viruses were characterized and classified into three genera (Jeilongvirus, Narmovirus, and Henipavirus). Jeilongvirus exhibited the highest diversity with seven novel and one known virus species, followed by Henipavirus with one novel and one known virus and Narmovirus with one known virus. The RdRp gene of all 11 Paramyxoviridae viruses consisted of the Mononeg_RNA_pol domain targeting RdRp in mononegaviruses as well as the paramyx_RNAcap domain specifically targeting mRNA cap structure in paramyxoviruses. Full-genome sequences were obtained for 7 of the 11 viral species within the Paramyxoviridae family. Notably, Crocidura olivieri henipavirus 1, a novel virus, clustered closely with LayV, exhibiting 78.8% amino acid (aa) identity in the RdRp gene. LayV was confirmed to be pathogenic to humans in China in 2018.

Within the Arenaviridae family, three strains of Lassa virus, a human pathogen, were identified in M. erythroleucus and R. rattus. All three strains possessed complete genome structures, including glycoprotein precursor, nucleoprotein, Z protein, and L protein, and displayed sequence identities ranging from 93.6 to 94.2% with previously reported Lassa virus strains associated with human disease in the RdRp gene encoding the entire L protein (2218 to 2220 aa).

Within the Rhabdoviridae family, a novel virus belonging to the genus Ledantevirus, designated as Bat ledantevirus 2, was identified. It shared 79.6% aa identity based on the L protein with Kolente virus, which was discovered in Amblyomma variegatum ticks in Guinea.

Positive-sense ssRNA viruses

Among the 23 positive-sense ssRNA viruses identified, 15 were newly identified, and 8 had been previously reported, spanning 7 viral families (Fig. 5). The Picornaviridae, Astroviridae, Flaviviridae, and Hepeviridae emerged as the predominant families based on virus counts.

Within the Picornaviridae family, six viruses were identified and classified into five genera: Hunnivirus (two viruses, including one known and one novel virus), Cardiovirus (one known virus), Kobuvirus (one novel virus), Limnipivirus (one novel virus), and Rosavirus (one novel virus). The novel virus in Kobuvirus and Rosavirus demonstrated aa identities of 62.6% and 71.0%, respectively, with their closest known relatives based on polyprotein sequences. Additionally, one known virus, Encephalomyocarditis virus, was identified within the Cardiovirus genus, demonstrating a 97.9% aa identity. In the Limnipivirus genus, a novel rat Limnipivirus with four strains was discovered, sharing only 29.3–33.0% aa identities with its closest known relatives in the polyprotein region (1995–2438 aa). Our four strains of Hunnivirus A exhibited aa identities ranging from 92.6 to 94.5% compared to previously reported strains in the polyprotein gene, while two strains of Lophuromys chrysopus hunnivirus shared 65.6 to 72.7% aa identity with their closest known relatives.

Within the Astroviridae family, four viruses were categorized under the genus Mamastrovirus (including two novel viruses and two known viruses), while two were grouped as unclassified astroviruses. All five rodent-derived viruses formed distinct clusters with their respective known relatives from rodents, whereas Miniopterus inflatus bastrovirus clustered with Bastrovirus/VietNam/Bat/16715_78 found in bats in Vietnam, showing an aa identity of 55.1% based on nonstructural polyprotein analysis. These findings suggest virus-host specificity within the Astroviridae family.

Within the Flaviviridae family, three rodent-borne viruses were identified and clustered with other species in the genera Pestivirus (one novel virus) and Hepacivirus (one novel virus and one known virus). These viruses possess both the complete Pegivirus_RdRp domain and Hepacivirus_RdRp domain, respectively. The known rodent Hepacivirus from R. rattus and L. chrysopus, were separately grouped into two clusters and exhibited aa identities ranging from 92.9 to 98.2% compared to other previously reported strains.

Within the Hepeviridae family, a single known virus (Rocahepevirus sp.) with five strains was identified within the genus Rocahepevirus. These strains exhibited aa identities ranging from 92.5 to 93.4% with a previously reported human strain from France in 2022, specifically in the nonstructural polyprotein gene region (1458–1631 aa), suggesting a subtype of rodent hepatitis E virus. Additionally, one novel virus was discovered within the genus Rocahepevirus, comprising two strains originating from Lophuromys sikapusi and M. erythroleucus, respectively. These viruses shared only 53.1 to 53.2% aa identities with other associated viruses.

Within the Arteriviridae family, three novel viruses belonging to the genus Muarterivirus were identified in Crocidura grandiceps and Lemniscomys striatus. All three viruses possessed the complete ORF1b structure (1280–1361 aa), which included the catalytic core domain of RdRp characteristic of Arteriviruses, with aa identities ranging from 52.9 to 54.2% compared to other associated viruses.

Within the Nodaviridae family, two novel viruses were identified within the genus Alphanodavirus originating from M. inflatus. Only the full structure of RNA1 (including the core domain of RdRp) was analyzed for bat alphanodavirus 1 and bat alphanodavirus 2, showing < 50% aa identity with their closest known relatives.

Within the Coronaviridae family, a single known virus was identified from H. jonesi, exhibiting 89.9% aa identity with a previously reported bat strain isolated in China based on the complete RdRp gene (949 aa). Notably, this strain of Betacoronavirus sp. clusters closely with severe acute respiratory syndrome-related coronaviruses, indicating a high risk for zoonotic transmission to human.

Cross-species transmission of viruses

Based on an integration of current data and virus records from the NCBI database, we have identified 15 viruses that exhibit cross-species transmission among different animal species. Zoonotic potential of viruses

The dataset for predicting zoonotic potential included representatives from nine viral families and unassigned viruses (Supplementary Table 8). Overall, 5.56% (2/36) of viruses were identified as having very high zoonotic potential, 11.11% (4/36) having high zoonotic potential, 47.22% (17/36) having medium zoonotic potential, and 36.11% (13/36) having low zoonotic potential (Fig. 6D). Among 10 known viruses, rodent Bastrovirus and Meliandou mastomys virus were predicted to have very high zoonotic potential (Fig. 6D). Among 26 novel viruses, four viruses (Bat ledantevirus 2, Rattus rattus jeilongvirus, Miniopterus inflatus ribovirus, and Rat mamastrovirus) were predicted to have high zoonotic potential. Among them, Bat ledantevirus 2 had the highest zoonotic potential among them, indicating its high priority for further virological research.

Detection of antibodies against a novel rhabdovirus from patients

Bat ledantevirus 2, a previously unknown rhabdovirus which predicted to have very high zoonotic potential, was identified from bat and encoded the complete N protein (426 aa), P protein (297 aa), M protein (209 aa), G protein (552 aa), and L protein (2120 aa) (Fig. 7A). Owing to its high zoonotic potential, novelty, and phylogenetic relatedness to known human-infecting virus Le Dantec virus (LDV), an epidemiological investigation for Bat ledantevirus 2 was conducted among local residents. Due to limited experimental conditions in Sierra Leone to perform virus isolation, we generated single-round infectious virus particle (SRIP) based on the G protein gene sequence of Bat ledantevirus 2 utilizing a fluorescence-based VSV system pseudovirus to evaluate its potential human infection. The plasmid containing the G gene of Bat ledantevirus 2 was transfected into 293 T cells, followed by infection with VSV carrying the green florescent protein (GFP) reporter gene but lacking the G gene, resulting in the preparation of single-cycle VSVΔG-LED-GFP pseudovirus [28] (Fig. 7B and C). The specificity of the Bat ledantevirus 2 VSV PBNA was assessed by testing a panel of 28 human sera from healthy individuals. An initial dilution of 1:3, followed by threefold serial dilutions, was used to determine the limit of detection (LOD). The LOD was calculated as the mean titer of negative samples plus 2.58 standard deviations (SD), resulting in a value of 23.6. A cutoff value of 30 was established for human serum samples [31, 32] (Fig. 7D), achieving 99% specificity for negative human sera. This higher threshold was chosen to ensure an extremely low false-positive rate in seroprevalence studies [31, 32]. Subsequently, a linear relationship was determined by establishing a direct correlation between signal and concentration within a specific range. For positive samples, log-transformed dilution was compared with inhibition rate, revealing typical four-parameter inhibition curves (Fig. 7E). Discussion

Human activities in close proximity to wildlife habitats have created a human-animal interface that facilitates the emergence of infectious disease in humans. Sierra Leone, with its diverse mammalian population, is recognized a zoonotic “hotspot” for surveillance. Previous studies have predominantly utilized conventional PCR methods to detect emerging zoonotic viruses, such as the Bombali virus, a novel Ebola virus in molossid bats [33]; Angola-like Marburg virus in Egyptian rousette bats [34]; Sosuga virus, a rare human pathogenic paramyxovirus from Egyptian rousette bat [35]; Magboi virus in Nycteris hispida collected from Magboi River within Gola National Park [36]; and Lassa fever from Mastomys natalensis [37, 38].

In this study, we characterize the virome associated with small mammals, including bats, rodents, and shrews from Sierra Leone using an unbiased meta-transcriptomics approach. Notably, we discovered a novel viral species belonging to the Rhabdoviridae family, Bat ledantevirus 2, which may pose a potential threat to human health based on its phylogenetic relatedness to known pathogens and seropositive results among local residents. This finding corroborated the critical role of active surveillance at the human-animal interface, enabling early detection of cross-species transmission prior to spillover into human populations.

Rhabdoviruses, negative-sense RNA viruses, exhibit a broad host range encompassing humans, vertebrate, invertebrates, and plants [39]. Despite their global presence and diverse host range, only four genera (Lyssavirus, Vesiculovirus, Tibrovirus, and Ledantevirus) have been identified as causing human diseases. The first Ledantavirus, LDV, was isolated in 1965 from a 10-year-old girl with fever and hepatosplenomegaly in Dakar, Senegal [40]. Recently, Rhinolophus rhabdovirus DPuer (DPRV), a bat-associated ledantevirus capable of replicating in vitro in mammalian cell lines and infecting newborn mice with potential transmission to humans, has been identified in China [41]. The current study identifies a potentially pathogenic novel species within the genus Ledantevirus in bats, provisionally designated as Bat ledantevirus 2. A fluorescent pseudovirus neutralization assay was established, revealing a seroprevalence of 2.8% against Bat ledantevirus 2 in Sierra Leone. This finding indicates a potential risk of human infection by Bat ledantevirus 2.

The first identification of 12 viruses belonging to 6 families (Astroviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Picornaviridae, Coronaviridae, Flaviviridae, and Hepeviridae) and 1 unclassified Riboviria in Sierra Leone is also noteworthy. With the exception of Encephalomyocarditis virus and Hunnivirus A [42, 43], all other identified in viruses emerged as threats in the twenty-first century [44–48]. Among these12 viruses, 4 were originally discovered in Guinea in 2018: melian virus, Maser virus, Meliandou mastomys virus, and Meleucus virus [49]. The identification of eight viruses in Sierra Leone represent their first report on the African continent, with four demonstrating cross-species transmission capacities. Further genetic and evolutionary analysis revealed that two viruses, Rat rocahepevirus and Rat mamastrovirus, are closely related to human pathogenic viruses within their respective genera. Rat rocahepevirus, belonging to the Rocahepevirus genus of the Hepeviridae family, is closely related to Rocahepevirus ratti, an emerging pathogen associated with acute hepatitis and distributed globally [50]. Consistent with previous study [51], we also determined rodents as hosts of a large diversity of other hepeviruses that grouped distantly to human-associated hepeviruses, in contrast to bat- and bird-associated hepeviruses. Rat mamastrovirus, a member of the Mamastrovirus genus of the Astroviridae family, shares genetic similarities with the distinct strain (VA1) of human astrovirus (HAstV), which was identified during an outbreak of acute gastroenteritis in 2009 [47] and has been implicated in fatal central nervous system disease [52, 53]. These findings emphasize the need for enhanced surveillance measures targeting these high-risk viruses with potential human pathogenicity among the wild small animals.

The virome data obtained from rodents, bats, and shrews were utilized to validate known hosts and identify novel hosts for viruses. Among the 13 viral species identified in this study, 8 were discovered in previously unexplored mammal species (including 5 rodent species and 1 bat species), specifically melian virus, Hipposideros armiger coronavirus, Hunnivirus A, meleucus virus, Murine astrovirus, Rocahepevirus sp., rodent Bastrovirus, and Rodent hepacivirus. It is worth noting that melian virus, which was previously only documented in shrews (C. grandiceps), has now been detected in rodent (R. rattus), indicating an expanded host range and a potential increased risk of spillover transmission. This finding suggests viral cross-order host shifts between sympatric animals. The remaining five viruses were identified from their previously established hosts as reported in earlier studies [37, 38, 49, 54, 55].

Only a limited number of viral diversity studies have been conducted in Africa, including Ghana [56], Nigeria [57], Guinea [58], Kenya, and Uganda [59]. These studies primarily focused on bats and rodents with specific emphasis on species such as Eidolon helvum, H. armiger, Miniopterus australis, Mops condylurus, Nycteris macrotis, Nanonycteris veldkampii, Micropteropus pusillus, M. natalensis, etc. Our study represents an initial high-throughput sequencing survey examining the virome associated with small mammal-specifically rodents, shrews, and bats — in Sierra Leone. Through a comparative analysis on the virome among three types of wild small mammals, we disclose that rodents harbor a significantly higher number of both novel and known viruses compared to bats and shrews. Furthermore, the viruses identified in rodents exhibit a greater potential for cross-species transmission, likely attributable to their extensive geographical distribution, large population sizes, and high population densities in Sierra Leone. To date, only a limited number of studies have explored the comparative virome of wild small mammals [3, 24]. For instance, Wu et al. conducted an extensive investigation into viral diversity among 3055 rodents and other small mammals in China, suggesting that host taxonomy plays a primary role, while geographical location exerts a secondary influence on viral diversity [3]. Chen et al. utilized meta-transcriptomic sequencing of internal organ and fecal samples from 2443 wild bats, rodents, and shrews in China, revealing that shrews harbored a high richness of viruses, whereas rodents carried viruses with a greater capacity for host jumping [24]. Here, by performing a viral metagenomics approach on wild small animals of Sierra Leone, we also found that the taxonomy of the animal host, especially rodents, plays a crucial role in shaping the variety of viruses. Our results also provide significant updates for the African, a “geographic hotspot” for zoonosis. Shifting surveillance toward rodents—especially in anthropized landscapes—will optimize early-warning systems for zoonotic spillover. Future efforts should combine metagenomics, serology, and ecological modeling to mitigate risks in this high-priority region.

Our study has certain limitations that need acknowledgement. First, the samples were collected in a cross-sectional manner during a single season. Given the influence of environmental and meteorological factors, this approach might have led to the oversight of low-abundance or high-risk zoonotic viruses. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal surveillance of wild small mammals and patients, which could be achieved through nucleic acid and antibody analyses. Second, although our pooling sampling design may be sufficient for assessing aggregate animal viromes, it could affect the detection of detecting recombination events, viral co-infection, and result in biased estimates of individual animal virome diversity at the population level. Third, to comprehensively assess the pathogenic potential of viruses with high zoonotic potential, viral isolation and in vitro infection assays are essential. These techniques can effectively evaluate the replication capacity and cellular tropism of novel viruses. Moreover, animal model experiments can further aid in the assessment of virulence and transmission potential. Fourth, when analyzing cross-species transmission, the host information for regarding novel viruses is sparser compared to that for known viruses due to limited research on novel pathogens. Subsequent epidemiological investigations of these novel viruses across a broader range of host species are needed to more accurately determine their spillover risks. Regarding Bat ledantevirus 2, to further clarify its spillover dynamics, conducting targeted sero-surveys in bat colonies (particularly Miniopterus spp.) and sympatric small mammals within Sierra Leone is a top priority for future research. Meanwhile, serology studies of other identified high-risk viruses (such as Encephalomyocarditis virus, Rocahepevirus sp., Lassa virus, melian virus, Rodent hepacivirus, and Hunnivirus A) should be emphasized as a crucial direction for future research endeavors. These strategies will offer a more robust and comprehensive assessment of the public health implications associated with the newly identified viruses. Despite these limitations, these findings significantly contribute to understanding wild animal-borne viruses in Sierra Leone, highlighting the diversity of mammalian viruses in local habitats and their potential to emerge in new hosts. This provides a scientific foundation for mitigating zoonotic pathogen risks. Continuous surveillance of wild animal populations is crucial for early detection of novel infectious diseases.
