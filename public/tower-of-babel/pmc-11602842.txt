RNF38 promotes gilteritinib resistance in acute myeloid leukemia via inducing autophagy by regulating ubiquitination of LMX1A
Authors: Yiyun Pan, Wen Zeng, Ting Liang, Xiaoming Nie, Kang Liu, Hailong Chen, Nengping Luo, Xiaodan Zhu, Keqiang Tian, Yijian Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGilteritinib is a commonly used targeted drug for acute myeloid leukemia (AML), but the emergence of gilteritinib resistance greatly reduces the therapeutic effect. RING finger protein 38 (RNF38), a protein with RING Finger domain and E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, has been implicated in tumorigenesis and drug resistance. However, the role and mechanism of RNF38 in the gilteritinib resistance of AML remains unclear.MethodsNormal AML cells were treated with gilteritinib to construct gilteritinib-resistant cells (MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib). CCK8 assay, TUNEL staining and EdU assay were used to assess gilteritinib resistance, cell apoptosis and proliferation. The protein levels of autophagy-related markers, RNF38 and LIM homeobox transcription factor 1 alpha (LMX1A) were determined by western blot. Also, RNF38 and LMX1A mRNA levels were tested using qRT-PCR. Autophagic flux was assessed using mRFP-GFP-LC3 labeling, and autophagosome numbers was counted under transmission electron microscopy. Co-IP assay was employed to analyze interaction between RNF38 and LMX1A. The effects of LMX1A and RNF38 on AML tumorigenesis were analyzed by in vivo experiments.ResultsIn gilteritinib-resistant AML cells, autophagy-related markers, mRFP-GFP-LC3 signals and autophagosome numbers were significantly enhanced. Autophagy inhibitor 3-MA could suppress gilteritinib resistance in AML cells. RNF38 knockdown inhibited gilteritinib resistance and autophagy in AML cells. Mechanistically, RNF38 reduced LMX1A expression by inducing its ubiquitination. RNF38 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of LMX1A on gilteritinib resistance and autophagy in AML cells, as well as AML tumor growth in vivo, while these effects could be abolished by proteasome inhibitor MG132.ConclusionsRNF38 induced autophagy to promote gilteritinib resistance in AML by increasing the ubiquitination of LMX1A.Graphical Abstract1. Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological heterogeneous disease originating from the bone marrow, which belongs to the malignant tumor of the blood system (Pelcovits and Niroula 2020; Yang and Wang 2018). The clinical manifestations of AML are anemia, bleeding and infection, which can be life-threatening in severe cases (Adimora and Rojas Hernandez 2022; Shimony et al. 2023). The most common mutation of Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) is internal tandem duplication (ITD), and it is closely related to AML development (Burchert 2021). Currently, FLT3 inhibitors are approved for treating FLT3-mutated AML (Daver et al. 2019; Smith 2020). Gilteritinib, a FLT3 tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has shown promising efficacy in AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutation (Zhao et al. 2019, 2022). However, gilteritinib resistance is an important cause of treatment failure in AML (Eguchi et al. 2020; Scholl et al. 2020). Therefore, it is of great significance to explore the potential molecular mechanism of gilteritinib resistance to improve the therapeutic effect of AML.

Ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), the main pathway of protein degradation, is closely related to human disease development (Luza et al. 2020; Park et al. 2020). RING finger proteins (RNFs), belong to the zinc finger protein family, have E3 ubiquitin ligases (E3s) activity and participate in the ubiquitination and degradation process of specific substrates (Ardley 2009; Cai et al. 2022). Previous studies have confirmed that RNFs are involved in the malignant behavior and drug resistance of tumors, including AML. For example, RNF220 promoted leukemia cell proliferation by stabilizing Cyclin D1 (Pan et al. 2021), and RNF6 accelerated retinoblastoma drug resistance through JAK2/STAT3 pathway (Chai et al. 2022). As a member of the RNF family, RNF38 has been proved to be widely involved in regulating tumor malignant process and is associated with chemoresistance (Hu et al. 2020a, 2021). For example, RNF38 was upregulated in gastric cancer and could facilitate cell growth via inducing the polyubiquitination of SHP-1 (Zhang et al. 2018a). Importantly, RNF38 promoted 5-Fluorouracil resistance in colorectal cancer (Long et al. 2021), and enhanced cisplatin resistance in lung cancer (Wu et al. 2021). However, the expression of RNF38 in AML and whether RNF38 mediates gilteritinib resistance are unknown.

LIM homeobox transcription factor 1 alpha (LMX1A) is a member of LIM homeobox gene family, and its abnormal expression is associated with tumor development (Feng et al. 2020; Li et al. 2020). LMX1A could suppress gastric cancer cell invasion and migration (Feng et al. 2016), as well as inhibit ovarian cancer cell growth and metastasis (Chao et al. 2013). Thus, LMX1A may mediate tumorigenesis as a tumor suppressor. However, its role in AML progression has not been reported. In our study, LMX1A was identified as a potential ubiquitination substrate for RNF38 by UbiBrowser bioinformatics prediction. However, whether RNF38 regulates AML gilteritinib resistance by the ubiquitination of LMX1A is unknown.

This study aimed to reveal RNF38 roles and mechanisms in gilteritinib resistance of AML. Based on these results, we hypothesized that RNF38 promoted gilteritinib resistance in AML by regulating LMX1A ubiquitination and degradation.

Methods

Cell culture and gilteritinib-resistant cell construction

AML cells (MV4-11 and MOLM-13; Biovector, Beijing, China) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium plus 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA). MV4-11 and MOLM-13 cells were exposed to gradually increasing concentrations of gilteritinib (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NI, USA) for 3 months to construct gilteritinib-resistant cells (MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib).

Cell transfection and treatment

Cells were treated with gilteritinib (0 or 100 nM), autophagy inhibitor 3-MA (5 nM; MedChemExpress) or equal volume of PBS for 24 h, and then collected for functional analysis. For transfection, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were transfected with the siRNA against RNF38 (si-RNF38), pcDNA LMX1A/RNF38 overexpression vector (pc-LMX1A/RNF38) and negative controls (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) using Advanced Transfection Reagent (Zeta Life, Menlo Park, CA, USA). After that, transfected cells were treated with or without proteasome inhibitor MG132 (2 μM, MedChemExpress) or equal volume of PBS for 24 h.

CCK8 assay

Cells were treated with various concentrations of gilteritinib (0, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 nM) for 48 h in 96-well plates. Afterwards, cells were treated with CCK8 reagent, and cell viability was detected using a microplate reader to assess the gilteritinib resistance of cells.

TUNEL staining

Cells were collected and fixed by paraformaldehyde. After penetrated in Triton X-100, cells were incubated with TUNEL solution and DAPI solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cell smears were prepared and the signals were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot (WB)

After extracted by RIPA buffer (Beyotime), protein was separated by SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After incubated with anti-Beclin-1 (1:2000, ab207612, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-LC3 (1:2000, ab192890), anti-RNF38 (1:500, ab121487), anti-LMX1A (1:500, ab139726), anti-GAPDH (1:2500, ab9485), and secondary antibody (ab205718, 1:50,000, Abcam), protein signals were exposed by ECL reagent (Beyotime).

Autophagic flux detection

Cells were transfected with mRFP-GFP-LC3-expressing adenovirus (HanBio, Shanghai, China) and then stained with DAPI in the confocal dish. Fluorescence signals were analyzed by confocal microscope (Nikon A1 Ti) and autophagic flux was assessed by measuring GFP/mRFP punctate signals.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were fixed with glutaraldehyde and osmium tetroxide. After dehydrated, permeabilized and fixed with SPI-Pon 812, cells were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate in embedded plates. Autophagosome was imaged and counted by JEM-1400-Flash TEM (Japan).

qRT-PCR

After extracted by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), RNAs were reverse-transcribed by cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). SYBR Green (Takara) was mixed with cDNA and specific primers (Table 1) for PCR amplification. Relative RNF38 and LMX1A mRNA levels were analyzed using 2−ΔΔCt method.

EdU assay

Cells in 6 well plates were cultured with 50 μM EdU (RiboBio) for 2 h. Then, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained by Apollo solution. EdU positive cell rate was analyzed under flow cytometer.

Co-IP analyses

UbiBrowser 2.0 software (http://ubibrowser.bio-it.cn/ubibrowser_v3/home/index) was used to predict the ubiquitination substrate of RNF38. Cell lysates were treated with anti-RNF38, anti-LMX1A, or anti-IgG overnight followed by incubated with protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Beyotime). Then, protein was eluted for detecting RNF38 and LMX1A levels using WB analysis. Besides, anti-ubiquitin (Ub) was used to examine the ubiquitination levels of LMX1A.

Cycloheximide (CHX) assay

MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells transfected with si-NC/si-RNF38 were treated with protein synthesis inhibitor 100 mM CHX (MedChemExpress) for 0, 1, 3, and 6 h, and then LMX1A protein level was determined by WB.

Mice transplanted tumor models

BALB/c nude mice (Vital River, Beijing, China) were divided into 6 groups: LV-pc-NC group, LV-pc-NC + Gilteritinib group, LV-pc-RNF38 + Gilteritinib group, LV-pc-RNF38 + LV-pc-LMX1A + Gilteritinib group, LV-pc-RNF38 + Gilteritinib + PBS group and LV-pc-RNF38 + Gilteritinib + MG132 group. Briefly, mice were subcutaneously injected with MV4-11 cells transfected with lentiviral vector LV-pc-NC/LV-pc-RNF38/LV-pc-LMX1A (Ribobio), followed by intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg Gilteritinib, 2 mg/kg MG132 or equal volume of PBS for 3 times per week. After 28 days, mice were injected with 150 mg/kg VivoGlo Luciferin in vivo Grade solution (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) intraperitoneally. Bioluminescence signals were visualized by in vivo imaging system. Then, mice were euthanatized and tumor samples were collected and weighted. In addition, tumor samples were prepared the paraffin sections for Ki-67 immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Suzhou Medical College of Soochow University.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD using the GraphPad 7.0 software. Comparison between groups was analyzed by Student’s t-test or ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of gilteritinib-resistant AML cells and assessed autophagy

Firstly, we evaluated the phenotype of established gilteritinib-resistant AML cells. As shown in Fig. 1A, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells could resistant to gilteritinib compared with parental cells. The observing cell morphology results showed that parental cells were almost killed after 100 nM gilteritinib induction, while gilteritinib-resistant AML cells presented normal morphology (Fig. 1B). Gilteritinib treatment induced apoptosis in parental cells, whereas MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were resistant to gilteritinib-induced apoptosis (Fig. 1C). Beclin-1 level and LC3-II/LC3-I ratio were increased in MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells, indicating the enhanced basal autophagic activity in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells (Fig. 1D). Gilteritinib treatment further increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and Beclin-1 level in MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells, showing that the autophagic activity of gilteritinib-resistant cells could be further enhanced by gilteritinib (Fig. 1E). In addition, mRFP-GFP-LC3 signal at autolysosomes was promoted in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells, and this effect was further increased by gilteritinib induction (Fig. 1F). Under TEM, we observed a significant high autophagosome numbers in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells, and gilteritinib treatment further increased autophagosome numbers (Fig. 1G). The results confirmed that the gilteritinib-resistant AML cells were successfully constructed and their autophagy activity was significantly enhanced.

Inhibition of autophagy suppressed gilteritinib resistance in AML cells

To reveal the effect of autophagy on gilteritinib resistance in AML cells, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were treated with autophagy inhibitor 3-MA. After 3-MA treatment, LC3-II/LC3-I, Beclin-1 and autophagosome numbers were remarkably decreased (Fig. 2A-B), indicating that autophagy was indeed inhibited by 3-MA in AML cells. Further analysis showed that 3-MA treatment significantly reduced gilteritinib resistance and increased the apoptosis of cells (Fig. 2C-D). The results confirmed that autophagy inhibition could reduce the gilteritinib resistance of AML cells.

RNF38 knockdown inhibited AML cell autophagy and gilteritinib resistance

Compared to parental cells, RNF38 mRNA and protein levels were markedly increased in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells (Fig. 3A-B), suggesting that RNF38 was related to gilteritinib resistance in AML cells. To further confirm this hypothesis, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were transfected with si-RNF38 to reduce RNF38 protein expression (Fig. 3C). Knockdown of RNF38 significantly reduced gilteritinib resistance, inhibited EdU positive cell rate, and promoted cell apoptosis rate (Fig. 3D-F). In addition, RNF38 silencing also decreased LC3-II/LC3-I, Beclin-1 and autophagosome numbers (Fig. 3G-H). Above data confirmed that RNF38 might promote autophagy and gilteritinib resistance in AML cells.

RNF38 inhibited LMX1A expression by promoting its ubiquitination

LMX1A was downregulated in MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4A-B). Furthermore, we found that RNF38 knockdown did not affect LMX1A mRNA expression, but significantly increased LMX1A protein level in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells (Fig. 4C-D). Moreover, Co-IP assay confirmed the interaction between RNF38 and LMX1A using LMX1A and RNF38 antibodies, respectively (Fig. 4E). UbiBrowser 2.0 software predicted that LMX1A was a potential ubiquitination substrate for RNF38 (Fig. 4F). Besides, CHX assay suggested that RNF38 knockdown enhanced the stability of LMX1A protein (Fig. 4G). In addition, overexpression of RNF38 promoted endogenous LMX1A ubiquitination (Fig. 4H). These results revealed that RNF38 suppressed LMX1A protein expression by accelerating its ubiquitination and degradation.

RNF38 mediated AML autophagy and gilteritinib resistance by downregulating LMX1A

To explore the role of LMX1A in AML autophagy and gilteritinib resistance and whether RNF38 played a regulatory role by mediating LMX1A expression, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were transfected pc-LMX1A and pc-RNF38. The detection of LMX1A protein level confirmed that pc-LMX1A significantly enhanced LMX1A expression, and this effect was abolished by pc-RNF38 (Fig. 5A). LMX1A overexpression inhibited gilteritinib resistance, EdU positive cell rate, and enhanced apoptosis rate, while pc-RNF38 reversed these effects (Fig. 5B-D). Moreover, LMX1A overexpression decreased LC3-II/LC3-I, Beclin-1 and autophagosome numbers, while RNF38 overexpression also abolished the above effects (Fig. 5E-F). These data showed that LMX1A inhibited autophagy and gilteritinib resistance in AML cells, and RNF38 reduced LMX1A protein expression to regulate AML cell autophagy and gilteritinib resistance. To further confirm our conclusion, we performed animal experiments. Gilteritinib treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and weight, and RNF38 overexpression enhanced tumor growth by promoting gilteritinib resistance, while this effect was partially reversed by LMX1A overexpression (Fig. 5G-I). Further in vivo imaging results supported these findings (Fig. 5J). Meanwhile, gilteritinib treatment decreased Ki-67 positive cell rate, LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1, while this effect was abolished by RNF38 overexpression and further partially counteracted by LMX1A overexpression (Fig. 5K-L). All data confirmed that RNF38 promoted autophagy and gilteritinib resistance of AML tumors through LMX1A in vivo.

Inhibition of LMX1A ubiquitination reversed the regulation of RNF38 on AML autophagy and gilteritinib resistance

To further confirm that RNF38 regulated LMX1A ubiquitination to mediate AML autophagy and gilteritinib resistance, MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells were co-transfected with pc-LMX1A and pc-RNF38 and treated with proteasome inhibitor MG132. Our data showed that MG132 treatment markedly enhanced LMX1A protein expression in cells co-transfected with pc-LMX1A and pc-RNF38 (Fig. 6A). Further analysis suggested that MG132 treatment suppressed gilteritinib resistance, EdU positive cell rate, LC3-II/LC3-I, Beclin-1 and autophagosome numbers, while promoted apoptosis rate in cells co-transfected with pc-LMX1A and pc-RNF38 (Fig. 6B-F). Animal experiments confirmed that tumor volume and weight were declined in LV-pc-RNF38 + Gilteritinib + MG132 group, and this phenomenon was observed in vivo imaging (Fig. 6G-J). Ki-67 positive cell rate, LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1 also were reduced in the tumor tissues of LV-pc-RNF38 + Gilteritinib + MG132 group (Fig. 6K-L). These results suggested that RNF38 promoted LMX1A ubiquitination degradation to facilitate AML autophagy and gilteritinib resistance (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Autophagy, a conserved intracellular degradation system, is a physiological mechanism for cell survival (Glick et al. 2010; Onorati et al. 2018). In tumors, the activation of autophagy can effectively avoid cell death and induce drug resistance, and blocking autophagy can improve the efficiency of anti-tumor therapy (Hu et al. 2020b; Zamame Ramirez et al. 2021). It is reported that autophagy promotes cell proliferation by enhancing chemoresistance in liver cancer (Vempati and Malla 2020). Studies have shown that FLT3 inhibitors can induce the autophagy process of AML cells, and the combination of FLT3 and autophagy inhibitors can synergistically inhibit AML development and prolong the survival rate of AML patients (Koschade et al. 2022). Therefore, it is feasible to explore the mechanism of FLT3 inhibitor resistance in AML based on autophagy. In this, we generated gilteritinib-resistant AML cells and evaluated that their gilteritinib resistance and autophagy were indeed higher than normal cells. In addition, the gilteritinib resistance of MV4-11/Gilteritinib and MOLM-13/Gilteritinib cells was significantly reduced after treatment with autophagy inhibitor 3-MA. These data revealing that autophagy was indeed involved in gilteritinib resistance in AML cells, and inhibiting autophagy might be an effective measure for the treatment of FLT3 inhibitor resistance in AML patients.

UPS is an important regulatory mode of many life processes in cells, which is closely related to human disease development (Qiu et al. 2022). The abnormal changes of UPS are related to the occurrence of tumors, so it may be an important potential target for inhibiting tumorigenesis (Park et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023). As a member of E3s, RNF38 has been confirmed to be a tumor promoter in hepatocellular carcinoma (Hu et al. 2021) and breast cancer (Yang et al. 2021). Moreover, the high expression of RNF38 is confirmed to be related to drug resistance (Long et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021). In this, we detected a high RNF38 expression in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells. Consistent with the reported in previous studies (Long et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021), our study also found the promoting effect of RNF38 on drug resistance. This results showed that after RNF38 knockdown, the decreased gilteritinib resistance, reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis, and restrained autophagy abilities were detected in AML cells. Additionally, overexpression of RNF38 inhibited tumor sensitivity to gilteritinib and promoted AML tumorigenesis in vivo. These results confirm that RNF38 may accelerate FLT3 inhibitor resistance in AML by enhancing autophagy.

RNF38 has been demonstrated to mediate disease progression by regulating substrate ubiquitination. RNF38 could induce SHP-1 polyubiquitination to mediate gastric cancer progression (Zhang et al. 2018a). Besides, RNF38 ubiquitinated and degraded AHNAK, thereby promoting hepatocellular carcinoma cell metastasis and EMT (Peng et al. 2019). Here, we discovered that RNF38 reduced LMX1A protein expression by ubiquitination. In recent years, the tumor suppressor role of LMX1A has been gradually revealed. In gastric cancer, LMX1A knockdown had a promotion effect on cancer cell proliferation (Zhang et al. 2018b), and it could exert its anti-tumor role by inhibiting oncogene c-Myc (Qian et al. 2019). Downregulation of LMX1A accelerated cervical cancer cell proliferation and metastasis (Ke et al. 2021). Importantly, LMX1A is considered to be an autophagy transcription factor (Jimenez-Moreno et al. 2023; Jimenez-Moreno and Lane 2023). Although the role of LMX1A has been largely demonstrated, its function in AML progression remains to be investigated. Here, we confirmed that LMX1A was lowly expressed in gilteritinib-resistant AML cells, and its high expression inhibited AML cell gilteritinib resistance, promoted apoptosis, suppressed autophagy-related protein levels, and reduced autophagosome numbers. This indicated that LMX1A inhibited FLT3 inhibitor resistance and autophagy in AML. Additionally, RNF38 overexpression reduced LMX1A protein level and reversed LMX1A-mediated the inhibition on gilteritinib resistance and autophagy, while proteasome inhibitor MG132 could eliminate these results. Furthermore, LMX1A overexpression reversed the promotion of gilteritinib resistance by RNF38 in vivo, and this effect also could be abolished by MG132. These evidences suggested that RNF38 indeed promoted FLT3 inhibitor resistance and autophagy by facilitating LMX1A ubiquitination.

Conclusions

Collectively, our study revealed that RNF38 accelerated gilteritinib resistance in AML via enhancing autophagy by promoting LMX1A ubiquitination. These results have important clinical implications by providing new potential targets and therapeutic strategies to overcome FLT3 inhibitor resistance in AML.
