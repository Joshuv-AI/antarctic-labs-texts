Positioning accuracy and dosimetric evaluation of customized immobilization for FBCT guided VMAT in nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Xuemei Chen, Xiaotong Huang, Yajuan Wang, Wenchao Diao, Lecheng Jia, Hua Li, Dehuan Xie, Aoqiang Chen, Xiaobo Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to expand and enhance traditional fixation techniques by comparing positioning errors and dosimetric variations in the target area and organs at risk (OARs) between conventional and customized immobilization strategies during radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). Additionally, we evaluate the benefits and necessity of continuous improvements in immobilization devices. Specify beam technique (VMAT) and sample size (n = 30; 15 per group): the traditional group (standard styrofoam, a thermoplastic mask (TM) with a universal oral stent (OS)) and the customized group (modified styrofoam, TM with a self-developed customized OS). Using diagnostic fan-beam computed tomography (FBCT) scans, we quantitatively assessed positioning errors in the overall target area as well as the oropharynx and neck regions with the two fixation methods. Additionally, we evaluated dosimetric changes in target areas and principal OARs during three stages of NPC radiotherapy (initial plan, week 4, and week 6) to ascertain dosimetric benefits. Compared to the traditional group, the customized group exhibited notably smaller positional errors throughout individual treatment sessions. The modifications implemented in the customized group had a more substantial impact and weight on reducing errors in the neck region. The customized group showed improved target dose coverage, such as the dose covering 95% of the target volume (D95), dose covering 98% of the target volume (D98) of primary planning gross tumor volume (PGTVp), the D95 and D98 of planning clinical target volume 1 (PCTV1), etc. Additionally, the customized group demonstrated a lower radiation dose to brainstem, temporomandibular joint, parotid-R, thyroid gland and other OARs. The customized immobilization strategy significantly enhances the reproducibility of patient positioning during NPC radiotherapy. A notable advantage of customized solutions may allow margin reduction and selective OAR sparing. Moreover, the “customized” clinical application should be utilized individually, taking into account multiple factors.Keywords: NPC radiotherapy, Customized, Immobilization, Dosimetric benefits, Positioning errorSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) is becoming increasingly important for the treatment of newly diagnosed nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). It involves dynamic adjustments of gantry velocity, MLC position, and dose rate during rotational irradiation, creating a highly conformal dose distribution that matches the tumor’s irregular contours1,2. Changes in body contour due to chemoradiotherapy and setup uncertainties can potentially lead to the exclusion of tumors or increased exposure to organs at risk (OARs)3,4. Therefore, maintaining patient posture stability is essential for optimal target dose coverage and local control rates while minimizing side effects. However, existing immobilization methods do not adequately balance “individualized precision” with “clinical practicality” for NPC patients.

Traditional NPC immobilization relies on standardized A-E headrests and thermoplastic masks (TM), but their non-customized design results in significant positioning errors (such as neck rotation and longitudinal sliding), which diminish treatment efficacy5. The commonly used endoscopic oral stent (OS) stabilizes the head pitch angle by fixing the maxilla and mandible during mouth opening6–8, yet it lacks dental fixation, resulting in poor reproducibility and discomfort. Although advancements such as 3D-printed devices have been investigated, they have shown a reduction in pitch rotation and improved positional reproducibility9,10. Nevertheless, the benefits of 3D-printed OS in enhancing setup precision and reducing oral irradiation dosage are constrained by high fabrication costs and complexity, hindering their widespread adoption11,12.

Notably, daily positioning uncertainties during NPC radiotherapy directly cause discrepancies between planned and actual organ positions, with well-documented impacts on dosimetry13,14. Aliotta et al.14 observed that the accuracy of OAR delineation is tied to setup reproducibility, which influences dosimetric variations in head and neck radiotherapy; Yan et al.15 added that while setup errors often relate to tumor or isocenter localization, factors like OAR-isocenter distance, neck rotation, and anatomical deformation contribute to residual errors, introducing OAR interfractional dose uncertainty. Kaur et al.3 further noted that such setup uncertainties, if left uncorrected, lead to a significant reduction in target volume coverage and a nonsignificant increase in OAR doses, potentially compromising tumor control and elevating normal tissue toxicity. These studies underscore the importance of addressing immobilization-related errors to optimize NPC radiotherapy outcomes, although current solutions remain inadequate.

This study aims to develop a practical immobilization strategy: building on conventional TM and universal OS, it integrates modified styrofoam (with crotch constraints for enhanced buttock stability) and a self-developed customized OS (avoiding the high cost of 3D printing). Study Hypothesis: Compared with traditional immobilization (standard TM and universal OS), the customized strategy (modified styrofoam and customized OS) will reduce positioning errors in the oropharynx and neck regions of NPC patients, and improve dosimetric outcomes (enhanced target coverage and reduced OAR irradiation) during mid- to late-treatment (weeks 4 and 6 of VMAT). To test this hypothesis, we quantitatively compared positioning errors between the two immobilization methods using pre-treatment fan-beam CT (FBCT) images, and analyzed dosimetric changes in targets and major OARs to validate the clinical value of the customized approach.

Methods

Patient selection

A total of thirty patients who underwent VMAT on the URT-linac506c linear accelerator at our institution between January 2023 and December 2023 were enrolled in this retrospective observational study.

Enrollment Process:

First, we identified all 126 newly diagnosed NPC patients who underwent VMAT on the URT-linac506c during the study period. Subsequently, we applied the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to screen these patients: 81 patients adopted an all-in-one online adaptive radiotherapy process, and only 45 patients met the initial inclusion criteria. Among them, 15 patients were excluded (8 due to incomplete FBCT data before treatment, 5 due to adjustments of the online/offline plan during treatment, and 2 due to a history of neck lymph node surgery). Finally, 30 patients were enrolled for the study to ensure the integrity and consistency of the data.

Enrollment Criteria:

(1) Pathologically confirmed primary NPC (World Health Organization [WHO] classification: Type I–IV; American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] 8th edition TNM stage: T1–4N0–3M0).

(2) Presence of radiologically and pathologically evaluable metastatic cervical lymph nodes (short-axis diameter ≥ 10 mm on contrast-enhanced CT).

(3) Age 18–70 years, with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–1.

(4) Completed full-course VMAT without any online/offline plan adjustments (to avoid dosimetric interference from plan modifications).

(5) Availability of complete pre-treatment FBCT data (≥ 5 sets of FBCT images per patient during treatment) for error analysis.

Exclusion Criteria:

(1) History of prior head and neck radiotherapy or neck lymph node dissection.

(2) Severe cervical spine deformity, temporomandibular joint disorder, or unable to wear OS.

(3) Severe systemic diseases (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, heart failure) or acute infections during treatment.

(4) Incomplete clinical data (e.g., missing pathology reports, FBCT images, or dosimetric records).

(5) Withdrawal of informed consent during treatment.

The group assignment was based on the treatment period and institutional immobilization protocol update: the traditional group (n = 15) included patients treated between January 2023 and April 2023, when our institution routinely used the traditional immobilization method (composed of styrofoam, TM with a universal OS); the customized group (n = 15) included patients treated between May 2023 and December 2023, after our institution updated the protocol to adopt the customized immobilization strategy (utilizing modified styrofoam, TM with a customized OS). To further confirm no selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics of the two groups. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups, presented in Table 1, indicating balanced baseline characteristics.

Fabrication of the Customized Group’s OS:

Materials: The self-developed customized OS was created from medical-grade ABS resin, featuring a fixed tooth occlusion groove and three securing holes for attachment to the TM (Fig. 1d).

Fabrication Steps:

(1) Pre-preparation: The patient’s oral cavity was cleaned to remove debris, ensuring no interference with impression accuracy. Additionally, the patient was briefed on the specific operation procedures and shown a 1-minute video demonstrating the fabrication process to enhance cooperation during molding.

(2) Fabrication: The resin mold was first soaked in a 70 °C constant-temperature water bath for 6 min to soften (Fig. 1e). Alternatively, soaking it in 100 °C boiling water only required 90 s to achieve softening. Notably, ABS resin material has low thermal conductivity, even after softening, it stays cool enough for safe patient occlusion with no discomfort. The patient was instructed to bite firmly into the pre-softened resin mold, which accurately formed a dentition model in approximately 10 s. The impression material was allowed to set via either natural cooling at room temperature (≈ 25 °C) for 2 min or rinsing with tap water for 40 s; upon completion of cooling, distinct tooth impressions were evident (Fig. 1g and h).

Sterilization: This medical-grade material property inherently eliminates the need for additional sterilization procedures after fabrication. The customized OS is rinsed with tap water after daily treatment, air-dried at room temperature, and stored in a clean, dry container for its subsequent use.

Per-device cost/time: The material cost for a single customized OS is relatively low, accounting for only 15% of the total expense for the customized group. The total time for the customized OS fabrication process was approximately within 10 min, and it can be performed concurrently with TM fabrication without additional time cost. During the study period, the remake rate was negligible (no remakes occurred in practice).

Customized Group: A lengthened styrofoam measuring 1.2 to 1.5 m was utilized to mold the patient’s body contour, incorporating constraints for the hands and crotch. The shaping process followed the same protocol as the traditional group (Fig. 1c). The patient engaged with the personalized OS by aligning it with the imprinted tooth marks before positioning the TM over the craniofacial region. Three strategically placed holes ensured a secure connection between the oropharynx and the TM (Fig. 1i). The ensuing cooling process adhered to the same protocols as that of the traditional group.

CT simulation and treatment planning

CT simulations were performed using a Philips 16-slice CT scanner (Philips, Netherlands), covering a region from 2 cm superior to the frontal sinuses to 2 cm inferior to the clavicle, with a slice thickness of 3 mm. For magnetic resonance (MR) simulation, a Philips Ingenia 3.0 T scanner was used, maintaining the same scanning range and slice thickness as the CT scans. Following completion of imaging, datasets were transferred to the treatment planning system (TPS, uRT-TPOIS R001, China). Radiation oncologists delineated the gross tumor volume (GTVp) of the primary site on MR images, performed image registration and fusion with CT images, and contoured additional target and OARs based on MR data. Physicists employed the volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) technique to establish the initial treatment plan, designated as PLANct.

Calculation of setup error and analysis of dose differences

The URT-linac 506 C linear accelerator, equipped with a diagnostic-quality helical CT imaging system featuring co-rotating axes and a treatment couch, underwent weekly FBCT scans throughout the treatment regimen to identify and rectify setup errors. Two processing approaches were employed for the captured FBCT images.

Setup error data acquisition

The first approach involved analyzing grayscale intensities and making manual adjustments to document overall target displacements and local displacements in the oropharynx and neck regions (Fig. 2), following the International Electrotechnical Commission 61,217 (IEC 61217) standards for spatial directions. In the supine position with the patient’s head advanced, the X-axis represented the left-right direction (LAT), the Y-axis denoted the head-foot direction (LNG), and the Z-axis indicated the anterior-posterior direction (VRT). Rotational angles were designated as pitch, roll, and yaw around the X, Y, and Z axes, respectively.

(a) The overall registration region; (b) the local oropharynx registration region; (c) the local neck registration region.

IGRT Process: Imaging acquisition (140 kVp, 120 mA, 3 mm slices) was executed per oncologist’s prescription, with a total of 5–7 fractions of FBCTs obtained throughout the entire treatment. Every registration process was conducted by two experienced senior radiation therapists following a standardized protocol. First, registration regions of interest (ROIs) were focused on overall target, and initial alignment was achieved through automatic bone registration with the planning CT as the reference. Then, manual adjustments were made to optimize GTVp contour alignment and consistency with segmented regions (oropharynx/neck), ensuring both local and overall target accuracy. Rotational corrections were not applied throughout the process. Translational errors exceeding 3 mm in any dimension required repositioning; sub-threshold deviations (≤ 3 mm) were deemed clinically acceptable and the couch corrections were directly applied before beam delivery.

A retrospective analysis was performed to quantify the 6D errors across the three registration modalities: oropharyngeal, neck, and overall registration. Anatomically, the nasopharynx (bony-stable) and the neck (soft-tissue deformable) exhibit marked stability differences, while the oropharynx naturally serves as the transitional region to avoid discontinuities from rigid to soft tissues. Dosimetry data acquisition

The second approach selected FBCT images from the 4th and 6th weeks of treatment. In the TPS, contours of the target and OARs were modified layer by layer while maintaining original plan parameters, such as beam incidence angles, subfield numbers, and segmental arrangements, with dose recalculation performed without further optimization to generate the corresponding simulated FBCT plans. Previous research has indicated that NPC patients undergo significant anatomical changes, leading to a decline in target dose coverage after 4 weeks, necessitating plan modifications at this time19,20. Consequently, we dedicated the fourth and sixth weeks for observational analysis, calculating percentage differences (Diff4 and Diff6) in dosimetric parameters relative to the initial plan using the formula: Diff = (FBCTs - PLANct) / PLANct.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS or Origin software. Prior to analysis, datasets were assessed for normality. For normally distributed data, independent t-tests were conducted, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test, and results are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR), adopting a significance level of p < 0.05 for statistical significance.

Analysis of setup errors between the customized and traditional groups

Interfractional setup errors

The traditional group comprised a total of 97 FBCT images, with a median of 7 FBCTs per patient (IQR: 1), while the customized group included 104 FBCT images, with a median of 7 FBCTs per patient (IQR: 0). The total number of fractions analyzed across both groups was 201 (refer to Table 3). For oropharyngeal local errors, the customized group demonstrated lower errors in all but the lateral and yaw directions. Notably, no statistical differences were observed in local neck region errors between the two groups for the lateral direction only. Rotational errors in the customized group consistently remained below 2° across fractions and directions. Conversely, the traditional group exhibited 18 instances where rotational errors surpassed 2°, with neck rotation accounting for 77.8% of these occurrences.

Composition error calculation

To compare errors between the two groups, we calculated composite errors for translational and rotational directions separately. To ensure consistent sample sizes between the two groups, a random subset of 7 datasets was first automatically excluded from the original 104 datasets in the customized group. This was implemented via SPSS’s “Select Cases” function with the “Random sample of cases” option, specifying the selection of 97 cases out of the 104. Subsequently, the oropharynx, neck, and overall composite errors were ranked in ascending order and contrasted. Analysis revealed that the customized group exhibited significantly smaller in translational and rotational directions (Figs. 3 and 4).

Notably, the neck region demonstrated the largest discrepancy in translational and rotational directions between the two groups and thus held greater weight in the overall error assessment. The customized group showed a greater reduction in neck region errors compared to oropharyngeal errors.

Exploring the relationship between neck errors and PCTV2 coverage

We found that the two fixation methods had a more pronounced impact on neck errors. We attempted to analyze the target areas PTV2 and errors in both groups, focusing on the proportion of fractions reaching the clinical threshold (≥ 54 Gy), which is directly related to therapeutic efficacy in NPC radiotherapy. Notably, we observed a substantial difference between the two groups: only 20% of fractions in the traditional group met this threshold, compared to 47% in the customized group.

In the traditional group of 30 FBCT images, the composite translational error exceeded 3 mm in 18 fractions. This finding was not observed in the customized group. Therefore, we conducted a correlation analysis between composite translational error and D95 of PTV2 in the traditional group. We observed a moderate negative trend (r=-0.378, p > 0.05).

Comparison of OAR dose distribution between the customized and traditional groups

Compared to the original plan, the 1% volume (D1%) of the brainstem in the traditional group received a higher irradiation dose (1.2% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.012). The temporal lobe-L03 in the customized group experienced a significant decrease in dose (− 0.7% vs. 0.5%, p = 0.007). Both groups conformed to clinical dose constraints for the left and right temporal lobes. However, the customized group showed improved outcomes for the temporal lobe-R03 (− 1.8% vs. − 0.8%, p = 0.033).

Moreover, the customized group exhibited a statistically significant decrease in the mean dose (Dmean) (− 0.9% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.001) and the max dose (Dmax) (− 2.2% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.001) for the temporomandibular joint-R (TMJ), reflecting a substantial reduction in radiation exposure relative to the original plan. Conversely, dose delivery increased for the traditional group.

For the parotid gland, both groups met clinical dose limits for Dmean. Specifically, in the customized group, volumes receiving 26 Gy (V26) and 30 Gy (V30) for the left parotid were significantly higher than those in the traditional group (5.0% vs. − 6.0%, p = 0.019) and (3.4% vs. − 11.6%, p = 0.042). Conversely, the right parotid’s Dmean in the customized group was significantly lower than in the traditional group (− 1.3% vs. 2.3%, p = 0.047).

Regarding the thyroid gland, the initial plan kept Dmean below the dose constraint of 45 Gy for both groups (44.3 Gy). However, during actual treatment, the traditional group received a dose of 45.9 Gy, while the customized group received 44.3 Gy. The difference between the two groups and the original plan was statistically significant (2.6% vs. 0.45%, p = 0.009).

Discussion

At our center, NPC patients undergo immobilization using TM combined with styrofoam adhesive for head, neck, and shoulder stabilization. Given the natural curvature of the neck, significant movement persists despite wrapping, resulting in positioning uncertainties that adversely affect target dose coverage and increase OAR irradiation. To enhance this, we extended the styrofoam and incorporated customized OSs. Utilizing the uRT-linac 506c system, pre-treatment FBCT images were obtained for error assessment. By revisiting the initial plan and recalculating doses, we evaluated the influence of these techniques on dose distribution. This pioneering study demonstrates the application of these improvements in NPC radiotherapy, with observed benefits including reduced setup errors (overall, oropharyngeal, and neck displacements), alongside improved outcomes in this cohort: enhanced target coverage, and decreased OAR irradiation compared to traditional methods.

The customized group exhibited significantly reduced setup errors in the anterior-posterior direction relative to the traditional group. The modified styrofoam enhanced fixation in the crotch region, effectively minimizing downward sliding and pitch rotation. Notably, the customized group showed improvements in neck errors across all directions except laterally. There was no significant difference in the production time between the two groups of fixation devices, both of which were made synchronously with the TM. However, the traditional group’s universal OS, due to its oversized design, caused restricted mouth opening and occlusal difficulties for patients during the second half of treatment, and this was compounded by the tongue lacking a fixed occlusal position, failing to establish a stable locking relationship with the TM. In contrast, the customized OS only requires a 1.5 cm mouth opening; its curved tongue depressor effectively restricts tongue movement, allowing patients to wear it easily throughout treatment. Additionally, its three equilateral triangle grooves can be precisely embedded with the TM, achieving one-to-one rigid limitation, and these factors likely contributed to the observed reduction in setup errors. When significant soft tissue changes were detected, larger vertical direction errors (0.14 cm vs. 0.08 cm, p = 0.001) aligned with previous findings21.

Compared with the traditional group, the standard 3 mm PTV margin may be insufficient in the lower neck region. For patients with obvious nodal involvement in this area, further margin expansion should be considered. At the overall PTV margin level, the customized group allows for margin shrinkage ranging from 0.2 to 2.3 mm across all directions relative to the traditional group. While such shrinkage may have limited clinical significance in the global direction (0.7 mm in the anterior-posterior direction), a similar magnitude of reduction (2.3 mm) in the oropharyngeal region could be crucial for treating locally invasive tumors. Studies have shown that such tumors often extend to within a few millimeters of critical organs like the brainstem, spinal cord, and optic nerve, and that reducing PTV margins helps decrease OAR-related toxicities22,23. This refinement in PTV margin, coupled with the improvements noted in neck errors, underscores the potential clinical benefits. Notably, a small number of customized group patients still had neck margin exceedances (max 3.2 mm). This doesn’t contradict group-level benefits, but reflects the balance between “population-level efficacy of customized fixation” and “clinical individual variability”. Likely causes include inevitable inter-individual anatomical heterogeneity or occasional small deviations from uncontrollable physiological motion, and for such cases, further refinement of setup error control (like real-time monitoring) is necessary.

Significant reductions in target volumes were noted when comparing PLANct with FBCTs. The most substantial decrease occurred in the traditional group’s GTVnd-L (-31.1%), followed by a reduction in the customized group’s GTVnd-R (-27.5%). The least reduction was observed in PGTVp (-5.6% and − 6.0% in both groups). These findings are consistent with prior studies documenting reductions in target volumes24,25, indicating that anatomical changes can directly impact dose distribution within the target area, with factors such as weight loss and lymph node contraction leading to suboptimal dosing26–28. In this study, D95% for bilateral PGTVnd declined in both groups, while the mean dose for all PTVs exceeded that of PLANct. Though the customized group demonstrated enhanced target coverage for PGTVp and PCTV1, the clinical implications of reduced D95% for PGTVnd on local control rates remain unclear and warrant long-term evaluation.

Our analysis concentrated on the clinical dosimetric endpoint of PCTV2 (D95) coverage. The nearly 2.4-fold improvement in the customized group is encouraging, though we acknowledge the clinical impact of its ~ 1% median gain requires nuance—this magnitude falls within the typical dose-calculation uncertainty (± 2%) in NPC VMAT29. However, the maximum reduction in PCTV2 D95 was only − 6.6% in the customized group, whereas it reached − 13.2% in the traditional group. This difference is meaningful because PCTV2 encompasses high-risk nodal regions, where severe D95 declines are known to substantially increase local recurrence risk in NPC30,31. Although we noted a moderate negative correlation trend between PCTV2 (D95) and composite translational error in the traditional group, this correlation did not achieve statistical significance (p > 0.05). This is likely due to our relatively small sample size (n = 18 FBCTs after exclusions). Nonetheless, the observed trend aligns with clinical logic. Reduced setup errors, as seen in the customized group, would theoretically minimize target displacement relative to the radiation field, thereby improving dose conformity.

In NPC radiotherapy, central nervous system-related OARs include the brainstem, optic nerves, optic chiasm, spinal cord, and temporal lobes. Excessive irradiation to these areas can induce radiation-related complications such as radioactive brain injury, temporal lobe necrosis, chiasmatitis, optic neuritis, and radiation myelitis17,32. Minimizing radiation dose while meeting clinical dose constraints is vital for enhancing patient quality of life post-treatment. Although both groups exceeded the PLANct Dmax for the spinal cord, the increment was lesser in the customized group, which also displayed a 5.1% reduction in D1% relative to PLANct. While most dosimetric parameters for adjacent organs did not show statistically significant differences, the customized group exhibited lower median or mean values of Dmax and D1%, indicating advantages in fixation effectiveness.

Post-treatment evaluations indicated a decrease in the parotid and submandibular glands’ volumes, aligning with previous research33,34. In the customized group, the parotid gland volume reduced significantly more (-42.7% vs. -25.8%, -41.5% vs. -23.0%) compared to the traditional group; however, no significant differences were observed in submandibular gland volumes. In the customized group, the left parotid showed increased Dmean, V26, and V30, while reductions were noted for the right parotid, likely attributed to the distribution of positive lymph nodes (more extensive left-sided cervical nodal involvement). Notably, the proximity of the parotid gland to the target area was associated with greater volume reductions, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The centripetal shift of the gland and facial contour changes from chemoradiotherapy positioned the parotid gland closer to the target area, enhancing irradiation exposure. These observations are similar to findings by Jiang et al.35, suggesting that timely plan adjustments could be beneficial36. Furthermore, we speculate that it might be due to the slight anatomical changes caused by the neck flexion, which could pull the left parotid gland inward towards the left target area, thereby further increasing its irradiation dose.

In the neck target area, the thyroid gland represents a primary OAR. Chow et al.37 highlighted the importance of minimizing thyroid radiation exposure to prevent late-onset hypothyroidism. Zhai et al.38 found that patients with thyroid Dmean ≥ 45 Gy exhibited a 4.9-fold increased risk of developing hypothyroidism compared to those with lower Dmean. The thyroid Dmean in the customized group was consistent with that of PLANct (44.3 Gy), while in the traditional group, it exceeded 45 Gy. Notably, studies indicate that incidence peaks within 1 to 3 years post-radiotherapy39,40. Over the next three years, we will continue monitoring and reporting changes in thyroid function.

Within the context of this study, the customized approach significantly enhances the delivery of irradiation doses to the target area while more effectively protecting OARs. This study adopted a sequential cohort design (traditional group enrolled first, followed by the customized group) that carries inherent risks of workflow optimization or learning-curve drift. We conducted post-hoc analyses of key covariates, including identical plan parameters, a uniform median number of FBCT scans per group, and consistent radiation therapists-performed setup, which helped minimize such risks. However, this study has limitations. Although the customized group demonstrated reduced radiation exposure to OARs such as the spinal cord and temporal lobes compared to the traditional group, these results did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited sample size. Another limitation is the absence of intrafraction motion analysis, which prevents us from evaluating how real-time organ displacement during radiotherapy impacts the actual delivered dose. Additionally, the clinical impact of reduced target dose parameters (e.g., the decline in D95% for PGTVnd) on local control rates still requires long-term follow-up evaluation.

Conclusion

The integration of a customized OS with modified styrofoam significantly improves the reproducibility of patient positioning during NPC radiotherapy in this study. This approach notably decreases spatial rotation errors and three-dimensional linear errors in the head and neck region. Furthermore, a notable advantage of customized solutions may allow margin reduction and selective OAR sparing. Importantly, the clinical application of customized immobilization devices should remain individualized, taking into account patient anatomy, disease stage, and institutional resources.
