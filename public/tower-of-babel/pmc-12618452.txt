Evaluation of hydrophilicity and surface morphology of nanosecond-pulsed laser-engineered surface textures on stainless steel, cobalt-chromium, and titanium alloys
Authors: Mahmood Al Bashir, Talha Khan, Rajeev Nair
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The need to improve biocompatibility and to ensure successful integration of biologically compatible metals or bio-metals with biological tissues has resulted in the development and creation of engineered surfaces as biomaterials for use as implants and bio-medical devices. Through laser surface texturing, precise control over surface micro-topography, and microstructure pattern can be achieved, that optimize and enhance cellular adhesion, growth and differentiation—key factors that prevent implant rejection and improve device functionality and performance. This study investigates nanosecond-pulsed, laser-engineered surface texturing on stainless steel, titanium, and cobalt-chromium alloys, particularly for use in biocompatible implants. Coupons of each material were textured using uniform laser parameters, resulting in engineered surfaces with distinct and defined peaks and valleys, creating micro-topographies influenced by the Gaussian profile of the laser, as analyzed via SEM (scanning electron microscopy) and optical profilometry. Surface analysis showed that engineered textures on stainless steel demonstrate high uniformity with surface roughness measured to be 0.897 μm (Ra), facilitating better cellular adhesion, an essential feature for implant integration. This was confirmed via water contact angle test that showed a moderately hydrophilic surface showing consistent behavior (mean Water Contact Angle (WCA)) close to 71.1°, variance 0.17). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) indicated minimal surface oxidation across all samples, consistent with processing under an inert gas environment. Additionally, a computational model was created to verify and validate the “experimental surface-textured” profiles of each of the materials within a 5% margin, confirming the accuracy and reproducibility of the laser-processing technique. The uniform micro-scale surface topography and preserved surface chemistry of SS316L show that it promotes cell-adhesion and enhanced potential for biomedical implant applications compared to Co-Cr and Ti-6Al-4V.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Surface engineering denotes the modification of the outer surface of a material for attaining specific applications. Also known as texturing, the reason for surface engineering varies by application. Reducing or improving mating parts friction, increase surface area by roughening the surface for better adhesion or coating application, or simply for esthetics can be motivations for surface texturing. Myriad surfaces are textured according to the required application, ranging from automotive parts to simple book covers [1]. Braun et al., described how surface engineering can help reduce carbon emission by reducing the amount of friction in different mechanical devices [2]. Along with that, surface texturing is increasingly being used in biomaterials for various purposes. Biologically compatible metal or bio-metals, are used in developing numerous biomedical devices. There has been a surge of technological advancement by the implementation of biomaterials in bone transplant, osteosynthesis, dental, and cardiovascular components. The usage can be attributed to the higher yield strength, fatigue resistance and deformation of biomaterials as mentioned by Petrochenko et al. [3]. Further research into biomaterials has enabled the use of these as specialized implants targeting very specific-use body parts. Li et al., have stated that enhancing the surface of the metal, for example, has increased the use of biomaterials, as it can behave as a hydrophilic or hydrophobic material [4]. In the past decades, laser texturing has been used as a viable medium for enhancing material surfaces for different application requirements. Laser texturing has its own advantages compared to many other surface enhancing methods. The surface morphology plays a vital role in cell regeneration, cellular response, and many different biomaterial applications. Various key issues related to the topological controls of substrates as well as cells were investigated by Curtis and Wilkinson [5]. These interactions between cells and substrates are seen to occur from microscale to nanoscale. Dumas et al. [6], and Tan & Saltzman [7], also stated that this interactions regulate cell adhesion, proliferation of cells as well as differentiation’s. Along with that, the hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity and capillary forces is critical for the protein and cell attachment from a molecular point of view. These features have encouraged scientists to work with micro and nanometer surface texturing. Although textured materials have exhibited improved performance and possibilities of new applications, there has not been any indication of an ideal surface texture. De Oliveira and Nanci [8], and Schwarts and Boyann [9] studied on surface energy and claimed that laser surface engineering attains better hydrophilic/phobic features. During the experiments, different shapes and textures such as cliffs, ridges, grooves, and spikes were used to understand the cell behavior in respect to topography of the material. Also known as contact guidance, the phenomenon that governs the cell adaptation and growth direction was found to be influenced by this topography change of materials. According to Craighead et al., the depth of a microgroove is prominent in governing contact guidance than the width [10]. The study also provides evidences that better integration of bones and materials can be achieved by surface texturing of the material. As Karacs et al., described, three different characteristics of the metallic surface is of grave importance for implants, cleanliness, microscale structure, and a stable layer of oxide [11]. The cleanliness of the surface mostly depends on the surface contamination originated from the bulk material. However, the contamination also occurs during the laser texturing of the material. The microscale structure on the surfaces, as studied by Karacs et al., had significant effect in comparison with machined smooth surfaces [11]. In experiments on implants, Karacs et al., found that the sand-blasted, laser-etched or machined surface had higher torque removal than smooth surface. This is positively attributed to the microscale structure of the laser-textured surface and higher retention force [11]. Gaggl et al. [12], and Groessner & Tuan [13], also studied the effect of cell topography by changing the surface texture. However, because of the use of blast-textured surface, specific patterns were not possible to be developed, and irregularity of the topography was difficult to be linked with cell integration. Hence, many patterning techniques were developed as described in Table 1. The technologies have evolved rapidly over the last few decades [14–16]. Because of the requirement of a vacuum chamber for electron and ion beam machining processes, sample size limitation becomes a concern. Compared to that, photolithography doesn’t require any vacuum chamber, but it is a time consuming multistage process. However, laser machining process does not require a vacuum chamber and can be conducted under normal air or with the assistance of an inert gas [17]. Moreover, laser surface processing can be performed with better precision and repeatability compared to other processes. The aim of this study was to examine the different behaviors of nano-textured surfaces of Titanium, Stainless Steel, and Cobalt Chromium using a nano-second pulsed laser. The selection of a nanosecond pulsed laser was considered above any other surface modification techniques such as ultra-short pulse lasers (femtosecond and picosecond) to more conventional microsecond laser systems, as well as non-laser methods such as electric arc texturing, sandblasting, electron beam texturing, etc. Ultrafast lasers such as femtosecond lasers offer superior accuracy and negligible thermal impact through extremely fine features with minimal thermal diffusion and damage and negligible heat-affected zones [18]. However, these systems demand complex, and higher capital investment, more delicate maintenance, and lower throughput when larger processing volumes are required [19]. Longer pulsed lasers such as nanosecond pulsed lasers in particular offer a favorable balance between precision and controlled ablation to create repeatable patterning along with scalability for lab-scale research and potential industrial use. In comparison, microsecond lasers provide a much higher resulting heat impact through allowable diffusion of heat causing significant remelting and as such degradation of feature quality [18]. Nanosecond lasers used in this study permit operational simplicity and scalability along with tight control over texture depth and surface morphology [19]. Nanosecond lasers have been successfully used to surface texturing stainless steels for improving corrosion resistance through sub-surface melting and alteration of surface wettability [19]. Non-laser-based methods techniques such as sandblasting create a quicker surface texture however, the control over the feature and uniformity of the created surface texture is limited due to the creation of stochastic textures [20]. Techniques such as photolithography and reactive ion etching offer advantages such as well-defined micro-scale patterns, however, these processes require multi-step material processing and are not feasible for bulk surface texturing [21]. Particle beam approaches like electron beam texturing provide high precision and control but are inherently slow processes are suitable for small areas and require extremely expensive vacuum equipment [22]. In contrast, laser surface texturing is a single-step process, in particular nanosecond pulsed lasers, that can produce repeatable results with tailored micro-topographies with high precision that meet the functional requirements for controllable texture depth with minimal damage to the substrate. Moreover, the use of our nanosecond laser was not only technically justified by these performance benefits but also practically driven (it was the only system available in our lab) underlining how equipment accessibility can guide method selection in research. Although according to Li et al., titanium is better in respect of wear and thermal resistance, nano-textured titanium doesn’t behave as the untextured material [4]. Also, depending on the application of the material they behave in different ways. In this study, all the metals were nano-textured and SEM images of the surfaces were taken and EDX data was used to understand the material composition at the surface where nano-texturing was performed. A volume-of fluid (VOF) based Flow3DⓇ model was developed and validated to simulate the behavior of the material under pulsed laser. This helped us to better understand the laser-material interaction under different parameter changes.

Computational procedure

To validate the findings from the experimental results of the laser surface texturing, a scaled down multi-physics based numerical model was developed and simulated using Flow3DⓇ AM and Flow3DⓇ AM Weld, which uses a VOF based approximation to accurately predict and simulate the hydrodynamics of the melt pool region. Designed to replicate the physical phenomena observed in the experiments, the model provides a comprehensive comparison between the numerical and experimental outcomes. Based on the model developed by Harimkar et. al, it relates the input parameters such as laser power, scan speed, frequency to the surface and temperature profiles, and accurately captures hydrodynamics, phase change and resolidification of the melt pool region due to material ablation through the Gaussian profile of the nanosecond laser [23]. The coupons were modeled using an in-house-developed MATLABⓇ script that generates an “stl” file. The coupon dimensions are 1 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm, and the developed “stl” file is used as an input geometry for the numerical simulation setup in Flow3DⓇ AM and Flow3DⓇ AM Weld. The model is able to accurately predict the 3D surface profile, and a quantitative study reveals good agreement of the numerical simulation results with the experimental outcomes. However, due to computational limitations, the nanosecond pulsing was approximated to a “continuous width” laser-based model developed by Harimkar et al., and this model is able to accurately account for physical phenomena occurring during the ablation process [23]. In laser ablation processes, it is necessary to accurately model the physical phenomena occurring in the melt pool region. The key mechanisms include heat input, phase change, plasma formation, and vapor dynamics which influences the major governing equations. Figure 2 illustrates these major phenomena, starting from the absorption of irradiated laser heat given by Fresnel absorption and multiple internal reflections, followed by phase change in the heat-affected zone due to elevated temperatures, and then the subsequent movement of the melted material under the influence of localized vapor pressure and the laser induced forces, leading to changed surface morphology. However, for simplifying the numerical model, dynamics of plasma formation are not considered, but its effects on the creation of surface textures have been taken into account [24].Fig. 2Major physical phenomena in laser ablation process highlighting the sequence of events starting from laser irradiation, leading to phase change and vaporization and culminating in complex melt pool hydrodynamics [52]

Temperature dependent properties and feedback loop

As laser induces a high temperature in a small amount of time, the material is heated and cooled in rapid succession and the temperature varies greatly. Hence, for accuracy of the model, the thermal conductivity k(T) and specific heat capacity Cp(T) are considered as a function of temperature, T.

Approximation of pulsed laser to continuous laser for computational efficiency

Simulating laser surface texturing, especially with nanosecond-pulsed lasers, can be computationally intensive, as the solver must process each pulse for each grid cell and subsequently average the values in the heat-affected region. With the computation becoming extremely intensive due to the short pulse duration, and high pulsing frequency, a fine temporal mesh resolution is necessary to capture the rapid heating and cooling cycles as the laser raster scans the surface of the substrate. To address these challenges, a pulsing approximation method is often applied to reduce the computational expenses for simulation of pulsed lasers, where the pulsed laser is approximated as a continuous wave laser. This approach utilizes the average energy deposition over time, significantly reducing the computing requirements, to predict the ablation hydrodynamics without sacrificing the overall accuracy of the results. The pulsing approximation technique simplifies the time-dependent energy profile of the laser by treating the pulses as continuous heat/energy source based on average power over the time dependent duty cycle of the laser. The nanosecond pulsed laser in this study operated at 6.25 MHz with pulse energy as 75 μJ; therefore, the equivalent power or the average power for the continuous width was approximated using equation (8).8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{avg}={E}_{pulse}f$$\end{document}Pavg=EpulsefUsing this approximation, it was determined that the laser was continuously delivering 468.75 W power, which simplified the dynamic memory allocation during the simulation, thereby eliminating the need for computation for every laser pulse [23].

Numerical model setup

In this study, Flow3DⓇ AM and Flow3DⓇ Weld was employed to numerically simulate the laser surface texturing of stainless steel, titanium alloy and Co-Cr alloy substrates which have been proposed as biometals for implants. This allows an in-depth analysis and validation with the experimental outcomes through topographical comparison. In the following section, the setup of this numerical model in Flow3DⓇ, highlighting configuration of model analysis is discussed. Flow3DⓇ is a commercial computational fluid dynamics-based software that uses VOF-based free surface tracking technique and dynamically enforces the boundary conditions on the free surfaces in complex flows. Numerical controls for convergence

To ensure stability of the simulation and its accuracy, the “Split Lagrangian” scheme is used for “volume-of-fluid” advection calculations. The order of approximation of the momentum equations is set to first order, since second order calculations are computationally extensive. The minimum time set is set to 1 × 10−3 s to ensure that incremental time-stepped results are recorded by the solver.

Results and discussion

Experimental results

Patterned and unpatterned surfaces of all the three materials under an SEM shows distinct features prominent with texture marks. Stainless steel and cobalt chromium have more distinct peaks and valleys under 300× magnification as shown in Fig. 11. At a magnification of 1000× and 5000×, it is seen that the grain boundaries are more visible in all the samples as shown in Fig. 12. This was also noticed during the surface roughness measurement of the samples. As shown in Fig. 13, the surface roughness of titanium alloy is higher than that of both stainless steel and cobalt chrome alloy. Under the operation of identical nanosecond laser processing parameters, it was observed that the Ti alloy exhibited higher surface roughness as compared to SS 316L and CoCR alloy. This is attributed to three major factors; laser absorption characteristics at the 355 nm (UV) wavelength, thermal conductivity and the heat dissipation characteristics of the materials, and intrinsic thermo-physical properties, such as melting point, heat capacity, and laser irradiation-plasma plume behavior. Ti alloy is said to have a greater absorption fraction of the 355 nm laser as compared to SS316L and CoCr alloy, which directly relates to the energy deposited on the material surface. It has been previously researched that metals exhibit higher absorptivity for UV wavelength lasers as compared to IR lasers where studies show that Ti alloys absorb around 65–75% of the incident 355 nm of laser irradiation, compared to 40–45% absorption for SS316L and 30–40% for CoCR alloys [31, 32]. This enhanced energy coupling results in the absorption of more heat of the incident laser creating localized heating and surface ablation/vaporization of the material substrate producing a more vigorous melting and evaporation, leading to rougher topographies. The greater amount of absorbed fluence produces a deeper micro-scale level melt pool and pronounced ablation craters in Ti alloy. Additionally, Ti exhibits low thermal conductivity (approximately 6.7 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{W}{mK}$$\end{document}WmK) which inhibits rapid heat dissipation away from the impact zone, prolonging localized melting and leaving behind uneven, textured surfaces by the time the heat is dissipated. The localized heat at the impact zone results in higher temperatures at the HAZ, creating a hotter and deeper melt pool at a smaller focal area translating into slower solidification dynamics leading to resolidified structures due to reheating at the localized area. The low thermal conductivity of the Ti alloy induces micro-scale spatter of the ablated material due to high recoil pressure, resulting in the solidification of the molten material at the surface [33, 34]. On the other hand, SS 316L and CoCr alloys with their lower absorptance at 355 nm and higher conductivities (close to 16 and 13 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{W}{mK}$$\end{document}WmK respectively), receive smaller effective energy on the surface of the substrate under identical laser parameters leading to milder melting/ ablation, often yielding finer and refined surface textures while their high thermal conductivities result in rapid cooling of the substrates, leading to insufficient time for the heat to create deeper localized craters. Studies have shown that metals with higher thermal conductivities tend to have higher ablation thresholds making the melting and evaporation of the material difficult due to lateral heat conduction, creating smaller and shallow melt pools, allowing for uniform solidification of the material substrates [33, 34]. In essence, it can be said that the higher laser absorptivity of Ti alloys amplifies the effect of the laser fluence on the substrate, creating rougher surface topographies. Studies have also revealed that Titanium readily forms a thin oxide layer that potentially increases the absorption of the laser irradiation. It is also necessary to take into consideration the response of the material based on the thermophysical properties of the alloys, where properties such as melting point and heat capacity differentiate how these materials behave under the action of the laser beam. Ti alloy has a melting point of 1600–1650 °C (Titanium Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5), Annealed specification) which is higher than the melting point of 316L (close to 1400 °C, Stainless Steel 316L specification) and Co-Cr alloy (1300 °C, Co-Cr Properties). It can be assumed that a higher melting point for Ti alloy would result in lesser melting of the material as compared to SS and Co-Cr for the same energy input, however, when combined with high absorptivity and low thermal conductivity as discussed above, Ti alloy reaches its melting point and beyond the focal area of the laser, which implies that once the laser pulse passes, the molten Ti begins to solidify at a higher temperature than SS and Co-Cr potentially freezing in a more uneven topology. For cases where the energy is just sufficient to reach the melting temperature, Ti6Al4V may experience incomplete melting in some regions resulting in partial ablation and re-solidification leaving an irregular surface with high roughness. Ti-6Al-4V, Co-Cr, and SS 316L have similar specific heat capacities which imply that all the materials require similar threshold energy to raise the temperature of the substrate by a given amount per unit mass. However, the role played by the difference in material densities creates a difference in laser energy absorption for the same volume of the material. The density of Ti alloy (4.4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{g}{cc}$$\end{document}gcc) being half as dense as compared to SS (8.0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{g}{cc}$$\end{document}gcc) and Co-Cr (8.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{g}{cc}$$\end{document}gcc) alloys results in the same laser energy, heating Ti to a higher temperature as compared to the other two materials. A lower mass requirement coupled with a slightly higher heat capacity allows Ti6Al4V to undergo larger temperature swings under the nanosecond pulses, which results in Ti readily reaching extreme temperatures leading to evaporation, while steel or CoCr, with roughly double the mass in the laser interaction zone, will require a much higher amount of heat to completely evaporate the material substrate [33]. The influence of plasma plume formation of the vaporized metal above the surface can result in the interaction of the plasma with the incoming laser pulses (absorbing or defocusing some of the laser energy, a phenomenon known as plasma shielding), and also exert recoil pressure on the melt pool and interfering with the melt pool dynamics. Research on the generation of plasma plume for Ti and SS have shown that Ti generates a larger plasma plume as compared to SS which means that Ti-6Al-4V is more prone to rapid vaporization and plasma formation than SS316L. A large plasma will absorb laser energy via inverse Bremsstrahlung before it reaches the surface, causing non-uniform energy delivery where some pulses might be heavily absorbed by the plasma, causing less melting on the surface below, while others (after the plasma dissipates slightly) strike the surface without being disturbed, subsequently creating highly uneven surface texture. Furthermore, an intense plasma plume above Ti exerts a strong recoil pressure on the surface of the substrate driving molten metal out of the laser crater which can fling droplets of molten Ti alloy away from the melt pool region. These droplets either are lost as spatter, or re-deposit around the processing zone as irregular raised spots adding to a much more pronounced surface roughness. In contrast, SS316L and CoCr form less vigorous plumes, thus less molten material is ejected and as such the melted region tends to stay in place creating a much more uniform morphology [34, 35].Fig. 11SEM view of the coupons at 300× Magnification a Unpatterned and b Patterned Stainless Steel c Unpatterned and d Patterned Ti Alloy e Unpatterned and f Patterned Cobalt ChromiumFig. 12SEM comparison of the coupons at 1000× and 5000× Respectively of a, b Patterned Stainless Steel c, d Patterned Ti Alloy e, f Patterned Cobalt ChromiumFig. 13Surface roughness of SS, Ti and Cobalt Chromium textured surface (from left to right)

Numerical simulation results

In this study, numerical simulations were performed using Flow3DⓇ and Flow3DⓇ Weld to investigate the effects of “continuous width-approximated” nanosecond-pulsed laser texturing on cobalt-chromium, stainless steel, and titanium alloy. The primary objective was to investigate how different input parameters affect the laser-material interactions and influence the temperature distribution, melt pool dynamics, and surface morphology, which is extremely crucial for optimizing hydrophilicity and biocompatibility. The simulation model was set up to replicate and validate the results from the experimental findings. However, a critical factor that is taken into consideration while the experimental and simulation results are compared, is the difference in the initial surface conditions. For the numerical simulation setup, the substrate surface is assumed and modeled to be perfectly smooth, whereas in the experimental setup, the actual surface of the coupons has inherent peaks and valleys before laser processing. This discrepancy in the experimental substrate can cause localized variations in heat absorption and material response, leading to differences in surface texturing. To address this discrepancy, an adjusted error function was used, that considers the initial surface roughness in the coupons before laser irradiation. The error function is designed to mitigate the impact of the initial roughness and focus on the changes due to irradiation on the materials by the laser. The correction factor Ri is estimated based on the measured surface roughness profile of the substrate before the laser surface texturing is taking place, such as using an average surface roughness parameter, Ra. The application of this adjusted error function explains the minute discrepancies between the experimental and numerical results, resulting in an rms (root mean square) error value of 0.011692 μm for stainless steel, 0.0059 μm for Co-Cr, and 0.00318 μm for the titanium alloy approximately, using the initial three data points for each material, showing discrepancy due to initial roughness on the experimental surface. This approach provides a much more realistic approach and assessment of the accuracy of the simulation by accounting for non-ideal and uncontrollable conditions of the experimental surface, which are otherwise challenging to replicate in numerical models. In this study, the roughness profile and the average surface roughness (Ra) values were obtained from Flow3DⓇ Post for each of the three materials.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy results

As Curtis and Wilkinson described, surface topography is more prominent than the chemical composition of the surface towards cell adhesion [36]. Moreover, Niederauer et al., supported the claim stating that surface roughness also increases surface wettability increasing adhesion of the cells [37]. An energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis of both the peaks and valleys of the setup are shown in Fig. 17. According to the EDX analysis, the amount of oxygen in the valleys has depleted compared to amount of oxygen in the peaks of the texturing, in all the cases. This can be attributed to the amount of heat created during the texturing process and reduction of oxygen. Moreover, for stainless steel and cobalt chromium, the oxygen percentage became near zero in the valley. However, for the titanium alloy, oxygen percentage depleted from 23.68% to 14.5%, respectively from peak to valley (%wt). This can be explained by the higher thermal conductivity of the titanium alloy in comparison with that of stainless steel and cobalt chrome alloy, which distributed the heat quicker than the other two materials, and thus contributing to lesser oxygen. This can be further bolstered by the temperature attained during the texturing operation. It was observed during the texturing procedure that the titanium alloy reached the lowest temperature compared to that of the other two materials, during the operation. The temperature was measured using an embedded thermocouple in the XY bed during the process. Consistent with the literature on previous studies conducted for laser surface texturing, argon gas was used as a shielding agent against potential substrate oxidation that could influence the micro/nano surface topography. The EDX study shows that a negligible oxygen content was detected due to natural oxidation or surface hydrolyzation upon post-processing contact with air. No visible discoloration or oxide layer was observed on the laser-treated surfaces of the three material coupons which suggests that oxide formation was avoided. Previous studies have shown that in ambient air, laser irradiation produces metal-oxide surface films that raise surface energy and create low water contact angles due to the polar nature of the oxides. The inert gas atmosphere prevented surface oxidation, and the water surface contact angles also show that native surface chemistry and texture were maintained [38, 39].Fig. 17EDX of the patterned coupons at Peaks and Valleys Respectively of a, b Stainless Steel, c, d Ti Alloy, e, f Cobalt Chromium

Wettability and contact angle measurement results

The contact angle of all the three materials were measured to find any affect on the wettability by the surface texturing. The water contact angle was measured using a standard droplet of water using the drop shadow method where a solid-liquid interaction was taken into consideration while projecting a light on the drop to capture the outline of the liquid drop on the surface. This process is similar to the one used in a previous study [25]. As shown in Fig. 18 and Table 5, the contact angle of all three of the materials changes because of the textured surface. The contact angle measurement was carefully performed by introducing a specific amount of deionized water (7 μL) on the surface of the textured coupon and measure the contact angle. According to experimental values, the contact angle of 316L stainless steel is near 82° [40]. The surface texturing of 316L has reduced the contact angle to an average of 71.62°. Conclusion

Stainless steel, titanium alloy and cobalt-chrome alloy coupons were laser surface-textured on one side. The Gaussian profile of the laser beam and overlapping passes created unique features. The study mainly focused on the topographical changes of three different materials exposed under the same laser parameters. Upon the completion of the enhanced surface texturing, the samples were observed under an SEM with different magnification. It was observed that although the same laser parameters were used for all the materials, the surface textures were different in different materials. Flow3DⓇ AM was used to compare the surface temperature with that of the experimental one. The laser surface profiling created enhanced surface textures that facilitate cell adhesion by incorporating surface roughness. The study showed that among the three different materials, Co-Cr exhibits the least amount of Ra development during the texturing process which shows that Co-Cr is the least suitable for implants and bio-metal applications. The higher Ra in the experimental and simulation results for stainless steel suggests greater potential for enhanced hydrophilicity and biocompatibility, which are critical factors in biomedical applications, such as implants and tissue engineering as the laser processing of SS316L yielded a more uniform and higher surface roughness as compared to Co-Cr and Ti6Al4V. This is extremely important in terms of biocompatibility, because cell adhesion - the initial attachment of the cell to an external material, is the first crucial step in tissue integration and cell proliferation. For biomedical implants, a roughened and uniform surface texture acts as a better scaffold for the anchorage of cells as compared to smoother and non-uniform surfaces [43]. Results observed in biological assays have established that micron and submicron-scale roughness can significantly improve the biological response to implant materials. Increased roughness in the order of micrometers improves biological response to implant materials as it provides increased surface area for absorption, promoting cell adhesion and growth. Microscale asperities and pores create a three-dimensional landscape for increased protein adsorption from body fluids, depositing key adhesive proteins. Additionally, micro-rough surfaces offer mechanical interlocking sites for cell filopodia and lamellipodia, for stronger and stable cell anchorage. It has been studied that making stainless steel rough improves osteoblast cell adhesion and morphology compared to a smooth polished surface [44–46]. It has also been studied that enhancing cell adhesion through increased surface roughness has resulted in improved tissue integration of implants which allows cells to easily proliferate, differentiate, and form extracellular matrix at the implant interface. The increased roughness provides more docking points for bone-forming cells to attach and encourages “ingrowth” of tissue into surface irregularities. Even though the CoCr and Ti6Al4V samples did not achieve the same roughness level as SS 316L, the laser-induced texture on them should still confer some biocompatibility benefits over a fully smooth finish. In summary, the surface morphology analysis allows us to infer positive biological implications even in the absence of direct cell culture data. The SS 316L surface, with its higher and more uniform roughness, is anticipated to support superior cell adhesion; a prerequisite for downstream cell proliferation and tissue integration. This roughened stainless steel surface falls within an advantageous topographical regime for anchorage-dependent cells, providing ample surface area and 3D micro-features for the cells to latch onto [43, 47]. In aspect of oxygen content, both cobalt chromium and stainless shows zero quantity of oxygen in laser-affected zone compared to that of titanium. Moreover, surface roughness of stainless steel is more uniform in comparison to that of cobalt chromium. It has been theorized that towards cellular adhesion and bio-implants, surface topography is a much more dominant factor as compared to chemical composition [36]. The theory of increased surface wettability due to increased surface roughness, which itself is mainly due to increased surface area for absorption and cellular adhesion, has also gained much acceptance [37]. Biological assay conducted via biocompatibility/cell cytotoxicity based in accordance to ISO 10993-5 [48] to analyze in-vitro cell tissue cell growth on textured surface, and observed on optical microscope, show that the number of live cells increased for the duration of study (24 h) for stainless steel, and prove the viability of textured stainless steel as a biological implant [25]. Similar results were observed on samples laser-textured in the present study [42].
