A study of ferronematic thin films including a stray field energy
Shilpa Dutta, James Dalby, Apala Majumdar, Anja Schlömerkemper

Abstract
Ferronematic materials are colloidal suspensions of magnetic particles in liquid crystals. They are complex materials with potential applications in display technologies, sensors, microfluidics devices, etc. We consider a model for ferronematics in a 2D domain with a variational approach. The proposed free energy of the ferronematic system depends on the Landau-de Gennes (LdG) order parameter 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} and the magnetization 𝐌 \mathbf{M} , and incorporates the complex interaction between the liquid crystal molecules and the magnetic particles in the presence of an external magnetic field 𝐇 e ​ x ​ t \mathbf{H}_{ext} . The energy functional combines the Landau-de Gennes nematic energy density and energy densities from the theory of micromagnetics including (an approximation of) the stray field energy and energetic contributions from an external magnetic field. For the proposed ferronematic energy, we first prove the existence of an energy minimizer and then the uniqueness of the minimizer in certain parameter regimes. Secondly, we numerically compute stable ferronematic equilibria by solving the gradient flow equations associated with the proposed ferronematic energy. The numerical results show that the stray field influences the localization of the interior nematic defects and magnetic vortices.



Keywords

Ferronematic materials, stray field, thin-films, gradient flow equations, Crank-Nicolson finite difference scheme.

Introduction

We investigate a ferronematic model in a two-dimensional domain that combines the Landau-de Gennes (LdG) theory of nematic liquid crystals
[
1
]
and the theory of micromagnetics
[
2
]
allowing for the study of both the nematic ordering and the magnetization (from the suspended magnetized particles), including their interaction in the presence of an external magnetic field in a unified framework.

Nematic liquid crystals (NLCs) are prominent examples of mesophases or liquid crystalline phases that combine the fluidity of liquids with the directional order of solids
[
1
]
. The nematic liquid crystal phase (NLCs) is characterized by the averaged local alignment of the nematic molecules along common axes, known as directors. Consequently, NLCs are directional materials and are sensitive to external factors such as electric fields, magnetic fields, light, and temperature
[
3
]
. Their anisotropic or direction-dependent response to light and electric fields makes them ideal materials for various electro-optic applications. Since the response of NLCs to an external magnetic field is much weaker than that to an external electric field, the use of NLCs in magneto-optics applications is found to be limited
[
4
]
. Therefore, a natural question arises of how to or whether one can strengthen the nematic response to an external magnetic field.

This concept was first introduced by Brochard and de Gennes more than five decades ago in their pioneering work
[
5
]
, where they proposed the existence of a ferromagnetic phase in a colloidal suspension of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) in nematic liquid crystals (NLCs) at room temperature, i.e., a spontaneous magnetization without an external magnetic field. These so-called ferronematics exhibit a stronger response to external magnetic fields compared to pure NLCs. The directors of NLCs can be controlled by the coupling between MNPs and NLCs (NLC-MNP interaction). Similarly, the spontaneous magnetization induced by the suspended MNPs can be tailored by nematic directors via NLC-MNP interactions. In
[
6
,
7
]
, the authors experimentally confirm the existence of a ferromagnetic phase via a suspension of MNPs in a NLC host. For recent theoretical work on ferronematics, we refer to
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
, see below for details.

The present model of a two-dimensional ferronematic material is inspired by the Landau de Gennes (LdG) energy for nematic materials in two dimensions
[
1
,
13
]
and the micromagnetic energy for ferromagnetic materials reduced to two dimensions. Our model extends the ferronematic model outlined in
[
8
]
. The ferronematic phase is described by two state variables on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one of which is the reduced LdG order parameter
\mathbf{Q}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with
\mathbf{Q}^{T}=\mathbf{Q},\mathrm{tr}{\mathbf{Q}}=0,Q_{3i}=Q_{i3}=0
,
i=1,2,3
(only valid for two-dimensional scenarios and for certain choices of the boundary conditions). The eigenvector corresponding to the largest positive eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
models the nematic director
\mathbf{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, c.f. (
2
). The second state variable is the spontaneous magnetization
\mathbf{M}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The micromagnetic part of the energy contains the exchange energy, the stray field energy, and the Zeeman energy. We refer to
[
2
,
14
,
15
]
for an introduction to micromagnetics. The exchange energy
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{M}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x
is invoked as an energy penalty to penalize jumps and rapid gradients in
\mathbf{M}
.
The stray field
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{M}}
is generated by the magnetization
\mathbf{M}
. In three dimensions, the stray field is given by a solution to the stationary Maxwell equation, cf. e.g.
[
15
]
. The stray field energy is then given by
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{M}}|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\mathbf{M}
with
\mathbf{M}
being extended by zero to the whole space. The stray field energy may contribute to the formation of boundary vortices, edge-curling domain walls, interior walls, etc.
[
16
]
.
In this article, we approximate the stray field energy by a reduced model as derived in Gioia and James
[
17
]
for very thin materials, see also
[
16
,
18
, Sec. 10]
. Then the stray field energy on the two-dimensional domain
\Omega
reads
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(M_{3}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x
, where
M_{3}
denotes the third component of
\mathbf{M}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
; see Section
3
for more details.
Furthermore, we assume that the ferronematic material is exposed to a given external magnetic field
\mathbf{H}_{ext}
; this is a three-dimensional vector field that in experiments is given on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, in our analytical and numerical investigation, only its regularity on
\Omega
is of relevance. The interaction of
\mathbf{H}_{ext}
with
\mathbf{M}
is modelled by the Zeeman energy
-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{M}\,\mathrm{d}x
.

Finally, the energy not only contains Ginzburg-Landau type potentials for
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
but also a coupling of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{Q}
, via
-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}
, cf. also
[
19
]
. Following,
[
20
,
7
]
, we also consider the coupling between an external magnetic field
\mathbf{H}_{ext}
and
\mathbf{Q}
in the form
-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}
.
As we show in Section
3.1
, the dimensionless form of the ferronematic energy in two dimensions is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{l_{1}}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\xi l_{2}}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{M}|^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}\xi\left(M_{3}\right)^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\;\;-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where

l_{1}
,
l_{2}
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
and
\xi
are positive parameters that are specified in the nondimensionalization in Section

.

The present work, based on the proposed ferronematic energy (
1
), has two main objectives: first, to investigate the existence of a minimizer of the energy (
1
) and to show the uniqueness of the energy minimizer in certain parameter regimes;
and second, to numerically examine the influence of the stray field, as well as the combined role of a stray field and an external magnetic field on stable ferronematic equilibria in Sections
5
and
6
. The existence of a solution for the energy minimization problem (Theorem
4.3
) follows from the direct method in the calculus of variations and the uniqueness result (Theorem
4.5
) is based on an application of the maximum principle. To prove the uniqueness theorem, we derive
L^{\infty}
-bounds for
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
and estimates of the second partial derivatives of the energy density; the result then follows by contradiction.

The latter part of the manuscript focuses on numerical computations of physically observable ferronematic profiles (modelled as local minimizers of the ferronematic energy). Using the energy dissipation law, we derive the gradient flow system that corresponds to the Euler-Lagrange equations for the ferronematic energy (
1
) in the stationary setting. We then employ the Crank-Nicolson finite difference technique (see e.g.
[
21
]
) to solve the gradient-flow system supplemented with initial and boundary conditions. The choice of the prescribed initial and boundary conditions is motivated by
[
10
]
. Since the gradient flow system is a coupled non-linear system of PDEs, we use Newton’s linearization technique to formulate the Crank-Nicolson discretized scheme as this allows us to tackle the convergence issues that arise due to nonlinearities.

Before we outline the results of our article further, we comment on previous work in the literature. The proposed energy (
1
) reduces to the ferronematic energy in
[
8
]
when
M_{3}=0
and
\mathbf{H}_{ext}=\mathbf{0}
. The ferronematic energy in
[
8
]
is based on the Landau description for ferronematic phase transition
[
19
]
, that includes the magnetic energy due to the penalization of magnetic saturation and does not account the contribution of the stray field. In
[
8
]
, the authors first numerically compute stable ferronematic equilibria on a 2D square domain with tangent boundary conditions and report two types of stable equilibria featuring magnetic domain walls with pairs of interior and boundary nematic defects. Also, they show that the NLC-MNP coupling tailors the location and multiplicity of nematic defects and magnetic vortices. Later, Han et al.
[
22
]
extend the previous work to 2D pentagons and hexagons and observe the co-existence of similar stable states. In this article, we investigate the effect of an approximated stray field energy and a Zeeman energy on the microstructure of the materials and consider
[
8
]
as a reference case for our numerical simulations.

Next, we summarize relevant work with different perspectives.
In
[
12
]
, the authors work on the asymptotic analysis of the global minimizers of a rescaled ferronematic energy in the limit of vanishing elastic constant, i.e., in the
l_{1}=l_{2}=l\rightarrow 0
limit. Their results focus on the strong
H^{1}
-convergence and an
l
-independent
H^{2}
-bound for global energy minimizers on a smooth, bounded 2D domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions and the results are complemented by numerical experiments. Canevari et al.
[
10
]
have shown that in the super-dilute regime, the LdG order parameter is a canonical harmonic map and the limit of the magnetization is a singular vector field with line defects that connect point defects along a minimal connection. The super-dilute regime is a limiting case for which the magnetic energy has a significantly lesser contribution than the NLC energy and the NLC-MNP interaction remains weak. Their theoretical results are also supported by numerical experiments. Recently, that work has been extended to the mixed boundary conditions (Dirichlet type for
\mathbf{Q}
and Neumann type for
\mathbf{M}
) in
[
9
]
.
In
[
11
]
, the authors study order reconstruction phenomena and perform bifurcation analysis of a ferronematic energy, in one-dimensional channel geometries.

The aforementioned studies do not consider the presence of stray fields and external magnetic fields.
However, the stray field is relevant because it can tailor arrangements of magnetic vortices for purely magnetic materials
[
14
]
and these phenomena can be further influenced by an external magnetic field. Some interesting features, e.g., switching mechanisms and birefringence
[
6
,
7
]
of ferronematic substances when exposed to an external magnetic field have been observed experimentally. From the theoretical perspective, a variational homogenized ferronematic model that accounts for the presence of an external magnetic field in the Oseen-Frank framework can be found in
[
20
]
.

The Gioia-James approximation
[
17
]
for the stray field energy does not lead to any criterion on the thickness of the material sample and is applicable for many practical purposes, e.g. films used in storage devices with a thickness of about 250 Å, and recording devices with a thickness less than 100 Å. Moreover, we refer to
[
23
]
, where the authors deal with the coupling of the three-dimensional magnetization with the two-dimensional fluid flow in an evolutionary magnetoviscoelastic model, counting the contribution of the stray field using the Gioia and James approximation. As an aside, we mention that there are other stray field limiting cases available in the literature, e.g.
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
for the pure ferromagnetic thin-film regime and these different limiting cases are constructed based on their varied behavior within the film’s interior and at the domain edge. It will be a challenging and interesting task to deal with other limiting cases or the full stray field energy in the study of ferronematic thin films in future works.

3
Ferronematic energy

As mentioned in the introduction, we study ferronematics confined to two-dimensional domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The ferronematic phase is described by two order parameters: the reduced Landau-de Gennes (LdG)
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor order parameter for the nematic phase that describes the orientational ordering of the ferronematic phase (see
[
13
]
), and a magnetization vector
\mathbf{M}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
to describe the magnetic properties of the ferronematic phase. In three dimensions, the full LdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor order parameter is a symmetric, traceless
3\times 3
matrix with five degrees of freedom
[
1
]
.
In two-dimensional scenarios such as ours, one can use the reduced LdG (rLdG) description for which the physically relevant
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors have a fixed eigenvector with an associated fixed eigenvalue and all quantities of interest are independent of the
z
-coordinate
[
22
]
; this automatically implies that the rLdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor has two degrees of freedom as opposed to five for the fully three-dimensional scenario. This dimension reduction from three dimensions to two dimensions can be rigorously justified by using
\Gamma
-convergence techniques
[
27
]
. We refer to
\mathbf{Q}
as the LdG order parameter, instead of the rLdG order parameter in the remainder of the text, for brevity. We define the set of matrices:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\bigl\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}:A^{T}=A,\operatorname{tr}(A)=0,A_{3i}=A_{i3}=0,\,i=1,2,3\bigl\},

and
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}
in subsequent sections. One can also write
\mathbf{Q}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=s\left(2\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbb{J}\right),

(2)

where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},0)
is the nematic director or the eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
with the largest positive eigenvalue,
s
is the scalar order parameter that measures the degree of the orientational order about
\mathbf{n}
and

\mathbb{J}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, one can label planar defects in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as being the nodal set of the scalar order parameter
s

[
22
]
.

In what follows, we measure the magnetization
\mathbf{M}=\left(M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\right):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
in units of a saturation magnetisation,
M_{s}
, so that
\mathbf{M}
is dimensionless by definition, as is the LdG order parameter.

The magnetization
\mathbf{M}
generates a non-local magnetic field which is denoted by
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and is called the stray field
[
2
]
. Following the theory of micromagnetics, we assume the material to be a non-conducting ferromagnetic material.
Here, instead
we work with an approximated stray field energy derived by Gioia and James
[
17
]
to overcome the technical difficulties by the non-locality. The approximation is a 3D to 2D reduction of the stray field energy obtained by using asymptotic analysis based on weak convergence methods. In this approximation, the stray field contributes locally to the film’s interior without any additional penalization on the film’s edges, the stray field energy is independent of the space coordinate normal to the film and there is no remnant of the stationary Maxwell equation. In the approximation by Gioia and James
[
17
]
for very thin films that we use in our two-dimensional framework, the stray field energy
-\frac{1}{2}c_{3}\xi\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\mathbf{M}\,\mathrm{d}x
is replaced by
\frac{1}{2}c_{3}\xi\int_{\Omega}(M_{3})^{2}\mathrm{d}x
.

Further, we assume that there is a constant dimensionless (re-scaled) external magnetic field denoted by
\mathbf{H}_{ext}=(H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}):\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that interacts with the nematic order parameter and the magnetization. Following
[
20
,
7
]
, we model the interaction between an external magnetic field and the nematic order parameter by the energy density

\displaystyle-\chi_{1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext},

(3)

where
\chi_{1}
is a characteristic positive material-dependent constant related to magnetic susceptibility/anisotropy of the ferronematic material.

The total ferronematic energy comprises several contributions: the LdG energy for the nematic/orientational ordering in two dimensions
[
1
,
13
]
, a Ginzburg-Landau type energy for
\mathbf{M}
including the exchange energy for the magnetization
[
2
,
14
]
, a nemato-magnetic coupling energy that describes the coupling between
\mathbf{Q}
(or the nematic director
\mathbf{n}
) and
\mathbf{M}
via the surface-induced coupling on the magnetic nanoparticles
[
8
]
, an approximated stray field energy
[
17
]
, the Zeeman energy that accounts for the interaction between
\mathbf{H}_{ext}
and
\mathbf{M}

[
2
]
and the interaction energy between
\mathbf{H}_{ext}
and
\mathbf{Q}
, as in (
3

Therefore, the total ferronematic energy is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{K}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{A}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{C}{4}|\mathbf{Q}|^{4}+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{M}|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}|\mathbf{M}|^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}|\mathbf{M}|^{4}-\frac{\gamma_{1}\mu}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}

(4)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left(M_{3}\right)^{2}-\mu\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}-\frac{\chi_{1}\mu}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}\;\mathrm{d}x.

In the above,

is referred to as the positive nematic elastic constant
[
1
]
with unit N (newton) and

[

]
, also with unit N.
The parameters

are LdG bulk coefficients typically related to the temperature and material properties, with unit N

-2

[
28
]
. More precisely,

is often interpreted to be a rescaled temperature and the bulk ferronematic system favours an ordered nematic state with
\mathbf{Q}\neq 0

, and a disordered isotropic phase with

. The coefficients

and

are Landau coefficients that describe the ferromagnetic transition

]

and the Landau potential for

), in the absence of spatial inhomogeneities. Further,
\gamma_{1}
is a constant that measures the strength of the nemato-magnetic coupling
[
7
]
and we assume that
\gamma_{1}>0
in what follows, so that it coerces co-alignment between
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{M}
(see
[
8
]
). Finally,
\mu
is the re-scaled magnetic permeability of the vacuum

14
]
. The coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\mu
have units N
\cdot
m
-2
,
while
\gamma_{1}
is unitless.

We notice that the energy (
4
) reduces to the ferronematic energy of
[
8
]
when
M_{3}=0
and
\mathbf{H}_{ext}=\mathbf{0}
i.e., for planar magnetization vectors and hence without the considered stray field energy approximation, and in the absence of external magnetic fields.

3.1
Nondimensionalization

Next, we re-scale the ferronematic energy (

).
The non-dimensional/re-scaled ferronematic energy then reads (up to a constant)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\right)=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{M}^{\prime},\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},

(5)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{M}^{\prime},\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{l_{1}}{2}|\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{\xi l_{2}}{2}|\nabla^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(|\mathbf{M}^{\prime}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{M}^{\prime}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}\xi\left(M_{3}^{\prime}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\mathbf{H}_{ext}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}^{\prime}-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}^{\prime}.

Here,
\Omega^{\prime}
is the re-scaled two-dimensional domain.
The relevant dimensionless parameters are defined to be

l_{1}=\frac{K}{2|A|L^{2}},~l_{2}=\frac{\kappa}{|\alpha|L^{2}},~\xi=\frac{C}{|A|^{2}}\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{\beta},~c_{1}=\frac{\gamma_{1}\mu}{|A|}\sqrt{\frac{C}{2|A|}}\frac{|\alpha|}{\beta},

c_{2}=\frac{\chi_{1}\mu}{|A|}\sqrt{\frac{C}{2|A|}}\frac{|\alpha|}{\beta},~c_{3}=\frac{\mu}{|\alpha|}.

We drop the primes in what follows and all subsequent results are to be understood in terms of the re-scaled variables. Hence, we obtain (
1
).

We briefly discuss the physical interpretation of the dimensionless parameters. The re-scaled elastic constants,
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are the ratios of the characteristic material-dependent length scales (e.g.,
\sqrt{\frac{K}{|A|}}
,
\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{|\alpha|}}
) and geometric length scales, such as
L
in this setting. For simplicity,
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are sometimes assumed to be equal to
l
, which is regarded as the elastic constant. The non-negative parameter
\xi
is interpreted as a measure of the relative strengths of the magnetic energy (the Ginzburg-Landau potential for
\mathbf{M}
and the exchange energy) and the LdG energy (depending on
\mathbf{Q}
and
\nabla\mathbf{Q}
). The magnetic energy is dominant for large values of
\xi
and we expect to see the nematic director profiles being tailored by
\mathbf{M}
for large
\xi
as in
[
8
]
. The parameter
c_{1}>0
is simply a re-scaled measure of the nematic-magnetic coupling strength constant
\gamma_{1}
. The parameter
c_{2}
is a measure of the relative strength of the interaction energy (
3
), i.e., this interaction energy between the NLC and the external magnetic field becomes increasingly dominant as
c_{2}
increases. In addition, we have the parameter,
c_{3}
, which accounts for the stray field energy and the Zeeman energy. Below, we elaborate on the effects of these parameters with some analytic and numerical results, although far more comprehensive studies are needed for a complete understanding.

Remark 3.1
.

The energy density in (
5
) is frame-indifferent.

4
Existence and uniqueness

In this section, we study existence and uniqueness properties of energy minimizers of the functional
\mathcal{E}
as introduced in (
1
).
For convenience, we also write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{l_{1}}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\xi l_{2}}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{M}|^{2}+f\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)+\frac{c_{3}}{2}\xi\left(M_{3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where

\displaystyle f\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}.

(8)

We define the following admissible spaces

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{\mathbf{Q}\in W^{1,2}\left(\Omega,\mathcal{Q}\right):\mathbf{Q}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{Q}_{bd}\mbox{ in the sense of traces}\Big\},

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\mathbf{M}\in W^{1,2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3}\right):\mathbf{M}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{M}_{bd}\mbox{ in the sense of traces}\right\},

\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}=\left\{\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right):\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{A}\mbox{ and }\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

for some given Lipschitz mappings
\mathbf{Q}_{bd}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}_{bd}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which define our Dirichlet boundary conditions.
We prove the existence of global minimizers of the ferronematic energy functional
\mathcal{E}
in (
1
), in the admissible space
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
, using the direct methods in the calculus of variations. Firstly, we check that
\mathcal{E}
is bounded below by a fixed constant independent of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
. To do so, it is enough to show that the coupled terms are bounded below since the other terms are non-negative.
We set
\mathcal{I}_{1}=-c_{1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}
and observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}Q_{ij}M_{i}M_{j}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{2}Q_{11}\left(M_{1}^{2}-M_{2}^{2}\right)-c_{1}Q_{12}M_{1}M_{2}~~(\mbox{since }Q_{i3}=Q_{3j}=0,Q_{12}=Q_{21},Q_{22}=-Q_{11})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{4}\left(\epsilon_{1}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}}|\mathbf{M}|^{4}\right)

for some arbitrary constant
\epsilon_{1}>0
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}\geq-\frac{c_{2}}{4}\left(\epsilon_{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{4}\right)

(10)

for some arbitrary
\epsilon_{2}>0
. Finally, note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}\geq-c_{3}\xi\left(\epsilon_{3}|\mathbf{M}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{3}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{2}\right)

(11)

for some arbitrary
\epsilon_{3}>0
.

Combining (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle f\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}

(12)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{4}\left(\epsilon_{1}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}}|\mathbf{M}|^{4}\right)-\frac{c_{2}}{4}\left(\epsilon_{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{4}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~-c_{3}\xi\epsilon_{3}|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-\frac{c_{3}\xi}{\epsilon_{3}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-\left(1+c_{1}\epsilon_{1}+c_{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\xi}{4}-\frac{c_{1}}{4\epsilon_{1}}\right)\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-\frac{\frac{\xi}{4}+\frac{c_{3}\xi\epsilon_{3}}{2}}{\frac{\xi}{4}-\frac{c_{1}}{4\epsilon_{1}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~-\frac{c_{3}\xi}{\epsilon_{3}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{2}-\frac{c_{2}}{2\epsilon_{2}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|^{4}+g\left(c_{1},c_{2},\xi,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\right),

where we choose
\epsilon_{1}>\frac{c_{1}}{\xi}
and
g
is an explicitly computable constant that is independent of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
. The lower bound of the energy functional (
1
) follows immediately due to the estimate (
12
) together with
\mathbf{H}_{ext}\in C\left(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
.
The existence result in Theorem
4.3
below then relies on the following compactness and lower-semicontinuity arguments.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathbf{H}_{ext}\in C\left(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
. Then the following compactness result holds:

If
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)\leq\Lambda
for a sequence
\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
and some
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
independent of
k
, then
\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)
has a weakly converging subsequence in
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
be an infimizing sequence for the functional
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
such that
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)<\Lambda
for some positive
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, in particular,

\int_{\Omega}\frac{l_{1}}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{k}|^{2}+\frac{l_{2}\xi}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{k}|^{2}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}\xi\left(M_{3k}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\Lambda

uniformly for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We fix
\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}},\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
. Then
\mathbf{Q}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in W^{1,2}_{0}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)
and
\mathbf{M}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\in W^{1,2}_{0}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
. Thus, Poincaré’s inequality yields:

\displaystyle||\mathbf{Q}_{k}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle||\mathbf{Q}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}+||\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}\leq C_{p}||\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{k}-\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}+\tilde{C}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(||\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{k}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}+||\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)}\right)+\tilde{C}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C

for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly, we have that
||\mathbf{M}_{k}||_{L^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}\leq C
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here
C>0
is a generic constant and
C_{p}>0
is the Poincaré constant.
Hence, the sequence
\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)_{k}
is bounded in the reflexive Banach space
W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, we can extract a subsequence without relabeling such that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)\rightharpoonup\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\mbox{ in }W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)\quad\mbox{ as }k\to\infty

(13)

for some
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M})\in W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
.
To show that the limit pair
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
satisfies the prescribed Dirichlet boundary condition, that is

\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=\left(\mathbf{Q}_{bd},\mathbf{M}_{bd}\right)\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

in the sense of traces, it is enough to note that the trace operator
\gamma:W^{1,\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Omega;~\cdot~\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\partial\Omega\right)
is compact (see e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 6.1-7(b)]
). By (
13
),
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
are bounded in
W^{1,2}
and hence in
W^{1,\frac{3}{2}}
, we obtain, up to subsequences that

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow~

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)

in

\displaystyle L^{2}\left(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)

as

\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty

,

(14)

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow~

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\mathbf{M}\right)

in

\displaystyle L^{2}\left(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)

as

\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty

.

(15)

By extracting a pointwise converging sequence, we show that

\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=\left(\mathbf{Q}_{bd},\mathbf{M}_{bd}\right)\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

in the sense of traces. Hence the limit pair,
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
, and Proposition
4.1
follows.
∎

Next, we show weak lower semicontinuity of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
in (
1
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{H}_{ext}\in C\left(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right),

(16)

when
\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)\rightharpoonup\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\mbox{ in }W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times W^{1,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)\mbox{ as }k\to\infty.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=f\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)+\frac{c_{3}}{2}\xi\left(M_{3}\right)^{2}-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}_{ext}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}-c_{3}\xi\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{ext}

and assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{G}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
is non-negative, c.f. (
12
).
By the lower semicontinuity property of the norm, we have that

\displaystyle||\nabla\mathbf{Q}||^{2}_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{k}||^{2}_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)},

(17)

\displaystyle||\nabla\mathbf{M}||^{2}_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla\mathbf{M}_{k}||^{2}_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}.

(18)

Using Rellich-Kondrašov’s embedding (see e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 6.1-5]
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{k}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{Q}

in

\displaystyle L^{q}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)

\displaystyle\quad q\in[1,\infty)

,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{k}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{M}

in

\displaystyle L^{q}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\quad q\in[1,\infty)

.

(20)

Up to a subsequence without relabelling, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{k}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega,

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{k}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega.

(22)

Using the closure property of pointwise convergence and Fatou’s lemma
[
30
]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{G}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\mathrm{d}x\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{G}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k}\right)\mathrm{d}x.

(23)

Equations (
17
), (
18
) and (
23
) collectively yield the lower semi-continuity (
16
) of the ferronematic energy (
1
).
∎

Now, we state and prove the existence theorem.

Theorem 4.3

(Existence of energy minimizers)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathbf{H}_{ext}\in C\left(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
. Then the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
given by (
1
) admits a global minimizer in the set
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Using Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
, we prove Theorem
4.3
by the direct method in the calculus of variations (see e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 2.1]
).
∎

Next, we show that the minimizer is unique for sufficiently large
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
.
The Euler-Lagrange equations associated with the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
given by (
1
) in the strong form, correspond to a vanishing
L^{2}
-gradient of
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
, i.e.,
\nabla_{L^{2}}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=0
. The
L^{2}
-gradient of the functional
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
,
\nabla_{L^{2}}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
is obtained by the Riesz representative of the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
with respect to the given inner product. A standard calculation yields

\displaystyle 2l_{1}\Delta Q_{11}

\displaystyle=Q_{11}\left(Q_{11}^{2}+Q_{12}^{2}-1\right)-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\left(M_{1}^{2}-M_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\left(H_{1}^{2}-H_{2}^{2}\right),

(24a)

\displaystyle 2l_{1}\Delta Q_{12}

\displaystyle=Q_{12}\left(Q_{11}^{2}+Q_{12}^{2}-1\right)-c_{1}M_{1}M_{2}-c_{2}H_{1}H_{2},

(24b)

\displaystyle l_{2}\xi\Delta M_{1}

\displaystyle=\xi M_{1}\left(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+M_{3}^{2}-1\right)-c_{1}\left(Q_{11}M_{1}+Q_{12}M_{2}\right)-c_{3}\xi H_{1},

(24c)

\displaystyle l_{2}\xi\Delta M_{2}

\displaystyle=\xi M_{2}\left(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+M_{3}^{2}-1\right)-c_{1}\left(Q_{12}M_{1}-Q_{11}M_{2}\right)-c_{3}\xi H_{2},

(24d)

\displaystyle l_{2}\xi\Delta M_{3}

\displaystyle=\xi M_{3}\left(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+M_{3}^{2}-1\right)-c_{3}\xi\left(H_{3}-M_{3}\right).

(24e)

As an aside we mention that the Euler-Lagrange equations are the stationary case of the gradient flow equations considered in (
49
) below.

In the following, we derive local
L^{\infty}
-bounds for
H^{1}
weak solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations (
24a
)–(
24e
). It is worth mentioning that we work with a bounded Lipschitz domain
\ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which only technically allows us to obtain a local bound for this elliptic system of PDEs.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
be an
H^{1}
weak solution of the system of PDEs (
24a
)–(
24e
), where
\mathbf{H}_{ext}\in C\left(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
with
\sup_{\bar{\Omega}}|\mathbf{H}_{ext}|\leq k_{1}
. Then the weak solution
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)
has an interior local bound in terms of the positive parameters,
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},

k_{1}
and
\xi
, i.e., for any compact set
K\subset\Omega
, there exists
\sigma=\sigma\left(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\xi,k_{1}\right)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |Q_{11}(z)|&\leq\sigma~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\forall z\in K,\\
|Q_{12}(z)|&\leq\sigma~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\forall z\in K,\\
|\mathbf{M}(z)|^{2}&\leq Ac_{1}\frac{\sigma}{\xi}+Bc_{3}k_{1}+1~~\forall z\in K,\end{aligned}

(25)

for some positive constants
A
and
B
independent of
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\xi,l_{1},l_{2},k_{1}
.

Proof.

By the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,2}\left(\Omega\right)\hookrightarrow L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)~\left(1\leq q<\infty\right)
(see e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 6.1-3]
),
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\in L^{q}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times L^{q}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
for
1\leq q<\infty
. Applying the elliptic regularity theorem (c.f.
[
31
]
, sec. 6.3.1), we obtain that
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\in W^{2,2}_{loc}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times W^{2,2}_{loc}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
. Then by the Sobolev embedding
W^{2,2}\left(\Omega\right)\hookrightarrow C^{0}\left(\overline{\Omega}\right)

[
29
, Theorem 6.1-3]
and continuing boot-strapping, we have
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)\in C^{k}_{loc}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\right)\times C^{k}_{loc}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
for
k=0,1,2,\ldots
. Thus, we can assume that
|\mathbf{Q}|=\sqrt{2\left(Q_{11}^{2}+Q_{12}^{2}\right)}
and
|\mathbf{M}|=\sqrt{M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}+M_{3}^{2}}
attain their maxima on a compact set
K\subset\Omega
. Suppose that
|\mathbf{Q}|
attains its maximum at
z_{1}\in K
and
|\mathbf{M}|
attains its maximum at
z_{2}\in K
. Then the symmetric Hessian
D^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\right)
is negative semi-definite at
z_{1}
, and the symmetric Hessian
D^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{M}|^{2}\right)
is negative semi-definite at
z_{2}
. Consequently, we have

\Delta\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\right)\left(z_{1}\right)\leq 0\mbox{ and }\Delta\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{M}|^{2}\right)(z_{2})\leq 0.

To proceed, we multiply (
24a
) by
Q_{11}
and (
24b
) by
Q_{12}
, and add the resulting two equations. We then make use of the following identity
\Delta\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\right)=2Q_{11}\Delta Q_{11}+2Q_{12}\Delta Q_{12}+|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\leq 0,

and get

\displaystyle\bigg\{\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-1\right)\frac{|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}}{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}Q_{11}(M_{1}^{2}-M_{2}^{2})-c_{1}Q_{12}M_{1}M_{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{2}}{2}Q_{11}\left(H_{1}^{2}-H_{2}^{2}\right)-c_{2}Q_{12}H_{1}H_{2}\bigg\}\bigg|_{z=z_{1}}\leq 0.

(26)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg\{\xi\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-c_{1}M_{1}\left(Q_{11}M_{1}+Q_{12}M_{2}\right)-c_{1}M_{2}\left(Q_{12}M_{1}-Q_{11}M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle-c_{3}\xi\left(H_{1}M_{1}+H_{2}M_{2}+H_{3}M_{3}\right)+c_{3}\xi M_{3}^{2}\bigg\}\bigg|_{z=z_{2}}\leq 0.

(27)

Using the substitutions

\displaystyle Q_{11}

\displaystyle=\tau\cos\left(\theta\right),Q_{12}=\tau\sin\left(\theta\right),

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma\cos\left(\phi\right),M_{2}=\gamma\sin\left(\phi\right)

(28)

for arbitrary
\theta,\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, where
\sqrt{2}\tau=|\mathbf{Q}|>0
and
\gamma=\sqrt{M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}}>0
in (
4
), it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq

5
Numerical approach

We now define the numerical method and setup for our numerical experiments in the next section. We take the re-scaled domain
\Omega
to be such that:

\Omega=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0\leq x,y\leq 1\right\}.

We use the following energy dissipation law
[
32

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\eta_{1}|\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\eta_{2}|\partial_{t}\mathbf{M}|^{2}\right),

(48)

where
\eta_{1}>0
and
\eta_{2}>0
are arbitrary friction coefficients
[
10

that control the energy dissipation. The

-gradient flow equations for the ferronematic energy (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \eta_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}\\
Q_{12}\end{pmatrix}&=2l_{1}\Delta\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}\\
Q_{12}\end{pmatrix}-\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}-1\right)\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}\\
Q_{12}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{c_{1}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}^{2}-M_{2}^{2}\\
2M_{1}M_{2}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}^{2}-H_{2}^{2}\\
2H_{1}H_{2}\end{pmatrix},\\
\eta_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}\\
M_{2}\\
M_{3}\end{pmatrix}&=\xi l_{2}\Delta\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}\\
M_{2}\\
M_{3}\end{pmatrix}-\xi\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}\\
M_{2}\\
M_{3}\end{pmatrix}+c_{1}\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}M_{1}+Q_{12}M_{2}\\
Q_{12}M_{1}-Q_{11}M_{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+c_{3}\xi\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}\\
H_{2}\\
H_{3}-M_{3}\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\right.

(49)

We complete the system of PDEs (
49
) by the continuous
+k
degree boundary and initial conditions where

measures the number of rotations by the director or by the magnetization around the boundary. We set
\theta\left(x,y\right)=\operatorname{arctan2}\left(y-0.5,x-0.5\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(Q^{b}_{11},Q^{b}_{12}\right)&=\left(\cos(2k\theta),\sin(2k\theta)\right),\\

(51)

The choice of boundary condition is motivated by
[
10

on the boundary.
To define a suitable boundary condition, (inspired by
[

) we let

for some

and redefine the bulk potential

\displaystyle f_{\xi}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}}{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(|\mathbf{M}|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\beta\xi\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}+k_{\xi}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(Q_{11}^{2}+Q_{12}^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{\xi}{4}\left(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\beta\xi\sum_{i,j=1,2}Q_{ij}M_{i}M_{j}+k_{\xi},

, and satisfies

as

and

For any

5.1
Spatial and temporal discretization

The time-independent or equilibrium solutions to the
L^{2}
-gradient flow system, are solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations associated with (
5
). From the numerical point of view, solving the
L^{2}
-gradient flow system is more straightforward than solving a coupled Euler–Lagrange system, primarily due to the inclusion of time relaxation that allows us to use the Crank–Nicolson finite difference method
[
21
,
33
]
as outlined below.
We discretise the unit square
\Omega
with a spatial grid of
N
points in the
x
-direction and
N
points in the
y
-direction. We assume that each point in the 2D grid is represented by
x_{i}=i*\delta x
and
y_{j}=j*\delta y
with equal step sizes
\delta x
,
\delta y
and spatial indices
i,j

(i,j=1,2,\ldots,N)
. Similarly, we represent temporal grids by
t_{n}=n*\delta t
with step size
\delta t
and temporal index
n

(n=0,1,2,\ldots)
. Therefore, the numerical solution of the system of PDEs, at a given spatial point and temporal point, can be denoted by
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}
.

5.2
Crank–Nicolson method

To obtain the working formula of the Crank–Nicolson method, we introduce the following forward and central finite differences
[
21
,
33
]
to replace the partial derivatives in the system of PDEs with their discrete versions:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}\approx\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{ij}-\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}}{\delta t}

and

\partial_{xx}\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}\approx\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i-1j}-2\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i+1j}}{\delta x^{2}};~\partial_{yy}\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}\approx\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij-1}-2\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij+1}}{\delta y^{2}}.

Here,
\mathcal{Z}
is the prototype of the variables
Q_{11},Q_{12},M_{1},M_{2}
and
M_{3}
. In the general setting, the gradient flow system (
49
) can be written as

\displaystyle\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}}{\partial t}=l\Delta\mathcal{Z}+f\left(\mathcal{Z}\right),

(53)

where
\eta\in\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\}
and
l\in\{2l_{1},l_{2}\xi\}
.

The finite difference-based Crank–Nicolson discretization of (
53
) yields:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \eta\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{ij}-\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}}{\delta t}=\frac{l}{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{i-1j}-2\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{ij}+\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{i+1j}}{\delta x^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i-1j}-2\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i+1j}}{\delta x^{2}}\right)\\
+l\left(\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij-1}-2\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij+1}}{\delta y^{2}}\right)+f(\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{ij}).\end{aligned}

(54)

We implement the finite difference scheme along the
x
-direction i.e., the derivative in the
x
-direction is computed as the average of two time steps at the
\left(n+1\right)
th and
n
th time-steps, while the derivative in the
y
-direction is discretized at the
n
th time step. To linearize the nonlinear terms, we employ Newton’s linearization technique which involves the substitution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}_{ij}=\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij},

(55)

where
\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}
labels the error committed at each grid point in two consecutive approximations (i.e., at the
(n+1)
th and
n
th time steps). Since the error
\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}
is very small, terms involving the square and higher powers of
\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}
are disregarded. The resulting system of algebraic equations leads to a tri-diagonal system of the form

a_{i}\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i-1j}+b_{i}\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}+c_{i}\delta\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{i+1j}=d_{i}(\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}),

where
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
d_{i}
are known from the solutions at the
n
th time step. The system of algebraic equations is solved for
\delta\mathcal{Z}_{ij}^{n}
by employing the Tri-diagonal matrix algorithm
[
21
]
. Once we determine the error at each grid point
\left(i,j\right)
, the solution is improved at the
\left(n+1\right)
th step by equation (
55
). We refer to a complete implementation of the algorithm as an iteration
and we iterate until convergence is achieved.
At each fixed
n
, we obtain a sequence of approximations
\{(\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij})^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
that converge to a solution until

\left\|\left(\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}\right)^{(k+1)}-\left(\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{ij}\right)^{(k)}\right\|<\epsilon\left(=10^{-6}<\mathcal{O}\left(\delta x^{2}+\delta t^{2}\right)\right)

is satisfied.

7
Conclusions

Our study is based on a ferronematic energy that comprises contributions from the Landau-de Gennes’ theory of nematic liquid crystals and the theory of micromagnetics. In particular, we include a stray field energy contribution and energy contributions from external magnetic fields, and hence generalize the ferronematic model in
[
8
]
. Our numerical results concerning the influence of the stray field and the external magnetic field on ferronematics in two-dimensional geometries
suggest that additional magnetic nanoparticles can substantially affect observable nematic profiles: their director profiles, defect sets, stability etc. and hence, they can affect concomitant optical, mechanical and rheological properties too.
In this context, we refer to the experimental works
[
6
,
7
]
that report that optical properties of manufactured ferronematics can be instantly controlled by using a weak strength external magnetic field (up to 1 mT); under an alternating magnetic field, such ferronematics exhibit an optical switching frequency above 100 Hz, which is comparable to commercial liquid crystal devices based on electrical switching. Thus, the inclusion of the stray field in our study will be more effective for such applications that demand weak-strength external magnetic fields.

However, our observations are of qualitative nature.
For example, we have not used physically motivated values for the parameters
l_{1}^{\prime}=2l_{1},l_{2},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\xi
in our numerical results.
Such values are for instance reported in

[
34
,
28
,
39
]
.
We do not comment on this further here because our purpose is to illustrate the rich solution landscapes of ferronematics in two dimensions and give examples of how these landscapes can be tuned by means of
\xi,c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
and the re-scaled elastic constants.

There are open questions about the choice of physically pertinent boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}
. Dirichlet conditions for
\mathbf{Q}
are widely used in theoretical studies and are physically relevant e.g.,
[
34
,
22
,
35
,
40
]
. However, it may not be experimentally feasible or practical to prescribe
\mathbf{M}
on the boundary, especially when
\mathbf{M}
is induced by suspended magnetic nanoparticles. In such cases, natural or Neumann boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}
might be more appropriate, with
M_{3}=0
. For example, natural boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}
on a square domain would reduce to

\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}}{\partial x}=0
on
x=0,1

and

\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}}{\partial y}=0
on
y=0,1.

For Neumann boundary conditions, we conjecture that the effects of geometric frustration would be lost for the
\mathbf{M}
profiles and the
\mathbf{M}
profiles would be largely tailored by
\mathbf{Q}
profiles and the external fields. For example, we might still get the interior magnetic defects induced by the interior nematic defects in Figures
8
and
9
, but the topological degrees of the magnetic defects and their locations might be different for Neumann as opposed to Dirichlet boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}
. This is simply because the system is less constrained with Neumann boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}
. We do not comment further but acknowledge that there are several physically relevant generalisations of our work.

Our mathematical and numerical study of a complex multi-physics system with multiple order parameters yields mathematical and some practical insight into the relevance of stray field energies and external magnetic fields for quasi two-dimensional ferronematics that can contribute to future experimental and theoretical studies in the same vein.
