Carbon- and boron-based nano-inclusions for thermoelectric nanocomposites: Matrix matching and interface design
Authors: Al Jumlat Ahmed, Yannan Lu, Guangsai Yang, Xizu Wang, Xiaolin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNanocomposite engineering offers a route to improve thermoelectric (TE) performance by modifying the coupled transport of charge carriers and phonons. This review examines carbon- and boron-based nanoinclusions across bismuth chalcogenides, Cu2Se, oxide matrices, and flexible polymer- and film-based thermoelectrics, with emphasis on inclusion chemistry, dimensionality, loading, processing route, and interface engineering. Bismuth chalcogenides generally require a narrow loading window because additional interfaces can degrade carrier mobility, whereas Cu2Se more readily accommodates such interfaces and has high zT values > 2. In oxides, improved electrical transport is often insufficient to offset high lattice thermal conductivity, while flexible systems require consideration of power factor, mechanical durability, and device output. Cross-system comparison shows that effective nanoinclusion design depends on balancing phonon suppression with carrier transport and interfacial stability, providing practical guidance for thermoelectric materials and device development.

Introduction

Thermoelectric devices are solid-state devices that can convert heat into electricity and vice versa. These devices have a range of applications, including power generation for portable devices1 and biomedical sensors,2 solid-state refrigerators, or thermal management for microelectronics and medical devices.3 The efficiency of a thermoelectric (TE) device is defined as the combination of its Carnot efficiency (ΔT/Thot) and the TE material figure of merit (zT), given by4,5(Equation 1)η=ΔTThot×1+zTavg−11+zTavg+TcoldThotwhere Thot and Tcold are the temperatures of the hot and cold ends of the device, respectively. ΔT is the temperature difference between the two ends. Tavg is the average temperature of the device. The equation indicates that a large average zT value over a wide temperature range is required to increase efficiency. The zT is mainly determined by material transport properties, given by6(Equation 2)zT=S2σTkwhere S, σ, κ, and T are the Seebeck coefficient (μV·K−1), the electrical conductivity (S·m−1), the thermal conductivity (W·m−1·K−1), and the absolute temperature (K), respectively. The term S2σ is called the power factor. Theoretical studies show that TE materials require a zT value greater than 1 for practical applications.7 To realize a high zT value, the TE material needs a large S to generate high thermal voltage, high σ to minimize internal resistance and Joule heating, and low κ to maintain the temperature difference between the ends of the material.7,8

This complex interdependence of thermoelectric properties in terms of carrier concentration makes it difficult to optimize the performance of thermoelectric materials. The TE parameters are complexly intercoupled, posing a challenge in maximizing the zT value and conversion efficiency. For instance, σ and S show a trade-off relationship9 that is affected by the carrier concentration and effective mass, and the electron thermal conductivity (ke) and σ are limited by the Wiedemann-Franz law, Ke = LTσ, where L is the Lorenz number.

In recent years, various approaches have been adopted to optimize these critical TE parameters, such as power factor enhancement via band structure engineering and reducing phonon thermal conductivity through nano-structuring and nanoinclusions. One of the approaches is the introduction of nanostructures in bulk TE materials, which leads to high zT by reducing kL due to phonon-interface scattering.10,11,12 It was reported that the zT of Half-Heuslers alloys can be improved by reducing grain size to 100–300 nm, which increases phonon scattering by grain boundaries.13 However, the effect of nano grain on electrical conductivity was not discussed in the article. In another case, Jood et al. reported that the thermal conductivity of Al-doped ZnO nanocomposite with ZnAl2O4 nanoprecipitates (nano grain) was significantly reduced with an increase in Al doping, though the electrical conductivity was also reduced after 0.025 at% Al doping.14

Nanocomposite thermoelectric materials with inclusions in different formats have shown the potential to suppress the lattice thermal conductivity and can improve the figure of merit; however, these strategies may often face the challenges of the interdependency of electrical and thermal conductivity. In most cases, these nano inclusions effectively reduce the lattice thermal conductivity through phonon scattering, but they also tend to degrade the electrical conductivity by scattering charge carriers. In addition, these methods have some limitations, such as chemical instability. Wang’s group reported for the first time that a nanocomposite of Bi2Te3 with graphene can improve the figure of merit, by 1.5 times by suppressing thermal conductivity as well as by improving electrical conductivity.15 Subsequently, other research groups have also adopted a similar approach and reported high-performance TE materials. The concept of nanocomposite has been developed based on specific selection criteria: a) the inclusion should be nano-size to scatter phonons effectively, and b) it should provide sufficient phonon scattering without an unacceptable sacrifice of carrier transport. c) it should be chemically inert to the thermoelectric material matrix to prevent any chemical reactions; d) a sharp interface between the nano inclusion and base material is crucial to avoid any reactions between them; e) Low thermal conductivity of the nanoinclusion is desirable. f) Lastly, the inclusion should be a low-cost material that can be easily incorporated with the base material. In this review article, we provide a comprehensive summary and analysis of related articles published by our group and other research groups. Additionally, we offer insights into the future directions of thermoelectric research to further enhance performance.

Design considerations for carbon- and boron-based nanoinclusions

The successful incorporation of carbon- or boron-based nanoinclusions into a thermoelectric matrix demands careful attention to inclusion chemistry, dimensionality, loading, processing route, and interface chemistry. These experimental choices collectively determine whether the inclusion will function as an effective phonon scatterer, a benign electronic component, or a detrimental second phase.

Inclusion chemistry and dimensionality

The dimensionality of a carbon inclusion strongly influences both phonon scattering and the probability of forming an electrically conductive pathway through the composite. Zero-dimensional carbon dots provide a high density of localized interfaces and can introduce energy-dependent carrier transmission. One-dimensional carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and carbon fibers can bridge grains or, at sufficient loading, form percolating networks that dramatically alter the electrical transport. Two-dimensional graphene and reduced graphene oxide offer large interfacial contact areas and high in-plane electrical conductivity, making them especially effective at modifying both thermal and electronic properties simultaneously. Three-dimensional networks can improve mechanical continuity but risk creating continuous thermal paths that partially offset the phonon-blocking benefit.16

For boron-containing inclusions, the design space is different. Amorphous boron can act as a grain refiner and generate defect-rich regions that scatter phonons across multiple length scales. Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) is chemically and mechanically robust but electrically insulating. Carbon-coated boron particles represent a deliberate interface-engineering strategy: the carbon shell improves wetting and electrical compatibility with the matrix, while the boron-rich core provides mass and elastic contrast for phonon scattering. The electrical and thermaltransport is expected to be decoupled via the hierarchical microstructures, which significantly minimize the thermal conductivity through multiple phonon scattering mechanisms and simultaneously enhance the power factor due to the optimization of electrical transport.

Loading

Across the thermoelectric matrices reviewed here, the useful loading range of carbon or boron inclusions is remarkably narrow, typically lying below approximately 2 wt %. In Bi2Te3, single-wall CNTs was optimal within a 0.5 wt % series; in graphene/Bi-Sb-Te composites, optima are reported from approximately 0.05 wt % to 0.4 vol %; in Cu2Se, optimal loadings include 0.3 wt % carbon fiber, 0.8–2 wt % carbon dots, and 0.45 wt % graphene nanoplates.17,18,19,20,21,22,23 These values are not directly interchangeable because weight percent and volume percent encode different interface densities, and the inclusions differ markedly in aspect ratio, surface chemistry, and aggregation tendency. Below a connectivity threshold, a conductive inclusion may improve grain-to-grain transport without dominating S. Near percolation, σ can increase sharply; however, beyond it, agglomerates reduce effective interface area, create carrier-scattering constrictions, or increase κ through connected carbon paths.

Processing methodologies

The method by which the nanoinclusion is introduced into the matrix is as important as the loading. Ex situ incorporation offers direct control over nominal composition, but the final interface is highly sensitive to mixing and consolidation. Ball milling is a very common method to mix solid powders. Agglomeration, surface oxidation, weak interfacial contact, and inclusion damage during milling can all obscure the intrinsic effect of the additive. Ag nanowires in Bi2Te3, as a one-dimensional interface comparator, suppressed grain growth and generated additional phase boundaries, showing that inclusion geometry and consolidation-induced microstructure cannot be considered independently.24 Mechanical alloying of Bi2Te2.7Se0.3/graphene illustrates a trade-off: short milling preserved graphene crystallinity but produced poor dispersion, whereas prolonged milling improved dispersion at the cost of structural disorder; adding 0.05 wt % graphene during the final 10 min gave the best reported compromise.25 High-pressure synthesis, melt spinning, hot pressing, and spark-plasma sintering (SPS) likewise alter grain size, relative density, texture, defect chemistry, and contact resistance.26,27 Consequently, performance differences attributed to the inclusion may partly originate from the consolidation route and should be evaluated against process-matched controls.

In situ growth or precipitation can produce cleaner, more conformal interfaces and reduce the agglomeration associated with ex situ mixing. Ag2+ySe grown directly on SWCNTs achieved a power factor of 19.36 μW cm−1 K−2 at 300 K and a flexible-generator output of 0.72 μW at ΔT = 30 K, although the absence of thermal-conductivity data prevents a complete zT assessment.28 In situ YSi2 in SiGe and Ag nanodomains in PbS provide useful comparators: chemically integrated interfaces can suppress phonon transport while retaining or enhancing carrier conduction, but precipitate size, composition, and coherency become strongly dependent on reaction temperature and atmosphere.29,30 It is necessary to separate the effect of interface formation from concurrent changes in matrix stoichiometry, phase purity, and densification. In this case, only a few sintering technologies are able to maintain the resulting morphology, such as SPS or cold sintering. The next section will discuss interface engineering.

Interface engineering

The inclusion can create a secondary ohmic contact, a Schottky-like barrier, an insulating interlayer, or a reacted phase depending on work-function alignment, defects, surface oxygen, and processing atmosphere. Oxygen-mediated bonding promoted CNT dispersion in Bi2Te3, whereas B at Bi2Te3 grain boundaries altered Te-vacancy energetics. In SrTiO3, reduced graphene oxide or a graphene-assisted reducing atmosphere modified double-Schottky barriers and oxygen-vacancy populations.31,32,33,34 These examples demonstrate why the term “interface engineering” should always specify the chemical nature of the interface, the sign and approximate magnitude of any potential barrier, and the transport regime that is affected. Deliberate interface engineering strategies include oxygen-mediated functionalization to improve dispersion, carbon coating to adjust work-function alignment and inhibit chemical reactions, and the insertion of a thin intermediate layer that provides phonon contrast while maintaining electronic transparency. Energy-selective transport is one possible consequence of a deliberately designed barrier, but only within an appropriate energy range. A barrier that is too low is non-selective, whereas an excessively high barrier suppresses σ and μ. An increase in S accompanied by a decrease in κ therefore does not establish energy filtering, which will be discussed in matrix-dependent thermoelectric performance.

Transport mechanisms

Nanoinclusions influence thermoelectric transport through several distinct physical mechanisms. Correctly identifying which mechanism is dominant in a given composite is essential, because different mechanisms place different constraints on the design of the inclusion and the interface. In this review, several mechanisms are considered: multiscale phonon scattering and interfacial thermal resistance, changes in carrier concentration and mobility, energy filtering, and percolation or other competing transport effects.

Phonon scattering

Carbon- and boron-based inclusions can reduce lattice heat transport across multiple length scales. Point defects predominantly scatter shorter-wavelength phonons, dislocations and strain fields affect intermediate wavelengths, and grain or phase boundaries act on longer-wavelength modes. Additional suppression may arise from acoustic-impedance mismatch or a mismatch in phonon density of states across an interface. BiSbTe/carbon-microfibre composites provide comparatively strong support for an interfacial-scattering interpretation. The calculated phonon density of states of the matrix and carbon fiber differs markedly across the measured frequency range, while the composite microstructure introduces grain boundaries, defects, and carbon-matrix interfaces at multiple length scales.35 The spectral mismatch makes low phonon transmission across the interface, but it does not constitute a direct measurement of thermal-boundary conductance. As illustrated in Figure 1, the low-frequency phonon states are concentrated in the BiSbTe matrix, whereas the carbon fiber contributes a broader distribution at higher frequencies; this limited spectral overlap, together with multiscale scattering centers, provides a mechanistic basis for the reduced κL.35 The evidence is therefore stronger than a simple correlation between added inclusions and lower κ. Recent modeling of Bi2Te3/graphene interfaces provides a quantitative complement to this interpretation. Carrier concentration and mobility

The addition of even a small quantity of a carbon phase (0.05–2 wt %) can change both the carrier concentration and the carrier mobility of the host thermoelectric matrix. Carbon is chemically flexible, and it can act as an electron donor, an electron acceptor, or a passive conductive filler depending on its surface chemistry and the matrix. An electrical-conductivity increase cannot be assigned to “better carrier transport” without separating n and μ. In Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3/graphene, σ increased because the rise in n outweighed a smaller μ loss; in melt-spun Bi0.36Sb1.64Te3/rGO, the power factor rose by 20% and was attributed to higher μ; in undoped SrTiO3/rGO, both n and μ reportedly increased as the grain-boundary barrier weakened.21,27,32 These distinct outcomes should not be collapsed into a single “carrier modulation” mechanism. Graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide carry oxygen-containing functional groups (carboxyl, hydroxyl, epoxide). In a chalcogenide matrix such as Bi2Te3, these groups can act as electron acceptors, p-doping the matrix and raising the hole concentration. Conversely, heavily reduced graphene or pristine graphene can donate electrons into an n-type matrix, increasing the electron concentration. In Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3/graphene, the observed increase in σ was driven primarily by a rise in n that outweighed a small mobility penalty, consistent with graphene acting as a hole-dopant. The magnitude of this charge transfer is governed by the work-function difference between the carbon and the matrix, which can be altered by the degree of oxidation of the carbon surface. This is why two graphene-based composites with nominally the same matrix can exhibit opposite trends in charge carrier concentration when different graphene precursors are used. In some matrices, the inclusion changes the formation energy of native point defects without being a direct dopant itself. In n-type Bi2Te3, amorphous boron that segregates to grain boundaries raises the formation energy of tellurium vacancies, which are donor-like. The vacancy concentration falls, reducing the background electron concentration and thereby increasing the Seebeck coefficient. The inclusion thus acts as a defect-chemistry modifier rather than a donor or acceptor.

When an interfacial potential barrier is introduced for energy-filtering purposes, it preferentially scatters low-energy carriers. Because the remaining high-energy carriers have a longer mean free path, the Seebeck coefficient increases. An interfacial filtering barrier typically introduces a mobility penalty, the magnitude of which depends on barrier height, barrier width, interface density, and the carrier-energy distribution. The design challenge is to keep the barrier height within a few kBT so that the gain in S outweighs the loss in μ.11 This will be further discussed in the following subsection.

Energy filtering, percolation, and competing transport effects

Energy filtering aims to preferentially scatter lower-energy carriers so that the average transport energy and thus S increases, while useful high-energy carriers retain sufficient mobility. Its benefit is determined by the balance between the Seebeck gain and the conductivity sacrifice. Carbon dots in Cu2Se and insulating nanoparticles in Bi-Sb-Te exhibit increased S at selected loadings, but most studies infer filtering from transport trends rather than directly measuring the interfacial potential.19,20,37,38 Percolation and interface formation can improve σ while simultaneously reducing S, increasing κ, or degrading mobility. Carbon fiber in CoSb3 increased σ without improving zT; graphene in MnTe reduced both power factor and κ by approximately 34% at 600 K, leaving zT essentially unchanged; and BN in Bi-Sb-Te lowered total κ partly because σ and therefore κe also decreased.39,40 These examples show why phonon suppression or electrical-conductivity enhancement alone is not a sufficient success criterion. At a fixed temperature and comparable density, zT increases only when the fractional improvement in S2σ exceeds any increase in κ, or when the fractional reduction in κ is greater than the accompanying loss in S2σ. The most consistently supported trend is that additional interfaces tend to reduce κL, whereas the direction and magnitude of the electrical response depend on whether those interfaces alter n, μ, effective mass, connectivity, or contact resistance. The contradictory results reported for graphene/Bi-Sb-Te can therefore be understood as matrix- and process-dependent outcomes of loading, stoichiometry, graphene disorder, dispersion, grain size, and atmosphere rather than as evidence for a universal graphene mechanism. The resulting design principle is to engineer interfaces that are phonon-opaque yet electronically transparent or selectively transmissive before attributing a zT increase to transport decoupling. This framework is applied to individual host matrices in matrix-dependent thermoelectric performance.

Matrix-dependent thermoelectric performance

In most cases, the performance of thermoelectric materials incorporated with carbon or boron has shown improvement, primarily attributed to two factors: the reduction of lattice thermal conductivity resulting from the inclusion of carbon between grain boundaries and the enhancement of electrical conductivity due to the highly conducting nature of the carbon inclusions.16 The thermoelectric effect of a nanoinclusion cannot be evaluated independently of the host matrix. Similar carbon- or boron-based inclusions may enhance carrier transport in one matrix, introduce mobility-limiting interfaces in another, or primarily affect lattice thermal transport in a third. This section therefore compares nanoinclusion strategies within individual thermoelectric matrices, with emphasis on the balance among power-factor modification, lattice-thermal-conductivity suppression, interface mechanisms, and practical stability.

Bismuth chalcogenide matrices

Bismuth chalcogenides are among the most established and commercialized near-room-temperature thermoelectric materials. Their lattice thermal conductivity is already relatively low, whereas their electrical transport is highly sensitive to composition, grain-boundary chemistry, and carrier scattering. Nanoinclusions therefore need to introduce additional phonon-scattering centers without disrupting the anisotropic carrier-transport pathways or excessively reducing mobility. Within bismuth chalcogenide matrices, carbon- and boron-based inclusions improve thermoelectric performance through distinct but partially overlapping transport pathways.

Early studies of carbon nanotube-Bi2Te3 composites showed that low CNT loadings can increase the Seebeck coefficient and suppress thermal conductivity, although the corresponding changes in carrier concentration and mobility were not always fully resolved due to the intrinsic trade-off. Wang’s group reported that Bi2Te3 samples doped with varying concentrations (ranging from 0 to 5 wt. %) of single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) were fabricated using a solid-state reaction. The sample with 0.5 wt. % SWCNTs showed a significant enhancement in the Seebeck coefficient, reaching as high as −231.8 μV/K, while also showing a reduction in thermal conductivity. As a result, the zT of the optimum SWCNT-doped Bi2Te3 showed a remarkable increase of 25%–40% across a wide temperature range when compared to the undoped sample.17 Our group also investigated the effect of incorporating carbon microfibers on the thermoelectric and mechanical performance of n-type Bi2Te2.7Se0.3 (BTS) materials. This inclusion effectively reduced the lattice thermal conductivity due to phonon scattering at multi-scale boundaries as well as the introduction of a large interfacial thermal resistance arising from phonon mismatch between the constituent phases. Simultaneously, it also gives rise to an enhancement of the electrical conductivity, which originates from the increased carrier density without significantly limiting its weighted mobility. Consequently, we achieved a high peak zT of 1.1 at 400 K in the temperature range 300–550 K, corresponding to a calculated conversion efficiency of η = 9%.41

Kim et al.31 reported that the incorporation of carbon nanotubes into Bi2Te3 (CNTs/Bi2Te3) composites resulted in a microstructure characterised by a homogeneous dispersion of CNTs within the Bi2Te3 matrix. This was achieved by interfacial bonding agents, with oxygen atoms attaching to the surface of CNTs. The dimensionless figure of merit of the composite shows significantly increased values compared to those of pure binary Bi2Te3 in the temperature range of 298–498 K, and a maximum zT of 0.85 was obtained at 473 K. The improved thermoelectric performance of the composite mainly originated from the reduction of thermal conductivity due to effective phonon scattering at the CNT/Bi2Te3 interface. A more recent work on Bi2Te3/MWCNT composites has continued to demonstrate the strong dependence of thermoelectric transport on carbon-nanotube incorporation, which was optimized at 1.5 vol %.42 In another study, it was demonstrated that the addition of silicon carbide (SiC) nanoparticles into the bismuth antimony telluride (BiSbTe) matrix effectively enhanced its thermoelectric properties. By incorporating only 0.4 vol % SiC nanoparticles into Bi0.3Sb1.7Te3, a high zT value of up to 1.33 at 373 K was obtained. The SiC nanoinclusions, which exhibited coherent interfaces with the Bi0.3Sb1.7Te3 matrix, contributed to an increase in the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity, while simultaneously reducing lattice thermal conductivity through enhanced phonon scattering.43 Composites incorporating CNTs, SWCNTs, and carbon microfibers show simultaneous gains in Seebeck coefficient, reduced thermal conductivity, and enhanced zT. However, these benefits are highly dependent on uniform dispersion and well-controlled interfaces. Poor distribution or excessive carbon content can introduce carrier scattering, reducing electrical performance. Moreover, many studies lack detailed analysis of carrier transport mechanisms, particularly the balance between carrier concentration and mobility. Thus, while carbon inclusions offer a promising route to partially decouple thermal and electrical transport in Bi2Te3 systems, precise control of microstructure and interface properties remains essential for consistent performance enhancement.

Graphene-based inclusions exhibit an even stronger loading and processing dependence because their high electrical and thermal conductivities can be either beneficial or detrimental depending on dispersion, interfacial contact, and matrix stoichiometry. Li et al. conducted a study on nanocomposites of Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 (BiSbTe) with graphene nanosheets (GNs). The incorporation of GNs led to a remarkable increase in electrical conductivity at a low content of graphene nanosheets, as the reduction in carrier mobility was significantly less than the increase in carrier concentration. Simultaneously, graphene nanosheets cause a 20–30% reduction in thermal conductivity owing to phonon blocking by nanosheets as well as phase boundaries. As a result, high zT values of up to 1.29 (300 K) and 1.54 (440 K) are obtained in Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 when incorporating with only 0.3 vol. % and 0.4 vol. % graphene nanosheets, respectively.21 Zhang et al. also reported that polycrystalline Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 composites with varying graphene content (x = 0, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 wt %) synthesized through high-pressure-and-high-temperature (HPHT) methods. At low graphene content (≤0.05 wt %), the addition of graphene exhibited a doping effect and phonon scattering, leading to a reduction in the Seebeck coefficient, electrical resistivity, and lattice thermal conductivity. However, this trend reversed as the graphene content continuously increased to x = 0.1 wt %. The doping effect disappeared, generating an increased Seebeck coefficient and electrical resistivity. The lattice thermal conductivity appeared to be enhanced by a volume effect, although it remained lower than that of the pristine sample. The highest achieved zT value was 1.26 at 423 K for the Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 sample with 0.05 wt % graphene.22 A similar study also shows that the synergistic optimization of electrical and lattice properties resulted in a significant enhancement of the zT value, reaching 1.25 at 320 K for the sample with 0.05 wt % graphene.23 In layered Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 composite with 0.5 and 1.0 vol % of graphene nanoplates were processed by the pressure assisted high frequency induction heated sintering. With the addition of graphene, the electrical conductivity of the composites degraded somewhat gradually, while the thermal conductivity was reduced, which was attributed to increased phonon scattering by the enormous nanostructured phase boundaries and graphene. The enhanced Seebeck coefficient, accompanied by the reduction in thermal conductivity, leads to a high zT of 1.2 at ∼ 375 K for 0.5 vol % graphene composite.26 The reduced graphene oxide (RGO) incorporated Bi0.36Sb1.64Te3 composites were prepared by a melt spinning process. This method enables the uniform distribution of RGO within the Bi0.36Sb1.64Te3 matrix. The incorporated RGO increased the carrier mobility, leading to an enhancement of the power factor by 20%. Additionally, the presence of RGO exerted a considerable effect on the grain sizes of Bi0.36Sb1.64Te3, thereby promoting grain boundary scattering. Owing to the above effects, the optimum zT value reached to 1.16 at 393 K.27 The large-area graphene flakes with negligible Raman disorder mode were prepared by the microwave-induced expansion of graphite intercalation compounds. The addition of expanded graphene (0.1 vol %) in p-type Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 improved both carrier concentration and electrical conductivity of composites due to the high intrinsic p-type carrier concentration of graphene itself. Besides, the lattice thermal conductivity of the composites was significantly reduced due to phonon scattering at the phase boundaries, despite the inherent high thermal conductivity of graphene. The increased carrier concentration also suppressed the bipolar conduction, resulting in a moderate increase in power factor and a slight decrease in bipolar thermal conductivity at elevated temperatures.44 The compositing of nanostructured graphene with Bi2Te3 powder and nanowires (NWs) resulted in composites with a graphene content of 0.5 wt. %, leading to noticeable improvements in power factors. This enhancement can be attributed to an improved Seebeck coefficient in the samples, which outweighed the reduction in electrical conductivity. Furthermore, the incorporation of graphene into the composites resulted in a reduction of the lattice thermal conductivity, primarily due to the formation of numerous boundaries within the Bi2Te3 matrix. The maximum zT of 0.4 was achieved at 300 K with the 1 wt.%-graphene/Bi2Te3-NW composite; it was larger than that of the pristine Bi2Te3 NWs and graphene/Bi2Te3-powder composites.45 However, the zT value in this article is much lower than it is reported in other articles with similar composites. This may be due to a reduction in the electrical conductivity of composite samples. The effect of monolayer graphene on the electrical and thermal conductivities of Bi2Te3 was also investigated. The simultaneous increase in the value of power factor and decrease in the value of thermal conductivity of the Bi2Te3/G composite sample led to an enhanced value of zT = 0.92. This increase in the value of zT is attributed to increased phonon scattering and a limited effect on electron transport due to the large interface area and 2D nature of graphene.46 Graphene at different vol % was included with nanostructured Bi2Te3. The thermal conductivity of composites decreased due to the reduction in the particle size of the pristine Bi2Te3 with the addition of graphene. This decrease can be attributed to the enhanced scattering of phonons occurring at the phase boundaries within the composite.47 The electronic structure and thermoelectric properties of Bi2Te3 single crystals and graphene-doped Bi2Te3 polycrystalline samples were investigated. Experimental results showed that the graphene-doped Bi2Te3 exhibits lower thermal conductivity and a higher figure-of-merit than the single crystals.15 Graphene/Bi2Te2.7Se0.3 composites were prepared via mechanical alloying and spark plasma sintering techniques. Different graphene concentrations (0.05 and 0.5 wt %) and addition times (20 h, 10 min, and 1 min) were considered. The thermoelectric properties were measured, and the results showed that the milling time affects the graphene structure and its agglomeration.48 It was revealed that the optimum time to introduce the two-dimensional filler was during the last phase of mechanical milling, as it will preserve graphene’s structure and boost the electrical conductivity. it was observed that an increase in the milling time of graphene led to an improvement in the Seebeck coefficient. Although it was expected that the thermal conductivity would increase due to the high electrical conductivity, the introduction of graphene resulted in a noticeable reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity, primarily due to increased scattering at the newly formed interfaces. The figure-of-merit for the optimized composite, with 0.05 wt % graphene added during the last 10 min of milling, exhibited a remarkable improvement of 19% at room temperature, reaching a final zT value of 0.81 at 433K.25

Graphene incorporation in Bi2Te3-based materials consistently demonstrates reduced lattice thermal conductivity due to enhanced phonon scattering at graphene-matrix interfaces and grain boundaries. However, the graphene literature does not show a universally positive electrical response, with several studies reporting increased resistivity or reduced power factor as loading, agglomeration, or structural disorder increases. At low concentrations (typically ≤0.05–0.5 wt. %), graphene can also improve electrical conductivity by increasing carrier concentration or mobility, leading to notable zT enhancements (≈1.1–1.5). However, the improvement is highly sensitive to graphene content and processing conditions. At sufficiently low graphene contents, increases in carrier concentration, interfacial connectivity, or mobility can outweigh the additional carrier-scattering penalty and produce simultaneous gains in power factor and reduced lattice thermal conductivity. Excessive graphene loading, agglomeration, or poor interface quality can reverse the beneficial effects, resulting in increased electrical resistivity and even higher thermal conductivity due to volume effects. Furthermore, some studies report trade-offs such as reduced Seebeck coefficient or inconsistent electrical transport behavior, indicating incomplete understanding of carrier transport mechanisms. Therefore, while graphene provides an effective pathway for reducing thermal conductivity, achieving simultaneous optimization of electrical properties in Bi2Te3 remains challenging and requires precise control of loading amount, dispersion, and interface engineering.

Amorphous boron is particularly effective in p-type BiSbTe because it introduces point defects, dislocations, nanostructured regions, and grain-boundary scattering while retaining comparatively favorable carrier transport. Zhang et al. reported that n-type Bi2Te3-based alloys were prepared by a one-step SPS process by adding amorphous boron. The added boron segregated at grain boundaries, and it increased the formation energy of surface Te vacancies, thus suppressing the donor-like effect. By tuning the content of boron, the peak and average zT values of sample x = 6% can reach 1.03 at 323 K and 0.91 in the range of 298–473 K, respectively. The boron-added Bi2Te3-based alloys also showed robust mechanical performance with enhanced hardness up to 665 MPa and average compressive strength up to 88 MPa due to the Hall-Petch and boron dispersion strengthening.34 In another study by Wang et al., improved power factor and zT values were achieved in p-type Bi0.48Sb1.52Te3 materials through the addition of amorphous boron and metal indium. These dopants effectively increased the hole concentration and the density of states effective mass, leading to a high power factor of 55 μW cm−1 K−2. Moreover, they introduced various phonon scattering centers, which significantly suppressed the lattice thermal conductivity. The combined effects resulted in a maximum zT value of 1.45 at 350 K and an average zT value of 1.20, along with an average power factor of 41 μW cm−1 K−2 in the temperature range of 300–500 K, for the Bi0.48Sb1.515In0.005Te3 + 0.6 wt % B sample.50 According to the report by Chen et al., p-type Bi0.48Sb1.52Te3 composites with varying weight percentages of boron nitride (BN) were prepared. The addition of BN particles effectively increased the Seebeck coefficient and maintained relatively high carrier mobilities, resulting in an improved power factor of 53 μW cm−1 K−2 at 300 K. Additionally, the total thermal conductivity was significantly suppressed due to the decreased electrical conductivity and the introduction of phonon scattering centers. The composite with 0.10 wt % BN exhibited a maximum zT value of 1.3 at 375 K.40 Bai et al. reported recently that a simultaneous enhancement of zT as well as mechanical properties was obtained in GeTe-based alloys by adding boron. The improved zT values were attributed to the increase in charge carrier mobility resulting from the reduced interfacial barrier height. A peak zT value of 2.2 at 773 K was achieved in Ge0.84Pb0.1Sb0.06TeB0.07 alloy, which exhibited a negligible change in the coefficient of thermal expansion upon the phase transition from the rhombohedral to the cubic phase, ensuring good thermal stability of the device. Furthermore, the addition of boron led to grain refinement and dispersion strengthening, resulting in enhanced average compressive strength (∼227 MPa) and Vickers hardness (∼202 Hv) in Ge0.9Sb0.1TeB0.07 alloy. The synergy of increased zT and mechanical strength enabled the achievement of a high output power density of ∼1.76 W cm−2 at ΔT = 425 K and an energy conversion efficiency of 7.4% at ΔT = 477 K in the thermoelectric modules.51 BN and B-In-containing systems further illustrate that boron chemistry can be used to tune vacancy formation, carrier concentration, effective mass, and mechanical strength, although these benefits may be accompanied by reduced electrical conductivity or more complex compositional control.

Boron-based nanoinclusions, similar to carbon, have emerged as an effective strategy for improving thermoelectric performance by simultaneously suppressing lattice thermal conductivity and enhancing electrical transport. In Bi2Te3-based systems, the incorporation of amorphous boron or boron-containing phases introduces multi-scale phonon scattering centers—such as point defects, dislocations, and nanostructured regions—leading to significant reductions in lattice thermal conductivity and high zT values up to ∼1.6 near room temperature. Moreover, boron can beneficially modify electronic transport by tuning carrier concentration, increasing carrier mobility, or altering defect chemistry, as evidenced by suppressed vacancy formation and enhanced power factors in both n- and p-type systems. Compared to carbon, boron offers additional advantages such as improved mechanical strength and structural stability. However, these benefits are highly sensitive to boron content, distribution, and chemical state; excessive incorporation or improper dispersion may introduce carrier scattering or reduce electrical conductivity. Conductive carbon phases are most effective when electrical connectivity and carrier transport can be preserved, whereas boron-based phases are advantageous when additional phonon scattering, defect control, or mechanical strengthening is required. For bismuth chalcogenide matrices, the principal design requirement is therefore a narrow inclusion-loading window that maximizes interface-mediated phonon suppression without causing agglomeration, excessive carrier scattering, or mobility degradation.

Copper chalcogenide matrices

Cu2Se is one of the most widely studied copper chalcogenides, which differs fundamentally from conventional crystalline thermoelectric matrices because its high-temperature superionic phase combines intrinsically low lattice thermal conductivity with comparatively favorable electrical transport. This unusual transport regime makes Cu2Se relatively tolerant of nanoscale interfaces, provided that the inclusions do not disrupt copper-ion transport, induce detrimental secondary phases, or compromise high-temperature stability. Carbon- and boron-based inclusions are therefore used primarily to further suppress residual heat transport, introduce energy-selective carrier barriers, and stabilize the microstructure while preserving the electrical response of the host matrix to some extent.

Carbon-fiber reinforcement provides an early example of the strong thermoelectric response of Cu2Se to the combination of conductive inclusions. It is found that incorporating a small weight fraction of carbon (0.3 wt % carbon fiber) resulted in a zT value greater than 1 for temperatures above 520 K. Remarkably, a record-level zT of ∼2.4 was achieved at 850 K. The low thermal conductivity observed in the nanostructured composite was attributed to the high density of interfaces resulting from small grain diameters (30–60 nm), as well as the strong acoustic mismatch between Cu2Se and carbon phonon states, which enhances the thermal boundary resistance.18 Carbon dots (CDs) produce a different transport response from carbon fibers because their nanoscale dimensions and carbon-interface engineering52 can introduce both phonon-scattering interfaces and energy-dependent carrier barriers. Hu et al. reported the incorporation of carbon nanodots into Cu2Se nanopowder through hydrothermal synthesis followed by hot-pressing. The inclusion of CDs led to a significant increase in the Seebeck coefficient due to the energy filtering effect within the matrix. Additionally, an increase in the content of CDs resulted in a substantial reduction in lattice thermal conductivity due to the presence of numerous new interfaces between the CDs and Cu2Se. Notably, a sample with 0.8 wt % CDs exhibited the lowest thermal conductivity of 0.45 Wm-1 K−1 at 973 K and the highest zT value of 1.98.19 Thanks to the low loading amount, the electrical properties are preserved. In a similar work, stoichiometric Cu2Se samples were doped with gel-like CDs using a solvothermal method followed by spark plasma sintering. The results showed that the sample with 2 wt % of CDs had the maximum zT value of 2.1. This improvement in zT was attributed to the energy filtering effect of CDs interfaces along the grain boundaries and phonon scattering, which increased the Seebeck coefficient and reduced the thermal conductivity.20 The result suggests that the electrical transport of Cu2Se can remain comparatively robust even when a high density of carbon-matrix interfaces is introduced.

These improvements in zT values are primarily driven by strong suppression of lattice thermal conductivity due to high interface density, nanoscale grain refinement, and pronounced acoustic mismatch between Cu2Se and carbon, which increases thermal boundary resistance. Additionally, energy filtering effects at carbon-Cu2Se interfaces contribute to enhanced Seebeck coefficients. Unlike many other nanocomposite systems, these carbon inclusions do not significantly degrade electrical transport; instead, they often help maintain or even improve carrier transport due to their conductive nature. However, the enhancement remains highly conditional on the formation of an interconnected transport network and is sensitive to the type, content, and dispersion of carbon inclusions. Thus, while carbon nanocomposites in Cu2Se systems show exceptional promise, achieving reproducible and optimized performance requires precise control of microstructure and interface characteristics.

Graphene nanoplatelets provide the clearest evidence that phonon-spectrum mismatch can strongly suppress heat transport in Cu2Se at a slight sacrifice of electrical properties. We reported that, such as Bi2Te3, the graphene inclusion in Cu2Se also positively impacts the thermoelectric performance. Cu2Se incorporated with up to 0.45 wt % of graphene nanoplates exhibited an ultra-high thermoelectric figure-of-merit of zT = 2.44 ± 0.25 at 870 K.53 High-temperature X-ray diffraction showed that the graphene-incorporated Cu2Se matrix retains a chemically stable cubic phase. Across Cu2Se-based nanocomposites, carbon fibers, carbon dots, graphene nanoplatelets, and carbon-coated boron all produce substantial zT enhancement, but they do so through different combinations of phonon suppression and carrier modulation. The common feature is not the chemical identity of the inclusion alone, but the formation of a low-loading, well-dispersed interface network that strongly impedes heat transport without disrupting the favorable electrical response of Cu2Se.

Oxides

Oxide thermoelectrics offer advantages in thermal stability, oxidation resistance, elemental abundance,55 environmental protection, and high-temperature durability, but their performance is commonly constrained by low carrier mobility and comparatively high lattice thermal conductivity. Like other thermoelectric materials, the graphene nanocomposite method was also applied to oxide thermoelectric materials. For example, nanocomposites of Nb-doped SrTiO3 with conducting graphite were shown to achieve zT > 1 at 850 K by increasing carrier mobility along with scattering of phonons.56 However, lower zT value were measured at room temperature, mostly due to their higher thermal conductivity (5.5 W m−1 K−1) at 300 K. Replacing graphite with reduced graphene oxide was also found to lead to significant improvement of the power factor, up to 1.98 mW K−2m−1 at room temperature.57 Still, zT at room temperature remains unsatisfactory, due to the high thermal conductivity (7 Wm−1K−1). When RGO was incorporated in undoped SrTiO3, it could weaken the double Schottky barrier at grain boundaries, resulting in a simultaneous, unexpected increase of both carrier concentration and mobility.32 This caused a significant improvement of the electrical conductivity (up to 85 Scm−1) and the power factor (0.11 mWK−2m−1) for undoped SrTiO3 with 0.7 wt % of RGO at 300 K. Nonetheless, low zT values (0.04) were measured at room temperature because of the high lattice thermal conductivity (7.5 Wm−1K−1). Because rGO incorporation and oxygen-vacancy formation frequently occur simultaneously during reducing processing, their individual contributions to carrier transport are difficult to separate without carefully controlled reference samples. The use of graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) to improve the power factor of La-doped SrTiO3 (LSTO) was also reported.33 The green pellet of LSTO was sintered in a reduced atmosphere created by a mix of LSTO powders and graphene nanoplatelets to input oxygen vacancies in the LSTO bulk sample. This approach modulated charge transport at grain boundaries, leading to a significant improvement in the electrical conductivity (1285 Scm−1) and of the power factor (1.7 mWK−2m−1) at 330 K. No beneficial effect was instead observed concerning the thermal conductivity (8 Wm−1K−1 at 330K) and, therefore, on zT at room temperature (0.08).

As discussed, different approaches have been taken to improve the performance of SrTiO3-based thermoelectrics at room temperature. However, its performance is still inferior mainly due to high thermal conductivity. We developed a unique fabrication method of incorporating nanoscale pores into La-doped SrTiO3 bulk samples using a polymeric micelle self-assembly method followed by a rapid densification process by spark plasma sintering.58 Such an innovative method attained much lower thermal conductivity (3.8 Wm−1K−1) around room temperature (350 K). The reduction of the lattice thermal conductivity is due to the anisotropic scattering of phonons by the nanopores.59,60,61 Figure 5 shows the fabrication process of La-doped SrTiO3 with nanoscale pores. The nanoscale pores in the system can reduce lattice thermal conductivity and, hence, improve the figure of merit. While graphene or RGO inclusions can improve power factor, their ability to suppress thermal conductivity in oxide matrices remains limited, thereby restricting zT enhancement. This highlights a key limitation of graphene incorporation in oxide systems: the inability to sufficiently reduce heat transport despite improved electrical properties. Alternative approaches, such as introducing nanoscale porosity, have proven more effective in reducing lattice thermal conductivity via enhanced phonon scattering, leading to comparatively better zT improvement. A recent study on SrTiO3/graphene-oxide nanocomposites further demonstrates the sensitivity of oxide thermoelectrics to interface formation and processing conditions. A 48 h hydrothermal treatment produced a power factor of 214 μW m−1 K−2 and zT = 0.13 at 550 K, showing measurable improvement while confirming that the absolute thermoelectric performance of these oxide composites remains modest.62 Carbon-based inclusions are more effective at improving electrical transport than at overcoming the dominant lattice thermal-conductivity limitation. Graphite, graphene, and rGO can reduce grain-boundary barriers, modify oxygen-vacancy concentrations, and enhance carrier mobility or electrical conductivity, but these gains frequently yield only modest zT improvement because κL remains high and the associated increase in κe partly offsets the enhanced power factor. The role of carbon is also complicated by its influence on the reducing atmosphere, defect chemistry, densification, and grain growth during sintering, making direct interface effects difficult to isolate. Future studies should distinguish retained carbon interfaces from processing-induced defect effects and include physical parameters, such as carrier concentration, mobility, lattice thermal conductivity, oxygen stoichiometry, phase stability, and so forth.

Flexible polymer- and film-based thermoelectrics

Flexible polymer- and film-based thermoelectrics offer advantages in mechanical compliance, bending durability, power density, and operation under small temperature gradients, apart from peak zT. Carbon nanotubes, graphene, carbon dots, and related conductive phases are attractive for these systems because they can form percolating electrical networks while retaining low mass, flexibility, and solution-processability. Their thermoelectric benefit, however, is not universal. Polymer-carbon composites often exhibit high electrical conductivity but modest Seebeck coefficients, whereas inorganic-carbon hybrid films can achieve higher power factors but may suffer from uncertain thermal-conductivity measurements, contact resistance, and limited long-term bending stability. Processing of the carbon network is also critical in flexible inorganic-carbon hybrids. Chiba et al. selected thin and long SWCNTs by ultracentrifugation before combining them with Bi2Te3 nanoplates, increasing the electrical conductivity by 6-fold relative to the composite prepared with original SWCNTs and achieving a power factor of 6.3 μW cm−1 K−2.63 This result emphasizes the importance of inclusion morphology and network connectivity in addition to nominal carbon loading. Carbon-based inclusions are particularly attractive in these systems because one- and two-dimensional conductive networks can improve electrical transport while retaining flexibility and solution processability. Carbon-based inclusions are particularly attractive in these systems because 2- and 3-dimensional conductive networks can improve electrical transport while retaining flexibility and solution processability. In polymer matrices, carbon nanotubes and graphene primarily act as conductive networks that enhance electrical connectivity, although the resulting power factor remains strongly limited by the low Seebeck coefficient of the composite.It has been reported that the power factor can be improved by adding microscale silica particles within a three-dimensional network of CNTs in a polydimethylsiloxane polymer matrix.64 Chemically doped CNT films and buckypaper provide an alternative route in which both n- and p-type thermoelectric legs can be fabricated without a conventional polymer matrix. SWCNT was positively doped with electron donor diethylenetriamine (DETA) and was treated with CaH2 to convert it into n-type material.65 A 14-coupled multilayer n- and p-type thermoelectric module was made using SWCNT bucky paper, which gives an open-circuit voltage of 62 mV and output power of 649 nW at a temperature difference of 328K. Across flexible thermoelectric systems, carbon inclusions serve three distinct functions: forming electrical percolation networks in polymers, enabling polarity-controlled CNT films, and mechanically reinforcing high-power-factor inorganic films. The thermoelectric composite of commercially available conducting polymers such as PEDOT (poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene),1,66 PPy (Polypyrrole), PANi (Polyaniline) with carbon in the form of graphene (2D) and CNT (1D) has been investigated and these types of thermoelectric materials normally have high electrical conductivity but low Seebeck coefficient due to the conducting polymers of the composite.67 Interestingly, it was observed that a critical window of the carbon nano dots (CND) loading concentration on the cellulose-based substrate, with significantly enhanced Seebeck coefficient and Power Factor of thin films demonstrating that the trade-off principles for thermoelectric materials must be carefully tuned. The highest Seebeck coefficient of 358.45 μV K−1, power factor of 0.03 μW m−1 K−2 and the lowest thermal conductivity of 0.086 W m−1 K−1 at room temperature were achieved with a CNDs loading concentration of 9.32 g/cm2. Carbon-based materials in flexible thermoelectric systems exhibit distinct advantages in thermal conductivity suppression and limitations in the power factor. CNT-based polymer composites offer excellent flexibility and high electrical conductivity; however, their thermoelectric performance is often limited by low power factors. Similarly, doping strategies in SWCNTs enable tunable n- and p-type behavior and functional device fabrication, yet the achieved output power remains relatively low, indicating limited efficiency. Conducting polymer composites with graphene or CNTs generally suffer from low Seebeck coefficients due to the metallic-like transport behavior of the polymer matrix, despite improved electrical conductivity. Carbon-based flexible thermoelectric materials face inherent trade-offs between electrical and thermal transport, requiring optimization of composition and structure to achieve significant performance improvements. For flexible polymer- and film-based thermoelectrics, the principal design objective is therefore to establish mechanically robust and chemically stable conductive pathways while maintaining a sufficiently large Seebeck coefficient. Device-level studies are beginning to address this limitation. A recent CNT/BiSbTe foam-based flexible generator delivered an output power of 15.7 μW at ΔT = 21.8 K and retained stable performance after 10,000 bending cycles, demonstrating the importance of evaluating mechanical durability and generator output together with material-level transport properties.68 Most of studies on flexible thermoelectric systems focus on material-level transport parameters. As device-level metrics are limited by the narrow operational temperature difference, for this system a high Seebeck coefficient is in high demand.

Other systems

Beyond bismuth- and copper-based chalcogenides, carbon nanoinclusions have also been incorporated into SnSe, PbTe, MnTe, CoSb3 and Ag2Se, where their thermoelectric effects vary substantially with the intrinsic transport characteristics of the host matrix. In these systems, graphene, reduced graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes, and related phases are generally introduced to increase interface density, refine grains, and modify carrier transport. However, the resulting changes in power factor and thermal conductivity are not consistently synergistic, providing useful counterexamples to the assumption that carbon incorporation necessarily improves zT.

In SnSe-based composites, graphene and reduced graphene oxide primarily improve thermoelectric performance by increasing interface density and suppressing lattice heat transport. The uniform distribution of graphene nanosheets in the SnSe matrix formed a sharp interface with refined SnSe grain sizes and defects near the interfaces. This resulted in a notable decrease in thermal conductivity, reaching as low as approximately 0.18 W m−1 K−1 Consequently, the zT value of the SnSe/graphene composite was enhanced by at least 50% compared to pristine SnSe. The substantial reduction in thermal conductivity can be attributed to phonon scattering at the densely distributed phase interfaces, as well as defects and grain boundaries.69,70 The intimate incorporation of rGO nanosheets into the SnSe matrix resulted in two key effects. Firstly, it significantly reduced the lattice thermal conductivity of the material by enhancing phonon scattering at the high-density SnSe/rGO interfaces. Secondly, it improved the electrical conductivity due to an increased carrier concentration, particularly in the low-temperature range. The thermoelectric performance of the sintered pellets was optimized by tuning the rGO mass fraction, and the SnSe/rGO composite with 0.3 wt % achieved a lattice thermal conductivity of 0.36 W m−1 K−1 at 773 K, leading to a maximum zT value of 0.91 at 823 K.71 The graphene-containing SnSe study showing an ultralow thermal conductivity demonstrates the effectiveness of dense interfaces and grain refinement, whereas the more moderate zT reported for SnSe/rGO indicates that κL suppression alone does not guarantee high performance at the sacrifice of the electrical conductivity.

In a separate study, tin selenide with graphene nanocomposites (SnSe/GNPs) were fabricated using high-energy ball milling and hot-pressing techniques, with variations in the milling time of graphene. The impact of ball milling time on the integrity of graphene and the homogeneity of its dispersion within the SnSe matrix was investigated. Furthermore, the corresponding variations in the electrical properties of SnSe/GNPs were analyzed. The study revealed that, at short milling times, graphene was able to maintain its crystallinity. However, it exhibited agglomeration and poor dispersion within the matrix. On the other hand, longer milling times had a significant effect on increasing the disorder in the graphene structure, but it provided a better dispersion of graphene within the matrix. The increased power factor observed in the composites was attributed to the homogeneous distribution of graphene, resulting in a significant enhancement in electrical conductivity.48 However, the article did not discuss the effect of graphene nanocomposites and the milling time on the thermal conductivity and the overall figure of merit of samples. Boron-containing nanoinclusions have also recently been extended beyond Bi-based chalcogenides. In tin-enriched SnSe2, 0.8 wt % BN simultaneously modified carrier concentration and mobility and reduced κL by approximately 37%, producing a maximum zT of 0.68 at 767 K, approximately 76% higher than that of pristine SnSe2.72 The reported improvement in stress distribution further shows that BN-containing interfaces can contribute to mechanical as well as thermoelectric performance.

Graphene incorporation in n-type PbTe represents a more successful case in which grain-boundary engineering suppresses lattice thermal conductivity while retaining sufficiently favorable electrical transport. The incorporation of graphene into n-type PbTe compound resulted in a highly boosted peak value of the figure of merit reaching 1.73 at 843 K. The lattice thermal conductivity was suppressed due to the grain boundary scattering induced by graphene, particularly affecting low-frequency phonons. Simultaneously, due to the grain boundary scattering induced by graphene, particularly affecting low-frequency phonons.73 The improvement suggests that graphene-induced interfaces in PbTe can provide a more favorable balance between phonon scattering and carrier transmission than in systems where the power factor declines substantially after inclusion.

MnTe provides a useful counterexample because graphene nanoplatelets reduce both thermal conductivity and power factor by similar proportions, leaving the overall zT essentially unchanged. Bulk MnTe samples were synthesized using powder metallurgy and spark plasma sintering techniques, with the addition of graphene nanoplates (GNPs) in small quantities ranging from 0.25 to 1 wt %. The inclusion of GNPs had a notable effect on suppressing the presence of low-temperature ferromagnetic phase impurities in the sample. The thermal conductivity and power factor decreased almost equally by ≈ 34% at 600 K; as a result, the thermoelectric figure-of-merit is not affected by GNP reinforcement in the optimized sample.39 Although the inclusion suppressed heat transport, the approximately comparable decrease in power factor offset this benefit. This result directly demonstrates that interface-induced phonon scattering is not sufficient when the same interfaces impose a substantial carrier-transport penalty.

Carbon fiber/CoSb3 composites provide another important counterexample in which a substantial improvement in electrical conductivity does not lead to a higher thermoelectric figure of merit. The addition of carbon fiber in p-type CoSb3 enhanced its electrical conductivity in three times by a factor of three compared to the pristine sample. Two explanations were considered for this increase: either carbon atoms enter the CoSb3 lattice as a dopant, making it more conductive, or the increased conductivity was due to electrical percolation of the carbon fibers thanks to the interconnected morphology. Unfortunately, the resulting zT of the composite was not improved, as the effects of the carbon addition largely canceled each other out.74 In contrast to Bi2Te3 and Cu2Se systems, the incorporation of carbon fibers in p-type CoSb3 presents a more complex and less favorable thermoelectric response. Although electrical conductivity increased significantly, this improvement is likely due to either partial doping of carbon into the lattice or the formation of conductive percolation pathways, which was counterbalanced by adverse or compensating effects on other thermoelectric parameters. As a result, no overall improvement in zT was achieved. This highlights a critical limitation that simply enhancing electrical conductivity through carbon inclusion does not guarantee improved thermoelectric performance. Instead, it underscores the importance of simultaneously optimizing all interdependent transport parameters. Therefore, in CoSb3 systems, the role of carbon inclusion requires more careful design and deeper understanding to avoid competing effects that negate overall performance gains.

Ag2Se–carbon composites differ from SnSe, PbTe, and MnTe because their most distinctive advantage lies in the combination of near-room-temperature thermoelectric transport and mechanical flexibility. Wang et al. reported on the nano-compositing of silver selenide (Ag2Se) thermoelectric material with carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which resulted in low thermal conductivity and enhanced mechanical properties. The optimized concentration of 0.5 wt % CNTs with Ag2Se achieved interfacial effects, leading to a substantially low lattice thermal conductivity of 0.27 W m−1 K−1 and a slightly enhanced power factor of 2.71 mW m−1 K−2 at 375 K. Consequently, an exceptional peak zT value of 0.97 at 375 K and a high average zT value of ∼0.89 over the temperature range of 300–375 K were obtained.75 Furthermore, the single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) were incorporated into an Ag2+ySe film using an in situ growth technique, resulting in a high-power factor of 19.36 μWcm−1 K−2 at 300 K, due to the enhanced carrier concentration and enlarged effective mass induced by the additional interstitial Ag in the in situ grown Ag2Se on SWCNTs. In situ precipitation can generate cleaner, more coherent, and better-distributed interfaces within the matrix, which may reduce carrier scattering while maintaining strong phonon scattering. The film, when used in a flexible thermoelectric power generator, can achieve an output power of 0.72 μW and an open-circuit voltage of 18.17 mV at a temperature difference of 30 K.28 The article did not discuss the thermal conductivity due to difficulty in measurement. Moreover, the distinction between in situ precipitation and ex situ mixing is especially important for carbon-containing nanocomposites. Ex situ addition allows direct control of loading, but it can suffer from agglomeration and weak interfacial contact if dispersion is incomplete. In situ precipitation or segregation during sintering or synthesis can generate cleaner interfaces and stronger grain-boundary pinning, although the precipitate size, chemistry, and reproducibility are more sensitive to processing temperature and matrix composition. These findings highlight that while carbon inclusions can effectively improve electrical transport in Ag2Se due to their conductive nature, the overall thermoelectric performance critically depends on achieving simultaneous control of thermal transport.

Among these systems, Cu2Se systems demonstrate outstanding thermoelectric enhancement, with ultrahigh zT values (∼2.4) achieved due to strong phonon scattering arising from acoustic mismatch at well-defined interfaces, while preserving the favorable electrical transport of the host matrix. The structural stability and uniform dispersion of graphene nanoplates further support consistent performance, highlighting Cu2Se as one of the most effective systems for graphene-based optimization. In contrast, other materials such as SnSe, PbTe, and MnTe also benefit from graphene inclusion primarily through significant reduction in lattice thermal conductivity via interface- and defect-driven phonon scattering, along with moderate improvements in electrical conductivity linked to increased carrier concentration. However, these enhancements are often more limited, and the resulting zT improvements are comparatively modest. Furthermore, processing parameters such as milling time critically influence graphene dispersion and structural integrity, directly affecting electrical transport, while their impact on thermal transport is not always fully addressed. Overall, while graphene inclusions are highly effective in suppressing lattice thermal conductivity across various systems, their impact on electrical transport is strongly system-dependent, emphasizing the need for precise control of dispersion, interface quality, and processing conditions to achieve consistent thermoelectric enhancement. Critical comparison of several carbon-based systems shows that thermoelectric performance is strongly matrix-dependent. In Bi2Te3/BiSbTe, small carbon additions can simultaneously improve carrier concentration and suppress κL, but excessive loading introduces agglomeration and thus carrier scattering. In Cu2Se, carbon and graphene inclusions are particularly effective at high temperature because phonon-mode mismatch and dense interfaces suppress heat transport while the liquid-like copper sublattice maintains favorable charge transport. In CoSb3 and polymer/CNT systems, however, improved electrical conductivity does not simultaneously increase zT because the Seebeck coefficient and thermal conductivity can offset the electrical gain. These contrasts indicate that the inclusion should be selected for both interfacial phonon mismatch and electronic compatibility with the specific host matrix. Collectively, these studies suggest that carbon nanoinclusions are highly effective in suppressing lattice thermal conductivity and can simultaneously enhance electrical performance; however, achieving consistent and optimized thermoelectric performance is still limited due to the trade-off between each thermoelectric parameter.

Practical limitations

Carbon- and boron-based inclusions can improve these properties, but they may also introduce additional failure pathways, including oxidation, interfacial reactions, inclusion segregation, thermal-expansion mismatch, and microcrack formation. For practical thermoelectric modules, nanocomposite design must also satisfy stability, biocompatibility,1,2 mechanical, cost-effectiveness76 and manufacturing constraints. Carbon-based inclusions may oxidize, react with the matrix, or segregate during high-temperature operation if the interface is not chemically stable. Boron and BN inclusions can strengthen grain boundaries and reduce thermal conductivity, yet their benefits depend on uniform dispersion and controlled interfacial chemistry. Reports on Cu2Se/graphene and C/B-Cu2Se are encouraging because they include high-temperature structural stability, engineering zT, mechanical properties, or cycling stability; however, comparable evidence is still missing for many Bi2Te3-, SnSe-, PbTe-, and oxide-based composites. Carbon fibers and CNTs can also improve mechanical properties by bridging grains, inhibiting crack propagation, or increasing fracture resistance. Carbon-microfibre-reinforced bismuth chalcogenides and CNT/Ag2Se composites demonstrate that a conductive inclusion may simultaneously modify transport and mechanical behavior. Scalable processing should therefore prioritize low loading, reproducible powder mixing or in situ precipitation, compatibility with hot pressing, thin film printing, or other low-cost sintering technologies. Material scientists usually focus on the performance of their products, while few of them would move on to the next stage at device level for industrial applications. There are concerns that nanoinclusions may also affect metallization and joining, which will affect device operation. Carbon-rich grain boundaries can alter solder wetting, brazing reactions, diffusion-barrier adhesion, and electrical contact resistance. Boron-containing phases may improve bulk strength but could form resistive interlayers near electrodes if they segregate during bonding.

Conclusion

Carbon- and boron-based nanoinclusions can improve thermoelectric performance, but their effectiveness is strongly dependent on the host matrix, inclusion loading, processing route, and interfacial chemistry. Across the systems reviewed, the most successful composites are those in which nanoscale interfaces suppress lattice heat transport without imposing a comparable penalty on carrier mobility or the power factor. Bismuth chalcogenides generally require a narrow loading window because their electrical transport is highly sensitive to interface-induced scattering and agglomeration. Cu2Se is comparatively tolerant of carbon- and boron-based interfaces and has consequently produced several reported peak zT values above 2, although copper-ion migration, high-temperature interface stability, and long-term operation under sustained thermal gradients remain important concerns. In oxide matrices, carbon-based additions can improve carrier concentration, mobility, or grain-boundary transport, but the persistently high lattice thermal conductivity continues to limit zT. Flexible polymer- and film-based systems should therefore be evaluated not only by zT, but also by power factor, normalized output, contact resistance, environmental stability, and bending durability.

No single inclusion chemistry emerges as universally superior. Conductive carbon phases are most beneficial when they improve intergranular connectivity or carrier concentration while preserving the Seebeck coefficient, whereas amorphous boron and BN give an edge in defect engineering, multiscale phonon scattering, or mechanical reinforcement. Carbon-coated boron illustrates how multiple interfacial functions can be combined, although such architectures introduce additional complexity in synthesis, interfacial control, and scale-up. Conversely, the MnTe and CoSb3 examples demonstrate that lower thermal conductivity or higher electrical conductivity alone does not guarantee a higher zT.

Further progress therefore requires more rigorous and standardized evidence for the proposed mechanisms. Peak zT should also be considered together with average or engineering zT, oxidation resistance, thermal cycling, mechanical reliability, batch reproducibility, electrode compatibility, and device-level output. The most practically relevant nanocomposite will not necessarily be the material with the highest isolated peak zT, but the system that maintains favorable transport, stable interfaces, mechanical integrity, and reproducible performance throughout fabrication and long-term operation. What matters most is not which inclusion is chosen, but how its interface is engineered, which can turn the interface from a scattering obstacle into a deliberate regulator that reconciles phonon blocking with carrier transport.
