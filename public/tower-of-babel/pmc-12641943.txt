Global, regional, and national burden of tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer attributable to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons occupational expose from 1990 to 2021: worsening or improving?
Authors: Xuanyu Wu, Xiang Xiao, Mengqiu Chen, Jiajing Chen, Yan Zhu, Sijie Lei, Zizheng Li, Jinna Tian, Han Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) directly contributes to tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer. However, it remains a challenge to accurately determine the epidemiological relationship between PAH and lung cancer.MethodsThe incidence, deaths, disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), years lost due to disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and corresponding estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) were used from the Global Burden of Disease 2021. Trends were stratified by region/country, age, sex, and sociodemographic index (SDI). Joinpoint regression, decomposition analysis, and frontier analysis were conducted to explore potential characteristics of the disease burden.ResultsThe burden of occupational PAH exposure is globally rising. The lung cancer’s age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and DALYs rate (ASDR) attributable to occupational PAH exposure were 0.07 (95%UI: 0.06, 0.08) and 1.98 (95%UI: 1.63,2.40) per 100,000 population, with EAPC of 1.14 (95%CI: 1.10, 1.39) and 0.61 (95%CI: 0.58, 0.72), correspondingly. The highest disease burden was observed among individuals aged 65–69 years. The disease burden increased in high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI levels, with the most pronounced rise observed in high-middle SDI countries during thirty years. Joinpoint regression analyses showed that ASMR generally increased for both males and females. The population contributed to the fluctuations in DALYs and deaths in the decomposition analysis. The frontier analyses suggested the greatest potential between burden reduction and high-SDI countries. We demonstrated that the burden of occupational PAH exposure is escalating globally, and the burden of lung cancer attributable to occupational PAH exposure is also increasing, with significant variations in age, sex, and socioeconomic development.ConclusionsThis study underscores the urgent need for public health interventions, especially in high-medium SDI regions. With economies developing, it is imperative to consider additional measures to mitigate occupational exposure to PAH, with the aim of alleviating both the societal and economic burdens, as well as the associated health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25156-z.Keywords: Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, Tracheal, Bronchus, And lung cancer, Global burden of disease, Occupational exposure

Background

Tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers constitute a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) report, approximately 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer were reported in 2022, with 1.82 million new deaths attributed to the disease, both ranking first among all cancers [1, 2]. In recent years, the diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer have advanced rapidly, for example through 18F-labeled fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-based radiotherapy, which can effectively improve the quality of life of patients with lung cancer [3]. However, lung cancer imposes a tremendous financial burden associated with cancer [4] and has emerged as a significant public health issue with global implications for human health in numerous countries, such as China and the United States. Various factors play crucial roles in the onset, progression, and metastasis of lung cancer, including smoking, environmental pollution, and occupational exposure [5–8].

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) is a major environmental and occupational pollutant. It is widely present in sediments, soil, organisms, and the atmosphere and consists of two or more condensed benzene ring unlinked heteroatoms. PAH comes from various sources and is mainly produced by the incomplete combustion or pyrolysis process of organic substances' smoke (mainly fire, automobile exhaust, incinerator, tobacco, charcoal baked food) [9]. The United States Environmental Protection Agency has designated sixteen subtypes of PAH as priority pollutants [10]. PAHs exhibit carcinogenic, mutagenic, teratogenic, and other harmful effects on human health [11], with their strong carcinogenicity being of greatest concern [11]. The IARC has classified multiple subtypes of PAH as probable carcinogens [12, 13]. PAH mainly enters the human body through the respiratory tract [14] and is deposited in the lung, liver, kidney, and other organs through alveolar, capillary, and other pathways [13]. PAH is associated with the incidence of lung cancer. Occupational exposure to PAH, such as firefighting, public work, and road construction, significantly increases the risk of lung cancer [15, 16]. As a foreign substance in the airway, PAH continuously damages the tissue, induces chronic inflammation in the lung, and reduces respiratory function [17]. In addition, PAH induces DNA damage and impaired repair, leading to allelic mutations [18], such as induction of human apyrimidinic endodeoxyribonuclease 1 protein mutation, which in turn increases lung cancer susceptibility [19].

To date, the association between PAH exposure and lung cancer has emerged as a key issue in global environmental science and public health. Therefore, comprehensive research is urgently needed to better characterize and address this challenge. Previously, the lung cancer-causing characteristics of PAH have been reported in relevant literature, and some mechanisms have been explored [18, 20, 21]. Some studies have conducted cohort observations of PAH exposure and lung cancer incidence, demonstrating regional PAH exposure and its disease burden with lung cancer [22–24], but targeted studies that clearly focus on PAH occupational exposure on a global scale are still limited. The Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) in the United States led the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, establishing a broad and publicly accessible database covering various health conditions, including PAH. The dataset describes the global health situation and points to key areas for intervention. Although there is a GBD study on occupational exposure and lung cancer, it used data from 2019 and did not discuss the distribution of PAH in detail [25]. Hence, this study aims to utilize updated global exposure data to analyze temporal trends, regional variations, sex- and age-specific distributions, and the lung cancer burden attributable to PAH exposure using the GBD framework. These results are intended to inform the decision-making process for managing and preventing PAH globally.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The overall workflow of this study is summarized in Fig. 1. Data were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database compiled by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), accessed via the Global Health Data Exchange tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). This study follows the GBD 2021 harmonized methodology on the attributable burden of occupational carcinogens. The included risk-outcome pair was occupational polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons exposure and lung cancer. The strength of evidence for this risk–outcome pair has been classified by the World Health Organization as Group 1 (i.e., clearly carcinogenic to humans). The dataset encompasses 21 regions and seven super-regions across 204 countries and territories, addressing 371 diseases, injuries, and 88 risk factors. Based on the sociodemographic index (SDI), the 204 countries and regions were stratified into five groups: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI. Initially, our study examined the overall disease burden associated with occupational PAH exposure and collected epidemiological data for 1990 and 2021. Our analysis was based on the Comparative Risk Assessment framework of the GBD. Accordingly, the estimates were derived from epidemiological studies that had been adjusted for confounders such as smoking. The summary exposure value (SEV), number of deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UIs) were extracted. Subsequently, we collected epidemiological data from 1990 to 2021, focusing specifically on occupational PAH exposure as the risk factor and lung cancer as the outcome. We extracted death rates (also named mortality), DALYs, years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and 95% UIs, stratified by age, sex, and region. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The SEV, annual percentage change (APC) from 1990 to 2021, number and rate of deaths and DALYs were used to estimate the global exposure level of PAH. The number and rate of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs were used as the primary indicators to describe the GBD of lung cancer attributed to occupational exposure to PAH. DALYs, summary measure of overall health loss, were calculated by adding YLLs and YLDs. YLLs were calculated as the number of deaths attributable to PAH-related lung cancer multiplied by the standard life expectancy at the age of death. YLDs were calculated by multiplying the prevalence of PAH-attributable lung cancer by the corresponding severity-adjusted disability weight (DW). The GBD algorithm reports each rate per 100,000 population, with 95% UIs. Standardized age distribution and demographic characteristics were assessed using age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR), age-standardized YLLs rate (ASYLL), and age-standardized YLDs rate (ASYLD). Estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was used to analyze the dynamics of PAH risk exposure to lung cancer to determine time trends in GBD. An increasing ASR was identified when both the EAPC and the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval (CI) were positive. Conversely, a decreasing ASR was observed if the upper bound of the 95% CI of the EAPC was negative. If neither condition was met, ASR was considered stable for the duration of the study.

The joinpoint regression model (version 4.9.0.1) was used to divide longitudinal changes into different parts and identify statistically significant segment trends to reflect changing disease burden trends [26]. Regression fitting was used for the natural logarithm of prevalence and mortality across the different periods, and the APC for each period was calculated. Compared with the null hypothesis of no change, APC using percentage (%) to report, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Decomposition analysis in epidemiology disaggregates changes in health indicators, revealing the impact of factors such as population growth, aging, risk factors, or medical advances [27]. In this study, we used decomposition analysis to evaluate changes in lung cancer deaths and DALYs attributable to PAH exposure, decomposed into aging, epidemiological change, and population growth (Formula S1). Considering socio-demographic development in each region, frontier analysis was applied to explore the potential for reducing the disease burden [28]. This approach identifies opportunities for improvement and provides strategic guidance by estimating the achievable minimum ASDR of lung cancer at each SDI level. We fit an efficiency frontier to the lowest 5th percentile of ASDR values using LOESS regression. For each country and year, the avoidable burden was calculated as the difference between its observed ASDR and the frontier-predicted ASDR for its SDI level (Formula S2). All study analyses were performed with R studio (v.4.3.0).

Results

Global trend of occupational exposure to PAH

In 2021, global occupational exposure to PAH accounted for 175,901 DALYs and 6,056 deaths. Asia and South America had the highest SEV for occupational exposure to PAH (Table S1, Figure S1). China ranked first worldwide in mortality, DALYs, and SEV, with values of 0.26 (95% UI: 0.20–0.34), 7.39 (95% UI: 5.63–9.67), and 1.87 (95% UI: 1.73–2.02), respectively (Table S1-3, Fig. 2). Compared with 1990, the global SEV of occupational PAH exposure continued to rise in 2021, with the Solomon Island showing the largest increase (3.79%) (Table S4, Figure S1).

GBD analysis of lung cancer for occupational PAH exposure

Global level

The study estimated 6,060 deaths (95% UI: 4,450–6,507) and an ASMR of 0.07 (95% UI: 0.06–0.08) per 100,000 population attributable to occupational PAH-related lung cancer in 2021. The mortality trended upward over the past thirty years, with an EAPC of 1.14 (95% CI: 1.10–1.39). Correspondingly, DALYs from occupational exposure PAH- related lung cancer were 176,013 (95% UI: 144,213–212,938), with an ASDR of 1.98 (95% UI: 1.63–2.40) per 100,000, and an EAPC of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.58–0.72). Additionally, occupational PAH -related lung cancer contributed to 1,796 (95% UI: 1,244–2,424) YLDs and 174,217 (95% UI: 142,749–210,739) YLLs globally, with ASYLD and ASYLL rates of 0.02 (95% UI: 0.01–0.03) and 1.96 (95% UI: 1.61–2.37) per 100,000, respectively. The EAPC was 2.26 (95%CI: −0.74–2.76) for YLDs and 0.59 (95%CI: −0.06–5.72) for YLLs (Table 1).

Age and sex patterns

In 2021, mortality, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs were significantly higher in males than in females, although the absolute number of cases was greater among females. The ASMR of PAH exposure-induced lung cancer among individuals aged ≥ 30 years was higher in males and increased with age, peaking in the 65–69-year group. Age-standardized DALY, YLL, and YLD rates by sex showed patterns similar to the ASMR (Fig. 3). An increase in the ASMR, ASDR, ASYLD, and ASYLL was observed between the ages of 30 and 69 years, followed by a decline after age 70 (Fig. 4).

Burden by SDI

The ASMR, ASDR, ASYLL, and ASYLD of PAH-related lung cancer were significantly lower in countries with low SDI levels. Although the absolute number of cases of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs increased in high SDI regions, their standardized rates remained stable and slightly decreased. Between 1990 and 2021, all four indicators, including ASMR, ASDR, ASYLL, and ASYLD, increased in high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions, with the largest rise in high-middle SDI countries. Similar trends were observed between these indicators and SDI, except in high-SDI regions (Table 1).

Regional level

In 2021, the ASMR of PAH-related lung cancer was highest in Southeast Asia [0.16 (95% UI: 0.12–0.21) per 100,000], followed by Central Asia [0.08 (95% UI: 0.06–0.1) per 100,000] and the Caribbean [0.08 (95%UI: 0.06–0.09) per 100,000]. The lowest rates were in Central Latin America [0.01 (95% UI: 0.01–0.01) per 100,000], and Oceania [0.01 (95% UI: 0.01, 0.02) per 100,000 population]. The Caribbean, Central Asia, Central Europe, and Southeast Asia showed rising ASMR trends for three decades, with Central Asia exhibiting the steepest increase (EAPC = 3.18, 95% CI: 1.6–5.0). In contrast, several regions-including East Asia, high-income Asia Pacific, high-income North America, North Africa, Middle East, Oceania, Southern Latin America, Tropical Latin America, Western Europe, and Western Sub-Saharan Africa showed declining ASMR, with Western Sub-Saharan Africa showing the sharpest decrease(EAPC = −5.56, 95% CI: −7.5–3.3) (Table 1).

Joinpoint regression

Joinpoint regression showed that the ASMR of males and females generally showed an upward trend. We found that the ASMR of females consistently showed an upward trend and reached the highest level from 2015 to 2021 (APC = 2.68, p < 0.05). The disease standardized mortality of males was higher than that of females, and it peaked between 1999 and 2004 (APC = 1.19, p < 0.05). However, it showed a significant downward trend from 2012 to 2017. The ASDR showed similar characteristics to ASMR (Fig. 5).

Decomposition analysis was conducted to track the trends of the absolute number of DALYs and deaths. The analysis identified that population growth was the primary driver for the increase in the absolute number of DALYs and deaths linked to PAH-related lung cancer across various SDI regions. Population aging was also a notable contributor, particularly among males (Fig. 6).

Frontier analysis

Frontier analysis was used to benchmark the observed ASDR against the theoretical minimum achievable rate at each SDI level. The results showed that the effective difference (EF) generally increased with SDI, while variance decreased. The frontier trend of ASDR stabilized once the SDI exceeded approximately 0.5. Countries with the smallest EF (Singapore, Somalia, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Lao People's Democracy Republic, Guam, Thailand, Nicaragua, Maldives, and Vietnam), indicating performance close to the frontier, were primarily low- to middle-SDI countries (range: 0.00–2.354). In contrast, countries with the largest EF (Netherlands, Germany, Canada, the United States of America, Guinea-Bissau, Greenland, Iceland, France, Gambia, and Australia), suggesting the greatest potential for burden reduction relative to their SDI peers, were primarily high-SDI countries (range: 41.272–72.986) (Fig. 7, Table 1).

Discussion

PAH has become an important public health issue and a prominent topic in environmental protection research. The pathogenic role of PAH in lung cancer has become increasingly evident. Here, our results provided new insights into the GBD of lung cancer attributable to occupational PAH exposure over the past three decades, mainly focusing on age, sex, and regional division. Our findings showed that occupational PAH exposure remained a serious concern worldwide, particularly in Asia, South America, and China. Compared with 1990, the incidence of occupational PAH exposure has continued to rise globally. The burden of ASMR and ASDR of PAH-related lung cancer also showed an increasing trend, especially in countries with high-medium SDI levels. The burden was concentrated in populations aged ≥ 30 years, with the greatest impact observed among older males (65–69 years). The burden varied by geography and demographic structure, highlighting the importance of comprehensive epidemiological assessments to guide prevention and management strategies.

PAH is a component of various air pollutants, including tobacco smoke, barbecue smoke, coal and fuel smoke. Occupational exposure to PAH occurs mainly through inhalation and dermal absorption [29]. High-risk groups include individuals working in environments with heavy smoke, oil, or toxic gases—such as firefighters, traffic police, chefs, and coal miners—who therefore face an elevated risk of lung cancer [7]. Among the numerous PAH subtypes, benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P) is particularly carcinogenic, it metabolized into B[a]P diolepoxide (BPDE), which binds covalently to DNA and proteins [30], thereby impairing DNA repair and inducing genomic instability. PAH can lead to abnormal expression of miRNAs [21], such as miR-494 and miR-205, which in turn lead to excessive BPDE adducts, causing gene mutations, affecting cell cycle regulation and DNA damage response, and thereby increasing the susceptibility to lung diseases [18]. In addition, PAH emitted by industry can also exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation and activate tumor-related signaling pathways [31, 32].

Our study confirmed a clear sex disparity in lung cancer burden attributable to occupational PAH exposure, which aligns with previous studies [33]. The number of female patients was higher than that of male patients, but the growth rates of ASMR and ASDR in male patients were faster than in females. It may be related to the higher degree of socialized work and male participation. However, it is undeniable that the disease burden of lung cancer caused by PAH exposure in females is also worthy of attention. With the development of society, the female workforce proportion has gradually increased, and females may have to undertake work and family responsibilities in the social division of labor. Double exposure to PAH means a more dangerous signal. The most exposed occupations among females are servers, cooks, and machine operators [29]. Females always take on the responsibilities in the traditional family, which means they must be exposed to the cooking smoke at home. In addition, there are physiological differences between females and males, in which females have higher CYP1A1 expression and DNA adducts and are more sensitive to oxidative stress and chromosome damage induced by PAH. There may be evidence of potential sex differences in TBLC associated with increased PAH exposure [34, 35]. Of course, it is undeniable that the healthy worker effect may lead to overestimation or underestimation of the study results in epidemiological studies [36]. Therefore, in addition to further reducing PAH exposure, future occupational health research needs to combine gender differences and social division of labour to optimize working hours and methods. In addition, our study showed that the global age distribution of lung cancer caused by PAH exposure was after 30 years old and mainly distributed in the 50–70 age group. It may be due to the exposure to excessive PAH caused by long-term work in the past few decades, leading to more cases of lung cancer in this age group.

Through the trend and frontier analyses, significant socioeconomic differences persisted in the disease burden of PAH-related lung cancer. In high-SDI regions, observed ASDRs approached or exceeded the theoretical minimum risk boundaries, suggesting limited scope for further reduction. Developed countries such as the Netherlands, Germany, Canada, the United States, Greenland, Iceland, France, and Australia, played an important role in the ASDR of PAH-related lung cancer. It reflected the industrialization, urbanization, and cumulative occupational exposure that occurred decades ago in regions/countries in these high-middle SDI quintiles [25]. However, the more advanced diagnostic technology in these developed countries may also be an important reason for the high detection rate of tumors. In recent years, technological innovation in the medical field has been swift and continues to evolve. For example, the introduction of the concept of Internet of Things (IOT) into medical practice has improved the detection rate of lung cancer [37]. Furthermore, some developed countries, such as Singapore and Malaysia, showed lower ASDR, which suggested that the relevant policies of these countries may be worth further learning. For example, the Singapore government has continuously improved its infrastructure since the 2000s. A total of 22 telemetry air-quality-monitor stations on the island of Singapore continuously record environmental pollutant levels [38], while the government also has promoted a ‘Digital first’ mindset to control occupational hazards and optimize worker workspace [39]. Of course, the main races in these countries differed from those in European and American developed countries, which may have impacted the results. Notably, Guinea-Bissau and Gambia had high EF values and low SDI levels, which were more concerned with disease management capacity and medical resource gaps than other countries with similar socio-demographic conditions.

In addition, the ASDR of PAH-related lung cancer also gradually showed an increasing trend in the middle-low SDI region. The burden of lung cancer attributable to occupational exposure to PAH will also gradually increase [25, 40, 41]. In the future development, these countries should focus on reducing occupational PAH exposure to the greatest extent to reduce the expected disease burden of lung cancer and alleviate the local socioeconomic pressure. For example, the government should optimize workplace ventilation standards and establish a more complete environmental monitoring system. The companies should promote the wearing and optimization of personal protective equipment and use more advanced technology to improve the occupational health monitoring. For instance, future studies integrating genetic data with occupational exposure assessments could help identify high-risk worker subgroups. This paves the way for personalized prevention strategies, where workers with high-risk genetic profiles could be prioritized for more stringent protective measures, more frequent health surveillance, and potentially earlier screening, optimizing the allocation of resources. At the same time, it is also a good development prospect for governments to coordinate the construction of cross-national and cross-regional IOT medical systems to improve the efficiency of diagnosis and treatment [37].

This study used the latest GBD 2021 data to provide a detailed analysis of the global burden of lung cancer attributable to occupational exposure to PAHs. It offers valuable insights for improving PAH-related occupational environments and supplies crucial public health evidence for policymakers and clinicians. Although the GBD study methodologies are rigorous, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the GBD relies on diverse data sources, including national surveillance systems and individual-level surveys, which may introduce selection bias and incomplete reporting, particularly in regions with underdeveloped health information systems. Secondly, while GBD data excel at capturing macro-epidemiological trends at national and global levels, they cannot reflect local variations. For instance, as an air pollutant, the severity of PAH exposure and its consequent lung cancer risk may fluctuate with seasons or specific local industrial activities, a level of granularity beyond the scope of this analysis. Third, a key limitation is the inability of the GBD data to distinguish between the numerous individual subtypes of PAHs (e.g., B[a]P vs. less carcinogenic compounds). PAHs are treated as a single risk factor, whereas the potency and mechanisms of different PAH compounds can vary significantly. This amalgamation might affect the precision of our burden estimates. Fourth, occupational settings often involve complex mixtures of exposures. Workers exposed to PAHs are frequently also exposed to other established lung carcinogens, such as diesel engine exhaust, silica dust, and asbestos. The GBD's comparative risk assessment framework struggles to fully disentangle the independent contributions of these overlapping risk factors, potentially leading to over attribution or under attribution of burden to PAHs alone. Finally, the concept of PAH-related lung cancer is broad and does not address specific pathological subtypes (e.g., adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma). Future studies with individual-level data are needed to explore these associations more deeply. Despite these limitations, our findings provide a robust and comprehensive assessment of the GBD of occupational PAH exposure, highlighting at-risk populations and informing priority-setting for public health interventions.

Conclusions

Our study provides a comprehensive overview of the GBD of lung cancer attributable to occupational exposure to PAH. First, we reveal the overall disease burden of occupational exposure to PAH. In 2021, China had the most cases of occupational exposure to PAH, and Asia and South America were the continents with the most occupational exposure to PAH. Compared with 1990, global occupational exposure to PAH showed an upward trend. Subsequently, from the perspectives of mortality, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs, we revealed that the PAH-related lung cancer burden varied significantly by age, gender, and socioeconomic development. The mortality and DALYs of lung cancer caused by occupational exposure to PAH showed an increasing trend worldwide, and the 65–69 age group was the leading group with disease burden. Sex differences and the corresponding differences in occupational exposure were always of concern. In the future, more attention should be paid to reducing PAH-related exposure in high-middle SDI countries/regions. In summary, lung cancer attributable to occupational PAH exposure is a serious topic worldwide, and there is an urgent need for national governments and healthcare professionals around the world to develop more cost-effective and targeted strategies to reduce the socioeconomic burden and corresponding health risks.
