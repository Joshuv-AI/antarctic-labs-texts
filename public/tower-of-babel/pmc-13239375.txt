Diagnostic yield and safety of probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscope in diagnosing patients with unknown pleural effusion: a protocol for a multicentre, randomised controlled trial (COLLABORATION-I)
Authors: Ming-Ming Deng, Yi Hu, Xiaoping Liu, Yu Feng, Ziwen Zheng, Run Tong, Jose Porcel, Gang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSemirigid thoracoscopy plays an important role in the diagnosis of pleural diseases. However, its diagnostic performance remains unsatisfactory particularly in terms of the negative likelihood ratio. Therefore, more effective supplementary diagnostic tools are required. Probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy (pCLE), which allows live tissue imaging at the cellular level, can discriminate between malignant and benign pleura during medical thoracoscopy. However, the clinical relevance of pCLE in pleural disease remains unclear. This protocol describes a randomised controlled trial that evaluates the additional diagnostic value of pCLE in diagnosing pleural diseases using semirigid thoracoscopy.Methods and analysisThis study is a multicentre, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial that will be conducted at ten sites in China. A total of 158 adult patients with undiagnosed exudative pleural effusions will be enrolled and randomly allocated (1:1) to undergo either a conventional pleural biopsy (control group) or a pCLE-guided pleural biopsy (intervention group) via semirigid thoracoscopy. In the intervention group, a pCLE system will be applied during thoracoscopy to identify suspicious pleural areas for targeted biopsy. The primary outcome is the diagnostic yield of the procedure in patients with unknown causes of pleural effusion. Secondary outcomes include negative likelihood ratio, diagnostic sensitivity in specific diseases, procedural time, rate of adequate specimens for achieving molecular diagnosis and complications.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was obtained from the China-Japan Friendship Hospital Ethics Committee (2025-KY-018). Written informed consent will be obtained from all the participants. The findings will be disseminated through journal publications and conference presentations.Trial registration numberNCT06741839.Keywords: Clinical Trial, Protocols & guidelines, Thoracic medicine, Randomized Controlled Trial

Introduction

Medical thoracoscopy is widely recommended as the technique of choice for diagnosing exudative pleural effusions of uncertain aetiology.1 2 Current medical thoracoscopy can be performed using rigid or semirigid thoracoscopes.3 While semirigid thoracoscopy offers advantages such as enhanced flexibility and patient tolerance, several studies have suggested that the diagnostic yield remains suboptimal in the intention-to-treat population.4 5 Moreover, the negative predictive value of semirigid thoracoscopy is low, and a 15.4% rate of delayed malignancy diagnosis has been reported among patients with initially benign histology results (nonspecific pleuritis/fibrosis) via semirigid thoracoscopy.6,8 This may be associated with the biopsy success rate, sampling quality and visual findings during the thoracoscopy.

For patients with chronic pleuritis or pleuritis due to asbestos exposure, obtaining biopsy specimens using semirigid instruments and flexible forceps is more difficult because of the lack of mechanical power.9 10 To overcome the disadvantages of semirigid thoracoscopy and improve the diagnostic yield, previous studies have introduced cryobiopsy and electrocautery-assisted biopsy to obtain larger specimens.11 12 Despite a recent meta-analysis showing a significant improvement in sampling quality, no difference was observed in diagnostic yield.13 Thoracoscopic findings also significantly affect the diagnostic yield of semirigid thoracoscopy. A previous study suggested that the presence of nodules predicts a high probability of obtaining a definitive histological diagnosis on pleural biopsy specimens, while it remains difficult to select the sampling location in patients with diffuse pleural thickening.4 These findings suggest that improving the accessibility of representative specimens rather than sampling quality is important for improving the diagnostic value of semirigid thoracoscopy. Although novel imaging techniques, such as autofluorescence imaging (AFI), can improve the negative predictive value, their specificity is limited by the interference of tissue component heterogeneity (eg, fibrous plaques and necrotic tissue).14 15

Probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy (pCLE) is a real-time cellular imaging technique that can achieve ‘optical biopsy’ in vivo. Bonhomme et al demonstrated distinct features between benign and malignant pleural involvement and showed the potential of pCLE as a guidance tool for pleural biopsy.16 In addition, a prospective study assessed the use of pCLE to guide biopsies in the specific indication of suspected pleural mesothelioma and suggested that pCLE could distinguish malignant mesothelioma from pleural fibrosis with high precision.17 Therefore, we hypothesised that pCLE could improve the diagnostic yield by sampling representative areas detected by pCLE imaging of the pleura. However, evidence on the diagnostic yield and safety of pCLE-guided pleural biopsy using a semirigid thoracoscope with flexible forceps is lacking.

Hence, the COLLABORATION-I trial aims to evaluate whether pCLE-guided biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy can improve diagnostic yield while maintaining safety.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This inferiority randomised parallel study will be conducted in eight experienced diagnosis and treatment centres for pleural disease teaching hospitals in China (China-Japan Friendship Hospital, Shanghai Ruijin Hospital, Henan Provincial People’s Hospital, Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, Anhui Chest Hospital, Wuhan Pulmonary Hospital, The Second People’s Hospital of Gansu Province, Shandong Public Health Clinical Centre). All participating sites are equipped with a semirigid thoracoscope and pCLE probe.

Enrolment began on 11 March 2025, with the first patient recruited that day. The trial aims to finish by May 2027 and is actively recruiting participants.

Sample size calculation

This is a non-inferiority study with a 10% margin. It should be considered that the diagnostic yield of semirigid thoracoscopy with flexible forceps in patients with exudative pleural effusion is approximately 93%, and our initial experience showed a diagnostic yield of 95% for pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy.18 Assuming a dropout rate of 20%, a total of 158 patients with exudative pleural effusion need to be included in this study to achieve a precision level of 0.9 with α=0.05. Randomisation and grouping

Eligible patients will be randomly assigned to the conventional semirigid thoracoscopy and pCLE-guided semirigid thoracoscopy groups at a 1:1 ratio. An independent investigator from the chief centre (China-Japan Friendship Hospital) will use a Sealed Envelope online randomisation programme with block sizes of four (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists) to generate the randomisation sequence. The randomisation sequence and block size are concealed from the study investigators and staff.

Recruitment process

Eligible patients will be identified by attending physicians from both outpatient clinics and inpatient wards across the eight participating centres. Subsequently, the study team will screen these patients against the established eligibility criteria, furnish them with comprehensive study information and secure written informed consent before initiating any study-related procedures. Recruitment will be conducted on an ongoing basis until the target sample size of 158 patients is achieved.

Intervention procedure

(1) A total of 158 patients with exudative pleural effusion who meet the inclusion criteria will be enrolled in the study after providing written informed consent. No financial or other incentives will be offered to participants for their involvement in the study. Reimbursement of reasonable travel expenses will be provided when applicable.

(2) All participants will undergo complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, coagulation profile determination and electrocardiography. The size of effusions on chest radiographs is commonly graded, both in daily practice and research studies, according to a semiquantitative rule described in a previous study,19 as follows: 0 (no effusion), 1 (blunting of the costophrenic angle), 2 (<25% of the hemithorax but more than blunting), 3 (25%–50% of the hemithorax), 4 (50%–75% of the hemithorax) and 5 (>75% of the hemithorax).

(3) After excluding contraindications, participants will undergo semirigid thoracoscopy under mild sedation and local anaesthesia. Randomisation envelopes will be opened immediately prior to the procedure, and all participants will receive the randomly assigned procedure (conventional or pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy) according to the protocol.

(4) Procedure: patients with spontaneous breathing (fasting for 8 hours) under conscious sedation using incremental doses of midazolam 0.5–5 mg and observing complete aseptic precautions in a bronchoscopy suite will undergo thoracoscopy. Supplemental oxygen will be administered using an air-entrainment mask. Blood pressure will be monitored every 10 min, and oxygen saturation, heart rate and rhythm will be continuously monitored. The operators are consultant pulmonologists or pulmonary fellows with 2.5 years of training and assisted in at least 25 procedures under the direct supervision of one of the consultants. A standard training course in pCLE image identification will be used to train the thoracoscopists; only thoracoscopists who pass the final examination will be allowed to participate in this trial.

The patient is first placed in the lateral decubitus position with the affected side up and the arm raised above the head. A single-entry site is selected in the fourth or fifth intercostal space along the midaxillary line. The site is infiltrated with 2% lidocaine, and a 1–3 cm skin incision is made. Blunt dissection will be used to enter the pleural space, and the incision will be extended if necessary. A flexible trocar will be inserted, followed by the introduction of a semirigid thoracoscope (LTF-260; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The pleural fluid is aspirated, and air is allowed to enter the pleural space.

For patients assigned to receive pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy alone, the parietal, visceral and diaphragmatic pleura will be inspected, and eight biopsies will be obtained under direct vision in all suspected areas of the parietal pleura.

For patients assigned to receive pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy, pCLE will be introduced to detect suspected areas of the parietal pleura. The pCLE inspection will follow a standardised protocol with two main steps. First, any visible abnormalities under white light, such as nodules or thickening, will be examined with pCLE to evaluate intralesional heterogeneity and determine the best biopsy site. Second, the basal pleura will be systematically swept, covering at least two intercostal spaces above the costophrenic recess, including the diaphragmatic pleura and lower posterior chest wall, which are common sites for malignant involvement.20 21 Real-time pCLE images will be analysed for abnormal cellular patterns, guiding biopsy to these areas.16 The pCLE images will be interpreted in real-time by the operator during the procedure. Abnormal findings in pCLE are delineated according to established criteria from prior studies. Indicators of malignancy encompass pleomorphic cells exhibiting variability in size and shape, indistinct intercellular borders, a clustered or papillary architectural pattern and the disruption of the normal mesothelial arrangement. Conversely, features indicative of tuberculous or necrotic lesions include unstructured regions, disrupted cellular architecture and a heterogenous inflammatory milieu.17 22 The identification of any such abnormal characteristics designates the respective pleural region as the target for biopsy. After confirming the targeted area of the parietal pleura for pleural biopsy, the pCLE probe will be withdrawn, and a flexible forceps biopsy will be performed (figure 2A–F). This process will be repeated eight times, and eight samples will be obtained. All specimens will be transported in separate formalin containers. with biopsy sequences for histopathological analysis.

If an adequate pleural space could not be created, the procedure will be stopped. A 16–20 French intercostal drainage tube will be inserted before wound closure to evacuate air and fluid. If the thoracoscopic findings suggest malignancy (variable-sized nodules), iodopovidone (20 mL of 10% solution) pleurodesis will be performed during the procedure or later through a chest tube. Chest radiographs will be obtained 3 hours after the procedure. The chest tube will be removed only once the drainage decreases to <50 mL/d for two consecutive days, provided the effusion is non-purulent.

(5) Morphometrical and morphologic analysis of thoracoscopic biopsies: all biopsies will be processed conventionally by serial sectioning of at least eight H&E-stained sections, as per the standard procedure. All biopsies will be surveyed in terms of size and quality. The pathologist will be blinded to the technique used for obtaining the biopsies. For each patient, a definitive diagnosis will be made based on the pathology results. In addition, molecular analysis of malignant pleural diseases will be conducted as part of routine practice. Reference standard

The diagnosis in this trial will be established by the treating clinician based on all clinical, radiological, microbiological and pathological information. In addition, all the final diagnoses will be verified by an independent diagnosis verification panel, which includes three pulmonary and critical care medicine physicians, one radiologist and one pathologist.

Outcome

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the diagnostic yield of conventional pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy and pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy in patients with unknown exudative pleural effusion.23 The diagnostic yield in this study is identified as the proportion of all individuals undergoing pleural biopsy under evaluation in whom a specific diagnosis is established.24 Based on the existing literature and our preliminary experience, the expected diagnostic yield is approximately 93% for the control group (conventional pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy) and 95% for the intervention group (pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy). A pre-established non-inferiority margin of 10% has been set for this comparison.

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcomes are diagnostic sensitivity for specific diseases (including pleural mesothelioma, metastatic cancer and tuberculous pleurisy), procedure time, rate of adequate specimens for achieving molecular diagnosis and incidence of any complications.

Complications related to the procedure include prolonged air leaks, haemorrhage, subcutaneous emphysema, postoperative fever, empyema, wound infection, cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension and seeding of the chest wall from mesothelioma. Statistical analysis

According to the Reporting Standards for Diagnostic Testing, patients diagnosed with nonspecific pleurisy after 12 months of follow-up will be excluded from the calculation of diagnostic yield. Sensitivity is defined as the proportion of individuals with a specific disease (malignant pleural mesothelioma, metastatic cancer or tuberculous pleurisy), as determined by the reference standard, who have a positive test result. The negative likelihood ratio will be calculated as (1−sensitivity)/(specificity). Because there will be no false-positive findings in the biopsy results, this value will equal (1−sensitivity). In addition, a higher-dimensional contingency table will be used in this study to demonstrate the results of each biopsy method.24 The accuracy value with a 95% CI will be reported in this study. Subgroup analysis will be conducted according to pleural thickness and lesion morphology. In addition, an exploratory subgroup analysis will be conducted based on the white light semirigid thoracoscopic appearance, categorising cases into those with obvious tumour deposits visible and those with normal or slightly abnormal pleura. Then, the difference in diagnostic accuracy between pCLE-guided pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy and conventional pleural biopsy via semirigid thoracoscopy will be evaluated.

The chi-square test will be used to analyse the relationships between the categorical variables. For continuous variables, differences between three or more groups will be assessed using one-way analysis of variance and post-hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test (for normally distributed data) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (for non-normally distributed data). Differences between the two groups will be assessed using the t-test (for normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney test (for non-normally distributed data). Statistical significance will be set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS software (V.13.0; IBM). The final results of this trial will be reported following the STARD checklist. The STARD reporting guideline is available at https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/stard/.

Data collection and management

Each site will use standardised case report forms to gather baseline and outcome data. Outcome assessors will receive training, and pathologists will not know the group assignments. Data will be input into a centralised electronic database that is password-protected, with double entry and range checks, anonymised and stored securely at the coordinating centre. Data collection forms can be obtained from the corresponding author.

Data monitoring

A formal data monitoring committee will not be set up for this trial due to the established safety of semirigid thoracoscopy and pCLE, which are minimally invasive and have a low risk of serious adverse events. Instead, an independent endpoint adjudication committee will periodically review safety data and report serious adverse events to the ethics and steering committees. The steering committee will make safety-related decisions based on these reviews. No interim analyses or early stopping rules are planned. Final analyses will occur after the last patient completes follow-up. Adverse events will be recorded through both solicited and spontaneous reports, with serious events reported to the coordinating centre.

Discussion

This multicentre randomised controlled trial was designed to evaluate whether pCLE can improve the diagnostic accuracy (including diagnostic yield, sensitivity and negative likelihood ratio) of pleural biopsies performed using semirigid thoracoscopy in patients with undiagnosed exudative pleural effusion. While semirigid thoracoscopy is a well-established, minimally invasive technique with acceptable safety, challenges in diagnostic accuracy remain. This limitation is largely attributed to sampling errors and the mechanical constraints of flexible forceps compared with rigid instruments.

Several alternatives have been reported in previous studies, such as cryobiopsy and electrocautery-assisted biopsies. These methods could increase specimen size and quality but have not consistently translated into higher diagnostic yields.31 32 Similarly, adjunctive imaging modalities,33 34 such as AFI and narrow-band imaging, have shown potential to enhance the negative predictive value of thoracoscopy. However, their specificity is often compromised by confounding tissue characteristics such as fibrosis, necrosis and haemorrhage. Rapid on-site evaluation may offer additional benefits in selected cases but is limited by interpretative challenges, particularly in distinguishing reactive mesothelial proliferations from malignancy and in the context of ongoing pleural repair.35

pCLE offers a distinct advantage in this context by providing real-time in vivo microscopic imaging, which enables more precise targeting of biopsy sites based on cellular features rather than gross morphological cues. Previous studies have demonstrated its high sensitivity and specificity in differentiating malignant from benign pleural involvement, and our preliminary observations suggest that it may detect malignant cells even within fibrotic tissue. Consequently, the primary inquiry is whether pCLE provides additional diagnostic value beyond that of white light semirigid thoracoscopy. The application of pCLE is limited, as biopsies can be directly obtained from these observable lesions. Conversely, in patients exhibiting normal or only slightly abnormal parietal pleura, which is often characterised by non-adhesive fibrotic changes or subtle thickening, malignant or granulomatous cells may still be present at the cellular level despite an unremarkable macroscopic appearance. In such conditions, random or visually unguided biopsies are prone to missing the target, resulting in a high false-negative rate. Therefore, we posit that pCLE will be most beneficial in the subgroup of patients lacking obvious tumour deposits, particularly when fibrosis obscures the lesion boundaries. However, existing evidence is limited to small, single-centre studies.17 To evaluate this hypothesis, a pre-specified exploratory subgroup analysis based on thoracoscopic white light appearance, differentiating between the presence and absence of specific pathology findings

This trial incorporates several methodological safeguards to optimise internal validity. Participating physicians will undergo standardised pCLE training and competency assessments to reduce operator-dependent variability. Subgroup analyses will be conducted according to pleural thickness, with or without pleural nodularity, to explore whether pCLE is particularly advantageous for malignant pleural effusions with fibrotic adhesions that are difficult to localise visually.

This protocol has several limitations. First, physicians without pCLE experience may find it difficult to identify pCLE features during the procedure. To ensure that biopsies are taken from areas with abnormal pCLE images, standardised training and a pCLE image recognition test will be conducted, and only those who pass will participate. Second, stratified randomisation based on thoracoscopic appearance is challenging in practice and will not be used in this study design. We hypothesised that pCLE primarily improves the diagnostic efficiency of semirigid thoracoscopy by detecting invisible lesions. However, differences in preoperative preparation (sodium fluorescein injection in the pCLE group), lack of correlation between chest CT and thoracoscopic appearance, and the absence of a standard classification system make stratification impractical. Instead, subgroup analyses will be performed based on pleural thickness and lesion type (nodular/diffuse) to assess whether pCLE benefits the diagnosis of small pleural lesions. Additionally, an exploratory subgroup analysis will be conducted based on the semirigid thoracoscopic appearance under white light, distinguishing between cases with evident tumour deposits and those with normal or slightly abnormal pleura. It is important to acknowledge that this trial is not statistically powered to support this comparison, and therefore, the results should be interpreted as exploratory.
