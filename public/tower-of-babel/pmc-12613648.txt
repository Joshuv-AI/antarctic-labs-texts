Impact of delayed graft function on overall and graft survival in recipients of deceased donor kidney transplants
Authors: María Andrea Negret, Martha Patricia Rodriguez, Cándida Díaz-Brochero, Santiago Abril, Santiago Grillo, Paola Garcia-Padilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Delayed graft function (DGF) is a frequent complication among recipients of deceased donor kidney transplants, with an incidence rate ranging from 20% to 50%. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of DGF on both graft and overall patient survival. In a retrospective cohort of 293 patients who underwent transplantation between 2007 and 2021, demographic, clinical, and long-term follow-up data were analyzed. Patients with DGF had a significantly lower 5-year graft survival rate than did those without DGF (log-rank test: p = 0.004; DGF: 80.0%, 95% CI: 70.0% − 91.0% vs. no DGF: 92.0%, 95% CI: 89.2% − 95.9%); nevertheless, without significant differences over 10 years (log-rank test: p = 0.098; DGF: 80.0%, 95% CI: 70.0% − 91.0% vs. no DGF: 87.9%, 95% CI: 83.8% − 92.1%). Regarding the overall survival, no significant differences were observed over 5 years (log-rank test: p = 0.9; DGF: 92.7%, 95% CI: 86.1% − 99.0% vs. no DGF: 92.0%, 95% CI: 89%-96%) or at 10 years (log-rank test: p = 0.81; DGF: 89.1%, 95% CI: 81.2%-97.7% vs. no DGF: 87.9%, 95% CI: 83.8%-92.1%). Multivariate analysis indicated that DGF was not associated with an increased risk of graft loss or reduced recipient survival. These findings underscore the need for larger, prospective studies to further elucidate the long-term implications of DGF and outline clinical management strategies for kidney transplant recipients.Clinical trial number Not applicable

Introduction

In renal transplantation, delayed graft function (DGF) is mainly defined as needing dialysis within the first week after the transplant. This condition is primarily due to ischemia‒reperfusion injury, which involves microvascular inflammation, cell death, and apoptosis and can be influenced by various donor and recipient factors, including donor age, cold ischemia time, and the recipient body mass index [1]. This condition is particularly prevalent among recipients of deceased donor kidneys, with an incidence of 20%–50%, compared with 4%–10% in recipients of living donor kidneys [2]. The presence of DGF complicates the postoperative course, leading to longer hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and delayed post-transplant recovery [3].

While the immediate postoperative consequences of DGF are well documented [3], its long-term effects remain controversial. Some studies have demonstrated a significant association between DGF and reduced graft survival [4–6], whereas others have reported no such relationship [7]. Similarly, the impact of DGF on patient survival is a debated issue. Several studies have reported no significant differences in 5-year patient survival between those with DGF and those with immediate graft function [3]. In contrast, other analyses suggest a detrimental effect of DGF on patient outcomes. For instance, a retrospective study of 871 recipients of deceased donor kidneys revealed that DGF was associated with decreased patient survival, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.4 [8]. Additionally, data from the US Renal Data System registry involving 50,246 first-time transplant recipients revealed that DGF increased the risk of death with a functioning graft (HR: 1.83) [9].

Given these conflicting findings, this study aimed to assess whether there are differences in long-term graft and overall patient survival between kidney transplant recipients who develop DGF and those who do not.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to analyze survival outcomes among adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent deceased donor kidney transplantation at Hospital Universitario San Ignacio, Bogotá, Colombia, between December 2007 and December 2021 were considered for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they (1) received a living donor kidney transplant, (2) underwent combined organ transplantation (e.g., kidney–pancreas, kidney–liver), or (3) had incomplete follow-up data or missing key variables required for the analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of Pontificia Universidad Javeriana and Hospital Universitario San Ignacio, Bogotá (act number: 19/2023).

Data for this analysis were obtained from the standardized nephrology service database, where information is systematically recorded and updated at each patient visit. In cases of missing data, the information was supplemented through the institutional electronic medical record system.

The collected data included recipient characteristics such as age, sex, cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), dialysis modality, duration of dialysis, comorbidities, history of transfusions, and pregnancy; transplant-related factors such as transplant date, prior transplants, cold ischemia time, HLA mismatch, and induction therapy; and follow-up information such as follow-up duration, cause of graft loss, development of DGF and cause of death. For patients lost to follow-up, vital status data were obtained from the web portal of the General Social Security System in Health-ADRES, which registers information for affiliates of the Colombian health system. DGF was defined based on the need for dialysis within the first week [1, 2]. The etiology of CKD, graft loss, and cause of graft loss were determined from medical records and electronic registries.

The sample size for the study was calculated to detect a significant difference in survival functions via the log-rank test, adjusted for unbalanced allocation as recommended by Hsieh [10–12]. Based on prior literature evaluating the association between DGF and graft survival in deceased donor kidney transplants, reported hazard ratios have ranged from 1.8 to 2.5. Considering our patient population’s higher baseline prevalence of known DGF risk factors, including prolonged cold ischemia times and higher sensitization rates, we adopted an effect size (HR) of 2.4, which lies within the upper range reported in these studies. This value was chosen to ensure adequate sensitivity for detecting effect sizes previously described in comparable cohorts. Assuming a hazard ratio of 2.4 between patients with DGF and those without DGF, with a ratio of 1:4 between the DGF and non-DGF groups, an alpha level of 0.05, and 80% power, the required total sample size was estimated at 219 patients in the non-DGF group and 55 in the DGF group.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using relative and absolute frequencies, whereas continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges, as none of the continuous variables met the assumption of a normal distribution according to the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Differences between patients with and without DGF were assessed via the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann‒Whitney U test for continuous variables. Overall survival (OS) and graft loss were analyzed via the Kaplan‒Meier method. Patients who died with a functioning graft were censored in the calculation of graft loss. The log-rank test was used to compare survival curves between groups.

To assess the impact of DGF on overall recipient survival and graft loss, we applied Cox proportional hazards regression models adjusting for variables identified as significant in the univariate analysis, as well as factors previously associated with kidney transplant outcomes, including the presence of autoimmunity [13], HLA mismatch [14], number of transplants [15], and history of transfusions [13, 16]. Variables were selected for inclusion based on statistical significance in univariate analyses and biological plausibility, following recommendations from prior transplantation studies. Immunologic factors such as previous transplantation, prior transfusions, and HLA mismatch were prioritized given their well-established role as potential mediators between DGF and graft or patient survival. To minimize the risk of model overfitting given the number of events in our dataset, we applied the principle of parsimony, selecting covariates with stronger prior evidence of confounding or mediation. The statistical analysis followed a two-step approach: initial bivariate analysis, followed by multivariable modeling. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and confirmed through graphical inspection, which demonstrated parallelism between the DGF and non-DGF groups.

In a supplementary analysis, we repeated the graft survival analysis considering death (regardless of graft function) as an event rather than censoring these cases, using the same covariate adjustment strategy as in the primary model. This approach allowed us to evaluate whether the inclusion of death as an event altered the observed association between DGF and graft survival.

Finally, to account for competing events, we conducted a competing risk analysis to evaluate the effect of DGF on overall recipient survival, considering infection-related death and other causes of death as competing risks. In this analysis, we additionally adjusted the models for key recipient variables such as age and sex. Competing risk models were estimated using Fine and Gray’s subdistribution hazard model. All analyses were performed in RStudio, version 4.4.1.

Results

During the study period, a total of 344 kidney transplants were performed, of which 321 were from deceased donors and 23 from living donors. Following application of the exclusion criteria, 293 patients remained and were included in the final analysis. This corresponds to the entire eligible cohort during the study period. (Fig. 1).

The most common causes of CKD among patients without DGF were glomerulonephritis (primarily IgA nephropathy and focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis), followed by miscellaneous causes (including unknown etiology, obstructive uropathy, and neoplastic etiology), and type 2 diabetes mellitus. In contrast, the most common causes of CKD in patients who developed DGF were miscellaneous causes (including those mentioned), followed by hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. These differences between groups were statistically significant (p = 0.007). Additionally, a greater proportion of patients in the DGF group had undergone a previous transplant (p = 0.002) and had received prior transfusions (p = 0.001).

With respect to graft loss, in both groups, the most frequent cause was classified as “other” (50%), which primarily involved unknown causes, a lack of adherence to pharmacological treatment, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Specifically, in the DGF group, infections were the second most common cause (16.7%), whereas in the non-DGF group, acute rejection was the second most common cause (23.3%). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (Table 2). The leading cause of death in both groups was infection, with COVID-19 (37.9%) being considered separately due to the significant proportion of deaths attributed to this cause, followed by septic shock of bacterial etiology (37.9%).

The mean follow-up time was 110.0 ± 28.3 months (range: 12–120 months). The median follow-up was 120 months (IQR: 120–120 months), reflecting that most patients remained under follow-up until the end of the study period. Figure 2 shows the Kaplan Meier curves for death-censored graft survival. At 5 years of follow-up, the death-censored graft survival rates were 80.0% (95% CI 70.0% − 91.0%) for patients with DGF and 92.0% (95% CI 89.2% − 95.9%) for those without DGF (log-rank test P = 0.004). At the 10-year follow-up, the corresponding graft survival rates were 80.0% (95% CI 70.0% − 91.3%) for patients with DGF and 87.9% (95% CI 83.8% − 92.1%) for those without DGF (log-rank test P = 0.098).

Figure 3 shows the Kaplan‒Meier curves for overall patient survival. Overall survival at 5 years of follow-up was 92.7% (95% CI 86.1% − 99.0%) for patients with DGF and 92.0% (95% CI 89% − 96%) for those without DGF (log-rank test P = 0.9). At the 10-year follow-up, the overall survival rates were 89.1% (95% CI 81.2% − 97.7%) for patients with DGF and 87.9% (95% CI 83.8% − 92.1%) for those without DGF (log-rank test P = 0.81). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed a statistically significant association between overall survival and HLA mismatch (HR 1.37; 95% CI 1.05–1.79). (Table 4)

In a supplementary analysis, we evaluated overall graft survival including death—regardless of graft function—as an event rather than censoring these cases. The results were consistent with the primary death-censored analysis, as DGF was not significantly associated with an increased risk of overall graft loss when death was included as an event (HR 1.59; 95% CI: 0.79–3.18; p = 0.19).

In the competing risk analysis, DGF was not associated with mortality in either the infection-related death group (SHR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.31–2.86) or the all-cause death group (SHR = 1.08, 95% CI = 0.12–9.18) (Table 5).

Discussion

Kidney transplant recipients have the highest graft survival rates among all organ transplant recipients, with approximately 92% survival at one year for kidneys from deceased donors [17–19]. Identifying complications that affect graft and recipient survival, such as DGF, is crucial in renal transplantation research. DGF is a common complication, particularly in recipients of deceased donor kidneys [1]. While its short-term effects, such as prolonged hospital stays, are well understood, its long-term impact remains unclear [20]. This study aimed to assess differences in overall survival and graft survival between patients with and without DGF. At 5 years, graft survival was significantly greater in patients without DGF, but no difference was observed at 10 years, nor did DGF affect overall survival.

Our findings regarding graft survival at five years are consistent with those of previous studies. Redfield et al. (2014) reported that graft survival was significantly lower in patients with DGF than in those without (65% vs. 85%, respectively, p < 0.001) [21]. Similarly, Schiavelli et al. (2015) reported that patients who developed DGF after cadaveric kidney transplantation had lower 5-year graft survival, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.473) [22]. Furthermore, Foroutan et al. (2019), in a systematic review, identified DGF as a significant risk factor for graft loss one-year posttransplant (HR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.46–2.47) [18]. These findings underscore the importance of early DGF detection to guide the management and development of risk prediction models for high-risk recipients and donors.

We did not observe significant differences in overall survival between patients with and without DGF at the 5- and 10-year follow-ups. The association between DGF and overall survival remains underexplored and inconsistent in literature. Perez Gutierrez et al. (2013) reported a significant association between DGF and overall survival in a cohort of deceased donor renal transplant recipients [23], whereas Schiavelli et al. (2015) reported no such association, which aligns with our results (14). A UK study involving donors after circulatory death demonstrated that DGF lasting less than two weeks had no impact on graft or overall survival; however, if DGF persisted beyond 14 days, the risk of graft failure and recipient death nearly doubled (HR, 1.7; 95% CI, 1.3–2.3; p < 0.001) [24]. In contrast, our study focused solely on the presence of DGF, rather than its duration, which may explain the absence of an association with overall survival. The clinical implications of prolonged DGF highlight the need for further research to investigate how the duration of DGF influences both graft and recipient survival.

Importantly, when we repeated the survival analysis treating death—regardless of graft function—as an event rather than censoring these cases, the results were consistent with the primary death-censored analysis. This alternative definition of graft survival also showed no statistically significant association between DGF and overall graft loss, reinforcing the robustness of our primary findings and suggesting that the prognostic impact of DGF in our cohort is primarily driven by graft-related mechanisms rather than mortality with a functioning graft.

The most common cause of chronic kidney disease in patients without DGF was glomerulonephritis (including IgA nephropathy and focal segmental glomerulonephritis), followed by miscellaneous causes such as unknown etiology, obstructive uropathy, neoplastic conditions, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. In contrast, among patients who developed DGF, the predominant causes were similarly categorized as miscellaneous, with factors such as those seen in the non-DGF group, followed by arterial hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. A statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups, indicating that certain risk factors, particularly hypertension and diabetes, may play a more significant role in the development of DGF. These findings highlight the importance of identifying the underlying causes of chronic kidney disease, as specific etiologies may influence the likelihood of developing DGF posttransplant. Additionally, our results showed that patients with prior transplants or transfusions had a higher incidence of DGF, suggesting a potential immunological factor, as previously outlined in the extant literature. In a study conducted in the United States, Redfield et al. sought to evaluate the predictive factors of DGF in living donor recipients and its impact on the graft. The study results demonstrated that previous transplantation constituted a risk factor for DGF development, with a statistically significant association [21]. Although an association was identified between DGF and prior transplantation or transfusions, the impact of these factors on overall and graft survival remained nonsignificant in both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Consequently, while the identification of risk factors for DGF remains crucial, it is equally imperative to assess the impact of these factors on overall survival and graft survival to determine the necessity for intervention based on risk factors.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its results. One limitation is its retrospective design, which introduces the possibility of bias in data collection and patient sample selection. Additionally, the relatively small sample size and varying group sizes may limit the precision of our estimates, as the smaller group may not fully represent the characteristics or distribution of the population studied. To address this, we performed a differential sample calculation and conducted subgroup analyses, independently assessing the different causes of mortality by competing risk analysis.

Related limitations are the assumptions used for the sample size calculation. The effect size assumption (HR = 2.4) was derived from previous studies reporting hazard ratios between 1.8 and 2.5 for graft loss associated with DGF in deceased donor kidney transplants [11, 12]. While this approach ensured adequate power to detect effect sizes consistent with the higher end of previously published data, it also means that the study is underpowered to detect smaller but potentially clinically relevant differences. This should be taken into account when interpreting our findings, as smaller effect sizes may have gone undetected.

Also, our study did not account for factors such as variations in surgical techniques, immunosuppression protocols, and comorbidities, which could influence clinical outcomes. Furthermore, unmeasured confounding variables may impact the interpretation of the findings. The use of single-center data also limits the external validity of our results, as patient characteristics and management protocols may vary across institutions. Additionally, the study did not investigate other potential biomarkers or molecular factors that could affect DGF outcomes, which might offer a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying graft dysfunction. Future research with larger sample sizes, more diverse data, and a broader consideration of risk factors is needed to confirm these findings.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. The use of clearly defined criteria for DGF ensures consistency in the analysis. The sample size was adequate to achieve good representation of the population, and the median follow-up period of 120 months enabled the evaluation of both the short- and long-term effects of DGF on graft and recipient survival. Moreover, the retrospective cohort design allows for the assessment of a large number of patients over an extended period, providing valuable insight into real-world clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

DGF is a common complication in kidney transplant recipients from deceased donors. While its immediate effects on recovery are well established, its long-term impact on graft and patient survival remains unclear. This study revealed no significant association between DGF and reduced overall patient or graft survival at the 5- and 10-year follow-ups. However, DGF was linked to lower graft survival at 5 years, suggesting its more pronounced short-term impact, although this difference diminished over time. Additionally, factors such as previous transplantation and transfusions were associated with an increased risk of DGF, suggesting possible immunological influences. In conclusion, while DGF is an important consideration in post-transplant management, the findings suggest that it should not be a primary factor in long-term survival assessments, allowing for a more nuanced approach in follow-up and treatment.
