Stroke prevention in the Americas: from knowing to doing
Authors: Mikk Jürisson, Ave Voit, Margus Viigimaa, Jan O Johansson
Published: 2026

Stroke in the Americas is a story of preventable loss at scale. The region records over one million new strokes annually, yet most of this burden falls on populations with the least access to the interventions that could avert it.1,2 The Series on Stroke in the Americas published in the May 2026 issue of The Lancet Regional Health – Americas provides a comprehensive regional assessment of stroke epidemiology, risk factors, and prevention implementation across the Americas.3,4 The contributions are timely — but they also underscore how far the region remains from translating what is known into what is done.

The epidemiological picture is sobering. Although age-standardized stroke mortality declined across much of the Americas between 1990 and 2015, progress has stalled or reversed in several countries, and incidence is now rising among younger adults.1 The gradient between Haiti, where stroke mortality exceeds 185 per 100,000, and high-income settings such as Canada illustrates inequalities that are structural, not incidental. Stroke carries one of the highest disability weights of any non-communicable disease, meaning that behind every mortality statistic lies a far larger burden of long-term disability, lost productivity, and impoverished households.2 The INTERSTROKE study demonstrated that ten modifiable risk factors account for approximately 90% of the population-attributable risk of stroke globally5 — predominantly hypertension, but also dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and tobacco use — factors shared almost entirely with broader cardiovascular disease.2,5 Prevention strategies targeting these common pathways yield benefits extending well beyond stroke to ischemic heart disease, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease. Such prevention is also cost-effective: population-wide primary prevention of cardiovascular disease has been estimated to yield a return on investment of more than tenfold, even in low- and middle-income settings.6

The WHO HEARTS program and its regional adaptation, HEARTS in the Americas, represent scalable frameworks for translating this evidence into practice. Now covering 33 countries and over 7000 primary healthcare centers, HEARTS demonstrates that standardized clinical pathways for hypertension management are feasible across diverse health systems.7 The evolution toward HEARTS 2.0, integrating cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic care and opportunistic atrial fibrillation screening, represents a logical next step toward integrated chronic disease prevention at the primary care level.8

Yet critical questions remain. First, the region lacks evidence on comparative effectiveness–which combinations of interventions work best, for whom, and in what contexts? The HOPE-4 trial in Colombia showed that community health worker–led interventions can substantially improve blood pressure control and reduce cardiovascular risk,9 but such trials remain rare. The polypill (a fixed-dose combination now on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines) exemplifies an evidence-based innovation with strong potential to simplify treatment and improve adherence across the region.10

Second, health disparities in the Americas demand more than clinical pathways. Indigenous communities, Afro-descendant populations, and rural residents bear a disproportionate disease burden driven by structural determinants. Embedding equity metrics into program monitoring as mandatory performance indicators would represent a meaningful advance.

Third, the research gaps deserve stronger emphasis. Beyond descriptive epidemiology, the region needs investment in implementation science and innovative risk stratification. Emerging tools — including polygenic risk scores, which have shown promise in predicting cardiovascular and stroke risk,11 novel biomarkers, and refined risk prediction models — could identify high-risk individuals earlier and complement traditional assessment but require rigorous evaluation in the diverse populations of the Americas through pragmatic real-world trials before clinical integration.

The path forward demands integrated cardiovascular disease prevention at the primary care level, using HEARTS and similar platforms as operational backbones, national stroke policies embedded within universal health coverage frameworks, and simplified protocols including fixed-dose combinations that reduce therapeutic inertia. Good practices exist, and the Americas harbor genuine success stories — but not yet at scale. Closing the gap between evidence and implementation demands sustained political commitment, smarter research investment, and an unwavering focus on reaching those who carry the greatest burden. The science of cardiovascular and stroke prevention is mature enough to save millions of lives — the science of its delivery is not. That is where the next decade of work must concentrate.
