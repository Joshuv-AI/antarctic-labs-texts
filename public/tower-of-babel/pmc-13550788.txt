Unsampled large cribriform and intraductal carcinoma and the limits of risk stratification by prostate biopsy

Large cribriform glands (LC) and intraductal carcinoma (IDC) are dominant morphological features of lethal prostate cancer, associated with non‐organ‐confined disease, biochemical recurrence, metastasis, and prostate‐cancer specific mortality [1, 2, 3]. In a secondary analysis of the Prostate Testing for Cancer and Treatment (ProtecT) trial, all 21 metastatic or lethal events after radical prostatectomy (RP) occurred exclusively in cribriform‐positive patients over 15 years of follow‐up [4]. Similarly, a simplified histological model largely based on LC/IDC, and termed ‘unfavourable histology’, predicted metastasis and prostate cancer death with 100% sensitivity, outperforming Grade Group (GG) and pathological stage [2]. Identification of LC/IDC on biopsy influences clinical decision‐making, including active surveillance (AS) eligibility and treatment planning. However, biopsy detection remains limited, with poor sensitivity even with MRI‐targeted sampling [5], and absence does not reliably exclude aggressive disease.

To better describe markers of unsampled LC/IDC, greater attention has been placed on ‘borderline histology’ herein termed ‘borderline pattern 4 (BP4)’, defined by Gleason pattern 4 morphologies such as small cribriform, glomeruloid/glomerulation, and persistently poorly formed glands, which are distinct from but strongly associated with LC/IDC at RP [6]. Atypical intraductal proliferation (AIP), which falls short of IDC criteria but is considered a marker of unsampled high‐risk prostate cancer, likely represents a related phenomenon [7, 8]. However, whether these signals reliably identify patients harbouring unsampled aggressive disease remains incompletely defined.

We therefore evaluated the rate of biopsy‐to‐RP undersampling in a large contemporary RP cohort with detailed histological data to assess the diagnostic performance of biopsy for LC/IDC and characterise the pathological consequences of biopsy under‐detection.

The cohort comprised 1167 consecutive RP patients at our institution between 2023 and 2025 with paired biopsy and RP histology data. Biopsy grading was routinely combined with reporting of LC/IDC, BP4, AIP, or favourable (no LC/IDC or BP4). RP histology was classified similarly with the traditional RP grade and staging as well as using the relevant categories (favourable or LC/IDC [unfavourable]). Organ‐confined disease was defined as pathological T stage (pT) 2 and pathological N stage (pN) 0 (pT2N0) and non‐organ‐confined disease as ≥pT3 and/or nodal involvement (pN1).

Diagnostic performance metrics for biopsy LC/IDC predicting RP LC/IDC were calculated. To assess the impact of biopsy‐missed LC/IDC, we compared outcomes between false LC/IDC‐negative patients (biopsy favourable/BP4 with LC/IDC at RP) and true LC/IDC‐negative patients (biopsy favourable/BP4 without LC/IDC at RP). Multivariable logistic regression (Firth penalised for pN1) evaluated associations with adverse pathology, adjusting for age, pre‐biopsy PSA, percentage of biopsy cores involved, and biopsy GG.

The cohort had a median (interquartile range [IQR]) age of 66 (61–70) years, median (IQR) PSA level of 6.3 (4.7–9.4) ng/mL, and median (IQR) time from biopsy to RP of 2.8 (1.9–4.1) months. On biopsy, most patients had GG 2–3 disease (n = 867 [74.3%]). Biopsy classification was favourable in 35%, BP4 in 31%, LC/IDC in 28%, and AIP in 6%. At RP, LC/IDC was identified in 62% of cases, while 38% had favourable histology.

Biopsy LC/IDC showed high specificity (95%) but low sensitivity (42%) for predicting LC/IDC at RP (positive predictive value 93%, negative predictive value 49%, accuracy 62%), with only 7% of LC/IDC calls unconfirmed on final pathology. LC/IDC was identified at RP in 47% of tumours initially classified as favourable or BP4 on biopsy. When stratified, rates were 40% for favourable, 55% for BP4, and 85% for AIP cases (Fig. 1).

In all, LC/IDC at RP accounted for 77% of non‐organ‐confined cases. False LC/IDC‐negative patients had markedly higher rates of ≥pT3 than true negatives (64% vs 34%; P < 0.001). On multivariable analysis, false‐negative biopsy was independently associated with ≥pT3 disease after adjustment for PSA, age, percentage of cores involved, and biopsy GG (odds ratio [OR] 3.09, 95% CI 2.24–4.29; P < 0.001).

Nodal metastases occurred in 5.5% of false LC/IDC‐negative patients vs 0.5% of true negatives (20/361 vs 2/406; P < 0.001), and on Firth regression false‐negative biopsy remained independently associated with pN1 (OR 6.56, 95% CI 1.86–34.94; P = 0.002), while biopsy GG was not independently associated with ≥pT3 or pN1 after adjustment. Time from biopsy to RP was not associated with re‐classification to LC/IDC at RP (OR 0.99 per month, 95% CI 0.98–1.02; P = 0.95), supporting sampling limitations rather than short‐term progression as the main driver.

To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest contemporary analyses evaluating biopsy‐to‐RP concordance of LC/IDC in prostate cancer using detailed histological classification. Biopsy under detection of LC/IDC was frequent and clinically consequential: nearly half of favourable/BP4 tumours harboured LC/IDC at final pathology, and these patients faced substantially worse pathology despite appearing LC/IDC‐negative on biopsy, findings with direct implications for long‐term oncological outcomes. Importantly, LC/IDC were present in the vast majority of non‐organ‐confined tumours, reinforcing their central role in aggressive prostate cancer behaviour.

Biopsy detection of LC/IDC was highly specific but insensitive, identifying only 42% of LC/IDC cases ultimately present at RP. The predominance of undersampling and the absence of an association with biopsy‐to‐RP interval argue against overclassification and rapid tumour progression, instead reflecting intrinsic sampling limitations of prostate biopsy [5]. Accordingly, absence of LC/IDC on biopsy does not reliably predict indolent final pathology.

These findings have important implications for treatment selection and risk stratification. Patients with occult LC/IDC, indistinguishable from truly LC/IDC‐negative patients on biopsy, faced significantly higher odds of ≥pT3 and pN1 disease after full adjustment. Biopsy GG did not retain independent significance after adjustment, suggesting that LC/IDC capture aggressive tumour behaviour not fully reflected by grade alone, consistent with prior reports [1, 2].

This is particularly consequential given that LC/IDC are established contraindications to AS, yet their missed detection on biopsy, due to sampling, resulted in adverse outcomes in up to 47% of patients initially deemed eligible [8]. More broadly, prostate cancer treatment decisions, including surgical planning, radiation strategies, adjuvant therapy, and selection for focal treatment, rely heavily on biopsy findings, so failure to recognise adverse patterns may lead to underestimation of disease risk and inappropriate treatment selection.

Borderline (BP4) features, such as small‐gland cribriform, represent an emerging consideration in pre‐treatment risk stratification. We found that over half of tumours with BP4 on biopsy harboured LC/IDC at RP, consistent with recent descriptions of ‘borderline histology’ as a marker of unsampled aggressive disease, rather than a biologically distinct entity [6]. AIP appears to follow a similar pattern: 85% of biopsy AIP cases demonstrated LC/IDC at RP, reinforcing that AIP frequently reflects incompletely sampled high‐risk morphology, in line with current recommendations for repeat biopsy when it is detected [7, 8].

These considerations are especially relevant in the intermediate‐risk group, where treatment intensity varies widely and biopsy‐based risk assessment drives clinical decisions. Routine documentation of these features by pathologists and familiarity with their predictive significance among urological oncologists may improve risk stratification and treatment selection in this population.

Importantly, future work should improve preoperative LC/IDC detection and identify predictors of unsampled disease. Integrating detailed histological assessment with imaging, genomic, and molecular data offers a promising path toward more precise tumour classification and tailored therapeutic decision‐making.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and lack of long‐term outcome data. Nonetheless, the large contemporary cohort and robust findings support the clinical relevance of these results and highlight the importance of histological subtype in disease characterisation and subsequent management.

In summary, LC/IDC are markedly under‐sampled on prostate biopsy, and their absence does not reliably exclude prostate cancer with lethal potential. Patients with occult LC/IDC face substantially higher rates of non‐organ‐confined disease than truly LC/IDC‐negative patients, independent of standard clinical parameters. Incorporating related patterns, particularly BP4 and AIP, alongside GG may improve risk stratification and identify patients harbouring unsampled adverse disease, directly informing treatment selection and patient counselling.
