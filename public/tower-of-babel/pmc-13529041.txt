Evaluating the impact of blue-filtering intraocular lenses on macular oxidative stress: A comparative analysis of 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine levels in the retinal tissue of donor eyes
Authors: Karvannan Sevugamurthi, Padmapriya Sivashanmugam, Sujay Jaju, P. S. Janani, Vijayakrishna Sakthivel, Navaneeth Kumar, Vignesh Elamurugan, Jaishree Pandian, Sankha Amarakoon, Tos T. J. M. Berendschot, Siddharth Narendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the impact of blue-filtering intraocular lenses (BFIOLs) on retinal oxidative stress by comparing 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) levels in the macular retina and submacular retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) of donor eyes with BFIOLs and non-BFIOLs.Design:Cross-sectional laboratory study using postmortem human donor eyes.Methods:Fifty-eight eyes from 39 donors were categorized as BFIOL (n = 16), non-BFIOL (n = 24), or phakic (n = 18). Macular retina and submacular RPE were dissected, genomic DNA extracted, and 8-OHdG quantified using ELISA. 8-OHdG levels were compared across lens groups, and associations with age and sex were examined.Results:Submacular RPE had higher 8-OHdG levels than the macular retina (0.95 vs 0.44 ng/mL; P < 0.0001). No significant differences in 8-OHdG were observed between BFIOL and non-BFIOL eyes in either tissue (all P ≥ 0.09). Age was not associated with 8-OHdG, whereas male donors showed higher RPE 8-OHdG levels than female donors (P = 0.03).Conclusions:Macular retinal and RPE 8-OHdG levels did not differ significantly by IOL type. These biochemical observations, considered alongside existing clinical data, suggest that any retinal protective effect of BFIOLs on oxidative DNA damage is likely to be modest.

The introduction of blue-filtering intraocular lenses (BFIOLs) was primarily driven by the proposed benefits of attenuating short-wavelength visible light to reduce photochemical injury and retinal oxidative stress.[1] Short-wavelength blue light (400–500 nm) has been implicated in driving oxidative stress within the neurosensory retina and RPE, and removal of the age-yellowed crystalline lens during cataract surgery may increase retinal exposure to this potentially phototoxic wavelength range.[2345] Against this background, BFIOLs were designed to mimic the spectral transmission of the aged lens and thereby limit cumulative oxidative damage to macular tissues.

However, despite these theoretical underpinnings and supportive preclinical data indicating that blue light attenuation can reduce oxidative injury and cell death in experimental models, clinical studies have not conclusively demonstrated that BFIOLs confer meaningful protection at the level of retinal structure or disease endpoints.[6] Randomized trials and large observational cohorts comparing BFIOLs with conventional UV-only filtering IOLs have generally failed to show clear reductions in age-related macular degeneration incidence, progression, or other macular outcomes, highlighting a persistent gap between mechanistic plausibility and clinically observable benefit.[7]

A substantial body of preclinical investigations into short-wavelength light–induced retinal injury has used 8 hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a DNA guanine oxidation product, as a pivotal biomarker of oxidative stress.[8910] 8-OHdG is widely regarded as a sensitive and specific indicator of cumulative oxidative DNA damage in both the neurosensory retina and RPE and has been employed to quantify blue light–induced injury as well as to evaluate the protective potential of optical blue light shielding strategies.[11]

In this study, we aimed to determine and compare the levels of 8-OHdG in the macular retina and submacular RPE of human donor eyes implanted with BFIOLs and those with non-BFIOLs, thereby providing a direct, tissue-level assessment of whether long-term blue-light filtering by BFIOLs is associated with reduced macular oxidative DNA damage.

Methods

Human tissue

This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board. Deidentified human cadaver donor eyes were obtained from the Rotary Aravind Eye Bank (Aravind Eye Care System, India). All donor eyes were graded by two independent retina specialists to exclude any posterior segment pathology prior to inclusion. Only eyes preserved within 6 hours of donor death were included in the study. Donor age, sex, and medical history were noted; eyes with any evidence of retinal disease or other significant ocular pathology were excluded from analysis.

Lens status classification

Lens status was categorized as phakic, pseudophakic with a blue-filtering IOL (BFIOL), or pseudophakic with a non–blue-filtering IOL (non-BFIOL). In pseudophakic eyes, the presence or absence of a blue-light filter in the IOL was confirmed by visual assessment and expert identification. During dissection, each IOL was carefully extracted and photographed under standardized lighting conditions against a neutral background. Two experienced cataract surgeons, masked to the 8-OHdG results, independently reviewed the photographs to determine the specific IOL model and its blue-filtering status based on optic coloration, manufacturer engravings or markings where visible, and overall lens characteristics. Discrepancies were resolved by joint discussion and consensus.

Ocular tissue dissection and sample collection

Human donor eye dissection and tissue isolation were performed following established protocols for retinal studies.[12] Under sterile conditions, a circumferential sclerotomy was made and the anterior segment (cornea, iris, lens, and if present the IOL) was removed to expose the posterior pole. The posterior eyecup was inspected to confirm an intact macula without gross pathology. A 6 mm diameter trephine punch was used to delineate a central macular tissue button, centered on the fovea and within the major vascular arcades. Using fine forceps, the macular neural retina was gently lifted and peeled off from the underlying RPE–choroid complex. This provided two matched samples from the same region for each eye: the macular retina and the underlying submacular RPE (RPE-choroid) tissue. The RPE-choroid tissue was carefully separated from sclera, ensuring the RPE layer remained included. Each tissue sample was immediately placed into a sterile cryovial, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80°C until further analysis.

8-OHdG quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from each macular retina sample and its corresponding submacular RPE sample using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen Inc., Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for tissue samples. 8-OHdG was quantified from extracted DNA using a commercially available competitive ELISA kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a Tecan Infinite 200 PRO® multimode plate reader, and concentrations were calculated from a standard curve. All samples were assayed in duplicate, with negative controls included to account for background signal.

Statistical analysis

Data distributions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality, and because 8-OHdG measurements deviated from normality in one or more groups, nonparametric methods were used for univariate comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used as an omnibus test to compare 8-OHdG levels across the three lens groups. When the omnibus test was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, with Holm–Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Therefore, a significant Kruskal–Wallis result was interpreted as evidence of overall group heterogeneity, whereas specific between-group differences were inferred only if Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons remained significant. To account for the nonindependence of fellow eyes from the same donor, log-transformed 8-OHdG levels were compared across lens groups using linear mixed-effects models with donor as a random intercept and lens type, age, and sex as fixed effects, after exclusion of three donors whose fellow eyes belonged to different lens-type groups; pairwise contrasts were back-transformed to fold-change ratios with 95% confidence intervals and adjusted for multiplicity using the Holm method. Approximate normality of mixed-model residuals on the log scale was verified by visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Donor characteristics

A total of 58 eyes from 39 human donors were included in the analysis. Of these, 16 eyes had BFIOLs, 24 eyes had non-BFIOLs, and 18 eyes were phakic (natural lens present). The mean age of the donors was 75.9 ± 11.0 years (range 48–95 years), and there was no significant age difference among the BFIOL, non-BFIOL, and phakic groups (P = 0.07). Donor gender was 59% male (23 male, 16 female donors, corresponding to 34 male eyes and 24 female eyes), with a similar gender distribution across the lens groups (P = 0.27). Influence of age and gender on macular and submacular 8-OHdG levels

Across all 58 eyes, submacular RPE 8-OHdG levels were significantly higher than macular retinal levels, with median concentrations of 0.95 ng/mL (IQR 0.67–2.37) versus 0.44 ng/mL (IQR 0.33–0.60), respectively (P < 0.0001) [Fig. 1a]. RPE and retinal 8-OHdG levels were strongly correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.87, 95% CI 0.77–0.91; Spearman’s ρ =0.73; 95% CI 0.58–0.83; both P < 0.001) [Fig. 1b]. Age was not associated with 8-OHdG levels in either tissue (RPE: ρ =0.06, 95% CI − 0.20 to 0.31, P = 0.65; retina: ρ =0.10, 95% CI − 0.16 to 0.35, P = 0.46), and post-mortem interval likewise showed no significant relationship (RPE: ρ = –0.17, 95% CI − 0.41 to 0.10, P = 0.21; retina: ρ = –0.10, 95% CI − 0.35 to 0.16, P = 0.46). In contrast, gender showed a consistent association with 8-OHdG levels. Eyes from male donors had higher RPE 8-OHdG (median 1.27 ng/mL, IQR 0.81–3.27) than eyes from female donors (median 0.79 ng/mL, IQR 0.60–1.16; P = 0.03 [Fig. 1a]. The macular retina also showed higher 8-OHdG levels in males (median 0.51 ng/mL, IQR 0.36–0.65) than in females (median 0.39 ng/mL, IQR 0.24–0.46), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.08). Among 19 donors with both eyes, submacular RPE 8-OHdG showed a strong inter-eye correlation [Pearson r = 0.93, 95% CI 0.81–0.97; Spearman ρ =0.73, 95% CI 0.41–0.89; both P < 0.001; Fig. 1c], whereas macular retinal 8-OHdG showed a moderate, nonsignificant Spearman correlation (ρ = 0.42, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.73; P = 0.07).

Impact of IOL type on macular 8-OHdG levels

Macular 8-OHdG levels were comparable across BFIOL, non-BFIOL, and phakic eyes. For the submacular RPE, the median (IQR) 8-OHdG values were 0.79 ng/mL (0.49–1.89) in BFIOL eyes, 1.31 ng/mL (0.95–3.29) in non-BFIOL eyes, and 0.86 ng/mL (0.70–1.11) in phakic eyes. For the macular retina, the corresponding medians were 0.35 ng/mL (0.23–0.45), 0.57 ng/mL (0.34–0.66), and 0.44 ng/mL (0.36–0.51), respectively. An overall comparison across the three lens groups showed a statistically significant difference in RPE 8-OHdG levels (Kruskal–Wallis ε² =0.110, 95% CI 0.018–0.338, P = 0.04), whereas retinal 8-OHdG levels did not differ significantly (ε² = 0.078, 95% CI 0.006–0.300, P = 0.11) [Fig. 2a and b]. However, in pairwise post-hoc testing, none of the between-group comparisons for RPE remained statistically significant (BFIOL vs non-BFIOL: Hodges–Lehmann median difference −0.51 ng/mL, 95% CI −1.50 to 0.00, Holm-adjusted P = 0.10; phakic vs non-BFIOL: −0.44 ng/mL, 95% CI −1.77 to −0.06, Holm-adjusted P = 0.09; BFIOL vs phakic: −0.10 ng/mL, 95% CI −0.37 to +0.45, Holm-adjusted P = 0.52). In the mixed-effects analysis (52 eyes from 36 donors; RPE intraclass correlation 0.94), no statistically significant differences were detected between lens groups in either tissue (RPE Wald χ²(2) = 2.35, P = 0.31; retina χ²(2) = 2.79, P = 0.25); the BFIOL versus non-BFIOL contrast was nonsignificant for both RPE 8-OHdG (95% CI 0.32–1.16, P = 0.13) and retinal 8-OHdG (95% CI 0.53–1.66, P = 0.82).

Discussion

In this study, we found no statistically significant differences in macular 8-OHdG levels between eyes implanted with BFIOLs and those with non-BFIOLs, in either the neurosensory retina or the submacular RPE. To our knowledge, this is the first study to address this question using direct biochemical quantification of oxidative DNA damage in human donor macular tissue. We selected 8-OHdG as the primary endpoint because it is a well-validated marker of oxidative DNA injury and has been shown to accumulate in ocular tissues during the development and progression of macular degeneration. It has also been widely used in preclinical models of light-induced retinal damage, where reductions in 8-OHdG serve as a marker of protection by yellow-tinted or blue-attenuating filters.[11131415] These data support 8-OHdG as an appropriate marker for assessing the proposed photoprotective effect of BFIOLs. The absence of a detectable difference in our study suggests that any such effect is likely modest in the human macula.

Our findings are consistent with the broader clinical literature evaluating blue-light filtering optics. Two meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials, including a Cochrane review of 51 trials involving more than 5000 eyes, found no clinically meaningful differences in postoperative visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, or overall visual performance between blue-filtering and standard UV-only IOLs.[71617] Similarly, large retrospective and registry-based studies have not demonstrated consistent reductions in AMD incidence, delayed neovascular conversion, or meaningful slowing of macular atrophy with BFIOL implantation.[1819] Population-based studies have also not shown consistent advantages for broader systemic or neurologic outcomes, although one cohort reported a lower risk of glaucoma development, but not progression, among BFIOL recipients.[20212223] Thus, the absence of a measurable 8-OHdG difference in our study is concordant with clinical data showing no consistent macular protective signal.

The discrepancy between supportive preclinical data and limited human evidence may reflect fundamental differences in exposure conditions. Experimental models typically use acute, high-intensity, narrow-band blue or blue-violet light to induce retinal injury, whereas human exposure is chronic, lower intensity, spectrally broader, and behaviorally modulated.[2425] In addition, cataract surgery occurs late in life, after decades of cumulative oxidative stress, and individual differences in sunlight exposure, external photoprotection, systemic antioxidant status, and macular pigment density may obscure modest IOL-dependent effects.[26] Although narrow-band filter studies suggest that approximately 60% attenuation of 440-nm light can prevent retinal damage, many contemporary BFIOLs operate near the lower end of this range, which may partly explain why experimental photoprotection does not translate into measurable reductions in macular 8-OHdG or consistent clinical benefit in human eyes.[1127] Together, these differences help explain why the robust protection seen in acute preclinical paradigms does not translate into measurable reductions in macular 8-OHdG, or into consistent clinical benefit, in human eyes implanted with BFIOLs.

In addition to lens-type comparisons, we noted that 8-OHdG levels were higher in both the macular retina and submacular RPE in eyes from male donors than in those from female donors, reaching statistical significance in the RPE (P = 0.03) and showing a similar nonsignificant trend in the retina (P = 0.08). Similar sex-related differences in oxidative stress have been reported across human and animal studies, in which males typically exhibit greater oxidative damage and reduced antioxidant capacity compared with females.[282930] Consistent with established biology, 8-OHdG concentrations in our dataset were also higher in the RPE than in the neurosensory retina, reflecting the greater metabolic activity and chronic oxidative load of the RPE.[3132]

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest, which reduces power to detect small effect sizes and limits our ability to adjust for potential confounders. Second, although the use of postmortem tissue can introduce variability related to death-to-preservation interval and agonal events, we restricted inclusion to eyes preserved within 6 hours and followed a standardized protocol across all specimens. Moreover, multiple independent studies across human autopsy tissue, urine, CSF, and experimental models have shown that 8-OHdG remains stable over typical postmortem intervals, suggesting that postmortem delay is unlikely to have materially influenced our measurements.[3334] Third, we did not have access to the date of cataract surgery or duration of IOL implantation for pseudophakic donor eyes; therefore, the cumulative duration of exposure to BFIOL or non-BFIOL optics could not be assessed. Although diabetes mellitus status was available and did not differ significantly across lens groups, data on glycemic control, duration of diabetes, hypertension, other systemic comorbidities, systemic medications, antioxidant supplementation, smoking status, occupation, lifetime sunlight exposure, and use of external photoprotection such as sunglasses or hats were not routinely available in the deidentified donor records. Similarly, precise IOL model details and spectral transmission curves were unavailable for all eyes. As an observational tissue study, our analysis can address associations but cannot establish causal effects of IOL type on macular oxidative injury. In addition, because a subset of donors contributed both eyes, residual intra-donor correlation in 8-OHdG levels may have reduced the effective sample size. The modest number of donors and eyes per lens category limited our ability to perform robust donor-level or mixed-effects analyses with stable estimates; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and confirmed in larger donor cohorts. IOL classification was based on standardized photographs of explanted lenses, independently reviewed by two experienced cataract surgeons masked to the 8-OHdG results; nevertheless, residual misclassification, particularly between subtle blue-filtering and non-blue-filtering optics, cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, 8-OHdG represents only one dimension of oxidative injury; complementary metrics such as lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, or mitochondrial dysfunction were not assessed and may reveal subtler or pathway-specific differences not captured by a single biochemical endpoint.

Conclusion

Taken together with existing clinical evidence, our findings indicate that BFIOLs were not associated with a detectable reduction in macular oxidative DNA damage, as measured by 8-OHdG, in this donor-eye cohort. This biochemical observation does not establish broader clinical equivalence between BFIOLs and non-BFIOLs but suggests that any photoprotective benefit of blue-light filtration on macular oxidative DNA damage, if present, is likely to be modest.
