Interest in Genetic Testing for Hereditary Cancer Risk: Associations with Genetic Familiarity, Self-Efficacy, and Sociodemographics
Authors: DaLaina Marie Cameron, Emerson Dusic, Tesla NikolaTheoryn, Faith Beers, Sarah Knerr, Catharine Wang, Deborah Bowen, Elizabeth Swisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGenetic testing (GT) for hereditary cancer risk enables early detection and prevention, yet many individuals who could benefit do not pursue testing. Limited genetic familiarity and lower genetic self-efficacy may act as barriers. This study examined relationships among patient-reported genetic familiarity, genetic self-efficacy, sociodemographic characteristics, and interest in GT.MethodsData were drawn from the Early Detection of Genetic Risk (EDGE) baseline survey (n = 2,319), distributed to a random sample of patients aged ≥25 years from participating primary care clinics in Washington, MT, and WY. Patients were contacted via email and mailed letter in January 2021, prior to implementation of the EDGE intervention. Bivariate logistic regression assessed associations between sociodemographic factors and genetic familiarity and self-efficacy. Multivariable logistic regression examined independent associations between sociodemographic factors, genetic familiarity, genetic self-efficacy, and interest in GT.ResultsIn multivariable analyses, higher genetic familiarity was strongly associated with interest in GT (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.67; 95% CI: 1.47–4.86). Higher income (aOR = 2.43; 95% CI: 1.43–4.13) and female sex compared with male (aOR = 1.80; 95% CI: 1.16–2.40) were also positively associated with interest in GT. Participants aged over 65 years were significantly less likely to express interest in GT (aOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.13–0.60).ConclusionThe strong association between genetic familiarity and interest in GT suggests that increasing basic genetic knowledge may be an effective starting point for educational interventions aimed at improving testing uptake. Understanding drivers of interest in GT can inform targeted strategies to enhance hereditary cancer prevention and early detection efforts.

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death in the USA, taking nearly 600,000 lives in 2020 alone [1]. Genetic testing (GT) for hereditary cancer can facilitate early cancer detection and prevention for a significant fraction of these cases [2]. Still, some individuals who may benefit from GT for hereditary cancers decline or do not pursue testing when it is offered [3–5]. The low uptake of GT raises the critical question of what key, modifiable factors influence a person’s interest in completing testing. Studies in both the clinical and direct-to-consumer (DTC) space have shown that interest in and positive attitudes toward GT increase the likelihood of an individual proceeding with testing [6–9]. Thus, understanding the drivers of interest may be a critical step toward increasing uptake in GT for hereditary cancers. However, whether this low uptake is due to a lack of interest in testing or testing barriers is less clear.

Genetic familiarity is defined as an individual’s subjective recognition and understanding of key genetic terminology [10, 11]. Though there are limited studies that explicitly explore the relationship between familiarity with genetics and interest, prior studies have demonstrated that genetic familiarity is a predictor of how individuals understand genetic information in broader contexts, such as the media [12–14]. Genetic self-efficacy is a related but distinct psychosocial construct that is defined as one’s confidence in their ability to understand genetic information as it relates to their health [15, 16]. This construct has been shown to be positively associated with increased motivation to pursue GT for hereditary cancer syndromes [17]. Though genetic familiarity and self-efficacy are often studied alongside each other, they are separate constructs that do not always operate in tandem [13, 16, 18]. Further, demographic factors such as age, educational attainment, income, and race/ethnicity have been associated with both genetic literacy, of which genetic familiarity is one component [11, 13, 19, 20], and interest in GT [21–23]. Assessing an individual’s genetic literacy, genetic self-efficacy, and other personal factors is key to understanding what motivates their interest in GT.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the relationship between sociodemographics, genetic familiarity, and genetic self-efficacy. Further, we explored the subsequent association between genetic self-efficacy, genetic familiarity, and interest in testing. We hypothesize that genetic familiarity and self-efficacy will be positively associated with interest in GT.

Methods

EDGE Study: Baseline Survey

This study uses data from the Early Detection of Genetic Risk (EDGE) Study – a cluster-randomized trial which aimed to compare and evaluate two strategies for implementing GT for hereditary cancer into the primary care setting [24, 25]. EDGE worked with two healthcare systems, one in Washington state and one in Montana and northern WY. Eligible individuals included active patients over 25 years old from either healthcare systems. Participants for the baseline survey used in this study were recruited using a random sample of patients from six primary care clinics in each healthcare system. We contacted 6,588 patients across all twelve clinics – 549 from each clinic – with the intent to recruit 200 from each clinic. Selected patients were contacted via email to complete a patient baseline survey in January 2021, before the implementation of the EDGE program. Patients who did not respond by email were mailed a paper copy of the survey to fill out and return. Participants were compensated with a USD 10 Tango gift card for completing the survey.

Measures

Sociodemographic Characteristics. Instruments and items from the survey used for analysis included age, sex, race, ethnicity, education, household income, personal cancer history, and family cancer history (see online suppl. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552069). We created a composite variable grouping individuals who identified their race as White and their ethnicity as non-Hispanic relative to individuals who identified their race as Black or African American, Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, Native American/American Indian/Alaskan Native, multiracial, other, or ethnicity as Hispanic, who we describe as People of Color (POC).

Genetic Familiarity. To measure genetic familiarity, we used a shortened version of the Genetic Literacy and Comprehension (GLAC) instrument (see online suppl. 2), which asks individuals to rate their familiarity with 8 genetic terms (genetic, chromosome, susceptibility, mutation, variation, abnormality, hereditary, and sporadic) on a 7-point Likert scale [13]. Internal consistency across the 8 genetic terms was high (α = 0.95). Previous research has measured genetic familiarity using the same approach [10].

Genetic Self-Efficacy. Genetic self-efficacy was measured by 5 questions related to an individual’s confidence in their ability to understand the relationship between genetics, health, and disease risk, using a 5-point Likert scale (see online suppl. 3) [15, 16]. This measure had high internal consistency in our sample (α = 0.92).

Interest in GT for Hereditary Risk. Interest in GT for hereditary cancer risk was measured with the question, “If your personal and familial history suggested you were at high risk for cancer, how interested would you be in having GT?” Response structure was a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all interested” to “extremely interested.”

Data Analysis

Statistical Analyses

Data from the patient baseline survey were analyzed in R. Demographic variables were checked for outliers. Individuals who responded “other” or “prefer not to answer” to sociodemographic questions were excluded from analyses using those variables. Descriptive statistics are provided for each variable overall and by each of the three outcomes (Table 1). Genetic self-efficacy, genetic familiarity, and interest in GT were all relatively high in the sample (Fig. 1). Genetic self-efficacy, genetic familiarity, and interest in GT were, therefore, dichotomized into 2 groups (higher versus lower) based on the distribution of responses. Groups were created by calculating the 10th percentile cutoff and labeling anyone at or below that threshold as lower and anyone above the threshold as higher. Splitting the measures this way ensures that individuals with higher genetic familiarity, self-efficacy, and interest are not placed in the lower group simply because they are below the population median.

Results

Sociodemographics

A total of 2,319 patients responded to the survey (35.2% response rate), with an average of 194 responses per clinic. The sample was largely composed of individuals who were over the age of 65 years old (43%), had first-degree relatives with cancer (61%), and did not have a personal history of cancer (74%). Additionally, participants largely had higher genetic self-efficacy (89%) and interest in GT (79%) (Table 1).

Genetic Self-Efficacy

The odds of having higher genetic self-efficacy were moderately increased for women when compared to men (OR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.04–1.77; p = 0.03). The odds of higher genetic self-efficacy were also greater for individuals with a higher education level (OR, 2.75; 2.10–3.61; p < 0.001) and a higher household income (OR, 2.17; 95% CI, 1.57–2.99; p < 0.001). Age, race/ethnicity, personal cancer history, and familial history of cancer were not significantly associated with genetic self-efficacy (Table 2).

Genetic Familiarity

In bivariate analyses, the odds of having higher genetic familiarity were lower for those over 65 years of age versus those aged 25–44 (OR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.31–0.76; p = 0.002) and for POC versus White individuals (OR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.41–0.97; p = 0.03). Conversely, the odds of having higher genetic familiarity were moderately increased for women (OR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.04–1.78, p = 0.03) and significantly increased for those with an associate’s degree or higher (OR, 6.47, 95% CI, 4.75–8.81, p < 0.001) and those with a household income greater than USD 75,000 (OR, 4.14, 95% CI 2.87–5.99; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Interest in GT for Hereditary Cancer Risk

While genetic self-efficacy was independently associated with interest (OR = 1.82 [95% CI = 1.36–2.44]), it was no longer significant in the adjusted model (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.89 [95% CI = 0.47–1.70]). Individuals with higher genetic familiarity had 2.67 greater odds (95% CI = 1.47–4.86) of reporting higher interest in GT. Being 65 or older versus 25 to 44 years old was negatively associated with interest (aOR = 0.28 [95% CI = 0.13–0.60]). Female versus male sex (aOR 1.80 [95% CI = 1.16–2.40]) and higher household income (aOR = 2.43 [95% CI = 1.43–4.13]) were both positively associated with interest in testing.

Discussion

This study sought to examine the associations between genetic familiarity, genetic self-efficacy, and interest in GT. In our multivariable model, genetic familiarity, sex, and income were associated with increased interest in testing. These results demonstrated that an individual’s self-reported familiarity with key genetic terminology, specifically, was independently associated with interest in GT within our sample. Genetic familiarity’s positive relationship with interest in GT aligns with and expands upon the existing literature. This finding also poses a possible explanation for the success of mass-media campaigns in the DTC space in increasing interest and subsequent uptake in testing. This is exemplified by DTC consumer marketing by Myriad Genetics and Angelina Jolie’s op-ed regarding her personal healthcare decision-making following GT [26], which served to functionally increase the public’s familiarity with terms such as “BRCA,” “gene,” and GT for hereditary cancer syndromes [27–29]. These campaigns raise awareness, and subsequently familiarity with GT for hereditary cancer, by exposing the public to genetic terminology, which our findings suggest may be sufficient to increase interest in GT for hereditary cancer risk. The lack of association between genetic self-efficacy and interest in GT for hereditary cancer risk is also informative because it suggests that one’s confidence in their ability to utilize the results of GT may be a less critical factor in prompting initial interest in GT for hereditary risk. However, self-efficacy may play a more vital role later in the process, such as when patients are tasked with following up on referrals, ordering their kit, interpreting results, or making decisions regarding follow-up care [15, 18].

The persistent association between higher income and interest in GT for hereditary cancer risk, even after adjustment, is well documented in the literature and underscores that perceived financial access (affordability) may limit interest in GT separately from genetic familiarity or self-efficacy [23]. The relationship between interest in testing and personal/familial history of cancer has been inconsistent across the literature; our study found no association between personal/familial cancer history and interest in testing [30, 31]. It is possible that having a history of cancer impacts interest in a multitude of ways that could impact the directionality, or presence, of associations between the two. For instance, some individuals with a familial history of cancer may already engage in increased cancer surveillance due to their family history alone, thus negating the importance of GT for hereditary cancer risk. Conversely, a separate cohort of individuals with a family history of cancer may have increased interest in GT to validate the assumed risk and subsequently their surveillance behaviors.

Age was differentially associated with genetic familiarity, self-efficacy, and interest. While older age (≥65) was associated with having lower genetic familiarity and interest in GT, it was not associated with lower genetic self-efficacy. This illustrates that for individuals 65 and over, they are not less confident in their ability to utilize genetic information but are less likely to be familiar with, or interested in, genetics and GT. This may reflect a cohort effect in which older adults may not have been exposed to genetics during their formal education due to the relatively recent development of the scientific field. Lack of interest in testing may stem from limited perceived utility of GT, which has been documented in prior research [13]. Given that older family members who complete GT play a vital role in cascade testing by enabling younger relatives to pursue preventative testing themselves, there is a need to explore what interventions might be most effective [32]. Our findings suggest those aimed at increasing testing among individuals over 65 years of age should focus predominately on increasing familiarity and interest in testing rather than on increasing self-efficacy or skills needed for testing.

Our findings also found differences between sexes, with men reporting lower rates of genetic familiarity, self-efficacy, and interest in GT. Prior literature indicates that there are inconsistent data on how gender influences interest in testing [33], and this reflects the complex ways in which gender impacts health behaviors and access to care. Perceived susceptibility has been shown to predict interest in testing [6] and may, in part, explain lower interest among men. These findings align with evidence that women are more frequently positioned as recipients and communicators of genetic information through caregiving and reproductive roles [33]. Together these findings align with the need for gender-responsive strategies that increase men’s familiarity with genetics and communicate the relevance of GT.

Lastly, the trends in our data that we observed for POC align with prior research and history of systemic inequities within the healthcare system. Underrepresentation of people from historically marginalized communities in genetics research and patient-reported experiences of implicit bias within healthcare settings continue to persist [20, 34]. These sociocultural factors create significant barriers that extend beyond the two constructs explored here and must be addressed through systemic change that builds trust, diversifies research participation, and ensures equitable access to GT.

The primary strength of this study is its large sample size and recruitment from two distinct healthcare systems located in different states throughout the USA. However, several limitations must be considered. Notably, the EDGE study did not use the component of the GLAC measure that evaluated genetic knowledge, which measures objective knowledge rather than familiarity with genetics terminology. As a result, we do not examine the impact of genetic knowledge on interest in GT or its relationship with demographic factors. However, the positive association between increased genetic knowledge and willingness to pursue testing is well documented in the literature [19, 35–37]. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits our ability to identify a causal relationship between our outcomes and variables of interest. Lastly, we condensed responses such that all POC were grouped together (descriptive statistics for all races/ethnicity responses is provided in online suppl. 2) due to our sample consisting of predominantly White and not Hispanic and/or Latino individuals. The small proportion of POC in our study means that no major conclusion can be made regarding how systemic racism impacts genetic familiarity, genetic self-efficacy, and their association with testing interest in our sample.

Future research should explore whether educational interventions centered around exposing the public to core genetics topics can effectively increase testing and ultimately improve GT within primary care settings. The DTC GT landscape also presents an opportunity to expand access to preventative care. Additional research is needed to fully understand how the increased availability of these services has shaped the public’s familiarity with genetics and whether it can be utilized as a gateway to clinical GT.

Conclusions

This study suggests self-reported genetic familiarity has a positive relationship with interest in GT for hereditary cancer risk after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, whereas no independent association was found with genetic self-efficacy. These findings provide practical insight that can be used to design educational interventions aimed at increasing GT uptake. Streamlining and simplifying key concepts around GT may be a more effective strategy for increasing interest in hereditary cancer GT than focusing on building a person’s confidence in their ability to utilize their test results. Ultimately, making GT more accessible may increase interest by reducing the barrier to preventative care and subsequently enable early detection of heritable conditions, such as hereditary cancer syndromes.
