Acral Vitiligo: A Comprehensive Review of Clinical Features, Pathogenesis, and Novel Therapeutic Advances
Authors: Ting Song, Shun Guo, Jia Liu, Cheng Tan, Yue Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcral vitiligo, a distinct subtype predominantly affecting the distal extremities (hands and feet), presents significant clinical challenges due to its treatment resistance and profound psychosocial impact. This narrative review systematically synthesizes current knowledge on the epidemiology, unique clinical presentations, and the specific pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this recalcitrant variant. We critically appraise recent advances in diagnosis and treatment, integrating findings from clinical studies, experimental researches, and case reports. The central aim is to provide a nuanced, evidence-based understanding of acral vitiligo to guide clinical management. We critically evaluate contemporary treatment modalities, including the emerging roles of Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors and biologic agents, while also addressing the crucial dimension of psychosocial burden. Finally, we delineate key knowledge gaps—particularly the lack of acral‑specific outcome measures and head‑to‑head comparative trials—and propose future research directions to improve outcomes for patients with this therapeutically orphaned subtype.

Introduction

Acral vitiligo, which primarily affects the extremities—especially their distal parts, including the hands, feet, and digits—poses a formidable clinical challenge. Clinically, it is notorious for its recalcitrance to conventional therapies, consistently yielding inferior and delayed repigmentation compared with lesions on the face, neck, or trunk. Moreover, acral lesions on exposed and functionally critical areas profoundly impair quality of life, body image, and social interactions, imposing a substantial disease burden.1 The resistance to conventional treatments results from multifactorial, encompassing anatomical barriers—impaired drug penetration and limited melanocyte reservoirs—and a local immune milieu characterized by persistent tissue-resident memory T cells.2 Conventional monotherapies and even optimized phototherapy achieve only negligible repigmentation in acral areas. While JAK inhibitors offer a promising mechanistic breakthrough, their limited efficacy at acral sites indicates that systemic immunomodulation alone is insufficient and must be combined with strategies that directly target local anatomical and immunological barriers.3–15

Nevertheless, the integration of mechanistic and therapeutic insights into a cohesive clinical framework for acral vitiligo remains hampered by substantial knowledge gaps. No existing synthesis has systematically translated mechanistic insights into clinical strategies specifically for acral vitiligo. There is a lack of evidence-based guidance for clinicians, given the absence of acral-specific outcome measures, the paucity of comparative trials, and unresolved maintenance therapy questions. Accordingly, this review has three primary objectives: (i) to consolidate current understanding of the anatomical and immunological underpinnings of recalcitrance; (ii) to critically appraise emerging medical, procedural, and surgical interventions, with emphasis on combination strategies; and (iii) to delineate key knowledge gaps—including the lack of standardized endpoints, comparative evidence, and long‑term management data—thereby charting a roadmap for future research and fostering more personalized, biology‑driven management of this therapeutically orphaned subtype.

Epidemiology and Clinical Features of Acral Vitiligo

Epidemiological Distribution and Demographic Characteristics

Acral vitiligo represents a significant subtype within the broader spectrum of non-segmental vitiligo. A systematic review focusing on pediatric vitiligo populations indicates that the acral type is the third most common subtype of non-segmental vitiligo, with a prevalence of approximately 17.8%, ranking only behind generalized and localized forms.16 This epidemiological data underscores its clinical relevance, particularly in younger populations. The condition is not exclusive to children; it can manifest across both pediatric and adult age groups. However, variations in prevalence have been observed across different geographical regions and ethnicities, suggesting potential influences from environmental triggers or genetic predispositions.

Furthermore, specific clinical associations highlight the distinct nature of acral involvement. Research points to a significant correlation between acral vitiligo and a positive Koebner phenomenon (isomorphic response), where new lesions develop at sites of skin trauma.17 This association indicates a higher disease activity and instability, suggesting that these patients may suffer a more dynamic and progressive course. The link to disease activity is further supported by the results that vitiligo vulgaris (which often includes acral presentations) showed a higher frequency of metabolic syndrome (48.9%) compared to purely acral and segmental types. Moreover, metabolic syndrome was more common in patients with higher disease activity (VIDA) and severity (VASI) scores.18 This intertwining of epidemiological patterns, demographic variability, and markers of activity paints a complex picture of acral vitiligo, positioning it as a subtype with unique pathogenic and prognostic implications that warrant careful clinical assessment and monitoring.

Unique Clinical Manifestations and Typing

The clinical presentation of acral vitiligo is characterized by distinctive, well-demarcated, milky-white depigmented macules and patches. These lesions predominantly affect specific anatomical sites, most notably the distal extremities including the fingers, toes, palms, and soles, as well as the wrists and ankles.19 The distribution of these patches is typically symmetrical, aligning with the generalized nature of non-segmental vitiligo. The rate of progression, however, is highly variable among individuals; some cases remain stable for extended periods, while others exhibit rapid expansion. Clinical complexity arises as acral vitiligo can coexist with other types, such as segmental vitiligo, or may serve as an initial presentation that later evolves into a more widespread, generalized form.20

To further refine clinical characterization, a recent Japanese study systematically analyzed hand vitiligo lesions in non-segmental vitiligo patients using cluster analysis and proposed a classification into four distinct subtypes based on lesion distribution patterns (n=140): focal/scattered (46.4%), distal digit (31.4%), universal (12.9%), and proximal digit (9.2%).21 The focal/scattered type shows a distinctive prevalence in pediatric cases. The distal digit type is characterized by lesions predominantly distributed on the periungual and distal portions of the digits and is associated with smoking or the Koebner phenomenon. The universal type is a distinct subtype with onset at older age and the poorest treatment response, while the proximal digit type is the rarest subtype, with onset at a young age.21 This classification underscores the heterogeneity within acral/hand vitiligo and highlights the need for subtype-specific management strategies.22 The clinical picture is thus defined by its specific topographic predilection, variable course, potential for co-morbidity with other subtypes, and the notable therapeutic resistance of acral lesions, all of which dictate a tailored management strategy (Figure 1). Research Progress on the Pathogenesis of Acral Vitiligo

Autoimmunity and Cytokine Network

The core mechanism of vitiligo, including its acral variant, remains an autoimmune attack targeting melanocytes, with CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.23 This autoimmune pathogenesis is further highlighted by its strong association with other autoimmune conditions, particularly thyroid disease. A systematic review showed patients with concurrent thyroid disease are prone to acral vitiligo and wrist depigmentation; autoimmune thyroid disease is the only comorbidity linked to distinct vitiligo lesion distribution.24 Generally, this correlation is strong enough that lack of hand depigmented macules may rule out concurrent autoimmune thyroid lesions.24 This link extends to disease prognosis, as acral involvement and thyroid disease are independent predictors of recurrence/relapse in non-segmental vitiligo, with patients having both factors facing a significantly elevated hazard.25

The cytokine milieu is integral to this autoimmune dysregulation. Studies investigating the interaction between vitiligo and sleep have measured various serum cytokines. Interestingly, it was found that higher levels of IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 were associated with previous narrowband UVB phototherapy, suggesting a treatment-mediated immunomodulatory effect.26 Furthermore, more severe insomnia in vitiligo patients was related to increased IL-17A levels, hinting at a potential neuro-immune-circadian axis that may influence disease activity.26 The recalcitrant nature of acral lesions to treatment, such as topical ruxolitinib, further underscores the localized intensity of the pathogenic immune response in these areas.22 Collectively, these findings reinforce that acral vitiligo is deeply rooted in autoimmune pathophysiology, driven by cytotoxic T cells and modulated by a complex network of cytokines and systemic autoimmune associations, particularly with thyroid dysfunction.

Oxidative Stress and the Role of Circadian Rhythm Genes

Systemic oxidative stress is particularly pronounced in acral vitiligo, although direct tissue catalase data across subtypes are lacking. The consistent resistance of acral lesions across diverse treatments—including microneedling with 5‑fluorouracil27 and topical ruxolitinib22—indirectly points to a severely impaired local microenvironment, likely characterized by oxidative damage and compromised melanocyte viability.

The interplay between oxidative stress, immune function, and circadian biology is an emerging area of interest. Sleep disruption is prevalent in vitiligo patients and is associated with impaired quality of life.26 Although no specific circadian gene polymorphisms like BMAL1 rs2279287 were identified, previous study establishes a tangible relationship among sleep quality, immune cytokines, and vitiligo.26 Since circadian rhythms govern metabolic and inflammatory pathways, their disruption could exacerbate local oxidative stress and inflammatory signals in susceptible areas like the acral skin. This concept is supported by the finding that sleep efficiency was statistically worse in the vitiligo group, and several domains of general health were significantly impaired.26 The acral regions, being extremities, may be more vulnerable to circadian-driven variations in blood flow, temperature, and local antioxidant capacity. Therefore, the convergence of systemic oxidative stress, antioxidant defense failure in acral skin, and circadian rhythm disturbances likely creates a permissive environment for sustained autoimmune attack and melanocyte loss, leading to the treatment resistance in acral lesions.

Drug Induction and Special Associations

Emerging evidence highlights that certain drugs can induce or exacerbate vitiligo, particularly the acral phenotype. A case series on the dermatological toxicities of tebentafusp, a bispecific drug for metastatic uveal melanoma, reported that patients developed vitiligo-like lesions.23 This is a direct example of a drug-induced, immune-mediated attack on melanocytes, as tebentafusp specifically targets the melanocyte-associated antigen gp100, engaging CD8+ T cells and leading to depigmentation.23 Though not a PIK3CA inhibitor, this mechanism illustrates that targeted therapies disrupting intracellular signaling or directly activating immunity against melanocyte antigens may induce vitiligo.

Another case report describes a patient with both vitiligo and peripheral T-cell lymphoma who presented with digital acral vascular syndrome, a condition of digital ischemia.28 Furthermore, a study on the cutaneous pathology of erythromelalgia, a painful acral vascular disorder, found that 1 out of 9 patients had concurrent vitiligo.29 These associations suggest shared or intersecting pathogenic pathways involving microvascular compromise, autonomic dysfunction, and autoimmunity that may preferentially affect acral sites. These drug-induced and special association cases are crucial as they provide mechanistic insights, suggesting that vitiligo, especially on acral sites, can be precipitated by a confluence of factors including direct immune activation by drugs, underlying systemic autoimmune or neoplastic conditions, and local vascular or metabolic dysregulation (Figure 2). Comorbidities and Systemic Impact of Acral Vitiligo

Associated Autoimmune Diseases

Acral vitiligo is strongly linked to autoimmune comorbidities, reflecting its systemic nature. Among these, thyroid disease shows the most robust association. Interestingly, patients with thyroid pathology are predisposed to acral and wrist depigmentation, and this pattern is so specific that the absence of hand macules may effectively exclude concurrent autoimmune thyroid disease, though the underlying mechanism remains unclear.24 Crucially, when acral involvement and thyroid disease co-occur, the risk of relapse escalates synergistically (HR 2.44), far exceeding that of either factor alone.25 Beyond thyroid disease, other autoimmune conditions independently predict recurrence in non-segmental vitiligo (adjusted HR 1.73).25 These findings position acral vitiligo—particularly when progressive or extensive—as a marker of heightened systemic autoimmune burden, underscoring the need for routine screening for thyroid and other immune-mediated disorders.

Psychosocial Impact and Metabolic Associations

The psychosocial burden of acral vitiligo is substantial, stemming from its predilection for exposed, poorly concealable areas such as the hands and face. This visibility drives significant stigmatization, life stress, and quality-of-life impairment.1 A study in which 60% of the vitiligo sample had acral involvement documented significantly poorer sleep quality (PSQI) and sleep efficiency versus controls; moreover, Insomnia Severity Index scores positively correlated with serum IL-17A levels, suggesting a bidirectional loop wherein sleep disruption and inflammation reciprocally amplify each other.26 Beyond psychological distress, the chronic inflammatory state of non-segmental vitiligo may predispose to dyslipidemia and heightened cardiovascular risk. Sleep and circadian disruption, also prevalent in this population, further disturb lipid and glucose homeostasis.26 Notably, elevated IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 levels were associated with prior narrowband UVB therapy, indicating that immunomodulatory treatments can reshape cytokine networks with potential metabolic consequences.26 However, direct evidence specifically addressing the acral subtype as an independent determinant of these systemic risks remains limited, and current data are largely derived from broader non-segmental cohorts.

Diagnosis and Disease Activity Assessment

Clinical Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnosis of acral vitiligo is primarily based on its characteristic clinical presentation, which involves the appearance of acquired, milky-white depigmented macules and patches specifically localized to acral sites such as the hands, feet, fingers, and toes.19 A critical diagnostic tool is Wood’s lamp examination, which enhances the visibility of these lesions by revealing a bright blue-white fluorescence, thereby confirming the complete loss of melanin pigment.20 This examination is particularly useful for distinguishing subtle depigmentation from hypopigmentation and for assessing the true extent of involvement. However, accurate diagnosis necessitates careful differentiation from several other conditions that can present with hypopigmented or depigmented lesions on acral skin. Key differential diagnoses include post-inflammatory hypopigmentation, which follows a history of prior skin inflammation; nevus depigmentosus (achromic nevus), a stable, congenital hypomelanosis; pityriasis versicolor, caused by Malassezia yeast, which may show a yellowish fluorescence under Wood’s lamp; and morphea (localized scleroderma), which presents with indurated plaques and may have associated pigmentary changes.12

In diagnostically challenging cases where clinical features are atypical or overlap with other disorders, a skin biopsy can provide definitive histopathological confirmation. The hallmark pathological finding in vitiligo is a marked reduction or complete absence of melanocytes and melanin pigment in the epidermis, often accompanied by a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate at the dermo-epidermal junction in active lesions, supporting the autoimmune basis of the disease.12 This histopathological evidence is crucial for ruling out other mimics, such as hypopigmented mycosis fungoides or early amelanotic melanoma, which requires a different management approach.30 Another case linking acral lentiginous melanoma with subsequent spontaneous vitiligo vulgaris underscores the shared antigenic targets between melanoma cells and normal melanocytes, which can trigger an autoimmune depigmentation response.31 Therefore, a comprehensive diagnostic approach combining thorough clinical examination, Wood’s lamp assessment, and histopathological evaluation when indicated is essential for the accurate diagnosis of acral vitiligo.

Disease Activity and Prognosis Assessment Tools

Quantifying disease activity and prognosis in acral vitiligo is essential for guiding treatment decisions and management. Standardized clinical scoring systems are widely used in clinic. The Vitiligo Disease Activity (VIDA) score is a temporal tool that categorizes disease activity based on the appearance of new lesions or the extension of existing ones within specific time frames, providing a semi-quantitative measure of stability or progression.4 For assessing the extent of depigmentation, the Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI) is a commonly employed method that calculates the percentage of body surface area affected, factoring in the degree of depigmentation within each lesion.4 A critical clinical marker of active disease, particularly relevant to the acral variant, is the Koebner phenomenon or isomorphic response, where new depigmented lesions develop at sites of specific trauma.32 This phenomenon is a strong indicator of ongoing disease activity.

Research into potential biomarkers aims to provide more objective measures. Investigations into melanocyte biology have identified molecules like Lymphocyte Enhancer-Binding Factor 1 (LEF1), which is involved in melanocyte proliferation and migration. A follow-up study demonstrated that LEF1 expression levels increased in both acral and non-acral vitiligo lesions after treatment with NB-UVB phototherapy, and this increase was associated with the degree of repigmentation achieved.2 While the change in LEF1 expression from baseline did not differ significantly between acral and non-acral sites, its modulation is implicated in the repigmentation process, suggesting its potential as a tissue-based marker of treatment response.2 Furthermore, dermoscopic features offer a non-invasive means to assess activity. A prospective observational study found that features like satellite lesions and a micro-Koebner phenomenon on dermoscopy were strongly associated with active, unstable vitiligo, regardless of the lesion site.32 Conversely, perifollicular repigmentation was a dermoscopic sign of stabilizing or repigmenting disease, although this association was noted to be significant primarily at non-acral sites, highlighting a potential difference in repigmentation patterns for acral lesions which lack hair follicles.32

Prognostically, acral involvement itself is a significant predictor of a more challenging disease course. A large retrospective cohort study identified acral involvement as an independent predictor of recurrence or relapse in non-segmental vitiligo (adjusted HR 1.56).25 The risk was substantially higher when acral involvement coexisted with thyroid disease, indicating that phenotypic and autoimmune factors combined create a high-risk profile for disease instability post-treatment.24 This underscores the importance of integrating clinical scores, dermoscopic findings, and comorbidity assessment for a comprehensive evaluation of disease activity and long-term prognosis in acral vitiligo.

Established Therapeutic Modalities: Mechanisms, Barriers, and the Rationale for Synergy

Phototherapy and Topical Agents: Intrinsic Limitations

The unsatisfactory response of acral vitiligo to conventional phototherapy and topical immunomodulators stems from convergent anatomical, pharmacokinetic, and immunological barriers. High-quality split-body randomized trials have demonstrated that NB-UVB, while effective in stabilizing disease activity, yields negligible visible repigmentation distal to the wrists and ankles, irrespective of device or dose (evidence level: 1b).4 This establishes a definitive ceiling effect for phototherapy in these locations. Retrospective analyses confirmed that most acral lesions achieve only minimal or no meaningful improvement with NB-UVB alone (evidence level: 4).33 Likewise, topical calcineurin inhibitors and corticosteroids, though efficacious on the face and trunk, are consistently outperformed at acral sites (evidence level: 1b).34 Monotherapy, regardless of the agent, is therefore insufficient for most patients with acral vitiligo, necessitating combination strategies that address distinct aspects of the pathogenic cascade.

Systemic Immunomodulation: Stabilization and Synergy

In progressive disease, systemic corticosteroids (eg, oral mini‑pulse regimens) remain a pragmatic approach to rapidly achieve stabilization, creating a therapeutic window for subsequent repigmentation.35 Oral JAK inhibitors have broadened this armamentarium. By blocking the IFN‑γ/JAK‑STAT axis, they directly suppress CD8⁺ T‑cell recruitment and activation. However, site‑specific heterogeneity persists; acral lesions consistently show inferior repigmentation relative to facial or truncal sites, as documented in case series (evidence level: 4).9 Topical ruxolitinib and oral agents (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) have demonstrated reproducible efficacy in refractory vitiligo, including acral subtypes. Nevertheless, a consistent finding is the inferior response at acral sites compared to facial and cervical areas, observed both in randomized trials (evidence level: 1b).34 These results suggest that systemic immune suppression alone cannot overcome local constraints.

Emerging Targeted Therapies: Current Evidence and Unresolved Questions

JAK Inhibitors: Established Efficacy and Site‑Dependent Heterogeneity

JAK inhibitors represent the most significant pharmacological advance in vitiligo management. However, therapeutic efficacy exhibits substantial heterogeneity, which likely reflects multifactorial resistance—inadequate penetration, sparse melanocyte reservoirs, and a persistent local immune milieu—rather than intrinsic failure of JAK inhibition.

To overcome this limitation, combination with phototherapy has proven particularly effective. Systematic reviews and meta‑analyses confirm that JAK inhibitors combined with NB‑UVB significantly enhance repigmentation in non‑segmental vitiligo, with pronounced benefit in acral regions (evidence level: 1a).11 Observational data further suggest that postoperative JAK inhibitors following autologous melanocyte transplantation (eg, NCES) may improve outcomes in resistant sites, presumably by protecting grafted melanocytes from immune destruction (evidence level: 2b).15 Collectively, these findings position JAK inhibitors as a key component of multimodal therapy for acral vitiligo, rather than a standalone definitive treatment.

Beyond JAK: Biologics, Oxidative Stress Modulation, and Regenerative Approaches

Several parallel avenues are under investigation. Isolated case reports of repigmentation with the IL‑23 inhibitor tildrakizumab provide preliminary signals,3 but controlled trials are needed to define its role. Topical pseudo‑catalase formulations, targeting oxidative stress, have shown limited efficacy as monotherapy in acral vitiligo and remain adjunctive at best.3

Regenerative strategies have also advanced. The identification of dermal melanocyte progenitors as a follicle‑independent melanocyte source in acral skin provides a biological rationale for new interventions. Split-body RCT have shown that platelet‑rich plasma (PRP) promotes their differentiation via the IGFBP‑2/RhoA/YAP pathway.36 Clinical translation, however, has yielded variable results. One split‑body randomized trial found that PRP combined with fractional CO2 laser and excimer lamp was superior to a tacrolimus‑based regimen for acral lesions (evidence level: 1b),37 whereas another double‑blind study reported no additional benefit when PRP was used as the NCES suspension medium (evidence level: 1b).38 These divergent findings underscore that delivery context and combinatorial partners are as critical as the biological agent itself.

Physical enhancement techniques have also been evaluated. A randomized trial comparing fractional CO2 laser versus microneedling as adjuncts to NB‑UVB and topical corticosteroids found both significantly improved repigmentation in resistant areas, including acral sites, with microneedling showing marginally superior marginal repigmentation (evidence level: 1b).39 Other small‑scale studies have reported efficacy with fractional CO2 laser combined with topical 5‑fluorouracil7 or phenytoin,40 and with excimer light plus topical calcipotriol (evidence level: 1b).6 Carboxytherapy combined with NB‑UVB has also shown significant improvement in recalcitrant acral areas (evidence level: 1b).5 Conversely, a randomized trial found that needling or microneedling added no therapeutic benefit to NB‑UVB in stable acral vitiligo and was associated with Koebnerization risk (evidence level: 1b),41 indicating that physical trauma is not universally beneficial and must be applied with caution in active or unstable disease. Treatment Challenges and Future Prospects

Mechanisms of Treatment Resistance and Countermeasures

The treatment resistance in acral vitiligo arises from multiple anatomical, immunological, and regenerativebarriers, which result in suboptimal outcomes with surgical grafting;13 even when narrowband UVB upregulates LEF1, repigmentation remains unsatisfactory at acral sites;2 moreover, long‑lived TrM cells constitute a persistent source of relapse and resistance.28 Therefore, future strategies must transcend single interventions and shift towards integrated, multi‑targeted combinatorial approaches.

Enhancing drug penetration via physical approaches (eg, microneedling or fractional lasers) is conceptually sound, but the clinical benefit of such adjuncts remains context‑dependent and should be weighed against the risk of Koebnerisation in active disease. Likewise, combining systemic immunomodulators with phototherapy or surgical grafts can theoretically target both immune and regenerative defects, yet the optimal timing, sequence, and duration of such combinations are undefined. A particularly critical unanswered question is when to discontinue treatment for acral lesions. In the absence of evidence‑based guidelines, a pragmatic approach is to continue maintenance therapy, aiming to consolidate response and mitigate the high relapse risk characteristic of these sites. More fundamentally, durable remission likely requires direct depletion or functional silencing of TrM cells, a frontier that awaits clinically applicable strategies.

Individualized Treatment Strategy

The management of acral vitiligo must be highly individualized, integrating disease stage (active vs stable), lesion location, patient age, psychological status, and systemic comorbidities. In active disease, stabilization takes priority, and prophylactic topical immunomodulators (eg, tacrolimus) may reduce new lesions;42 in stable disease, surgical grafting (NCES, SBEG, or STSG43) becomes a primary option, with choice tailored to lesion size, site, and patient tolerance. Phototherapy offers negligible repigmentation for lesions distal to the wrists and ankles, necessitating realistic expectations. Given the prominent psychosocial burden from exposed sites, a multidisciplinary collaboration involving dermatology, psychology/psychiatry, endocrinology (for screening autoimmune thyroid diseases), and ophthalmology is essential for holistic care. Treatment decisions should be shared with patients, respecting their preferences and quality‑of‑life goals; for those unsuitable for or declining active therapy, cosmetic camouflage provides a valuable adjunctive measure.

Research Directions and Unmet Clinical Needs

Future research on acral vitiligo should converge on three core priorities. First, large‑scale prospective longitudinal studies are urgently needed to define the natural history and prognostic factors of this subtype. Second, there remains a marked paucity of high‑quality randomized controlled trials specifically targeting acral sites. Most current evidence derives from subgroup analyses or uncontrolled studies; rigorous RCTs are essential to systematically evaluate the long‑term efficacy and safety. Such trials should also incorporate head‑to‑head comparisons to determine the optimal physical enhancement strategy and standardized treatment protocols for distinct acral subsites. Third, the development of sensitive and specific predictive biomarkers represents a critical step towards precision medicine. The current reliance on subjective clinical assessments limits individualized management; promising candidates include immune activation markers (CXCL9/CXCL10), melanocyte stress signals, and TrM‑related molecules.1 Transcriptomic analyses have already identified pathways such as IGFBP‑2/RhoA/YAP,36 and histological markers including HMB‑45 and Melan‑A8,41 show preliminary value, yet all require translation from research tools to clinically accessible detection.

Conclusion

Current evidence establishes acral vitiligo as a distinct, multifaceted subtype driven by autoimmunity, oxidative stress, genetic susceptibility, and circadian disruption. Its strong associations with koebnerization, autoimmune comorbidities, and psychosocial burden mandate integrated physical and psychological care, including routine ophthalmological evaluation. The therapeutic landscape has shifted toward mechanism‑based interventions; JAK inhibitors and IL‑23 antagonists show promise by targeting core immune dysregulation, yet these findings remain preliminary and require validation in dedicated controlled trials with acral‑specific endpoints and extended follow‑up.

Future research must unravel the unique vulnerability of acral skin and develop predictive biomarkers to enable personalized treatment. Ultimately, the integration of early targeted therapy, refined local modalities, and robust psychosocial support promises to redefine management. This review crystallizes a pivotal paradigm shift: acral vitiligo must be recognized as a distinct clinical entity, separate from vitiligo at large. Embracing a comprehensive, mechanism-informed strategy enables clinicians and researchers to transcend the narrow goal of repigmentation, and instead pursue durable improvements in long-term clinical outcomes and quality of life.
