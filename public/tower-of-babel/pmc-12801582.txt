Nurse educators’ experience of studying while working full-time at a public nursing education institution in South Africa: a focus group study
Authors: Felicia Cecilia van Rooyen, Emmerentia du Plessis, Anneke Catherina van Graan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe expectations that nurse educators should continue to engage in postgraduate studies remain high. This is also true in South Africa, where legislation namely the National Policy on Nursing Education and Training and the South African Nursing Council guidelines for programme implementation requires nurse educators to have research experience through their own research and/or studies toward higher education qualifications. This prompts nurse educators to pursue postgraduate studies to ensure that the public nursing education institution they are employed at obtains and/or maintains higher education status. This places pressure on nurse educators to study while working. Studying while working full-time puts strain on their coping mechanisms. Exploring and describing the experience of nurse educators of studying while working full-time is important so that insight can be gained into how to offer support.MethodsAn explorative and descriptive qualitative design was followed. Purposive sampling technique was used to select nurse educators who are studying while working full-time. Forty-three nurse educators participated in the study. Six unstructured focus group discussions were conducted at the six campuses of the public Nursing Education Institution in Gauteng, South Africa. Content analysis was done through the eight-step conventional qualitative content analysis method.ResultsEight main themes and two sub-themes emerged under two categories. Category 1 (negative aspects) included being required to participate in a “balancing act”, high workloads and exhaustion, negative outcomes and impact, inadequate support systems and organizational problems in the educational system. The systemic problems included the role of nursing education institution policies and practices which hinder nurse educators’ ability to study, particularly that of leave allocation, workload management and lack of support. It also included the influence of internal and external pressures on nurse educators’ choice to study, looking at issues of autonomy and control. Category 2 (positive aspects) included opportunities created for development, improved confidence and competence, and building networks and relationships.ConclusionsThe focus of this study was to get a deeper understanding of the experiences of nurse educators who are studying while working full-time. The results create awareness of their experiences in developing structured support systems and in reviewing policies that will improve institutional processes. This study will significantly contribute to the body of knowledge regarding supporting nurse educators. Future research could focus on developing guidelines to support nurse educators who are studying while working full-time.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

This article reports on research that addressed the need to uncover a deeper understanding of how nurse educators experience studying while working full-time. Globally, the health profile of the population is constantly evolving; this prompts healthcare professionals (including nurse educators) to remain current and proactive by engaging in further studies [1]. Transformation is an inevitable part of growth and South Africa, as part of the global community, is no exception. The World Health Organization (WHO) [2, 3] also accentuates the need to transform and strengthen the nursing and midwifery workforce and to enhance the professional status of nurses.

In South Africa, the amended Higher Education Act (Act 101 of 1997) [4] incorporated nursing education into the Ministry of Higher Education and Training. This integration contributed to strengthening the unified post-school education and training system as stipulated in the White Paper (The White Paper for Post-School Education and Training [PSET]) [5]. The Draft Policy for the Recognition of South African Higher Education Institutional Types [6] ensured that the pressing national needs were met while responding to new realities and opportunities. These legislative changes that nursing education and training are currently undergoing have added the requirement that all nursing education institutions (NEIs) be positioned in the higher education band. The nursing qualifications (as stipulated by the Nursing Act 33 of 2005) [7], are pegged on the higher education band of the National Qualifications Framework (NQF) as stipulated by the NQF Act no 67 of 2008. The Council on Higher Education (CHE) is responsible for the accreditation of new higher education programs offered at higher education institutions [8] and the South African Qualifications Authority (SAQA) registers the obtained qualifications on the NQF [9]. “The NEIs are therefore required to adhere to the criteria set out by the CHE and the NQF . Students enrolled for accredited programs by CHE must be registered with the South African Nursing Council (SANC). These changes suggest that nurse educators must engage in postgraduate studies, while still working full-time. Mohoaduba clarifies that the revised curriculums offered by NEIs in South Africa require nurse educators and midwives to function at the expected NQF level, namely having a postgraduate qualification, and possess skills for self-directed learning to become lifelong learners [10]. This is in line with the CHE requirement that nurse educators must have a qualification higher than the one being taught.

In addition, the WHO State of Nursing Education Report reveals that “nurse education and training programs must graduate nurses who drive progress in primary health care and universal health coverage. Actions include investment in nursing education, availability of clinical placement sites and accessibility of programs offered to attract a diverse student body” [11]. Nurse educators are therefore at the center stage regarding supporting students’ readiness to enter working life and should be adequately prepared to fulfil this role. The main role of nurse educators is to promote student learning and student professional development [12]. Nurse educators are in high demand and play a pivotal role in learning and teaching of students [13]. According to Berland et al.., the competencies of a nurse educator are multidimensional [14]. It includes them being well versed in educational theory and practice, clinical expertise, research and evidence-based practice, leadership and management, communication and interpersonal skills, cultural competence and technological proficiency. In contrast, Zlatavonovic et al. supported by Mikkonen et al.. point out that learning in higher education should focus more on analytical knowledge and complex problem-solving; they also note that educators need good pedagogical competence to teach those skills [15, 16]. Zlatanovic et al.. further highlight that nursing competence and pedagogical competence are regarded as essential competence areas for nurse educators and they should pursue the advancement of their studies [15]. Such competencies include scholarship of teaching and learning, academic and student management, curriculum development, management and leadership, personal development of the nurse educators and research and knowledge creation [17]. It is therefore evident that nurse educators should possess a positive attitude, be disciplined and portray effective organizational and leadership abilities.

The first author witnessed in her daily functioning as a nurse educator at a public nursing education institution in Gauteng that nurse educators have an exceptionally full workload. They have duties such as teaching and advising students, assessing students, evaluating and revising educational programs and individual classes, and promoting academic dialogue among students. Nurse educators are also responsible for overseeing students’ clinical practice, and they serve as role models and mentors for students, and they document the outcomes of educational processes. They also engage in scholarly work (e.g., peer review, research) and in speaking at nursing conferences, they contribute to the academic community. They also achieve the latter via leadership roles. Another critical aspect of their work is maintaining clinical competence, in themselves and their students [17]. According to their job description, nurse educators not only facilitate in the classroom and clinical areas, but they must also maintain sound relationships with clinical stakeholders to ensure that students’ placements in the clinical areas are done according to the required outcomes for the students. Matahela emphasises that nurse educators need to be creative, innovative, resilient and self-leaders [18]. Their role in the assistance of shaping the professional development and careers of student nurses compels them to act as role models to the students they teach and have effective communication and collaboration skills [3]. Nurse educators therefore play a key role (and are seen as experts in) the advancement of nursing careers in all their stages of education [19].

By engaging in their own postgraduate studies, nurse educators could be adding to this demanding workload. Working and studying simultaneously is associated with many demands and these negatively affect the working students’ academic performance [20]. Ost et al. mention that most of the students who attend university face a lot of pressure in terms of workload, and stress from family, friends and educators. They are required to work regularly and properly, to maintain good quality work and to complete their assignments on time without failing [21]. Although nurse educators also maintain full-time jobs, they are not exempt from these aspects.

In her daily interactions in the work environment, the first author has noticed that there are high levels of stress among nurse educators who are simultaneously studying and working full-time. The requirement to obtain an additional qualification puts strain on them as they are trying to cope with both studying and their high workload. The aim of this study was therefore to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the work–study phenomenon among nurse educators. This is anticipated to contribute to research in the field of nursing education in that it may assist stakeholders in creating an enabling academic environment that promotes supportive interventions for nurse educators who are studying while working.

Research question

The following research question arose:

What are NEs experience of studying while working at a public NEI in the Gauteng province?

Method

Design

A explorative and descriptive qualitative design was followed. This design was suitable for gaining a deep understanding of nurse educators’ experience of studying while working full-time at a public NEI. Hunter et al. explain that an exploratory descriptive research design aims to describe phenomena by exploring them from the participants’ perspective [22]. The population comprised nurse educators who at the time of the study were studying while working full-time at a public nursing education institution in Gauteng (N = 100). These nurse educators were studying towards postgraduate qualifications including Master’s and Doctoral degrees at various universities. They were selected because, at the time the research was conducted, they were either studying or had completed their studies within the preceding 6 to 24 months.

Study setting

The study was conducted at six campuses of the public NEI in Gauteng. The first author is employed as a nurse educator in a managerial position at one of the six campuses. Two of the campuses offered postgraduate programs only. The other four campuses offered undergraduate programs inclusive of the Diploma in Nursing R.171 and the Advanced Diploma in Midwifery R.1497 that had recently been phased in. Two of the four campuses also offered the Postgraduate Diploma in Primary Care Nursing R.635. At the time of the study, the NEI in Gauteng was also still offering a range of courses that were in the process of being phased out, namely the Diploma in Nursing R.425, the Diploma in Midwifery R.254, and Bridging course leading to registration as a General Nurse R.683. At the time of the study, the public NEI in Gauteng employed about 250–300 nurse educators and just over 100 nurse educators were studying while working.

Sample

The campus research coordinators acted as mediators (sharing research information between researcher and potential participants) and the researcher conducted the first information session to recruit prospective participants. The sampling method took into consideration the different campuses where nurse educators are working to obtain different opinions and diverse data. Purposive sampling was then conducted according to the inclusion criteria requiring that: (i) the participants needed to be willing to voluntarily participate in the English language. (ii) they needed to have been employed full-time at the public nursing education institution for more than six months, (iii) nurse educators should be enrolled on a postgraduate course, (iv) if they had completed their studies, it should have been within the previous 6–24 months.

Ethical considerations

Research ethics, as highlighted by Brink et al. (2018), [23] focus on the principles of right and wrong conduct. When human participants are involved, researchers must prioritize the protection of their rights and social well-being. Emphasis is placed on the notion that ethical considerations are crucial at all stages of a research project: recruitment, data collection, and the dissemination of findings. Burns and Grove (2017) [24] stress the importance of adhering to fundamental ethical principles, ensuring participants’ rights to self-determination, fair treatment, privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality, while also safeguarding them from any discomfort or harm.

Legal authorisation

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Scientific Review Committee (SRC) of the Quality in Nursing and Midwifery (NuMIQ) research focus area and the North-West University Human Sciences Research Ethics Review Committee and from the Gauteng Department of Health (GDoH). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the NWU HREC Ethics number: NWU-00042-23-S1and the Gauteng Provincial Protocol Review Committee (PPRC) with Protocol number GP202306 011. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Anonymity

The researcher ensured the anonymity of the transcripts by keeping a separate list of participants’ names. Consequently, the co-coder was unaware of the focus group participants’ identities. All electronic transcripts were stored on a password-protected computer. The independent coder received the data through a password-protected email, with access granted via a password provided by the researcher (refer to Annexure J). The information on the researcher’s computer will be retained for five years post-study completion, after which it will be deleted from the relevant folders and the recycle bin, with the deletion process documented in the presence of a witness.

Confidentiality

Polit & Beck (2021) [25] depict confidentiality as the researcher’s responsibility to handle private information shared by participants in a way that it is not disclosed to others without their consent. This ensures that the data cannot be traced back to the participants. During unstructured focus group discussions, participants were asked to establish ground rules to maintain the confidentiality of shared information, agreeing not to share it outside the group. For this study, the researcher transcribed the audio recordings into written form and provided these transcripts only to an independent coder for analysis.

Data collection

Unstructured focus group discussions were held. The study comprised of six unstructured focus group discussions, of which five were conducted by the first author and one by the independent interviewer at the campus where the first author is working. Having an independent interviewer conducting one of the discussions helped reduce potential bias that might have arisen from the first author’s familiarity with the participants or the setting. Using two interviewers also enhanced the credibility of the study by ensuring that the data collection process is not influenced by a single perspective. The sessions took place in a quiet, well-ventilated room where the seating was strategically arranged to adhere to the COVID-19 principles. They both underwent training on how to effectively conduct focus group discussions and provided feedback to the study supervisors after each discussion. In total six unstructured focus groups were conducted with between five to nine participants per focus group session. A total of 43 participants were interviewed (n = 43). The sessions took place at the six different Gauteng campuses between October 2023 and November 2023. All unstructured focus group discussions were digitally voice-recorded.

During each discussion session the first author (or independent interviewer, implied when referring to the researcher/interviewer) welcomed participants in a warm and welcoming manner to make them feel at ease. They provided an overview of the topic and ground rules were set jointly with the participants. An open-ended question was asked to encourage the participants to open up and share their experiences, namely: What is your experience of studying while working full-time? The open-ended question was followed by probing, paraphrasing, clarification, reflection and summaries, to elicit rich data. A pilot study was conducted to assess whether the research question would extract the responses concerning the purpose. The three nurse educators who met the sampling criteria and who were not part of the main study participated in the pilot study. The results were not included in the main study. No changes were needed regarding the interview question, and the researcher had the opportunity to refine her interactions to explore participants’ responses in a more in-depth manner. The first author involved the independent interviewer by giving her feedback regarding the outcome of the pilot study so that she could follow the same approach.

The first author encouraged active participation and directed and redirected the discussion. She used skills for effective communication such as minimal nonverbal responses by nodding to acknowledge all interactions and to encourage dialogue, discussion and debate among the participants on topics related to the research phenomenon.

The first author took field notes during and after the unstructured focus group discussion sessions to ensure that she would remember events during the discussion. It included notes on the unstructured focus group discussion process, interaction, verbal and nonverbal communication, group dynamics and emotions expressed during the discussion. Theoretical notes were made by the first author when she documented her thoughts about how to make sense of what was going on. These notes served as a starting point for subsequent analysis [25]. The first author made use of personal notes by putting down her feelings regarding her emotions that could challenge her assumptions. The audio recording device was placed strategically to be inconspicuous but to capture all the information. The interviews for this study continued until data saturation was reached after the sixth focus group discussion was conducted.

Data analysis

Before data analysis, the audio recordings of the unstructured focus group discussions were transcribed verbatim by the first author. After the focus group discussions, data analysis was conducted according to the eight-step conventional qualitative content analysis method [26]. This method is used to describe a phenomenon through a systematic classification process of coding and identifying themes or patterns in qualitative data. It allows researchers to interpret the content of text data subjectively but systematically.

Step 1→Prepare the data.

The first author transcribed the discussions into text, read it multiple times to understand context, ideas, and patterns.

Step 2→Define the unit of analysis.

The first author identified themes from words, phrases, sentences, or paragraphs and assigned codes to these themes.

Step 3→Develop categories and a coding scheme.

The first author created a coding manual with category names, definitions, rules, and examples, and updated it with interpretive memos.

Step 4→Test the coding scheme on a sample of text.

The first author discussed coding rules within the research team, coded a sample, and checked for consistency, iterating it until consistency was reached.

Step 5→Code all the text.

The first author made notes and memos, assigned codes to new content, and applied coding rules to the entire text, updating the coding manual as new themes emerged.

Step 6→Assess coding consistency.

The first author rechecked coding consistency for the whole data set, corrected errors, and reflected on the process.

Step 7→Draw conclusions from the coded data.

The first author identified relationships and patterns between categories, and organized codes into categories and subcategories.

Step 8→Report methods and findings.

The first author reported findings truthfully, explaining relationships between categories and the larger theme, and detailed the coding process and methods to establish trustworthiness.

The. co-coding was conducted by an independent co-coder, with both the first author and the co-coder analysing the data and holding regular discussion meetings to reach a consensus on the results.

Trustworthiness

The aim of trustworthiness in a qualitative inquiry is to support the argument that the inquiry’s findings are “worth paying attention to” (Lincoln & Guba, 1985:290). Trustworthiness includes five aspects, namely: credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability and authenticity.

Credibility

Ensures confidence in the truth of the data. Techniques used included:

Prolonged engagement: Detailed questions were asked during the focus group interviews, and participants were encouraged to provide examples.

Site triangulation: Data was collected from different campuses to get varied perspectives.

Peer debriefing: Regular meetings were held among the authors and with the co-coder to discuss the research process and to refine interpretations.

Dependability

Refers to the stability and consistency of data over time. An audit trail was maintained throughout the study and an independent co-coder was involved to verify themes.

Confirmability

Describes the degree of neutrality. The findings are supported by data through an audit trail, site triangulation, and reflexivity (being aware of personal biases).

Transferability

The extent to which findings can be transferred to other contexts. Transferability is ensured by providing detailed demographic data and thick descriptions of the data collection and analysis process.

Authenticity

The extent to which the findings fairly represents a range of realities. Authenticity was achieved through establishing rapport with participants, recording discussions, and including quotes and transcripts in the report.

The findings of the study revealed that the participants experienced simultaneous study and work to be a difficult balancing act and that they needed support from the nursing education institution where they were employed. Nevertheless, they found the experience enriching, especially when they had support from their colleagues, their friends and family and the educational institution where they are studying. The challenges they reported experiencing had a significant impact on their academic performance and ultimate success. They implemented measures aimed at enhancing their academic performance, work performance and interpersonal relationships.

A pattern emerged in all six focus groups where participants shared their experience of studying while working full-time. The participants started by describing the negative/hindering aspects as challenges they encountered while they were simultaneously working and studying (Category 1). They then acknowledged the support they received from family, colleagues and study supervisors, as the positive (Category 2) aspects encountered during their journey as indicated as summarized in Table 2.

Aspects experienced by participants

Category 1: negative aspects experienced

Participants expressed how challenging it was to manage expectations and responsibilities regarding their professional duties, family life and personal well-being, all while also pursuing further education. The demands of this balancing act generated several challenges, including role conflict, emotional exhaustion and negative outcomes in various areas of their lives. These difficulties were frequently exacerbated by the lack of adequate support systems, whether occupationally, from the institution where they were studying, or personally.

Theme 1.1→Balancing act

Participants described a recurring struggle with juggling competing demands and having to balance work and family life. Participants reported role conflicts between work responsibilities, family obligations and personal well-being alongside their studies. This often resulted in feelings of guilt, frustration and insufficient time. The researcher also noted in her field notes that she became aware of facial expressions, hand gestures and the change of tone in the voices of participants, confirming this finding.

Participants indicated the balancing act as follows:It is stressful because we don’t have enough time, we are working Monday up to Friday. We have to attend to the task of the activities of the campus. After that, you have to check your studies and that is the other issue, we are adults. We are raising kids; we are having some other stressful or strenuous activities at home. So sometimes as an individual I failed to balance, because I have to attend to my kids. I have to attend to my social life and then I have to attend to my studies. So, it is stressful and at work I am supposed to be productive because there are some expectations from me. (P2, Campus A)Your line manager is giving you work even though there’s many other people in your department that can assist. Then they will bombard you with all of these extra activities that you must do, and they sort of put the trust in your hands to do it for them. (P2, Campus B)I am becoming aware of how participants are relating that juggling work responsibilities and personal life is challenging for them. They seem to struggle to strike the right balance in ensuring that neither family, work nor studies suffers. For instance, managing a demanding job while also being present for family and personal well-being. Theme 1.3→Negative outcomes on work performance and relationships

Studying often took a toll on other aspects of participants’ respective lives. Participants reported declines in work performance, strained work relationships, and disruptions to family life and personal relationships. Participants also reported how these sometimes further negatively impacted their studies, making the process even more difficult. Additionally, external factors such as COVID-19 and loadshedding were also reported to negatively influence participants and their studies.

Theme 1.5: Systemic issues experienced by participants

Systemic issues within NEIs play a pivotal role in shaping the experiences of participants pursuing further studies. Participants described hindrances posed by institutional practices and policies, particularly in areas such as leave allocation, workload management and the overall lack of support structures. They also expressed how unclear communication and poor accountability within organisations hindered the effective execution of duties by supervisors and management. Additionally, participants explained the influence of power dynamics and external pressures on the decision-making process of participants who chose to study.

Sub-Theme 1.5.1→Institutional practices and policies

Nursing education institution policies were often cited as obstacles to participants’ further studies. Leave allocation, workload management and lack of financial or structural support emerged as key areas requiring improvement. The compounding effects associated with educational resources were a concern, and in the nursing education context referred to capacity building, infrastructure and resources. For these participants, these could either enhance or hinder their ability to complete their studies in the prescribed time. These compounding effects are important to consider as they affect the preparation and transition of nurse educators into higher education.

Discussion

The study explored and described the experience of nurse educators who are studying while working full-time at a public NEI in Gauteng province South Africa. Unstructured focus group discussions were conducted with 43 nurse educators who had more than six months of working experience as a nurse educator. The data analysis produced two categories with eight themes and two sub-themes: Category 1 which is the negative aspects experienced, includes the act of balancing work, family life and studying, workload and exhaustion, negative outcomes on work performance and relationships, lack of support systems and environments, organizational issues experienced by participants, institutional policies and practices, power dynamics and pressure to study. Category 2 which is the positive aspects experienced were professional and personal development, improved confidence and competence, building networks, and experiencing the value of supportive relationships. Based on the experiences of the participants revealed in the study findings, it is clear that the situation of studying and working at the same time is challenging. The interpretation of the results indicates that their experience is multidimensional, showing a pattern of reflecting on the challenges that accompany studying while working, but also acknowledging the reward of continued studies and the support they experienced.

The negative aspects of studying while working full-time as a nurse educator are experienced as demotivating and challenging to deal with. Studying while working full-time requires the act of balancing work, family life and studying, and if such balance is not achieved, they experience exhaustion owing to the high workload. They also suffer a negative impact on their work performances and relationships. Furthermore, the nurse educators reported the need for support, and they identified that systemic issues add to the negative aspects of their experience. In contrast, they experienced that studying contributes to their professional development, improves their confidence and competence and adds to their relationships and the building of their networks.

A balancing act is known as “a difficult situation in which someone must try to give equal amounts of importance, time, attention, etc. to two or more different things at the same time” [27]. Participants reported that they often find themselves teetering, attempting to maintain equilibrium amid competing demands. This act of balancing involves juggling multiple spheres: the relentless workload, the nurturing of family bonds, and the cultivation of social connections, all while steadfastly pursuing their studies. For these participants finding a work–life balance (namely a state of equilibrium between an individual’s work and personal life managing your social life, family time, school and work) is undoubtedly tough. The effort to create a work–life balance can cause an increased risk of emotional exhaustion, burnout and a negative experience of occupational well-being [28]. It is clear that nurse educators who study while working full-time need to be resilient. Available literature confirms that even though working while studying full-time can be challenging, students tend to find alternative ways to overcome these difficulties; they adopt various coping mechanisms that assist in achieving academic success and they remain persistent in the face of adversity [29]. Consistent with previous research [30], the results of this study indicate that support for nurse educators is of crucial importance. Participants in this study can enhance their resilience by adopting creative strategies, including fostering positive thinking, maintaining flexibility, and achieving a harmonious equilibrium between work, family, and academic life. Additionally, participants must establish clear boundaries between their academic and work responsibilities.

Owing to the nature of their work, nurse educators are inevitably central in ensuring student readiness for working life [31]; this means that nurse educators should always be in a position to function optimally. Most of the participants in the study verbalized that they experienced struggles in their workload schedules which had a direct negative effect on their academic and work performance. This includes lack of sleep and exhaustion because of conflicting schedules. The results of a European study investigating the professional competence, personal occupational well-being, and mental workload of nurse educators revealed that nurse educators reported high levels of competence in self-management [32]. It also indicated that even though they showed competence in managing their work and tasks, their mental workload was too high [32]. Rinne et al.. further explain that “The excessive scope of the work can become problematic in terms of workload. The well-being of both the individual educator and the wider health care community requires that the educator’s resources and workload factors are balanced within the job” [33]. The literature outlines that an overly high workload for nurse educators may potentially prevent them from coping either at work or academically.

Organizational issues in the workplace verbalized by participants can have significant consequences on both work performance and interpersonal relationships. Findings from a study conducted on nurses working in America revealed that “nurses fear partner unresponsiveness or futility of discussion, especially if the conversational partner has little familiarity with the organization or the nature of the work” [34]. The authors also discovered that these nurses had challenges communicating with family members and friends about their stressful experiences [34]. Literature indicates that the negative impact can lead to a decrease in self-esteem, self-doubt and helplessness [35].

The participants also stated that they experienced institutional challenges inclusive of institutional policies and practices as well as power dynamics and pressure to study. Aspects of inconsistency in the application of study leave policies, inadequate support from management and pressure to study came forth strongly. Participants felt unhappy and unfairly treated, especially because of the lack of funding and resources to assist them in their study journey. A range of expectations and norms arise from the laws and regulations that stipulate how an NEI must operate, and these requirements must be adhered to. This includes issues such as taking one study day from the institution must be matched by one annual leave day taken from the annual leave allocation of the nurse educator. This reduces the amount of leave that the nurse educator can take at the end of the year. In New Zealand, a study conducted on health workforce governance and professions revealed that the relevant authorities’ influence, interrelationships and positions on divisive workforce strategic issues [36].

Participants in the present study verbalized that the lack of support systems is incredibly challenging to them. It also became apparent that a support system is needed for them when they are facing personal struggles, mental health issues, or life difficulties. Dyrbye et al.. emphasize that when working conditions are not good nurses seem to rely more on their colleagues for social support. This in turn can make them struggle with work–life integration [37]. This lack of support can allow nurses “to compartmentalize their feelings about work and therefore suffer more ill effects due to a lack of support” [34]. Literature clarifies the impact of a lack of support system. The importance of a support system at work and at home is therefore of paramount importance. It can contribute significantly to the journey participants undertake in studying while working full-time. Participants should therefore be encouraged to include family members, friends, supervisors, faith leaders, neighbors, peers, or anyone they trust and feel comfortable talking to.

Participants mentioned the effects of small, everyday issues and choices that compound over time. It included not having enough study leave days, annual leave allocation, inadequate resources (human and material) and external pressures. These compounding issues can be a hindrance because this includes the problems related to inadequate infrastructure and resources. Giddens et al.. state that academic nursing has an obligation to transform nursing education in alignment with current and future healthcare needs [38]. In this context, the master educational plan of the NEI should be designed in such a manner that participants can plan and schedule their leave when students are on recess. This will guide the annual leave allocation and allow participants to take their annual leave without fear of it impacting their teaching function and their studies. Giddens et al.. continue by emphasizing that “academic nursing has an obligation to transform nursing education in alignment with the current and future needs for healthcare” [38]. In this regard, the NEI is obliged to provide the necessary resources (especially human resources) to ensure that participants execute their duties without being overworked. The NEI should attend to these compounding effects to allow participants to ensure that the next generation of nurses is well-prepared to meet the growing demand for healthcare services.

Despite the challenges they have experienced, the nurse educators reported feeling empowered, and they have undergone personal growth and development. Results from a study conducted in the United States on developing nurses and interdisciplinary staff in the caring sciences revealed that continuous development has positively impacted nurses’ well-being and organizational excellence. This was due to staff visibility of caring nurse leader attributes (e.g., valuing unique personalities and strengths) [35]. Participants in the present study also emphasized how they have grown and developed during their journey of studying while working full-time. A study conducted by Ingwua et al.. on perceptions of mandatory continuous professional development revealed that learning is a lifelong process which involves keeping up to date with knowledge and skills, which in return supports flexible career paths. Such professional development further engenders self-confidence and a high standard of excellence [39]. Dylan Houle claims that professional development is “the continuous process of self-awareness, application, and reflection on how to best apply your strengths and skills to the world of work” [40]. By engaging in the process to further their studies, participants in the present study were acting in line with the continuous professional development (CPD) prescripts of the SANC [41]. The SANC views CPD to maintain and improve their knowledge, skills, attitudes and professional integrity, to keep up to date with new science, innovation and healthcare developments, to enable safe, ethical, competent and legal practice within their evolving scope and to provide quality care to the South African community [41].

The literature illustrates that professional development is indeed beneficial to the participant and the organization. It allows participants to access career advancement opportunities, increase confidence, obtain higher job satisfaction and remain loyal to the organization. Ryan et al.. state that the term “competency” is often used interchangeably with “mastery”, “skill”, “proficiency” and “competence” [42]. Haggard et al.. explain that intellectual humility can be seen as the desire for knowledge and love for learning. This equips individuals to engage more fully in cognitive efforts like applying what they have learnt in their studies to their place of work and area of specialization [43]. Competency refers to the values and abilities that employees must apply to meet the standard work performance levels of the organization [18]. By furthering their studies, participants equip themselves with new knowledge which in turn makes them feel more confident and self-assured, thus enabling them to proficiently execute their tasks. Abenoja et al.. identified that financial support, self-development and internal motivation to integrate theory and practice are among the driving forces of working while studying at university [27].

Participants also verbalized that by studying they were allowed to liaise with colleagues within the NEI and externally (with stakeholders from other specialties). They deemed it important because they established new working relationships with like-minded people. For some, the dynamics of the research fraternity interested them and motivated them to venture into further studying. Fan et al.. highlight that being a supportive supervisor refers to displaying relations-oriented behavior while expressing concerns for subordinates’ psychological preferences and well-being [44]. Yang et al.. add that the doctoral supervisor plays a crucial role in academic success during doctoral candidature [45]. This is supported by Khuram et al.. who reiterate that students are more productive, their engagement increases, and their psychological resources grow when they receive supportive supervision. This in turn significantly increases their research productivity [46].

Limitations

The scope of the study was limited to a public NEI in the South African context. The results therefore cannot be generalized to other NEIs in South Africa. However, the discussion of the findings shows that there are similarities between the results of this study and existing international literature, and the results and recommendations thus have wider value. The first author being aware of potential bias like being employed as a nurse educator at one of the campuses of the NEI. She ensured that the focus group discussions were conducted by an independent researcher at the campus where she is working. This helped in ensuring the validity and reliability of research findings. The independent researcher was remunerated by the first author who was unable to have her conduct the focus group discussions at the other campuses.

Recommendations

Recommendations for the nursing education institution

To support nurse educators who are balancing dual responsibilities, it is essential to implement flexible class schedules and deadlines. This flexibility allows them to manage their professional and educational commitments effectively. Additionally, providing access to counseling and stress management resources can help them cope with the pressures of their roles. Establishing mentorship programs where experienced nurse educators can guide those who are juggling work and study responsibilities is also crucial. These initiatives collectively create a supportive environment that fosters both personal and professional growth.

Recommendations for nursing education

To better accommodate the needs of working professionals, it is important to develop curricula that are adaptable, including options for online and part-time study. Incorporating practical assignments that can be integrated into their professional practice makes the learning process more relevant and manageable. Additionally, facilitating the formation of peer support groups encourages the sharing of experiences and strategies for managing workload. These measures collectively enhance the educational experience for those balancing work and study.

Recommendations for policy

Advocating for policies that support a balance between work and study, such as reduced teaching loads or study leave options, is crucial for nurse educators. Increasing funding opportunities and scholarships specifically for those pursuing further education can alleviate financial burdens. Additionally, encouraging policies that recognise and reward continuous professional development and further education helps to foster a culture of lifelong learning and professional growth. These measures collectively support nurse educators in their dual roles.

Conclusions

Participants provided their overall experience of working while studying, namely that they experienced it as a challenging work–study–life balancing act for which they needed support. They experienced their studies as enriching, with opportunities for professional and personal growth. Interestingly, the participants noted that organizational issues within NEIs play a pivotal role in shaping the experiences of nursing educators pursuing further studies. This specifically applies to the availability of support and the degree to which they have freedom of choice regarding their studies.

The results create awareness of their experiences in developing structured support systems and reviewing policies to improve institutional processes. Future research could focus on the development of a study policy to guide and support nurse educators who are studying while working full-time.
