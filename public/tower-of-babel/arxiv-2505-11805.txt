On the Waring Problem for Matrices over Finite Fields
Simion Breaz

Abstract
. We prove that if k \displaystyle k is a positive integer, then for every finite field 𝔽 q \displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q} of cardinality q ≠ 2 \displaystyle q\neq 2 and for every positive integer n \displaystyle n such that q n > ( k − 1 ) 4 \displaystyle q^{n}>(k-1)^{4} , every n × n \displaystyle n\times n matrix over 𝔽 q \displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q} can be expressed as a sum of two k \displaystyle k -th powers.

11P05; 11T06; 11T30; 12E05; 12E20; 15A24; 15B33; 16S50

1.
Introduction

Waring’s Problem for matrices asks, for a given positive integer
\displaystyle k
, for decompositions of square matrices over a fixed ring into sums of
\displaystyle k
-th powers. Such decompositions are called Waring decompositions, and they have been investigated in many papers. For instance, it was proved in
[
14
]
that if all
\displaystyle n\times n
matrices are sums of
\displaystyle k
-th powers,
\displaystyle k\leq n
, then they are sums of seven
\displaystyle k
-th powers. These results were extended in
[
7
]
, where the existence of Waring decompositions for a matrix is characterized using the trace. A detailed study of Waring decompositions for finite rings, in particular for matrix rings over finite fields, is presented in
[
6
]
.

For matrices over finite fields
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
(of cardinality
\displaystyle q
), it was observed in
[
9
]
that the problem of finding minimal conditions for
\displaystyle q
and
\displaystyle k
such that every matrix is a sum of three or two
\displaystyle k
-th powers corresponds to a similar question, solved in
[
16
]
and
[
12
]
, for non-commutative simple groups.
Such minimal conditions are also described in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
. For instance, it was proved in
[
9
, Theorem 6.1]
that for every
\displaystyle k
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{k}\leq k^{16}
depending only on
\displaystyle k
such that if
\displaystyle q>C_{k}
then every
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix (
\displaystyle n\geq 2
in
[
10
]
) is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers. For the case
\displaystyle\gcd(k,q)=1
this was improved in
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
, where it is proved that we can take
\displaystyle C_{k}=k^{3}-3k^{2}+3k
. These results are connected to a conjecture of Larsen,
[
10
, Problem 1]
, which states that for every
\displaystyle k
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{k}
such that if
\displaystyle q^{n^{2}}>C_{k}
, then every
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers. Unfortunately, the above-mentioned results yield only lower bounds for
\displaystyle q
given by polynomial expressions in
\displaystyle k
, involving radicals, and do not capture bounds of the form
\displaystyle q^{f(n)}>C_{k}
, where
\displaystyle f
is an increasing function (for instance, affine or quadratic).

We will prove in Theorem
2.10
that if
\displaystyle q\neq 2
and we have the inequality

(1)

k<\frac{q^{n/2}-\omega(n)q+q-1}{q-1},

then every non-scalar matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers (
\displaystyle\omega(n)
represents the number of prime divisors of
\displaystyle n
).
In particular, we obtain in Corollary
2.14
a weak version of Larsen’s conjecture: if
\displaystyle q^{n}>(k-1)^{4}
then every
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix over a finite field
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}\neq\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers. The case of matrices over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
was investigated in
[
10
]
. For instance, it is proved in
[
10
, Theorem 5.1]
that for
\displaystyle k
odd, all matrices over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
are sums of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers.

In this paper
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
will be a field with
\displaystyle q
elements, and the references used here for Finite Field Theory are
[
13
]
and
[
15
]
. The ring of all
\displaystyle n\times n
-matrices over a field
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. If
\displaystyle P=X^{n}-\alpha_{n-1}X^{n-1}-\dots-\alpha_{1}X-\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{F}[X]
then we call
\displaystyle\alpha_{n-1}
the
trace
of
\displaystyle P
, and we denote it by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(P)
. We also denote by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(A)
the trace of every square matrix.

For every monic polynomial
\displaystyle P=X^{n}-\alpha_{n-1}X^{n-1}-\dots-\alpha_{1}X-\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{F}[X],
there exists a
companion matrix

\mathbf{C}_{P}=\mathbf{C}_{\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{0}\\
1&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n-2}\\
0&0&\cdots&1&\alpha_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},

such that the characteristic polynomial of
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
is
\displaystyle P
. Note that the minimal polynomial of
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
is also
\displaystyle P
. A matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is called
non-derogatory
if the degree of its minimal polynomial is
\displaystyle n
.
As a reminder, every matrix over a field
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
is similar to a
Frobenius canonical form
, which is a unique block-diagonal matrix
\displaystyle\text{diag}(\mathbf{C}_{P_{1}},\mathbf{C}_{P_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{P_{s}})
, where each
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{i}}
is the companion matrix of a monic polynomial
\displaystyle P_{i}
such that
\displaystyle P_{i}\mid P_{i+1}
for all
\displaystyle 1\leq i<s
. These polynomials are the invariant factors of the initial matrix. In particular, from the Frobenius canonical form, it follows that every non-derogatory matrix is similar to a companion matrix. Moreover, two non-derogatory matrices are similar if and only if they have the same characteristic polynomial.

2.
Waring decomposition for matrices

We fix a finite field
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a positive integer
\displaystyle n\geq 2
. We will work in the finite field extension
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
\displaystyle\phi:\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
,
\displaystyle\phi(x)=x^{q}
the Frobenius automorphism associated to this extension. For every
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
we consider the tuple of its
conjugates

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}=(x,\phi(x),\dots,\phi^{n-1}(x))
with respect to this field extension. We will say that
\displaystyle\mathbf{t}
is the
orbit
of
\displaystyle x
. We will also use the polynomial

P_{\mathbf{t}}=P_{x}=(X-x)(X-\phi(x))\dots(X-\phi^{n-1}(x))\in\mathbb{F}_{q},

induced by
\displaystyle\mathbf{t}
. We will denote by

\mathrm{Tr}_{q^{n}/q}:\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q},\ \mathrm{Tr}_{q^{n}/q}(x)=x+\phi(x)+\dots+\phi^{n-1}(x),

the trace of
\displaystyle x
with respect to the extension
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We remind the reader that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{q^{n}/q}(x)
coincides with the trace of the associated polynomial.

Remark 2.1
.

Using
[
13
, Theorem 2.14]
and the remark after
[
13
, Definition 2.17]
, it follows that
\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{t}}
is irreducible if and only if the orbit
\displaystyle\mathbf{t}
has no repetitions. Moreover, every monic irreducible polynomial is of the form
\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{t}}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{t}
is an orbit without repetitions.

In order to construct Waring decompositions for matrices over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we need to identify polynomials
\displaystyle P
such that the associated companion matrices
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P}
are
\displaystyle k
-th powers (we refer to
[
11
]
for more details about such polynomials). We will apply the following

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle k
be a positive integer. If
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
and the values

a^{k},\phi(a^{k}),\dots,\phi^{n-1}(a^{k})

are pairwise distinct (i.e., the polynomial
\displaystyle P_{a^{k}}
is irreducible), then the companion matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{a^{k}}}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th power in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
.

Proof.

According to our hypothesis,
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{a}}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{a^{k}}}
are diagonalizable in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})
. If
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})
is a matrix such that
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{-1}\mathrm{Diag}(a,\phi(a),\dots,\phi^{n-1}(a))\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{C}_{P_{a}}
then

\mathbf{U}^{-1}\mathrm{Diag}(a^{k},\phi(a^{k}),\dots,\phi^{n-1}(a^{k}))\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{C}_{P_{a}})^{k}.

It follows that the matrix
\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}_{P_{a}})^{k}
is diagonalizable in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})
and its eigenvalues are pairwise distinct. Using the Frobenius canonical form, it follows that
\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}_{P_{a}})^{k}
is similar to the companion matrix associated to the polynomial
\displaystyle P_{a^{k}}
, which completes the proof. ∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
is a primitive element. Then, for every positive integer
\displaystyle k<q^{\frac{n}{2}}+1
the polynomial
\displaystyle P_{b^{k}}
is irreducible, and the companion matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{b^{k}}}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th power in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\displaystyle P_{b^{k}}
is not irreducible. Therefore,
\displaystyle b^{k},\phi(b^{k}),\dots,\phi^{n-1}(b^{k})
are not pairwise distinct. It follows that
\displaystyle b^{k}
belongs to a proper subfield of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
. Since
\displaystyle b^{k}\notin\mathbb{F}_{q}
, there exists an integer
\displaystyle 1<u\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
such that
\displaystyle b^{k(q^{u}-1)}=1
and
\displaystyle u\mid n
. Since
\displaystyle b
is primitive, we have
\displaystyle q^{n}-1\mid k(q^{u}-1)
. Using the hypothesis
\displaystyle k<q^{\frac{n}{2}}+1
we obtain a contradiction.

For the last statement, we apply Lemma
2.2
to conclude that the matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P_{b^{k}}}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th power. ∎

In the following result, we will prove that under a suitable hypothesis there exist irreducible polynomials as in Lemma
2.2
with arbitrary traces. The existence (up to some trivial cases) of primitive polynomials with arbitrary traces was proved in
[
3
]
. For a survey on results concerning the existence of irreducible polynomials with prescribed coefficients, we refer to
[
2
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle k
are positive integers with
\displaystyle\gcd(k,q)=1
and

(1)

k<\frac{q^{n/2}-\omega(n)q+q-1}{q-1}

then for every
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
there exists an irreducible polynomial
\displaystyle P
of degree
\displaystyle n
such that the companion matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th power and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(P)=a
.

Proof.

We denote by
\displaystyle M_{q}(n)
the cardinality of the set

M=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}\mid\textrm{the orbit of }x\textrm{ has no repetitions}\}.

Let us observe that two orbit tuples without repetitions,
\displaystyle t_{1}
and
\displaystyle t_{2}
, induce the same polynomial if and only if
\displaystyle t_{2}
is obtained as a cyclic permutation of
\displaystyle t_{1}
. It follows that
\displaystyle M_{q}(n)=nN_{q}(n)
, where
\displaystyle N_{q}(n)
is the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree
\displaystyle n
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We apply
[
13
, Theorem 3.25]
to obtain

(2)

M_{q}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\left(d\right)q^{\frac{n}{d}}\geq q^{n}-\omega(n)\cdot q^{{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}},

where
\displaystyle\mu(-)
represents the Möbius function, and
\displaystyle\omega(n)
is the number of prime divisors of
\displaystyle n
.

For every
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, let
\displaystyle N_{a}
be the number of solutions for the equation
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{q^{n}/q}(x^{k})=a
.
By Weil Theorem,
[
13
, Theorem 6.61]
, it follows that

(3)

\left|N_{a}-q^{n-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{q}\cdot(q-1)\cdot(k-1)\sqrt{q^{n}}

Using the discussion presented in Remark
2.1
, we observe that, in order to conclude that there exists a polynomial
\displaystyle P
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(P)=a
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th power, it is enough to verify if
\displaystyle N_{a}+M_{q}(n)>q^{n}
. Under the hypothesis (
1
), this follows from (
2
) and (
3
).
∎

In order to prove our main result about Waring decompositions, we will use some additive decompositions for matrices.
Recall that a matrix is an
unreduced upper Hessenberg matrix
if for every
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
we have
\displaystyle a_{(j+1)j}\neq 0
and for every
\displaystyle i>j+1
we have
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
. For the reader’s convenience, some details about the proof of the following well-known lemma are added. The second statement says that every non-derogatory matrix is similar to an unreduced Hessenberg matrix whose first
\displaystyle(n-1)
columns are prescribed.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
be a field and
\displaystyle n
a positive integer.

(i)
If
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is an unreduced upper Hessenberg matrix then it is non-derogatory.

(ii)
Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a non-derogatory matrix and that we have fixed some elements
\displaystyle a_{ij}\in\mathbb{F}
with
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
and
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq n-1
such that
\displaystyle a_{(j+1)j}\neq 0
for all
\displaystyle j
, and for every
\displaystyle i>j+1
we have
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
. Then there exist
\displaystyle a_{1n},\dots,a_{nn}\in\mathbb{F}
such that
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}
is similar to the matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})
.

Proof.

(i) If
\displaystyle e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{t}
and
\displaystyle c_{j}(\mathbf{A})
represents the
\displaystyle j
-th column of
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
then
\displaystyle(e_{1},c_{1}(\mathbf{A}),\dots,c_{n-1}(\mathbf{A}))
is a basis for
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Using this basis, we observe that
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
is similar to a companion matrix.

(ii) For simplicity, we can assume that
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}
is a companion matrix. It follows that the vectors

f_{1}=e_{1},f_{2}=a_{21}^{-1}(Bf_{1}-a_{11}f_{1}),f_{3}=a_{32}^{-1}(Bf_{2}-a_{12}f_{1}-a_{22}f_{2}),\dots

form a basis of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Using this, we transform
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}
into a matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})
which has the desired form.
∎

In the next lemma we will prove that every matrix over a field with at least three elements can be decomposed as a sum of two matrices with prescribed characteristic polynomials. There are several other results regarding such decompositions. We refer the reader to
[
1
]
and
[
4
]
for recent developments in this area.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\neq\mathbb{F}_{2}
be a field, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
a non-scalar matrix. Suppose that
\displaystyle P\in\mathbb{F}[X]
is a polynomial of degree
\displaystyle n-1
and
\displaystyle Q\in\mathbb{F}[X]
is of degree
\displaystyle n
. If
\displaystyle t=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{A})-\mathrm{Tr}(P)-\mathrm{Tr}(Q),
there exist two (non-derogatory) matrices
\displaystyle\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{F})
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
with the minimal polynomials
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
, respectively, such that
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
is similar to a matrix of the form

(4)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{D}&\begin{array}[]{c}d_{0}\\
\vdots\\
d_{n-1}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\dots&0\end{array}&t\end{array}\right)+\mathbf{E}.

Proof.

We can assume that
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
is in Frobenius canonical form
\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{s})
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
are companion matrices,
\displaystyle 1\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{s}
, and
\displaystyle n_{s}>1
.

Since
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\neq\mathbb{F}_{2}
, it follows from Lemma
2.5
that there exists an unreduced upper Hessenberg matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=(d_{ij})
such that it is similar to
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{P}
, and
\displaystyle d_{(j+1)j}-1_{\mathbb{F}}\neq 0
for all
\displaystyle j\leq n-1
. Since
\displaystyle n_{s}>1
, the coefficients of matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}-\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{D},t)
satisfy the condition (ii) from Lemma
2.5
. Therefore, there exists a non-derogatory matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
such that it is similar to
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{Q}
, and its first
\displaystyle(n-1)
columns coincide with the corresponding columns of
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}-\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{D},t)
.
Since
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
is equal to the sum described in (
4
), the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

We note that the only restriction, other than the degrees, on the selection of the polynomials
\displaystyle P
,
\displaystyle Q
, and the element
\displaystyle t
required to obtain, up to a similarity, a decomposition as (
4
) is
\displaystyle t=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{A})-\mathrm{Tr}(P)-\mathrm{Tr}(Q).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{F})
and
\displaystyle k
a positive integer. If
\displaystyle b\neq 0
is an element of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
such that
\displaystyle b^{k}
is not an eigenvalue for
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{k}
then every matrix of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{B}^{k}&\begin{array}[]{c}d_{0}\\
\vdots\\
d_{n-1}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\dots&0\end{array}&b^{k}\end{array}\right)

is a
\displaystyle k
-th power.

Proof.

We consider the equation

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{B}&\begin{array}[]{c}x_{0}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\dots&0\end{array}&b\end{array}\right)^{k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{B}^{k}&\begin{array}[]{c}d_{0}\\
\vdots\\
d_{n-1}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\dots&0\end{array}&b^{k}\end{array}\right).

Since
\displaystyle b^{k}
is not an eigenvalue for
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{k}
, the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{k-1}+b\mathbf{B}^{k-2}+\dots+b^{k-2}\mathbf{B}+b^{k-1}I_{n-1}
is invertible, hence the equation has a solution.
∎

\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\displaystyle P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
\displaystyle n
then
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}\mathbf{C}_{P}
divides

.

We are ready to prove the main result of this note.

Theorem 2.10
.

If
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle k
are positive integers which satisfy the condition (
1
) then every non-scalar
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-powers.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be a non-scalar matrix.

From (
1
) it follows that
\displaystyle k<q^{(n-1)/2}+1
, hence we can apply Lemma
2.3
to construct a
\displaystyle k
-th power matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
whose characteristic polynomial is irreducible. We observe that we can take this matrix to be of the same form as in the proof of Lemma
2.6
. Moreover, we consider an element
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
whics is
\displaystyle k
-th power.

Since
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, the condition (
1
) implies
\displaystyle n\geq 3
, hence
\displaystyle t
is not an eigenvalue for
\displaystyle\mathbf{D}
. It follows from Lemma
2.8
that the matrices of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{D}&\begin{array}[]{c}x_{0}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\dots&0\end{array}&t\end{array}\right)

are
\displaystyle k
-th powers.

We write
\displaystyle k=p^{a}k^{\prime}
, with
\displaystyle\gcd(k^{\prime},q)=1
. Since
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle k^{\prime}
satisfy the condition (
1
), we can apply Proposition
2.4
to observe that there exists an irreducible polynomial
\displaystyle Q
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(Q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{A})-\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{D})-t
and the associated companion matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{Q}
is a
\displaystyle k^{\prime}
-th power. If
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{Q}=(\mathbf{E}^{\prime})^{k^{\prime}}
, it follows from Lemma
2.9
that the order of
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\prime}
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is coprime with
\displaystyle p
. Then there exists a matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}
such that
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\prime}=\mathbf{E}^{p^{a}}
, hence
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{Q}=\mathbf{E}^{k}
. Since every matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with the characteristic polynomial
\displaystyle Q
is similar to
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{Q}
, it follows that we can apply Lemma
2.6
to obtain the conclusion.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

a) If
\displaystyle n
is a prime, the condition (
1
) can be written as

(5)

k<\frac{q^{n/2}-1}{q-1}

b) If
\displaystyle n\geq 4
then the condition (
1
) can be replaced with

(6)

k\leq q^{\frac{n}{2}-1}.

In order to see that the condition (
6
) implies (
1
), it is enough to consider the following cases:

case 1:
\displaystyle\omega(n)=1
.
In this case, we observe that it is enough to have

k\leq\frac{q^{n/2}-1}{q-1}.

case 2:
\displaystyle\omega(n)=2
.
The condition (
1
) becomes

k\leq\frac{q^{n/2}-q-1}{q-1},

which is satisfied if (
6
) is true.

case 3:
\displaystyle\omega(n)\geq 3
.
In this case
\displaystyle n\geq 30
. Since
\displaystyle\omega(n)<\log_{2}(n)
, it is easy to observe that the condition (
6
) is enough in order to obtain the conclusion.

In the following, we will include the scalar matrices in our results. Let us remind that
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
can be viewed as an
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra

\mathbb{F}_{q}[\mathbf{C}]=\{\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\mathbf{C}+\dots+\alpha_{n-1}\mathbf{C}^{n-1}\mid\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\},

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a matrix whose characteristic polynomial is irreducible. Therefore, in order to decompose a non-zero scalar matrix from
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
as a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers, it is enough to construct such a decomposition in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}[\mathbf{C}]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of cardinality
\displaystyle q
. If
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle n
are positive integers and
\displaystyle q^{n}>(k-1)^{4}
then
every
\displaystyle n\times n
scalar matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers.

Proof.

From
[
17
, Corollary 6.12 (a)]
it follows that every non-zero element of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}[\mathbf{C}]
is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers. In particular, every scalar
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

In fact, the bound
\displaystyle q^{n}>(k-1)^{4}
can be replaced by a weaker condition, see
[
17
, Corollary 6.12]
or
[
13
, Example 6.38]
.

Now, using Theorem
2.10
and Lemma
2.12
, we obtain the following

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
\displaystyle F_{q}
be a finite field of cardinality
\displaystyle q
. If
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle q
are positive integers such that
\displaystyle q^{n}>(k-1)^{4}
then every
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers.

The following corollary can be of independent interest.

Corollary 2.15
.

If
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field of cardinality
\displaystyle q
and
\displaystyle n\geq 4
is an integer, then for every
\displaystyle k\leq q
, every
\displaystyle n\times n
matrix is a sum of two
\displaystyle k
-th powers.
