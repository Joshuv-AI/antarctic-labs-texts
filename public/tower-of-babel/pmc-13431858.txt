Emergency Department Triage Nurses' Scope of Practice: An Observational Study
Authors: Julie Considine, Elizabeth Oldland, Judy Currey, Philippa Blencowe, Katrina Maybury, Leanne Stella, Amy Pearson, Paul Buntine, Kate Curtis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore emergency department triage nurses' scope of practice and activities related to their triage role and management of patients located in emergency department waiting areas.DesignExploratory, descriptive, observational study using naturalistic decision making.MethodsData were collected using semi‐structured non‐participant observation: researchers recorded their observations using a lapel microphone and recorder from 8 January to 7 May 2025. Fifteen triage nurses from three emergency departments in Melbourne, Australia were observed for 2 to 2.5 h each. Audio‐recordings were transcribed verbatim and analysed using deductive content analysis. The HIRAID emergency nursing framework (History including Infection risk, Red flags, Assessment, Interventions, Diagnostics, reassessment and communication) was used as the coding frame.ResultsParticipants had a median of 10 (interquartile range 7.5–21) years nursing and 5 (interquartile range 3.2–13) years triage experience. During a total of 33 observation hours, there were 303 interactions, including 237 interactions with 169 different patients; the remainder were with carers or other clinicians. In total, 1183 tasks were coded ranging from 12 to 128 tasks per triage nurse. The most common tasks were: taking a history (n = 475); post‐triage communication with patients, carers and other clinicians (n = 288); patient assessment including vital signs and focussed assessments (n = 165); and interventions including medications and psychological care (n = 134). All elements of taking a history, identifying red flags and assessment were more common during the triage process, with few instances during post‐triage care. Interventions (medication administration, psychological care), diagnostics (pathology, imaging, urinalysis) and communication with patients, carers and other clinicians occurred during both triage and post‐triage care.ConclusionTriage nurse practice is complex, multifaceted and extends beyond triage category allocation.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThe traditional perspective that triage and post‐triage care are linear and clearly divided is not fit for purpose in contemporary triage practice.ImpactThe scope of triage nurses' practice both in the context of triage of incoming patients and care of patients in ED waiting areas is poorly understood. The role of triage nurses extends far beyond rapid assessment and triage category allocation and triage nurses use a breadth of expertise and skills to maintain safety, promote comfort and expedite emergency care for patients in ED waiting areas. History, red flags and assessment were more likely during the triage process than post‐triage, but interventions, diagnostics and communication spanned both triage and post‐triage care. Future triage and waiting area models of care and educational preparation of triage nurses should be co‐designed with triage nurses and consumers, so they reflect care as delivered rather than care as imagined.Reporting MethodConsolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ).Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

The emergency department (ED) triage nurse is critical to patient safety (Fekonja et al. 2023). Triage nurses work in a risk‐laden environment and are responsible for assessment and prioritisation of every patient attending the ED (Suamchaiyaphum et al. 2026); they assess and manage undiagnosed and differentiated patients of all ages and with varying degrees of clinical urgency, and their work environment is noisy, crowded and chaotic with no control over incoming patients (Suamchaiyaphum et al. 2024).

In many countries, triage nurses are also responsible for the care and safety of patients in the ED waiting room (Innes et al. 2015), adding a further layer of complexity to their role. Despite few published studies regarding how patient safety is maintained in ED triage and waiting areas (Fekonja et al. 2023), triage nurses perform this work daily.

Background

Australian triage nurses manage over 9 million ED attendances annually and demand for emergency care increases year‐on‐year (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW] 2026). If ED treatment spaces are not available, patients are allocated to ED waiting areas after triage where the triage nurse is responsible for their care and safety (Innes et al. 2021). Up to 47% of Australian ED patients spent time in the waiting room following triage and only 31% of patients progress to receiving care in an ED treatment space (Pryce et al. 2021), meaning that a considerable proportion of emergency care is delivered in ED waiting areas. Whilst there is limited understanding of emergency care provision in ED waiting rooms (Innes et al. 2018), Australian data show activities such as initiating analgesia, pathology testing and diagnostic imaging and interventions such as intravenous cannulation, electrocardiography and urinalysis are commonly performed (Innes et al. 2018).

The role of triage nurses in caring for, and maintaining the safety of, patients in ED waiting rooms is all but absent in triage research. To date, the three major research foci related to ED triage have been focused on triage nurses' (i) consistency in applying triage scales (van der Wulp and van Stel 2010), accuracy of triage category allocation (Suamchaiyaphum et al. 2024) and triage decision making processes during clinical urgency assessment (Gorick et al. 2023); (ii) predictive accuracy for specific events such as mortality (Iversen et al. 2019) or ED disposition (hospital admission vs. home) (Afnan et al. 2021); and (iii) interventions such as patient streaming, fast track, team triage, point‐of‐care testing and nurse initiated x‐ray to improve ED patient flow (Franchini et al. 2026; Gottlieb et al. 2021). There is a major gap in understanding the broader scope of triage nurses' practice both in the context of triage of incoming patients and care of patients in ED waiting areas.

The Study

Aim

The aim of this study was to explore ED triage nurses' scope of practice and activities related to their roles of triage (assessment of clinical urgency) and management of patients located in ED waiting areas. For this paper, ED triage nurses will be referred to as ‘triage nurses’, and waiting areas refers to the ED waiting room and ambulance off‐load spaces.

Methods

Design

This was a qualitative, descriptive, observational study informed by naturalistic decision making as the conceptual framework. Naturalistic decision making is the study of complex decision making in dynamic real‐world settings, particularly where decisions are made under time pressure, conditions of uncertainty and with changing or ill‐defined goals and high stakes (Currey and Botti 2003; Klein 2008); all of which apply to the ED triage context. Further, naturalistic decision making is grounded in maximising the ecological validity (real‐world relevance) of the phenomena of interest (Currey and Botti 2003; Klein 2008) and has been used successfully to study acute care nurses' decision making in clinical practice (Nibbelink and Reed 2019), making naturalistic decision making a suitable conceptual framework for this study. A qualitative, descriptive, observational approach enabled capture of complexities of triage practice such as assessing and managing undiagnosed and undifferentiated patients of all ages with varying degrees of clinical urgency, inconsistency and ambiguity of available clinical cues, need for rapid and accurate decisions under significant time constraints and conditions of uncertainty. This study is reported according to Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (Data [Link], [Link]) (Tong et al. 2007).

Setting

The study setting was Eastern Health, Australia. In Australia, triage is undertaken by registered nurses with specialist educational preparation in emergency nursing including specific, nationally consistent, triage education (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care [ACSQHC] 2024). National recommendations state that triage assessment should take 2 to 5 min and comprise six core elements: (i) environmental red flags; (ii) patient's general appearance; (iii) presenting problem; (iv) assessment to inform triage category allocation; (v) identify clinical red flags; and (vi) triage category assignment (ACSQHC 2024). Environmental, historical or physiological red flags are indicators of clinical urgency (ACSQHC 2024).

The three EDs at Eastern Health are located in metropolitan Melbourne and collectively manage over 148,000 presentations annually. The Australasian Triage Scale (ATS) is used in Australian EDs and the patient profile at Eastern Health is 1% ATS 1 (immediate emergency care), 17% ATS 2 (emergency care within 10 min), 43% ATS 3 (emergency care within 30 min), 34% ATS 4 (emergency care within 60 min) and 5% ATS 5 (emergency care within 2 h). At the time of the study, approximately 20% of patients were children aged under 14 years and 8% were patients aged over 85 years; the hospital admission rate was 32%; and the average ED length of stay was 10.4 h for patients requiring hospital admission and 5.2 h for patients discharged from the ED. The triage area in each ED varied in size and structure. All were secure areas with the triage nurse located behind a clear screen with cut outs so they could talk with patients and undertake limited assessments and interventions. Each triage area had one or two assessment spaces with a trolley or reclining chair: triage nurses could bring patents into these assessment spaces via swipe card access. The intent of the assessment spaces was short term further assessment or interventions, after which, patients returned to the waiting room. In all EDs, the waiting room was directly in front of the triage desk. All EDs had an ambulance offload area which was typically corridor space where patients arriving by ambulance are held until an ED treatment space is available.

In Victoria, EDs have legislated nurse‐to‐patient ratios, according to size and level of the ED (Department of Health and Human Services 2015). ED waiting area staffing is not considered in nurse‐to‐patient ratios, so these areas are staffed from existing nursing resources. At Eastern Health, fully rostered triage staffing at each campus was one triage nurse on morning and night shifts, two triage nurses on afternoon shifts at hospital A; two triage nurses on all shifts (morning, afternoon and night shifts) at hospital B; and two triage nurses on morning and afternoon shifts, and one triage nurse on night shift at hospital C. Triage at Eastern Health is undertaken by senior registered nurses, the majority of whom have postgraduate qualifications in emergency nursing. In contrast, waiting room nurses vary from being newly graduated registered nurses to experienced nurses with triage experience. Fully rostered waiting room staffing was one waiting room nurse on afternoon shifts at hospital A, one waiting room nurse on all shifts at hospitals B and C.

Participants

Purposive sampling was used to recruit study participants, who were all ED triage nurses. All permanently employed ED nurses, able to independently perform the triage role, were invited to participate (n = 215). Nurses undertaking postgraduate studies in emergency nursing, working at triage with nurse educator support, were excluded. Triage nurses were invited to participate via emails distributed by the nurse manager and in person by one of the researchers (JCo) at nursing huddles, handovers and staff meetings. Triage nurses who expressed interest voluntarily contacted the principal researcher via telephone or email and were emailed a Participant Information and Consent Form. Triage nurses willing to participate replied to the principal researcher, and a time for data collection was negotiated. Fifteen triage nurses agreed to participate: all were observed and interviewed. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and participants were provided a $50AUD gift voucher in recognition of their contribution.

The final sample was informed by information power which prioritises data depth and meaning over number of participants and is supported by the notion that the more information participants hold (via their experiences and knowledge), the fewer participants are needed (Malterud et al. 2016). Given the highly specific experiences and knowledge of ED triage nurses, the narrow study aim and purposive sampling technique, 10–15 triage nurse participants were likely to be appropriate to address the research aim. Information power was evaluated in an iterative manner during data collection (see Section 4.4) with 15 triage nurses being the final number of participants.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured non‐participant observation was used to collect data from real‐world triage practice from 8 January 2025 to 7 May 2025. Observations were focused on triage nurses' interactions with patients, carers and clinicians in the ED triage area and/or ED waiting areas, and were guided by the HIRAID emergency nursing framework (History including Infection risk, Red flags, Assessment, Interventions, Diagnostics, reassessment and communication) (Curtis et al. 2026), which was implemented in Eastern Health's emergency departments in 2023 (see Table S1 for observation guide). Each triage nurse was observed for 2 to 2.5 h and observations occurred at various times of the day and days of the week, dependent on triage nurse rostering and availability (Table 1). The observation period of 2 to 2.5 h was selected because it enabled collection of rich data given the intensity of triage activity, reduced participant burden on triage nurses, and minimised researcher fatigue and risk of data collection deterioration (Pope and Allen 2020).

The observations were conducted by JCo and EO who were female PhD‐prepared nurse researchers from emergency and critical care nursing backgrounds and experienced in qualitative health services research. JCo holds a joint academic professorial appointment within the same health service and was known professionally to some participants but had no clinical relationship with participants and no line management authority. EO was not known to participants.

The researchers recorded their observations by speaking into a lapel microphone attached to an audio recorder. Consistent with non‐participant observation, researchers did not interact with the triage nurses or patients during the observations nor participate in patient care. All observations took place from a suitable place in the triage area to avoid obstruction or interruption to care provision. Data were transcribed and coded following every three observations and reviewed to consider the distribution of codes and gaps in the study data. After 15 observations, the team determined that the information power was sufficient to address the study aim.

Data Analysis

The audio‐recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. Excel was used to manage the study data. A deductive content analysis was performed (Graneheim and Lundman 2004) using the HIRAID emergency nursing framework as the coding frame. HIRAID was chosen as the coding frame as it is the only validated emergency nursing framework (Figure 1) (Curtis et al. 2026). Further, HIRAID reflects Australian recommendations regarding the approach to triage and initial emergency nursing assessment and management. Alignment between HIRAID (Curtis et al. 2026) and the ACSQHC's (2024) recommended approach to triage is shown in Figure 2.

The approach to deductive content analysis was as follows (Graneheim and Lundman 2004). Transcript data were imported into Excel. Text segments from each interaction were imported into separate transcript cells, each on separate lines and columns were each of the HIRAID elements. Transcripts were read numerous times to gain a sense of the data in their entirety. Then, sentences and paragraphs in the text were coded according to the HIRAID framework by one researcher (JCo) (see Table S2 for an example of data organisation). The text and codes were reviewed by a second researcher (KC). Any differences of opinion were resolved by re‐reading the transcripts and discussion. There were few instances of data that did not fit into the categories of the HIRAID emergency nursing framework. Triage tasks were those occurring during the triage of an incoming patient, whether they self‐presented or arrived by ambulance. Post‐triage activities were those involving patients who had already been triaged and were in the waiting room or in the care of paramedics whilst waiting for an ED treatment space.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by Eastern Health's Human Research Ethics Committee [approval number LR24‐056‐112324]. Patient and triage nurse anonymity were protected, as no identifying information was collected and all exemplars drawn from the study data in this manuscript are de‐identified. Only investigators with no operational or line management roles (JCo, EO, JCu, KC) had access to raw data; other project members had access to deidentified data.

Written informed consent was sought from all triage nurse participants. Consent was not sought from other staff, patients or families. Patient consent was not sought for three reasons. First, the focus of the study was triage nurse scope of practice, rather than collection of patient data. Second, approaching patients attending the ED for consent may delay the triage assessment, which was deemed an unacceptable clinical risk. Third, some patients may not have been able to consent due to issues such as pain, limited English proficiency or cognitive impairment: trying to obtain patient consent in these circumstances may have caused patients' further distress.

Other staff, patients or families were provided with an opt‐out option after receiving information as follows. During data collection periods, posters were placed in the triage area informing patients and families about the researcher's presence, purpose of the study, and confirmation that no personal information about patients was being collected. Patients and families were invited to inform the triage nurse if they wished the researcher to leave during their interaction with the triage nurse. The researcher introduced themselves to other staff in the triage and waiting areas (clerical staff, other nurses and medical staff) and explained the nature of the study and that data collection was focused on the triage nurse participant. There were no objections to researcher presence raised by patients, carers or other staff.

Rigour and Reflexivity

The tenants of rigour of qualitative research are credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability leading to trustworthiness (Nowell et al. 2017). Credibility was achieved by the research team's deep knowledge of emergency and triage nursing over many years, researchers who collected data spending adequate time with participants and rapport building to understand their perspective and practice, and the semi‐structured approach to observations of real‐world triage practice. The research team was diverse comprising expert emergency nursing clinicians, educators, researchers and managers; and an emergency physician and executive director of nursing. The team collectively had 176 years of emergency care experience, with each member bringing a specific perspective to the study. Transferability was achieved by collecting detailed data specific to the triage context and recruiting triage nurses from each of the three EDs, all of which are governed operationally by the same organisation but are different in their physical environment, staffing and skill mix, patient cohorts and department culture.

Dependability was assured by a sound methodological approach underpinned by a validated coding frame, and rigorous documentation of decisions made throughout the research process. Confirmability was achieved by using HIRAID as the coding frame, thus maintaining impartiality and objectivity of the findings. The objective nature of the HIRAID framework mitigated risks related to researcher bias or preference. Confirmability was also enhanced by review of data and codes by the research team who were experts in emergency care, qualitative research and organisational governance, which facilitated discussions of interpretations to confirm the accuracy of findings. Examples from observations also ensured that emergency nurses' voices were represented.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Fifteen triage nurses (four from hospital A, seven from hospital B and four from hospital C) with a median 10 years (interquartile range 7.5–21) nursing and 5 years (interquartile range 3.2–13) triage experience participated.

Findings

The 15 nurses were observed for a total of 33 h during which there were 303 interactions. The 237 patient interactions with 169 different patients included 111 new patients self‐presenting to triage, 48 new patients arriving by ambulance, 72 patients in the waiting room post‐triage and six patients in the care of ambulance paramedics post‐triage awaiting a treatment space. Participants were observed to interact with 2 to 24 patients during each period of observation. There were 56 interactions with other clinicians and six encounters with persons or visitors (n = 6).

In total, 1183 tasks were coded (Table 3) ranging from 12 to 128 tasks per triage nurse. The most common tasks were taking a history (including infection risk) (n = 475), post‐triage communication (n = 288), patient assessment (n = 165) and interventions (n = 134) (Table 3). The major elements of history were signs and symptoms (n = 145), allergies (n = 84), medications (n = 69) and past medical/surgical history (n = 62). Assessment was focused on various elements of A to G assessment, including vital signs (assessment of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure, fluids in and out, glucose) (n = 125). Assessments were tailored to the patient's presenting health concern and included respiratory and cardiovascular assessments if required. Additional focused assessments such as neurovascular and abdominal assessments were observed in 40 instances. The most common interventions were medication administration (n = 40), psychological care (n = 28), and moving the patient to an ED treatment space (n = 24). There were 63 diagnostic tasks (Table 3). Seven tasks coded as ‘other’ included managing unauthorised vehicles in the ambulance bay, assisting with taxi calls, obtaining pager numbers for staff members, organising housekeeping of public toilets; and, in one instance, negotiating with inpatient teams regarding removal of an inpatient's plaster cast. All elements of taking a history, identifying red flags and assessment were more common during the triage process, with few instances during post‐triage care. Interventions, diagnostic and communication occurred during both triage and post‐triage care (Table 3).

All participants (n = 15) were observed to collect data related to signs, symptoms and events leading to ED attendance, and allergies; evaluate one or more elements of A to G assessment; and communicate with patients. Confirming identity of the patient and/or nurse; asking about medications; exploring infection risk; undertaking focussed assessments in addition to A to G assessment; and communicating with medical staff were also common and each observed in 14 of the 15 participants (Table 3).

History Including Infection Risk

Taking a history was the most common task observed (n = 475) in triage nurse participants. The most common sub‐element of history was ascertaining the patient's signs and symptoms and the events leading to ED attendance (n = 145). While this information came largely from patients, triage nurses were also observed using paramedics (n = 16) and general practitioner letters (n = 13) to collect historical data. Other sub‐elements of history were asking about: allergies (n = 84); identification by either confirming identification of patient or the triage nurse introducing themselves (n = 77); medications (n = 69); and establishing past medical or surgical history (n = 62).

Assessment of infection risk took different forms. Some triage nurses asked about overseas travel in preceding 12 months (n = 19), and whether the patient had spent any time in an overseas hospital (n = 9) demonstrating awareness of infection risk revealing issues such as patient returned from overseas 4 weeks ago …. rash came on after the trip (P6) and patient had been in hospital with COVID (P7). Questions about vaccination status (n = 3) were observed in children (n = 2) or for specific presentations such as lacerations. On two occasions, a history of diarrhoea triggered triage nurses (P11 triaging a child, P15 triaging an older adult) to seek more information related to infection risk.

Red Flags

Overt identification of red flags by triage nurse participants (historical or physiological data of concern) was observed on 43 occasions through triage nurses' additional questioning, actions or observed discussions. For example, triage nurses were observed to ask a patient who had fallen and hit her head if she was taking ‘blood thinners’ (P3), ask about hallucinations or feelings of self‐harm or suicide (P5) in a patient with mental health concerns, and ask about sleep or stress in a patient who reported that being rough with her new baby had precipitated her speaking to the maternal child health nurse about her postnatal symptoms (P10). Identification of red flags sometimes manifested in triage nurses' explanations to the patient … you are likely to be having an allergic reaction…. need further observation for potential anaphylaxis (P1). On other occasions, triage nurses' actions reflected concern for the patient such as requesting a bed for a patient with quadriplegia who was on an ambulance trolley (P2), or transferring a patient presenting with possible aortic dissection to resuscitation room in a wheelchair when the patient asked to walk (P3).

Observed discussions with other staff such as discussing possible sepsis with paramedics (P2) or medical colleagues (P13), showing a colleague the cracked helmet of a patient following a bicycle accident (P5), handing over to a colleague that a patient is not to be left unsupervised as they had expressed suicidal thoughts and intentions to harm their child (P12) or alerting other staff about a patient with a history of violence brought in by police (P12) reflected triage nurse concern.

Assessment

Of the 165 assessment tasks observed, most (n = 125) comprised elements of A to G assessment, either by the triage nurse (n = 93) or obtaining those data from paramedics (n = 32). Respiratory and cardiovascular assessments, if required, were embedded in A to G assessments. A total of 134 interventions by participants were observed. The most common was medication administration (n = 40); most being analgesics (n = 30), followed by antiemetics (n = 3), anxiolytics (n = 3), antipsychotics (n = 2), antihistamine (n = 1) and in three observations, the medication was unable to be ascertained by the researcher. Psychological care was a common intervention (n = 28) observed largely during or at the conclusion of the triage process. For example, P1 was observed to tell a patient arriving by ambulance with chest pain that the ED are taking their symptoms seriously … the plan will be for further investigations … When the patient communicated they were concerned about cancer, P1 reassured the patient that that will be explored as well. Triage nurse (P5) reassured a paediatric patient with an injury that her father will be with her the whole time, that she is safe and that she is okay. In some instances, psychological care related to the presenting health concern and informing patients that assessment findings were normal. For example, P1 informed a patient they were likely to be having an allergic reaction, but the lung sounds were normal, the oxygen saturation was good and the plan is for further observation; P2 reassured a patient presenting by ambulance with hypoglycaemia that the blood glucose was stable.

On other occasions, triage nurses reassured patients and carers that attending the ED was the right thing to do. Triage nurse (P15) was observed to tell a patient with mental health issues and distress that they were okay, it's great they came in, and it's great that they are seeing somebody; triage nurse (P1) assured a patient with alcohol withdrawal and the patient's mother that the team is going to support her through the ED care process; and triage nurse (P10) reassured a postpartum patient with a mental health crisis that they had done the right thing by presenting. There were also examples of triage nurses reassuring patients about the ongoing management of their chronic health issue whilst they were in the ED. After triaging a patient who presented following a fall, P9 was observed to ask the patient when they were next due for their Parkinson's medications and reassured them that there was time until the next dose but understood the importance of having those medications at regular intervals.

Diagnostic Tests

The need for diagnostic testing was commonly identified during the triage process. The most common diagnostic tests observed were pathology testing (n = 23) and electrocardiography (n = 18), whereby triage nurses identified the need for the test and communicated that to the patient or clinician colleague. Alternatively, nurses performed the test themselves (e.g., taking blood for pathology testing or performing an electrocardiograph).

Communication

Communication was the second most common task observed (n = 288), with three specific categories of communication. First, communication with patients and, or carers was common (n = 121) with triage nurses often explaining triage and post‐triage processes. These conversations were often focused on where the patient physically needed to go; for example, the patient needs to go to the clerks and then will be called into the annex area, where fast track is located (P15 and patient 154 with ear infection) and asked patient to see the ward clerk and then take a seat where they can be seen by the triage nurse (P4 and patient 58 with abdominal pain). Closely related were conversations regarding waiting times…. patient has already been triaged, they just wanted to know how long it's going to be before she is seen (P1 with Patient 6) and conversations about the meaning of the allocated triage category: triage nurse informs the patient they've been allocated triage category four … ideally they should be seen within an hour (P5 and Patient 32 with vaginal bleeding). There were also many conversations with patients to confirm the plan of care. For example, triage nurse (P3) explained that when there was a room free, they'd bring patient through to take bloods and do an ECG [electrocardiograph] … and P13 explained that she's going to put a cannula in, and get some medication for nausea and pain relief. There were a number of safety‐related conversations, inviting the patient to come back and let triage nurse know if anything changes or they feel unwell (P2 and asking patient to come back up to the [triage]) desk if they feel like the symptoms are getting worse or if there are any new symptoms (P14).

Second, was communication to escalate care of patients with actual or potential deterioration (n = 32), and this occurred during both triage and post‐triage care (Table 3). Triage nurses were observed to escalate care to medical staff or specialist teams for immediate assessment or intervention, and/or the nurse‐in‐charge to expedite movement of the patient from the ED waiting areas to a more suitable ED treatment space. For example, there were six occasions when triage nurses were observed to identify actual or potential stroke and either … liaised with the emergency physician … with a view to calling the stroke team (P9 with Patient 103) or …paged stroke team (P7 with Patient 75). Other conditions observed to trigger triage nurses' escalation of care included trauma (P1, P4, P5), anaphylaxis (P1 and P14), sepsis (P2, P3), vital sign abnormalities (P8, P12), severe pain (P3), abdominal signs and symptoms (P2) and chest pain (P5). In one instance, the triage nurse (P14) was observed to call a Code Blue (medical emergency) and deploy a team to an unresponsive patient in the car park. Triage nurses were also very attuned to behaviours of patients with mental health issues and observed to intervene in various ways from active de‐escalation … nurse participant entered waiting room to bring patient back to the triage desk, calm them down and expedite call for the mental health team and for a cubicle or a bed space for the patient (P1 with Patient 2) to activating an emergency response … nurse (P9) calls the code grey [emergency response for actual or potential violent, aggressive, or threatening behaviour] and then walks into the emergency department main area to let the nurse in charge know that there's a code grey.

Third was communicating with other clinicians. Communication with other nursing staff (n = 32) usually targeted other triage nurse(s) or waiting room nurse(s) both triage and post‐triage care (Table 3). Nurse‐to‐nurse communication was for two main reasons. First, individual patient management (n = 14) often involving task delegation such as pathology testing, electrocardiography or analgesia administration to the waiting room nurse(s). Second, was to ascertain the collective status of patients in the waiting room (n = 10), which was commonly determined by the triage nurse calling a ‘huddle’. For example, the triage nurse (P1) was observed to initiate a staff huddle … gather waiting room doctor, waiting room nurse, other triage nurse around the computer and work through all of the patients currently in the waiting room, the progress and plan for each patient. Of the 25 observed communications between the triage nurse and nurse‐in‐charge of the shift, 12 were to move an individual patient from a waiting area into an ED treatment space, and 10 were related to queue management and patient flow, typically prioritisation of patients when ED treatment spaces became available. Communication with the nurse‐in‐charge occurred more often during triage than post‐triage care (Table 3). Communication with medical staff was observed on 35 occasions, most commonly for medication orders (n = 12) (analgesia, antibiotics, antihistamines, antipsychotics and anxiolytics) or immediate assessment (n = 6) for issues such as suspected stroke, chest pain and trauma. Communication with medial staff occurred during both triage and post‐triage care (Table 3). Other communications observed were with paramedics (n = 25) and specialist team(s) such as stroke, trauma and mental health teams (n = 11).

In summary, these results highlight the diversity of triage nurses' practice that occurs both during and beyond the triage process of clinical urgency assessment and triage category allocation. The key findings will be discussed in the section to follow.

Discussion

Key Findings

The major findings of this study were that: (i) the role of triage nurses extends far beyond rapid assessment and triage category allocation; (ii) history, red flags and assessment were more common during the triage process than post‐triage but interventions, diagnostics and communication spanned both triage and post‐triage care; and (iii) triage nurses use a breadth of expertise and skills to maintain safety, promote comfort and expedite emergency care for patients in ED waiting areas.

First, rapid assessment and triage category allocation was only one component of the role of triage nurses observed in this study. Framing ED triage as only rapid assessment and triage category allocation, to be conducted in 2–5 min, is a narrow and incomplete view of the triage nurse role. Studies of triage nurses' consistency of triage category allocation captures a critical but single element of ED triage. Many studies of triage category allocation have significant methodological limitations with few conducted in real time, and many using written hypothetical case scenarios or retrospective record reviews to evaluate triage nurse decision‐making (van der Wulp and van Stel 2010). The few studies of triage practice using real‐time observation have used induction to identify themes (‘negotiating patient flow and care delivery through the Emergency Department’; ‘interdisciplinary team communicating and collaborating to provide appropriate and safe care to patients’; and ‘varying levels of competence of the triage nurse’) (Hitchcock et al. 2014) or have generated theory conceptualising the triage process as ‘determining acuity’, ‘anticipating needs’, ‘managing space’ and ‘creating space’ and showing that triage nurses continually balance individual patients and the ED as a whole (Reay et al. 2016). The results of these studies have similarities to our study with patient safety and communication as central tenets of triage nurse practice. Our study provides observational evidence of the depth and breadth of triage nurse practice in real‐time, real‐world triage practice.

Second, as expected, history, red flags and assessment were common during the triage process, but our study clearly shows that interventions (including psychological care), diagnostics and communication spanned both triage and post‐triage care. These findings are at odds with the traditional presentation of triage as a linear process clearly divided into triage and post‐triage care (ACSQHC 2024). Australian triage education resources (ETEK) state that first aid interventions such as slings and wound dressings are part of the triage process (ACSQHC 2024, 7); however, pain management, pathology testing and diagnostic imaging are framed as ‘extra tasks’ and not the ‘primary role of the triage nurse’ (ACSQHC 2024, 30). Triage nurses' integration of interventions, diagnostics and communication into the triage process is likely driven by significant delays in their capacity to return to ED waiting areas after the conclusion of the triage episode. Australian triage nurses manage 9.1 million ED attendances annually (AIHW 2026), and 33% of patients wait longer than their triage category recommendation (AIHW 2026), so are solely in the care of emergency nurses. Almost half of ED patients (47%) of ED patients spend time in the waiting room, 30% spend time in triage assessment spaces and waiting room stays can be as long as 348 min (Pryce et al. 2021). Triage nurses are responsible for the safety of significant numbers of patients for many hours with no control over incoming patients and beyond safe nurse‐to‐patient ratios. It may be that the integration of interventions, diagnostics and communication into the triage process is a deliberate safety strategy to ensure timely care and manage extreme cognitive loads.

One of the most common interventions observed was psychological care, which occurred despite the many challenges of the triage environment where processes are often focused on patient flow rather than person‐centred care (Gamst‐Jensen et al. 2025; Janerka et al. 2024). Participants were regularly observed to validate patients' reasons for attending the ED, reassuring patients they would be cared for or that they were safe, and acknowledging patient and carer vulnerabilities. Our findings contradict the narrative that emergency nurses are burned out and compassion fatigued (Adriaenssens et al. 2015) and prioritise biomedical tasks over relational care (Gamst‐Jensen et al. 2025). Our findings resonate with other studies showing that compassion and caring are central to emergency nurses' interactions with patients and carers (Curtis et al. 2025; Fry et al. 2013) and that experienced emergency nurses use professional skills to integrate procedural tasks and psychosocial care (Gamst‐Jensen et al. 2025).

Third, triage nurses in this study applied their advanced emergency nursing knowledge to a unique and highly pressured context, reinforcing their status as expert emergency nurses. Our study participants had a median of 5 years triage experience reflecting Benner's description that proficiency is attained after 3 to 5 years' experience working ‘with similar patient populations’ (Benner 1984, 31). Expert nurses use experience (situational encounters that add nuance to knowledge, rather than the passage of time) to rapidly develop perceptually accurate understanding of situations (Benner 1984). Whilst decision‐making per se was not the intent of this study, the observed tasks were the endpoint of decisions (Tanner 2006). There were many observations of triage nurses undertaking multiple tasks simultaneously and rapidly pivoting between completely disparate clinical situations within a few minutes (e.g., a child with respiratory illness, adult with mental health concerns, older person with sepsis, distressed family member and attending to visitors seeking directions). The triage nurses in this study used multiple sources of information to get the information they needed, and priority setting was observed as a constant and dynamic activity. They also demonstrated advanced critical thinking skills and were highly attuned to red flags or emerging issues of clinical risk that were not always obvious. The depth, breadth and concurrency of tasks in such a dynamic environment would not be typical of novice, advanced beginner or competent nurses (Benner 1984).

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

This study was conducted in three EDs within one metropolitan health service and was qualitative in nature, thus the findings may not be transferable to other EDs. That said, Australia does have a nationally consistent approach to triage and triage education via the ATS (Australasian College for Emergency Medicine [ACEM] 2023) and Emergency Triage Education Kit (ETEK) (ACSQHC 2024), and the HIRAID emergency nursing framework is used in nearly 200 Australian EDs (Curtis et al. 2026). The limited duration of each observation (2–2.5 h) may not have captured the entirety of care delivered in ED waiting areas and may have limited opportunities to observe reassessment. The findings presented in this paper are from triage nurses who consented to participate, and their visible interactions with patients, carers and clinicians. It is possible there were other elements of care delivered in ED waiting areas not captured in this study. Finally, there was potential that observations were influenced by the Hawthorne effect, whereby participants change their behaviour when being watched (Mccambridge et al. 2014). Given the intensity of activity observed, it was unlikely that the Hawthorne effect had a major influence on the study data.

Strengths of this study were observation of 15 triage nurses from three different EDs, which was sufficient to ensure information power and thus confidence in both depth (data comprehensiveness) and breadth (enabling differences to be highlighted). Further, there were few data unable to be coded to the HIRAID emergency nursing framework, confirming that HIRAID was an appropriate coding frame. The use of naturalistic decision making as a guiding framework was a strength, given its alignment with many characteristics of triage decision making in a complex clinical environment.

Recommendations for Further Research

The findings of this study have added to the body of knowledge regarding triage nurse scope of practice and moved triage science beyond triage category allocation. Studying triage nurse practice and decision‐making is methodologically complex. To understand triage nurses' patient safety decisions in the real‐world context, there is a need to move beyond studies of artificial situations (such as scenarios). In our study, the observational nature of the work limited the ability to collect in‐depth patient data. Future studies should focus on capturing triage nurse practice in real time, for a longer duration and with the inclusion of actual conversations between triage nurses and patients, carers and clinicians. Observations of practice and follow‐up interviews are a common and practical way to understand nursing practice and nurse decision‐making, but it is resource intensive, complex work that can still have gaps in data. Alternative research methods such as real‐time video and audio recordings captured via video or body cameras could provide rich data but present ethical considerations requiring careful navigation (Iserson et al. 2019). That said, the use of body cameras in emergency care research is an emerging method of data collection (Wong et al. 2023).

Implications for Policy and Practice

Triage nurses led patient safety from clinical and organisational perspectives. They drew on their own clinical skills and collaboration with others to maintain safety, promote comfort and expedite emergency care for patients in ED waiting areas. The study findings have implications for policy and practice, and the educational preparation of triage nurses.

The triage environment (physical and personnel) and workflows need to support triage nurses to facilitate medications and diagnostic tests they know the patient needs but are unable to order. This may take the form of standing orders or protocols (Curtis et al. 2023) or access to medical staff or nurse practitioners as part of the triage team, noting that integration of medical staff into ED triage teams can reduce ED length of stay, waiting time and patients leaving before being seen, but did not change adverse events (mortality and ED reattendance), nor patient satisfaction or cost effectiveness (Abdulwahid et al. 2016). ‘Front‐of‐house’ models of care should be critically appraised given the increasing demand for emergency care. Activities critical for quality of patient care need differentiation from activities that default to the triage nurse because they are a visible ‘front‐of‐house’ clinician. The waiting room nurse is intended to mitigate clinical risk for patients unable to access an ED treatment space (Innes et al. 2021). However, triage nurses delegating to waiting room nurses may further fragment care, increasing clinical risk. An alternative model may be a larger triage nurse team, and acceptance that triage and post‐triage care are integrated as part of comprehensive triage practice. Increasing the number of triage nurses would need considerable financial and human resources. Innovation in triage and ‘front‐of‐house’ models of care has been driven by ED overcrowding and focused on patient flow: they do not address the root causes of access block, reduced access to primary care and increasing ED demand (Pryce et al. 2021). Whole‐of‐system reform will be necessary to return triage practice to rapid assessment and timely transfer of care to dedicated staff in ED treatment spaces.

Educational preparation of triage nurses is focused on ‘correct’ triage category allocation (ACSQHC 2024). Whilst accurate assessment and triage category allocation are essential components of the triage role, this study highlights that contemporary triage practice has many other elements, including interventions and diagnostics. Effective communication with patients, carers and other clinicians was critical to patient safety (physical and psychological) in our study. Communication expertise requires theoretical knowledge, experiential learning (practice) and role‐modelling (Kerr et al. 2022). Integration of communication skills (including role‐modelling and real‐time practice) into emergency nursing education is possible (Considine et al. 2025) and significantly improves patient experience of emergency care (Curtis et al. 2025).

Conclusions

In this study, observational evidence demonstrating the breadth of triage nurses' activities across both triage and post‐triage care is presented. Triage nurse practice is complex, multifaceted and extends beyond triage category allocation. The complexity of practice in this study reaffirms that ED triage is the domain of expert emergency nurses, who require a range of expertise to maintain safety, promote comfort and expedite emergency care in a resource constrained context. The results of this study call into question the traditional perspective that triage and post‐triage care are linear and clearly divided and show a clear difference between care as delivered rather than care as imagined. Future triage and waiting room models of care, and educational preparation of triage nurses, should be co‐designed with triage nurses and consumers to ensure they reflect the reality of contemporary triage nurse practice.
