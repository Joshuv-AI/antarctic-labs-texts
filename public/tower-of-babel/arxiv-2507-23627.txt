1
Introduction

Consider a post office that has envelopes with space for up to
h
stamps and that wants to be able to charge any amount between
1
and
n
cents for postage. What denominations of stamps should they use? What is the fewest possible number of denominations needed under these constraints?

To formalize these problems, we define the sumsets we are interested in as follows. Given a subset
A
of an abelian group
\Gamma
and a natural number
i
, define
iA=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{i}:a_{1},\cdots,a_{i}\in A\}
. For a set
A
, define the representation function by

r_{iA}(x)=\left|\{(a_{1},\cdots,a_{i})\in A^{k}:a_{1}+\cdots+a_{i}=x\}\right|,

for each
x\in\Gamma
. That is,
r_{iA}(x)
tells us how many ways there are to represent
x
as a sum of exactly
i
elements from
A
. The problem posed above asks for the smallest set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for any
x\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
we have that
r_{1A}(x)+\cdots+r_{hA}(x)\geq 1
. We call this
the postage stamp problem
.

We phrase this problem in the language of
additive bases
. Let
S
be a subset of
\Gamma
. We say
A
is an additive basis of
S
if every
s\in S
is representable in at least one way as a sum of elements from
A
. If this can be done using at most
h
(not necessarily distinct) elements from
A
, we say
A
is an order-
h
(or
h
-fold) basis of
S
. That is, for each
s\in S
, there are
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}\in A
with
k\leq h
such that

a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{k}=s.

Using our sumset and representation function notation, this is equivalent to saying that

S\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}iA,

or to saying that
r_{1A}(x)+\cdots+r_{hA}(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in S
. The postage stamp problem then asks for the smallest size of an
h
-fold basis of
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.

Some authors consider only sums with exactly
h
elements. This does not make a meaningful difference to this work because we may include
0
in our set, and because we are only interested in sizes asymptotically. We define
F_{h}(S)
to be the minimum size of an additive basis for
S
. That is,

F_{h}(S):=\min\{|A|\mid A\subseteq\Gamma,A\text{ is an }h\text{-fold basis of }S\}.

We denote
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
by
[n]
. When
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
and
S=[n]
, we define
F_{h}(n)=F_{h}([n])
. In this paper, we will be interested in the bounds on
F_{h}(n)
when
n
tends to infinity and
h
is a large constant.

Notation:
Throughout the paper,
A\gtrsim B
(resp.
A\lesssim B
) means
A\geq(1+o(1))B
(resp.
A\leq(1+o(1))B
), where the
o(1)
term tends to 0 as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We will usually omit floor and ceiling symbols throughout the paper.

There were numerous early papers written on the postage stamp problem/additive bases of integers up to
n
including
[
22
,
24
,
11
,
18
,
20
,
16
,

,

,
12
,

]
. We note that some previous papers consider the dual problem: given the size of the set
|S|
, what is the largest
n
such that one can find
S
which is an additive basis of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
; these two problems are equivalent. Other work on additive bases has an extensive history which we do not try to summarize here. We consider bases only of finite sets, though bases of the integers or natural numbers themselves are also interesting objects, leading to, for example, the famous Erdős-Turán conjecture
[
4
]
. A good history of work on
F_{h}(n)
and other related functions is given in Bajnok’s book
[
1
]
, particularly in Section B. We also refer the reader to
[
6
]
, which was an early paper formalizing similar problems and is one of the most well-cited papers on these topics.

We first give the “trivial” upper and lower bounds on
F_{h}(n)
. For the upper bound, any integer up to
n
can be written in base
n^{1/h}
using at most
h
summands, giving the upper bound

F_{h}(n)\lesssim hn^{1/h}.

This fact is well-known, but a proof is given for completeness in Lemma
2.1
. For the lower bound, by the definition of an additive basis
A
, we have that every integer between 1 and
n
is representable as a sum of at most
h
elements from
A
. Then, as there are
\binom{|A|+i-1}{i}
ways to sum
i
elements of
A
(with possible repetition), if each integer up to
n
can be written as a sum of at most
h
elements of
A
, we have that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{h}\binom{|A|+i-1}{i}\geq n,

implying that
F_{h}(n)\gtrsim(h!n)^{1/h}
.

Thus, the trivial bounds are given by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
. Then we have

h!n\;\lesssim\;F_{h}(n)^{h}\;\lesssim\;h^{h}n.

We define

\underline{c_{h}}:=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{h}(n)^{h}}{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{c_{h}}:=\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{h}(n)^{h}}{n},

which both exist by Theorem
1.1
. Therefore, we have

h!\;\leq\;\underline{c_{h}}\;\leq\;\overline{c_{h}}\;\leq\;h^{h}.

Most of the previous work on
F_{h}(n)
has considered the case that
h=2
. In this case, the trivial lower bound has been gradually improved over the years beginning in 1937 by Rohrbach
[
22
]
and continuing throughout the 20th century and into the 21st century using combinatorial methods and/or Fourier analysis
[
22
,
20
,
19
,
16
,
21
,
8
,
9
,
14
,
26
]
. For the upper bound, Rohrbach conjectured that the trivial bound is tight in
[
22
]
, but this was disproved in
[
10
,
13
,
25
]
.

The current best results are given by Yu’s lower bound
[
27
]
and Mrose’s upper bound
[
25
]
:

2.1810\;\leq\;\underline{c_{2}}\;\leq\;\overline{c_{2}}\;\leq\;3.5.

For
h>2
, less is known. In particular, for
h>2
, there is no better upper bound known than the trivial
\overline{c_{h}}\leq h^{h}
. For lower bounds, there have been some improvements. Moser, Pounder, and Riddell
[
19
]
improved
[
20
]
to show that

\underline{c_{h}}\geq\frac{h!}{1-\epsilon_{h}},

with

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}

\displaystyle=0.0221,\quad\epsilon_{4}=0.0115,\quad\epsilon_{h}=[1.02\cdot s(h)]^{h}\text{ (for }5\leq h\leq 7),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{h}

\displaystyle=[1.1\cdot s(h)]^{h}\text{ (for }h\geq 8),

where
s(h)=\frac{\cos(\pi/h)}{2+\cos(\pi/h)}
. Note that
\epsilon_{h}
is tending to
0
exponentially fast in
h
.

The following theorem by Yu
[
27
]
improves upon these prior results and gives the current best lower bound.

Theorem 1.2

(Yu
[
27
]
)
.

For an integer
h\geq 2
, we have

\underline{c_{h}}\geq\frac{h!}{1-\epsilon_{h}},

where, for even
h
,
\epsilon_{h}=0.0830
; and for odd
h
,
\epsilon_{h}
increases and
\epsilon_{h}\rightarrow 0.0830
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In particular,

\epsilon_{3}=0.0724,\quad\epsilon_{5}=0.0789,\quad\epsilon_{7}=0.0806,\quad\epsilon_{9}=0.0813,\dots.

In this paper we improve these results for large enough
h
. Summarizing the previous discussion, we have that

\frac{h!}{1-0.083+o_{h}(1)}\leq\underline{c_{h}}\leq\overline{c_{h}}\leq h^{h}.

Our main theorems are as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Given any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
H
such that for all
h\geq H
we have that

\overline{c_{h}}\leq\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\epsilon\right)h\right)^{h}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
H
such that for all
h\geq H
we have that

\underline{c_{h}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)h!\sqrt{2\pi e}.

To compare to the previous results when
h
is a large constant, Yu’s lower bound in
[
27
]
gives a multiplicative constant of
\frac{1}{1-0.083}\approx 1.0905
and our Theorem
1.4
gives a constant
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\pi e}\approx 2.066
, and for the upper bound, our Theorem
1.3
gives a multiplicative constant of about
(\sqrt{3}/2)^{h}\approx 0.866^{h}
, which improves the trivial bound, which has constant
1
. We also note that the proof of our upper bound gives an improvement not just for large enough
h
, but actually improves the trivial upper bound for all
h
(see Theorem
2.4
). To prove Theorem
1.4
, we use a probabilistic approach and the Berry-Esseen Theorem. To prove Theorem
1.3
, we leverage known constructions of bases in finite cyclic groups. Note that a basis of
[n]
is also a basis of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, and hence
F_{h}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})\leq F_{h}(n)
. However, better upper bounds than the trivial bound are known in this setting and we use these to improve the trivial upper bound on
F_{h}(n)
. In particular, we use a construction in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
by Jia and Shen
[
15
]
.

We also point out that whether or not
\underline{c_{h}}=\overline{c_{h}}
remains open. It seems very likely that these limits are the same, and so we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.5
.

For
\underline{c_{h}}
and
\overline{c_{h}}
as we’ve defined them,

\underline{c_{h}}=\overline{c_{h}}.

Interesting problems related to those discussed in this paper abound, and we mention two of them. First, understanding bases of other groups is wide open. In particular, there has been work done on cyclic groups, but much is unknown (see, for example, Problem 33 in
[
7
]
). Second, in this paper we consider the minimum size of sets such that the representation function is bounded
below
, but even more work has been done on maximizing the size of sets satisfying that the representation function is bounded
above
. One particularly intriguing problem is a conjecture made in Section 1.1 of
[
3
]
which parallels Conjecture
1.5
.

We prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
in Sections 2 and 3, respectively.

2
Upper bound

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
. We begin by proving the trivial upper bound for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
h
be a natural number with
h\geq 2
. There exists a basis of order
h
of
[n]
of size at most
h\lceil n^{1/h}\rceil
.

Proof.

Let
b=\lceil n^{1/h}\rceil
and let
A=\{1,2,\dots,b-1\}
. Hence
|A|=b-1
.
We define

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=A\cdot b^{i}=\{b^{i}a\mid a\in A\},\quad 0\leq i\leq h-1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\{0,n\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{h-1}A_{i}.

We have

|\mathcal{S}|=\left|\{0,n\}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{h-1}A_{i}\right|\leq 2+h|A|\leq h\lceil n^{1/h}\rceil.

Since
n\in\mathcal{S}
, we can clearly represent it as a sum of at most
h
elements from
\mathcal{S}
. Since
b^{h}\geq n
, it follows that for any
s\in[n-1]
, we may write
s
using a unique base-
b
expansion

s=a_{0}b^{0}+a_{1}b^{1}+\dots+a_{h-1}b^{h-1},\quad 0\leq a_{i}\leq b-1,

where all
a_{i}b^{i}\in A_{i}\cup\{0\}
. Since
A_{i}\subset\mathcal{S}
, we have
a_{i}b^{i}\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, as every element of
[n]
can be written as the sum of at most
h
elements from
\mathcal{S}
, we have that
\mathcal{S}
is an order-
h
basis of
[n]
of size at most
h\lceil n^{1/h}\rceil
.
∎

Having established the trivial upper bound, we now improve upon it by leveraging additive bases of finite cyclic groups. In particular, we use the following theorem of Jia and Shen
[
15
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
15
]
, Theorem 2)
.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{N}
and define
n=(3u^{2}+3u+1)v^{2}
. Then

F_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})\leq(3u+2)v+u.

Before proving Theorem
1.3
, we give the following lemma which allows us to construct bases of integers from bases of cyclic groups.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
b,k,m
be integers with
m\leq b-1
. Let
A
be a basis for
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
of order
k
, and let
C
be a basis for
[-k,m]
of order
g
. Then for any natural number
t
, there exists a basis of
\{1,2,\dots,mb^{t}\}
of order
tk+g
and size at most
t|A|+|C|
.

Proof.

We define the following sets:

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=A\cdot b^{i}=\{b^{i}a\mid a\in A\},\quad 0\leq i\leq t-1,

\displaystyle C^{*}

\displaystyle=C\cdot b^{t}=\{b^{t}c\mid c\in C\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{t-1}A_{i}\right)\cup C^{*}.

It follows that

|\mathcal{S}|=\left|\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{t-1}A_{i}\right)\cup C^{*}\right|\leq\left|\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{t-1}A_{i}\right|+|C^{*}|\leq t|A|+|C|.

Since
m\leq b-1
, for any
s\in\{1,2,\dots,mb^{t}\}
, we may write
s
using a unique base-
b
expansion

s=a_{0}+a_{1}b+a_{2}b^{2}+\dots+a_{t-1}b^{t-1}+cb^{t},\quad 0\leq a_{i}\leq b-1\text{ and }c\leq m.

By telescoping sums, for any
\delta_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=(a_{0}+\delta_{0}b)+(a_{1}-\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}b)b+(a_{2}-\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}b)b^{2}+\dots

\displaystyle\dots+(a_{t-1}-\delta_{t-2}+\delta_{t-1}b)b^{t-1}+(c-\delta_{t-1})b^{t}.

As
A
is a basis for
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
of order
k
, for any integer
\delta_{i-1}
, each
a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}
(as an element of the cyclic group) is expressible as a sum of at most
k
elements from
A
, i.e.

a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}\equiv\alpha_{i,1}+\alpha_{i,2}+\dots+\alpha_{i,k}\text{ (mod }b),

(2.1)

where each
\alpha_{i,j}\in A
. We define
\delta_{i}
recursively as follows.

First, consider

a_{0}\equiv\alpha_{0,1}+\alpha_{0,2}+\dots+\alpha_{0,k}\text{ (mod }b).

This implies that there exists an integer
\delta_{0}\in[0,k-1]
such that

a_{0}+\delta_{0}b=\alpha_{0,1}+\alpha_{0,2}+\dots+\alpha_{0,k}.

Now, recursively assume that
\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{i-1}
are defined. Then by (
2.1
), we have that

a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}\equiv\alpha_{i,1}+\alpha_{i,2}+\dots+\alpha_{i,k}\text{ (mod }b),

and so for some integer
\delta_{i}\in[0,k-1]
, we have that

a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}+\delta_{i}b=\alpha_{i,1}+\alpha_{i,2}+\dots+\alpha_{i,k}.

Hence we have

s=(a_{0}+\delta_{0}b)+\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}(a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}+\delta_{i}b)b^{i}\right)+(c-\delta_{t-1})b^{t}.

We have shown that
(a_{0}+\delta_{0}b)
can be written as a sum of at most
k
integers from
A_{0}
, and that
(a_{i}-\delta_{i-1}+\delta_{i}b)b^{i}
can be written as a sum of at most
k
integers from
A_{i}
, and that
(c-\delta_{t-1})b^{t}
can be written as a sum of at most
g
integers from
C^{*}
. Therefore, for any integer
s\in\{1,2,\dots,m\cdot b^{t}\}
, we have that
s
can be represented using at most
tk+g
elements from
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

With the previous lemma, we may now incorporate the Jia-Shen construction of an additive 2-fold basis in the cyclic group
[
15
]
to improve the known upper bound on
F_{h}(n)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
h
be a natural number with
h\geq 2
. If
h
is odd, there exists an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
of size at most
(\sqrt{3}(\frac{h-1}{2})+1+o(1))n^{1/h}
. If
h
is even, there exists an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
of size at most
(\sqrt{3}(\frac{h-2}{2})+2+o(1))n^{1/h}
, where the
o(1)
terms tend to
0
as
n
goes to infinity.

Proof.

If
h
is odd, let
g=1
and if
h
is even, let
g=2
. Then define
t=\frac{h-g}{2}
. Let
u=\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2h}}}{\sqrt{3}}
,
v=n^{\frac{1}{2h}}
,
m=n^{\frac{g}{h}}
, and
b=(3u^{2}+3u+1)v^{2}
. Then by Theorem
2.2
, there exists a 2-fold basis of
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
of size
(3u+2)v+u
. By Lemma
2.1
, there exists a
g
-fold basis of
[-2,m]
of size
gm^{\frac{1}{g}}+2
.

With these parameters as chosen, note that
mb^{t}\geq n
and hence any basis for
\{1,\cdots,mb^{t}\}
is also a basis for
\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

By Lemma
2.3
, there is a basis for
[mb^{t}]
of size

\displaystyle\frac{h-g}{2}((3u+2)v+u)+gm^{\frac{1}{g}}+2

\displaystyle=\frac{h-g}{2}\left(\left(\frac{3n^{\frac{1}{2h}}}{\sqrt{3}}+2\right)n^{\frac{1}{2h}}+\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2h}}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)+gm^{\frac{1}{g}}+2

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{3}\left(\frac{h-g}{2}\right)+g+o(1)\right)n^{1/h}.

∎

Theorem
1.3
follows as a corollary.

3
Lower Bound

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
by giving a lower bound on the size of an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
. To do this, we consider choosing an element in the sumset randomly and the resulting probability distribution. We then apply the Berry-Esseen Theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Berry-Esseen Theorem
[
2
,
5
]
)
.

There exists a positive constant
C
such that the following holds. Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{h}
be independent and identically distributed random variables with
\mathbb{E}(X_{1})=0
,
\mathbb{E}(X_{1}^{2})=\sigma^{2}>0
, and
\mathbb{E}(|X_{1}|^{3})=\rho<\infty
. Let

Y_{h}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\dots+X_{h}}{h}.

Then if
F_{h}
is the cumulative distribution function of

\frac{Y_{h}\sqrt{h}}{\sigma},

and
\Phi
the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, then for all
x
,

|F_{h}(x)-\Phi(x)|\leq\frac{C\rho}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{h}}.

When looking at a randomly chosen sum in the sumset, most terms will be comprised of
h
distinct elements of our basis. However, a small annoyance is that a negligible proportion of
[n]
may be covered by sums with fewer than
h
terms or by sums with repeated terms. To handle this, we define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{h-1}iA

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\{a_{1}+\dots+a_{h}\;:\;a_{i}\in A,\;|\{a_{1},\dots,a_{h}\}|<h\}.

Lemma 3.2
.

|\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}|\leq C|A|^{h-1}
, where
C\in\mathbb{R}
is a positive constant.

Proof.

Observe that each
iA
consists of
i
-fold sums of elements from
A
, and therefore has size at most
|A|^{i}
. Hence

|\mathcal{B}|\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{h-1}|iA|\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{h-1}|A|^{i}\leq C_{1}|A|^{h-1}.

To estimate
|\mathcal{R}|
, for each
k<h
, there are
\binom{|A|}{k}
choices of elements from
A
to form this set and less than
h!
ways to order them. Hence

|\mathcal{R}|\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{h-1}\binom{|A|}{k}h!\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{h-1}\frac{|A|^{k}h!}{k!}\leq C_{2}|A|^{h-1}.

It follows that for some positive constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

|\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}|\leq|\mathcal{B}|+|\mathcal{R}|\leq C_{1}|A|^{h-1}+C_{2}|A|^{h-1}=C|A|^{h-1}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
be an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
. Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{h}
be independent and identically distributed random variables which take value
a_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{m}
, and let
Z=X_{1}+X_{2}+\dots+X_{h}
. Define
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}|^{2})
and note that
\rho=\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}|^{3})\leq n^{3}<\infty
. To apply the Berry-Esseen Theorem, we normalize and consider the random variable

\frac{Z}{\sqrt{h}\sigma}.

Let
\mu
be the expected value of this random variable. For the rest of the proof, we assume that

\mu\leq\frac{n}{2\sqrt{h}\sigma}.

(3.1)

If the inequality goes in the other direction, a symmetric argument will hold.

By the Berry-Esseen Theorem, we have

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{Z}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\leq b\right)-\int\limits_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2Cn^{3}}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{h}}.

(3.2)

Define
\mathcal{I}:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}\setminus(\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R})
.
Then because
A
is an
h
-fold basis of
[n]
, for any
c\in\mathcal{I}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Z}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}=\frac{c}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\right)\geq\frac{h!}{|A|^{h}}.

(3.3)

If
\mu\leq a\leq b
, using (
3.2
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{Z}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\leq b\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{\frac{-(x-\mu)^{2}}{2}}+\frac{2Cn^{3}}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{h}}

\displaystyle\leq(b-a)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{\frac{-(a-\mu)^{2}}{2}}+\frac{2Cn^{3}}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{h}}.

As long as
\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\leq a<b\leq\frac{n}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}
, we have that the interval
[a,b]
contains
(b-a)\sigma\sqrt{h}
real numbers
c
of the form
\frac{k}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}
with
k
an integer. Therefore using (
3.3
), we have

\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{Z}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\leq b\right)\geq\left((b-a)\sigma\sqrt{h}-|\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}|\right)\cdot\frac{h!}{|A|^{h}}.

Comparing the lower and upper bounds, we have

\left((b-a)\sigma\sqrt{h}-|\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}|\right)\cdot\frac{h!}{|A|^{h}}\leq(b-a)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{\frac{-(a-\mu)^{2}}{2}}+\frac{2Cn^{3}}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{h}}.

We let
b=\frac{n}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}
and
a=\frac{(1-\epsilon)n}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}
. By Lemma
3.2
, this implies that for this choice of
a
and
b
, we have that
|\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}|=o\left((b-a)\sigma\sqrt{h}\right)
. Additionally, the random variable
Z
takes every value in
\{1,\cdots,n\}
with positive probability and hence
\sigma=\Omega(n)
. Therefore, rearranging and recalling (
3.1
) gives

|A|^{h}\gtrsim\sigma\sqrt{2\pi h}h!\cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\right)^{2}}.

We minimize the right hand side over all possible
\sigma
, which then gives an unconditional lower bound on the size of
A
. To this end, we define

f(\sigma)=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi h}h!\cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\sigma\sqrt{h}}\right)^{2}}.

Let
C=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)n}{\sqrt{h}}\right)^{2}
. Then

f^{\prime}(\sigma)=\sqrt{2\pi h}h!\left(e^{\frac{C}{\sigma^{2}}}\left(1-\frac{2C}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right).

Setting
f^{\prime}(\sigma)=0
yields

\sigma=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\sqrt{h}}.

Substituting this into our inequality above, we have

\displaystyle|A|^{h}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\sqrt{h}}\sqrt{2\pi h}h!\cdot e^{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)n}{\sqrt{h}}\sqrt{h}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)h!\cdot\sqrt{2\pi e}\cdot n.

∎
