Impact of musical instrument practice on baseline psychomotor and technical skills in novice dental students: a prospective controlled study
Authors: Roselyne Clouet, Claire Gogendeau, Jeanne Tourtelier, Alexis Gaudin, Tony Prud’homme, Samuel Serisier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe acquisition of surgical skills in dental education relies on psychomotor ability, visual-spatial reasoning, and manual dexterity. Given the increasing strain on training environments, identifying factors predictive of technical proficiency has become a priority. Musical practice, which engages similar cognitive and motor skills, has been hypothesized to enhance surgical performance. This study aimed to determine whether dental students with musical experience outperform their peers in visuospatial awareness, gesture precision, and technical aptitude, and whether they perceive these tasks as easier.MethodsIn this prospective, single-blind (blinded investigators), controlled cohort study, 85 first-year dental students with no prior clinical training were recruited and classified into musician (n = 33) and non-musician (n = 52) groups based on self-reported musical activity. Three primary outcomes were assessed: visuospatial awareness (via the Vandenberg and Kuse mental rotation test), gesture precision (via a modified Precision Manual Dexterity test), and dental technical aptitude (measured by time to perform a correct simulated pulpotomy on a 3-D printing tooth containing a pulp chamber). Perceived ease and difficulty of each task were also surveyed. Statistical analyses included the Mann–Whitney U test with a significance threshold set at 5%.ResultsMusicians completed a correct pulpotomy task significantly faster than non-musicians (median time: 125.4 vs. 155.4 s; p = 0.02), suggesting an association with enhanced technical aptitude. However, there were no statistically significant differences between the groups in visuospatial awareness (median score: 20 vs. 16.5; p = 0.24) or precision tests (median score: 86 vs. 89; p = 0.55). No marked differences were observed in perceived ease or perceived difficulty of the tasks between the two groups.ConclusionsMusical experience appears to be associated with faster execution of dental procedures in novices, although its effect on visuospatial and precision skills remains inconclusive. These findings support the potential value of musical training as an indicator of technical aptitude and underscore the importance of personalized training strategies in dental education. Longitudinal studies are warranted to assess whether early musical experience translates into sustained performance benefits over time.Keywords: Psychomotor performance, Dental students, Dental education, Music

Background

Acquiring technical skills in dental education demand advanced psychomotor abilities, visuospatial awareness and manual dexterity as it has been demonstrated in surgical training [1]. This is especially true in dental practice, where working with indirect vision using a mirror requires good spatial perception and bimanual coordination. Achieving proficiency in dental technical skills necessitates substantial time and effort. Innate aptitudes are increasingly recognized as critical factors in surgical training outcomes. Individuals with strong psychomotor skills such as hand–eye coordination and visuospatial awareness tend to achieve proficiency in surgical tasks more efficiently [2]. Moreover, superior academic knowledge does not always correlate with better results in skill tests or intraoperative assessments [3]. The training environment is challenged by reduced training time due to an increasing number of trainees and a decreasing number of simulators and teachers. Consequently, numerous studies have aimed to improve dental students’ fine motor skills and to predict their manual dexterity [4, 5].

Some researchers have recommended assessing innate manual dexterity as part of the surgical training selection process to identify medical students with lower aptitude for surgery in order to advise them to consider other non-craft medical specialties [6–8]. In dental education, some researchers have suggested that manual dexterity tests could potentially be used as part of the admissions process [9, 10], although their use as a gatekeeping tool remains debated and raises ethical considerations.

However, research on the predictors of technical skills remains inconclusive but some factors are often cited such as gender, age, and practising non-surgical activities that require manual dexterity, such as playing musical instruments or video games [7].

The brains of musicians have been the subject of numerous comparative studies with those of non-musicians, and it has been clearly established that musicians display both structural and functional brain specializations [11]. Playing a musical instrument is a complex activity that mobilizes numerous cognitive skills. Musical performance engages several brain areas, including the motor and sensory cortex in the frontal and parietal lobes, the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe, the anterior part of the corpus callosum connecting the two hemispheres, and the cerebellum. Cerebral modifications are linked to the age at which musical practice began and to the intensity of that practice. The musician's brain is thus considered an exemplary model of neuroplasticity [12]. According to some authors, the qualities developed by musicians, such as concentration, rigor, anticipation and listening skills, are largely transposable to surgical practice [13]. These qualities, which also stimulate on physiological, emotional and intellectual levels, are crucial in high-stakes environments, where both activities take place under time pressure, with a potential impact of nervousness on the final result. This neuroplasticity and the qualities found in musicians could therefore be particularly interesting in the field of dentistry and more specifically for dental students.

Throughout history, there have been many notable individuals who excelled as both musicians and physicians or surgeons [14]. The skills cultivated through musical practice such as concentration, discipline, anticipation, and active listening can be highly applicable to surgical practice [13]. Musical activities also share similarities with surgical tasks in their demands for advanced manual dexterity and visuospatial temporal reasoning. However, research on the association between a musical background and surgical aptitude has yielded mixed results. Most studies suggest a positive association with enhanced surgical performance notably laparoscopic skills [8, 15–21], while others found no significant difference [3, 8, 22]. Harrington et al. found that musical experience predicted higher performance in laparoscopic tasks [17]. Specifically, they demonstrated that musical instrument experience was significantly correlated with the speed of both dominant and non-dominant hand movements in various laparoscopic tasks [17]. Additionally, Osborn et al. found that among those assessed, musicians who began playing before the age of 6 achieved the highest scores in a microsurgical task, suggesting a potential link between early musical training and surgical aptitude [21].

To our knowledge, no study has assessed the association between playing a musical instrument and technical aptitude and visuospatial awareness in dental students. These skills are essential for both graduation and competent dental practice, and early assessments may help identify students who require additional support and training needs.

Methods

Aim, design and setting of the study

The main objective of our prospective, single-center, single-investigator-blinded comparative study was to evaluate, at baseline, among completely novice dental trainees, whether students practising regularly a musical instument performed better than those without any musical background in three psychomotor skills: 1) visuospatial awareness, 2) precision test and 3) dental technical aptitude. The secondary purpose was to determine whether musicians felt more ease performing these various tests compared to the no musician ones. This study protocol (DERR1-10.2196/55738) has already been validated by a process of peer review [23].

Study population

All the second-year dental students (n = 91) were invited to participate in the study before beginning their practical training. After receiving study-related information from the investigators, students who were willing to participate and met the inclusion criteria were enrolled upon signing a consent form allowing anonymized data collection and analysis and their consent to participate to the study. All students agreed to participate; however, five were excluded due to exclusion criteria (repeating the year). Thus, a cohort of 86 dental students with no previous theoretical or practical courses were finally recruited to participate. Ethic approval was previously obtained by the Ethics, Deontology and Scientific Integrity Committee of Nantes Université, France (No. IRB: IORG0011023). At the beginning of the study, participants completed preliminary questionnaire that contained questions regarding demographics (gender, age, and academic background) and hand dominance. Participants were assigned to the "musicians" group if they practice musical instrument at least once a week. If that was the case, they were asked to specify how often they play instrument per week (less than 60 min per week, between 61–300 min per week or more than 300 min per week) in order to divide into occasional, moderate and regular player. Each student was given an anonymous number, with the investigator blind to group assignments, making the study single-blind. A paper version of the Case Report Form accompanied the students during their tests. Based on the answers of questionnaire, participants were assigned to the "musician" or “non-musician” groups.

Primary outcomes

The experimental phase was conducted over a single day, divided into four 1.5-h sessions, with the total sample of 86 students split into four groups of approximately 25 participants each. After the presentation of the study and the instructions, and once the informed consent form and questionnaire had been completed, participants performed the three tests, always in the same order and all at the same time: 1) Visuospatial awarness test, 2) Precision test and 3) Dental technical aptitude test.

Visuospatial awareness test

Visuospatial aptitude evaluation was assessed by Vandenberg and Kuse mental rotation test [24], an example is presented in Fig. 1.

Sample item for the Vandenberg and Kuse mental rotation test

Briefly, it consists of three-dimensional structures formed by a line of 9 to 10 cubes bent at three points. Each item consists of five figures: a model positioned on the left, followed by four structures to its right. The subject was asked to identify which of these structures were similar to the model. There are always two correct answers per item. All that's needed is a mental rotation to match the different proposals with the model. The test comprises 20 items divided into 4 pages of 5 items (Fig. 2).

Example of one page of the visuospatial awareness test

In half of the items, the incorrect figures are rotated versions of the model’s mirror image, while in the other half, they are rotations of one or two different structures. Participants had a time limit of 6 min to answer. Two points are awarded for each line with two correct choices, one point if only one of the drawings is chosen and is correct, and zero point if one of the two choices is correct but the other is not, or if both are incorrect. The maximum possible score was 40.

Precision test

To assess gesture precision and hand–eye coordination, we used the modified Precision Manual Dexterity Test (PMD Test) as described by Bowers et al. [25]. This test consists of the most precise penetration possible of 80 targets printed on a sheet of paper stretched over a soft support using a hand-held 10/100 steel endodontic hand file, with the right hand on the 4 rectangles on the right-hand side of the sheet (i.e. 40 targets), and with the left hand for the 4 rectangles on the left-hand side of the sheet (i.e. 40 targets) (Fig. 3).

Example of one page of the precision test

This enabled us to test students on both hands to compare groups on their dominant and non-dominant hands and to assess their ability for ambidexterity. The test sheet is divided into 8 targeting areas, each containing 10 randomly placed targets, whose diameter has been increased to 1 mm; the initial PMD-Test using optical aids, magnified × 8 and × 2.5. For each target, a score of three was given if the penetration point was 100% in the target; two points if the penetration point was more than 50% but less than 100% in the target, one point if the penetration point was less than 50% in the target and zero point if the penetration point was completely off target (Fig. 4).

Scoring method of the precision test

Each sheet was compared visually to a pre-punched sheet used as a reference with the help of a backlight for scoring. The maximum total of points to be obtained was 120 for the right hand and 120 for the left, i.e. a maximum of 240 for both hands.

Dental technical aptitude test

Dental technical aptitude was assessed by measuring time to execute correctly a technical gesture commonly used in dental surgery that is a complete pulpotomy on a right mandibular permanent first molar (Fig. 5).

Dental technical aptitude test

The procedure was performed on a 3-D printing tooth containing a pulp chamber simulating a real clinical situation. Briefly, a digital model of the tooth of interest was obtained by scanning an intact right mandibular first molar from a Frasaco AG3 teaching model (Frasaco GmbH®, Tettnang, Germany) using a Trios intraoral scanner (3Shape®). This process has also been used in various dental simulation contexts [26, 27]. The STL file obtained was then modified using Meshmixer software (Autodesk®) to create a standardized cavity in the chamber portion of the future printed tooth. This modified STL file was duplicated to obtain sheets of 50 identical printed teeth using a Form2 3D printer (Formlabs Inc.) with Grey Resin, a photopolymer resin for dental models (Formlabs Inc.).

Participants received the necessary instructions and a video demonstration to reach success criteria just before beginning the technical procedure. The criterion for success was the absence of any overhang above the chamber and the integrity of the cavity floor. Participants stopped their stopwatch as soon as they reached the chamber and called investigators, after which they were required to remove any remaining overhangs. Participants stopped their stopwatch whenever they considered that the success criteria had been achieved and requested evaluation by the investigators. The investigators then visually determined whether the chamber opening met the quality criterion. If it was deemed insufficient, participants were instructed to continue; if it was satisfactory, the procedure was considered complete. For each participant, the investigators recorded the time (in seconds) taken to correctly perform the pulpotomy. If the success criterion was not met, the time was not recorded.

Secondary outcomes

At the end of each test, participants were asked to answer two single choice 4 points-scale questions about their perceptions on the psychomotor tests. Firstly, participants were asked to evaluate their perceived ease in performing the three psychomotor tests by answering the following question: during the test, you felt: very at ease; at ease, moderately at ease, in difficulty. Secondly, participants were asked to rate the difficulty perception to perform the three psychomotor tests by answering the following question: You found the exercise: very easy, easy, moderately complicated, complicated.

Statistics

The data collected were described and compared between the "musicians" and "non-musicians" groups for the main and secondary criteria at the end of this first iteration. Results were expressed as percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for parametric values and medians (interquartile range) for non-parametric values. The comparison between the two groups was carried out using a non-parametric Mann–Whitney test for small independent samples. To compare the dominant and non-dominant hands in each group in Precision test, we used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired non-parametric data. The significance level was set at 5%. Statistical tests adapted to the type and size of the samples were carried out using BiostaTGV® software. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for the three primary outcomes.

Results

Study population

One participant was excluded from the analysis due to the loss of their case report form. The study finally involved 85 students (Table 1). The male/female ratio was in favor of women (64.7%). The average age of the subjects surveyed was 20 ± 3.2 years. Almost all students were right-handed (n = 77 vs. n = 8). Of the overall results, 2 subjects did not indicate their completion time for the dental technical aptitude test because they failed to meet the success criterion (perforation of the floor of the pulp chamber). Nine subjects did not report their perceptions at the end of the tests.

Study flow diagram

We divided the 85 students into two groups, “musicians” and “non-musicians”, according to whether or not they played music, and how long they played. We thus obtained a population of 33 musician students, whom we divided into occasional players when they practiced less than 60 min per week (n = 13), moderate players between 61 and 300 min per week (n = 17) and regular players when they practiced more than 300 min per week (n = 3). Among musicians, they were all right-handed, and the majority were females (n = 23).

Primary outcomes

We observed a significant difference between the two groups concerning the dental technical aptitude test, where the musicians were significantly quicker to perform a correct pulpotomy on a 3-D printing tooth containing a pulp chamber than the non-musicians (p = 0.02) with 33% of non-overlap between groups (Cohen’s d = 0.51) without any effect of sex (p = 0.14). For the other two tests, statistical analysis revealed no difference between the two groups.

The results related to the secondary objective were not statistically compared, as they were derived from a subjective questionnaire.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to assess the impact of musical instrument practice on baseline psychomotor and technical skills in novice dental students. We found a significant difference between students who play a musical instrument and those who do not in the speed required to reach the success criterion for a common technical gesture in dentistry, i.e. the correct opening of a resin tooth containing a pulp chamber. This result is in line with several studies that showed musical instrument practice positively influences basic surgical skills in terms of manual dexterity, quality of execution and speed of completion of the required task [15–21].

Although there was no significant difference between the groups, there was also a tendency for students who play a musical instrument to score higher on the visuospatial awareness test. Although it has been argued that spatial expertise is highly domain-specific and does not easily transfer to others [28], performance on visuospatial tests has been shown to be positively correlated with performance on a laparoscopy simulator [17].

The lack of significant difference for some tests between the two groups could be explained by the difficulty of the tests imposed on completely novice students. In fact, they had been deliberately chosen to take part in the study because, as novices, they were able to eliminate as many of the biases associated with the technical learning process as possible. However, because of their inexperience, they knew neither the use of the various dental tools nor the anatomy of the tooth, which may have made performing and understanding the tests more challenging. A longitudinal study would therefore be valuable to follow the evolution of these students over the two pre-clinical years to determine whether the acquisition of pre-clinical skills is faster in a student with a musician profile compared to a non-musician profile or whether preclinical practice allows to non-musician students to perform as well as musician ones.

Moreover, studies have shown that playing video games correlates positively with initial and future laparoscopic performance in virtual reality simulators [17, 29]. As musical instrument practice, playing video games intensively would modify the plasticity of the cortical network and have an effect on other dissociated tasks [30]. It would be interesting to cross-reference and compare the results obtained in musicians and video game players, given the existence of common and complementary aptitudes and skills [8].

One of the strengths of our study is that our sample size (n = 85) is, to our knowledge, larger than any previous study related to musical practice in the medical field (n = 30 to 51) particularly in novice students [8, 15, 16, 18, 20].

However, our study has a number of limitations. First of all, the sample distribution is questionable, and in particular the inclusion criteria for the musician group. In this study, students were categorized as musicians or non-musicians according to the amount of time they played a musical instrument. In a recent study on the subject, a detailed survey objectively assessed students' musicality using the Mini-Profile of Music Perception Skills test [20]. As this test is complex in its implementation, it was chosen to evaluate only the time spent practising music. However, it would be relevant to take into account the length of time students have been playing their instruments, their level of certification and the type of instrument they play. Although our sample size was larger than in any previous study on musical practice in medical education, the total number of participants we were able to recruit remained limited. To preserve sufficient statistical power, we therefore chose not to create subgroups based on the type of instrument played or the number of years of practice. Furthermore, we have not measured the reliability and reproducibility of the modified PMD test and the pulpotomy test which have never been used before, as no other study in novice dental students has yet been carried out, offering us the opportunity to evolve our psychomotor tests notably to objectively compare the pulp chamber opening performed by students with that performed by the investigators. Moreover, these results cannot be generalized to all preclinical dental students. Depending on the culture of each country, the percentage of those practising a musical instrument can vary considerably. Our study remains monocentric but could serve as a starting point for multicentric work.

For us, this study is part of an approach to personalizing and individualizing practical teaching in dentistry, and not part of a student selection approach as may have been recommended by other authors [9, 10]. Indeed, thanks to simple and reproducible tools, it may be possible to predict novice students’ baseline predispositions toward their intrinsic technical abilities to adapt the content of their practical training according to their initial aptitudes. In this way, we could divide students into groups of different levels, or even arrange timetables according to each individual's need for support.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, preliminary evidence suggests that musical instrument practice is associated with a faster completion of a simulated pulpotomy among novice dental students. However, no effects were observed on visuospatial ability or precision. Future longitudinal studies should include validated quality metrics, objective measures of musical aptitude, and multivariable analyses to evaluate the durability and educational significance of this association. These findings suggest that extracurricular activities may give students different starting points when entering the program and highlight the importance of early identification of those with specific training needs in dental education.
