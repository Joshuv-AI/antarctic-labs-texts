Experimental and Theoretical Study of the Kinetics of the OH + Methyl Ethyl Ketone Reaction as a Function of Temperature
Authors: Aaron Petersen, Vinay Kumar, Philip S. Stevens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The kinetics of the reaction of OH radicals with methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) (2-butanone), methyl ethyl ketone-d3, and methyl ethyl ketone-d5 were measured over the temperature range of 273–373 K between 4 and 5 Torr using discharge-flow techniques coupled with laser-induced fluorescence or resonance fluorescence (RF) detection of OH. The average room temperature (298 K) rate constant for the OH + methyl ethyl ketone reaction was found to be (11.53 ± 0.35) × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1, in reasonable agreement with previous studies at higher pressures. In contrast to some previous measurements that displayed non-Arrhenius behavior at low temperatures, measurements of the temperature dependence of the reaction between 273 and 373 K followed Arrhenius behavior characterized by k = (6.53 ± 1.24) × 10–12 exp­(−(523 ± 63)/T) cm3 molecule–1 s–1. Ab initio calculations of the potential energy surface for this reaction were performed at the CCSDT//M06–2X/6–311+G­(2dp,2f) level of theory, indicating a reaction mechanism that is dominated by hydrogen abstraction of the secondary α-carbon and the primary β-carbon, consistent with the measured kinetic isotope effect (KIE). Hydrogen-bonded complexes were found for multiple pathways, resulting in a multistep reaction mechanism. High-pressure limiting (HPL) kinetic calculations were performed using transition state (TS) theory along with low-pressure limiting (LPL) calculations using RRKM theory to evaluate the impact of the prereactive complexes (PRC) on the reaction kinetics. The experimental and theoretical results suggest that this reaction may exhibit a pressure dependence below room temperature.

Introduction

Methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) is the second most abundant ketone emitted into the atmosphere. Approximately, 11 Tg yr–1 is emitted from numerous sources, which puts MEK behind only acetone in terms of its abundance in the atmosphere. About 6 Tg yr–1 of MEK emissions is the result of biogenic sources via terrestrial vegetation, fungi, and bacteria. , Its concentration has been shown to exhibit diel variability correlated with biogenic VOC emissions and temperature. Indirect anthropogenic sources make up the rest of MEK’s emissions, with 2 Tg yr–1 emitted from biomass burnings, and an additional 2–3 Tg yr–1 from the oxidation fossil fuel generated C4-C6 alkanes. , Less than 0.1 Tg yr–1 is produced from the use of MEK as an industrial solvent.

MEK has been measured in both urban and rural sites, with median mixing ratios in the clean troposphere of approximately 20 ppt. In the lower troposphere, photolysis along with OH oxidation are the main removal paths of MEK. , Photolysis of MEK leads to the production of alkyl peroxy radicals, which, in the presence of NO, can impact the oxidative capacity of the atmosphere through the formation of OH and HO2 radicals. While photolysis dominates the fate of MEK in the upper troposphere, reaction with OH can account for approximately 15% of the overall loss of MEK.

The OH-initiated oxidation of MEK is thought to proceed primarily by hydrogen abstraction from the secondary α-carbon (R1a), leading to the formation of alkyl radicals, which react quickly with O2 in the atmosphere to form peroxy radicals (). Subsequent reaction of peroxy radicals with NO leads to the production of alkoxy radicals and NO2 (), which can photolyze, leading to the production of ozone in the troposphere. Decomposition of the alkoxy radicals produces acetaldehyde (), which upon further oxidation can lead to the production of peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN). PAN is an eye and lung irritant and can thermally dissociate to form NO2. Because the lifetime of PAN is longer at lower temperatures, it can transport NOx regionally, making it an important contributor to O3 production. , As a result, the OH-initiated oxidation of MEK can impact ozone formation and the oxidizing capacity of the atmosphere. The kinetics of the OH + MEK reaction has been studied several times, but there is considerable scatter in the reported rate constants over atmospherically relevant temperatures, especially at temperatures relevant to the upper troposphere. Absolute and relative rate studies result in reported room temperature rate constants between 9 × 10–13 and 1.2 × 10–12 cm3 molecule–1 s–1. ,− The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) recommends a value of 11 × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1 for the overall rate constant at 298 K and a modified Arrhenius expression of k(T) = 3.28 × 10–12 T 2 e 402/T cm3 molecule–1 s–1 over the temperature range 213–598 K.

The modified Arrhenius expression is needed to fit the measured rate constants for this reaction due to the significant curvature that is often observed at lower temperatures, suggesting a complex reaction mechanism. The OH-initiated oxidation of VOCs often involves the formation of hydrogen-bonded prereactive complexes (PRC) with a relative energy a few kcal/mol lower than the reactants. − This chemically activated intermediate can dissociate back to reactants, proceed to products, or be collisionally stabilized. The possibility of stabilization introduces a potential pressure dependence on the rate constant for this reaction, which could contribute to the significant scatter in the measurements. Additional measurements and theoretical studies of the kinetics of the OH + MEK reaction are needed to help resolve the discrepancies associated with measurements of the temperature dependence of this reaction and to improve our understanding of the impact of MEK emissions on atmospheric chemistry.

This paper presents measurements of the rate constant for the reaction of OH with MEK at 4–5 Torr over the temperature range of 273–373 K using discharge-flow techniques. In addition, measurements of the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) for the reactions of OH with MEK-D5 (CD3C­(O)­CD2CH3) and MEK-D3 (CH3C­(O)­CH2CD3) are presented. Theoretical calculations of the potential energy surface together with calculated high-pressure limiting and low-pressure limiting rate constants over the temperature range of 200–600 K are presented to gain insight into the mechanism of this reaction.

Experimental Methods

The rate constant for the OH + MEK reaction was studied using the discharge-flow (DF) technique coupled with laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) or resonance fluorescence (RF) detection of OH. These techniques have been described previously, − but are briefly summarized here. The DF-LIF and DF-RF systems consist of 100-cm long, 2.5-cm i.d. flow tubes connected to a Teflon-coated aluminum detection chamber (Figure ). Zero grade Helium (Indiana Oxygen, 99.995%) was added through a MKS 1179 flow controller as the carrier gas, and the excess reactant (MEK) was added through a movable injector with a 3 mm outer diameter. The flow tubes were coated with halocarbon wax (Halocarbon Products Corp.) to reduce OH loss to the walls, and O2 was added to the systems to further reduce the heterogeneous loss of OH. Reaction distances between 3 and 20 cm were used throughout the study and the plug-flow approximation was used to convert the distances to reaction time. Total pressures of 4–5 Torr were maintained and monitored with MKS Baratron 722A absolute pressure transducers. Typical flow velocities of 10–13 m s–1 were achieved using a Leybold D16B rotary vane vacuum pump located downstream of the systems’ detection chambers. The DF-LIF system used a jacketed flow tube, and either heated silicone oil or liquid nitrogen cooled ethanol was circulated to control the temperature measured with a thermocouple (Fisher Scientific) placed at the center of the reaction zone. For the DF-RF system, heating tape connected to a variable autotransformer (Staco energy) was wrapped around the reaction zone and monitored with a thermocouple placed between the flow tube and the heating tape. Temperatures determined this way were consistent with measurements with a thermocouple placed inside the reaction zone.

Schematic of the discharge-flow systems used in this study.

OH radicals were produced by either the H + NO2 → OH + NO or the F + H2O → OH + HF reaction. Atomic hydrogen and fluorine were produced by passing H2 (Indiana Oxygen, 99.999%) or CF4 (Matheson Tri-Gas, 2% in helium) combined with ultrahigh purity (UHP) helium (Indiana Oxygen, 99.999%) through a ceramic tube fitted with an Evenson microwave discharge cavity connected to an Opthos MPG-4 microwave generator. The H/F atoms then reacted either with NO2 (Matheson Tri-Gas, 1% in helium) or with H2O vapor produced from a bubbler to produce OH radicals. The OH radicals were detected downstream by either LIF or RF. For the DF-LIF system, excitation of the A-X (1,0) band via the Q1(1) transition near 282 nm was achieved using the frequency doubled output of a dye laser (Lambda Physik) pumped by a 3 kHz diode-pumped Nd:YAG laser (Spectra Physics). The resulting A-X (0,0) fluorescence near 308 nm of the OH in the flow tube was then detected. For the DF-RF system, a microwave discharge of water vapor in helium was used to create the excitation radiation using the A 2∑(v = 0) → X2Π­(v = 0) transition near 308 nm. For both systems, the excitation radiation entered the detection chamber perpendicular to the direction of gas flow, which was fitted with light traps and baffles to reduce scatter. The OH fluorescence was then detected using a Hamamatsu H6180-01 photomultiplier tube equipped with photon counting electronics placed perpendicular to the excitation axis. An interference filter centered at 308 nm (10 nm bandpass, 20% transmission, Esco Products) was placed in front of the detector to isolate the OH fluorescence. To ensure pseudo-first order conditions throughout experiments, the two systems were calibrated using the H + NO2 → OH + NO reaction. The DF-LIF systems had a minimum detectable limit of approximately 2 × 108 molecules cm–3 and the concentration of OH was kept below <2 × 1011 molecules cm–3 for all experiments. The DF-RF system’s limit of detection was approximately 1 × 109 molecules cm–3 and the concentration of OH was kept below <3 × 1011 molecules cm–3 for all experiments.

MEK (Sigma Aldrich, 99%), MEK-D3 (CD3C­(O)­CH2CH3, 98%, Cambridge Isotopes), and MEK-D5 (CH3C­(O)­CD2CD3, 98 atom % D, Sigma Aldrich; 98%, Cambridge Isotopes) were purified by several freeze–pump–thaw cycles, and mixtures were prepared by vacuum-distillation of known aliquots into a calibrated glass reservoir and diluted with UHP helium. The MEK mixtures entered the flow tube via a 3-mm o.d. glass injector coated with halocarbon wax. The concentration of MEK entering the flow tube via the injector was determined by measuring the decrease in the reservoir’s pressure over time.

Addition of MEK can increase the loss of OH in the flow reactor. The loss is thought to be due to OH reacting with MEK absorbed into the walls of the reactor. Conditioning the reactor with high concentrations of F radicals or adding approximately 10% UHP O2 (Matheson Tri-Gas, 99.999%) was found to minimize the MEK catalyzed wall loss of OH. Exposing the reactor to high concentrations of fluorine radicals may reduce the polarity of the walls by removing hydrogen atoms from the halocarbon wax coating, while addition of O2 may inhibit these active sites on the reactor walls. − ,

Theoretical Methods

Geometry optimizations for the OH + MEK reaction system were performed using the Gaussian-16 software package. Geometries and frequencies for all structures were optimized at the M06–2X/6–311+G­(2df,2p) level of theory. Single point energy calculations on the optimized geometries were performed at the CCSD­(T)//M06–2X/6–311+G­(2df,2p) level of theory. All transition states (TS) were confirmed to connect to their reactants and products by performing intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations. The counterpoise correction was also employed on prereactive complexes (PRC) and postcomplexes (PC) to minimize basis set superposition error. The geometry optimization of MEK had a convergence issue that caused significant fluctuations in the lowest vibrational frequency. This was corrected by calculating the harmonic oscillator frequency using the method provided by Lin et al. (see Supporting Information, Figure S1).

Reaction pathways involving a PRC were treated as a multistep reaction using a chemical activation mechanism. Since the PRCs are vibrationally excited when formed, they can either dissociate back to reactants, cross the TS to form products, or can be collisionally stabilized. The high-pressure limiting (HPL) mechanism assumed that the PRCs were always at thermal equilibrium. The low-pressure limiting (LPL) mechanism assumed that the PRCs did not undergo any collisions and stayed vibrationally excited. In both extremes, collisional stabilization processes were not considered. This meant the reaction mechanism could be described in three steps (–). The PC going backward to reform the PRC was disregarded in this mechanism due to the large backward barrier height. The rate constants at intermediate pressures were not calculated due to the challenge associated with determining the energy transfer parameter (<ΔE down>) for master equation calculations, which are usually calculated based on experimental measurements. The calculated TS barrier heights were adjusted to match the experimental results, constrained by the product study by Cox et al., and the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measured in this study. Additional details on the theoretical methods used are available in the Supporting Information.

The low temperature regime was of particular interest for our calculations due to the observed non-Arrhenius behavior, as well as the scatter in the previous experimental measurements. Because of this, only the lowest energy conformation of each reactant, PRC, TS, and PC, were considered. Variational transition state theory (VTST) was also not considered for similar reasons, as at low temperatures, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of activation is dominated by the enthalpy (ΔH) of activation, which is the largest at the saddle point.

Experimental Results

The rate constant measurements were conducted between 4 and 5 Torr under pseudo-first order conditions with and without the addition of oxygen. Pseudo-first order decay rates of OH (k′decay) were obtained from the slope of the logarithm of the OH fluorescence signal versus reaction distance and were corrected for axial diffusion and OH loss on the injector (). In this equation, k′decay is the uncorrected rate constant and D is the diffusion coefficient for OH in He (0.145 3/2/P Torr cm2 s–1). The value for k injector, the loss rate of OH on the movable injector, was ≤8 s–1 for the RF system and ≤5 s–1 for the LIF system. The diffusion correction was typically <5%. The slopes from the pseudo-first order plots were then combined to produce the second order plot shown in Figure , and a weighted least-squares fit of the second order plots provided the second order rate constants.

The room temperature rate constants measured for the OH + MEK reaction were (11.65 ± 0.66) × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1 (LIF system) and (11.40 ± 0.74) × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1 (RF system). These values fall within 6% of the recommended value of 11 × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1. Intercepts of the second order plots, reflecting heterogeneous loss of OH on the walls of the reactor, were below 20 s–1 for all temperatures and was approximately 15 s–1 for the DF-RF system and approximately 8 s–1 for the DF-LIF system at room temperature with and without added oxygen. The addition of O2 to the reactor results in the formation of peroxy radicals, which could lead to OH regeneration through isomerization and decomposition reactions. The measurements over the temperature range 273–373 K display Arrhenius behavior, and a weighted fit of the data results in an Arrhenius expression of k = (6.53 ± 1.24) × 10–12 exp­(−(523 ± 63)/T). Measurements of the KIE are consistent with a mechanism involving hydrogen abstraction from both the α-carbons and the β-carbon, as the KIEs for the OH + MEK-D3 and OH + MEK-D5 reactions are similar. Experimental values of 1.37 ± 0.18 and 1.43 ± 0.17 for MEK-D3 and 1.48 ± 0.14 and 1.50 ± 0.17 for MEK-D5 at 298 and 353 K, respectively, were observed.

Theoretical Results

Five different abstraction pathways were found for the OH + MEK reaction and are shown in Figure (see also Supporting Information). Both direct abstraction pathways and pathways involving a PRC intermediate were found. An abstraction pathway involving a PRC was found for each carbon, and the primary α-carbon (alpha1) and primary β-carbon each have a direct abstraction pathway. T1-diagnostic values for all structures were ≤0.02 and spin contamination for open shell structures were ≤0.7535 for the CCSD­(T) single point energy calculations, indicating no significant impact from the use of single-reference and unrestricted wave function methods. Figure summarizes the zero-point-corrected energies for each of the five pathways, with the lowest energy pathways involving abstraction of the secondary α-carbon (alpha2) and primary β-carbon.

The transition state barrier heights were adjusted in calculations of the LPL and HPL rate constants by constraining the calculation to our experimental KIEs and the product study of Cox et al. A best fit of the measurements resulted in adjusting the barrier heights between ±32 and 66% which fall within the standard chemical accuracy threshold of ±1 kcal/mol for all pathways except for the β TS2 pathway (−1.4 kcal/mol). While this is outside the ±1 kcal/mol of chemical accuracy, it is a reasonable adjustment given the lack of basis set extrapolation in the calculations and the use of a triple-ζ Pople style basis set instead of the much larger aug-cc-pVTZ basis set. The adjusted potential energy surface is shown in Figure S2, and the resulting calculated rate constants are shown in Figure . The unadjusted calculated rate constants are shown in Figure S3.

As illustrated in Figure , the adjusted LPL rate constants can reasonably reproduce the measured temperature dependence for the OH + MEK reaction as well as the OH + MEK-D3 and MEK-D5 reactions, while the HPL rate constants overestimate the measurements, suggesting that the results presented here at 4–5 Torr are near the low-pressure limit. While the measurements appear to follow Arrhenius behavior, the theoretical results suggest that non-Arrhenius behavior would be more significant at lower temperatures for both the LPL and HPL conditions. Unfortunately, enhanced heterogeneous interactions with the walls of the reactor in this study prevented measurements below 273 K.

The calculated branching ratios (Figure ) show that both the secondary α (alpha2) and β hydrogen abstraction pathways contribute significantly to the overall reaction, while the abstraction of the primary α (alpha1) hydrogen is negligible. This agrees with a theoretical study by Alvarez-Idaboy et al. which determined that secondary α and primary β hydrogens should have similar reactivity for ketones reacting with OH. Cox et al. determined a 62% contribution from the alpha2 pathway at 295 K which agrees with our calculated value of 61% at 300 K.

Discussion

Figure shows the results of previous measurements of the temperature dependence of the rate constant for the OH + MEK reaction compared to the results of this study. As illustrated in this Figure, there is considerable scatter in the data, especially at lower temperatures. The results of this study are in good agreement with previous measurements at temperatures above 300 K, but the results at 273 K fall within the lower range of previous measurements at this temperature. The results of Le Calvé et al. at 273 K using a pulsed laser photolysis laser-induced fluorescence (PLP-LIF) technique are approximately 28% greater than the results presented here and appear to be independent of temperature below 273 K, consistent with the low temperature result of Wallington and Kurylo using a flash photolysis resonance fluorescence technique. The results of Jiménez et al. and Carr et al. at 273 K using the PLP-LIF technique are similar to the results reported here, but also appear to be independent of temperature at lower temperatures. The IUPAC recommendation is based on an unweighted fit of the results of Wallington et al., Le Calvé et al., Jiménez et al., and Carr et al. over the temperature range 213–598 K (Figure ).

A summary of reported measurements and theoretical studies of the temperature dependence for the OH + MEK reaction.

One possible explanation for the difference between the low temperature results reported by Le Calvé et al. with the results reported here and the results of Carr et al. and Jiménez et al. could be a pressure dependence of the rate constant for this reaction at temperatures below 300 K. Figure shows both the low-pressure limiting and the high-pressure limiting rate constants calculated using the adjusted ab initio potential energy surface described above. As can be seen in this figure, the calculated LPL and HPL rate constants begin to diverge at temperatures below 300 K, with the calculated HPL rate constants approaching the measurements of Le Calvé et al., and the calculated LPL rate constants in good agreement with the results of Carr et al., Jiménez et al., and the results in this study for the measurements below 300 K.

Nguyen and Stanton showed that OH + VOC reactions involving a hydrogen-bonded intermediate (PRC) have the possibility for a pressure dependence which originates entirely from tunneling. At low pressure, the competition between the forward reaction of excited PRCs over the tight transition state and the fast dissociation reaction back to the reactants can lead to a reduction in the overall rate of the reaction. When the PRC is collisionally stabilized at higher pressures, it does not have enough energy to cross the TS and react, but tunneling can enhance the rate of reaction, especially at lower temperatures. At high temperatures, the impact of tunneling decreases, since the stabilization of PRCs in the well decreases. As a result, the LPL rate constant converges with the HPL rate constant, as illustrated in Figure .

The measurements reported by Carr et al. were done at pressures between 25 and 41 Torr of helium, with a single measurement at 503 Torr at 398 K, while the results of Le Calvé et al. were done at higher pressures between 100 and 300 Torr of helium. These latter authors did not observe a pressure dependence between 100 and 300 Torr at room temperature but did not conduct measurements as a function of pressure at lower temperatures. Given the difference in pressure between these two studies, it is possible that the difference in the measured rate constants is due to a pressure dependence of the reaction at low temperatures.

However, the measurements reported by Wallington and Kurylo at 240 K were conducted between 25 and 50 Torr of argon, resulting in a measured rate constant that is approximately 26% greater than the results of Carr et al. at a similar pressure and temperature and agree with the results of Le Calvé et al. at 100 Torr. ,, Similarly, the measurements reported by Jiménez et al. at 100 Torr are consistent with the lower pressure measurements of Carr et al. at lower temperatures (Figure ). , These results suggest that the scatter in the reported measurements at lower temperatures may be due to uncertainties associated with the experimental measurements rather than a pressure dependence.

Computational studies have shown that the OH + acetone reaction also proceeds through a multistep hydrogen abstraction mechanism involving the formation of a PRC. − At temperatures above 200 K, no pressure dependence has been observed. , Below 200 K, both Shannon et al. and Blázquez et al. using pulsed Laval nozzle instruments observed a negative temperature dependence and at temperatures below 80 K saw a pressure dependence. , There is only one study for the OH + MEK reaction at temperatures below 200 K. A study by Shannon et al. measured the rate constant for this reaction at 93 K and less than 1 Torr using a pulsed Laval nozzle instrument and found a value of (8.0 ± 0.02) × 10–11 cm3 molecule–1 s–1. Unfortunately, these authors did not perform a pressure-dependent study of this reaction. However, the measured rate constant is consistent with the calculated LPL value presented here.

Two other theoretical studies of the OH + MEK reaction have been reported. , The study by Zhou et al. focused on temperatures above 500 K, while Gao et al. reported HPL calculated rate constants using the steady-state method and an Eckart tunneling correction as well as LPL (“well skipping”) rate constants over the temperature range reported here (Figure S3). These authors found five reaction pathways at the DLPNO-CCSD­(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ//M06–2x-D3/may-ccpVTZ level of theory and located a PRC structure for each pathway. In contrast to the results reported here, they found PRCs for the two direct abstraction pathways, with well depths of less than −0.5 kcal/mol. These authors also found a distinct PRC structure for each pathway, in contrast to this study, which found the same structure for the secondary α (alpha2) and β pathways. They found PRC well depths of −3.1 kcal/mol (alpha2 PRC) and −2.66 kcal/mol (β PRC) for these pathways compared to the value of −3.80 kcal/mol used here. They also found a higher barrier height for the alpha2 TS1 pathway, the most important pathway for both studies, of 1.68 kcal/mol compared to the unadjusted value of 0.95 kcal/mol presented here. While this larger barrier height leads to the lower calculated rate constants reported by Gao et al. compared to the results reported here, their results also suggest a pressure dependence for this reaction (Figure S3).

Summary and Conclusions

The kinetics of the OH + MEK reaction were measured over the temperature range 273–373 K at pressures of 4–5 Torr. The observed temperature dependence appears to follow Arrhenius behavior over this temperature range, in contrast to the results of Le Calvé et al. and Wallington and Kurylo, which displayed non-Arrhenius behavior below 300 K. , The results were in better agreement with the measurements of Carr et al. and Jiménez et al. which also displayed non-Arrhenius behavior but were lower than the results of Le Calvé et al. and Wallington and Kurylo. − Theoretical calculations of the potential energy surface were used to calculate both the LPL and HPL rate constants as a function of the temperature. The resulting calculated branching ratios showed that hydrogen abstraction from the secondary α and β carbons dominate the overall reaction mechanism, consistent with the measured KIE for this reaction.

One possible explanation for the difference in the observed temperature dependence is the emergence of a pressure dependence of the rate constant for this reaction at lower temperatures due to the formation of chemically activated PRCs in multiple reaction pathways. When a PRC forms, it is vibrationally excited and can be collisionally stabilized at higher pressures. As a result, the rate of this reaction may be enhanced at higher pressures and lower temperatures due to increased tunneling under these conditions. Additional measurements of the temperature dependence of this reaction at higher pressures and at lower temperatures near that of the upper troposphere are needed to improve our understanding of the impact of MEK emissions on atmospheric chemistry.
