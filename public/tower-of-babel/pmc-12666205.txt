Increased risk of acute exacerbation in obstructive airway disease: the impact of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and small airway dysfunction
Authors: Che-Hao Tseng, Bor-Yang Jou, Hsiao-Chin Shen, Hsiao-Yun Yeh, Shiao-Ya Hong, Yi-Hsuan Lin, Hung-Cheng Tsai, Tzu-Hao Li, Chien-Wei Su, Kun-Ta Chou, Diahn Warng Perng, Ying-Ying Yang, Ming-Chih Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and study aimMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a common metabolic and liver condition that leads to systemic inflammation. Obstructive airway diseases (OADs) are characterised by chronic airway inflammation, with small airway dysfunction (SAD) regarded as an early indicator. However, it is unknown whether MASLD contributes to SAD or the risk of acute exacerbation (AE) in OAD.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 2572 patients who underwent spirometry, impulse oscillometry (IOS) and abdominal CT scans were enrolled. Patients were divided according to MASLD status to compare their pulmonary function and clinical outcomes, including OAD AE over a follow-up period of up to 6 years.Results349 patients exhibited MASLD (13.6%). The peripheral airway resistance measured by IOS is significantly higher in MASLD patients (X5 values: −0.14 vs −0.16 kPa/(L/s), p=0.002; AX: 0.76 vs 0.89, p=0.044). After 6 years of follow-up, patients with both MASLD and SAD exhibited the highest rate of OAD AE (34.8%, p=0.016) of all groups, and the coexistence of MASLD and SAD remained an independent predictor of exacerbations after adjustment by logistic regression. Hepatic steatosis was also identified as a potential contributing factor of OAD AE.ConclusionMASLD is associated with a higher risk of SAD, and together they markedly increase OAD AE risk. Hepatic steatosis appears to be a major driver of this risk. These findings highlight the need for integrated management of patients with MASLD, addressing both liver and respiratory health to improve outcomes.Keywords: COPD Exacerbations; Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Respiratory Function Test

Introduction

Hepatic steatosis, the morphological change of the liver by excessive fat accumulation, is an important manifestation of metabolic disorder. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is hepatic steatosis diagnosed without alcohol consumption history1 and affects >30% of the world’s population.2 With increasing knowledge on the disease, a revised nomenclature was introduced in 2023, replacing NAFLD with a new term, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).3 This novel nomenclature was able to better encompass the multifaceted nature of steatotic liver disease. Due to its multiorgan involvement, besides its known relation to cardiovascular diseases, MASLD has also been linked to an increased risk of other extrahepatic diseases, such as chronic kidney disease.4,6 Consequently, recent research on hepatic steatosis has gradually expanded to explore its links to other distant organs, such as the lungs.7,9

Obstructive airway diseases (OADs) such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are driven by chronic airway inflammation, while MASLD induces chronic systemic inflammation. Basic research has demonstrated a potential link between the two conditions,10 11 with real-world data showing that hepatic steatosis and its associated complications are risk factors for respiratory diseases.12 13 However, most existing studies focused on NAFLD rather than the newly defined MASLD, with only one study using the MASLD diagnostic criteria.14 Additionally, there are only limited data on how it may affect the individuals with early respiratory symptoms who require pulmonary function tests (PFTs).

Spirometry is the most used PFT that plays a critical role in clinical decision-making and diagnosis of various pulmonary disorders as it provides essential insights into the presence of obstructive or restrictive ventilatory defects. Previous studies have demonstrated that lung function serves as an independent predictor of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, irrespective of smoking status or respiratory symptoms, thereby underscoring the clinical importance of regular spirometry monitoring.15 16 Traditional spirometry requires patient cooperation and effort, which can be challenging for certain populations.17 However, spirometry alone has limited sensitivity in detecting early airway changes, especially in patients without fixed airway obstruction, leading to missed diagnoses of small airway dysfunction (SAD), which is the impairment of airways <2 mm in diameter and is the early pathological basis of various respiratory diseases.18 On the other hand, impulse oscillometry (IOS) is a technique that uses sound waves at frequencies from 5 to 35 Hz to assess respiratory mechanics during spontaneous breathing.19 IOS is effort-independent, while being more sensitive in detecting SAD, which is also shown to correlate with poorer prognosis in conditions such as COPD, asthma or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, including survival and acute exacerbation (AE) rates, further emphasising its clinical significance.18 20 21

Despite the observed effects of MASLD on cardiovascular events and mortality, to date studies that explore the exact impact of MASLD on respiratory outcomes, including clinical events such as AE and airway disease prognosis, are lacking. Thus, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to investigate PFT results and the incidence of SAD in individuals with and without MASLD. Additionally, we examined the influence of MASLD in combinations with SAD for various clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

In this retrospective cohort study, we analysed medical records of participants aged 18 years or older who underwent PFTs, including spirometry and IOS, for health check-ups, respiratory symptoms or follow-up for pulmonary disease, along with having CT imaging that included liver within 1 year of PFTs from January 2018 to December 2020. 8286 patients had PFTs performed, 5714 patients were excluded due to lack of CT, leaving 2572 participants eligible for further analysis. The participants were then grouped according to the diagnosis of MASLD and matched with age >65 years old. The diagnosis of MASLD was made according to the diagnostic guideline published in 2023.3 We have listed the diagnosis criteria and standard of MASLD, hepatic steatosis in online supplemental material. Various demographic parameters and biometric indices were collected, including but no limited to estimated Glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL).

Spirometry and impulse oscillometry

Two flow-sensing spirometers (MS-IOS Jaeger, Würzburg, Germany, and Vmax 22 SensorMedics, Yorba Linda, California, USA) connected to a computer were used in our study and followed the protocols of the European Respiratory Society.22 Measurements were done with technicians with appropriate technique and proper instructions to ensure apt inhalation and exhalation of test subjects. The peak expiratory flow (PEF), forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) were recorded from the forced expiratory flow–volume curve. The predicted values for these indices were calculated as percentages. The measurement techniques of spirometry and definition of measured parameters are listed in online supplemental material. IOS parameters were selected to identify SAD, with the area under the reactance curve between 5 Hz and the resonant frequency (AX) >0.44 as the cut-off point due to AX showing high specificity in diagnosing SAD and as predictor for patient-reported outcome measures of SAD based on recent literature.23,25

Outcomes assessment

Clinical events included OAD AE, which is defined as reported record of systemic corticosteroid administration.26 27 Cardiovascular (acute decompensated heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, ischaemic stroke) and pulmonary disease (COPD AE, asthma AE, pulmonary fibrosis, bronchiectasis)-related emergency room (ER) visit and hospitalisations were all documented. Additionally, participant deaths were recorded as all-cause mortality. All clinical outcomes were tracked until September 2024, with the longest follow-up period extending to approximately 6 years.

Statistical analysis

The findings were presented as either the median with IQR or as proportions (percentages) as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test (for the analysis of patients’ baseline characteristics), while categorical variables were analysed using Pearson’s χ² test. Logistic regression was employed to calculate ORs and corresponding 95% CIs. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were calculated for clinical outcome analysis. Clinical variables exhibiting between-group differences with p values <0.1 in the univariable model were included in the multivariable analysis. To evaluate the associations between components of MASLD and SAD to OAD AE events, the percentage of excess risk-mediated (PERM) of SAD and hepatic steatosis was calculated. PERM was calculated as OR (age >65, smoking, eGFR, neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and albumin adjusted) – OR (age >65, smoking, eGFR, NLR, albumin, MASLD and SAD components adjusted)/OR (age >65, smoking, eGFR, NLR and albumin adjusted). The threshold for statistical significance was set at a p value <0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA) and MedCalc, V.20.215.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of the research.

Results

Study participants and baseline characteristics

Among 2572 patients who enrolled to final analysis, 349 patients exhibited MASLD (13.6%), as demonstrated in online supplemental figure 1. Propensity score matching for age was successfully performed at a 1:1 ratio between MASLD and non-MASLD groups. In online supplemental table 1, patients in the MASLD group exhibited higher body mass index (BMI) (non-MASLD vs MASLD group, median 24.4 vs 26.7 kg/m2, p<0.001), with a higher prevalence of hypertension (49.6% vs 61.6%, p=0.001), diabetes (19.5% vs 28.7%, p=0.005) and hyperlipidaemia (26.9% vs 34.7%, p=0.027).

Pulmonary function comparison between MASLD and non-MASLD groups

Figure 1 shows the baseline spirometry data of the enrolled patients. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of percentage of predicted FEV1 (85.0% vs 83.0 %, p=0.474), FVC (81.0% vs 81.0 %, p=0.935) or PEF (71.5% vs 73.0 %, p=0.268). However, the MASLD group had more negative X5 values (−0.14 vs −0.16 kPa/(L/s), p=0.002) and greater AX (0.76 vs 0.89, p=0.044), while other IOS parameters, including R5–R20, X5 and Fres, all indicating that despite no significant differences being observed in central airway resistance, patients with MASLD exhibited increased peripheral airway resistance compared with those with non-MASLD. Further details are presented in online supplemental table 2.

Distribution of spirometry and impulse oscillometry (IOS) parameters of patients with and without metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Spirometry parameters include forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC) and peak expiratory flow (PEF). IOS parameters include resonant frequency (Fres), difference between resistance in 5 Hz and 20 Hz (R5–R20) and area under the reactance curve between 5 Hz and resonant frequency (AX). In spirometry testing, no significant differences were observed in FEV1, FVC or PEF. However, in terms of IOS parameters, the MASLD group exhibited a significantly higher AX value (p=0.044), suggesting a greater incidence of small airway disease. Although the MASLD group also showed higher R5–R20 and Fres values, these differences were not statistically significant.

Demographics of patients stratified by MASLD and SAD status

In table 1, participants were divided into four groups based on their MASLD and SAD status: non-MASLD and non-SAD, non-MASLD and SAD, MASLD and non-SAD, and MASLD and SAD. Patients in the MASLD and SAD group demonstrated a higher BMI (27.3 kg/m², p<0.001) . Additionally, the MASLD and SAD group showed the highest prevalence of hypertension (66.3%, p<0.001), diabetes (30.0%, p=0.018) and hyperlipidaemia (37.1%, p=0.015). This group also had the highest white blood cell counts (6.9× 109/L, p<0.001), TG levels (118.0 mg/dL, p<0.001), higher fasting blood sugar levels (106.0, p=0.022) and HbA1C levels (6.1 %, p=0.003), as well as the lowest HDL levels (46.0 mg/dL, p=0.014). These findings suggest that patients with coexisting MASLD and SAD exhibited more pronounced metabolic abnormalities.

Risk of OAD AE according to the presence or absence of MASLD and SAD

In figure 2, Kaplan-Meier survival curves for OAD AE demonstrated the probability of remaining free from AEs among patients with or without MASLD and SAD. Patients with MASLD and SAD developed OAD AE in a shorter period. To verify whether other confounders might have an impact, a logistic regression analysis was done to determine independent relationships between OAD AE and MASLD and SAD status, as shown in table 3. Baseline characteristics of patients with and without OAD AE are also listed in online supplemental table 3. The unadjusted model showed that age (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.719, 95% CI 1.223 to 2.417, p=0.002), smoking (aOR 2.032, 95% CI 1.422 to 2.904, p<0.001), eGFR (aOR 0.992, 95% CI 0.986 to 0.999, p=0.021), high NLR (aOR 1.042, 95% CI 1.000 to 1.080, p=0.023) and albumin (aOR 0.562, 95% CI 0.391 to 0.808, p=0.002) were factors associated with OAD AE. We then designed two adjusted models. The first model adjusted for age, while the second model adjusted for age, smoking, eGFR, NLR and albumin. In both models, MASLD and SAD status remained significantly associated with the occurrence of OAD AE (p=0.042, MASLD and SAD group aOR 1.362, 95% CI 0.831 to 2.232, p=0.221 in model 1, p=0.012, aOR 1.252, 95% CI 0.658 to 2.385, p=0.493 in model 2), indicating that having both MASLD and SAD is an independent and significant risk factor for OAD AE.

Contribution of different components of MASLD and SAD to the association with future OAD AE events

Online supplemental figure 2 illustrates the associations between the components of MASLD and SAD group and OAD AE. SAD was found to play a notable role, contributing to the association with a PERM of 28.32%, while hepatic steatosis was the most significant contributor to the association between MASLD and SAD and OAD AE, with percentages of PERM at 74.57%. We also conducted various logistic regression models to investigate whether a higher number of cardiometabolic factors correlates with OAD AE in patients with MASLD and SAD (online supplemental table 4). Among the analysis, the number of cardiometabolic factors consistently showed no significant association with OAD AE in patients with MASLD and SAD. These findings suggest that while hepatic steatosis and SAD significantly contribute to OAD AE, the risk does not significantly increase with a higher number of cardiometabolic factors.

Discussion

This study presents novel insights into the relationship between MASLD, SAD and OAD AE. First, the prevalence of SAD is significantly higher in patients with MASLD compared with non-MASLD patients. Second, patients with both MASLD and SAD exhibit the highest rate of OAD exacerbations. Furthermore, in addition to the direct respiratory involvement of SAD, we identified hepatic steatosis as a potential contributing factor of OAD AE.

Recent studies have found an association between metabolic MASLD and COPD, particularly in terms of incidence and lung function,14 28 as well as between NAFLD and asthma.29 It is also established that lung function decline is related to AEs.30 31 Therefore, it is reasonable to suggest that patients with MASLD who experience a more rapid decline in lung function may have a higher risk of OAD exacerbations. Additionally, SAD is not only considered a precursor to OAD such as COPD and asthma32 33 but also a risk factor of AEs in OAD.34 35 Together, these results may explain our study’s findings that MASLD and SAD are associated with an increased risk of OAD AE.

Previous studies have also suggested hepatic steatosis as an independent risk factor for respiratory morbidity, further reinforcing the concept of a liver–lung axis.7 36 Several mechanisms may explain this observation. First, hepatic steatosis is associated with chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, which can contribute to airway hyperresponsiveness. Elevated inflammatory markers in hepatic steatosis patients, including CRP and NLR, also suggest that systemic inflammation may be a key driver of airway disease progression.36 37 Second, MASLD-related metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia, has been implicated in lung function decline as they can alter pulmonary homeostasis and predispose patients to airway obstruction.12 38 Additionally, hepatic steatosis and airway disease may share certain genetic factors, further strengthening the connection between the two conditions.9 39

It is known that higher cardiometabolic burden may exacerbate systemic inflammation,40 41 which also contributes to OAD AE. In the meantime, SAD was also found to be aggravated by cardiometabolic factors. Diabetes could induce small airway remodelling,42 with hyperlipidaemia and central obesity being shown as independent risk factors of SAD.43 Therefore, we investigated whether a greater burden of cardiometabolic factors in patients with both MASLD and SAD correlated with an increased risk of OAD AE. Surprisingly, no such cumulative effect was observed. A key implication of our findings is that metabolic dysfunction itself, rather than the number of affected organs, could independently contribute to pulmonary disease progression.1236,38 Another possible explanation for our findings is that genetic factors may also play an important role in the association between MASLD and OAD AE.9 39

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a single-centre retrospective cohort study, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to broader populations. Second, among all the enrolled patients, the exact indication for PFTs was not documented at the time of enrolment and could not be clarified due to the retrospective design. Furthermore, due to the lack of serial follow-up PFTs, we were unable to assess longitudinal changes in lung function and whether MASLD contributes to progressive airway dysfunction over time. Finally, although we controlled multiple potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, as factors such as physical activity levels, dietary habits and genetic predisposition were not accounted for. These unmeasured variables may influence both metabolic dysfunction and respiratory outcomes.

Our study also has several strengths. To begin with, the long follow-up period, spanning from January 2018 to September 2024, allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of clinical outcomes. Additionally, to our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically investigate the association between MASLD, SAD and OAD AE. Unlike previous research that primarily focused on spirometry-based assessments of large airway function, our study incorporated IOS to assess peripheral airway resistance, providing a more nuanced understanding of how MASLD affects pulmonary physiology.

Several implications could be made from our study for clinical practice. Traditionally, airway disease and hepatic steatosis have been managed separately. However, our results suggest a close link between MASLD, SAD and respiratory outcomes. Hepatic steatosis should be recognised as a key risk factor for respiratory exacerbations. Screening for MASLD in patients with SAD or recurrent OAD AE may provide valuable prognostic information and facilitate earlier intervention. Conversely, for patients with MASLD, assessing pulmonary function is worthy of consideration. In addition to traditional spirometry, performing IOS to evaluate SAD may offer further clinical insights. To further clarify the mechanism underlying the association between steatotic liver disease and airway disease, well-designed prospective studies are necessary.

Conclusion

Our results suggested that MASLD is associated with higher risk of SAD. Nevertheless, the coexistence of MASLD and SAD may contribute to a greater impact on OAD AE. Among patients with MASLD and SAD, hepatic steatosis and SAD are identified as a potential contributing factor to OAD AE. These results emphasise the need for a multidisciplinary approach in managing patients with MASLD, taking respiratory health into account alongside traditional metabolic and hepatic considerations to improve clinical outcomes.
