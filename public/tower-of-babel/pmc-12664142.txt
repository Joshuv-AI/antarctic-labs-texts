Maximizing tendency and meaning in life: a cross-cultural network analysis of Chinese and American samples
Authors: Min Ma, Jiaxin Deng, Shenglin Zhai, Keke Jia, Jiahui Sui, Nansheng Lin, Mengcheng Wang, Li Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the link between maximizing tendency and meaning in life across the cultural contexts of China and the United States, revealing the uniqueness and universality of the impact of culture on this relation. Survey data from 1,288 Chinese participants and 1,008 American participants underwent cross-cultural comparison and network analysis. The findings revealed [1] differences in the network structure and core characteristics of maximizing tendency across the two cultural contexts; [2] a consistent positive correlation between maximizing tendency and meaning in life across the two cultures; and [3] cultural differences in the network structure and core characteristics of the relation between maximizing tendency and meaning in life. Maximizing goal was more strongly connected to meaning in the Chinese sample, whereas both maximizing goal and strategy were central in the American sample. These findings provide theoretical and practical insights into how culturally shaped decision-making styles influence the pursuit of meaning in life.Keywords: Maximizing tendency, Meaning in life, Cross-cultural comparison, Network analysis

Introduction

A sense of meaning in life—defined as the perception that one’s existence is purposeful, coherent, and significant—is a crucial component of their psychological well-being [4, 5]. Previous research shows that lacking meaning in life is associated with existential distress, diminished motivation, and even mental dysfunction [6]. Given that daily decision-making is closely tied to one’s identity and long-term goal pursuit, scholars have begun to explore how decision-making styles may shape one’s experience of life meaning and overall well-being [7, 8].

Among these styles, maximizing tendency—the inclination to search for the best possible outcome—has been a subject of particular interest [8, 9]. Unlike satisficers, who accept the first option that meets their standards, maximizers expend more cognitive and behavioral resources in pursuit of the optimal choice. Although this tendency may theoretically support better outcomes, research in Western contexts suggests that maximizing tendency is often linked to emotional exhaustion, regret, and lower subjective well-being [2, 10, 11]. This paradox has spurred debate about whether maximizing tendency is universally maladaptive, or whether its impact depends on cultural and contextual factors.

Indeed, recent studies indicate that maximizing tendency may relate differently to well-being across cultural settings. For example, while maximizing tendency is negatively correlated with well-being in Western countries, studies conducted in the Philippines [12] and China [13] have found a positive association between maximizing tendency and meaning in life. These results raise important questions: Do different cultures encourage different forms of maximizing tendency? Does the structure of the maximizing tendency–meaning relationship vary by cultural context? And can a more fine-grained analysis of this structure deepen our understanding of the psychological role of maximizing tendency?

To address these questions, the current study introduces two major theoretical innovations.

First, we move beyond traditional variable-centered approaches by adopting a psychological network framework. Rather than assuming linear, unidirectional relationships among constructs, network analysis allows us to examine how specific components of maximizing tendency (e.g., goal orientation, decision strategy) and meaning in life (e.g., presence and search dimensions) interconnect within a dynamic system. This structure-centered modeling approach enables a more nuanced analysis of psychological configurations across cultural contexts—not only identifying what matters, but how those components relate within different cultural realities. This approach builds upon existing structural distinctions in the literature, such as the two-component model of maximizing tendency [14, 15]; Vargová, 2020), which differentiates between maximizing goal (the desire for the best outcomes) and maximizing strategy (the active search for options). While this model provides a robust measurement framework, it does not address how individuals enact these components in practice—especially across different cultural contexts.

Second, we offer a conceptual refinement by proposing a dual-process model of maximizing tendency. This framework distinguishes between waiting for the optimal—a cognitive strategy emphasizing ideal standards and restraint—and searching for the optimal—a behavioral strategy driven by active exploration and option-seeking. Specifically, “waiting for the optimal” is conceptualized as a cognitive strategy that emphasizes ideal standards and evaluative restraint and is operationalized via the maximizing goal subscale; “searching for the optimal” is conceptualized as a behavioral strategy that emphasizes active exploration and option seeking and is operationalized via the maximizing strategy subscale [16]. Drawing on prior cultural research, we argue that Chinese participants are more likely to adopt the former due to Confucian ideals of moderation and dialectical reasoning [17], while American participants are more inclined toward the latter, rooted in individualistic values emphasizing autonomy and proactive agency [18]. Unlike the two-component model, which describes what maximizing is made of, our dual-process model explains how individuals culturally express and implement their maximizing tendency. This framework enables us to move beyond simplistic East–West dichotomies by identifying specific culturally shaped decision strategies that link to life meaning.

Importantly, we further propose that these culturally embedded decision tendencies also shape how strongly maximizing tendency relates to meaning in life. Specifically, in cultures where active exploration aligns with cultural norms of self-determination—such as the U.S.—the act of searching for the optimal may contribute more directly to one’s construction of life meaning than the more contemplative cognitive stance of “waiting”. Thus, although both forms of maximizing tendency may serve self-development goals, their affective and motivational outcomes may differ cross-culturally.

In addition, cultural variation in how individuals value process versus outcome may further shape the expression of maximizing tendency. Chinese culture tends to prioritize outcome-based achievement and delayed gratification [19, 20], which may reinforce a more evaluative, internally oriented decision style. In contrast, American cultural contexts often emphasize expressive autonomy and real-time agency in choice behavior [1], aligning with behavioral maximizing tendency. These differences may explain why behavioral forms of maximizing tendency (i.e., searching) are more strongly linked to meaning in American samples.

In sum, the current study aims to explore the culturally contingent structure linking maximizing tendency and meaning in life using network analysis. By doing so, we contribute to both theory and method—clarifying how decision styles are culturally patterned and revealing how different elements of maximizing tendency interact with meaning in life in varying sociocultural environments. Based on this framework, we propose three hypotheses:

Methods

Participants

This study analyzed data from 2,296 participants, including 1,288 from China and 1,008 from the United States. Participants were recruited using non-probability convenience sampling via two platforms: Credamo for the Chinese sample and Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) for the American sample. To ensure data quality, only high-credibility participants were invited (approval rating ≥ 95% on the platforms), with each IP address restricted to a single submission. A total of 1,300 invitations were distributed via Credamo, of which 1,288 valid cases were retained after excluding participants with response times under 3 min (n = 12). Similarly, 1,030 invitations were distributed via MTurk, yielding 1,008 valid cases after excluding 22 participants with response times under 3 min. Attention check failures (e.g., instructed-response items such as “Please select ‘strongly agree’ for this question”) were automatically terminated by the survey platforms, preventing incomplete submissions. Eligibility criteria further required participants to be aged 18 years or older and fluent in their respective languages.

Participants reported gender, age, education, occupation, marital status, and income; the distributions by country are summarized in Table 1. Race/ethnicity was not collected in this study; this reflected the IRB-approved data-minimization protocol and platform constraints on the China panel (where fine-grained ethnic identification is uncommon and low-base-rate categories raise confidentiality concerns). All descriptive statistics are unweighted and reported in the same categories used in Table 1.

Measures

Maximizing tendency scale

Maximizing tendency was assessed in line with our dual-process framework, where “waiting for the optimal” is operationalized as maximizing goal (a cognitive strategy) and “searching for the optimal” is operationalized as maximizing strategy (a behavioral strategy). This Maximizing tendency was assessed using a combination of two established subscales: the 7-item Maximizing Tendency Scale (MTS; Dalal et al., [21] to measure maximizing goal, and the 12-item alternative search subscale from the Maximization Inventory (MI; Turner et al., [22] to measure maximizing strategy [14]. The final scale consisted of 19 items. American participants completed the original English versions, while Chinese participants used the Chinese-translated version [23]. The translation followed the standard back-translation procedure: the scale was first translated into Chinese by a bilingual psychologist, then independently back-translated into English by another bilingual expert. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by a committee comprising two psychologists and two translation specialists to ensure semantic and cultural equivalence. The two versions were equivalent in content, format, instructions, and scoring procedures. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = entirely agree), with higher scores indicating a stronger maximizing tendency. The internal consistency was excellent for both samples (Cronbach’s α = 0.93 for Chinese; 0.90 for American participants).

Meaning in life scale

The American participants used Steger et al.’s [24] Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ), while the Chinese participants used Wang and Dai’s [25] Chinese Meaning in Life Questionnaire (C-MLQ), which is based on the MLQ. These instruments are consistent in content, format, instructions, and scoring methods, ensuring the validity of cross-cultural comparisons. The scale has 10 items equally divided into two dimensions: presence of meaning (e.g., “I have discovered a satisfying life purpose”) and search for meaning (e.g., “I am seeking a purpose or mission for my life”). Each item is scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (entirely agree). Composite scores are computed by averaging the ratings, with higher scores representing a greater degree of life meaning. The scales’ Cronbach’s α coefficients for the Chinese and American samples were 0.79 and 0.72, respectively, indicating good internal consistency.

Control variables

This study controlled for gender, age, income, education level, and subjective socioeconomic status (SES), which reflects an individual’s self-perception of their economic status within society. The participants used the social ladder model [26] to mark their perceived economic status on a ladder ranging from 1 to 10 levels considering “economic status among acquaintances” and “economic status in society at large,” and their subjective SES scores were the average of these two dimensions. Survey results indicated that the scale for assessing subjective SES had good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.87 (China) and 0.83 (United States).

Procedure

This study administered surveys to the Chinese and American participants through the Credamo and MTurk platforms, respectively. Participants were recruited via non-probability convenience sampling on these platforms, which are widely used in cross-cultural psychological research. They voluntarily completed the surveys anonymously via their mobile phones or computers, and their data were automatically collected upon completion. The entire survey took approximately 12 min to complete. Compensation (12 RMB for Chinese participants; 2 USD for American participants) was consistent with prevailing platform standards. Location filters ensured that Credamo respondents were recruited from mainland China and MTurk respondents from the United States. Data collection was completed within one week. This study was approved by the School of Sociology and Psychology Academic Committee of the [Anonymized for review] University.

Data analyses

Measurement invariance

We assessed cross-cultural measurement invariance for the Maximizing Tendency and Meaning in Life scales using multi-group CFA with configural, metric, and scalar specifications, evaluating χ², df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR and judging changes by Chen’s (2007) criteria (ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 for loadings and ≤ 0.010 for intercepts). For the Maximizing Tendency scale, the configural model fit was acceptable, χ²(302) = 2180.881, CFI = 0.904, TLI = 0.892, RMSEA = 0.074, SRMR = 0.050, indicating a comparable factor structure across cultures; constraining loadings yielded minimal change (ΔCFI = 0.007, ΔRMSEA = 0.000, ΔSRMR = 0.014; Δχ² = 160.14, Δdf = 17, p <.001), supporting metric invariance; adding intercept constraints produced a clear deterioration in fit (CFI = 0.857, ΔCFI = 0.040; RMSEA = 0.085, ΔRMSEA = 0.011; SRMR = 0.072, ΔSRMR = 0.008; Δχ² = 795.75, Δdf = 17, p <.001), indicating that scalar invariance was not achieved. For the Meaning in Life scale, the configural model fit well, χ²(68) = 560.774, CFI = 0.963, TLI = 0.951, RMSEA = 0.079, SRMR = 0.067; imposing equal loadings yielded changes of ΔCFI = 0.021, ΔRMSEA = 0.015, and ΔSRMR = 0.035 (Δχ² = 284.73, Δdf = 8, p <.001), slightly exceeding conventional thresholds and consistent with only partial metric invariance; the scalar model showed a marked decline in fit (CFI = 0.877, ΔCFI = 0.065; RMSEA = 0.130, ΔRMSEA = 0.036; SRMR = 0.158, ΔSRMR = 0.056; Δχ² = 874.83, Δdf = 8, p <.001), indicating that scalar invariance was not supported. Taken together, the scales demonstrate configural invariance, the Maximizing Tendency scale additionally supports metric invariance, and the Meaning in Life scale shows only partial metric invariance. As scalar invariance is not met, we refrain from interpreting latent mean differences and focus our cross-cultural conclusions on structural associations, such as network edges and paths.

Network Estimation & robustness

This study initially analyzed the correlation between maximizing tendency and meaning in life across cultural contexts using SPSS 25.0. Network analyses were then conducted in R (RStudio 2025.09.0 + 387, Windows 10 × 64) to examine cross-cultural differences in network relations. Network analysis comprised four components: network estimation and visualization, centrality estimation, stability testing of centrality measures, and network comparison.

First, item-level partial correlation networks were estimated and visualized with qgraph using EBICglasso (γ = 0.50) to balance fit and sparsity. Prior to estimation, we screened multicollinearity (all VIFs < 3). Edge weights represent regularized partial correlations; for readability, figures show edges with |weight| >0.05. We conducted γ-sensitivity checks (γ = 0.25, 0.75), yielding virtually identical networks (edge-weight correlations > 0.99). Because Likert items are ordinal, we re-estimated the networks from polychoric correlation matrices to respect measurement scale. As a nonparametric robustness check, we additionally estimated zero-order Spearman networks and compared them to the main models.

Second, centrality estimation involves four indicators: (a) strength, which refers to the sum of a node’s direct connections to other nodes; (b) closeness, which refers to a node’s total distance from all other nodes in the network; (c) betweenness, which reflects the degree of intermediation between nodes among other nodes; and (d) expected influence, which is based on the sum of the original values of all edges connected to a node, reflecting the node’s importance in the network.

Third, using bootnet, we obtained nonparametric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (1,000 iterations per network) for edge weights and for centrality indices. We further evaluated stability via case-dropping bootstraps, reporting correlation-stability (CS) coefficients for all centrality indices. Indices with low CS (e.g., < 0.25–0.50) were interpreted cautiously. Primary inferences emphasize edge structure and strength/expected influence.

Fourth, between-group differences were tested with the NetworkComparisonTest (NCT) for global strength and edgewise contrasts. To complement edgewise tests and guard against multiplicity, we implemented cluster-level permutation checks based on data-driven communities and applied Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control (q = 0.05) when highlighting specific edges. Together with the Spearman and polychoric re-estimations, these procedures provide a comprehensive robustness assessment.

Matching sensitivity

To assess whether group differences might reflect demographic imbalance, we conducted a propensity-score 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching (caliper = 0.2 SD) using age, gender, education, and SES as covariates. Covariate balance was evaluated via standardized mean differences (SMD), targeting SMD < 0.10 for all covariates. We re-estimated networks on matched data and quantified pre- vs. post-balancing similarity using in-sample similarity coefficients (ISC) for both edge weights and node centrality.

Common-method variance sensitivity check

To evaluate potential common-method bias beyond the Harman single-factor test, we conducted a latent common methods factor (LCMF) sensitivity analysis. The measurement model specified two second-order constructs—Maximizing and Meaning in Life—and we estimated a structural path from Maximizing to Meaning in Life. In the LCMF specification, we added an orthogonal methods factor with equal loadings on all observed indicators (methods-factor variance fixed to 1; covariances with all substantive factors fixed to 0). We compared the baseline second-order model with the LCMF model, reporting model fit (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) and the change in the standardized focal path (Δβ) as the primary sensitivity index. We treat the LCMF as a sensitivity tool rather than a definitive decomposition of true method variance.

Results

Common method bias test

This study performed Harman’s single-factor test to examine common method bias. The results showed that the Chinese data had five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor explaining 32.20% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Similarly, the American data had five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor explaining 30.08% of the variance, which is also below the critical threshold of 40%. Hence, this study did not exhibit significant common method bias.

LCMF sensitivity analysis

The baseline second-order model showed acceptable fit (CFI = 0.922, TLI = 0.914, RMSEA = 0.054, SRMR = 0.054). The LCMF model produced comparable fit with small improvements in incremental and close-fit indices (CFI = 0.929, TLI = 0.922, RMSEA = 0.051, SRMR = 0.080; ΔCFI = + 0.007). The standardized path from Maximizing to Meaning changed minimally from β = 0.951 to β = 0.994 (Δβ = +0.042), while the unstandardized estimates remained non-significant in both models (baseline: b = 3.079, SE = 2.979, p =.301, 95% CI [− 2.760, 8.918]; LCMF: b = 8.759, SE = 89.617, p =.922, 95% CI [− 166.887, 184.406]). Taken together, these results suggest that our focal association is not sensitive to plausible common-method variance.

Comparative analysis of centrality measures for network nodes within the maximizing scale between the Chinese and American samples

Overall, both consistencies and differences emerged in the network structure and central characteristics of maximizing tendency across cultural contexts. First, consistency was observed in the most strongly connected nodes across both Chinese and American samples, indicating a shared core structure of the maximizing tendency network. Second, cultural differences were found in the pattern of associations between the two subdimensions of maximizing tendency. Specifically, the Chinese sample showed stronger intra-cluster connections within the cognitive component (“waiting for the best”), while the American sample displayed tighter associations within the behavioral component (“searching for the best”).

Third, in terms of centrality measures, the strength and expected influence indices—both of which demonstrated acceptable stability in our bootstrapping analysis—highlighted nodes from the maximizing strategy dimension as the most central in both samples. This suggests a shared emphasis on behavioral decision strategies across cultures. In contrast, closeness and betweenness centrality revealed divergent patterns (e.g., the most central nodes on these indices differed between samples), but these results should be interpreted with caution, as their stability coefficients from bootstrapping were well below recommended thresholds [3]. Therefore, we focused our interpretation primarily on the more robust indices (strength and expected influence), which underscore both the universality and the nuanced cultural variation in how maximizing tendency manifests across contexts.

Cross-sample comparison

A comparison of network relations between maximizing tendency and meaning in life in the Chinese and American samples (Figs. 6 and 7) can be summarized as follows (Table 5). First, in both groups, the relation between maximizing goals and presence of meaning was positive, indicating cultural consistency. However, the relation between maximizing strategy and presence of meaning was inconsistent between the two samples; in China, maximizing strategy was not directly correlated with presence of meaning whereas in the United States, a positive correlation was observed. Second, the maximizing goal and search for meaning were positively correlated in the Chinese and American samples, showing cultural consistency. Meanwhile, the relation between maximizing strategy and search for meaning differed between the two countries; in China, the direct link between maximizing strategy and search for meaning was negatively correlated whereas in the United States, a positive correlation was found. In summary, maximizing strategy directly enhanced an individual’s life meaning for the American sample but did not directly enhance meaning in life and may have even had a negative impact for the American sample. This contrast highlighted the nuanced ways in which cultural contexts shape the dynamics between maximizing tendency and one’s pursuit of a meaningful life.

Network comparison

Converging tests indicate no reliable global difference in overall connectivity, but robust local/cluster differences. The NCT detected edge-wise differences between the Chinese and U.S. networks (p <.01), whereas global strength did not differ: NCT p =.089 (China = 13.67; U.S. = 13.18). A complementary permutation test on global strength reached the same conclusion, yielding a small China and U.S. difference (China − U.S. = 0.497) that was not significant (two-tailed p =.057; n = 1,288 vs. 1,008; 1,000 permutations). At the meso level, cluster-level permutation analyses with BH–FDR control identified a systematic pattern: cross-cluster connectivity was stronger in the U.S., whereas within-cluster cohesion was stronger in China. Thus, while culturally specific local structures differ, the overall interconnectedness of the two networks remains broadly comparable.

Incremental validity analyses

To test the dual-process distinction, we ran hierarchical regressions predicting meaning in life (controlling for age, gender, education, and SES). In the Chinese sample, entering maximizing goal (waiting) at Step 2 explained a significant proportion of additional variance (ΔR² = 0.15, p <.001). Adding maximizing strategy (searching) at Step 3 yielded a further, albeit small, improvement (ΔR² = 0.005, p =.007), indicating that both the cognitive and behavioral components make unique contributions. In the U.S. sample, maximizing goal at Step 2 explained a moderate amount of variance (ΔR² = 0.21, p <.001), and adding maximizing strategy at Step 3 significantly improved model fit (ΔR² = 0.03, p <.001). Taken together, these results support the incremental validity of distinguishing goal- and strategy-based maximizing tendencies and are consistent with the cultural profiles observed in the network analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

Matching achieved good covariate balance (all SMDs < 0.10). Pre- vs. post-balancing network similarity was high (China: edge ISC = 0.975, centrality ISC = 0.951; U.S.: edge ISC = 0.971, centrality ISC = 0.876), indicating that demographic balancing did not alter the substantive network structure or conclusions. Consistent with our broader reliability checks, we emphasize edge structure and robust centrality indices (node strength/expected influence) in interpretation, while treating closeness/betweenness with caution.

Discussion

This study provides novel insight into the cultural shaping of the relationship between maximizing tendency and meaning in life by adopting a psychological network approach. Our analysis identified three core findings.

First, while the network structures of maximizing tendency show overall consistency across cultures—in terms of the strongest connections and central nodes—cultural differences emerged in how maximizing goal and maximizing strategy were expressed. Chinese participants emphasized maximizing goal (waiting for the optimal), a cognitive maximizing strategy characterized by internal deliberation and ideal standards, whereas American participants predominantly engaged in maximizing strategy (searching for the optimal), a behavioral maximizing strategy involving active exploration and choice-making. This supports Hypothesis 1 and highlights culturally distinct maximizing strategies, which are rarely differentiated in previous research.

Second, Hypothesis 2 received only partial support. Although the link between maximizing and meaning in life appeared stronger in the U.S. sample at the bivariate level, this difference did not hold once demographic covariates were taken into account. Accordingly, we do not claim a reliable cross-cultural difference in the magnitude of this association. Our interpretation therefore centers on the structural connectivity patterns highlighted by the network analyses rather than on mean-level contrasts.

Third, despite the lack of significant difference in global network strength, local structural variations were evident, supporting Hypothesis 3. In the American network, both maximizing goal and maximizing strategy were positively linked to meaning in life, particularly to the search for meaning component. In contrast, in the Chinese network, only maximizing goal showed a central role, while maximizing strategy (especially behavioral enactment) was weakly or even negatively associated with meaning. While these patterns reveal meaningful cultural divergence in structure, the non-significant global strength difference indicates comparable overall connectivity across cultures.

These findings support a dual-process model of maximizing tendency, distinguishing between waiting for the optimal (maximizing goal, cognitive restraint) and searching for the optimal (maximizing strategy, behavioral pursuit). The incremental validity analyses further support the dual-process model. Importantly, while the additional contribution of maximizing strategy in the Chinese sample was statistically significant, its effect size was minimal (ΔR² = 0.005), indicating that the practical impact should be interpreted with caution. By contrast, in the U.S. sample, maximizing strategy made a more substantial incremental contribution (ΔR² = 0.03), which is consistent with the stronger behavioral emphasis observed in the network results. This model moves beyond traditional individualism–collectivism dichotomies by identifying culturally embedded strategies: Chinese individuals, shaped by Confucian moderation and dialectical reasoning [17], may internalize maximizing as a reflective goal, whereas American individuals, guided by values of autonomy and agency [18], tend to enact it through action.

Cultural orientations toward outcome versus process may further explain these patterns. Chinese culture prioritizes outcomes [19], leading individuals to derive meaning more from goal-driven internal deliberation. American culture values both process and outcome, allowing individuals to find meaning through active goal pursuit and exploration [1].

Practically, maximizing goals can foster life meaning across cultures, but the effective strategies differ. Based on the findings of this study, interventions in China may benefit from emphasizing reflective goal-setting and cognitive evaluation, whereas in the U.S., promoting proactive decision-making and behavioral engagement may be more effective. Educational curricula could be designed to support long-term planning in China and promote engagement in the U.S. Likewise, counseling approaches and decision-support tools may be more impactful when culturally tailored—highlighting deliberation in China and active choice in the U.S.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Our cross-sectional network approach identifies conditional associations rather than causes; stronger tests of the proposed dual-process account should employ longitudinal designs (e.g., three-wave RI-CLPM) and experiments that orthogonally manipulate goal salience and strategy load, ideally complemented by EMA and behavioral tasks. Self-reports also introduce potential response-style differences and reference-group effects in cross-cultural ratings. To address possible common-method bias, we implemented a latent common methods factor (LCMF) sensitivity check as a diagnostic; nonetheless, future data collections should include a brief marker scale to enable a ULMC approach and incorporate mixed-method measures. Methodologically, while node strength and expected influence were relatively stable, indices such as closeness and betweenness were less reliable and should be interpreted cautiously; moreover, cultural differences appear to reflect specific structural patterns rather than global connectivity. Finally, construct coverage was limited to the widely used two-dimensional maximizing framework, and scalar invariance was not fully achieved, so cross-cultural claims should be restricted to associations and network structure (not means) until broader operationalizations and invariance-aware models are implemented.

Conclusions

This study reveals three main findings. First, maximizing tendency shows both shared and distinct network structures across cultures. Second, its link with meaning in life is consistent but stronger in the American sample. Third, cultural differences emerge in how maximizing tendency connects to meaning in life: Chinese participants emphasize maximizing goal—waiting for the optimal as a cognitive aspiration, while American participants highlight maximizing strategy—searching for the optimal as a behavioral pursuit. These results underscore culturally specific pathways between decision making style and meaning in life.
