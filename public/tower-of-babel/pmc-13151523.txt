Coexposure to asbestos, mineral wool, crystalline silica and refractory ceramic fibres and risk of lung cancer and mesothelioma
Authors: Fleur Delva, Céline Gramond, Isabelle Thaon, Aude Lacourt, Patrick Brochard, Julia Benoist, Antoine Gislard, Francois Laurent, Christophe Paris, Pascal Andujar, Bénédicte Clin, Jean-Claude Pairon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsbestos, mineral wool (MW), refractory ceramic fibres (RCF) and silica are among the most common exposures to mineral particles in the workplace.ObjectiveTo study the effect of coexposure to asbestos and MW, crystalline silica or RCFs and the risk of lung cancer and mesothelioma.MethodsThe Asbestos-Related Diseases Cohort is a surveillance programme in retired workers exposed to asbestos during their working life. Background

Asbestos, mineral wool (MW) and silica are among the most common exposures to mineral particles in the workplace. Many sectors of activity in which workers are exposed to asbestos are also ones in which exposures to crystalline silica, refractory ceramic fibres (RCFs) and MW may occur. Consequently, all four are encountered in many similar activities and workers could frequently be exposed to at least two of these substances. These exposures may have occurred during the same periods of employment (eg, asbestos and crystalline silica) or sequentially over time, for example, asbestos and ceramic refractory fibres. Indeed, ceramic refractory fibres were mainly used after the ban on asbestos, first in the USA and then in Europe. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), asbestos and crystalline silica are established as carcinogenic to humans, RCF was classified in group 2B (‘possibly carcinogenic to humans’) and MW was classified in group 3 (‘not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans’).

The association between occupational exposure to asbestos and lung cancer and mesothelioma has been shown in numerous cohorts and case–control studies.1 Mechanistic hypotheses have shown the determining role of the biopersistence of fibres in the lung and the pleura.1 In 1977, asbestos was classified as a definite lung, mesothelioma, larynx and ovary carcinogen by the IARC.2 Apart from asbestos, only erionite fibres are recognised as an aetiological factor for pleural mesothelioma.3 Crystalline silica has been classified by the IARC as a definite lung carcinogen since 1996. In men in a general population, exposure to asbestos and silica was independently associated with lung cancer after adjustment for other carcinogens.4 Lacourt et al identified an increased risk of pleural mesothelioma for subjects exposed to asbestos and silica.5 Additional coexposure to asbestos and silica could increase intrapleural retention of asbestos fibres and modify this carcinogenicity. These mechanisms could also be found in cases of coexposure to RCFs and MW. RCFs can persist in human lung tissue for more than 20 years after exposure.6 In toxicological studies, chronic inflammation and fibrosis can occur after exposure to RCFs. Epidemiological studies in the USA and Europe showed an association between exposure to RCFs and prevalence of respiratory symptoms and pleural plaques on chest X-ray, but no excess of interstitial fibrosis, mesotheliomas and lung cancers.7 An update study in 2022 did not identify an association between RCFs and mortality by lung cancer.8 However, a Chinese case–control study found that RCFs were associated with altered levels of lung biomarker and oxidative stress markers.9 Concerning MW, these were classified as not classifiable in 2002 by the IARC. In the epidemiological literature, no data were found on the joint effect of these exposures on lung cancer risk, but an increased risk of pleural mesothelioma was found for subjects exposed to asbestos and MW.5 In addition, a significant interaction between exposure to RCFs and MW fibres and exposure to asbestos on the risk of pleural plaques has recently been demonstrated, which could imply a modification of the effect of asbestos during coexposure to other mineral particles.10

The objective of our study was to examine the effect of coexposure to asbestos and MW, crystalline silica and RCFs on the risk of lung cancer and mesothelioma (in mortality and incidence).

Methods

Study design

The Asbestos-Related Diseases Cohort (ARDCO) cohort study has been described previously.11 ARDCO is a surveillance programme where retired workers exposed to asbestos during their working life were included between October 2003 and December 2005 in four regions of France (Aquitaine, Upper Normandy, Lower Normandy and Rhône-Alpes). Subjects were considered to be exposed to asbestos when they had previously worked in at least one job with a non-zero exposure level based on expertise provided by industrial hygienists. At their enrolment, they were offered a free medical check-up including chest CT scan and pulmonary function tests. All participants received information about the study and gave their written informed consent.

Data collection

The subjects were required to complete a standardised questionnaire describing all jobs held throughout their working lives as well as specific asbestos-exposing tasks. Available information included characteristics of civil status, social and occupational data (complete occupational record) and information concerning smoking status (non-smoker, former smoker or smoker) for a large proportion of subjects. A job was defined as an occupation held within an industry. Occupations and industries were coded, respectively, according to the International Standard Classification of Occupations and International Standard Industrial Classification of All Economic Activities.

Outcomes

A follow-up study was conducted in subjects who had previously been enrolled in the ARDCO programme. The number of lung cancer and mesothelioma cases recorded by the National Health Insurance fund was collected in order to conduct an incidence study with data up to and including 1 April 2021. Indeed, in France, all cancers must be reported to the French National Health Insurance fund in order to provide full coverage of medical costs, including treatment.

A follow-up study of mortality was also organised in the study population. In France, mortality data are available from the INSERM CEPI DC, which collects all death certificates. The vital status of each subject in the cohort was collected from the National Directory for the Identification of Physical Persons up to November 2018. For deceased subjects, both underlying and contributing causes of death according to death certificates available up to 31 December 2015 were then obtained from the INSERM CEPI DC.

Exposure characterisation

As previously described, industrial hygienists evaluated occupational exposure to asbestos for each subject using data from a standardised questionnaire, describing all job positions occupied during the subject’s occupational career, and calculated a cumulative exposure index (CEI).

Asbestos exposure was assessed according to occupation and industrial activities. The level of exposure was classified into four categories defined for each job occupied by each subject, comprising a four-level scale: low level (passive exposure), corresponding to a numerical value of ‘0.01 equivalent fibres/mL’; low-intermediate, corresponding to a numerical value of ‘0.1 equivalent fibres/mL’; high-intermediate, corresponding to a numerical value of ‘1 equivalent fibres/mL’; and high exposure, corresponding to a numerical value of ‘10 equivalent fibres/mL’. A CEI was then calculated for each job by multiplying this level (0.01; 0.1; 1 and 10, respectively) by the duration of each employment period (in years). The final CEI (in equivalent fibres·years/mL) for each subject was calculated as the sum of each employment period’s four-level CEI.

Exposures to MW, silica and RCF were characterised by means of three job-exposure matrices (JEMs) developed by the French Institute for Public Health Surveillance. For each job classified in the international classifications and for different time periods, the JEMs provided the probability of exposure. Statistical analyses

To study the effect of occupational coexposure to asbestos and MW, silica and RCF, Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate HR and 95% CI for lung cancer mortality and lung cancer incidence and for mesothelioma mortality and mesothelioma incidence. Age was the main time variable in addition to age at initial inclusion in the cohort. The cut-off date for the mortality analysis is 31 December 2015, and 1 July 2021. One model studied the effect of asbestos CEI adjusted for smoking for lung cancer and without adjustment for mesothelioma. A model was developed for each of the three occupational exposures (MW, silica and RCF). Then the interaction between each occupational exposure and asbestos cumulative index of exposure was tested. When there was an interaction, the dose–response relationship for occupational asbestos exposure and lung cancer and mesothelioma were presented in the presence or absence of a supplementary occupational exposure (MW, silica or RCFs). Finally, to estimate the impact of coexposure, we performed population analyses with several subgroups. Eight subgroups were defined and summarised in box 1: (1) subjects only exposed to asbestos (reference group), (2) subjects exposed to asbestos and MW, but not exposed to silica and RCFs, (3) subjects exposed to asbestos, MW and silica, but not exposed to RCFs, (4) subjects exposed to asbestos, MW and RCFs, but not exposed to silica, (5) subjects exposed to asbestos and silica, but not exposed to MW nor to RCFs, (6) subjects exposed to asbestos and RCFs, but not exposed to MW nor to silica, (7) subjects exposed to asbestos, silica and RCFs, but not exposed to MW and (8) subjects exposed to all of the above.

All analyses were carried out for mortality and incidence. The lung cancer models were adjusted for smoking status and CEI to asbestos, and the mesothelioma models for CEI to asbestos. Mesothelioma cases were excluded from lung cancer analyses, and lung cases were excluded from mesothelioma analyses. The analyses were performed on men.

Results

This cohort study included 14 218 subjects. Finally, in lung cancer analysis, 11 867 subjects were included in the mortality study and 11 326 in the incidence study. In mesothelioma analysis, 11 667 subjects were included in the mortality study and 10 978 in the incidence study (figure 1).

Discussion

In our population of workers exposed to asbestos, we identified an increased risk of mesothelioma and lung cancer with an increase in CEI in the incidence and the mortality analysis, and an interaction between exposure to silica and asbestos CEI on mesothelioma mortality. There was no association between coexposure to asbestos and MW, crystalline silica or RCFs and the risk of lung cancer in either the incidence or the mortality analyses.

In the incidence analysis, we observed an increased risk of mesothelioma for subjects exposed to silica after adjustment of the asbestos CEI (HR=1.75, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.62). In the mortality analysis, the estimation of HR was 1.37 (95% CI 0.78 to 2.37). In subjects exposed only to silica and asbestos but not to MW or RCFs, estimation of risk of mesothelioma in the incidence study was increased with HR 2.76 (95% CI 1.42 to 5.34). To our knowledge, only one epidemiological study evaluated the association between coexposure to silica and asbestos and the risk of pleural mesothelioma. This study included 1199 male cases and 2379 control subjects from a French pooled case-control study.5 Occupational exposure to asbestos and silica was assessed by means of French JEMs. In subjects exposed to silica and asbestos, the risk of pleural mesothelioma was estimated at 4.5 (95% CI 3.0 to 6.6). The authors reported that the results were difficult to interpret due to different methodological biases and that this study did not allow the effect of silica exposure on the risk of mesothelioma to be taken into consideration. In the present study, in another context, we found the same result taking into account the level of exposure to asbestos. Crystalline silica is known to be among the most biopersistent of non-fibrous mineral particles.12 Silica is not known to induce mesothelioma, but coexposure to asbestos could strengthen the toxicity of asbestos on mesothelium. Davis et al found a relatively high number of pleural mesotheliomas in groups of rats treated with both chrysotile, amosite and quartz.13 Quartz could increase the overall transport rate of asbestos from the site of deposition to the pleura. Davis et al also provide epidemiological data showing an unexpectedly high number of cases of mesothelioma in a Canadian plant using crocidolite with coexposure to silica which, given the results of Davis et al’s experiments, could be linked to this coexposure.

For lung cancer, we did not find any association between each type of coexposure to asbestos and mortality or incidence. A recent meta-analysis identified an increased risk of lung cancer in subjects exposed to asbestos and silica.14 In our study, there was no increased risk observed for silica in the presence of asbestos. Excess risk of lung cancer related to silica was shown in large populations, due to the low risk of silica-related lung cancer in the absence of silicosis. The size of the ARDCO population may result in a lack of capability to identify a risk associated with exposure to silica, especially with exposure to silica which is moderate (only 30 subjects are diagnosed with silicosis in the ARDCO population (data not published)). In the scientific literature, we identified only two studies that evaluated the effect of coexposure between asbestos and man-made vitreous fibres.15 16 The first study was a European multicentre case–control study. The authors did not observe any synergistic effect between asbestos and man-made vitreous fibres, nor any effect of man-made vitreous fibres studied alone.15 The second study did not identify any association between man-made vitreous fibres and risk of lung cancer. In subjects with coexposure to asbestos and man-made vitreous fibres, there was no association with lung cancer risk,16 although RCFs can persist in human lung tissue for more than 20 years after exposure.6

In our study, we used JEMs to characterise exposure to silica, RCFs and MW. The use of a JEM reduces differential information bias, as exposure is not characterised by knowing the presence or absence of the disease. However, it does not take into account the variability of exposure within the same occupation or between different countries, for example. This can lead to misclassification of subjects. However, these classification errors are non-differential, which will result in a risk estimate that tends towards unity.17 For asbestos exposure, in the ARDCO cohort, industrial hygienists carried out an expertise providing a quantitative assessment of asbestos exposure. This quantitative assessment enabled us to adjust the level of asbestos exposure in our analyses.

The ARDCO cohort is one of the largest cohorts of retirees formerly occupationally exposed to asbestos. Given the method used to construct the ARDCO cohort, we cannot rule out a selection bias. In the ARDCO cohort, people exposed to asbestos were invited to take part in this postoccupational surveillance study on a voluntary basis. The volunteers who agreed to participate are probably more concerned about their health and in better health than the asbestos-exposed population, as has been discussed in several ARDCO studies.18 19 Furthermore, in this study, all subjects were exposed to asbestos. We note as well that the estimated level of asbestos exposure in the current study was much higher than in previous case–control studies that reflect asbestos exposure in the general or hospital populations, making it difficult to compare the current study with prior studies with respect to the dose–response relationship between asbestos and mesothelioma or lung cancer. Further studies are needed to better characterise the effect of coexposure to asbestos and silica on mesothelioma risk.

Conclusion

In our study, in a population of workers exposed to asbestos, we identified an increased risk of mesothelioma associated with silica exposure in incidence analysis and an interaction between exposure to silica and asbestos CEIs on mesothelioma mortality. Silica is not known to induce mesothelioma but is biopersistent in the respiratory tract and may impair the clearance of other particles. Therefore, coexposure to asbestos could enhance the toxicity of asbestos on the mesothelium. Further studies are needed to better understand the role of coexposure to silica and asbestos on the occurrence of mesothelioma.
