Ergodicity for the Dean–Kawasaki Equation with Dirichlet Boundary Conditions: Taming the Square-Root
Shyam Popat, Zhengyan Wu

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the ergodicity of generalized Dean–Kawasaki equations with correlated noise and Dirichlet boundary conditions. In contrast to the ergodicity results of Fehrman, Gess, and Gvalani [ 19 ] , our analysis accommodates irregular, square-root type noise coefficients. For such irregular coefficients, we prove that the law of the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation converges exponentially fast to equilibrium, while for the porous medium type Dean–Kawasaki equation, the convergence occurs at a polynomial rate. Furthermore, we obtain a regularization by noise effect, showing that the polynomial convergence rate improves to an exponential one whenever the noise coefficient is sufficiently regular, including the case of conservative multiplicative linear noise. Our approach relies on establishing a supercontraction property in a suitably weighted Lebesgue space, achieved through a refined doubling of variables argument. The construction of the weight function crucially exploits the specific structure of the Dean–Kawasaki-type correlated noise.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the long-time behaviour of the generalised Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\Phi(\rho)-\nabla\cdot(\sigma(\rho)\circ\dot{\xi}^{F}+\nu(\rho)),&(x,t)\in U\times(0,T],\\
\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}(x),&(x,t)\in U\times\{t=0\},\\
\Phi(\rho(x,t))=\rho_{b}(x),&(x,t)\in\partial U\times(0,T].\end{cases}

Here
U
denotes a
C^{2}
-regular bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T>0
denotes a terminal time.
The Stratonovich noise
\circ\dot{\xi}^{F}
is white in time and correlated in space.
The assumptions on the non-linear functions
\Phi,\sigma
and
\nu
allow us to consider a wide range of non-linear SPDE, including the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise

(1.2)

\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho-\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho}\circ\dot{\xi}^{F}+\nu(\rho)).

The irregularity of solutions to (
1.1
) means that we can only express the boundary condition
\rho_{b}
indirectly via
\Phi(\rho)
.
This is done using the trace theorem, see Chapter 5.5 of Evans
[
17
]
.
Our results apply to the class of boundary data
\rho_{b}
for which well-posedness of equation (
1.1
) holds, see the paper of the first author
[
42
]
.
This includes all non-negative constant functions including zero and all smooth functions bounded away from zero.

The Dean–Kawasaki equation was first introduced by Dean
[
9
]
and Kawasaki
[
32
]
as a continuum stochastic PDE formally satisfied by the empirical density of an interacting diffusion system. The characteristic conservative square-root type noise is designed to statistically capture the fluctuations of the underlying particle system. From the perspective of fluctuating hydrodynamics, the Dean–Kawasaki equation can also be interpreted as possessing a gradient-flow type structure, with the stochastic term governed by the fluctuation-dissipation relation. In the case of a bounded domain, it is natural to impose Dirichlet boundary conditions on the density. Physically, this corresponds to coupling the particle system to reservoirs, sources, or sinks at the boundary, thereby enforcing a prescribed density (or chemical potential) at the walls. At the microscopic level, such boundary conditions arise, for instance, when particles are injected or removed at specified rates, or when the boundary acts as a particle bath maintained at fixed concentration.

To the best of authors’ knowledge, the ergodicity of Dean–Kawasaki type equations has previously been investigated by Fehrman, Gess and Gvalani
[
19
]
, where ergodicity was established for the Dean–Kawasaki equation on the torus, under Itô-type noise and with a regularized diffusion coefficient. The authors of
[
19
]
employed techniques from the theory of random dynamical systems, combined with gradient estimates, to demonstrate that the solution to the regularized Dean–Kawasaki equation generates a random dynamical system and admits a unique invariant measure. However, their approach encounters limitations when dealing with the irregular square-root noise coefficient. In contrast, the present work adopts a fundamentally different approach to prove ergodicity, specifically designed to handle irregular diffusion coefficients, including the particularly challenging square-root case. It is important to note, however, that the validity of this method is currently restricted to equations with Dirichlet boundary conditions. This distinction highlights both the novelty and the limitations of the approach in comparison to existing results.

1.1.
Main results

The main results of this paper consist of two parts: a qualitative ergodicity result for (
1.1
), and quantitative estimates on the convergence rate of the laws of (
1.1
) towards equilibrium. For the reader’s convenience, the precise assumptions on the coefficients
\Phi
,
\nu
, and
\sigma
will be specified later in Section
2.3
. However, we emphasize that our framework for
\Phi
and
\sigma
includes the typical case
\Phi(\xi)=\xi^{m}
and
\sigma(\xi)=\xi^{\frac{m}{2}}
for
\xi\geqslant 0
and any
m\geqslant 1
. This particular choice allows (
1.1
) to be interpreted as the fluctuating hydrodynamics of the zero-range process with polynomial jump rates. In the special case
m=1
, the equation (
1.1
) reduces to the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation introduced by Dean
[
9
]
, which describes the fluctuating mean-field limit of Brownian particles.

We will analyse the ergodicity of the system in a weighted Lebesgue space. Specifically, let
w:U\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-negative integrable function. For
p\in\mathbb{N}
we define
L^{p}_{w;x}
to be the space of
p
’th power integrable functions on
U
with respect to the measure
w(x)\,dx
. The precise construction of the weight function will be presented later. Under this weighted framework, the ergodicity result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy either Assumption
2.6
or
2.7
, and that
(\Phi,\nu)
further satisfy Assumptions
2.9
and
2.10
. Then there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(L^{1}_{x})
, where
\mathcal{P}(L^{1}_{x})
denotes the space of probability measures on
L^{1}_{x}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma,\nu)
satisfy Assumptions
2.6
,
2.9
, and
2.10
. These assumptions, in particular, cover the case
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi,\sqrt{\xi})
. Let
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the semigroup generated by (
1.1
), and let
\mu
be its invariant measure. Then there exist a constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
independent of
t
such that, for every
t\geqslant 0
,

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}}\sup_{\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1}\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\tilde{\rho})\,\mu(d\tilde{\rho})\right|\leqslant c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t},

where
\mathrm{Lip}(L^{1}_{w;x})
denotes the space of Lipschitz continuous functions from
L^{1}_{w;x}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

On the other hand, suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma,\nu)
satisfy Assumptions
2.7
,
2.9
, and
2.10
. These assumptions, in particular, cover the case
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi^{m},\xi^{\frac{m}{2}})
for any
m>1
. Then there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
independent of
t
such that, for every
t\geqslant 0
,

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}}\sup_{\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1}\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\tilde{\rho})\,\mu(d\tilde{\rho})\right|\leqslant c\,t^{-\frac{1}{q_{0}}},

where
q_{0}
is the exponent associated with Assumption
2.10
.

The results on the convergence rate exhibit a clear dichotomy. For the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation, corresponding to
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi,\sqrt{\xi})
, the laws of (
1.1
) converge exponentially fast to equilibrium. In contrast, for the more general porous medium case
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi^{m},\xi^{\frac{m}{2}})
with
m>1
, the convergence occurs at a polynomial rate.

The distinction between exponential and polynomial convergence to equilibrium stems from the qualitative difference in the dissipative structures of the operators
\Delta\rho
and
\Delta\rho^{m}=m\nabla\cdot(\rho^{m-1}\nabla\rho)
,
m>1
. Intuitively, the Laplacian induces a uniform smoothing effect across the domain, which gives rise to a spectral gap in the associated generator (see, for instance, Chen et al.
[
4
]
) and, consequently, may lead to exponential ergodicity. In contrast, in the porous medium case, the effective diffusion coefficient
D(\rho)=m\rho^{m-1}
degenerates in low-density regions, resulting in slower particle diffusivity and a weaker dissipation mechanism. From an analytical perspective, this degeneracy destroys the spectral gap, and the long-time dynamics are instead captured by alternative techniques, such as those developed in Dareiotis, Gess and Tsatsoulis
[
7
]
. In the present work, rather than relying on spectral gap arguments, we analyze the trajectories directly. A key technical tool for distinguishing the corresponding convergence rates is provided later in Lemma
4.1
.

With the aid of the weight function, the Stratonovich-to-Itô correction terms arising from the noise structure tend to contribute additional dissipation. However, as will be seen in the proofs of the above results, this contribution will be neglected due to the potential irregularity of the noise coefficient, which renders integrals involving
\log\rho
-type functions ill-defined. Consequently, utilizing only the dissipation from the porous medium term
\Delta\Phi(\rho)
yields convergence merely at a polynomial rate, consistent with the results in Dareiotis, Gess and Tsatsoulis
[
7
]
. To analyse the contribution of the conservative noise, we establish the following corollary. We show that when the noise coefficient is sufficiently regular, including the linear case
\sigma(\xi)=\xi
, the polynomial convergence rate of the porous medium type Dean–Kawasaki equation can be improved to an exponential rate. We interpret this enhancement as a manifestation of a regularization-by-noise phenomenon.

Corollary 1.3

(Regularization by noise)
.

Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy Assumptions
2.7
,
2.9
, and
2.10
. Let

\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi)=\int^{\xi}_{0}[\sigma^{\prime}(\xi^{\prime})]^{2}d\xi^{\prime},\quad\xi\geqslant 0.

Suppose additionally that
\Psi_{\sigma}
is well-defined and satisfies the lower Lipschitz bound

(1.3)

\displaystyle c|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|\leqslant|\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi_{1})-\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi_{2})|,

for some
c>0
, uniformly for every
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0
. Let
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the semigroup generated by (
1.1
), and let
\mu
be its invariant measure. Then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
independent of
t
such that, for every
t\geqslant 0
,

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}}\sup_{\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1}\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\tilde{\rho})\,\mu(d\tilde{\rho})\right|\leqslant c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t}.

The additional assumption on the coefficient
\sigma
ensures that the dissipative contribution arising from the Stratonovich-to-Itô correction term is well-justified. A representative example is
\sigma(\xi)=\xi
, which corresponds to the fluctuating zero-range process characterized by
\Phi(\xi)=\xi^{m}
and
\sigma(\xi)=\xi^{m/2}
with
m=2
. Moreover, the coefficient
\sigma
can also be chosen in a more general or artificial manner, for instance,
\sigma(\xi)=\xi+\frac{1}{2}\sin\xi
or
\sigma(\xi)=\xi+e^{-\xi}
.

1.2.
Key ideas and technical comments

The ergodicity argument is inspired by the approaches of Dareiotis, Gess and Tsatsoulis
[
7
]
and Dong, Zhang and Zhang
[
16
]
and further developed by combining it with the doubling variable method in the kinetic formulation framework, as employed by Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
and the first author
[
42
]
. More precisely, we construct a weight function
w:U\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, tailored to the specific structure of the Dean–Kawasaki equation, and analyse ergodicity within the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{1}_{w;x}
.

The fundamental step in establishing ergodicity is to prove a super-contraction property in
L^{1}_{w;x}
.
Specifically, let
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{\omega}L^{2}_{x}
, and denote by
\rho(\cdot;\rho_{0}):[0,T]\to U
the renormalized kinetic solution of (
1.1
) with initial condition
\rho_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.4
below. Under the assumptions in Section
2.3
, we show that there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
, depending only on
d,U,q_{0}
, and the weight
w
(but notably independent of
t
), such that for every
t\in[0,T]
,

(1.4)

\sup_{\rho_{1,0},\rho_{2,0}\in L^{2}_{\omega}L^{2}_{x}}\mathbb{E}\big\|\rho(t;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(t;\rho_{2,0})\big\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant c\,\mathrm{Decay}(t),

where
\mathrm{Decay}(t)
denotes the temporal decay rate, which will be specified later. To achieve this, the weight function
w
is constructed to satisfy, for every
x\in U
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta w<0\\
F_{1}\Delta w+c|F_{2}\cdot\nabla w|<0,\\
\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)<0\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,d,\end{cases}

where
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
depend on the specific structure of the noise
\xi^{F}
which will be specified in Section
2.2
and for constant
c\in(0,\infty)
that will be specified.

The super-contraction property.

The super-contraction property is established through the kinetic formulation, which characterizes the evolution of the renormalized kinetic function
\chi=\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<\xi<\rho(t;\rho_{0})\}}
. The proof employs the doubling of variables method utilized in Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
, combined with a weight function
w
. As a representative example, we take

\Phi(\rho)=\rho,\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(\rho)=\sqrt{\rho}.

Let
\rho_{1}=\rho(\cdot;\rho_{1,0})
and
\rho_{2}=\rho(\cdot;\rho_{2,0})
denote two stochastic kinetic solutions of (
1.1
) with initial conditions
\rho_{1,0}
and
\rho_{2,0}
respectively, and
\chi_{1}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<\xi<\rho_{1}\}}
,
\chi_{2}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<\xi<\rho_{2}\}}
denote the corresponding kinetic functions. Using the elementary property of indicator functions, one observes that

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}|\omega(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}|^{2}\,d\xi\right)\omega(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}-2\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\,d\xi\right)\omega(x)\,dx.

Taking the kinetic formulation into account and applying Itô’s product rule, one can expand the expression
\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}-2\chi_{1}\chi_{2}
analogously to Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
. However, in contrast to Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
and the first author
[
42
]
, the presence of the weight function
w
leads to terms whose sign can be quantified.
In the case of the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi,\sqrt{\xi})
the contributions read

\displaystyle I_{t}^{wgt}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left(\nabla\rho_{1}-\nabla\rho_{2}+\frac{1}{8}F_{1}\rho_{1}^{-1}\nabla\rho_{1}-\frac{1}{8}F_{1}\rho_{2}^{-1}\nabla\rho_{2}\right)\cdot\nabla w(x)\,\mbox{\rm sgn}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})\,dx\,ds,

and

\displaystyle I_{t}^{flux}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}w(x)\left(\nabla\cdot\nu(\rho_{1})-\nabla\cdot\nu(\rho_{2})\right)\mbox{\rm sgn}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})\,dx\,ds

(1.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)\,|\nu_{i}(\rho_{1})-\nu_{i}(\rho_{2})|\,dx\,ds.

Here
I_{t}^{wgt}
arises from the diffusion and the Stratonovich-to-Itô correction, while
I_{t}^{flux}
originates from the flux term
-\nabla\cdot(\nu(\rho))
.

Concerning the term
I_{t}^{wgt}
, the monotonicity of the identity function implies that the first two terms can be formally rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}(\nabla\rho_{1}-\nabla\rho_{2})\cdot\nabla w(x)\,\mbox{\rm sgn}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\nabla|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}|\cdot\nabla w(x)\,dx\,ds

(1.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}|\,\Delta w(x)\,dx\,ds.

To ensure that this contribution is negative, we require

(1.8)

(\Delta w)(x)<0,\quad\forall x\in U.

A similar argument applies to the logarithmic term, since
\xi\mapsto\frac{1}{8}F_{1}\log\xi
is increasing. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}F_{1}(\nabla\log\rho_{1}-\nabla\log\rho_{2})\cdot\nabla w(x)\,

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm sgn}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}F_{1}\nabla|\log\rho_{1}-\log\rho_{2}|\cdot\nabla w(x)\,dx\,ds

(1.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}|\log\rho_{1}-\log\rho_{2}|\,\nabla\cdot(F_{1}\nabla w(x))\,dx\,ds.

This yields the additional condition

(1.10)

\nabla\cdot(F_{1}\nabla w(x))<0.

We remark that, due to the potential lack of integrability of
\log\rho_{1}
and
\log\rho_{2}
, the negative contribution in (
1.2
) will be dropped in the rigorous proof when the truncation near the zero and infinity values of the solution are still present, ensuring that the integral is well-defined.
Furthermore, to obtain a negative contribution from the flux term
I_{t}^{flux}
, we impose

(1.11)

\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)<0,\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,d.

Combining (
1.8
), (
1.10
), and (
1.11
), we obtain the set of conditions (
1.5
) for the admissible weight function
w
.

In summary, compared with Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
, the presence of the weight function yields additional negative contributions (
1.2
) and (
1.2
) in the weighted
L^{1}_{x}
-contraction property. We will utilize these results to derive bounds on the difference between two solutions corresponding to distinct initial data, with the bounds exhibiting decay in time.

Construction of the weight function.

Let
e=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(1,\ldots,1)
be a
d
-dimensional unit vector. We construct the weight function as

(1.12)

\displaystyle w(x)=-\exp(\alpha\,x\cdot e)+C,

where
\alpha>0
is chosen sufficiently large depending on the noise structure
F_{1}
, and
C>0
is a constant ensuring the positivity of
w
. We refer the reader to Proposition
3.1
for further details and for the verification of conditions (
1.5
).

Since
U
is bounded,
\exp(\alpha\,x\cdot e)
admits both a positive lower bound and a finite upper bound. Hence, by choosing
C>0
appropriately, we can guarantee that

0<c_{\mathrm{low}}\leqslant w(x)\leqslant c_{\mathrm{up}}<\infty,\quad\text{for every }x\in U.

Consequently,

(1.13)

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{low}}\|f\|_{L^{1}_{x}}\leqslant\|f\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant c_{\mathrm{up}}\|f\|_{L^{1}_{x}},\quad\text{for all }f\in L^{1}_{x},

which shows the equivalence between the standard
L^{1}_{x}
-norm and the weighted
L^{1}_{w;x}
-norm.

Furthermore, we emphasize that there does not exist an admissible weight function on either the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
or
the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We take the case of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
for
illustration.
Since a function on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
cannot be strictly decreasing,
condition (
1.11
) cannot be satisfied. Moreover, integrating
over space yields

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta w(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\cdot(F_{1}\nabla w(x))\,dx=0.

This contradicts (
1.8
) and (
1.10
).
A similar argument applies to the case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This observation indicates that the present approach is only valid
for bounded domains.

Ergodicity.

Based on the choice of the weight function, in the classical Dean–Kawasaki case we obtain the existence of constants
c,q_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{low}}\mathbb{E}\|\rho(t;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(t;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant c_{\mathrm{up}}\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{1,0}-\rho_{2,0}\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}-

\displaystyle c\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\,dr

(1.14)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle c\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|^{q_{0}+1}_{L^{q_{0}+1}_{w;x}}\,dr.

We note that the negative contribution

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\,dr

arises from the specific choice
\Phi(\rho)=\rho
, and it dominates the right-hand side of (
1.2
) whenever
|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})|<1
. By neglecting the last term

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|^{q_{0}+1}_{L^{q_{0}+1}_{w;x}}\,dr

and applying Gronwall’s lemma, we deduce

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\rho(t;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(t;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t}.

In the porous medium Dean–Kawasaki case
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi^{m},\xi^{\frac{m}{2}})
for
m>1
, the contraction property (
1.2
) is replaced by

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{low}}\mathbb{E}\|\rho(t;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(t;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant c_{\mathrm{up}}\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{1,0}-\rho_{2,0}\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}-

\displaystyle c\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|^{m}_{L^{m}_{w;x}}\,dr

(1.16)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle c\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\rho(r;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(r;\rho_{2,0})\|^{q_{0}+1}_{L^{q_{0}+1}_{w;x}}\,dr,

from which one obtains a polynomial decay rate in time instead of the exponential decay in (
1.15
),

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\rho(t;\rho_{1,0})-\rho(t;\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant ct^{-1/q_{0}}.

This follows by applying a technical result stated in Lemma
4.1
.

Considering the cases (
1.15
) and (
1.17
), we conclude the decay rate
\mathrm{Decay}(t)
as stated in the super-contraction property (
1.4
).
Using (
1.4
), we establish ergodicity by proving the existence and uniqueness of invariant measures.
Furthermore, we conclude that the convergence of the laws of the classical Dean–Kawasaki equation

(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi,\sqrt{\xi})
is exponentially fast,
whereas in more general porous medium cases, the convergence occurs at a polynomial rate.

To improve the convergence rate toward equilibrium, we aim to exploit the contribution of the noise structure. Specifically, when the coefficient
\sigma
is sufficiently regular, the term corresponding to
\log\rho_{1}-\log\rho_{2}
in (
1.2
) is actually the well-defined expression

\displaystyle\bigl|\Psi_{\sigma}(\rho_{1})-\Psi_{\sigma}(\rho_{2})\bigr|=\left|\int_{\rho_{2}}^{\rho_{1}}[\sigma^{\prime}(\xi^{\prime})]^{2}\,d\xi^{\prime}\right|.

Under the growth assumption (
1.3
) on
\Psi_{\sigma}
,
the corresponding term in (
1.2
) is well-defined and provides an additional dissipative effect. By exploiting this extra negative contribution and applying Gronwall’s inequality, we obtain exponential convergence toward equilibrium. This leads to an enhanced convergence rate for the porous medium type Dean–Kawasaki equation, yielding the regularization-by-noise result stated in Corollary
1.3
.

1.3.
Comments on the literature

Fluctuating hydrodynamics.

The study of Dean–Kawasaki-type equations is closely connected to the theories of fluctuating hydrodynamics (FHD) and macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT). MFT provides a unified framework for describing systems driven far from equilibrium, extending and refining the classical linear response theories valid only near equilibrium, see Bertini et al.
[
2
]
, Derrida
[
13
]
.
At its core lies an ansatz formulated in terms of large deviation principles for interacting particle systems. Parallel to this perspective, the FHD approach aims to model microscopic fluctuations in accordance with the principles of statistical mechanics and non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Within this framework, one postulates conservative SPDEs that capture the essential fluctuation features of non-equilibrium systems, see Landau and Lifshitz
[
36
]
and Spohn
[
45
]
. The central ansatz of MFT can then be viewed as the zero-noise large deviation limit of such conservative SPDEs. A typical example within FHD is the Dean–Kawasaki equation, which effectively characterizes the fluctuation behaviour of mean-field interacting particle systems.

Kinetic formulation.

The kinetic formulation was first developed in the partial differential equation (PDE) literature and builds off the idea of entropy solutions.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge the formulation was introduced in the mid-nineties by Lions, Perthame and Tadmor
[
37
]
in the context of scalar conservation laws.
Relevant extensions to degenerate parabolic-hyperbolic PDEs were given by Chen and Pertame
[
3
]
, Bendahmane and
Karlsen
[
1
]
, and Karlsen and Riseboro
[
31
]
.

The first work extending the kinetic solution framework to the stochastic setting was in 2010 by Debussche and Vovelle
[
11
]
, who extended the deterministic results of Lions, Perthame and Tadmor
[
37
]
to the case of scalar conservation laws with stochastic forcing.
The results were subsequently extended by Hofmanová
[
30
]
to the degenerate parabolic case.

The Dean–Kawasaki equations and conservative SPDEs.

The Dean–Kawasaki equation was first introduced by Dean
[
9
]
and Kawasaki
[
32
]
. However, the model remained at a formal level until Konarovskyi, Lehmann and von Renesse
[
33
]
established a rigorous martingale framework, thereby providing a mathematically justified formulation. Subsequently, Konarovskyi, Lehmann and von Renesse
[
34
]
extended the well-posedness result to the case of nonlocal interactions by employing a Girsanov transform.
Further studies such as Konarovskyi and Müller
[
35
]
, Dello Schiavo and Konarovskyi
[
12
]
and Dello Schiavo
[
44
]
investigated additional properties of the Dean–Kawasaki equation driven by space-time white noise. More recently, Müller, von Renesse and Zimmer
[
38
]
extended the martingale approach to the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck-type Dean–Kawasaki equation.

Motivated by the theory of fluctuating hydrodynamics, the Dean–Kawasaki equation driven by spatially correlated noise has been extensively studied. The inclusion of correlated noise reflects more realistic mesoscopic features and reveals additional analytical structures of the model. To the authors’ understanding, these works rely heavily on techniques developed for stochastic conservation laws. Debussche and Vovelle
[
11
]
studied the Cauchy problem for stochastic conservation laws through the concept of kinetic solutions. This framework was later extended to parabolic-hyperbolic stochastic PDEs with conservative noise by Gess and Souganidis
[
26
]
, Fehrman and Gess
[
20
]
, and Dareiotis and Gess
[
8
]
. These methodologies have since been adapted to the analysis of Dean–Kawasaki-type equations. In the case of local interactions, Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
established the well-posedness of function-valued solutions to the Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise. Building on this framework, they further investigated small-noise large deviations in Fehrman and Gess
[
21
]
, aiming to rigorously connect FHD with MFT.

More recently, Clini and Fehrman
[
5
]
proved a central limit theorem for the nonlinear Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise, while Gess, the second author, and Zhang
[
28
]
derived higher-order fluctuation expansions for Dean–Kawasaki-type systems.
For models with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the first author established well-posedness, large deviation, and fluctuation results in
[
42
,
41
]
. For models with nonlocal interactions, Wang, the second author, and Zhang
[
47
]
, as well as the second author and Zhang
[
48
]
, analysed the well-posedness and large deviations for Dean–Kawasaki equations with singular interactions, with applications to the fluctuating Ising-Kac-Kawasaki model. Later on, the second author
[
49
]
investigated the nonlinear fluctuations of the fluctuating Ising–Kac–Kawasaki model. For Keller–Segel-type interactions, Martini and Mayorcas
[
40
,
39
]
established well-posedness and large deviation principles for an additive-noise approximation of the Keller-Segel-type Dean–Kawasaki equation. Additionally, Hao, the second author, and Zimmer
[
29
]
established a strong well-posedness result for the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck-type Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise.

Other well-posedness results for the Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise include the recent works of Fehrman and Gess
[
22
]
who consider the Dean–Kawasaki equation on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
, and the work of Fehrman
[
24
]
who considers the Dean–Kawasaki equation on bounded domains with homogeneous Neumann boundary data.
Finally, we mention that under additional mollification of the square root, Djurdjevac, Kremp and Perkowski
[
15
]
and Djurdjevac, Ji and Perkowski
[
14
]
show the well-posedness of strong solutions.
They also make a connection with MFT by establishing a quantitative bound on the weak error between the approximating SPDE and the empirical measure of the corresponding particle system.

Ergodicity for stochastic conservation laws.

We also mention related literature concerning ergodicity and long-time behavior of stochastic conservation laws and conservative SPDEs. Debussche and Vovelle
[
10
]
established ergodicity for stochastic conservation laws on the torus driven by additive noise. Subsequently Gess and Souganidis
[
26
]
applied an analogue of the averaging lemma, combined with the specific Gaussian structure of the noise, to investigate the regularity and long-time behaviour of conservation law equations with stochastic fluxes. This framework was later extended by Gess and Souganidis
[
27
]
to conservative SPDEs of porous medium type. Concerning ergodicity for equations on bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions, Dareiotis, Gess and Tsatsoulis
[
7
]
established ergodicity for the stochastic porous medium equation.
Building upon this, Dong, Zhang and Zhang
[
16
]
employed the methodology developed by Dareiotis, Gess and Tsatsoulis
[
7
]
to prove ergodicity for stochastic conservation laws driven by multiplicative noise.

Structure of the paper

This paper is organized as follows. In Section

, we introduce the basic notations and provide the precise definitions of the solution concepts.
Section
3
is devoted to establishing the super-contraction property.
Finally, the ergodicity results are analysed in Section

.

Preliminaries

We begin by introducing some of the notation that will be employed in the paper.

Notation

Let
T>0
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},(\{\beta_{k}(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})
be a stochastic basis. Without loss of generality, we assume that the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is complete and that
\{\beta_{k}(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, are independent
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-Wiener processes taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Expectation with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}
.

Recall that
U
is a
C^{2}
-regular, bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
p\in[1,\infty]
, we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}_{x}}
the norm in the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(U)
.
Let
C^{\infty}(U\times(0,\infty))
be the space of infinitely differentiable functions on
U\times(0,\infty)
, and
C_{c}^{\infty}(U\times(0,\infty))
its subspace consisting of compactly supported functions.
For a non-negative integer
a
, we write
H^{a}(U):=W^{a,2}(U)
where
W^{a,2}(U)
denotes the usual Sobolev space on the domain
U
with integrability
2
.
The space
H^{\alpha}_{0}(U)
is defined to be the closure of compactly supported smooth functions
C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
in the space
H^{a}(U)
.
For every
p,q,r\in[1,\infty)
, we denote the space
L^{p}(\Omega;L^{q}([0,T];L^{r}(U)))
using the shorthand
L^{p}_{\omega}L^{q}_{t}L^{r}_{x}
, where the subscripts denote the variables being integrated.
To emphasise the initial condition, we will sometimes denote solutions of equation (
1.1
) at time
t\geqslant 0
by

\rho(t;\rho_{0}):U\to\mathbb{R}.

Definition 2.1
.

(Weighted Lebesgue space) For a non-negative integrable weight
w:U\to\mathbb{R}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the weighted
L^{p}(U)
-space, denoted by
L^{p}_{w;x}
, consists of all measurable functions

satisfying

\|f\|_{L^{p}_{w;x}}:=\int_{U}|f(x)|^{p}w(x)\,dx<\infty.

We also define the function spaces

B_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x}):=\{f:L^{1}_{w;x}\to\mathbb{R}:f\,\text{is bounded and measurable}\},

C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x}):=\{f:L^{1}_{w;x}\to\mathbb{R}:f\,\text{is continuous, bounded and measurable}\}.

We use the notation
\lesssim
to mean “less than or equal to, up to a constant”.

2.2.
Setup and kinetic formulation

We begin by defining the noise in (
1.1
).

Definition 2.2

(The noise
\xi^{F}
)
.

For a sequence of continuously differentiable functions
F:=\{f_{k}:U\to\mathbb{R}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the independent Brownian motions
\{\beta_{k}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
introduced in Section
2.1
, the noise
\xi^{F}:U\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

\xi^{F}(x,t):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)\beta_{k}(t).

By converting the equation (
1.1
) into an equation with Itô noise, we see that it is convenient to define the three quantities related to the spatial coefficients of the noise
F
,

\displaystyle F_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{defined by}\quad F_{1}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}^{2}(x),

(2.1)

\displaystyle F_{2}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\text{defined by}\quad F_{2}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)\nabla f_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla F_{1},

\displaystyle F_{3}:U\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{defined by}\quad F_{3}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\nabla f_{k}(x)|^{2}.

We assume that the following assumption on the noise holds for every statement in the paper and will not repeatedly reference it.

Assumption 2.3
.

Assume that the spatial components of the noise
\{f_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are such that
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1,2,3}
are continuous on
U
.
We further assume that the noise is non-degenerate in
U
in the sense that

(2.2)

\inf_{x\in U}F_{1}(x)>0.

We consider the non-degeneracy condition (
2.2
) on the noise to be a very mild condition, and is satisfied if for example we assume that the first noise coefficient is non-degenerate
f_{1}=1
.

Using the Stratonovich-to-Itô conversion, the generalised Dean–Kawasaki equation (
1.1
) with Itô noise is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\Phi(\rho)-\nabla\cdot(\sigma(\rho)\dot{\xi}^{F}+\nu(\rho))+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(F_{1}[\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)]^{2}\nabla\rho+\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)\sigma(\rho)F_{2}).

As is well known by now, see for example Chapter 3 of
[
23
]
or Section 5.4 of the work of the first author
[
41
]
, the kinetic function

(2.3)

\chi(x,\xi,t)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<\xi<\rho(x,t)\}}

solves distributionally the equation

(2.4)

\partial_{t}\chi=\nabla\cdot(\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\Phi^{\prime}(\xi)\nabla\rho)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\left(F_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}(\xi))^{2}\nabla\rho+\sigma(\xi)\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)F_{2}\right))+\partial_{\xi}q\\
-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\xi}(\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\left(\sigma(\xi)\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)\nabla\rho\cdot F_{2}+\sigma^{2}(\xi)F_{3}\right))-\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\nabla\cdot(\sigma(\rho)\dot{\xi}^{F})-\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\nabla\cdot\nu(\rho),

where
q
denotes the kinetic measure, a non-negative, locally finite measure on
U\times(0,\infty)\times[0,T]
satisfying that

q\geqslant\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\Phi^{\prime}(\xi)|\nabla\rho|^{2},

and for every
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U\times(0,\infty))
,

\displaystyle(\omega,t)\in(\Omega,[0,T])\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\psi(x,\xi)\,q(dx,d\xi,dt)(\omega)=:\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\psi(x,\xi)\,dq(x,\xi,t)(\omega)

is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
-predictable.

Equation (
2.4
) forms the basis of a kinetic solution, where the distributional equality is made rigorous by integrating against a test function.
To capture the boundary data of the solution we introduce the harmonic extension of
\rho_{b}
, where the boundary data
\rho_{b}
is assumed to satisfy Assumption
2.11
below.
That is, define
h_{\rho_{b}}
to be the weak solution of

(2.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta h_{\rho_{b}}=0&\text{on }U,\\
h_{\rho_{b}}=\rho_{b}&\text{on }\partial U.\end{cases}

Well-posedness of
H^{1}(U)
-valued weak solutions of
h_{\rho_{\beta}}
follows from the fact that the boundary
\partial U
of our domain is assumed to be
C^{2}
-regular and the fact that
\rho_{b}
is smooth.

Definition 2.4

(Kinetic solution of the Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise (
1.1
))
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{\omega}L^{2}_{x}
be a non-negative
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable initial condition.
A stochastic kinetic solution of the generalised Dean–Kawasaki equation (
1.1
) is a non-negative, almost surely continuous
L^{1}_{x}
-valued
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
-predictable process
\rho\in L^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T];L^{1}_{x})
that satisfies

(1)

Integrability of flux: We have

\sigma(\rho)\in L^{2}(\Omega;L^{2}(U\times[0,T]))\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0pt\nu(\rho)\in L^{1}(\Omega;L^{1}(U\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})).

(2)

Boundary condition, local regularity of solution: For
h_{\rho_{b}}
the weak solution of equation (
2.5
), for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(2.6)

\left((\Phi(\rho)\wedge k)\vee 1/k\right)-\left((h_{\rho_{b}}\wedge k)\vee 1/k\right)\in L^{2}(\Omega;L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(U))).

Furthermore, there exists a kinetic measure
q
that satisfies:

(3)

Regularity: Almost surely, in the sense of non-negative measures,

(2.7)

\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho)\Phi^{\prime}(\xi)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\leqslant q\hskip 5.0pt\text{on}\hskip 5.0ptU\times(0,\infty)\times[0,T].

(4)

Vanishing at infinity: We have that

(2.8)

\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[q(U\times[M,M+1]\times[0,T])\right]=0.

(5)

The kinetic equation:
For every
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U\times(0,\infty))
and every
t\in(0,T]
, almost surely,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{U}\chi(x,\xi,t)\psi(x,\xi)\,dx\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{U}\chi(x,\xi,t=0)\psi(x,\xi)\,dx\,d\xi

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left(\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)\nabla\rho+\frac{1}{2}F_{1}[\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)]^{2}\nabla\rho+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)\sigma(\rho)F_{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\psi(x,\xi)|_{\xi=\rho}\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\partial_{\xi}\psi(x,\xi)\,dq+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left(\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho\cdot F_{2}+\sigma(\rho)^{2}F_{3}\right)\partial_{\xi}\psi(x,\rho)\,dx\,ds

(2.9)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\psi(x,\rho)\nabla\cdot(\sigma(\rho)\,d{\xi}^{F})\,dx-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\psi(x,\rho)\nabla\cdot\nu(\rho)\,dx\,ds.

The following well-posedness result was obtained by the first author in
[
42
]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Well-posedness of the generalised Dean–Kawasaki equation with correlated noise (
1.1
))
.

Suppose that the noise coefficients
F
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, the non-linear coefficients
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy either Assumption
2.6
or
2.7
as given in Section
2.3
below,
\nu
satisfies Assumption
2.9
, and the initial and boundary data satisfy Assumption
2.11
.

Then there there exists a unique stochastic solution
\rho
of equation (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.4
.
In particular, the solution is almost surely continuous in time
\rho\in C([0,T];L^{1}_{x})
.

Finally, any two kinetic solutions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
of (
1.1
) with initial data
\rho_{1,0}
and
\rho_{2,0}
respectively satisfy
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely the contraction

(2.10)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\rho_{1}(\cdot,t)-\rho_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}_{x}}\leqslant\|\rho_{1,0}-\rho_{2,0}\|_{L^{1}_{x}}.

This illustrates that the solution map
\rho_{0}\mapsto\rho(\cdot;\rho_{0})

is continuous from
L^{1}_{x}
to
L^{1}(U\times[0,T])
.

2.3.
Assumptions

We begin by presenting the assumptions that are needed on the coefficients
(\Phi,\sigma,\nu)
of equation (
1.1
).
Several of the assumptions are needed for the well-posedness of the equation (Theorem
2.5
from above) and are not used in the ergodicity arguments of the present work, but are stated for completeness.

As we motivated in the introduction, we will consider separately the classical Dean–Kawasaki case and the porous medium case.
In the classical case, the following choice of coefficients allows (
1.1
) to be interpreted as the fluctuating mean-field limit of Brownian particles, coinciding with the classical Dean–Kawasaki model introduced by Dean
[
9
]
.

Assumption 2.6

(Classical Dean–Kawaski assumption)
.

Suppose that
\Phi(\xi)=\xi
, and
\sigma(\xi)=\sqrt{\xi}
, for every
\xi\geqslant 0
.

On the other hand, the porous medium case
(\Phi(\xi),\sigma(\xi))=(\xi^{m},\xi^{\frac{m}{2}})
with
m>1
allows (
1.1
) to be interpreted as the fluctuating hydrodynamics of the zero-range process with polynomial jump rates.

Assumption 2.7

(Porous medium Dean–Kawasaki assumption)
.

Suppose
\Phi,\sigma\in C([0,\infty))
satisfy the following assumptions:

(1)

We have
\Phi,\sigma\in C^{1,1}((0,\infty))
.

(2)

The function
\Phi
is strictly increasing and is zero at zero:
\Phi(0)=0
with
\Phi^{\prime}>0
on
(0,\infty)
.

(3)

Growth conditions on functions related to
\Phi
: There exists constants
c,m\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
\xi\in[0,\infty)

(2.11)

\Phi(\xi)\leqslant c(1+\xi^{m})\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{\prime}(\xi)\leqslant c(1+\Phi(\xi)).

Let
\Phi
satisfy points 1 and 2 above.
Define
\Theta_{\Phi,2}\in C([0,\infty))\cap C^{1}((0,\infty))
to be the unique function satisfying

\Theta_{\Phi,2}(0)=0,\quad\Theta_{\Phi,2}^{\prime}(\xi)=(\Phi^{\prime}(\xi))^{1/2}.

We assume that there exist constants

c\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in[1,\infty)
such that for every
\xi,\eta\in[0,\infty)

|\xi-\eta|^{q}\leqslant c|\Theta_{\Phi,2}(\xi)-\Theta_{\Phi,2}(\eta)|^{2}.

Furthermore, assume that there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
\xi\in[0,\infty)
and
m\in(0,\infty)
as in (
2.11
), we have

\Theta_{\Phi,2}(\xi)\geqslant c(\xi^{\frac{m+1}{2}}-1).

(4)

Growth conditions on functions related to
\sigma
:
There exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
\xi\in[0,\infty)
,

(2.12)

|\sigma(\xi)|+|\sigma(\xi)\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)|^{2}\leqslant c(1+\xi+\Phi(\xi)).

Furthermore, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
c_{\delta}\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
\xi\in(\delta,\infty)
,

(2.13)

\frac{(\sigma^{\prime}(\xi))^{4}}{\Phi^{\prime}(\xi)}\leqslant c_{\delta}(1+\xi+\Phi(\xi)).

(5)

At least linear decay of
\sigma^{2}
at zero: There exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

\limsup_{\xi\to 0^{+}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\xi)}{\xi}\leqslant c.

In particular this implies that
\sigma(0)=0
.

(6)

Regularity of oscillations of of
\sigma^{2}
at infinity: There is a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

(2.14)

\sup_{\xi^{\prime}\in[0,\xi]}\sigma^{2}(\xi^{\prime})\leqslant c(1+\xi+\sigma^{2}(\xi)),\quad\text{for every }\xi\in[0,\infty).

(7)

Regularity of
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}
: The function
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is non-decreasing.
Furthermore we have that either
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\in C([0,\infty)])
with
(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})(0)=0
, or
\nabla\cdot F_{2}=0
, where
F_{2}
is defined in (
2.1
).

(8)

Lipschitz type bound for
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}
: Define the unique function
\Psi_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.15)

\Psi_{\sigma}(0)=0,\quad\Psi_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\xi)=(\sigma^{\prime}(\xi))^{2}.

Assume that
\Psi_{\sigma}
is well defined and that there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\sigma(\xi_{1})\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{1})-\sigma(\xi_{2})\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{2})|\leqslant c|\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi_{1})-\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi_{2})|,\quad\text{for every }\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0.

Remark 2.8
.

We note that the first seven conditions in Assumption
2.7
are parallel to those in Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
and in the work of the first author
[
42
]
, contributing to the well-posedness of (
1.1
). However, condition (8) in Assumption
2.7
is new and is introduced to establish the super-contraction property.
As a representative example, we take
\sigma(\xi)=\xi^{\frac{m}{2}}
with
m>1
to obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi):=\int^{\xi}_{0}[\sigma^{\prime}(\zeta)]^{2}\,d\zeta=\frac{m^{2}}{4(m-1)}\xi^{m-1},

and thus

\displaystyle\sigma(\xi)\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{m}{2}\xi^{m-1}=\frac{2(m-1)}{m}\Psi_{\sigma}(\xi).

Therefore, the bound (
2.16
) holds for the full range of the porous medium equations.

We now provide the assumption on the non-linear flux term
\nu
.

Assumption 2.9
.

Suppose that the non-linear function
\nu\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{1}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

(1)

Non-decreasing: We assume that
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})
is non-decreasing in each component.

(2)

Growth condition on
\nu
: There exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
\xi\in[0,\infty)
,

(2.17)

|\nu(\xi)|+\leqslant c(1+\xi+\Phi(\xi)).

(3)

Regularity of oscillations at infinity: There is a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

(2.18)

\sup_{\xi^{\prime}\in[0,\xi]}|\nu(\xi^{\prime})|\leqslant c(1+\xi+|\nu(\xi)|),\quad\text{for every }\xi\in[0,\infty).

We refer to Remark 4.2 of Fehrman and Gess
[
23
]
for a comprehensive discussion on conditions (
2.18
) and (
2.14
).
The assumptions are satisfied in the case that the functions
\sigma^{2},\nu
are increasing, are uniformly continuous, or grow linearly at infinity.
The assumption is more general than any of the above three examples and essentially amounts to a restriction on the growth of the magnitude of oscillations, rather than frequency of oscillations at infinity.

Let

(2.19)

\mathcal{A}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=|\Phi(\xi_{1})-\Phi(\xi_{2})|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\nu_{i}(\xi_{1})-\nu_{i}(\xi_{2})|,\quad\text{for every }\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0.

Owing to the second point of Assumption
2.7
,
\mathcal{A}
is strictly positive unless
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}
.
We further remark that if
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are two stochastic kinetic solutions of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.4
, then the space-time integral

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\mathcal{A}(\rho_{1}(y,s),\rho_{2}(y,s))\,dy\,ds

is well-defined owing to the growth conditions (
2.11
) and (
2.17
) on
\Phi
and
\nu
respectively, combined with the estimates of Propositions 4.12 and 4.14 of the work of the first author
[
42
]
.
For the ergodicity results we require the following upper bound on the growth of
\mathcal{A}
.

Assumption 2.10
.

Suppose that there exists a constant
q_{0}\geqslant 0
such that for every
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0
, we have

(2.20)

\mathcal{A}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\geqslant c_{q_{0}}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|^{q_{0}+1}.

Clearly the assumption is satisfied if either
|\Phi(\xi_{1})-\Phi(\xi_{2})|\geqslant c_{q_{0}}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|^{q_{0}+1}
, for every
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0
, or there exists an
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
|\nu_{i}(\xi_{1})-\nu_{i}(\xi_{2})|\geqslant c_{q_{0}}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|^{q_{0}+1}
, for every
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geqslant 0
.

In the classical Dean–Kawasaki case it is clear that the assumption holds with
q_{0}=0
, and in the porous medium case
\Phi(\xi)=\xi^{m}
, for every

) can be explicitly written as

(3.17)

-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right|\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)\,dy\,ds-\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)e_{i}|\nu_{i}(\rho_{1})-\nu_{i}(\rho_{2})|\,dy\,ds\\
\leqslant-\left(\inf_{y\in U}\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)\right)\left(\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right|\,dy\,ds-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}|\nu_{i}(\rho_{1})-\nu_{i}(\rho_{2})|\,dy\,ds\right)\\
\leqslant-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\inf_{y\in U}\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)\right)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left(\left|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\nu_{i}(\rho_{1})-\nu_{i}(\rho_{2})|\right)\,dy\,ds.

In the final inequality we used that
d\geqslant 1
and the fact that
\alpha
can be chosen to satisfy
\alpha>1
.

In this way, if we let
\mathcal{A}
be defined as in (
2.19
), we obtain a contraction of the form

\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{1}(\cdot,t)-\rho_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{1,0}-\rho_{2,0}\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\\
-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\inf_{y\in U}\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)\right)\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\mathcal{A}(\rho_{1}(y,s),\rho_{2}(y,s))\,dy\,ds.

To simplify notation we denote the constant appearing on the right hand side as

(3.18)

c_{\alpha,U,d}:=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\inf_{y\in U}\exp(\alpha y\cdot e)\right),

which we may subsequently denote by
c
if we do not want to emphasise the dependence on
\alpha,U
and
d
.
This completes the formal proof.
∎

3.2.
Rigorous proof of super-contraction

In this section we extend the super-contraction results to the case of general non-linear coefficients
\Phi,\sigma
and
\nu
and general boundary data
\rho_{b}
satisfying Assumption
2.11
. Before proceeding it, we clarify the choice of the weight function in detail.
Recall from the informal proof above that we required the conditions (
3.12
), (
3.14
), (
3.15
) on the weight function
w
.
We now construct an admissible weight function.

Proposition 3.1

(Choice of weight function)
.

There exists a non-negative weight function
w:U\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying for every
x\in U
,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta w(x)<0\\
F_{2}\cdot\nabla w(x)+F_{1}\Delta w(x)=\nabla\cdot(F_{1}\nabla w(x))<0,\\
\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)<0,\text{ for every }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

Proof.

We construct such a weight.
Let
e=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(1,...,1)
, and denote
e_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
, for every
i=1,...,d
. Let the weight function defined by

\displaystyle w(x)=-\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)+C,

for some
\alpha>0
to be decided later on, and
C
a positive constant that guarantees the positivity of
w
.

A straightforward computation shows that for every
i=1,...,d
,

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)=-\alpha\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)e_{i}<0,\quad\nabla w(x)=-\alpha\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)e,\text{ and }\Delta w(x)=-\alpha^{2}\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)<0.

That is, the first and final condition of (
3.19
) hold true.

Next, recall that we assumed that
F_{1}>0
in (
2.2
).
This implies that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(F_{1}\nabla w)=\nabla\cdot(-F_{1}\alpha\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)e)=-\alpha\exp(\alpha x\cdot e)(F_{1}\alpha+2F_{2}\cdot e).

Let
F_{1,inf}=\inf_{x\in U}F_{1}(x)>0
and
F_{2,sup}=\sup_{x\in U}|F_{2}(x)|
, which both exist by the assumed continuity of
F_{i},i=1,2,3
.
We choose
\alpha>0
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle F_{1,inf}\alpha-2F_{2,sup}>0,

which gives the final condition on the weight function.
∎

In the following, we make a remark illustrating how to formalise the computation in Section
3.1
.

Remark 3.2

(Formalising the computation of Section
3.1
)
.

The proof in Section
3.1
was not rigorous in the following ways.

(1)

We used the test functions
w(x)
and
\chi_{i}w(x)
in the kinetic equation to compute the terms on the right hand side of (
3.1
).
We can not do this because these choice of test functions are not compactly supported in space or velocity variables.
Furthermore the kinetic function
\chi_{i}
is not a smooth function.

We will get around this by using smooth approximations of cutoff functions in space and velocity as part of the test function and appropriately convolving (smoothing) the kinetic function.

(2)

As we mentioned in the informal proof, the singular logarithm term appearing in (
3.13
) is singular and only well defined in the presence of cutoff functions away from zero and infinity.

(3)

The distributional equalities for the derivatives of the kinetic function
\partial_{\xi}\chi_{i}
and
\nabla\chi_{i}
given in (
3.7
) are not rigorous due to the Dirac delta functions.

To deal with this rigorously we smooth the kinetic function via convolution and then integrate by parts.
The Dirac delta functions in (
3.7
) corresponds to what is left after taking the limits of the convolution kernels.

(4)

At various points we integrated by parts and did not pick up additional terms since we assumed boundary data was constant
\rho_{b}=0
.

To prove the statement for more general boundary data we use the cutoff in space as part of our test functions.
When integrating by parts, we avoid boundary terms appearing, but consequently need to control additional terms that appear when the derivative hits the spatial cutoff.

We now introduce the cutoff functions and convolution kernels.
To define the spatial cutoff, we will first need to define interior regions of the domain
U
.

Definition 3.3

(Interior region of the domain
U
)
.

Let
d(x,\partial U)
denote the usual minimum Euclidean distance from a point to a set.
For
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{U})
we define the interior regions

U_{\gamma}:=\{x\in U:d(x,\partial U)\geqslant\gamma\}\subset U,\hskip 20.0pt\partial U_{\gamma}:=\{x\in U:d(x,\partial U)=\gamma\}.

Define the real valued positive constant
\gamma_{U}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
to be the largest distance away from the boundary such that every point in the interior of the domain at most
\gamma_{U}
away from the boundary has a unique closest point on the boundary.
That is,

(3.20)

\gamma_{U}:=max\{\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}:\forall x\in\partial U_{\tilde{\gamma}},\hskip 3.0ptargmin\,d(x,\partial U)\hskip 3.0pt\text{is a singleton}\},

where
argmin\,d(x,\partial U)
denotes the points
x
that are closest to the boundary
\partial U
.
For a non-trivial
C^{2}
-regular domain,
\gamma_{U}
is strictly positive, see page 153 of Foote
[
25
]
.

Definition 3.4

(Convolution kernels and cutoff functions)
.

(1)

Convolution kernel in space and velocity:
Let
\kappa_{d}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\kappa_{1}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be non-negative and integrate to one.
For every
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
we define
\kappa^{\epsilon}_{d}:U\to[0,\infty)
and
\kappa_{1}^{\delta}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
to be convolution kernels/ mollifiers of scale
\epsilon
and
\delta
on
U
and
\mathbb{R}
respectively, defined by

(3.21)

\kappa^{\epsilon}_{d}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d}}\kappa_{d}\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right),\hskip 5.0pt\kappa^{\delta}_{1}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\delta}\kappa_{1}\left(\frac{\xi}{\delta}\right).

We define the convolution on
U
as follows. For any integrable function
f
and every
x\in U_{2\epsilon}
, let

(f\ast\kappa^{\epsilon}_{d})(x)=\int_{U}f(y)\kappa^{\epsilon}_{d}(x-y)dy.

Finally, for convenience we define
\kappa^{\epsilon,\delta}
to be the product of the convolution kernels

\kappa^{\epsilon,\delta}(x,y,\xi,\eta):=\kappa^{\epsilon}_{d}(x-y)\kappa_{1}^{\delta}(\xi-\eta),\hskip 15.0pt(x-y,\xi,\eta)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(2)

Cutoff of small velocity: For every
\beta\in(0,1)
we define the piecewise linear function function
\phi_{\beta}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
by

\phi_{\beta}(\xi)=1\hskip 5.0pt\text{if}\hskip 5.0pt\xi\geqslant\beta,\hskip 10.0pt\phi_{\beta}(\xi)=0\hskip 5.0pt\text{if}\hskip 5.0pt\xi\leqslant\beta/2,\hskip 10.0pt\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbbm{1}_{\beta/2\leqslant\xi\leqslant\beta}.

When multiplied with another function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the product
(f\phi_{\beta})
takes the value
0
for small arguments
\xi<\beta/2
and leaves the function
f
unchanged for
\xi>\beta
.

(3)

Cutoff of large velocity: For every
M\in\mathbb{N}
we define the piecewise linear function
\zeta_{M}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
by

\zeta_{M}(\xi)=1\hskip 5.0pt\text{if}\hskip 5.0pt\xi\leqslant M,\hskip 10.0pt\zeta_{M}(\xi)=0\hskip 5.0pt\text{if}\hskip 5.0pt\xi\geqslant M+1,\hskip 10.0pt\zeta_{M}^{\prime}(\xi)=-\mathbbm{1}_{M\leqslant\xi\leqslant M+1}.

When multiplied with another function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the product
(f\zeta_{M})
takes the value
0
for large arguments
\xi>M+1
and leaves the function
f
unchanged for
\xi<M
.

(4)

Spatial cutoff around boundary:
For
0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma<\gamma_{U}
we define the piecewise linear function
\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}:U\to[0,1]
by

(3.22)

\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\hskip 3.0ptd(x,\partial U)>\gamma,\\
(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}d(x,\partial U_{\gamma^{\prime}}),&\text{if}\hskip 3.0pt\gamma^{\prime}\leqslant d(x,\partial U)\leqslant\gamma,\\
0,&\text{if}\hskip 3.0pt0\leqslant d(x,\partial U)\leqslant\gamma^{\prime},\end{cases}

When multiplied with another function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
, the product
(f\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}})
takes the value
0
for points within a
\gamma^{\prime}
-distance of the boundary and leaves the function
f
unchanged for points more than distance
\gamma
from the boundary.

Compared to the work of the first author
[
42
]
, above we explicitly define
\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}
to be compactly supported, and so we do not need to employ an additional approximation argument.

We will also need to establish how to define the gradient of the spatial cutoff.
To do this we use Lemma 3.3 of the work of the first author
[
42
]
.

Lemma 3.5

(Derivative of spatial cutoff)
.

The spatial derivative of the cutoff function
\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}
is only non-zero on the
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
-strip around the boundary
U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}
(otherwise
\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}
is constant so its derivative is zero).
For every
x\in U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}
there is a unique closest point
x^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}=x^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(x)
on the boundary layer
\partial U_{\gamma^{\prime}}
to
x
.
Letting
v_{x,\gamma^{\prime}}
denote the inward pointing unit normal at the boundary
\partial U_{\gamma^{\prime}}
to point
x\in U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}
, the first derivative of the spatial cutoff is given by

\nabla\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(x)=(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}\frac{x-x^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{|x-x^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}|}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}}(x)=(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}v_{x,\gamma}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}}(x),\quad x\in U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}.

This implies that the size of the first derivative is of the order
(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}
,

|\nabla\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(x)|=(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{\gamma^{\prime}}\setminus U_{\gamma}}(x).

To formalise the distributional equality
\nabla\chi_{i}=\delta_{0}(\xi-\rho_{i})\nabla\rho_{i}
, we have the below lemma.
The proof can be found in Lemma 3.4 of the work of the first author
[
42
]
.

Lemma 3.6

(Integration by parts against kinetic function)
.

Let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U\times(0,\infty))
be a compactly supported test function (in both arguments) and let

be the kinetic function corresponding to solutions of (

Then it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{U}\nabla_{x}\psi(x,\xi)\chi(x,\xi,s)\,dx\,d\xi=-\int_{U}\psi(x,\rho(x,s))\nabla\rho(x,s)\,dx.

We are now in a position to prove the
L^{1}_{\omega}L^{1}_{w;x}

Above we used the standard notation

+\lim_{M\to\infty,\beta\to 0}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left|\Psi_{\sigma,M,\beta}(\rho_{1})-\Psi_{\sigma,M,\beta}(\rho_{2})\right|\nabla\cdot\left(F_{1}\nabla w(y)\right)\,dy\,ds\\

\nabla\rho_{i,M,\beta}
independent of
\tilde{\gamma}
.
We therefore bound the terms of (

) by

(3.62)

\gamma^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U\setminus U_{\gamma}}|\rho_{i,M,\beta}(y,s)-\rho_{i,M,\beta}(y^{*})|\,dy\,ds\\
=\gamma^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\gamma}\int_{\partial U_{\tilde{\gamma}}}|\rho_{i,M,\beta}(y,s)-\rho_{i,M,\beta}(y^{*})|\,dS(y)\,d\tilde{\gamma}\,ds\\
\leqslant\gamma^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\rho_{i,M,\beta}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\left(\int_{0}^{\gamma}\tilde{\gamma}^{1/2}\,d\tilde{\gamma}\right)\,ds\\
\leqslant\gamma^{1/2}\|\nabla\rho_{i,M,\beta}\|_{L^{1}([0,t];L^{2}_{x})},

which converges to
0
in the

limit for fixed
M,\beta
.
Therefore conclude that final two lines of the cutoff (
3.2
) consisting of the new terms are non-positive in the
\gamma\to 0
limit.

Putting (

) and subsequent computations together, we conclude

\lim_{M\to\infty,\beta\to 0}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\lim_{\gamma^{\prime}\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon,\delta\to 0}I_{t}^{cut}\leqslant 0.

Martingale term.

By analogous computations to the weight and flux terms, we have as
\epsilon,\delta\to 0
that

\lim_{\epsilon,\delta\to 0}I_{t}^{mart}\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\mbox{\rm sgn}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}(y)w(y)\left(\zeta_{M}(\rho_{1})\phi_{\beta}(\rho_{1})\nabla\cdot\left(\sigma(\rho_{1})\,d\xi^{F}\right)-\zeta_{M}(\rho_{2})\phi_{\beta}(\rho_{2})\nabla\cdot\left(\sigma(\rho_{2})\,d\xi^{F}\right)\right)\,dy.

Just as in the formal proof, we realise that we are only aiming to prove the contraction in expectation.
Since the martingale term is a true martingale in the sense that the integrand at time

is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable, it vanishes in expectation

\mathbb{E}I_{t}^{mart}=0,

and no further analysis is necessary for this term.

Conclusion.

Putting everything together, from equation (

\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
in the weighted

-norm can be upper bounded

\mathbb{E}\left.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{U}|\chi^{1}_{s}-\chi^{2}_{s}|^{2}w\right|_{s=0}^{t}=\lim_{\beta\to 0,M\to\infty}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\lim_{\gamma^{\prime}\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon,\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{U}|\chi^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s,1}-\chi^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s,2}|^{2}w\phi_{\beta}\zeta_{M}\iota_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\right|_{s=0}^{t}\\
=\lim_{\beta\to 0,M\to\infty}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\lim_{\gamma^{\prime}\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon,\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(I_{t}^{err}+I_{t}^{meas}+I_{t}^{mart}+I_{t}^{cut}+I_{t}^{flux}+I_{t}^{bound}\right)\\
\leqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\left|\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})\right|\Delta w(y)\,dy\,ds+\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\partial_{x_{i}}w(y)|\nu_{i}(\rho_{1})-\nu_{i}(\rho_{2})|\,dy\,ds.

Compared to (

\begin{cases}\Delta w<0\\
F_{1}\Delta w+\frac{1}{2}(c+1)|F_{2}\cdot\nabla w|<0,\\
\partial_{x_{i}}w(x)<0\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,d.\end{cases}

We realise that similarly to Proposition

The result then follows by repeating the computation in the formal proof, from (

∎

\rho(s;\rho_{0})
.
The other regularity conditions of Definition
2.4
hold by the fact that
\rho(t;\rho_{0})
was assumed to be a stochastic kinetic solution on the whole interval
[0,t]
.
By the uniqueness of solutions, Corollary
4.5
, the flow property (
4.9
) holds.
∎

Proposition 4.7

(Semigroup is Feller)
.

Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy either Assumption
2.6
or
2.7
, and that
(\Phi,\nu)
further satisfy Assumptions
2.9
and
2.10
.

The family
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Feller semigroup.
That is,
P_{t}
maps
C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
to
C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
.

Proof.

We prove the properties of a Feller semigroup one by one.

•

P_{0}=Id
: This is trivial since
P_{0}F(\rho_{0}):=\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho(0;\rho_{0}))\right]=F(\rho_{0})
.

•

Positive preserving: If
F\geqslant 0
then using the definition of
P_{t}
, it holds that
P_{t}F\geqslant 0
.
Furthermore, it holds that
P_{t}1=1
.

•

P_{t}
is a contraction: This follows by standard properties of the expectation.
For a given
F\in C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
and
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
, we have

|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})|=|\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho(t;\rho_{0}))\right]|\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[|F(\rho(t;\rho_{0}))|\right]\leqslant\sup_{\rho\in L^{1}_{w;x}}|F(\rho)|,

where the right hand side is well-defined by the fact that
F
is bounded.
Taking the supremum over
\rho_{0}
in the above equation gives the contraction property.

•

P_{t}
is continuous on
C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
: Since
F\in C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
and solutions to (
4.7
) are continuous in time, it holds that for every
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
we have
\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}F(\rho_{0})=F(\rho_{0})
.

•

P_{t}
satisfies the semigroup property
P_{t+s}=P_{s}P_{t}
:
Without loss of generality consider
-\infty\leqslant s\leqslant t
.
Taking
F\in C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
and
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
, applying the proof of Theorem 9.14 of Da Prato and Zabczyk
[
6
]
, due to the time-homogeneity of the coefficients and the flow property, we have that

\displaystyle P_{t+s}F(\rho_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho(t+s;\rho_{0}))|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho_{s}(t;\zeta))\right]|_{\zeta=\rho(s;\rho_{0})}\right]=P_{t}(P_{s}F(\rho_{0})).

∎

Remark 4.8
.

Although it is not necessary for the Feller property above, we mention that if we work over a bounded subset of the space of
L^{1}_{w;x}
, for instance
L^{1}_{w;x;M}:=\{\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}:\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\leqslant M\}
, we can strengthen the continuity property of the fourth point above to uniform continuity.
This is a consequence of the fact that

(4.10)

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-F(\rho_{0})|\\
\leqslant\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-P_{t}F_{n}(\rho_{0})|+\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|P_{t}F_{n}(\rho_{0})-F_{n}(\rho_{0})|+\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|F_{n}(\rho_{0})-F(\rho_{0})|\\
\leqslant 2\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|F_{n}(\rho_{0})-F(\rho_{0})|+\sup_{\xi\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}|DF_{n}(\xi)|\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{1}_{w;x;M}}\mathbb{E}\|\rho(t;\rho_{0})-\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}},

where
(F_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C^{1}_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})
satisfies

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in A}|F_{n}(\rho_{0})-F(\rho_{0})|\to 0,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$, for every bounded set $A\subseteq L^{1}_{w;x}$}.

The first term in equation (
4.10
) converges to zero as
n\to\infty
due to the fact that
F_{n}\to F
, and the second term converges to zero as
t\to 0
due to the time continuity of solutions.
The strong continuity follows by taking the limits
n\to\infty
followed by
t\to\infty
in equation (
4.10
).

Theorem 4.9
.

We have the following dichotomy.

(i) Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy either Assumption
2.7
, and that
(\Phi,\nu)
further satisfy Assumptions
2.9
and
2.10
. Then there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(L^{1}_{w;x})
for the semigroup
P_{t}
.
Furthermore, there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
\alpha,d,U,w
and
q_{0}
(but notably independent of
t
) such that for every
t\geqslant 0
, we have

(4.11)

\sup_{\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}}\sup_{\|F\|_{Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1}\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\xi)\mu(d\xi)\right|\leqslant ct^{-1/q_{0}}.

The space
Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})
denotes the space of functions from
L^{1}_{w;x}
to
\mathbb{R}
that are Lipschitz continuous.

(ii) Suppose that
(\Phi,\sigma)
satisfy either Assumption
2.6
, and that
(\Phi,\nu)
further satisfy Assumptions
2.9
and
2.10
. Then there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(L^{1}_{w;x})
for the semigroup
P_{t}
. Furthermore, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
w
,
d
, and
U
(but notably independent of
t
) such that, for every
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
and
t\geqslant 0
,

(4.12)

\sup_{\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1}\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)\right|\leqslant c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t}.

Proof.

Step 1: Defining and proving well-posedness of the invariant measure

For
s<0
we define
\eta_{s}(\rho_{0}):=\rho_{s}(0,\rho_{0})
to be the solution of equation (
4.7
) time
0
initiated at
\rho_{0}
at time
s
.
Using the flow property, Proposition
4.6
, it holds that for every
s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}\leqslant-1
that

\eta_{s_{1}}(\rho_{0})=\rho_{s_{2}}(0;\rho_{s_{1}}(s_{2};\rho_{0})),\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{\omega}L^{1}_{w;x}.

By the polynomial decay of solutions, Proposition
4.2
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\|\eta_{s_{2}}(\rho_{0})-\eta_{s_{1}}(\rho_{0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}=\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{s_{2}}(0,\rho_{0})-\rho_{s_{1}}(0,\rho_{0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{q^{*}}_{x}}^{q^{*}}|s_{2}|^{-1/q_{0}}.

The last inequality tells us that
(\eta_{s}(\rho_{0}))_{s\leqslant-1}
is a Cauchy sequence in the space
L^{1}_{\omega}L^{1}_{w;x}
.
Consequently, it holds that
\eta_{s}(\rho_{0})\to\eta(\rho_{0})
as
s\to-\infty
for some random variable
\eta(\rho_{0})\in L^{1}_{\omega}L^{1}_{w;x}
.

It holds that
\eta
is independent of the initial condition
\rho_{0}
, which can be seen by again using the polynomial decay of solutions, Proposition
4.2
,

\mathbb{E}\|\eta_{s}(\rho_{1,0})-\eta_{s}(\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}=\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{s}(0,\rho_{1,0})-\rho_{s}(0,\rho_{2,0})\|_{L^{1}_{w;x}}\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{q^{*}}_{x}}^{q^{*}}|s|^{-1/q_{0}}

and sending
s\to-\infty
.

We now define

\mu:=\mathcal{L}(\eta)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(L^{1}_{w;x})

where we can choose arbitrary initial condition, for instance
\eta=\eta(0)
.

For
s\leqslant t
, denote by
P_{s,t}
the semigroup associated to equation (
4.7
) at time
t
, defined in a similar way as Definition
4.4
by
P_{s,t}F(\rho_{0}):=\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho_{s}(t;\rho_{0}))\right]
.
In the notation of Definition
4.4
,
P_{t}=P_{0,t}
.
By analogous reasoning as Proposition
4.7
,
P_{s,t}
is Feller for every
s<t
and further satisfies the identities

\begin{cases}P_{s,t}=P_{s+\tau,t+\tau},\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R},\\
P_{s,\tau}P_{\tau,t}=P_{s,t},\quad s<\tau<t.\end{cases}

We further note that the first property follows directly from the
homogeneity of the coefficients in (
1.1
) together with the
stationarity of Brownian increments. In particular, by the identity in law

\xi^{F}(t+\tau)-\xi^{F}(\tau)\overset{d}{=}\xi^{F}(t),

the desired conclusion follows.

Using these properties and the definition of
\mu
gives for every
F\in C_{b}(L^{1}_{w;x})

\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}P_{0,t}F(\tilde{\rho})\mu(d\tilde{\rho})=\lim_{s\to\infty}P_{-s,0}(P_{0,t}F)(0)=\lim_{s\to\infty}P_{-s,t}F(0)=P_{-(t+s),0}F(0)=\int_{L^{1}_{w;x}}F(\tilde{\rho})\mu(d\tilde{\rho}).

This implies that
P_{t}^{*}\mu=\mu
as measures on
L^{1}_{w;x}
, i.e.
\mu
is an invariant measure for the semigroup
P_{t}
.

Step 2: The bound (
4.11
)

By the polynomial decay of Proposition
4.2
, it holds that for
F\in Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})
and
\rho_{1,0},\rho_{2,0}\in L^{2}_{x}

\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{1,0})-P_{t}F(\rho_{2,0})\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[F(\rho(t;\rho_{1,0}))-F(\rho(t;\rho_{2,0}))\right]\right|\lesssim\|F\|_{Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})}\|w\|_{L^{q^{*}}_{x}}^{q^{*}}|t|^{-1/q_{0}}.

This implies that any two invariant measures
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
on
L^{1}_{w;x}
coincide.
This also gives us that

\left|P_{t}F(\rho_{1,0})-\int_{L^{1}_{w,x}}F(\tilde{\rho})\mu(d\tilde{\rho})\right|\lesssim\|F\|_{Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})}\|w\|_{L^{q^{*}}_{x}}^{q^{*}}|t|^{-1/q_{0}},

which proves the first claim (
4.11
) after taking the supremum over
\|F\|_{Lip(L^{1}_{w;x})}\leqslant 1
and
\xi\in L^{1}_{w;x}
.

For the second claim, we consider the case that either Assumption
2.6
is satisfied or that
\nu
is Lipschitz.
By following precisely the same steps as above but using the exponential decay rate (
4.2
) rather than the polynomial one, we get the exponential decay (
4.12
).
∎
