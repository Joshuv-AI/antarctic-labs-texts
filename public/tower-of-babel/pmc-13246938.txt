Econometric assessment of seasonal volatility and predictive uncertainty in Albania’s hydropower-dependent electricity market
Authors: Luan Arapi, Raimonda Dervishi, Sander Kovaçi, Benedict Celestine Agbata, A K Awasthi, Hambeer Singh, Younis A Sabawi, Aseel Smerat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Albanian energy sector is highly reliant on hydropower, making the national energy balance vulnerable to seasonal variability and hydrological shocks. This study analyzes quarterly electricity production data for 2012Q1–2025Q3 using Seasonal–Trend decomposition via Loess (STL) and Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) modelling. STL is used to isolate trend and seasonal strength, while a SARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)4 model is estimated to generate production forecasts for the 2025Q4–2030Q4 horizon (Q = calendar quarter). Results confirm a dominant seasonal component (FS = 0.635) and a recurring seasonal trough in Q3. A post-2022 increase in volatility is reflected in widening 95% prediction intervals, indicating that while the seasonal timing of stress is predictable, the magnitude of future outcomes becomes increasingly uncertain over the medium term. To operationalize this risk, the study implements a hydrological drought stress test on Albania’s quarterly net domestic production (Yp), using the SARIMA baseline as the reference path. The sensitivity results quantify sizable, seasonally concentrated supply gaps under proportional production shocks (− 10% and − 25%), reinforcing the structural nature of the recurring third-quarter (Q3) deficit and its implications for adequacy and import coverage. The findings inform resilience planning by quantifying the scale and timing of seasonal shortfalls and by motivating diversification and flexibility options that are seasonally complementary to hydropower. In particular, solar PV is discussed as a potential counter-cyclical contributor conditional on scale-up, grid hosting capacity, and balancing resources. The paper concludes that hydropower-centric strategies alone are insufficient for medium-term stability and highlights the role of diversified non-hydro resources, grid-efficiency improvements, and storage in hedging forecasted production troughs and reducing exposure to external balancing needs.Keywords: SARIMA, Energy Forecasting, STL Decomposition, Seasonal Energy Security, Time Series Analysis, Operational Efficiency Index (OEI)Subject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Electricity systems increasingly face systemic risk arising from the interaction of climate variability, resource seasonality, operational constraints, and evolving market integration. In systems where generation is highly concentrated in a single weather-dependent resource, seasonal and inter-annual shocks can propagate beyond the energy sector by tightening adequacy margins, increasing import dependence, and amplifying exposure to regional price volatility. This framing is particularly relevant for hydropower-dominant countries, where hydrological fluctuations translate directly into production variability and recurring seasonal stress1. Recent academic work increasingly treats adequacy risk as a system-level exposure problem—where predictable seasonal structure coexists with uncertain shock magnitudes—motivating uncertainty-aware forecasting and scenario-based stress testing as planning tools in climate-sensitive power systems2,3.

The Albanian electricity sector represents a distinctive case in European energy economics because it is structurally dominated by hydropower. While this “green” profile supports decarbonization objectives, it also creates a concentrated systemic exposure to hydrological volatility. In Albania, hydropower accounts for around 95% of electricity generation/overall supply in recent years and the country’s largest hydro assets are concentrated in the Drin River cascade administered by KESH, with a total installed capacity of about 1,350 MW4. This combination—high hydro concentration in a relatively small, increasingly interconnected system—makes Albania a useful empirical setting to study how seasonality and hydrological shocks scale into external balancing needs under market integration. In hydro-dominant systems, this concentration can transform hydrological shocks into economy-wide exposure through imports, prices, and fiscal pressures, making the problem explicitly “systemic” rather than purely hydrological.

This hydro-centric architecture produces a recurring security-of-supply dilemma: the national balance swings between surplus conditions (enabling exports) and deficit conditions (necessitating imports) when inflows and reservoir levels fall. Under such deficit regimes, Albania becomes a price taker in regional markets, and import exposure transmits wholesale price shocks into fiscal pressure and tariff-policy constraints. This vulnerability is reflected even in domestic regulatory pricing mechanisms: for example, the Energy Regulatory Authority’s purchase-price methodology for priority hydropower producers explicitly references the Hungarian Power Exchange day-ahead baseload price (HUPX/DAM) and compares outcomes to ALPEX clearing prices5. In parallel, Albania’s energy-policy environment is being reshaped by regional market integration. The Energy Community Secretariat reports that Albania’s day-ahead market coupling with Kosovo began on 31 January 2024, and that the intraday market started on 31 October 2024—has remained relatively thin, with traded volumes around 12% of the day-ahead level6. These developments matter for energy security because greater liquidity and cross-border integration can, in principle, reduce the cost of balancing hydrological shortfalls yet they may also amplify exposure to price spikes during regional scarcity events. The macro-risk is likely to intensify under climate change. The IPCC’s Mediterranean cross-chapter synthesis documents increasing heat and hydrological stress risks across the basin, with implications for water-dependent systems and seasonal resource availability7. Consistently, recent country diagnostics emphasize that Albania’s high reliance on hydropower makes the electricity system particularly vulnerable to climate change, reinforcing the strategic need to commission alternative renewable sources more rapidly8. These dynamics motivate a shift from a reactive hydro-dependent model toward a proactive diversification strategy. Solar photovoltaics (PV) is frequently highlighted as a strong candidate because of its seasonal alignment with Albania’s summer demand and hydro constraints: IRENA notes that PV can be particularly relevant for meeting increasing summer demand and improving security of supply when hydropower output is limited4. More broadly, the international literature identifies meaningful complementarity between solar and hydropower, with the strength of complementarity depending on climate characteristics and spatial scale9. At the micro level, Albania-focused evidence is also emerging. Dervishi et al.10, for instance, model rooftop PV in Tirana’s household sector and highlight how electricity consumption and PV generation relate to weather-driven indicators (e.g., cooling/heating conditions), improving forecasting and supporting better balancing decisions at the distribution/consumer interface. This is relevant to the macro seasonal problem because cooling-driven load typically peaks in the same period when hydropower availability is most constrained, strengthening the case for PV as a counter-cyclical resource10. However, the extent to which PV can mitigate seasonal hydro shortfalls is conditional on scale-up, grid hosting capacity, balancing resources, and investment implementation1,4; therefore, PV is treated in this study as a potential seasonal hedge rather than an automatic solution.

Against this background, the present study analyzes Albania’s 2012–2025Q3 energy cycle using an advanced time-series approach to separate structural change from predictable seasonality. We employ Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess (STL), originally developed by Cleveland et al.11 and widely adopted in applied forecasting practice3. Our analysis identifies a Trend Strength of 0.71010, indicating meaningful long-term structural change, and a Seasonal Strength of 0.63500, validating the persistence of quarterly seasonality. Despite these institutional and technological developments, a research gap remains in quantitatively linking Albania’s high-frequency seasonal production cycle to import dependence and system-wide fiscal exposure under evolving market integration. Most policy discussions treat hydrological risk, market coupling, and renewable diversification as separate themes; fewer studies unify these elements in a single econometric framework that decomposes the seasonal structure of the national electricity balance and tests how production shocks propagate into import requirements under real-world market conditions.

Forecasting and risk assessment in power systems increasingly emphasizes uncertainty, not only point predictions. A probabilistic forecast is a predictive distribution whose quality is assessed by calibration and sharpness, often evaluated with proper scoring rules rather than point-error metrics alone2,12. In energy applications, this shift is well documented: probabilistic forecasting is increasingly used to support planning and operations under renewable variability and market uncertainty13. Recent work has also adopted explicitly probabilistic, multivariate uncertainty frameworks for “systemic” exposure assessment—e.g., copula-based joint distributions and percentile-based exposure metrics for power supply management14. For hydro-dominant small systems, however, many advanced multivariate approaches require higher-frequency data and/or multiple risk-factor series, whereas official national energy balances are often available at lower frequency and with limited disaggregation.

SARIMA is adopted as the primary forecasting baseline because the analysis is grounded in official quarterly electricity-balance statistics and the dependent series (quarterly net domestic production, Yp) exhibits strong seasonal periodicity (s = 4) over 2012Q1–2025Q3. In this setting, parsimonious seasonal time-series models provide an interpretable stochastic representation of quarterly dynamics and support uncertainty-aware forecasting via prediction intervals2. While multivariate probabilistic exposure models—such as copula-based dependence frameworks—are well suited to settings with multiple jointly modelled risk drivers14, the contribution of this paper is to develop a replicable macro-level quarterly framework that can be implemented directly on official balance series. Accordingly, we use SARIMA as a transparent seasonal benchmark, benchmark it against a competing state-space approach (ETS), and complement the forecasting baseline with scenario-based stress testing that translates production shocks into adequacy and import-coverage requirements in volume terms, aligned with the information content of the quarterly balance3.

Despite expanding work on hydrological variability, market integration, and renewable diversification, the empirical literature on hydro-dominant small systems provides limited macro-level evidence that links seasonal structure in official national electricity-balance data to import dependence (import-coverage needs in volume terms) under an uncertainty-aware forecasting and stress-testing framework. In Albania-focused research, closely related empirical contributions tend to emphasize micro-scale PV adoption or localized demand–generation forecasting10, while fewer studies offer a quarterly, replicable framework that decomposes production seasonality and evaluates how drought-like shocks scale into import requirements using the same official statistics.

Building on this gap, this study contributes to the systemic-risk and energy-security literature on hydro-dominant small-to-moderate electricity systems by integrating seasonal structure identification with an uncertainty-aware forecasting baseline and scenario-based stress testing grounded in official quarterly balance data. Unlike closely related Albania-focused empirical studies that emphasize micro-level PV adoption or localized demand–generation forecasting, this paper develops a macro-level, quarterly framework that connects repeatable seasonal production patterns to external balancing exposure (imports) and quantifies how import requirements scale under adverse drought-like shocks as market integration evolves. The new insight is that Albania’s vulnerability is not merely the presence of variability, but its systematic concentration in specific quarters (notably Q3) combined with widening post-2022 uncertainty in shock magnitude—implying that resilience planning can target predictable timing while hedging uncertain size through diversification and flexibility2,13,14. Taken together, the paper offers a replicable template for seasonal-risk diagnosis in data-constrained hydro-dominant systems and clarifies the volume-based pathway from domestic production shortfalls to import-coverage requirements under official quarterly reporting.

Methodology

Building on the decomposition framework of Cleveland et al.11 and modern forecasting practice in Hyndman and Athanasopoulos3, this study applies a multi-stage quantitative design to characterize and quantify Albania’s hydro-driven production volatility. Rather than treating hydro-dependency as a qualitative risk, the methodology formalizes it through (i) systematic preprocessing and stationarity diagnostics, (ii) seasonal–trend decomposition and statistical feature extraction to quantify predictability, (iii) model selection and validation using competing state-space approaches, and (iv) scenario-based stress testing to translate production shocks into implied adequacy pressures. In parallel, a quarterly Operational Efficiency Index (OEI) is computed to evaluate system performance over 2012Q1–2025Q3.

Data preprocessing, variable selection, and dependency diagnostics

The empirical analysis is based on quarterly observations of Net Domestic Production (Yp), Total Imports (Im), and Total Exports (Ex), which together represent the core components of the national electricity balance. These series are sourced from the official quarterly electricity-balance statistics published by the Albanian Institute of Statistics18. All series were transformed into quarterly time-series objects in R using year–quarter formatting, consistent with standard time-series workflows19. The sample spans 2012Q1–2025Q3, matching the availability of harmonized quarterly reporting.

Let t = 1,…,T index quarters from 2012Q1 to 2025Q3, and let Ypt, Imt, and Ext denote Net Domestic Production, Total Imports, and Total Exports observed in quarter t, respectively (all in MWh energy units as reported in the quarterly balance). For modelling, we define a transformed production series yt=g(Ypt), where g(⋅) is either the identity transform or a variance-stabilizing transform (e.g., log(⋅) when required by diagnostics. Non-seasonal and seasonal differencing operators are denoted ∇yt=(1 − B)yt and ∇4yt=(1 − B4)yt​, where B is the backshift operator (Byt=yt-1​). The SARIMA model is estimated on the stationary differenced series ∇∇4yt​, and forecasts are back-transformed to the original scale when a log transform is used (with standard bias-aware interpretation of prediction intervals)20,19.

Forecast robustness and diagnostics

To mitigate overfitting and ensure that conclusions are not driven by in-sample fit alone, we apply four complementary diagnostic layers. (i) Integration and seasonal differencing choices are triangulated across ADF, KPSS (stationarity as the null), and Phillips–Perron tests, reducing dependence on any single unit-root procedure and improving stability in finite samples30,31. (ii) Predictive performance is evaluated out-of-sample using a fixed holdout window (last 8 quarters), reporting MAE, RMSE, and the scale-free MASE metric to allow comparability across scales32. (iii) Residual adequacy is assessed using the Ljung–Box test (lag = 8 for quarterly data), complemented by distributional and conditional-variance diagnostics (Jarque–Bera; ARCH LM), since departures from Gaussian, homoscedastic innovations can affect the reliability of analytic prediction intervals33–35. (iv) For uncertainty quantification, analytic prediction intervals are reported by default, and where residual diagnostics indicate non-normality and/or heteroscedasticity, bootstrap-based prediction intervals are computed as a robustness check19.

The quarterly span 2012Q1–2025Q3 implies a small-to-moderate T = 55, so information-criterion differences may be less decisive and parameter uncertainty can be non-trivial. Accordingly, we emphasize parsimony using AICc (rather than AIC) and interpret model comparisons cautiously when differences are small; robustness is further supported by reporting scale-free errors (MASE) and by relying on interval-based uncertainty (including bootstrap intervals when warranted) rather than point forecasts alone28,19.

Sensitivity analysis

To support energy planning under hydro-variability, the study conducts a scenario-based sensitivity (stress-test) analysis that translates negative production shocks into implied adequacy and procurement pressure. This approach is consistent with the broader use of stress testing in climate- and energy-related planning, where baseline projections are perturbed by adverse scenarios to quantify exposure under uncertainty36. This separation is consistent with European practice that treats network losses/performance indicators as a distinct dimension of system assessment rather than a by-product of generation shortfalls16.

Non-hydro proxy and scenario scaling for seasonal hedging

Because the quarterly balance does not isolate solar PV as a standalone category, we construct a balance-based non-hydro generation proxy by subtracting reported total hydro generation from net domestic production. This proxy captures the aggregate contribution of non-hydro sources recorded in the balance (e.g., thermal, wind, solar, and other renewables) within a standard energy-balance accounting framework39,40. The proxy is used descriptively to (i) assess whether non-hydro output behaves counter-cyclically during the typical Q3 hydro trough and (ii) scale seasonal hedging requirements by translating a targeted coverage share of the Q2–Q3 seasonal gap into implied additional non-hydro generation needs. Findings are therefore interpreted as system-level non-hydro hedging capacity rather than PV-only effects.

Building upon the analytical framework established in the methodology, the following section presents the empirical findings derived from the 2012–2025 dataset. By applying descriptive statistics and volatility metrics to the quarterly production figures, we move from data processing to the identification of long-term patterns within the Albanian energy landscape.

Formal test of quarter-of-year differences. To statistically validate that the seasonal trough is not only descriptive, we test whether quarterly net domestic production (Yp) differs across calendar quarters using a one-way ANOVA. Results indicate significant quarterly differences, F(3,51) = 6.914, p = 0.00054, with a sizeable effect (η2 = 0.289; ω2 = 0.244). Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons show that Q3 is significantly lower than Q1 (mean difference Q3 − Q1 = − 825,688 MWh, p = 0.0005) and Q2 (Q3 − Q2 = − 571,903 MWh, p = 0.0252), while Q4 is also lower than Q1 (Q4 − Q1 = − 589,008 MWh, p = 0.0233). Because a variance homogeneity check indicates unequal variances (Levene p = 0.039), we additionally apply a non-parametric robustness test. Kruskal–Wallis confirms significant quarterly differences, H(3) = 14.362, p = 0.00245 (ε2 = 0.223). Dunn-type post-hoc comparisons with Holm adjustment again identify Q3 as significantly lower than Q1 (p = 0.00343) and Q2 (p = 0.02888), corroborating the structural nature of the summer trough.

This seasonal production structure directly maps into Albania’s external balancing behavior. The energy balance visualization (Fig. 1) shows a strong inverse synchronization between domestic output and import volumes: when domestic production falls—most visibly during the Q3 trough—imports rise sharply to maintain adequacy. A descriptive dependency diagnostic confirms this mechanism, with a Pearson correlation of r = − 0.8 (p˂0.001) between production and imports, indicating that imports function less as a marginal supplement and more as the primary adjustment margin for hydrological shortfalls21. Figure 1 also underscores the broader vulnerability implied by this mechanism: the quarters in which Albania systematically requires higher imports tend to coincide with periods of elevated regional demand (e.g., summer cooling loads), increasing exposure to market volatility and procurement stress. During high-output periods, exports rise as a counterpart to surplus production; however, the recurring Q3 deficit remains the defining structural constraint of the system. The persistence of this mirrored production–import relationship across 2012Q1–2025Q3, and its salience during the 2022 crisis, reinforces the conclusion that hydro-dominance translates seasonal variability into systematic external dependence—strengthening the case for diversification toward non-hydro renewables and complementary flexibility measures19.

To avoid implicitly assuming that solar PV will systematically offset summer hydro shortfalls, we operationalize a balance-based non-hydro generation (proxy) that captures aggregate non-hydro domestic output recorded in the quarterly electricity balance (i.e., net domestic production net of reported hydro generation). Over 2012Q1–2025Q3, this proxy peaks in Q3, averaging 41,236 MWh per quarter (≈ 3.33% of the mean Q3). Yet, its scale remains modest relative to Albania’s recurring seasonal production deficit: the mean Q1–Q3 production gap is 825,688 MWh and the mean Q2–Q3 gap is 571,903 MWh, implying that observed non-hydro output in Q3 offsets only about 5.0% (relative to the Q1–Q3 gap) to 7.2% (relative to the Q2–Q3 gap) of the average seasonal shortfall. To illustrate the implications for diversification, a simple scaling exercise indicates that offsetting 25% of the mean Q2–Q3 gap would require approximately 142,976 MWh (≈ 143 GWh) of additional non-hydro generation in Q3—around 3.47× the historical Q3 non-hydro level. Accordingly, solar PV is best framed as a conditional hedge embedded within broader non-hydro diversification: its capacity to reduce the recurring Q3 deficit depends on the feasible pace of scale-up and on system enablers—particularly grid hosting capacity and balancing flexibility—rather than being presumed to eliminate seasonal adequacy pressures on its own.

Decomposition of production dynamics

Advanced statistical analysis: sarima modelling and forecasting

To address the central research question regarding the predictability of Albania’s energy balance, a SARIMA model was deployed. This framework was selected due to its superior capacity to handle non-stationary time series that exhibit both seasonal cycles and stochastic shocks, a characteristic typical of hydroelectric-dependent systems.

Model selection strategy and validation

This study conducted a structured comparison between a seasonal ARIMA specification and an Exponential Smoothing state-space benchmark to identify the most reliable forecasting baseline for subsequent stress testing. Model selection was governed primarily by the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), which balances goodness-of-fit against model complexity and is particularly appropriate for finite samples. As a secondary validation layer, we assessed residual adequacy using the Ljung–Box test (lag = 8 for quarterly data) to verify whether remaining autocorrelation persists after model fitting. As summarized in Table 3, the SARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)4 model clearly outperforms ETS(A, N,A) across all information criteria. The SARIMA specification achieves a substantially higher log-likelihood and markedly lower AIC/AICc/BIC values. The AICc gap between the models is large, providing strong evidence in favor of SARIMA as the superior statistical description of the 2012–2025 quarterly production series. Residual diagnostics further reinforce this selection. The SARIMA model passes the Ljung–Box test (p = 0.2117), indicating no strong evidence of remaining autocorrelation and suggesting that the model captures the key temporal structure in the series. In contrast, the ETS(A, N,A) model fails the same diagnostic (p < 0.001), implying that systematic dependence remains in the residuals and that the ETS structure is not sufficient to fully represent the dynamics in the data.

Shock-driven (MA-dominated) short-run structure

The non-seasonal component is ARIMA(0,1,2), meaning the model relies on moving-average terms rather than autoregressive persistence to capture short-term dependence. Practically, this suggests that quarterly deviations in production are better described as responses to unanticipated shocks (e.g., hydrological or operational disturbances) and their subsequent correction, rather than as a smooth, self-propagating autoregressive process. The significant MA(1) and MA(2) terms indicate that shocks affect the series over a multi-quarter adjustment window.

“Carry-over” and correction over subsequent quarters

The negative MA coefficients (MA(1) = − 0.4814; MA(2) = − 0.5186) imply a systematic error-correction pattern: deviations in one quarter tend to be partially offset in the next one to two quarters. In energy terms, a large production shortfall is not fully absorbed within the same quarter; instead, its consequences propagate through balancing and adjustment mechanisms, generating measurable persistence in the production path after the initial disturbance. This provides an econometric rationale for why major hydrological anomalies can leave lingering effects beyond the quarter in which they occur.

Strong seasonal adjustment and seasonal persistence

The seasonal MA term is highly pronounced (SMA(1) ≈ − 1) and statistically significant, indicating very strong quarterly seasonality. A seasonal MA coefficient near − 1 suggests that the model requires a powerful seasonal correction mechanism to reconcile deviations at the annual cycle (period 4). This reinforces that the production process is tightly linked to a hydrological seasonal regime, where seasonal disturbances can reverberate across corresponding quarters of adjacent years.

Volatility remains substantial beyond predictable structure

Even with statistically identifiable seasonal and short-run correction dynamics, the innovation variance remains large (σ2 reported in the diagnostic table), implying that the system is exposed to high-amplitude stochastic variation that cannot be removed by seasonal averaging alone. For planning, this supports treating hydro variability as a material risk factor requiring explicit stress testing rather than relying purely on historical seasonal norms.

Residual diagnostics and robustness

The reliability of the selected SARIMA model is further supported by the diagnostic panel (Fig. 4). First, standardized residuals exhibit broadly stable variance across the sample, with a notable volatility spike around 2022, consistent with a temporary structural disturbance that increased unexplained variance relative to historical norms. Second, the ACF of residuals remains within the 95% confidence bounds across lags, consistent with residuals behaving as white noise—i.e., the model has extracted the systematic temporal structure available in the data. Third, the distributional diagnostics (KDE/Q–Q) show residuals centered close to zero with an approximately normal shape, supporting the model’s suitability for mid-term projection.

Taken together, the parameter interpretation and the residual diagnostics indicate a system characterized by strong seasonal dependence, shock-driven short-run adjustments, and meaningful residual volatility—conditions under which deterministic drought stress tests are informative. Accordingly, the − 10% and − 25% sensitivity scenarios are economically meaningful “shock experiments” that quantify how plausible hydrological disruptions propagate through the system and translate into multi-quarter production shortfalls relative to the baseline forecast.

Strategic forecasting (2025–2030): assessing medium-term systemic risks

The 20-quarter-ahead projection from the selected SARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)4 model provides a medium-term diagnostic of Albania’s hydro-dominated production profile. As shown in Fig. 5, the forecast preserves a stable seasonal signature while simultaneously revealing how uncertainty expands as the horizon extends an expected but policy-relevant property in systems where output is tightly coupled to variable hydrological conditions.

Quarterly Energy Production Forecast (2025–2030). The projection reproduces the recurring seasonal cycle (notably the Q3 trough and Q1 recovery), while widening 80% and 95% prediction intervals illustrate increasing medium-term uncertainty in a hydro-dependent system.

Figure 5 indicates that the timing of seasonal stress remains highly predictable: the forecast replicates the recurring third-quarter (Q3) trough and subsequent recovery toward the first quarter (Q1), consistent with a hydrological cycle that structurally shapes quarterly production. This persistence implies that adequacy pressure is not randomly distributed across the year; rather, it is repeatedly concentrated in specific quarters, which can be anticipated in procurement, balancing, and contingency planning. At the same time, the prediction intervals widen progressively through 2030, indicating increasing forecast uncertainty with horizon. This widening reflects the accumulation of uncertainty over time: while the model can reproduce the seasonal rhythm, the magnitude of future production becomes increasingly indeterminate as the forecast moves farther from the estimation window. In practical terms, this feature argues for interpreting the projection as a distribution of plausible outcomes rather than a single deterministic path, especially around structurally vulnerable quarters where small deviations in inflows can translate into material supply gaps. The expanding bands are also consistent with the model’s estimated shock-adjustment structure (MA-dominated dynamics), which implies that large disturbances can affect the series beyond the contemporaneous quarter through multi-quarter correction. In such a setting, adverse events need not be isolated to be consequential: clustered hydrological shocks can suppress recovery and amplify vulnerability across subsequent quarters. This reinforces the strategic value of complementarity and flexibility measures that reduce exposure during the seasonal trough and improve the system’s ability to absorb shocks without escalating import dependence. From a policy and investment perspective, the forecast therefore supports a resilience-oriented approach that targets the system’s most exposed seasonal window. Technologies and operational instruments that provide summer-peaking or fast-response capacity—including solar PV as a seasonally complementary resource, alongside storage, demand-side flexibility, and contracting strategies—can reduce the effective supply gap in periods when hydro output is predictably weakest and uncertainty is most consequential.

Scenario impacts

Two deterministic proportional shocks were imposed on the baseline path: Scenario A (− 10%) and Scenario B (− 25%). Because shocks are proportional, the implied deficit in each quarter scales mechanically with baseline output; consequently, Scenario B deficits are exactly 2.5× Scenario A in every quarter (Fig. 7). Under Scenario A, quarterly deficits range from 160,848 to 264,396, with a mean deficit of 209,855 per quarter (Table 5). Under Scenario B, deficits range from 402,121 to 660,991, with a mean deficit of 524,638 per quarter. Aggregated across the full 21-quarter horizon, the cumulative implied shortfall reaches 4,406,959 units in Scenario A and 11,017,396 units in Scenario B (Table 5), quantifying the additional balancing volume required under drought-like conditions. Quarter-of-year averages highlight the seasonal clustering of stress (Table 6). Absolute deficits are largest in Q1—when baseline output is highest and smallest in Q3 in absolute terms, reflecting the proportional construction of the scenarios. At the same time, Q3 remains the structurally weakest quarter in baseline supply, implying a persistent adequacy constraint even before shocks are applied. Figure 7 illustrates this concentration: the single largest modeled deficit occurs in 2030Q1, reaching 264,396 units under Scenario A and 660,991 units under Scenario B, reinforcing that procurement and operational pressure are seasonally concentrated rather than evenly distributed throughout the year.

Implied quarterly supply gap (baseline – scenario, MWh per quarter) under hydrological drought scenarios A (− 10%) and B (− 25%), 2025Q4–2030Q4.

Finally, the sensitivity analysis provides a transparent, volume-based stress test: even a moderate − 10% production shock generates systematic quarterly gaps, while the − 25% shock generates large, recurrent deficits with strong seasonal concentration. These results support aligning contingency procurement and operational readiness around the quarters with the largest implied gaps (notably Q1), while also recognizing that the lowest-output quarters (often Q3) remain structurally vulnerable in terms of baseline supply.

Evolution of systemic efficiency: operational efficiency index (OEI)

Consistent with the energy-balance evidence reported earlier, Albania continues to rely on imports as the principal balancing channel during hydrological shortfalls (r = − 0.8). Complementing this supply-side exposure, the Operational Efficiency Index (OEI) provides an internal lens on grid performance.

Complementing this supply-side mechanism, Fig. 8 demostrates the evolution of internal system performance through the OEI over 2012Q1–2025Q3. The series indicates a clear improvement in systemic efficiency over time, but with substantial volatility and episodic setbacks. While the early sample contains several quarters with very high OEI readings, the trajectory is punctuated by pronounced efficiency drops, notably a sharp decline around 2013, a historic low of approximately 36.5% in 2018, and another marked deterioration around 2021—consistent with periods when losses and/or system stress temporarily intensified. Following these episodes, OEI recovers and, in the later years, typically fluctuates within a higher band (often in the 70–90% range), indicating progress in loss mitigation and operational performance even as variability persists. The co-existence of strong import dependence and improving OEI highlights a strategic asymmetry: Albania’s grid appears increasingly capable of distributing available energy more efficiently, yet the system remains structurally exposed to seasonal production shortfalls due to limited diversification in non-hydro generation. The upward shift in OEI in the most recent observations provides empirical support for integrating counter-cyclical resources—most notably solar PV, given its seasonal complementarity with summer hydro weakness to better translate efficiency gains into reduced external balancing needs during the dry-season constraint.

Policy implications and recommendations

Policy implications follow directly from the empirical patterns documented in this study, and their strength should track the strength of the evidence. First, the seasonal asymmetry in production is not only descriptive but statistically supported (e.g., significant quarter-of-year differences with a sizeable effect), with Q3 consistently emerging as the structural trough. This makes the case strongest for targeting resilience planning at predictable seasonal stress windows—especially the recurring Q3 summer trough—while treating the magnitude of future shortfalls as uncertain, consistent with standard forecasting interpretations of uncertainty growth over time19. In practice, procurement calendars, reserve planning, and operational protocols can be aligned with the recurrent constraint window rather than activated only after hydrological underperformance materializes19.

Second, the evidence indicates that external balancing is systematically tied to domestic output (strong inverse production–import co-movement), implying that imports operate as an adjustment margin during hydrological shortfalls. This supports a high-confidence policy implication focused on import-risk management: institutionalizing forecast-informed contracting and contingency procurement for structurally vulnerable periods is likely to be more robust than reliance on reactive spot purchases19. This implication is further reinforced by the drought sensitivity scenarios, which translate plausible negative production shocks into large implied balancing volumes over the forecast horizon, highlighting the material scale of procurement exposure under adverse realizations.

Third, recommendations on new supply and flexibility should be framed more conditionally, given that the study does not model technology deployment pathways endogenously. Solar PV is a plausible seasonal contributor because its generation profile peaks in summer and may complement hydro seasonality44,45, but the results also imply that its hedging value depends on scale, grid hosting capacity, and balancing resources; PV should therefore be treated as part of broader non-hydro diversification rather than assumed to eliminate seasonal hydro shortfalls. Similarly, flexibility investments (notably storage and enhanced balancing capability) can be justified as “no-regret” options under widening forecast uncertainty because they reduce exposure to adverse realizations by smoothing shortfalls and improving fast-response capability during tight quarters19, but their optimal sizing and timing remain an area for detailed system studies.

Finally, demand-side flexibility and continued loss reduction are supported as complementary measures that can strengthen adequacy under uncertainty by dampening peak loads during constrained periods and ensuring that available supply translates into deliverable energy (European Commission,42. More broadly, embedding routine forecast-and-stress-test analytics within sector planning can shift system management from reactive balancing toward anticipatory operational readiness, reducing exposure to hydrological variability and recurrent external balancing needs19.

Conclusion

This study provides an empirical characterization of Albania’s hydro-dependent electricity system using quarterly data for 2012Q1–2025Q3 and a combined decomposition–forecasting design. STL results show a production process with substantial predictable structure, reflected in strong component metrics (FT = 0.7101, FS = 0.6350), indicating that long-run movement and seasonality both materially shape the series. Importantly, the seasonal profile is highly structured, with a persistent summer trough that repeatedly concentrates adequacy pressure in specific quarters. The energy-balance evidence further shows that external balancing remains central to system operation. The strong inverse co-movement between domestic production and imports (r = − 0.8) indicates that imports function as the primary adjustment channel during hydrological shortfalls, consistent with the recurring seasonal trough identified in the historical trajectory. Forecast evaluation confirms that SARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)4 provides a superior statistical baseline relative to an ETS benchmark, with residual diagnostics consistent with an adequate representation of the series’ temporal structure. The medium-term forecast (2025Q4–2030Q4) reproduces the seasonal rhythm while exhibiting widening prediction intervals, underscoring that the timing of seasonal stress is predictable, whereas the magnitude of production becomes increasingly uncertain with the forecast horizon. To translate this uncertainty into an operationally interpretable risk metric, the paper implements a hydrological drought stress test on quarterly net domestic production (Yp) over 2025Q4–2030Q4. The scenarios quantify sizeable, seasonally clustered implied supply gaps under proportional production shocks (− 10% and − 25%), yielding cumulative shortfalls of 4.41 million MWh and 11.02 million MWh, respectively, that would require import coverage or equivalent balancing to maintain adequacy. In parallel, OEI results indicate that system efficiency has improved relative to historical troughs but remains volatile, implying that modernization can reduce the effective balancing burden, yet cannot substitute for diversification in the generation mix.

Limitations and future research

This analysis relies on quarterly balance statistics and therefore cannot capture intra-quarter extremes or short-duration adequacy events. In addition, the balance does not isolate solar PV as a standalone category; consequently, diversification results reflect aggregate non-hydro hedging rather than PV-specific coverage. The drought sensitivity exercise applies proportional shocks to the baseline path and does not endogenize hydrology, demand, or market constraints. Future research could leverage higher-frequency and more disaggregated data, integrate multivariate drivers (e.g., inflows and demand), and adopt a constraint-aware adequacy framework that explicitly represents operational and network limitations to refine risk estimates under structural change.
