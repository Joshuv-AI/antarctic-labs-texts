Thermal Damage to the Skin From 5.6 GHz Microwave Exposures in Swine
Authors: James E Parker, Roberto A Rodriguez, Clarissa D Vázquez‐Colón, Randolph Stone, Joseph W Butterworth, Jeffrey N Whitmore, Jason A Payne, William B Voorhees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA study of burn thresholds from superficially penetrating radio‐frequency (RF) energy at 5.6 GHz for swine skin was conducted. The study estimated the thresholds for superficial, partial‐thickness, and full‐thickness burn severities after 20 s of exposure at power densities of 4–8 W/cm2. Biopsies were collected from each burn site at 1, 24, 72, and 168 h post exposure. Each sample was assessed by a burn pathologist against 20 histological factors to characterize the damage resulting from these RF overexposures. There were significant differences in the burn thresholds at the different severities compared to previous thresholds found at 8.2 and 95 GHz. A one‐dimensional, layered digital phantom that utilized realistic values for dielectric and thermal properties was consistent with the observed damage trends and empirical dosimetry values. The results of the heating and cooling response of the animal model, and histology scores of each exposure are provided to establish damage thresholds and support development of simulation tools to predict parameters associated with RF hazards.Keywords: burns, exposure limits, mathematical models, radio‐frequency, skin, thermal effects, thermal models

Introduction

This paper expands upon previous work conducted at 8.2 and 95 GHz (Parker et al. 2024) by examining skin burns resulting from 5.6 GHz exposures on swine. The main physical difference of exposures at 5.6 GHz compared to the other exposures is the deeper penetration of the RF energy due to the lower frequency (see Table 1). This distributes the RF energy deeper and reduces the peak temperature at the surface relative to higher frequencies at the same incident power density. Computational models reveal that the energy penetration depth may significantly affect the thresholds and margins between various burn severities. At higher frequencies, a temperature gradient is formed as superficial layers of skin are directly heated by the RF exposure, and then, conduction diffuses the heat into deeper tissue layers. As a result, tissue damage is most pronounced near the surface of the skin. However, at 5.6 GHz (and similar frequencies), all layers of the skin are directly heated in a more uniform manner, and damage models predict small differences between non‐injurious and full‐thickness burn thresholds.

A one‐dimensional (1D) model was presented in the previous work, and this paper will examine the accuracy of that model for exposures at 5.6 GHz. Of note, none of the model parameters were optimized using new data from the 5.6 GHz exposures. As in the previous paper, CEM43 and Arrhenius damage metrics associated with the temperature rise in the tissue over time are compared to histological assessments of tissue biopsies taken at the exposure sites. Lastly, these exposure levels are compared to the IEEE C95.1‐2019 standard (IEEE 2019) to determine the safety margin between RF exposure standards and burn damage.

Methods and Procedures

Animal Use

Five swine (Sus scrofa, Yorkshire, female, ages and weights shown in Table 2) were used in this study. One was used in a pilot study to establish the range of power densities applicable to skin burn damage levels, and the remaining four were used in the main study of skin burn damage at 5.6 GHz. Animals had access to food and water ad libitum, and were allowed to acclimate to the laboratory facilities for at least 7 days prior to exposure. Analgesia and anesthesia procedures were identical to the previous study (Parker et al. 2024). Briefly, analgesia was administered preemptively with Buprenex (0.007 mg/kg, subcutaneous (SQ)) prior to waxing and Buprenorphrine SR‐Lab (0.24 mg/kg, SQ) prior to RF exposures. Prior to anesthesia on the day of exposure, the animal was induced with an injection of tiletamine‐zolazepam (Telazol, 4–6 mg/kg, intramuscular (IM)) and anesthetized initially with 3–5% isoflurane in oxygen via a facemask and transferred to a ventillator that maintained anesthesia with 1–3% isoflurane in oxygen. For biopsy collection on post‐exposure days, the animals were sedated with tiletamine‐zolazepam (Telazol, 4–6 mg/kg, IM) and maintained under anesthesia (3–5% isoflurane) via mask.

Experimental Setup

All RF exposures were conducted in enclosed, RF‐absorbing, anechoic chambers. The 5.6 GHz microwave exposures used a pulsed 1 MW klystron tube source that emitted 2 μs pulses (i.e., 2 J/pulse). The average power for each exposure condition was controlled by setting the pulse repetition frequency (PRF) for the duration of the exposure. The use of repeated 2 μs pulses to represent a continuous‐wave (CW) exposure for 20 s is justified by the thermal time constants of blood perfusion and heat conduction associated with the RF heating of tissue, which are approximately 500 and 250 s, respectively (Foster et al. 2018). Following Foster et al. each 2μs pulse will generate no more than 0.01°C temperature rise above the expected rise of a CW pulse of equivalent energy, which for the purposes of estimating burn thresholds in skin is insignificant. The RF energy was transmitted through a WR‐187 rectangular waveguide terminated by a pyramidal horn and into the chamber. The RF beam emitted by the horn was redirected 90‐degrees by a custom‐built, parabolic mirror, whose main purpose was to focus the RF energy to a 7 cm full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM), Gaussian spot at the exposure site.

The experimental apparatus used to conduct all of the exposures is shown in Figure 1. With the exception of a different transmitter and inclusion of the parabolic mirror, the setup is essentially the same as the previous study (Parker et al. 2024). The animal was laid prone on an adjustable surgical table and positioned with the desired section of skin at the beam centerline for each RF or sham exposure. The distance from the mirror to the subject was verified within 0.5 cm. All data collection techniques were the same as the previous study.

Once positioned appropriately, the site was irradiated at the selected, time‐averaged, incident power density for 20 s. Sham sites were positioned at the beam centerline, but the transmitter was not energized. After exposure, the subject was positioned for the next site. Once all sites were complete, the subject was returned to the vivarium laboratory, where the positive control exposures, post‐exposure imagery, and biopsies were conducted. All exposures for a subject (RF + positive controls + shams) were completed within a 60–105 min time frame.

Dosimetry

Dosimetry was conducted to establish a nominal relationship between source power and measured incident power density. Using that relationship, the PRF was varied to set the four incident power density levels of 4.4, 5.5, 6.9, and 8.1 ± 0.5 W/cm2, where the uncertainty in incident power density represents an average 1 SD over the four groups. Measurements of the 5.6 GHz field intensity at the location where the subject would be exposed were made using a d‐dot probe (AD‐20R, ProDyn Technologies Inc, Albuquerque, NM) connected to an oscilloscope (DPO72004C, Tektronix, Beaverton, OR). The probe was mounted on a custom holder to ensure placement and alignment was repeatable within 2 mm. The two intersecting laser pointers used to position the subject were also used to verify placement of the probe.

Average power output of the transmitter was regulated by using computer‐controlled changes in the PRF of the transmitter. The transmitter output power was monitored with a diode power sensor (HP‐8481H, Hewlett‐Packard, Palo Alto, CA) connected to a directional coupler mounted before the output horn, with a power meter (HP 437B, Hewlett‐Packard, Palo Alto, CA) used to display the output. The power meter was connected via a GPIB interface to a digital computer to log the power readings. Since the D‐dot sensor could not be in place during exposures, the relationship between the D‐dot and forward power readings was determined each day. This was accomplished by adjusting the anode voltage, which controls the peak amplitude of the RF pulses, to generate a specified peak signal on the D‐dot sensor averaged over several hundred pulses. The forward power was then recorded from the power meter, and the relationship between time‐averaged, forward power and incident power density per pulse was calculated and assumed to be constant for all subsequent exposures on that day. Because the D‐dot signal amplitude was consistent each day, the incident power density per pulse was consistent; and thus, the expected average power density for each exposure could be set by appropriate variations of the transmitter PRF.

Although this dosimetry methodology does not directly measure the incident power density during exposures due to the removal of the D‐dot, the relationship between forward power and incident power density is linear; and thus, once the relationship is determined, incident power density can be estimated from forward power measurements. In this manuscript, the term “measured power density” (Smeas) refers to a power density derived from forward power measurements. The term “modeled power density” (Ssim) refers to power density values derived from fits of the 1D model to the temperature response of skin that has been spatially‐averaged over the pixels associated with the 6 mm diameter punch biopsy. Both power density values are temporally‐averaged over full cycles of the PRF associated with the exposure. Time‐averaged values are used because they are more closely correlated with the thermal response of the skin. Because the time‐averaged values will be auto‐correlated, block averaging is used to determine the standard deviations associated with the mean value.

During this experiment, the RF transmitter output for a small number of the exposures was variable; and specifically, the data from the last subject in the study involved significant variations in the power density to include switching from power on to power off states. A review of the forward power and spatially‐averaged, temperature traces showed that the RF power output had become increasingly variable. Despite these fluctuations, we proceeded with an analysis of the data for three reasons: (1) there was extensive dosimetric data available to characterize the exposures including forward power and IR traces, (2) the subject had already been irradiated and histological samples taken, and (3) the 1D model correlated well with exposures involving time‐dependent, forward power. For the variable power trials, the thermal response of the skin could be fit to the 1D model to estimate Ssim, which was incorporated into damage metrics that could be compared to the constant power exposures.

Imaging Burn Sites

Digital and IR imagery of burn sites was conducted essentially the same as Parker et al. (2024). Digital images were collected with a 35 mm camera (Canon EOS Rebel, Tokyo, Japan). A FLIR 6700 SC (Teledyne FLIR, Wilsonville, OR) was used for IR imagery for the pilot and the first two subjects, and a FLIR 8500 SC (Teledyne FLIR, Wilsonville, OR) was used for the last two subjects. IR thermography images were collected at 125 fps, and the pixel resolutions were 9.0 and 40.8 pixels/cm, respectively. A difference from the previous study was that the position of the IR camera was closer (80 cm) to the beam due to the constraints of the parabolic mirror and subject geometry. As a result, the high intensity pulses from the 2 MW transmitter did cause single line dropouts in the collected IR imagery, and one video file was completely corrupted. For the files with single line dropouts, the resulting imagery was post‐processed with a gradient search algorithm to automatically find corrupted horizontal lines of data and then remove them with a biharmonic inpainting process (Hamelin and Hoang 2018). In the worst cases, (500 Hz PRF associated with the highest fluence condition), up to two isolated single line dropouts occurred on a given frame. Additionally, 65% of the frames collected during the RF exposure would have a line dropped out. Because the frame rate and the PRF values were not integer multiples, the dropped out lines did not occur in the same vertical positions in successive frames, which reduced the risk of systematic bias on the temperature parameters derived from the IR data.

Exposure Trials and Sample Collection

A pilot study involving a single swine exposed to incident power densities of 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 W/cm2 for 15 or 20 s was used to establish the exposure conditions for the main study. The main study involved four swine (N = 4) exposed for 20 s at average, incident power densities of 4.4, 5.5, 6.9, and 8.1 ± 0.5 W/cm2. This span of power densities reflects the measured exposure levels and the variations that occurred due to the transmitter fluctuations. The main study involved some lower power density exposures compared to the pilot to enable determination of lower thresholds of burn damage. As in the previous study, all exposures for all subjects in the pilot and main study were confined to a 40 cm long × 10 cm wide section of skin along each flank, more than 3 cm laterally from the spine. Hair was removed using a commercial hair removal wax and razors. The exposure area was marked into a 2 row × 3 column grid on both sides. Twelve experimental treatments were conducted sequentially (8 RF conditions + 2 sham + 2 positive controls) in accordance with that subject's exposure matrix, viz., the top row on the left, then bottom row on the left, then top and bottom on the right, except the positive control exposures were not completed until the subject returned to the vivarium. The positive control burns involved exposures to brass slugs, 3 cm in diameter and heated to 100.0 ± 0.5°C for a set duration (10 or 13 s) as performed previously. After the positive controls were complete, the 1 h post‐exposure imaging session would commence followed by punch biopsies, again repeating the procedures outlined in the previous studies. The spot size was large enough to allow four non‐overlapping punches to be taken around the central part of the exposure site, one at each time point (1, 24, 72, and 168 h post‐exposure). Once all biopsies were complete, the sites were bandaged, and the subject was moved to its home pen to recover, again in accordance with previous procedures.

For the 24, 72, and 168‐h post‐exposure sessions, the subject was sedated, the wounds imaged, and 12 biopsies were collected. Following the biopsies at the 168‐h point, the subject was humanely euthanized. After death was confirmed, a strip of skin (approximately 1 × 5 cm and 1 cm thick) was collected at each site. The strip was excised through the center of the exposure site which overlapped some of the existing punch biopsy sites. The biopsies were reviewed by a board‐certified pathologist, who was blinded to the exposure conditions and scored according to a 20 factor, ordinal point scale created by Gibson et al. (2020). Burn severity of the sample (as described in Parker et al. (2024)) was classified using Table 3 applied to the rubric scores. Two rules were added to refine the assessments of a) waxing artifacts and b) superficial and partial thickness burns when epidermal injury score = 1. There were two sites, R3 on subject #2 and L3 on subject #4, that had indications of injury to the site due to waxing that made the histology scoring abnormal for the first three time points (1, 24, and 72 h). Those six samples indicated dermal injury but no epithelial injury. The final time point at 168 h for both sites indicated no burn. Thus, the dermal damage scores in the earlier time points were assessed as an artifact due to waxing, and no burn was assigned to all time points.

Exposure Simulation

The same 1D layered, thermally isolated (adiabatic boundary condition), computational phantom of the skin was used to simulate the thermal response expected from the RF exposures as in Parker et al. (2024). The layers consisted of epidermis, dermis, fat, and a terminating muscle layer, assumed infinite. These simulations were based upon an analytical solution to Maxwell's equations in 1D coupled to a numerical solution to the Pennes' bioheat equation similar to other studies (Alekseev et al. 2005; Foster et al. 2020; Christ et al. 2020). The parameters for thermal conductivity (0.6 W/(m ⋅°C)) and perfusion rate (2.0×10−3 mL/s/mL) were determined from fits to the previous Parker et al. study at 8.2 and 95 GHz. Dielectric properties of tissue were acquired from the IT'IS database (Hasgall et al. 2018) and thermal properties were taken from Ziskin et al. (2018).

As performed previously, the simulated temperature response from a 1 W/cm2 exposure of the 1D layered phantom was linearly scaled to fit the spatially‐averaged heating profile from the IR data over a 6‐mm diameter patch of skin associated with the punch biopsy to provide an estimate of Ssim for each trial, which should correspond to the power density at the center of the Gaussian beam. The estimated Ssim is updated at each IR data frame, and the reported Ssim is the mean of these values over all frames. The standard deviation of Ssim is corrected for time‐correlation by using block‐averaging (Newman and Barkema 1999). Each fit was constrained to a single free parameter, the estimated power density on target (Ssim). Because the relative temperature data is accurate within 20 mK and is independent of the RF power meters, these calculations provided a measure of the temporal variation in the absorbed power due to the sum of other experimental factors (e.g., breathing and source variability) in addition to power fluctuations for each trial.

As discussed in the Dosimetry section, the transmitter output was variable for some exposures. To estimate the effect the variable power would have on the thermal response of the skin, the model implementation was adapted to accept the time‐dependent forward power data to scale the thermal output. Nominally, the transmitter was set with a given PRF to generate the selected, time‐averaged, incident power density on target. The forward power data collected the number of pulses during an exposure, which would allow for scaling the output in the event that the number of pulses was not as desired due to transmitter malfunctions. A heuristic rule was defined that if an individual pulse did not have a full width of 2 μs it was deemed to be not propagating, and the power for that pulse was assigned 0 W. An additional concern with the use of the forward power traces was that only approximately one fifth of all pulses were collected during an exposure due to buffering issues. To extrapolate the data to cover the entire exposure, the sampled data points were assumed to be uniformly distributed over the 20 s exposure, i.e., the buffer saturation involved momentary drops in data that immediately recovered. The reliability of these assumptions are evaluated in the thermal response of the model compared to the IR data collected at the skin surface. Seven of the 32 RF exposures exhibited some fluctuations of power during the exposures.

The 5.6 GHz exposures showed distinct differences in burn thresholds compared to the previous studies at 8.2 and 95 GHz (Parker et al. 2024). For both the 5.6 and 8.2 GHz exposures, the epidermis and dermis had similar damage thresholds. In contrast, at 95 GHz, a much higher energy density is required to damage the dermis compared to the epidermis. The transition from superficial to deeper, partial and full‐thickness burns also differs markedly by frequency. At 5.6 GHz, this transition happens rapidly over a narrow power density range (5–6.5 W/cm2, 100–130 J/cm2); below this range, histology scores remain low (0–1), while above this range, scores jump to severe levels (4–5). At 8.2 GHz, there is a transition region occurring between 8 and 15 W/cm2(40–75 J/cm2) that is more gradual than the 5.6 GHz transition. At 95 GHz, the transition is even more gradual as burn severity increases across the entire range of energy densities tested (10–75 J/cm2). Both of these effects reflect the deeper penetration of the lower frequency microwave energy such that when the temperature is sufficient to damage the surface, the subsurface temperature has been heated to a similar temperature by the penetrating energy.

To demonstrate the effect the penetration depth has on subsurface temperatures, Figure 6 shows the estimated surface and dermis‐adipose tissue boundary temperatures after a 1 W/cm2 exposure for 5 s using the 1D model. As the frequency of exposure decreases, the penetration depth increases and the difference in temperature between the surface and deeper tissues becomes less. At 95 GHz, the dermis temperature at the adipose boundary (2780 μm depth) is only 2% of the surface temperature. At 8.2 GHz, that ratio is 51%, and at 5.6 GHz after 20 s of heating, the ratio is 95%. Since tissue damage is related to the temperature history of the tissue, the similar heating profiles throughout the skin at lower frequencies indicate that once damage is produced at the surface, similar damage is likely in the deeper tissues.

The figure highlights the rapid transition from non‐injurious exposures (less than 6 W/cm2) to severe burns (above 6 W/cm2).

Consistent with the previous study, Figure 7 shows that, for these exposures above the safety standard, the transition from healthy to damaged tissue (burns) is associated with CEM43 values of 10–100 (van Rhoon et al. 2013). This should be expected since superficial burns (lowest severity) involve damage to the epidermal layer, which is on the surface of the skin. Thus, only surface temperatures are of interest, and any damage model involving surface temperatures should give consistent results.

Once sub‐surface tissues are involved, which occurs when burns become more severe, the 5.6 GHz data confirms the previously observed frequency‐dependent trend that achieving similar burn severity levels requires exponentially higher CEM43 doses as the RF frequency increases (Parker et al. 2024). Here at the highest fluence, 7 of 7 exposures caused full‐thickness burns at CEM43 = 2×104. By comparison, to cause full‐thickness burns in 75% of the samples exposed at 8.2 GHz required average CEM43 values of 8×107, and at 95 GHz even though the highest fluence caused only 7% full‐thickness burns, it was associated with CEM43 value of 3×1020. Other studies have found CEM43 values for skin necrosis of 288–15,000 (Dewhirst et al. 2003a), which may involve a different endpoint than full‐thickness burn. The exposure durations were 20 s in this study versus 5 s in the 8.2 and 95 GHz studies, but the CEM43 damage metric is designed to account for differences in duration between two exposures (Dewhirst et al. 2003b). The exponentially different CEM43 values for the same damage endpoint indicate that damage models that solely rely upon surface temperatures will be difficult to apply when there are differences in energy penetration depth. This is further supported by the wide range of published CEM43 values for skin necrosis. This study also supports the previous observations that the IEEE safety factors are reduced in the 5–8 GHz band. Full‐thickness burns were likely (75% and 100% of samples tested at the highest two fluence conditions) at levels that were 18–24× the IEEE limit. Even at the lower fluences tested in this study, 25% of the samples exhibited at least a superficial burn at 13–16× the IEEE limit. These are physiological outcomes that are beyond what is considered acceptable in the standard, but at 5.6 GHz, these burns are occurring at levels that are near the 10× safety margin stated in the standard.

Next, the Arrhenius damage metric (Knox et al. 1979) using coefficients from Pearce (2009) is used to predict burn depths for each trial, and those results are paired with empirical, burn severity levels as described previously Parker et al. (2024). The results are presented in Figure 8. The dashed, vertical lines denote the boundaries of the epidermis and dermis in the 1D phantom (at 80 and 2780 μm, respectively), and the colored bands provide visual guides for the regions associated with each histologically determined, burn severity level. The 72 and 168 h time points were selected because, as stated above, the skin usually takes at least 72 h to fully manifest the severity response to a burn insult. The lines and colored bands break up the domain into boxed regions. If one adds a region associated with just the surface (zero depth), then ideally, the plot would have a block‐diagonal shape, e.g., all of the exposures associated with the “partial‐thickness” condition should have a burn depth in the dermis (80–2780 m). The model result here is dichotomous: either predicting no burn or full‐thickness. The model does not predict intermediate severities. The histological results do have some observations of superficial and partial‐thickness burns, but they are the minority of observations. Only 5–9 samples out of 32 (depending upon time point) exhibited superficial or partial‐thickness burns. Of interest, the two exposures involving time‐varying powers (unfilled black triangles in Figure 8) were assessed by histology as minimal burn damage (3 “no burn” and 1 superficial burn results aggregated over the 2 time points). This is consistent with the lower Smeas associated with these exposures. As shown previously in Figure 6, the 1D model predicts nearly identical temperatures at depth compared to the surface due to the larger penetration depth at 5.6 GHz. Since burn damage is correlated with temperature histories (Dewhirst et al. 2003b; Knox et al. 1979), the fact that the model predicts a dichotomous response of the skin at 5.6 GHz is expected. The observation of only a few superficial or partial‐thickness burns in the histopathology further supports the accuracy of the 1D model and damage metrics based upon damage at depth, which lends confidence to the use of the 1D model to predict burn severity.

Empirical Dosimetry

Figure 9 depicts the distribution of skin temperatures in the region of a punch biopsy for typical trials at (A) 95 GHz, (B) 8.2 GHz, (C) 8.2 GHz, and (D) 5.6 GHz. In addition, the blue line in each plot represents the temperature response of the 1D model for the best fit of the heating curve for that exposure. The empirical traces show the temperature history of the minimum, 5% quantile, 25% quantile, median, 75% quantile, 95% quantile, and maximum for the pixels in a region associated with the 6 mm biopsy punch plus any uncertainty in determining the punch location. As seen previously for some of the 8.2 GHz data (Parker et al. 2024), the cooling phase is not well represented by the 1D model, which is demonstrated in Figure 9C,D, representing exposures at 8.2 and 5.6 GHz, respectively. All four plots demonstrate that during RF exposure the heating kinetics of skin is well represented by the 1D model because the response is dominated by the short time scale power deposition compared to the longer time scale thermal conduction. Once the power is turned off, the kinetics are driven by thermal conduction as determined by the spatial distribution of temperature within the exposed volume at that instant. Thus, the differences in the distribution of subsurface temperature due to beam size, surface geometry, layer thicknesses, and blood perfusion become apparent in the response. The FWHM Gaussian beam sizes in these studies varied from 1.7 cm (95 GHz) to 10 cm (8.2 GHz). The flank of the pig at each site could be approximated as a cylindrical hull of approximately 10–20 cm diameter, but could have significant taper along the axial (rostral‐caudral direction). We have collected images of grids projected onto the skin surface to determine the 3D shape of the skin at each exposure site. The punch biopsies at each site will be used to determine the layer thicknesses of the epidermis, dermis, adipose, and muscle tissue. Due to the small size of the 95 GHz spots, but large enough to minimize radial diffusion, and the ease of determining the center of a visually symmetric spot, it is expected that the 95 GHz data would be best represented by the 1D model compared to the other two frequencies. A future effort will extend the 1D model to 3D to determine if the thermal response can be more accurately modeled across all frequencies by incorporating these additional parameters. Variable Power Dosimetry

For two of the exposures, the transmitter output varied such that distinctly different heating patterns were generated. The variations were not controlled due to a malfunction of the transmitter. Regardless, the exposures were recorded and histology samples taken of the exposed sites. These exposures were matched with the IR data, and the variable power traces were used as a source term for the 1D model. The results of the model predictions are shown in Figure 13. The double y‐axis plot shows the temperature rise observed in the IR camera compared to the prediction of the 1D model against the left axis, and it shows the average power trace against the right axis. At two distinct time points (approx. 1 s and 12 s after the start of the exposure) the transmitter output significantly drops and concomittantly the observed and predicted temperatures abruptly stop increasing. After a short duration, the transmitter power returns to normal and the temperature response also increases. The model and the observed temperatures remain within 1°C throughout the exposure over the full dynamic range of the 17.5°C temperature change. This agreement under complex, time‐varying, power conditions provides strong additional validation of the accuracy of the 1D thermal model.

Discussion

In this study, microwave exposures above the IEEE standard on swine at 5.6 GHz were used to determine thresholds for burn damage. Trends in damage were consistent with previous studies that exhibit a positive correlation between dose and burn severity. The empirically measured temperature response of the skin across all power densities tested is in agreement with a 1D model of heating that accounts for the conduction of heat during exposure. This model was applied to the 27 exposures that had valid IR data in this study and there was excellent correlation with the observed temperature responses, R 2 = 0.98, (Figure 12) with a standard deviation of 3%. Additionally, burn depth and severity was found to correlate better with Arrhenius integral values computed at each layer depth using the 1D model than from metrics that use surface values. The temperature histories, forward power traces, and histology results of all swine in the study are available in the Supplemental Information.

When comparing the results of this study to the IEEE C95.1‐2019 standard exposure reference limits for restricted environments, partial‐thickness burns were observed at a non‐negligible rate (minimum 25% of samples) at 13–16× the IEEE limit, which is consistent with the expectation that the limit corresponds to levels that are 10× below any significant health effect. At 95 GHz, partial‐thickness burns were caused at 90× the IEEE limit, and at 8.2 GHz, 7×. For full‐thickness burns, the safety factor 95 GHz is greater than 340×, for 8.2 GHz, 15–22× and for 5.6 GHz, 18–24×. Hence, the safety margins are significantly different over this spread of frequencies. The main contributor to this disparity is that the fluence limit does not apply for exposures less than 30 GHz. Although exposures in the 5–30 GHz band distribute energy over a larger volume of tissue than exposures at greater than 30 GHz, the severity of the injury rapidly changes from superficial to deeper burns with only modest changes in specific absorption. The confounding factors of the distribution of energy over larger volumes and the duration of the exposures appear to manifest in the worst case around 8.2 GHz as those exposures exhibited damage at the lowest multiple of the safety standard compared to 5.6 and 95 GHz.

Additionally, the exposures in this study generated elevated skin temperatures greater than 50°C in 20 s, which are likely very painful (Dewhirst et al. 2003b). But relative to the 8.2 or 95 GHz exposures, the energy is spread over larger volume, and thus the temperature rises are lower than those studies. This would imply that 5.6 GHz exposures at the same power density and duration would be less painful than at higher frequencies, which is consistent with previous studies on warmth sensation thresholds of microwave exposure (Blick et al. 1997; Riu et al. 1997).

These observed effects of skin heating might be of interest to ensure the IEEE safety margins are appropriate in the 5–30 GHz band, when pulsed exposures are much less than the averaging time.
