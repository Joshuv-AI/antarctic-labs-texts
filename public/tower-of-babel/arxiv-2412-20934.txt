Optimal Diffusion Processes
Saber Jafarizadeh

Abstract
Of stochastic differential equations, diffusion processes have been adopted in numerous applications, as more relevant and flexible models. This paper studies diffusion processes in a different setting, where for a given stationary distribution and average variance, it seeks the diffusion process with optimal convergence rate. It is shown that the optimal drift function is a linear function and the convergence rate of the stochastic process is bounded by the ratio of the average variance to the variance of the stationary distribution. Furthermore, the concavity of the optimal relaxation time as a function of the stationary distribution has been proven, and it is shown that all Pearson diffusion processes of the Hypergeometric type with polynomial functions of at most degree two as the variance functions are optimal.



Keywords:

Stochastic differential equation, Diffusion process, Fokker-Planck equation, Optimization

Introduction

Due to random nature of any real signal, propagation media, interference and even information itself,
the
stochastic approach has been one of the popular choices
towards problems associated with information transmission, as well as other applications in physics, mathematical biology, queueing, electrical engineering, economic and finance

[

1

A particularly effective application of the stochastic approach is the modeling of the phenomena as random processes which are the solution of an appropriate Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE),
i.e., an ordinary differential equation with random excitation, due to the presence of noise in the dynamics of the process
[
10
,
3
]
.
The basic idea behind this approach is that the state of the studied process is the solution of an SDE with a priori information, such as
the stationary distribution.

SDEs are generally identified by two main parameters, the drift and the variance functions.
Based on dependency of the drift and variance functions on the previous values of the process as well as the present values, the SDEs are categorized into Itô and diffusion processes.
In diffusion processes (which are the focus of studies in this paper), the drift and variance functions depend only on the present value of the process and their solution satisfy the Markov property
[
4
]
,
which results in a unique stationary distribution for the diffusion process.
Diffusion processes with invariant equilibrium distributions have found applications in approximating the process of sampling from high dimensional probability distributions

[
21
]
.
One of the important factors in such applications is the convergence rate of the diffusion process and its optimizations.

[
9
]

is regarded as one of the pioneering works that has studied the optimization of convergence rate of the diffusion processes.
In

[
9
,
21
]

(and the following works of

[
9
]
),
optimization of the convergence rate of the non-reversible diffusion processes have been addressed, but at the expense of limiting the variance function to be a constant function.
This is unlike the studied presented in this paper, where no such restrictions have been applied on the variance function.

In a nutshell, in this paper, for different types of diffusion processes with given average variance function and stationary distribution,
we have provided the diffusion process with the optimal convergence rate,
which has been possible by adjust the variance function.
In detail, for a given distribution
\pi(x)
,
our goal is
to construct the diffusion process with optimal convergence rate whose stationary distribution is
\pi(x)
.

We
have shown that
the optimal drift function is a linear function and
the optimal convergence rate of the diffusion process is equal to the ratio of the average variance to the variance of the stationary distribution.
Therefore, for all stochastic processes with given stationary distribution
\pi(x)
and average variance of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2
, the convergence rate of the stochastic process is bounded by
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2}{m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}}
, where
m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}
is the variance of the stationary distribution
\pi(x)
.
Furthermore, we have proved the concavity of the optimal relaxation time
(\tau)
as a function of the stationary distribution
(\pi(x))
.

We have shown that all Pearson diffusion processes of the Hypergeometric type with polynomial functions of at most degree two as the variance functions are optimal.
In a table, we have provided the optimal convergence rate along with other attributes for the Pearson diffusion processes. Pearson diffusion processes are diffusion processes
whose drift and variance functions are polynomials of degrees at most one and two, respectively.
Also, we have provided the optimal results for two other types of diffusion processes, namely
(i) diffusion processes with polynomial functions of degree higher than two as the variance function, and
(ii) diffusion processes with hyperexponential distribution as the stationary distribution, which is an example of a diffusion process with a non-polynomial function as its variance function.

2.1
Fokker-Plank Equation

Let’s consider the following time-homogeneous diffusion process (also known as Itô diffusion)
X(t)
,

\begin{gathered}\mathrm{d}X(t)=\mu(X(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\end{gathered}

(1)

where
W(t)
is a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}
, The function
\mu
is the drift coefficient of
X
and
\sigma
is known as the diffusion coefficient of
X
and it can be interpreted as a variance.
Imposing any control term to either the drift coefficient or the diffusion coefficient will transform the diffusion process (
1
) into a controlled diffusion process
[
12
,
11
]
.
The domain of
X(t)
is denoted by
I=(x_{1},x_{2})
where
-\infty\leq x_{1}<x_{2}\leq\infty
.

The process

X(t)
is Markov, and its transition density

p(t;x,y)
is defined as

\begin{gathered}P\left(X(t)\in A|X_{0}=x\right)=\int_{A}p(t;x,y)dy,\end{gathered}

(2)

for
t\geq 0
and any Borel set
A\subseteq I
.
Under regularity conditions, the transition density solves the backward Kolmogorov equation,

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial p(t;x,y)}{\partial t}=\mathcal{A}\left[\mu,\sigma^{2}\right]p(t;x,y),\quad t>0,\quad(x,y)\in I,\end{gathered}

(3)

where
\mathcal{A}
is an infinitesimal generator defined as below,

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{A}\left[\mu,\sigma^{2}\right]p(t;x,y)=\mu(x)\frac{\partial p(t;x,y)}{\partial x}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}p(t;x,y)}{\partial x^{2}}.\end{gathered}

(4)

p(t;x,y)
can be completely characterised by the generator
\mathcal{A}\left[\mu,\sigma^{2}\right]
.
Therefore, it is a functional of
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
and it can be written as

p(t;x,y)=p\left(t;x,y;\mu,\sigma\right)
. Suppose that

\{X(t)\}
is strictly stationary and ergodic, in which case it has a stationary marginal density which we denote
\pi(x)
,

P\left(X(t)\in A\right)=\int_{A}\pi(x)dx
, for
t\geq 0
and any Borel set
A\subseteq I
.
Note that
\pi
is invariant, i.e.

\begin{gathered}\pi(y)=\int_{I}p(t;x,y)\pi(x)dx,\quad\text{for}\quad t\geq 0.\end{gathered}

(5)

Alternatively, the characterization of the transition density can be obtained through the forward Kolmogorov equation,

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial p(t;x,y)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\left(\mu(y)p(t;x,y)\right)}{\partial y}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\left(\sigma^{2}(y)p(t;x,y)\right)}{\partial y^{2}},\end{gathered}

(6)

for
t>0

\begin{gathered}\left.\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\sigma^{2}(y)p(t;x,y)\right)-\mu(y)p(t;x,y)\right|_{y=y_{1},y_{2}}=0.\end{gathered}

(7)

Multiplying (
6
) with
\pi(x)
and integrating over

5
) the following is obtained,

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dy}\left(-\mu(y)\pi(y)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dy}\left(\sigma^{2}(y)\pi(y)\right)\right)=0.\end{gathered}

(8)

If the detailed balance condition is satisfied, i.e.

\begin{gathered}-\mu(y)\pi(y)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dy}\left(\sigma^{2}(y)\pi(y)\right)=0,\end{gathered}

(9)

then the process
X(t)
is reversible wrt
\pi(x)
.
Using the detailed balance condition (
9

\begin{gathered}\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi(x)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\right).\end{gathered}

(10)

All the control effort will be applied to the variance term, since considering (

(

17
]

Using (

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(t;x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi(x)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}p(t;x,y)\right)\quad\text{for}\quad t>0,\end{gathered}

(11)

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{p(t;x,y)}{\pi(y)}=\frac{1}{2\pi(y)}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\sigma^{2}(y)\pi(y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{p(t;x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)\right)\quad\text{for}\quad t>0.\end{gathered}

(12)

By separating the spatial and time variables in (

(13)

\begin{gathered}\left.\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{2}\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}\phi(x)\right|_{x_{1},x_{2}}=0.\end{gathered}

From the classical Sturm-Liouville theory, it is known that the spectrum of

\begin{gathered}p(t;x,y)=\pi(y)(\sum_{n}e^{-t\lambda_{n}}\phi_{n}(y)\phi_{n}(x)+\int e^{-t\lambda}\phi(\lambda,x)\phi(\lambda,y)d\lambda),\end{gathered}

(15)

where the summation is taken over all discrete eigenvalues and the integral is taken over the continuous range of eigenvalues.
The eigenfunctions

The Sturm-Liouville equation (

3
Optimal Diffusion Processes

For a given
\pi(x)
, our aim is to obtain the optimal function for
\sigma^{2}(x)

that maximizes

\lambda_{1}
for given
\pi(x)
, while satisfying the following constraint,

\begin{gathered}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}.\end{gathered}

(20)

The function

\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)
can be interpreted as a variance.
The constraint (
20
) is equivalent to fix average variance constraint. Thus, our optimization problem can be interpreted as searching for the optimal variance function corresponding to minimum relaxation time, among all variance functions with the same average variance.

For a given variance function
(\sigma^{2}(x)/2)
satisfying (
20
), based on Rayleigh Theorem
\lambda_{1}
can be written as below,

\begin{gathered}\max_{Q(x)}\;\;-\frac{\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}Q(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)dx}{\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)\left(Q(x)\right)^{2}dx}.\end{gathered}

(21)

This is equivalent to the following,

\begin{gathered}\max_{Q(x)}\;\;\frac{\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)^{2}dx}{\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)\left(Q(x)\right)^{2}dx}.\end{gathered}

(22)

Therefore, using the min-max optimization

[
6
]
, calculating optimal
\lambda_{1}
, reduces to the following problem,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\sigma(x)}\;\;\min\limits_{Q(x)}

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)^{2}dx,

(23)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)\left(Q(x)\right)^{2}dx=1,

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)Q(x)dx=0,

with the following boundary conditions

\begin{gathered}\left.\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right|_{x=x_{1}}=\left.\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right|_{x=x_{2}}=0.\end{gathered}

(24)

By introducing the relative Lagrange multipliers

\left(\lambda,\nu,\gamma\right)
the optimization problem in (
23
) is transferred to the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\sigma(x)}\;\;\min\limits_{Q(x)}

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)^{2}dx

(25)

\displaystyle+\lambda\left(1-\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)\left(\pi(x)\right)^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle+\nu\left(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)dx\right)

\displaystyle+\gamma\left(\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)Q(x)dx\right),

with the boundary conditions (
24
).
To this aim, we have to set the variation of (
25
) (with respect to
Q(x)
and

\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)
)
to zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle-2\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\delta Q(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)\right.\left.+\lambda\pi(x)Q(x)+\gamma\pi(x)\right)dx

(26)

\displaystyle+\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\pi(x)\delta\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\left(\left(\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right)^{2}-\nu\right)dx+\left.\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\delta Q(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right|_{x=x_{i}}=0,

for
i=1,2
.
Due to the boundary conditions (
24
),
the last term

\left.\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\delta Q(x)\frac{d}{dx}Q(x)\right|_{x=x_{i}}=0

for
i=1,2
.

The optimal
Q(x)
and
\lambda
are
\phi_{1}(x)
and
\lambda_{1}
, respectively.
Thus,
the variation of Lagrangian leads to the following,

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\pi(x)\frac{d}{dx}\phi_{1}(x)\right)+\lambda_{1}\pi(x)\phi_{1}(x)+\gamma\pi(x)=0,\end{gathered}

(27)

\begin{gathered}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\phi_{1}(x)\right)^{2}=\nu.\end{gathered}

(28)

Integrating Equation (
27
) from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
and considering the boundary conditions
(
24
), and the constraints in problem (

),
then

then we can be concluded that

From (

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dx}\phi_{1}(x)=\sqrt{\nu}.\end{gathered}

(29)

Substituting (

\begin{gathered}\mu(x)=\frac{-\lambda_{1}}{\sqrt{\nu}}\phi_{1}(x).\end{gathered}

(30)

Substituting

Main Results

In the following theorem, we state the main results of this paper deducted based on Section

Theorem 1

For given stationary distribution
\pi(x)
and average variance
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2
,
the optimal results corresponding to the diffusion process with fastest convergence rate are as below,

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{1}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)},\;\;\tau=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}=\frac{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}},\end{gathered}

(34)

\begin{gathered}\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{x-m_{1}}{\sqrt{m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}}},\end{gathered}

(35)

\begin{gathered}\mu(x)=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\left(m_{1}-x\right)}{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)},\end{gathered}

(36)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\left(\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)}\left(\left(m_{1}-x\right)\Pi(x)+\int_{x_{1}}^{x}\Pi(z)dz\right)\right),\end{gathered}

(37)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\tau=1/\lambda_{1}
are the optimal convergence rate and the optimal relaxation time of the diffusion process.

\phi_{1}(x)
is the optimal eigenfunction corresponding to the optimal
\lambda_{1}
.

m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are first and second moments of the stationary distribution
\pi(x)
.

\mu(x)
and
\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)
are the optimal drift and variance functions.

\Pi(x)
is the cumulative distribution of
\pi(x)
(i.e.,
\Pi(x)=\int_{x_{1}}^{x}\pi(z)dz
).
From
(
34
),
it can be concluded that among diffusion processes where the average of their variance function is equal to
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2
, the optimal achievable convergence rate is as in
(
34
).
Thus,
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)}
is an upper bound on the convergence rate of the diffusion process with given average variance
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2
and stationary distribution
\pi(x)
.

Lemma 1

The optimal relaxation time
(\tau)
is a concave function of the stationary distribution
(\pi(x))
.

Proof 1

Considering

\begin{gathered}\pi(x)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\pi_{i}(x),\quad\Pi(x)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\Pi_{i}(x),\end{gathered}

where

For the mean and variance of the resultant stationary distribution, we have

\begin{gathered}m_{1}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}m_{1}(i),\end{gathered}

(39)

\begin{gathered}m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}((m_{1}-m_{1}(i))^{2}+(m_{2}(i)-m_{1}(i)^{2})).\end{gathered}

(40)

Therefore,

\begin{gathered}m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\geq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(m_{2}(i)-m_{1}(i)^{2}).\end{gathered}

(41)

Hence, from (

\begin{gathered}\tau\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\pi_{i}\left(x\right)\right)\geq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\tau\left(\pi_{i}\left(x\right)\right),\end{gathered}

where
\tau\left(\pi_{i}\left(x\right)\right)
is the optimal relaxation time corresponding to the
i

\pi_{i}(x)
),
and

\tau\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\pi_{i}\left(x\right)\right)

is the optimal relaxation time corresponding to the equilibrium distribution

5.3
Diffusion Processes with Non-Polynomial Variance Function

As an example of the diffusion processes where their variance function is a non-polynomial function,
in this subsection, we have provided the optimal convergence rate for the diffusion process with the hyperexponential distribution as the stationary distribution.
The hyperexponential distribution is the description of exponential processes in parallel which can be written as below,

\begin{gathered}\pi(x)=\left(p_{1}\eta_{1}e^{-\eta_{1}x}+p_{2}\eta_{2}e^{-\eta_{2}x}\right),\end{gathered}

(53)

where,
p_{i},\eta_{i}\geq 0

p_{1}+p_{2}=1

The hyperexponential distribution is the convex combination of two gamma functions.
First moment and variance of this distribution can be written as below,

\begin{gathered}m_{1}=\frac{p_{1}}{\eta_{1}}+\frac{p_{2}}{\eta_{2}},\end{gathered}

(54)

\begin{gathered}m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}=\frac{p_{1}}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{p_{2}}{\eta_{2}^{2}}+p_{1}p_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{2}}\right)^{2}.\end{gathered}

(55)

The

distribution function of the hyperexponential distribution is as following,

\begin{gathered}\Pi(x)=1-p_{1}e^{-\eta_{1}x}-p_{2}e^{-\eta_{2}x}.\end{gathered}

(56)

Using (
37
), the variance function can be written as below,

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{2}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{2\left(m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}\right)\pi(x)}\times\left(p_{1}e^{-\eta_{1}x}\left(x+p_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{2}}\right)\right)-p_{2}e^{-\eta_{2}x}\left(x+p_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{2}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}\right)\right)\right),

(57)

and based on (

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{1}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}/2}{\frac{p_{1}}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{p_{2}}{\eta_{2}^{2}}+p_{1}p_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{2}}\right)^{2}}.\end{gathered}

6
Conclusions

Considering all diffusion processes with the given stationary distribution and average variance, here in this paper, we seek the diffusion process with the optimal convergence rate.
In doing so, we have shown that
the optimal drift function is linear and
the ratio of the average variance to the variance of the stationary distribution is an upper bound on the convergence rate of the diffusion processes with given average variance and stationary distribution.
Furthermore, we have proved that
the optimal relaxation time is a concave function of the stationary distribution.

An interesting topic worthy of further investigation is the extension of these analysis to fractional diffusion processes.
