Turing covalent organic framework membranes via heterogeneous nucleation synthesis for organic solvent nanofiltration
Authors: Fan Yang, Jing Guo, Chengzhe Han, Junhui Huang, Zhiwei Zhou, Shi-Peng Sun, Yanqiu Zhang, Lu Shao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Although covalent organic frameworks (COFs) demonstrate notable potential for developing advanced separation membranes, contemporary COF membranes still lack controlled stacking and highly efficient sieving. Here, Turing-architecture COF membranes were constructed by engineering a reaction-diffusion assembly process via heterogeneous nucleation synthesis with tannic acid (TA). TA covalently binds with amine monomers to form a composite precursor with increased reactivity and decreased diffusivity. This altered the pathway of Schiff base reactions with aldehyde monomers, fulfilling suitable reaction-diffusion conditions, and ultimately formed the labyrinthine stripe or spot-patterned Turing COF film with controlled stacking and uniform pore structure. This endows our COF membrane with highly efficient molecule sieving ability for organic solvent nanofiltration while exhibiting a flux that is 621% greater than that of commercial membranes. Thus, this study provides a paradigm for the in situ synthesis of highly efficient COF membranes for diversely sustainable separations.

INTRODUCTION

Separation processes are ubiquitous for resource recovery and environmental remediation within various crucial industries, among which heat-driven separation processes involving chemical phase changes, such as evaporation and distillation, account for nearly 8 to 12% of the world’s total annual energy consumption, exacerbating greenhouse gas emissions (1–4). Pressure-driven membrane separation has emerged as a sustainable alternative characterized by a low carbon footprint, absence of phase transition, and significantly reduced energy consumption (5–7). However, to fully realize their potential, the design of nanoporous membranes must adhere to structural precision and incorporate design principles essential for rational performance optimization. Amorphous polymer membranes, which are prevalent in current use, exhibit irregular pore structures and pore size distributions. This inherent characteristic leads to a challenging trade-off between selectivity and permeability, despite years of empirical optimization efforts (8–10). A promising breakthrough in this field is the emergence of two-dimensional (2D) nanoporous covalent organic frameworks (COFs), which are crystalline materials composed of laminar blocks connected by robust in-plane covalent bonds and out-of-plane π-π interactions (11–14). The precisely defined topology and well-organized nanoporous channel structures of COFs facilitate the rapid permeation of solvents through the membrane, effectively mitigating the commonly observed “trade-off” phenomenon in traditional polymeric membranes (15, 16). These distinctive features position COFs as compelling candidates for revolutionizing the design paradigm for membrane architecture.

Facile methods to fabricate thin COF films are necessary in the realm of membrane technology (17–19). Solution processes rooted in surface-induced mechanisms offer notable advantages for the in situ generation of COF films on substrates due to the flexible and precise regulation of reaction parameters without requiring harsh conditions or intricate exfoliation processes (20, 21). In this classical process, the presence of a surface leads to a reduction in the thermodynamic barrier and interfacial energy, facilitating the adsorption of precursor molecules and COF crystal bricks from the solution for nucleation and growth on the surface (22, 23). However, rigid 2D COF building blocks in solution, driven by robust π-π interactions, often lead to irregular out-of-plane stacking and grain boundary defects during the growth process (24, 25). This phenomenon hampers the precise design and construction of defect-free COF films. Although researchers have successfully modulated the arrangement and growth of COFs by adjusting the reaction conditions (temperature, pressure, solvent, etc.) or introducing unique functional groups into the structure, these efforts have focused predominantly on the nanoscale design of COF structures and interlayer conjugation stacking (26–29). A substantial challenge lies in concurrently achieving spatiotemporal control of macroscopic COF crystal brick arrangements on substrates, which is ultimately desirable for maximizing membrane performance. Considering that the synthesis of COFs is a thermodynamically regulated chemical reaction, the Turing pattern design, which is based on reaction diffusion dynamics, might be a promising approach to achieve the spatial regulation of COF films.

In a seminal paper entitled “The chemical basis of morphogenesis,” Alan Turing provided insight into how the coupling of appropriate diffusion and chemical reaction kinetics could disrupt a homogeneous stationary state, giving rise to the formation of temporally stationary, spatially heterogeneous structures (30). This principle has been validated in various reaction-diffusion chemical systems characterized by a non-thermodynamic equilibrium state. Zhang et al. (31–33) applied a mechanism to design Turing-structured polyamide nanofiltration membranes, which exhibit an increased effective separation area and precise sieving capabilities. However, the relatively low rate of reversible Schiff base reactions and the nearly synchronous diffusion of COF precursors in homogeneous solutions during COF synthesis tend to eliminate chemical gradients at reaction-diffusion interfaces, which do not easily satisfy thermodynamic instability conditions. Furthermore, the agglomeration of COF particles in solution is detrimental to the controlled growth of COFs on the substrate. Thus, the application of this mechanism to engineer COF membranes has been notably limited.

In this study, we used tannic acid (TA) as the heterogeneous nucleation site to regulate the reaction-diffusion kinetics for the in situ synthesis of COF thin films with a unique Turing architecture in a homogeneous solution. TA molecules can undergo Schiff base reactions with amino-based monomers to reversibly form a confining complex precursor. On the one hand, the reaction rate of the complex precursor increases because of carbonyl activation and amine protonation induced by TA, whereas the diffusion rate of the complex precursor decreases because of the increased molecular volume and mass. Well-tuned reaction diffusion kinetics allow COFs to grow on the substrate in a spatiotemporally controlled manner. On the other hand, the steric hindrance and charge repulsion of TA transform the COF crystal bricks in solution from flocculent aggregates into uniformly dispersed spheres, facilitating even stacking on the substrate and ultimately forming Turing-architecture COF films with uniform pore structures. The synthesized COF membranes were successfully used for ultrafast and energy-efficient organic solvent nanofiltration.

RESULTS

Heterogeneous nucleation synthesis for Turing-architecture COF membranes

The synthesis of TA/TpPa-COF membranes via TA heterogeneous nucleation is depicted in Fig. 1A. TA was introduced into an aqueous p-phenylenediamine (Pa) solution to form a TA/Pa complex precursor via a Schiff base reaction. The cross-linked polyimide (PI) substrate was then immersed into the TA/Pa solution, followed by the addition of an ethanolic solution containing the organic linker Tp to trigger the in situ growth of the COF membrane. The COF film originates from reaction-diffusion kinetics regulated growth, where the precursor diffuses to the substrate and undergoes a cross-linking reaction at its surface. This leads to the initial lateral growth of the amorphous COF layers. Subsequently, the COF crystals in the solution diffuse, adsorb, and grow on the newly formed layers, progressively transitioning into a more crystalline state due to the reversible nature of the dynamic imine bond (34), ultimately forming the final COF films. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy data of the COF membranes confirmed the Schiff base reaction and the formation of C═N bonds between TA and Pa, as indicated by the broadened O─H/N─H stretching vibration peak at 3280 cm−1, the C═N stretching vibration peak at 1656 cm−1 (fig. S1), and the notably intensified C═N peak at 398.97 eV compared with those of the original TpPa-COF membranes (fig. S2 and table S1). These results demonstrate that TA and Pa formed composite precursors. In addition, the characteristic C═C and C─N stretching bands at 1582 and 1247 cm−1 of the COF membranes align with the structural features of the 2D extended TpPa-COF framework in a keto-enamine–linked form. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the homogeneous surface of the original TpPa-COF membranes (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the TA/TpPa-COF membrane prepared by TA/Pa precursors exhibited distinct striped patterns with highly branched domains, especially at the intersections where the zigzag stripes faced different directions (Fig. 1, C and D). The labyrinthine pattern composed of these stripes topologically resembles typical Turing patterns (Fig. 1E), which generally emerge in far-from-equilibrium systems. This often requires the diffusion coefficient of the inhibitor to be much larger than that of the activator, resulting in a “local activation and lateral inhibition” state (35). We posit that our reaction-diffusion system for in situ COF growth satisfies this condition through the regulation of the TA/Pa composite precursor, which kinetically controls the Schiff base reaction in imine-based COF synthesis while also regulating the diffusion and stacking of the TA/Pa precursor and COF crystals, ultimately leading to the formation of the unique Turing structure.

Formation mechanism of heterostructure via reaction-diffusion kinetics

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the formation of the heterostructure, we investigated the reaction-diffusion kinetics of COF synthesis with TA heterogeneous nucleation. For the reaction process, time-dependent ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra and digital images were obtained to monitor the reaction rate. Compared with that of the TpPa-COF solution, the UV-Vis absorption spectrum of the TA/TpPa-COF solution presented a greater peak intensity in a short period, indicating faster formation of higher COF crystal concentrations (fig. S3). This is consistent with the more noticeable Tyndall effect observed in the real-time digital images of the incubation solutions containing the TA/Pa precursors (fig. S4). This may be attributed to the weak acidity of TA, which enhances the condensation rate by activating the carbonyl carbon and protonating the amine nitrogen. The phenolic hydroxyl groups of TA readily donate protons to water molecules, generating TA phenolate ions and creating a mildly acidic reaction environment. This is supported by the observed decrease in pH from 9.3 to 5.8 and the color change of the precursor solution from light yellow to purplish gray after the addition of TA (fig. S5). As a proof of concept, density functional theory (DFT) simulations were performed for the Schiff base reactions between Pa and Tp molecules with and without TA (Fig. 2A and fig. S6). The mechanism of TA-assisted synthesis is depicted in pathway A, whereas the conventional synthesis process is illustrated in pathway B. These calculations demonstrated a significant reduction in the energy barrier (Ea) for the C─N bond formation step (0.611 eV versus 1.27 eV) and dehydration of the amino radical (1.41 eV versus 2.23 eV) in the TA-assisted synthesis process. This evidence highlights the crucial role of TA in dynamically accelerating the reaction rate of COF synthesis. Faster reaction rates can create local imbalances, where nonlinear feedback and continuous energy input push the system far from thermodynamic equilibrium, facilitating the spontaneous formation of complex Turing patterns (30).

The diffusion process of COF precursors is an essential parameter in tailoring the growth of COF film on substrate. We analyzed the difference in diffusion coefficients between the Pa and TA/Pa precursors through a combination of experiments and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Using a microfiltration membrane to separate the diffusion cells, we calculated the concentration difference between the two precursors before and after diffusion, which demonstrated that the diffusion rate of the Pa precursor was higher than that of the TA/Pa precursor (fig. S7). In addition, mean squared displacement (MSD) analysis was conducted to study the diffusion process of the Pa and TA/Pa precursors in the COF synthesis system. According to the MSD curves, from 0 to 8000 ps, the TA/Pa precursor showed a slower increase in total displacement, with the slope for the TA/Pa precursor (0.43 × 10−3) being significantly lower than that of the Pa precursor (5.11 × 10−3), indicating an order of magnitude decrease in the diffusion coefficient of the TA/Pa precursor (Fig. 2B and fig. S8). This reduction can be attributed to the increased molecular size and molecular weight of the TA/Pa precursor. To visually compare the structural differences, we used the Bondi radii as a benchmark and calculated the precursor molecular dimensions via the matrix box model (fig. S9) (36). The volume of the TA/Pa precursor is ~10 times greater than that of the PA precursor. In alignment with the chemical basis of morphogenesis (30), the Pa precursor and the Tp precursor functioned as activators and inhibitors, respectively. These precursors diffuse across the substrate and react with each other. While no patterns typically emerge in a homogeneous system, a significant difference in their diffusion coefficients can destabilize the system, leading to the formation of periodic complex patterns. We explicated this phenomenon through reaction-diffusion model equations and executed pattern simulations via MATLAB. The assigned parameter values, derived from our chemical system, manifested distinct spatial patterns over time, as illustrated in Fig. 2C. We observed the evolution of the pattern from flat surfaces to nanoscale striped and spotted Turing structures as the numerical values of the diffusion coefficient ratios between the activator and inhibitor gradually decreased in the simulated system (diffusion coefficient ratios of 1, 0.5, and 0.1). This is consistent with the actual morphology evolution of the membrane surface with increasing TA concentration. The SEM images of the membrane surfaces revealed a transition from initially smooth surfaces free of apparent nanoscale particles to gradually forming labyrinthine striped and spotted structures (Fig. 2D). Therefore, we conclude that, when a certain amount of TA is added to the system, the formed TA/Pa composite precursor not only increases the reaction rate, driving the system further from thermodynamic equilibrium, but also results in an exponentially decreasing diffusion rate. This creates an appropriate diffusion difference with the inhibitor, leading to diffusion-driven instability and the formation of a well-defined Turing COF heterostructure. By adjusting the concentration of TA to modify the diffusion rate difference between the activator and inhibitor, different ranges of localized perturbation areas can be formed, leading to the emergence of either striped or spot-like Turing patterns. In addition, we investigated p-toluenesulfonic acid (pTSA), a commonly used catalyst, as a control in the COF reaction-diffusion system (fig. S10). Although pTSA can also catalyze the reaction, it does not chemically react with the Pa precursor. Instead, it interacts with both the activator Pa and the inhibitor Tp via hydrogen bonding without inducing a sufficient diffusion rate difference. As a result, the membrane surface lacked ordered nanostructures, and its rejection performance was significantly lower than that of the TA/TpPa-COF membrane. Therefore, the role of TA is unique to the regulation of the reaction-diffusion process and the mechanism of Turing membrane formation.

Transformation of the morphology and stacking mode of COF crystals

In addition, the TA/Pa complex precursor also altered the morphology and state of the COF crystals in the solution, further influencing their stacking mode on the membrane surface. To investigate the morphological evolution and underlying formation mechanism of the material, we obtained powder precipitates from a synthetic COF solution with varying amounts of added TA. SEM images of the TpPa-COF powder revealed irregular clusters consisting of short rods with an average size of ~200 nm (fig. S11). These rod-like clusters are attributed to the vertical stacking of 2D COF layers along the z axis, which is induced by strong π-π interactions. However, the rigid nature of these COF rods makes it challenging to precisely control their stacking on the substrate. Notably, the introduction of TA effectively defines the growth path of COF crystals. The branched structure of TA provides heterogeneous nucleation sites for the formation of COF crystals in solution, promoting their rapid formation and mitigating π-π conjugated stacking between COF layers. This results in a morphological transformation of the COF crystals from short rod-like structures to spherical structures (Fig. 3A). The FTIR spectra of the TA/TpPa-COF powders with increasing TA concentration revealed a gradual blueshift in both the C═C and C─N peaks of the keto-enamine–linked form at 1582 and 1260 cm−1, respectively. This shift signifies a reduction in interlayer conjugation of the COF by TA heterogeneous nucleation (Fig. 3, B and C, and fig. S12). Consequently, the COF crystals exhibited uniform radial growth along the “TA-anchored points,” resulting in the formation of spherical units. These units subsequently aggregated in solution, giving rise to larger spherical clusters with diameters ranging between 150 and 250 nm (fig. S13). Compared with those of TpPa-COF, the dispersibility and stability of the spherical TA/TpPa-COF crystal particles in solution were superior. We used dynamic light scattering (DLS) to analyze the dispersibility of the TA/TpPa-COF and TpPa-COF suspensions. For the TA/TpPa-COF suspension, the scattering intensity continuously increased with time, and the dispersion transitioned from optically clear to translucent and then to opaque (fig. S14). Monodisperse particle size and distribution analysis demonstrated that the particle size ranged from ~100 to 700 nm for the opaque sample (Fig. 3D). In contrast, unmodified TpPa-COF underwent rapid gelation within a short period, leading to precipitation aggregation, which was difficult to measure through DLS (Fig. 3E). This is attributed to the increased spatial hindrance and charge repulsion of TA/TpPa-COF crystals due to their branched structure and the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups (Fig. 3F). These measurements clearly demonstrate that spherical TA/TpPa-COF crystals exhibit smaller particle sizes and more stable dispersion in solution, allowing them to achieve spatiotemporally controlled in situ growth of COF crystal units on a substrate, forming the final Turing-patterned COF membrane structure (20, 37).

To gain insight into the structural characteristics of the Turing COF membranes, we conducted atomic force microscopy (AFM), Raman spectroscopy, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses. High-magnification AFM images revealed that the stripes observed on the membrane surface were composed of individual nanometer-sized COF grains (fig. S15). The COF crystals grew along the initial heterogeneous structure surface to form raised structures. AFM images of the COF membrane surface reveal these stripes at a depth of ~30 nm (fig. S16). The average root mean square roughness of the COF membranes increased from 1.64 nm to 2.06, 6.49, and 7.32 nm with increasing TA concentration (fig. S17). These raised stripes increase the effective separation area, enhancing the permeability. Notably, these increased stripes and increased roughness do not affect the integrity of the COF membrane, as COF growth is also distributed in the grooves, confirmed by EDS analysis (fig. S18). Raman mapping of C═O in the β-ketoenamine structure of the COF membranes revealed a smaller variance in the intensity distribution in the TA/TpPa-COF membrane than in the TpPa-COF membrane, indicating greater uniformity of the COF distribution in the TA/TpPa-COF membrane (fig. S19). The thickness of the TA/TpPa-COF selective layer is ~186 nm, nearly double the thickness of the initial TpPa-COF selective layer (fig. S20). Notably, no clear boundaries were observed between the underlying substrate and the separation layer for the Turing COF membrane, indicating their tight connection. In addition, high-resolution TEM of the TA/TpPa-COF membrane revealed lattice diffraction patterns, revealing the persistence of some stacked crystalline regions with an interplanar spacing of ~3.4 Å, characteristic of imine TpPa COF regions (figs. S21 and S22). However, the x-ray diffraction pattern of the TA/TpPa-COF exhibited reduced crystallinity compared with that of the initial TpPa-COF. This observation is further supported by the decrease in the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of TA/TpPa-COF (figs. S23 and S24). This suggests that the steric hindrance of TA inhibits the conjugated stacking of the COF, leading to an increase of amorphous regions. These amorphous regions can tightly and flexibly connect the crystalline regions, forming polycrystalline aggregates that effectively block intercrystalline defects (38). Although the pore size of the TpPa-COF structure is ~1.85 nm, the multilayer polycrystalline TA/TpPa-COF assembled membrane structure is able to achieve highly efficient nanofiltration separation.

Molecular separation across composite COF membranes

Compared with the original TpPa-COF membrane, the TA/TpPa-COF membrane exhibited fewer defects due to the controlled stacking of spherical COF and the multilayer polycrystalline structure. The rejection rate of the TA/TpPa-COF membrane gradually increased, whereas the flux decreased with the increasing amounts of TA (Fig. 4A). The TA/TpPa-COF membrane also showed a reduced effective pore size, decreasing from 1.05 to 0.56 nm, and a narrower pore size distribution, on the basis of pore size calculations using poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) as the solute (Fig. 4B). With a TA content of 0.2 wt % in the reaction system, the striated TA/TpPa-COF (0.2) membrane, featuring a labyrinthine Turing structure, demonstrated an impressive water permeance of 49.4 liter m−2 hour−1 bar−1, along with a 99.2% rejection rate of methyl blue (MB) molecules, which have a size of 1.62 nm by 2.03 nm [the optimal concentrations of Pa and Tp were set at 0.5 and 0.05 wt %, respectively (fig. S25)]. To gain insight into the sieving mechanism, the permeabilities of TA/TpPa-COF composite membranes with coating durations of 2, 6, and 12 hours were investigated for water and various organic solvents with gradients of polarity under a pressure of 4 bar. The permeance was plotted against δ/ηd2 [where δ is the solubility parameter (table S2), which reflects the strength of molecular interactions, and d is the molecular size of the solvents] and 1/η (the reciprocal of the viscosity of the solvents). With a coating time of 2 hours, the TA/TpPa-COF (2 hours) membrane exhibited a lower flux and a relatively good linear relationship between the solvent permeance and δ/ηd2 [coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.88] compared with 1/η (R2 = 0.30) (Fig. 4, C and D, and table S3). This can be attributed to the amorphous structures with poor pore connectivity that formed over a short period, where solvent-membrane interactions can significantly alter the pore configuration of the amorphous membranes. Solvents with a higher affinity for the membrane, such as water, can more easily penetrate the membrane structure, expand its pore structure, and thus show greater permeance (39–41). With an increasing coating duration to 6 hours, the crystallization peak intensity and BET surface area of TA/TpPa-COF (6 hours) increased compared with TA/TpPa-COF (2 hours), as a larger proportion of amorphous domains transform into crystalline domains due to the “error-correcting” process (fig. S26) (34). The permeance of the TA/TpPa-COF (6 hours) membrane demonstrated a marginal increase and a relatively good linear relationship with 1/η, attributed to the increased structural rigidity and the presence of permanent nanopores. The experimental results are consistent with recent mechanistic findings on solvent transport in organic solvent nanofiltration membranes, indicating pore flow transport of solvent in the TA/TpPa-COF (6 hours) membranes, where solvent permeance is mainly governed by solvent viscosity, which quantifies the internal frictional forces of flow within the membrane pores and the affinity between the solvent and polymer (42). Notably, when the coating time was extended to 12 hours, the thickness of the coating layer increased from ~85 to ~205 nm (fig. S27), significantly increasing the mass transfer resistance. As a result, the permeation flux of most solvents through the TA/TpPa-COF (12 hours) membrane slightly decreased compared with that through the TA/TpPa-COF (6 hours) membrane. By balancing the crystal structure and selective layer thickness, a coating duration of 6 hours was determined to be the optimal reaction condition (fig. S28). The membranes prepared under these optimized conditions exhibited a labyrinth-like heterostructure surface morphology and uniform pore structure and are referred to as Turing COF membranes in the following discussion.

The separation capability of Turing COF membranes for various dyes [MB, Congo red (CR), bromomethyl phenol blue (BTB), crystal violet (CV), methyl orange (MO), and methylene blue (MeB)] in water, ethanol, and methanol is illustrated in Fig. 4E. The Turing COF membrane exhibited greater rejection of CR [weight-average molecular weight (Mw), 697 Da; charge, −2] and MB (Mw, 778 Da; charge, −2) in both water and organic solvents owing to size exclusion. The molecular weight cutoff of the membrane was ~320 Da according to the PEG rejection results and pore size calculations. In addition, electrostatic repulsion plays a substantial role in solute rejection. The zeta potentials of the membranes were examined to further analyze the membrane separation performance (fig. S29). All the membranes were negatively charged under neutral pH conditions, which were attributed to the large number of phenolic hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on the membrane surface, resulting in better rejection of anionic dyes by the COF membrane. Under aqueous conditions, the rejection of negatively charged MO (Mw, 327 Da; charge, −1) by the membrane was greater than that of positively charged CV (Mw, 408 Da; charge, +1) and MeB (Mw, 320 Da; charge, +1), which have similar molecular weights, as well as uncharged BTB (Mw, 624 Da; charge, 0) (table S4). The high rejection rate is dependent mainly on the synergistic effects of size exclusion and electrostatic repulsion. However, under ethanol and methanol solvent conditions, the rejection of the Turing COF membrane toward these dyes followed the order CR (27.6 Å by 10.3 Å) > MB (23.9 Å by 16.0 Å) > BTB (14.4 Å by 12.1 Å), CV (14.2 Å by 15.3 Å) > MeB (18.5 Å by 8.1 Å) > MO (14.5 Å by 7.0 Å). The order of rejection aligns more with the size exclusion effect between dye molecules and membrane pores, with electrostatic repulsion playing a minor role due to the limited dissociation of ionic dyes in organic solvents (39). We compared the permeability of our prepared Turing COF membranes in methanol with that of recently developed advanced solvent-resistant nanofiltration polyamide membranes, commercial membranes, and emerging inherently porous membranes (Fig. 4F) (11, 43–50). The Turing COF membrane demonstrated superior performance in organic solvent nanofiltration, surpassing state-of-the-art nanofiltration membranes. This finding underscores the high potential of the Turing COF membrane for industrial organic solvent nanofiltration applications.

Membrane fouling poses a significant challenge, leading to performance degradation and a shortened membrane lifetime due to the irreversible adsorption of diverse pollutants. We measured the water contact angles of the TpPa-COF and TA/TpPa-COF membranes to characterize their hydrophilicity (fig. S30). With increasing amounts of TA, the contact angle on the membrane surface initially slightly increased from 39° to 46°, followed by a gradual decrease to 29°. This phenomenon may result from the combined effects of increased surface roughness and the introduction of hydrophilic phenolic hydroxyl functional groups. Consequently, the TA/TpPa-COF composite membrane displays excellent hydrophilicity. Moreover, the Turing COF membrane had a relatively high water adsorption capacity (fig. S31), which was beneficial for water penetration through the membrane while hindering the adsorption of pollutants. To eliminate the influence of dye adsorption on separation performance, we conducted mass balance calculations for solutes in the feed, permeate, and retentate solutions to study the proportion of adsorbed dye solute to retained dye solute (12, 34, 51). For all dyes, the adsorption proportion on the membranes was less than 5.9%. In addition, the MB rejection rate showed no significant change before and after adsorption equilibrium was reached, indicating that the rejection is primarily attributed to sieving rather than adsorption (figs. S32 to S34). There was almost no discernible degradation of the separation performance observed during long-term testing for up to 50 hours with 100–part-per-million MB solutions, demonstrating that the Turing COF membranes have good long-term separation stability. The antifouling property of the Turing COF membrane was studied via the use of humic acid (HA) and bovine serum albumin (BSA) as probe foulants. The results revealed that the permeation flux recovery ratios of the Turing COF membranes were 92.6% (HA) and 89.9% (BSA), and the total fouling ratios were 8.6% (HA) and 10.1% (BSA) after three-cycle filtration tests (fig. S35). The excellent antifouling behavior of the Turing COF membrane toward these hydrophobic and negatively charged biomolecules was attributed to the hydrophilic and negatively charged surface features of the membrane, which alleviated the contact and attachment of the BSA/HA molecules. We further evaluated the mechanical and thermal stabilities of the as-prepared TpPa-COF and Turing COF membranes. The results revealed that the tensile strength of the Turing COF membranes was greater than that of the TpPa-COF membrane, increasing from 16.1 ± 0.3 MPa to 19.2 ± 0.4 MPa, indicating a 19.3% increase (fig. S36). The enhanced mechanical properties of the Turing COF membrane were mainly due to the introduction of TA to reduce the conjugate stacking of the COF layers and increase the amount of amorphous polymer connection domains, thus improving the flexibility and toughness of the membrane structure. Moreover, thermogravimetric analysis curves revealed the high thermal stability of the membranes at 120°C, demonstrating the workability of the Turing COF membrane under harsh separation conditions (fig. S37). The TA-assisted approach allows the COF layer to grow uniformly on various other substrates, including polyacrylonitrile, polyether sulfone, glass plates, and silica nanospheres, without any pretreatment operation (fig. S38). These results strongly support the practical application potential of our TA-mediated COF film synthesis technique.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we designed a heterogeneous nucleation synthesis method to achieve unique Turing COF membranes with controllable stacking and spatiotemporal stationary patterns in a homogeneous solution. Our experimental results, combined with DFT and MD simulations, confirmed the underlying formation mechanism. The introduction of the TA altered the reaction pathway for COF crystal synthesis and influenced both the structure of the COF and its reaction and diffusion kinetics. This alteration can destabilize a homogeneous stationary state and result in the formation of temporally stationary, regular heterostructure structures. Significantly, physicochemical characterization and performance analyses were conducted to validate our designs, revealing sharp molecular sieving properties with remarkable permeation flux. Thus, this work provides a paradigm for the regulation and construction of high-performance COF membranes, demonstrating how the integration of microscale design of COF structures and macroscopic modulation of COF crystal brick can optimize the pore structure and separation properties of such membranes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of the PI substrate

A PI (P84)–supporting membrane was prepared via a conventional water-induced phase separation process. The casting solution was formulated by dissolving 24 wt % of the dried P84 powder in dimethylformamide, followed by stirring for 7 hours at 75°C to ensure complete dissolution. The solution was subsequently degassed for 12 hours at 75°C and then cast onto a glass plate at a thickness of 200 μm via a casting knife. Immediately after casting, the glass plate was immersed in a water coagulation bath, facilitating phase inversion and precipitating the PI membranes at room temperature. Afterward, the asymmetric PI substrates were immersed in isopropanol (IPA) to remove residual water and then cross-linked with hexamethylenediamine (HDA) in IPA (20 g/liter) for 5 hours. Last, the PI-supporting membranes were washed with IPA to remove any remaining HDA and stored in deionized (DI) water until use.

Preparation of TA/TpPa-COF composite nanofiltration membranes

The TA/TpPa-COF composite nanofiltration membranes were prepared via an in situ synthesis strategy. First, we dissolved the Tp monomer (0.05 g) in ethanol solution (50 ml), the TA monomer (0 to 0.6 g) in aqueous solution (25 ml of DI water), and the Pa monomer (0.5 g) in aqueous solution (25 ml of DI water). Then, we added the TA solution to the Pa solution and mixed it well for 5 min with ultrasonication. TA and Pa molecules interact through Schiff base reactions to form a complex precursor molecule. The PI substrate was immersed in the TA/Pa solution for 5 min, followed by the addition of the Tp solution and coating for a certain time (30 min to 12 hours) at ambient temperature. Last, the membrane was washed with water three times and then dried under dynamic vacuum at 60°C overnight. We named the prepared membranes the TpPa-COF membranes and TA/TpPa-COF membranes and named the membranes with different TA addition amounts the (x)TA/TpPa-COF membranes (x represents the amount of TA added to 100 ml of mixed solution).
