J-shaped relationship between physical activity and frailty risk in adults with sleep disorders: the additional protective role of the weekend warrior pattern
Authors: Yang Sun, Min Yin, Libin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty is a multifactorial syndrome associated with increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes. Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as a modifiable factor potentially related to reduced frailty risk; however, its association among individuals with sleep disorders remains unclear. Using data from 2706 participants with sleep disorders in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2018), we examined the association between PA and frailty. PA was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire and expressed as metabolic equivalent minutes per week. Multivariable logistic regression, nonlinear modeling, and subgroup analyses were performed. A nonlinear J-shaped association between PA and frailty was identified, with an inflection point at approximately 1,037 MET-min/week. Below this level, higher PA was associated with lower odds of frailty (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.33–0.76), while no significant association was observed at higher levels. Participants in the highest PA quartile had lower odds of frailty compared with those in the lowest quartile (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.91). The “weekend warrior” pattern was inversely associated with frailty compared with inactivity (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.96). These findings suggest that moderate levels of PA may be associated with lower frailty risk among individuals with sleep disorders. Introduction

Frailty is a multifactorial clinical syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, which predisposes individuals to adverse outcomes such as disability, falls, hospitalization, poor quality of life, and premature mortality1–3. Rather than being limited to aging alone, frailty is now recognized as a prevalent condition across contemporary populations, often coexisting with chronic diseases and lifestyle-related health problems4. In recent years, the prevalence of frailty has risen substantially, making it a growing public health concern across diverse populations5. Sleep disorders—including insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, and circadian rhythm disturbances—are highly prevalent in contemporary society, affecting nearly one-third of adults worldwide6. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that insomnia is significantly associated with frailty, with nearly a twofold increased risk observed in affected individuals7. Impaired or insufficient sleep may contribute to biological aging, dysregulation of hormonal and immune pathways, and increased risk of chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline8,9, which may further increase vulnerability to frailty.

Physical activity (PA), commonly quantified by metabolic equivalents (METs), is a modifiable factor recognized for its protective role against frailty. It promotes muscle strength, metabolic health, and functional independence, contributing to a lower risk of frailty and related adverse outcomes10,11. However, the majority of studies examining the PA–frailty relationship have been conducted in the general population, with limited attention to individuals with sleep disorders. This represents a notable research gap, as sleep disorders may alter the protective effects of PA through multiple biological pathways. For instance, disrupted sleep has been associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, impaired glucose metabolism, and increased insulin resistance, which could attenuate exercise-induced adaptations and may influence the relationship between activity levels and frailty risk12,13. In addition, sleep disorders have been linked to dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and reduced neurotrophic support, such as lower BDNF levels. These alterations may impair neuromuscular adaptation and recovery, thereby weakening the restorative benefits of PA14–16. These alterations may modify physiological responses to physical activity and thereby influence its association with frailty. Despite this, little is known about how different levels and patterns of PA relate to frailty in this high-risk population. In particular, it is unknown whether this relationship follows a linear or nonlinear pattern, and whether different physical activity patterns are differentially associated with frailty. Therefore, we hypothesized that the association between physical activity and frailty may be nonlinear, potentially following a J-shaped pattern in this population.

To address this gap, we used nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2018) to examine the dose–response relationship between PA and frailty in adults with sleep disorders. We further explored potential differences across activity intensity levels and behavioral patterns. By clarifying these associations, this study aims to provide epidemiological evidence to better understand the role of physical activity in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Data source and participants

Participants for this cross-sectional analysis were drawn from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. NHANES is a nationally representative program that assesses the health and nutritional status of the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population using a complex, multistage, stratified sampling design. Detailed information on survey procedures is available at the NHANES website. Data from six consecutive cycles (2007–2018) were combined to ensure adequate statistical power. Participants aged ≥ 20 years with available sleep questionnaire data were initially considered. Sleep disorders were defined based on self-reported physician diagnoses or responses to standardized sleep-related questions.

Participants were excluded if they were pregnant, had missing data on physical activity or frailty variables, or lacked information on key covariates (including demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors). After applying these criteria, 2,706 participants with complete data were included in the final analysis. A detailed participant selection process is presented in Fig. 1. The NHANES study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Variable definitions

Sleep disorders

Sleep disturbance status was assessed using self-reported data from the NHANES Sleep Questionnaire. Participants were asked: “Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following problem: trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or sleeping too much?” Response options included “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “nearly every day.” In accordance with previous studies17,18, participants who reported experiencing these symptoms “more than half the days” or “nearly every day” were classified as having sleep disturbance, whereas those reporting “not at all” or “several days” were classified as not having sleep disturbance. Participants with missing or incomplete responses were excluded from the final analysis. Additionally, to further confirm the presence of sleep disorders, participants who reported having been diagnosed with a sleep disorder by a physician were also classified as having a sleep disorder. This definition allowed for the identification of individuals with sleep disturbance based on standardized self-reported symptom frequency and physician-diagnosed information.

Exposure variable: physical activity

Physical activity was self-reported using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), developed by the World Health Organization (WHO). The questionnaire captures frequency, duration, and intensity of physical activity across occupational, transportation, and leisure-time domains. Moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) was defined as activities causing light sweating or a moderate increase in heart rate or breathing, whereas vigorous-intensity physical activity (VPA) was defined as activities causing heavy sweating or a substantial increase in heart rate or breathing. Each activity was assigned metabolic equivalent (MET) values of 4.0 for MPA and 8.0 for VPA, with 1 MET representing resting energy expenditure. Total PA volume was calculated by multiplying MET values by activity duration and frequency, and summing across all reported activities. Weekly PA levels were expressed as MET-min/week19.

For categorical analyses, participants were classified according to total PA level using a threshold of 500 MET-min/week, which corresponds approximately to current physical activity recommendations (≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity or ≥ 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity per week)20. Based on this threshold, participants were categorized into four groups: (1) inactive (0 MET-min/week), (2) insufficiently active (< 500 MET-min/week), (3) weekend warrior (≥ 500 MET-min/week accumulated in ≤ 2 sessions per week), and (4) regularly active (≥ 500 MET-min/week accumulated in ≥ 3 sessions per week). To further characterize PA patterns, exercise frequency per week was examined within the weekend warrior and regularly active groups. Weekend warriors were defined as individuals accumulating ≥ 500 MET-min/week in 1–2 sessions per week, whereas regularly active individuals accumulated the same amount across ≥ 3 sessions per week21.

Outcome variable: frailty

Although NHANES does not include a direct clinical measure of frailty, it allows for its evaluation through the construction of a Frailty Index (FI) based on the cumulative deficit model. The FI quantifies an individual’s biological vulnerability by summarizing the proportion of health deficits present relative to the total number of potential deficits. The score ranges from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater frailty and reduced physiological resilience. In this study, we developed a 49-item FI, consistent with previously validated methodologies. The selected items covered multiple dimensions of health, including cognitive function (1 item), functional limitations (15 items), depressive symptoms (7 items), comorbidities (13 items), healthcare utilization (5 items), physical performance (2 items), and laboratory abnormalities (6 items) (Supplementary Table S1). Participants were required to have valid data for at least 80% of the items (≥ 40 variables) to ensure reliability of the index calculation. The FI was computed as the ratio of the number of deficits observed to the total number of items considered. In line with established cutoffs, FI ≥ 0.21 was used to define frailty, while values below this threshold were classified as non-frailty22.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous studies and biological plausibility, including sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors that might confound the association between PA and frailty among adults with sleep disorders. Sociodemographic variables included age (< 40, 40–60, > 60 years), sex (male or female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American/Other Hispanic, and Other, including multiracial), education level (below, equivalent to, or above high school), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, or never married), and family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR; <1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5). Lifestyle variables included smoking status (current, former, or never smoker), alcohol consumption (mild, moderate, or heavy), and total energy intake, calculated as the two-day average dietary energy intake. Clinical variables included body mass index (BMI; <25, 25–30, > 30 kg/m²), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, current use of antihypertensive medication, or a self-reported physician diagnosis. Diabetes mellitus was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, use of antidiabetic medications, or self-reported diagnosis of diabetes. CVD was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of any of the following: congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Statistical analysis

We combined data from six NHANES cycles (2007–2008 to 2017–2018) and adjusted the MEC sample weights by dividing the 2-year examination weight (WTMEC2YR) by the number of cycles. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design by incorporating sampling weights, strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) to obtain nationally representative estimates.

Weighted descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as weighted means (standard errors, SE), and categorical variables as weighted counts and percentages. Among individuals with sleep disorders, group differences were assessed using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. The association between PA and frailty was evaluated using weighted multivariable logistic regression, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). PA was quantified as total weekly metabolic equivalent (MET)-minutes and expressed as MET-min/1000-wk for analytical convenience and interpretability. PA was also analyzed as quartiles and categorized into low- and high-intensity levels. Three hierarchical models (Models 1–3) with progressive covariate adjustment were constructed to assess the robustness of the associations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to assess potential nonlinear dose–response relationships between PA and frailty. Threshold effects were further evaluated using segmented (piecewise) regression, with the inflection point (K) identified via a maximum-likelihood approach. Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare linear and segmented models. Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed across demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, BMI, and comorbidities. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1) and EmpowerStats. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings based on quartile (Q1–Q4) PA analyses, two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, participants were stratified into low and high PA groups according to the 2018 U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines. Second, PA was reclassified into four categories (inactive, insufficiently active, weekend warrior, and regularly active) using a 500 MET-min/week threshold and session frequency to assess the consistency of associations across different PA definitions. All analyses were fully adjusted for covariates in Model 3. The results were consistent with the primary analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 2,706 participants were included, comprising 1,444 non-frail and 1,262 frail individuals. The mean age was 45.46 ± 0.48 years, with frail participants being older than non-frail participants (50.40 ± 0.60 vs. 41.98 ± 0.63 years, P < 0.001). Frail individuals also had lower mean daily energy intake (2100.33 ± 40.35 vs. 2170.96 ± 31.01 kcal/day) and lower PA levels (2.97 ± 0.19 vs. 4.59 ± 0.23 MET-min/1000-wk; both P < 0.01). Significant differences were observed in sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics. Frailty was more common among females, individuals with lower educational attainment, and those with lower income (PIR < 1.3). The frail group also had higher proportions of current smokers, low PA, and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), whereas non-frail participants were more likely to engage in regular PA and have normal BMI. Comorbidities, including hypertension (60.27% vs. 39.73%), diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, were more prevalent in the frail group. In addition, a higher proportion of frail participants were widowed, divorced, or separated, while alcohol intake did not differ significantly between groups (P > 0.05). Regarding PA patterns, regularly active individuals were less common in the frail group (15.02% vs. 36.61%, P < 0.001), whereas inactive patterns were more prevalent (66.93% vs. 44.24%, P < 0.001). The weekend warrior pattern was less frequent overall but more common among non-frail participants (5.23% vs. 2.07%, P < 0.001).The detailed weighted baseline characteristics of the study population according to frailty status are presented in Table 1.

Association between physical activity and frailty in adults with sleep disorders

In the unadjusted model, each standard deviation increase in physical activity (PA, per 1-SD MET-min/1000-wk) was significantly associated with a 26% lower risk of frailty (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.65–0.84, p < 0.001). This inverse association persisted, though attenuated, after adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.97, p = 0.019). However, in the fully adjusted model that further accounted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and metabolic factors, the association was no longer statistically significant (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.80–1.02, p = 0.109). The consistent direction of the point estimates across models, despite attenuation, suggests a potential nonlinear relationship that a linear assumption may not adequately capture.

When PA was modeled as quartiles, a nonlinear dose–response pattern emerged. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the second (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.36–0.73, p < 0.001), third (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.31–0.62, p < 0.001), and fourth (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.91, p = 0.013) quartiles all exhibited significantly lower odds of frailty compared to the lowest quartile (Q1). The most substantial risk reduction was observed in the third quartile, indicating that moderate levels of PA may confer the greatest protective benefit against frailty (Table 2). This nonlinearity was formally tested and confirmed by RCS analysis, which revealed a significant overall (P for overall = 0.001) and nonlinear (P for nonlinear = 0.002) relationship. The RCS curve demonstrated a steep decline in frailty risk with increasing PA up to an inflection point, beyond which the curve plateaued and exhibited a slight reversal in trend (Fig. 2). This J-shaped association implies that while low-to-moderate increases in PA are strongly protective, very high activity levels may not yield additional benefits and could even introduce physiological strain in adults with sleep disorders.

Sensitivity analyses results

Sensitivity Analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. When PA was categorized by weekly distribution, the fully adjusted model revealed that both “Regularly Active” (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.31–0.55, p < 0.001) and “Weekend Warrior” (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.96, p = 0.043) patterns remained independently associated with significantly lower frailty risk compared to the “Inactive” group. No statistically significant association was observed for the “Insufficiently Active” pattern (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.62–1.38, p = 0.691) (Table 3). In parallel, when PA was dichotomized into “Low” and “High” intensity according to guideline thresholds, engagement in high-intensity PA continued to show a substantial protective effect against frailty (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.43–0.70, p < 0.001) in the fully adjusted model (Table 2).

Stratified analysis

Stratified analyses were performed to assess the stability of the association between PA and frailty across demographic and clinical subgroups. As shown in Fig. 3, the inverse association between PA and frailty among individuals with sleep disorders was generally consistent across most subgroups, with the majority of odds ratios (ORs) below 1, indicating a broadly protective effect of higher PA levels. Notably, the association was more pronounced among participants who were widowed, divorced, or separated, and those with hypertension, where PA was inversely associated with frailty (OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.92–0.98, p = 0.002; OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, p = 0.016, respectively). These findings imply that individuals facing psychosocial stress or cardiometabolic strain may have a heightened physiological responsiveness to the restorative effects of PA. Although associations in other subgroups—such as by age, sex, smoking, and alcohol intake—did not reach statistical significance, the direction of the effect remained consistently negative. No significant interactions were observed (P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the association between PA and frailty was largely stable across demographic and clinical characteristics.

Threshold effect analysis of physical activity on frailty

To further verify the nonlinear relationship, we performed a threshold effect analysis using a two-piecewise linear regression model (Table 4). The likelihood ratio test indicated a significant threshold effect between PA and frailty (P < 0.001), with an inflection point (K) of 1.037 MET-min/1000-wk. Below this threshold, PA was strongly and inversely associated with frailty (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.33–0.76, p < 0.001), reflecting a robust protective effect. However, above the threshold, the association changed direction, with higher PA levels slightly increasing frailty risk (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04, p = 0.043). The consistency between the RCS curve and the threshold model reinforces the presence of a nonlinear, threshold-dependent relationship between PA and frailty. Specifically, moderate PA appears to represent the optimal range for frailty prevention among individuals with sleep disorders, whereas insufficient or excessive PA may weaken or even reverse the protective effect.

Discussion

Based on a large, nationally representative sample of U.S. adults with sleep disorders, this study identified a nonlinear, J-shaped association between PA and frailty. Moderate PA was associated with lower frailty, whereas this association appeared to plateau and slightly attenuate at higher activity levels. Threshold analysis identified an inflection point at approximately 1.037 MET-min/1000-wk, below which PA was associated with lower frailty and beyond which the association diminished, suggesting a potential saturation pattern. Beyond total PA volume, analyses of PA patterns provided additional behavioral insights. “Weekend warriors” showed lower frailty compared with inactive participants. Subgroup analyses indicated that these associations varied across subgroups, with more pronounced inverse associations observed among participants who were widowed, divorced, or separated, and those with hypertension. Overall, these findings suggest that both total PA volume and its temporal distribution are associated with frailty in adults with sleep disorders.

In modern societies, chronic sleep disorders, characterized by poor sleep quality and insufficient sleep duration, have emerged as a significant public health concern, contributing to an increased risk of frailty through complex physiological mechanisms. Our findings emphasize that, among individuals with sleep disturbances, the optimal range of exercise likely represents a physiological equilibrium between adaptation demands and recovery capacity, thereby maintaining systemic homeostasis and enhancing the resilience of multiple systems. Moreover, the observed benefits among “weekend warriors” suggest that even intermittent yet sufficient PA can confer substantial protection against frailty, underscoring that exercise need not be performed daily to be effective. Practical activities such as brisk walking, cycling, or light resistance training may serve as feasible and effective strategies to preserve functional capacity and mitigate frailty risk in this population23,24. Conversely, excessive exercise intensity without sufficient recovery may induce additional metabolic or neuromuscular stress, potentially attenuating the protective effects25,26.

Multiple interconnected pathways may underlie the observed nonlinear association between PA and frailty risk in individuals with sleep disorders. Sleep disturbances—including insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, and circadian rhythm disruptions—create a physiological milieu characterized by chronic oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation, HPA axis dysregulation, impaired neurotrophic support, and muscle catabolism, all of which are central drivers of frailty progression. First, sleep deprivation impairs mitochondrial function and increases the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby accelerating cellular damage27. In contrast, PA induces mild oxidative stress that activates Nrf2 signaling, enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses, and mitigates cumulative oxidative injury28,29. Second, chronic sleep loss elevates pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)30,31. However, moderate-intensity PA stimulates muscle-derived IL-6, which in turn promotes anti-inflammatory mediators, effectively suppressing systemic inflammation and reducing the inflammatory burden32,33. Thirdly, sleep disorders disrupt the HPA axis and reduce neurotrophic support, resulting in elevated and arrhythmic cortisol secretion and impaired neuromuscular adaptation14,16; as a result, PA can restore HPA negative feedback, normalize cortisol rhythms, enhance parasympathetic activity, and support neuromuscular recovery, thereby stabilizing neuroendocrine and neuromuscular function34,35. Fourth, sarcopenia, a hallmark of frailty, is further exacerbated by suppressed mTOR signaling and reduced growth hormone secretion due to sleep disruption36. Resistance and aerobic exercises activate the mTOR and PGC-1α pathways, promoting anabolic metabolism, mitochondrial biogenesis, and muscle preservation37. Therefore, PA may serve a compensatory role in individuals with sleep disorders by promoting the restoration of multisystem homeostasis. However, this relationship is likely to be nonlinear. Evidence suggests the presence of a threshold, beyond which its benefits may plateau; specifically, protective effects on frailty risk appear to diminish when PA exceeds approximately 1.037 MET-min/1000 wk. In the context of chronic sleep deprivation, regular and moderate PA may mitigate the adverse effects of sleep disturbances by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing chronic inflammation, stabilizing neuroendocrine function, and preserving musculoskeletal integrity, thereby maintaining systemic physiological balance28,32,34,37. Beyond this threshold, these protective mechanisms may reach saturation, such that further increases in activity confer minimal additional benefit. Moreover, high-intensity PA without adequate recovery may offset some positive effects. For instance, chronic sleep disorders can impair glucose metabolism and induce insulin resistance12; moderate PA canmaintain metabolic homeostasis, supporting normal metabolic adaptations38. In contrast, excessive PA may induce metabolic stress, disrupt energy homeostasis, and blunt exercise-induced metabolic benefits39. Sleep disturbances are also linked to reduced neurotrophic support, such as lower brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels15, which are essential for neuromuscular adaptation and recovery. Excessive PA may further deplete these limited neurotrophic resources, thereby impairing neuromuscular function and diminishing its protective effects against frailty-related decline40.

In addition, stratified analyses indicated that the inverse association between PA and frailty was more pronounced among participants who were widowed, divorced, or separated. These adults often face increased social isolation, emotional stress, and a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms, which exacerbate physiological dysregulation, such as neuroendocrine imbalance, chronic inflammation, and impaired neuromuscular function, thereby elevating the risk of frailty41. In this context, moderate PA may serve as a compensatory intervention by enhancing neuroendocrine regulation, improving mood, promoting social engagement, and maintaining the musculoskeletal function. Through these intertwined physiological and psychosocial pathways, PA mitigates the cumulative burden associated with social and emotional vulnerability, rendering its protective effects more pronounced in this subgroup of patients. For instance, Smith et al. demonstrated that regular PA significantly reduced depressive symptoms and improved sleep quality in widowed individuals, thereby enhancing their overall resilience to frailty42.

Similarly, hypertension accelerates endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, all of which compromise physical performance and functional reserves43. Regular moderate-intensity PA can counteract these processes by improving vascular elasticity, reducing blood pressure, lowering inflammatory markers, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and preserving muscle integrity44. By simultaneously addressing these converging pathways, PA exerts a stronger protective effect in hypertensive adults, highlighting the importance of individualized exercise interventions in mitigating frailty risk in populations with elevated physiological vulnerability. Recent research by Liu et al. (2024) corroborated these findings, showing that hypertensive individuals who engaged in regular PA experienced significant reductions in arterial stiffness and inflammatory markers, leading to improved physical function and reduced frailty risk44.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design of the NHANES dataset precludes causal inference, and reverse causation remains possible; for example, individuals with frailty may be less likely to engage in physical activity due to functional limitations, which may partly influence the observed associations. Second, physical activity was assessed using the GPAQ, a self-reported instrument, which may introduce recall bias and exposure misclassification, particularly at higher activity levels. Third, sleep disorders were identified based on NHANES questionnaire data rather than clinical diagnosis or objective assessments, which may lead to misclassification and should be considered in interpretation. Fourth, although comprehensive multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as dietary patterns, medication use, and neuromuscular or inflammatory conditions—cannot be excluded. Fifth, the relatively small proportion of participants with very high physical activity levels may have reduced statistical precision in the upper exposure range, particularly in nonlinear analyses. In addition, the number of underweight participants (BMI < 18.5) was very small, which precluded reliable and stable estimation; therefore, separate analyses for this subgroup were not formally presented. Notably, underweight individuals may represent a clinically vulnerable population with reduced physiological reserve and higher susceptibility to sleep disturbances and frailty, and thus potential heterogeneity related to this subgroup cannot be excluded. Finally, as this study is based on a nationally representative NHANES sample of U.S. adults with sleep disorders, generalizability to other populations with different demographic or healthcare characteristics may be limited. Interpretation should therefore remain restricted to epidemiological associations rather than causal or interventional implications.

Despite these limitations, this study makes several important contributions. First, it identifies a nonlinear, J-shaped association between physical activity and frailty among adults with sleep disorders, with evidence of a potential threshold. Second, by jointly considering physical activity volume and activity patterns, the findings suggest that both the total amount and distribution of activity are associated with frailty. Subgroup analyses further indicate heterogeneity in these associations, with stronger inverse associations observed among participants who were widowed, divorced, or separated, and those with hypertension. Overall, based on a large, nationally representative sample with comprehensive covariate adjustment, this study provides epidemiological evidence of an association between physical activity and frailty in this population. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporality and underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

Among individuals with sleep disorders, physical activity showed a nonlinear J-shaped association with frailty, with maximal protection observed below 1037 MET-min/wk. The protective effect was more pronounced in individuals who were widowed, divorced, or separated, as well as those with hypertension. Notably, the “weekend warrior” pattern was also associated with lower frailty risk, suggesting that achieving guideline-recommended levels of physical activity, even through low-frequency but goal-oriented patterns, may be a feasible strategy associated with reduced frailty risk.
