Comparative assessment of four treatments for post orthodontic white spot lesions over a 24-month period: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Yang Zhou, Qiqiu Wang, Tianqiang Cui, Jianbo Li, E C M Lo, Gaofeng Hao, Fang Huang, Liangyue Pang, Qinghui Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the effects of resin infiltration (RI), casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), fluoride varnish (FV), and fluoride toothpaste on white spot lesions (WSLs) over a 24-month period.Materials and methodsThis study was a 24-month prospective, randomized, double-blind, multicenter clinical trial. Stratified randomization by center was employed, and patients were randomized using allocation sequences, with allocation concealment achieved via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Seventy-nine participants who had finished orthodontic treatment were randomly assigned to be treated with RI, CPP-ACP, FV or fluoride toothpaste and were recalled every 6 months. The subjects in the RI group who received the RI treatment at baseline and had their teeth coated with fluoride toothpaste every six months starting from the second visit. In addition, all the participants used the provided fluoride toothpaste to brush their teeth twice daily for routine oral hygiene. The DMFT index was recorded at baseline and at each visit. Intraoral images were captured, and the changes in the WSLs area were assessed by ImageJ software.ResultsIn this study, we enrolled 20 participants in the FV group, CPP-ACP group, and RI group and 19 participants in the fluoride toothpaste group. Ultimately, 14, 17, 12, and 14 participants in each group completed the final follow-up, respectively. After 24 months, there were no significant differences in the mean DMFT index between the groups. All four treatments effectively reduced the WSLs over time. After 24 months, the rate of change in WSLs area in the RI group (-56.66 ± 20.70, 95%CI: -62.23, -51.09) was significantly lower than those in the FV group (-42.01 ± 18.12, 95%CI: -46.28, -37.75), the CPP-ACP group (-45.27 ± 18.13, 95%CI: -49.83, -40.70) and the fluoride toothpaste group (-35.19 ± 25.79, 95%CI: -40.99, -29.38). Besides, the rate of change in WSLs in the CPP-ACP group was significantly lower than that in the fluoride toothpaste group.ConclusionsDuring the 24-month period, the combined use of RI, FV, CPP-ACP with fluoride toothpaste and fluoride toothpaste alone were effective in treating WSLs. RI management achieved the best and most stable effect in terms of reducing WSLs after 24 months. In addition, after 24 months, CPP-ACP management was better than fluoride toothpaste management alone for reducing WSLs.RegistryChinese Clinical Trials Register, TRN: ChiCTR2000032516, Registration date: 1 May 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08740-6.Keywords: White spot lesions, Casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate, Resin infiltration, Fluoride varnish, Fluoride toothpaste

Background

White spot lesions (WSLs) represent the initial indication of the caries process caused by demineralization, and they are a common complication of orthodontic therapy [1].

These lesions most commonly present as milky white opacities on the labial surface of teeth [2], which are clinically evident to the naked eye when the teeth are dried [3]. WSLs can occur due to plaque accumulation over time, as well as several other factors, including nutrition, calcium, phosphate, bicarbonate, and fluoride levels in saliva, and hereditary factors [4]. Sustained plaque deposition causes the enamel surface to become microporosified by the acidic metabolites of bacteria, which leads to the formation of WSLs [5].

WSLs are frequently found in individuals following fixed orthodontic procedures, owing to plaque deposition induced by brackets and bands [4]. The prevalence of WSLs in orthodontic patients has been reported to be as high as 96% [6–9], depending on the detection method. The removal of fixed appliances may not fully resolve these lesions, and untreated WSLs can result in dental caries and require restorative treatments [10].

The early stage of WSLs can be remineralized or arrested [11], and the main principle underlying the treatment of WSLs is to promote spontaneous remineralization while hindering further demineralization [12]. Patients with WSLs can be treated using a range of techniques, such as topical fluoride treatments, casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), and resin infiltration (RI) therapy [4, 13]. A systematic review [14] indicated that 5% sodium fluoride varnish can enhance the remineralization of WSLs, whereas another systematic review [15] revealed that all the substances supplemented with fluoride contribute to the remineralization process. Fluoride-based approaches are the gold standard for preventing and controlling WSLs [16, 17]. It is well known that fluoride toothpaste can reduce the incidence and prevalence of dental caries [18]. Fluoride toothpaste at a concentration of 1100 µg F/g can effectively reduce enamel demineralization [19]. Remineralizing agents with 5% fluorine or CPP-ACP can be utilized as noninvasive therapies to address WSLs in the early stages [20]. However, owing to the agent’s limited ability to penetrate the outer layer of enamel, this minimally invasive method may leave a discoloured area untreated [4]. RI treats WSLs with low-viscosity light-cured resin, which permits the resin to infiltrate into the hydrochloric acid-etched WSLs by capillary action, halting the progression of demineralization by blocking the channel of acids created by bacteria [21]. Indeed, RI has been proven to be an effective and minimally invasive treatment for WSLs [22, 23].

These four methods are commonly used in the clinical treatment of WSLs [24, 25]. Studies have shown that RI therapy is superior to topical fluoride in preventing enamel lesions [26]. A systematic review and meta-analysis [27] also revealed that RI is superior to natural remineralization and fluoride varnish (FV) because of its masking effect. However, these conclusions are based on the results of very few controlled trials that lasted less than 12 months. In addition, one significant disadvantage of resin composite restorations is their tendency to undergo discoloration, stain accumulation, and experience a reduction in surface gloss over time when exposed to the oral environment [28]. Thus, a well-designed randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating various treatments for WSLs over a longer period is needed. Our previous study [29] revealed that RI can lead to immediate improvements in dental aesthetics and maintain improvement for more than 12 months. RI achieved superior results compared with FV, CPP-ACP, and fluoride toothpaste. The long-term effects of the four treatments remain to be observed beyond 12 months. The current study was an update of an RCT aimed at evaluating and comparing the effects of RI, FV, CPP-ACP, and the use of fluoride toothpaste alone in the treatment of WSLs over a 24-month period.

Methods

Trial design

The study was a 24-month prospective, randomized, double-blind, multicenter clinical trial. We aimed to evaluate various WSL therapies in seventy-nine young people aged 12 to 25 years who had undergone orthodontic therapy resulting in WSLs in their anterior teeth. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (KQEC-2020-01) and followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The areas of WSLs were determined using ImageJ (version 1.52e; National Institutes of Health). The trial has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trials Register (ChiCTR2000032516).

Sample size

On the basis of the mean values and standard deviations of previous studies [30, 31], sample size was calculated using a repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) framework, with group as the between-subject factor and time as the within-subject factor. A between-group difference of 8 units in mean change from baseline to 24 months, a standard deviation of 7, and a correlation coefficient of 0.5 between time points were assumed. A two-sided type I error rate of 0.05 and a power of 90% were applied. An inflation factor k = 1.1 was applied to account for multiple comparisons among the four groups in the repeated-measures ANOVA design. Given that 4 teeth were assessed for each participant, the data had a clustered structure. The sample size was adjusted for clustering using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.10, yielding a design effect of 1.30. After adjustment, 47 teeth per group (12 participants per group) were needed. To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate and the COVID-19 pandemic, no fewer than 18 subjects in each group were needed.

Participant recruitment and study settings

A total of 136 patients who had finished orthodontic treatment were screened at four hospitals, namely, the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University, the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Southern Medical University, Foshan Stomatological Hospital, and the Oral Disease Prevention Center of Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare. Follow-up examinations were performed from 2020 to 2022. The selection criteria listed below were as follows: 12 to 25 years of age, good general health, and at least 2 anterior teeth with a WSL, coded as 2 in the ICDAS on the buccal surface. Patients with a history of smoking, systemic diseases or syndromes were excluded from the study. Patients with verified milk protein or fluoride allergies and women who were pregnant or breastfeeding, were also excluded.

Randomization, intervention procedure, and blinding

This study used stratified randomization, stratifying by center and randomly assigning participants within each center. An independent assistant prepared the allocation sequence prior to the trial and stored it in opaque, sealed envelopes with sequential numbers. A dental assistant distributed envelopes to the patients at random. The intervention allocation was then visible after the sealed envelope was opened. The data analyst and subjects were both blinded.

The patients were randomly divided into four groups depending on the envelopes they received. For Groups 1, 2, and 4, the WSLs of the patients were treated with 5% sodium FV (Duraphat, Colgate, USA), a fluorine-free CPP-ACP mousse (GC Tooth Mousse, GC, Japan), and fluoride toothpaste (containing 1400 ppm fluoride), respectively, every 6 months. The fluoride toothpaste group was the control group. Following the removal of the orthodontic wires and auxiliaries, the teeth were isolated using cotton rollers. After the use of a disposable brush applicator to apply a thin layer of agent, the patients were told to avoid eating or drinking for 1 h. The teeth of the patients in Group 3 with WSLs were etched for 2 min using an etch gel containing 15% phosphoric acid, followed by thorough rinsing. The lesions were then injected with 99% ethanol for 30 s, followed by 3 min of resin infiltration (Icon infiltration resin, DMG, Germany). After the excess material was removed, the infiltrant was cured with LED light for 40 s and then applied again for 60 s, after which the excess material was removed again, and a final curing period of 60 s was held, after which the teeth were polished. Following baseline RI treatment, the lesions received fluoride toothpaste treatment every six months beginning at the second visit.

Every participant was instructed to brush their teeth twice daily using the allocated toothbrushes (CREST 772561, Procter & Gamble) and fluoride toothpaste (1400 ppm fluoride, Colgate 346416, Colgate-Palmolive). The dental hygienist instructed all the participants on product use and oral hygiene, both verbally and in writing. After each visit, the participants were asked to abstain from eating and drinking for 1 h and eat soft food for 4 h.

Questionnaire

At baseline, the patients’ socioeconomic backgrounds and oral health-related behaviors were examined using a modified validated questionnaire [14]. Snacking habits, regular dental brushing routines, flossing, and fluoride toothpaste use were among the topics covered.

Study procedure and outcomes

For each subject, intraoral images of the anterior teeth were captured. The rate of change in the WSL area was assessed by ImageJ software (version 1.52e; National Institutes of Health) [29], and was the primary outcome measure. After the images of the WSLs were obtained, the lesion boundaries on the corresponding tooth surfaces were outlined to determine the lesion areas. The percentage of the affected area was then calculated by dividing the WSLs area by the total tooth area [29].The DMFT index was recorded at baseline and at each visit. The assessment of the DMFT index was the secondary outcome. The hypothesis to be tested was that there were no differences in the changes in the WSLs area or DMFT index between the groups.

Examiner calibration

Every center was assigned one examiner responsible for assessing the oral examination indicators. The examiners were all specialists, with more than five years of professional experience, including one woman and three men. The assessors received initial training and calibration prior to the trial. Cohen’s kappa values ranged from 0.668 to 0.807 at baseline when the DMFT was examined. At each follow-up visit, measurements were repeated for 10 to 20% of the participants to evaluate the consistency of the examiners’ criteria. Following the data collection, the data analyst randomly selected digital oral photographs of 10 participants and re-evaluated them one week later to perform a consistency test. In this study, the Cohen’s kappa values for each examination at baseline, 12-month follow-up, and 24-month follow-up ranged from 0.668 to 0.850, 0.659 to 0.910, and 0.662 to 0.916, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All the data were processed and analyzed with SPSS (version 21.0; Chicago, IL, USA) and R program (version 4.1.3). For each variable, the mean and standard deviation were determined for each group. DMFT scores were summarized and analyzed at the participant level, whereas the change rate in WSL area was analyzed at the tooth level.

For participant-level outcomes (DMFT), owing to the repeated measurement of different indicators DMFTat three time points for the same individual in different groups, one-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied to evaluate group effects, time effects, and group-by-time interactions. Normality, homogeneity of variance, and sphericity assumptions were examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test, Levene’s test, and Mauchly’s test, respectively. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when sphericity was violated. If a significant group-by-time interaction was detected, simple effect analyses were performed, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) method for multiple comparisons.

For tooth-level outcomes, the rate of change in WSLs between the groups was compared using one- way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In addition, generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were used to account for the clustering of multiple teeth within participants, with participant ID as the cluster variable. A first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) working correlation matrix was specified, which is appropriate for longitudinal dental data. A positive regression coefficient indicates an increase in the outcome, whereas a negative coefficient indicates a reduction in the outcome. Variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate models. Partial eta squared is a statistical measure used to determine the effect size of different variables in ANOVA models. A partial eta squared value of 0.01 indicates a small effect, 0.06 indicates a medium effect, and 0.14 indicates a large effect. Assessment of WSLs changes

A total of 274 teeth were included in the analysis. The major endpoint of the four therapies was arresting or decreasing the size of WSLs area as measured by ImageJ. The WSLs area in all groups during follow-up are shown in Table S1. We found that all four treatments effectively reduced the WSLs over time. At baseline, the percentage of the WSLs area in the FV group (30.69) was greater than that in the CPP-ACP group (24.70, p = 0.002) and fluoride toothpaste group (26.28, p = 0.04). Thus, we analyzed the rate of change in WSLs area for between-group comparisons. In Table 2, after 12 months, the rate of change in WSLs area in the RI group (-46.62%) was significantly lower than that in the FV group (-25.86%, p < 0.001), the CPP-ACP group (-29.05%, p < 0.001) and the fluoride toothpaste group (-24.13%, p < 0.001). After 24 months, the therapeutic effect for reducing WSLs in the RI group (Fig. 2) was superior to that of the FV group (Fig. S1), CPP-ACP group (Fig. S2), and fluoride toothpaste group (Fig. S3). The change rate of WSLs area in the RI group (-56.66%) was significantly lower than that in the FV group (-42.01%, p < 0.001), the CPP-ACP group (-45.27%, p = 0.002) and the fluoride toothpaste group (-35.19%, p < 0.001). In addition, the rate of change in WSLs area in the CPP-ACP group (-45.27%) was significantly lower than that of the fluoride toothpaste group (-35.19%, p = 0.008) after 24 months. No statistically significant change was detected between the 12-month and 24-month follow-up(p = 0.21).

Considering the clustering effect of the data, GEE modelling was utilized to minimize the impact of confounding factors on the rate of change in the WSLs area [32]. The results of variance analysis of rate of change in the WSLs showed that age, group assignment, timing, DMFT index and consumption of sweets or sugary drinks were significant variables, with p values of less than 0.2. In the final GEE model (see Table 3), age, group assignment, and timing were statistically significant (p < 0.05). The RI group had a correlation coefficient (β) of -0.186 which indicated that the percentage decrease in the WSLs area was greater than that in the fluoride toothpaste group. The correlation coefficient (β) for timing was − 0.150, which again indicated that the treatments were more effective over time. In addition, the correlation coefficient (β) for age was 0.008 (p = 0.024).

Discussion

This trial assessed the effectiveness of four treatments, including RI, FV, CPP-ACP, and fluoride toothpaste alone, in the treatment of post orthodontic WSLs in vivo over a 24-month period. The results showed that, after 24 months, all four treatments were effective in reducing WSLs, and RI application was superior to the other treatments. Notably, the effectiveness of CPP-ACP application in reducing WSLs was significantly better than that of fluoride toothpaste alone after 24-month follow-up.

WSLs are the first step of the caries process and are characterized by subsurface enamel porosity resulting from carious demineralization [7]. Several therapies, such as microabrasion, fluoride-based products, CPP-ACP, and RI technique, have been documented to prevent possible WSL progression [4, 24, 33, 34]. However, there is no widely accepted therapy. To date, most of the studies compared the effectiveness of one or two treatments to evaluate changes in WSLs. Recently, ZZ Çiftçi et al. [35] found that RI was more successful for treating WSLs than fluoride varnish during a 3-month period. A systematic review showed that CPP-ACP demonstrated a strong ability to promote the remineralization capacity of WSLs, with no statistically significant difference compared with fluoridated toothpaste and fluoride varnish [8]. Another meta-analysis [4] revealed that the RI approach appears to be the most reliable and efficient treatment for the aesthetic resolution of WSLs, compared to remineralization and microabrasion treatments, which is consistent with our findings. However, the majority of the meta-analyses examined in this review are in vitro studies. Furthermore, resin composites have been shown to have several limitations, including marginal leakage, limited wear resistance, diminished color stability and a tendency for discoloration [36]. Considering the differences of opinion among researchers who have performed different studies about the various treatments for WSLs, as well as the lack of long-term follow-up studies of more than 12 months comparing the effectiveness of different therapies for treating WSLs, we conducted a 24-month follow-up RCT to compare the four treatments for WSLs. The study showed that RI treatment resists material ageing caused by the oral environment over a 24-month period, which is consistent with our previous in vitro study [37].

The DMFT index was used to evaluate caries development. We found that the DMFT index in all groups increased over time, which suggested that enhanced oral health instruction and education are needed in future clinical studies.

In the present study, we found that WSLs decreased in all groups over time, and the therapeutic effect of RI was observed to be greater than that of the other treatments after 24 months. CPP-ACP and FV primarily work through remineralization to reduce WSLs, whereas RI acts via immediate penetration of the resin into demineralized dental hard tissues, followed by further remineralization facilitated by fluoride toothpaste. Our study indicates that dental hard tissues are capable of continued remineralization following resin infiltration treatment. Furthermore, our results revealed that, the percentage decrease in WSLs area in the CPP-ACP group was significantly higher than that in the fluoride toothpaste group after 24 months, indicating that CPP-ACP may take longer than 12 months for CPP-ACP to exert a better remineralizing effect on WSLs. However, other studies [31, 38] showed that CPP-ACP could reduce the size of WSLs in less than 12 months when applied twice per day. One of the limitations in our study may be the insufficient frequency of CPP-ACP application, which suggested that a higher frequency might promote the therapeutic effect of CPP-ACP on WSLs. In future studies, we intend to increase the frequency of CPP-ACP application to examine its effectiveness and the duration required for it to exert its effects. In addition, the synergistic effect of CPP-ACP with fluoride was reported by Mendes et al. [39], and showed that the remineralizing effect of CPP-ACP on WSLs can be improved when combined with fluoride. This was consistent with our study, as we found that the combination therapies of CPP-ACP and fluoride toothpaste achieve better remineralization effects on WSLs than using fluoride toothpaste alone. There are some possible explanations of this result. CPP-ACP can combine with fluorine to form CPP-ACPF, resulting in the increased incorporation of fluoride ions into plaque, along with elevated levels of bioavailable calcium and phosphate ions, thereby promoting remineralization [40].

This study involved multifactor analysis, which can reduce the impact of confounding variables on the findings. Additionally, considering that WSLs were measured multiple times on the same tooth of one person, GEE modelling was utilized to reduce the clustering effect. The results of the GEE modelling showed that a long period of RI treatment could significantly reduce WSLs, while older age may decrease WSLs reduction.

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of our study were its long follow-up period (24 months) and use of microinvasive methods to reduce WSLs area. However, the main limitation of our study was the high drop-out rate in the study, although there was no significant difference between the drop-out rates of the groups. The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have prevented the timely follow-up of subjects. In the 12-month and 24-month follow-up periods, four more people in the RI group were lost to follow-up. Through telephone inquiries, we learned that three of these patients dropped out because they were satisfied with the therapeutic effect on their WSLs and were unwilling to continue follow up. Given the dropout rate, the intention-to-treat analysis was not feasible due to the lack of outcome data for the participants lost to follow-up. This study was conducted using a per-protocol analysis. However, per-protocol analysis may introduce bias by excluding participants who were lost to follow-up or deviated from the study protocol, which may compromise the internal validity and the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, during the 24-month period, the combined use of RI, FV, CPP-ACP with fluoride toothpaste and fluoride toothpaste alone were effective for reducing WSLs. RI management achieved the best and most stable effect in reducing WSLs after 24 months. In addition, after 24 months, CPP-ACP management was better than fluoride toothpaste alone at reducing WSLs.

Clinical relevance

In this study, four WSLs treatments were compared for 24 months, and compared with fluoride toothpaste alone, RI significantly reduced the lesion area at 24 months. However, owing to the high dropout rate, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger-scale trials with sufficient sample sizes are needed in the future to verify the long-term efficacy of these interventions.
