A study of the utilization profile of donor corneas with a death-to-retrieval time of more than twelve hours
Authors: Parul Jain, Umesh Sharma, Gahan Reddy, Isha Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of the paper is to study the utilization profile of donor corneas with death-to-retrieval time (DRT) >12 h in the Indian population and to assess the safety of endothelial keratoplasty using such corneas.Methods:This retrospective study (Jan 2018–June 2023) analyzed eye bank records of donor corneas with DRT ≥12 h. The primary outcome assessed was the utilization rate across different keratoplasties. Secondary outcomes included records of endothelial cell density (ECD) of donor tissue. Records of outcomes of endothelial keratoplasty using such corneas in the form of postoperative recorded visual acuity, graft clarity, postoperative ECD, signs of graft infection, and failure were evaluated.Results:Out of 333 donor corneas, 75.68% were suitable for transplantation, 21.3% were allocated for research, and 3% were unfit. Among transplantable corneas, 42.04% were used for optical penetrating keratoplasty (PK), 15.9% for Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty, 9.9% for therapeutic PK, 4.5% for deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty, 1.5% for patch grafts, 1.2% for tectonic PK, and 0.3% each in Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty and tuck in lamellar keratoplasty. Mean ECD for endothelial keratoplasty suitable corneas was 2633 ± 291 cells/mm². Minimum 6 months follow-up records of endothelial keratoplasty showed that 91% achieved best-corrected visual acuity ≥6/18 and ≥6/12 in 60%. The maximum follow-up period was 4 years. Mean ECD declined to 1857.02 ± 133.11 cells/mm² (P < 0.0001). Graft rejection was recorded in 13.34%, elevated intraocular pressure in 18%, and graft infections in none.Conclusion:Properly screened corneas with DRT >12 h lead to successful endothelial keratoplasty outcomes. Traditional DRT limits should be re-evaluated, especially in regions with a shortage of donor cornea. Preservation quality, ECD, and preoperative assessment are critical.

Corneal blindness is a leading cause of preventable blindness in India.[1] Currently, there are 1.2 million cases of corneal blindness in India.[2] This constitutes 0.36% of total blindness cases.[2] About 25,000–30,000 people are being added every year to this pool.[2] Around 50% of these cases are potentially treatable.[34] There is a significant gap between the demand and supply of donor corneas.[1]

The utilization rates of donor corneas range from 25 to 60%.[14] Factors affecting the success of corneal transplant include donor age, condition of the cornea at retrieval, time from death to retrieval and preservation, the presence of surface diseases, and endothelial function.[5] Traditional practices in Indian eye banks typically favor donors who are below 60 years of age, with a preference for death-to-retrieval time (DRT) and death preservation time (DPT) both being less than 6 h.[56] This is based on the belief that after death, the viability of corneal tissues decreases as tear production stops, tissue degrades, and microbial changes occur.[7] Therefore, a longer DPT can increase the chances of graft failure in the postoperative period.[7]

There is a potential advantage in challenging the established dogma of a 6-h DRT to reduce the number of corneas rejected for transplantation.[5] A study by Armitage et al.[3] suggested that DPT and DRT do not influence the suitability of corneas for penetrating keratoplasty (PK).[8] However, similar research for endothelial keratoplasty (EK) is limited. EK requires a good-quality donor cornea with a high endothelial cell density (ECD), ideally above 2300 cells/mm², as recommended by the Cornea Preservation Time Study (CPTS).[1] Recent international evidence also supports the safe use of tissues with prolonged postmortem intervals. Hofmann et al.[1] demonstrated that corneas retrieved and processed up to 72 h postmortem maintained transplant quality,[9] while Shehab et al.[9] reported no significant impact of extended DRT on outcomes of posterior lamellar keratoplasty.[10] Furthermore, the Council of Europe’s 2023 guidelines allow DRT up to 24 hours and DPT up to 48 hours when tissue quality standards are met.[11]

The effectiveness of tissue with longer DRT for EK would mean that such tissues maintained their quality and endothelial density despite a longer retrieval time. This could potentially broaden the donor pool and, therefore, increase the utilization of donor corneas.

Methods

Primary outcome assessed was the utilization rate of donor tissues with DRT >12 h across penetrating, endothelial, and therapeutic keratoplasties. Secondary outcomes assessed were the records of ECD of donor tissue. Records of outcomes of EK in the form of postoperative recorded visual acuity, graft clarity, postoperative ECD, signs of graft infection, and failure were evaluated. Factors such as graft edema, ciliary congestion, and swelling were recorded in cases of graft failure. Signs such as graft infiltrates, epithelial defect with stromal haze, loss of graft clarity, anterior chamber reaction (cells, flare, or hypopyon), congestion, and discharge were evaluated for graft infection. Microbiological examination records of graft infection cases, if any, were evaluated. Records of patients with a minimum completed follow-up of 6 months after EK were evaluated for this study.

DRT was defined as the time interval from death to actual retrieval of the donor tissue. Cornea tissue grading as recorded in the donor assessment form of the Eye Bank was evaluated. The grading system was adapted and modified from the Cornea Donor Study, Donor Eligibility Criteria. Corneal parameters such as epithelial defect, haze, exposure, stromal edema, and Descemet folds were recorded in the study. The specular microscopy records of patients were evaluated for quantitative assessment of corneal endothelium health by ECD.

Corneas were allocated for research/training when: (i) serology negative but tissue failed to meet clinical release criteria; and/or (ii) ECD below internal EK/optical thresholds yet usable for wet-lab training or validation studies; and/or (iii) anterior segment surface compromise (exposure, epithelial loss, stromal haze/edema, Descemet folds) precluding optical use but suitable for nonclinical applications. Corneas were labelled unfit only when not releasable for any purpose due to: (i) positive/indeterminate serology (HIV/ Hepatitis B virus (HBV)/ Hepatitis C virus (HCV)/syphilis); (ii) inadequate documentation/chain-of-custody; (iii) frank microbial contamination; or (iv) tissue integrity loss precluding even research handling. These categories were mutually exclusive and applied prior to any release.

Graft failure was defined as the occurrence of one of the following: regrafting for any reason, a cloudy or equivocally cloudy cornea on the first postoperative day that did not clear within eight weeks, or a cornea that was initially clear postoperatively but became and remained cloudy for 90 days.[9] Graft failure was defined as the need for regrafting due to any of the following causes: primary graft failure, graft rejection, secondary endothelial decompensation, uncontrolled secondary glaucoma, infection leading to graft opacity, or persistent epithelial defect with stromal melt.

This retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Guru Nanak Eye Centre, New Delhi. Eye bank records were evaluated from January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2023 (6 years). Ethical clearance was taken from the Institutional Ethics Committee. The utilization of donor tissues with a DRT equal to or more than 12 h was analyzed in detail. The records of patients who underwent EK with a cornea with DRT of more than 12 h were assessed.

Statistical analysis

The presentation of the Categorical variables was done in the form of numbers and percentages (%). The quantitative data were presented as the mean ± SD and as median with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range). The data normality was checked by using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired t-test was used for comparison of quantitative variables across follow up. The data entry was done in the Microsoft EXCEL spreadsheet, and the final analysis was done with the use of Statistical Package for Social Sciences software, IBM manufacturer, Chicago, USA, version 25.0. For statistical significance, P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Observations

Records of 333 corneal tissues with DRT more than or equal to 12 h were reviewed from our eye bank. These were retrieved from 170 donors. Out of these, 163 corneal tissues were received in pairs and 7 were from single eye retrievals. The majority of these tissues were retrieved through the Hospital Corneal Retrieval Program (HCRP) (94.59%) [Table 1]. Voluntary donations contributed to 4.8% donations. Two tissues (0.60%) were received from another eye bank due to the nonavailability of suitable patients at that time in their center. Various causes of death were identified during screening of the records. In case of retrievals via HCRP, the most common cause of death was hanging (29%), followed closely by sudden death due to cardiac arrest (24%). In the 16 corneas retrieved from voluntary donations, cardiac arrest (50%), hanging (37.5%), and natural death (12.5%) were the common causes of death.

The mean time between death and retrieval of the cornea was 13.84 ± 1.62 h, with the longest DRT of 20 h recorded in one donor. The mean pretransplant ECD of the donor tissue was 2436.77 ± 531.74. ECD measurements were not available for 80 eyes; among these, 68 were not utilized for surgery (either deemed unsuitable or allocated for research), 5 were used for patch grafts, and 7 were employed for therapeutic purposes. The mean ECD for the corneas which were found suitable for EK was 2633 ± 291. Records of serology testing indicated a 97.9% negative rate. Tissues with positive serology for HIV, Hepatitis B virus (HBV), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), or syphilis were not utilized for surgery.

Out of 333 corneal tissues, 252 (75.68%) were found to be suitable for corneal transplantation [Table 3]. Among the 81 eyes not used clinically, 71 (87.7%) entered the prespecified research/training pathway, while 10 (12.3%) met unfit criteria; no research-allocated tissue was released for surgery.

Among the donor corneas deemed suitable for surgery, the largest proportion, 140 (42.0%), were utilized for optical PK. Descemet’s stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK) was performed with 53 corneas (15.9%), while therapeutic PK accounted for 33 cases (9.9%) and deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) for 15 cases (4.5%). In addition, patch grafts were undertaken with five corneas (1.5%), tectonic PK with four corneas (1.2%), and both Descemet’s membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) and tuck-in lamellar keratoplasty were performed with one cornea each (0.3%).

The 6-month follow-up records of patients who underwent EK were reviewed [Table 4]. The maximum follow-up available was of 4 years. The records showed that 53 tissues were utilized in DSAEK and 1 in DMEK. The recipients’ age was recorded to be between 4 and 83 years, with an average age of 55.69 ± 18.78 years. Approximately, 48% of recipients were noted to be males, and 52% were females. In 87% of cases, no prior ocular disease was documented; however, pre-existing glaucoma was identified in four cases, and three patients had a recorded history of viral kerato-uveitis.

The primary indications recorded for EK were pseudophakic bullous keratopathy in 53.70% of cases, Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy in 18.81%, and graft failure in 14.81%. A history of previous ocular transplants was noted in eight patients (14.81%), with five having a previous history of PK, two with a history of DSAEK, and one with a history of DMEK. Additional indications were noted as congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (3.7%), postviral endothelial decompensation (7.4%), and iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (1.85%).

According to follow-up records of EK patients, 45 patients completed follow-up while 9 were lost to follow-up during this period [Table 5]. Among those who did follow up, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) recorded showed that the majority, 91% (41), of them had achieved 6/18 or better. 6/12 or better was recorded in 60% of recipients [Fig. 1].

At the 6-month follow-up, ECD in the recipient group ranged from 1568 to 2097 cells/mm², with a mean of 1857.02 ± 133.11 cells/mm². This showed a statistically significant decline in ECD post-transplantation (P < 0.0001), which also corroborated the follow-up period. Graft rejection was documented in six cases (13.34%) within the 6-month follow-up period. No cases of graft infection were recorded during this time. Approximately, 18% (8 patients) experienced episodes of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP).

Discussion

A number of quality and safety protocols have been established by eye banks worldwide to ensure that high-quality donor corneas are available for transplantation.[1] The quality of donor tissue is influenced by multiple factors, including donor age, corneal condition at retrieval, DRT, death-to-preservation time (DPT), presence of ocular surface disease, and endothelial cell function.[2]

Young donor age has consistently been associated with higher ECD and better postoperative graft outcomes. Armitage et al.[3] reported improved graft survival and reduced postoperative endothelial cell loss with younger donors. Our donor profile—mean age 38.6 years with nearly half (47%) between 21 and 40 years—likely contributed to the favorable outcomes observed in this study.

Traditionally, Indian eye banks have preferred donors under 60 years with DRT and DPT <6 h, based on concerns about declining corneal viability, loss of tear film protection, tissue degradation, and microbial proliferation postmortem.[45] As a result, tissues retrieved beyond this time window are often excluded from transplantation. However, such conservative criteria substantially reduce the usable donor pool in a country already facing a significant corneal blindness burden and donor shortage.

Our findings directly challenge this paradigm. Despite a mean DRT of 13.84 ± 1.62 h (maximum 20 h), 75.68% of donor tissues were suitable for transplantation, and 15.9% were successfully used for EK. These utilization rates are higher than those reported in Indian studies that restricted inclusion to corneas retrieved within 6 h, which range from 25% to 60%.[467] This suggests that extended DRT—when combined with rigorous donor screening, proper preservation, and tissue evaluation—does not compromise tissue suitability or surgical outcomes.

Corneas were designated as allocated for research/training if serology was negative, but they failed to meet clinical release criteria due to lower ECD, surface compromise, or other noninfectious factors. These tissues were subsequently used in wet-lab surgical simulation, histopathology, or validation research. In contrast, unfit tissues were those deemed unsuitable for any use—due to positive or indeterminate serology, incomplete documentation, gross contamination, or severe structural degradation. This distinction is critical because tissues allocated to research still contribute significantly to training and scientific advancement, even if not suitable for clinical transplantation.

Our results are consistent with international recommendations that permit longer postmortem intervals. The Council of Europe’s 2023 guide allows DRT up to 24 h and DPT up to 48 h.[8] Hofmann et al.[1] reported no compromise in transplant quality with DRT up to 72 hours, while Shehab et al.[9] showed comparable posterior lamellar keratoplasty outcomes with DRT as long as 48 h. Denmark’s national tissue law similarly allows DRT up to 24 h.[9]

Indian literature, however, remains more conservative. Patel et al.[2] found that DPT >6 h was associated with decreased utilization but did not conclusively show poorer clinical outcomes.[4] Mohamed et al.[5] demonstrated that donor corneas retrieved after >6 h could still achieve successful graft clarity and survival, though their utilization rates were lower. Our study expands on these findings by providing the first large-scale Indian data specifically focused on EK outcomes with DRT ≥12 h.

ECD is a key determinant of graft survival and visual outcomes. The CPTS recommended a minimum ECD >2300 cells/mm² for EK.[10] Our mean ECD for EK-suitable corneas was 2633 ± 291 cells/mm², well above this threshold, despite prolonged DRT. Post-EK ECD declined to 1857 ± 133 cells/mm² at six months, similar to the ECD loss reported in the CPTS and other EK series.[11]

Studies have shown mixed effects of prolonged DRT on ECD. Shehab et al.[9] and Gavrilov et al.[12] found no significant correlation between DRT and preoperative ECD. Borderie et al.[13] reported some ECD reduction with prolonged times but no difference in graft survival. Our findings support the latter view: although ECD decreased postsurgery, graft clarity and survival remained excellent.

EK outcomes in our series were comparable or superior to published Indian data using standard retrieval times. Ninety-one percent of recipients achieved a BCVA of ≥6/18, and 60% achieved ≥6/12, which is in line with or better than reported outcomes in previous Indian EK series.[514] Graft rejection occurred in 13.34%, slightly above the average 10% reported by Lee et al.,[15] possibly reflecting a higher proportion of patients with prior surgeries and pre-existing glaucoma. Postoperative IOP elevation occurred in 18%, consistent with global literature attributing IOP spikes to steroid response and surgical manipulation.[16] Importantly, no graft infections were reported, despite the extended DRT.

These findings are significant because most Indian studies on donor tissue utilization have focused on PK rather than EK. By demonstrating high utilization rates, robust ECD retention, and excellent clinical outcomes with prolonged DRT, our study fills a critical gap in the Indian literature and supports a paradigm shift toward more liberal retrieval criteria.

Discarding donor tissues solely due to prolonged DRT not only exacerbates graft shortages but also leads to avoidable economic losses from serology testing and retrieval logistics. Our results show that with proper screening, preservation, and assessment, tissues retrieved beyond 12 h can reliably support successful EK outcomes. This evidence supports reconsideration of India’s current guidelines, aligning them with international standards and helping to expand the available donor pool.

This study has some limitations. Its retrospective design may introduce selection bias, and some postoperative follow-up data, including ECD measurements, were incomplete. The relatively young donor pool could be a confounding factor for favorable outcomes. Furthermore, we did not directly compare outcomes with tissues retrieved within 6 h. A prospective, multicenter study comparing EK outcomes across different DRT intervals would be valuable in confirming these findings and refining clinical guidelines.

Conclusion

In summary, our results demonstrate that corneas with DRT ≥12 h, when properly screened and preserved, can achieve utilization rates and EK outcomes comparable to those retrieved earlier. This challenges the traditional 6-h retrieval dogma and suggests that quality metrics such as ECD and tissue condition should guide utilization decisions more than arbitrary time cut-offs. Adopting such evidence-based criteria could substantially expand the donor pool and improve access to corneal transplantation in regions with significant tissue shortages.
