Spurious Correlations in High Dimensional Regression: The Roles of Regularization, Simplicity Bias and Over-Parameterization
Simone Bombari, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Learning models have been shown to rely on spurious correlations between non-predictive features and the associated labels in the training data, with negative implications on robustness, bias and fairness. In this work, we provide a statistical characterization of this phenomenon for high-dimensional regression, when the data contains a predictive core feature x x and a spurious feature y y . Specifically, we quantify the amount of spurious correlations 𝒞 \mathcal{C} learned via linear regression, in terms of the data covariance and the strength λ \lambda of the ridge regularization. As a consequence, we first capture the simplicity of y y through the spectrum of its covariance, and its correlation with x x through the Schur complement of the full data covariance. Next, we prove a trade-off between 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and the in-distribution test loss ℒ \mathcal{L} , by showing that the value of λ \lambda that minimizes ℒ \mathcal{L} lies in an interval where 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is increasing. Finally, we investigate the effects of over-parameterization via the random features model, by showing its equivalence to regularized linear regression. Our theoretical results are supported by numerical experiments on Gaussian, Color-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 datasets.

Introduction

Machine learning systems have been shown to learn from patterns that are statistically correlated with the intended task, despite not being causally predictive
[
15
,
60
]
. As a concrete example, a blue background in a picture might be positively correlated with the presence of a boat in the foreground, and while not being a predictive feature per se, a trained deep learning model could use this information to bias its prediction. In the literature, this statistical (but non causal) connection is referred to as a
spurious correlation
between a feature and the learning task. A recent and extensive line of research has investigated the extent to which deep learning models manifest this behavior
[
16
,
60
]
and has proposed different mitigation approaches
[
48
,
29
]
, given its implications to robustness, bias, and fairness
[
65
,
64
]
. The phenomenon, also referred to as shortcut learning, is often attributed to the relative “simplicity” of spurious features
[
15
,
51
,
22
]
and to the implicit bias of over-parameterized models toward learning simpler patterns
[
5
,
45
,
26
]
. Consequently, the
core features
that are informative about the task (e.g., the boat in the foreground) may be neglected, as
spurious features
(e.g., the blue background) provide an easier shortcut to minimize the loss function.

:
Left two panels:
pictorial representation of the core (spurious) feature
x
(
y
) and an independent core feature
\tilde{x}
, taken from an image of a boat and a truck in the CIFAR-10 dataset.
Right two panels:
examples from a binary Color-MNIST dataset, where the labels correspond to the number shapes, and the zeros (ones) are colored in blue (red) with probability
(1+\alpha)/2
.

Prior work has attempted to formalize the
simplicity bias
relying on boolean functions
[
44
]
, model-specific biases
[
39
]
, one-dimensional features
[
51
]
and their pairwise interactions
[
42
]
. However, when considering high-dimensional natural data (e.g., the boat and its background in Figure
1
), it remains unclear, based on these notions, what exactly makes the features easy or difficult to learn, and to what extent a trained model relies on spurious correlations.
Furthermore, while
[
49
]
show that over-parameterization can exacerbate spurious correlations when re-weighting the objective on minority groups (e.g., boats with a green background), its effect on models trained via empirical risk minimization (ERM) is less understood. This is a critical point when additional group membership annotations are too expensive to obtain, and ERM is a key part of training
[
29
,
1
]
.

Our work tackles these issues: we provide a rigorous characterization of the statistical mechanisms behind learning spurious correlations in high-dimensional data, focusing on the solution obtained via ERM. Formally, we model the input sample
z
as composed by two distinct features,
i.e.
,
z=[x,y]
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the core feature and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the spurious one. The first panel of Figure
1
provides an illustration with a boat in the foreground (
x
) and its blue background (
y
). Then, we quantify spurious correlations via the covariance
\mathcal{C}
(see (
3.3
)) between the label
g
(“boat”) and the model output given
\tilde{z}=[\tilde{x},y]
as input. Here,
\tilde{x}
(a truck in the foreground) is a new core feature independent of everything else, see the second panel of Figure
1
. Now, if
\mathcal{C}
is positive, it means that the model is biased towards
g
only because of
y
, since
\tilde{x}
is independent from
x
and
g
.

More precisely, we provide a sharp, non-asymptotic characterization of
\mathcal{C}
for linear regression (Theorem
1
). Armed with such a characterization, we then:

\bullet
Interpret
\mathcal{C}
via upper bounds on its magnitude (Proposition
5.1
). This highlights the role of the regularization strength and of the data covariance via
(i)
its Schur complement with respect to the covariance of the core feature
x
, and
(ii)
the covariance of the spurious feature
y
. Specifically, we link the smallest eigenvalue of the Schur complement to the strength of the correlation between
y
and
x
, and the largest eigenvalue of the spurious covariance to the simplicity of
y
.

\bullet
Prove a trade-off between
\mathcal{C}
and the test loss (Proposition
5.3
), which implies that spurious correlations can be beneficial to performance when learning in-distribution. Specifically, we show that the optimal regularization minimizing the test loss lies in an interval where
\mathcal{C}
is positive and monotonically increasing.

\bullet
Investigate the role of over-parameterization via a
random features
(RF) model. Specifically, we show that the RF model is equivalent to linear regression with an effective regularization that depends on the over-parameterization (Theorem
2
). This allows to leverage the earlier analysis on regularized linear regression to quantify spurious correlations in over-parameterized, non-linear models.

Throughout the paper, the theoretical results are supported by numerical experiments on synthetic Gaussian data, Color-MNIST, and CIFAR-10, which validates our analysis even in settings not strictly following the modeling choices. Our code is publicly available at the GitHub repository
simone-bombari/spurious-correlations
.

Setting.

We consider supervised learning with
n

\{(z_{1},g_{1}),\ldots,(z_{n},g_{n})\}
and labels defined by a (not necessarily deterministic) function of the inputs
g_{i}=f^{*}(z_{i})
, where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
denotes the
i
-th training input and

the corresponding label. Input samples are composed by two distinct parts (or

),
i.e.
,
z_{i}^{\top}=[x_{i}^{\top},y_{i}^{\top}]
, with
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and they are sampled i.i.d. from the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
. We further denote with
\mathcal{P}_{X}
(

) the marginal distribution of the

-s (

-s). The features

and

have the same dimension

to ease the presentation. We opted to focus on the setting

due to its connection with the motivating example of images with background, but the analysis could be extended to other cases, such as

, briefly discussed in Appendix
D
.

We focus on the setting where the labels

depend only on

,
i.e.
,
g_{i}=f^{*}(z_{i})=f^{*}_{x}(x_{i})
for some (not necessarily deterministic) function

. Hence,

is independent from

, after conditioning on

. We highlight that the independence between

and

is conditional on

, and that the covariance between

and

is in general non-zero. We refer to

as the

of the

-th sample, and to

as its

. As an example,

may represent the main object in an image and

the (not necessarily independent) background, see Figure
1
.

In this setup, the training data is used to learn
f^{*}(z)
through a parametric model

via regularized empirical risk minimization (ERM). Specifically, we perform the following optimization in parameter space:

\hat{\theta}=\arg\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\left(f(\theta,z_{i}),g_{i}\right)+\lambda\left\|\theta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(3.1)

for some regularization term
\lambda\geq 0
, where
\ell
is a loss function
1
1

1

In general, existence and uniqueness of
\hat{\theta}
depend on the choice of the model
f(\theta,z)
, the loss function
\ell
and the regularization term
\lambda
. For the purposes of our work, we will precisely define
\hat{\theta}
for linear regression (Section
4
) and for random features (Section
6
).
. We define the test loss associated to the model

as

\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY},\,g=f^{*}(z)}\left[\ell\left(f(\hat{\theta},z),g\right)\right].

(3.2)

We note that this test loss is in distribution.

Spurious correlations.

We express the extent to which a model
f(\hat{\theta},\cdot)
learns spurious correlations between the spurious feature
y
and the label
g
as

\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})=\mathrm{Cov}\left(f\left(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}\right),g\right),

(3.3)

where the covariance is computed on the probability space of
[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}
,
g=f^{*}_{x}(x)
and of the independent core feature
\tilde{x}\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}
. In words,
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})
expresses how the output of the model
f(\hat{\theta},\cdot)
evaluated on an out-of-distribution sample
[\tilde{x}^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
(where the two features are sampled independently from the marginal distributions
\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
) correlates to the label associated to the in-distribution sample
g=f^{*}(z)=f^{*}_{x}(x)
. We highlight that, if the model
f(\hat{\theta},\cdot)

does not rely
on the spurious feature
y
, then
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})=0
as
x
and
\tilde{x}
are independent.

We note that (
3.3
) formalizes to the regression setting the definition in the survey
[
62
]
and the fairness metric in
[
65
]
(when interpreting

as the protected variable). Furthermore,
in the context of classification, it can connected to the worst group accuracy
[
48
,
49
]
. In fact, our definition (
3.3
) is also related to the

test loss: assuming
\mathbb{E}[f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])]=\mathbb{E}[f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})]=0
for simplicity and considering a quadratic loss, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x},y}\left[\left(f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])-f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right)^{2}\right]\geq 2-2\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})^{2}}.

(3.4)

This implies that an increase in
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})
hurts the performance of the model when core and spurious features are sampled independently (and, thus, the model is tested out-of-distribution). The proof of (
3.4
) is in Appendix
E
.

4
Precise analysis for linear regression

To study
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
as defined in (

), we focus on a high-dimensional

model,
i.e.
,

f_{\textup{LR}}(\theta,z)=z^{\top}\theta,

(4.1)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. The data also follows a linear model,
i.e.
,

g_{i}=z_{i}^{\top}\theta^{*}+\epsilon_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\theta^{*}_{x}+\epsilon_{i},

(4.2)

where
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
\theta^{*}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\epsilon_{i}
is label noise.
The second equality in (

) implies that
\theta^{*}=[{\theta^{*}_{x}}^{\top},\mathbf{0}_{d}^{\top}]^{\top}

\theta^{*}_{x},\mathbf{0}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and each entry of

is 0. We set
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\theta^{*}_{x}\right\|_{2}=1
and let the

-s be i.i.d. (and independent from the
z_{i}
-s), mean-0, sub-Gaussian, with variance
\sigma^{2}>0

We introduce the shorthands
Z=[z_{1}^{\top},\dots,z_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2d}
,
G=[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{E}=[\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to indicate the data matrix, the labels, and the noise vector respectively. Then, using a quadratic loss, (
3.1
) reads

\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda)=\arg\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\|Z\theta-G\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\theta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(4.3)

which admits the unique solution

\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda)=\left(Z^{\top}Z+n\lambda I\right)^{-1}Z^{\top}G,

(4.4)

for
\lambda>0
and, if
Z^{\top}Z
is invertible, also for
\lambda=0
.

Assumption 1

(Data distribution)
.

\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are
n
i.i.d. samples from the multivariate, mean-0, Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
, such that its covariance
\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}\left[zz^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
is invertible, with
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
,
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma\right)=\Omega(1)
, and
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Sigma)=2d
.

This requirement could be relaxed to having sub-Gaussian data. We focus on the Gaussian case for simplicity, deferring the discussion on the generalization to Appendix
B.2
.

Our first result concerns the amount of spurious correlations learned in the un-regularized setting.

5
Roles of regularization and simplicity bias

We now interpret
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
, which characterizes the
spurious correlations via Theorem
1
, in terms of the data covariance
\Sigma
and the regularization
\lambda
. To do so,
we express
\Sigma
as

\Sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\Sigma_{xx}&\Sigma_{xy}\\
\hline\cr\Sigma_{yx}&\Sigma_{yy}\end{array}\right),

(5.1)

where the block
\Sigma_{xx}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
(
\Sigma_{yy}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{P}_{Y}}\left[yy^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
) denotes the covariance of the core (spurious) feature sampled from its marginal distribution. The off-diagonal blocks are
\Sigma_{xy}=\Sigma_{yx}^{\top}=\mathbb{E}_{[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}}\left[xy^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let us denote by

S_{x}^{\Sigma}:=\Sigma_{yy}-\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}

(5.2)

the Schur complement of
\Sigma
with respect to the top-left
d\times d
block
\Sigma_{xx}
.
In our setting,
S_{x}^{\Sigma}
offers a helpful statistical interpretation. In fact, for multivariate Gaussian data, it corresponds to the conditional covariance of
y
given
x
,
i.e.
,

S_{x}^{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}\left(y|x=\bar{x}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}\left[\left(y-\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}[y]\right)\left(y-\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}[y]\right)^{\top}\right].

(5.3)

Therefore, the spectrum of
S_{x}^{\Sigma}
describes the degree of dependence between
y
and
x
: on the one hand, if its eigenvalues are small, the feature
y
is close to be determined by the knowledge of the feature
x
(
i.e.
,
y
is highly correlated with

As an intuitive example, let us interpret classification for the binary Color-MNIST dataset (see Figure
1
) as a 2-dimensional problem: the core feature is
x=+1
(
-1
) if the digit on the image is
1
(
0

y=+1
(
-1
) if the color is red (blue). If the digits and the colors have a correlation of
\alpha
, then
\Sigma_{xx}=\Sigma_{yy}=1
and
\Sigma_{xy}=\Sigma_{yx}=\alpha
. Thus,
S_{x}^{\Sigma}=1-\alpha^{2}
, which becomes 0 if
|\alpha|=1
(full correlation), and it is maximized in the setting of independence between colors and parity (
\alpha=0

At this point, leveraging the decomposition of
\Sigma
in (

) and the Schur complement in (

\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
and
S_{x}^{\Sigma}
be defined in (

), respectively. Then,

\left|\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)\right|\leq\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\left\lVert\Sigma_{yx}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},\frac{\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma\right)^{2}}{\tau(\lambda)},\tau(\lambda)\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(g)-\sigma^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)-\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S_{x}^{\Sigma}\right)}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S_{x}^{\Sigma}\right)\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma_{xx}\right)}}\right).

(5.4)

We discuss the three upper bounds in (
5.4
) below.

(i)
:
|\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)|\leq\left\lVert\Sigma_{yx}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
. The off-diagonal blocks
\Sigma_{yx}=\mathbb{E}[yx^{\top}]
and
\Sigma_{xy}=\Sigma_{yx}^{\top}
describe the correlation between
y
and
x
. In the limit case
\left\lVert\Sigma_{yx}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=0
, we have that
x
and
y
are uncorrelated and, therefore,
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)=0
, as there is no spurious correlation that the model can learn.

(ii)
:
|\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)|\leq\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma\right)^{2}/\tau(\lambda)
. From (

), one obtains that
\tau(\lambda)\to\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Thus, the bound implies that
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
approaches 0 as
\lambda
grows large. This captures the intuition that, when the regularization
\lambda
is large, the minimization in (
4.3
) is biased towards solutions with small norm and, therefore, the output of the model is small, which drives to
0
the spurious correlations as defined in (
3.3
). The behavior is confirmed by Figure

|\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))|
is decreasing for large values of

make the output of the model small, which in turn increases the test loss
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
.

(iii)
: The third bound in (

\frac{|\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)|\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma_{xx}\right)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(g)-\sigma^{2}}}\leq\tau(\lambda)\frac{\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)-\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S_{x}^{\Sigma}\right)}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S_{x}^{\Sigma}\right)},

(5.5)

where we have isolated on the LHS the terms depending on the covariance of the core feature

) and on the scaling of the labels (
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(g)-\sigma^{2}}
). We now discuss the dependence of the RHS of (

) w.r.t.
(a)

,
(b)

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S_{x}^{\Sigma}\right)
, and

\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)

(a)

\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)

Proposition 5.2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold,
n=\Theta(d)
and
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
be the in-distribution test loss of the model

\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda):=\frac{\sigma^{2}+\tau(\lambda)^{2}\left\|\left(\Sigma+\tau(\lambda)I\right)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{1-\frac{\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left(\left(\Sigma+\tau(\lambda)I\right)^{-2}\Sigma^{2}\right)}{n}},

Armed with the non-asymptotic bounds of Theorem

and Proposition

, we characterize the trade-off between
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
and
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
by studying the monotonicity of
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
be defined as in (
4.8
) and (
5.7
). Then, if
2d<n
, we have that
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
is monotonically decreasing in a right neighborhood of
\lambda=0
, and there exists
\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}>0
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
is monotonically increasing for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}
.
Furthermore, if
\Sigma_{xx}=I
, then
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
is non-negative and there exists
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
is monotonically increasing for
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Finally, as long as

\frac{2d}{n}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}(\Sigma)}{4}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(1,\frac{2\lambda_{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits}(\Sigma)/\sigma^{2}}{\left(\lambda_{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits}(\Sigma)/\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}(\Sigma)+1\right)^{2}}\right),

(5.8)

we have that
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}\geq\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}
.

In words, Proposition
5.3
shows that
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
grows with
\lambda
at least until the regularization equals a value
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
. For example, in Figure
2
,
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}\sim 1
for a Gaussian data and
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}\sim 10
for Color-MNIST. Furthermore, in this interval,
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
is initially decreasing and then increasing as
\lambda\geq\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}
. These trends in turn imply that the optimal value
\lambda^{*}_{\mathcal{L}}
that minimizes the test loss is s.t.
\lambda^{*}_{\mathcal{L}}\in(0,\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}]
– an interval where the spurious correlations are strictly positive and increasing.

The proof of Proposition
5.3
(whose details are in Appendix
B
) relies on the monotonicity of
\tau(\lambda)
in
\lambda
, and the last statement follows from showing that
\tau(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}})\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S^{\Sigma}_{x}\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma\right)\geq\tau(\lambda_{\mathcal{L}})
. The upper bound on
2d/n
in (
5.8
) is required to prove that
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma\right)\geq\tau(\lambda_{\mathcal{L}})
and, due to Assumption
1
, it is implied by taking
n=\omega(d)
. We note that the latter scaling holds in standard datasets, e.g., MNIST (
n=6\cdot 10^{4}
,
2d\approx 2\cdot 10^{3}
when considering the 3 color channels) and CIFAR-10 (
n=5\cdot 10^{4}
,
2d\approx 3\cdot 10^{3}
).

We conclude the section by noting that learning spurious correlations can be beneficial to minimize the (in-distribution) test loss. In fact, the spurious features in
y
are effectively correlated with the labels, due to their correlation with the core feature
x
, and hence they can be helpful at prediction time. This phenomenon is numerically supported by Figures
3
and
4
, where for a fixed value of
\lambda
, easier spurious features (or higher correlations) generate both higher values of
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
and lower values of
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\lambda))
. In words, while a blue background cannot strictly predict the label “boat”, it is a useful feature in prediction as long as the boats in the test data tend to have a blue background.

The same conclusion does not hold for the out-of-distribution test loss, where the features
x
and
y
are sampled independently: the lower bound in (
3.4
) increases with
\mathcal{C}
. Figure
6
in Appendix
E
provides an additional numerical validation of this statement.

6
Role of Over-parameterization

Our analysis has so far focused on linear regression, highlighting the role of data covariance and regularization. However, moving to complex predictive models, such as neural networks, may lead to differences in the degree to which spurious correlations are learned. As an example, in the left panel of Figure
5
, we train an over-parameterized two-layer neural network on the binary Color-MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets, for different values of the regularizer
\lambda
.
While for high values of
\lambda
the results are qualitatively similar to the ones in Figure
2
, a striking difference is that spurious correlations remain significant even when there is little to no regularization (i.e.,
\lambda\approx 0
), in sharp contrast with Proposition
4.1
. We also note that the phenomenon is in line with previous empirical work
[
48
]
.

We bridge the gap between linear regression and over-parameterized models by focusing on
random features
:

f_{\textup{RF}}(\theta,z)=\phi(Vz)^{\top}\theta,

(6.1)

where
V
is a
p\times 2d
matrix s.t.
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/(2d))
, and
\phi
is an activation applied component-wise. The number of parameters of this model is
p
, as
V
is a fixed random matrix and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
contains trainable parameters. The scaling of input data (
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Sigma)=2d
) and the variance of the entries of
V
guarantee that the pre-activations of the model (
i.e.
, the entries of the vector
Vz\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
) are of constant order.

We consider the ERM in (
3.1

\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}(\lambda)=\arg\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\|\Phi\theta-G\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\theta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(6.2)

where we set
\Phi:=[\phi(Vz_{1}),\ldots,\phi(Vz_{n})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. When
\lambda=0
, if
\Phi\Phi^{\top}
is invertible, the minimization above does not necessarily have a unique solution. In that case, we set
\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}(0)
to be the solution obtained via gradient descent with 0 initialization, which corresponds to the min-norm interpolator (see equation (33) in
[
4
]
). Then

4

.

\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}(\lambda)=\Phi^{\top}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}+n\lambda I\right)^{-1}G.

(6.3)

Assumption 2

(Activation function)
.

The activation
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-linear, odd, Lipschitz function, such that its first Hermite coefficient
\mu_{1}\neq 0
.

This choice is motivated by theoretical convenience and is similar to the one considered in
[
24
]
. We believe that our result can be extended to a more general setting, as the ones in
[
34
,
33
]
, with a more involved analysis. We refer to
[

Assumption 3

(Over-parameterization)
.

We let
p
grow s.t.
p=\omega\left(n\log^{4}n\right)
and
\log p=\Theta(\log n)
.

This requires the width of the model (and, hence, its number of parameters) to grow faster (by at least a poly-log factor) than the number of training samples.

Finally, our requirements on the data are less restrictive than those coming from Assumption
1
.

Assumption 4

(Data distribution, less restrictive)
.

\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are

i.i.d. samples from a mean-0, Lipschitz concentrated distribution

, with covariance
\Sigma

Note that the labels

are not required to follow a linear model

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{1/4}\log d}{p^{1/4}}+\frac{\log^{3/2}d}{d^{1/8}}\right)=o(1),

with probability at least

We point out some differences between Theorem

and earlier work on the equivalence of random features with regularized linear regression
[

. First, as mentioned in Section

, existing results prove equivalence of training and test loss, which does not imply either the equivalence of the covariance in (

are proportional, while Assumption

Proof sketch:

The proof builds on 3 core steps.

Step 1:
We show that, with high probability,

\left\lVert\frac{\Phi\Phi^{\top}}{p}-\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}-{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=o\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)}{p}\right),

which is a consequence of the concentration of
\Phi\Phi^{\top}
to its expectation with respect to
V
, then expressed in terms of the Hermite coefficients of
\phi
(Lemmas
C.2
and
C.3
).

Step 2:
In Lemma
C.5
we upper bound the term
\|\mathbb{E}_{z}[\tilde{\phi}(Vz)\tilde{\phi}(Vz)^{\top}]\|_{\textup{op}}
(where we set
\tilde{\phi}(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot)-\mu_{1}(\cdot)
), which is then used to show that

\left|\phi(Vz)^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}-\mu_{1}z^{\top}V^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}\right|=o(1),

with high probability, due to Markov inequality. This means that the non-linear component of
\phi(Vz)
has a negligible effect on the output (see (
C.67
) in Lemma
C.7
).

Step 3:
Using a similar intuition, we use matrix Bernstein inequality (Lemma
C.6
) to show that
\|(\Phi-\mu_{1}ZV^{\top})V\|_{\textup{op}}
is small with high probability, so that

\left|\mu_{1}z^{\top}V^{\top}\left(\Phi^{\top}-\mu_{1}VZ^{\top}\right)\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}+n\lambda I\right)^{-1}G\right|=o(1),

i.e., the non-linear component of
\Phi^{\top}
in (
6.3
) is also negligible (see (
C.63
)).

Finally, by combining
Step 2
and
Step 3
with standard concentration arguments, we conclude that

\left|\phi(Vz)^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}-\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{z^{\top}Z^{\top}}{2d}(\Phi\Phi^{\top}+n\lambda I)^{-1}G\right|=o(1),

and the thesis follows from
Step 1
and Woodbury matrix identity for the inverse. ∎

7
Conclusions

Our work provides a rigorous study of spurious correlations in high-dimensional regression models, with the purpose of connecting the statistical foundation of this phenomenon with its practical intuition. Specifically, we translate the problem into characterizing the deterministic object
\mathcal{C}^{\Sigma}(\lambda)
, which allows us to quantitatively capture the roles of ridge regularization, data covariance and over-parameterization.

An interesting future direction is to employ our principled approach to design algorithms that go beyond ERM to mitigate spurious correlations. In that regard, let us mention the usage of multiple models
with different ridge regularization or early stopping (connected to ridge penalties, see
[
47
]
), in order to generate additional supervision. Here, having a quantitative control on which features (core or spurious) are learned by which model would
allow to optimally use the extra labels (e.g., for up-weighting minority groups, as in
[
29
]
).

Appendix A
Additional Notation

We define a sub-Gaussian random variable according to Proposition 2.5.2 in
[
59
]
, and
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\{t>0\;:\;\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})\right]\leq 2\}
. If
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a random vector, then
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
. When we state that a random variable or vector
X
is sub-Gaussian, we implicitly mean
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
,
i.e.
its sub-Gaussian norm does not increase with the scalings of the problem.

We say that
X
respects the Lipschitz concentration property if, for all 1-Lipschitz continuous functions
\varphi
, we have
\left\|\varphi(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
. Notice that then, if
X
is Lipschitz concentrated, then
X-\mathbb{E}[X]
is sub-Gaussian.

Given two symmetric matrices
A,B
, we use the notation
A\succeq B
if
A-B
is p.s.d. Notice that if
A\succeq B\succ 0
, then we also have
B^{-1}\succeq A^{-1}
. We denote with
\left\|A\right\|_{F}
the Frobenius norm of
A
, and with
\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits(A)
its kernel space. If
A
is a square matrix, we use the notation
\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}(A)
to denote a matrix identical to
A
on the diagonal, and 0 everywhere else. We let
A\circ B
denote the Hadamard (component-wise) product between matrices, and
A^{\circ k}
denote
A\circ A\circ...\circ A
, where
A
appears
k
times.

. This gives the first claim.

To show that there exists

For the first factor of the second term of (

(B.31)

For the second factor of the second term of (

Lemma B.2
.

Let
z=\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]^{\top}\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}
be distributed according to a mean-0, multivariate Gaussian distribution with covariance
\Sigma
, such that
\Sigma
is invertible. Then, the Schur complement
S^{\Sigma}_{x}
of
\Sigma
with respect to the top left block
\Sigma_{xx}
(see (
5.1
)) corresponds to the conditional covariance of
y
given

,
i.e.

S_{x}^{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}\left(y|x=\bar{x}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}\left[\left(y-\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}[y]\right)\left(y-\mathbb{E}_{y|x=\bar{x}}[y]\right)^{\top}\right].

(B.45)

Proof.

Consider the expression
z^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}z
. According to the notation in (
5.1
) and in (
B.17
), we have

z^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}z=x^{\top}\left[\Sigma^{-1}\right]_{xx}x+y^{\top}\left[\Sigma^{-1}\right]_{yy}y+x^{\top}\left[\Sigma^{-1}\right]_{xy}y+y^{\top}\left[\Sigma^{-1}\right]_{yx}x.

(B.46)

Then, the formulas for the inverse of a block matrix give

\displaystyle z^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}z

(B.47)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\left(\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}+\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\right)x+y^{\top}{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}y+x^{\top}\left(-\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}\right)y+y^{\top}\left(-{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\right)x

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x+\left(y-\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x\right)^{\top}{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}\left(y-\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x\right).

Then, denoting with
p(x,y)
and

the probability density functions of
z=\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]^{\top}
and
x
respectively, we get that the probability density function of

conditioned on

takes the form

\displaystyle p(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)}

(B.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\left(2\pi\right)^{d}\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits\left(\Sigma_{xx}\right)}}{\sqrt{\left(2\pi\right)^{2d}\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits\left(\Sigma\right)}}\frac{\exp\left(-\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]\Sigma^{-1}\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]^{\top}/2\right)}{\exp\left(-x^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x/2\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(2\pi\right)^{d}\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits\left(S^{\Sigma}_{x}\right)}}\exp\left(-\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]\Sigma^{-1}\left[x^{\top},y^{\top}\right]^{\top}/2+x^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x/2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\left(y-\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x\right)^{\top}{S^{\Sigma}_{x}}^{-1}\left(y-\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}x\right)/2\right)}{\sqrt{\left(2\pi\right)^{d}\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits\left(S^{\Sigma}_{x}\right)}},

where we used Schur formula for the determinants in the third line, and (
B.47
) in the last step. Thus, we have that

describes the density of a multivariate Gaussian random variable, with covariance
S^{\Sigma}_{x}

∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
be a p.s.d., invertible matrix. Then, the Schur complement
S^{\Sigma}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
of
\Sigma
with respect to the top left block
\Sigma_{xx}
(see (
5.1
)) is such that

(B.49)

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times d}
be the rank-

matrix defined as

\Gamma=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c}\Sigma_{xx}^{1/2}\\
\hline\cr\Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1/2}\end{array}\right),

(B.50)

and

as the matrix containing

in its bottom-right

\Sigma=S+\Gamma\Gamma^{\top},

(B.51)

where both

are rank-

Denoting by

\lambda_{k}(S)=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{W,\,\mathop{\rm dim}\nolimits\left(W\right)=k}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in W,\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}Su\right),

]
.

For the last statement, we have

2d=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Sigma)=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left(\mathbb{E}\left[zz^{\top}\right]\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left(zz^{\top}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left(z^{\top}z\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|z\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(C.6)

where we used the cyclic property of the trace. Furthermore, we have

\left\|\left\|z\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|z\right\|_{2}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.7)

since
z
is Lipschitz concentrated. Then,

0\leq 2d-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|z\right\|_{2}\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left\|z\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|z\right\|_{2}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\leq C_{1},

(C.8)

for some absolute constant
C_{1}
. Thus, as
\sqrt{1-x}\geq 1-x
for
x\in[0,1]
, we obtain

1-\frac{C_{1}}{2d}\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{C_{1}}{2d}}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|z\right\|_{2}\right]}{\sqrt{2d}}\leq 1.

(C.9)

Plugging this last result in (
C.7
) gives the desired claim.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

We have that, denoting with
{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 2}\mu_{k}^{2}
, with
\mu_{k}
denoting the
k
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
,

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]-p\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p\log^{3}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.10)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

For all
i\in[n]
, we define the functions
\phi^{(i)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\phi^{(i)}(\cdot)=\phi(\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\cdot/\sqrt{2d})
. Note that
\phi^{(i)}
is odd, since
\phi
is odd by Assumption
2
. Thus, denoting with
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
the
k
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi^{(i)}
, for every
i\in[n]
, we have that
\mu_{k}^{(i)}=0
for all even
k
. This implies that, by denoting with
v
a random vector distributed as the rows of
V
,
i.e.
,
\sqrt{2d}\,v
is a standard Gaussian vector, we have

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(z_{i}^{\top}v)\phi(z_{j}^{\top}v)\right]

(C.11)

\displaystyle=p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{(i)}\left(\frac{z_{i}^{\top}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}\sqrt{2d}v\right)\phi^{(j)}\left(\frac{z_{j}^{\top}}{\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}\sqrt{2d}v\right)\right]

\displaystyle=p\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mu_{k}^{(i)}\mu_{k}^{(j)}\left(\frac{z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=p\mu_{1}^{(i)}\mu_{1}^{(j)}\frac{z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}+p\sum_{k\geq 3}\mu_{k}^{(i)}\mu_{k}^{(j)}\left(\frac{z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{k}.

Then, denoting with
D_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the diagonal matrix containing
\mu_{k}^{(i)}/\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{k}
in its
i
-th entry, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]=pD_{1}ZZ^{\top}D_{1}+p\sum_{k\geq 3}D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}.

(C.12)

Notice that, due to the last statement in Lemma
C.1
, we have that, jointly for all
i\in[n]
,

\left|\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.13)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\log^{2}d)
. Then, conditioning on such high probability event and denoting with
\rho
a standard Gaussian random variable, for all
i\in[n]
we have

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{1}^{(i)}-\mu_{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho\phi^{(i)}(\rho)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho\phi(\rho)\right]\right|

(C.14)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho\left(\phi\left(\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}\rho\right)-\phi(\rho)\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho\left(\phi\left(\left(\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right)\rho+\rho\right)-\phi(\rho)\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\left|\rho\right|\left|\phi\left(\left(\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right)\rho+\rho\right)-\phi(\rho)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq L\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\left|\rho\right|\left|\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right|\left|\rho\right|\right]

\displaystyle=L\left|\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where we used Jensen’s inequality in the fourth line, the
L
-Lipschitzness of
\phi
in the fifth line, and (
C.13
) in the last step. With a similar approach, denoting with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}}
the
L^{2}
norm with respect to the Gaussian measure, we have that for all
i\in[n]

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\phi^{(i)}\right\|_{L^{2}}-\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\phi^{(i)}-\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}

(C.15)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\left(\phi^{(i)}(\rho)-\phi(\rho)\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\left(\phi\left(\left(\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right)\rho+\rho\right)-\phi(\rho)\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq L\left|\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}-1\right|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

which directly implies that, for all
i\in[n]
,
\left\|\phi^{(i)}\right\|_{L^{2}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\mu_{k}^{(i)}\right)^{2}=\Theta(1)
, and that

\left|\sum_{k\geq 3}\left(\mu_{k}^{(i)}\right)^{2}-\sum_{k\geq 3}\mu_{k}^{2}\right|\leq\left|\left\|\phi^{(i)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\left\|\phi\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right|+\left|\left(\mu^{(i)}_{1}\right)^{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.16)

Thus, we are ready to estimate the operator norm of the off-diagonal part of the second term on the RHS of (
C.12
), specifically

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{k\geq 3}D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}-\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}\left(\sum_{k\geq 3}D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.17)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 3}\left\|D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}-\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}\left(D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 3}\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\neq j}\left(\frac{\left|z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}\right|}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{k}\left(\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[n]}\left(\mu_{k}^{(i)}\mu_{k}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\neq j}\left(\frac{\left|z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}\right|}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{3}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{k\geq 3}\left(\mu_{k}^{(i)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d^{3/2}}\log^{3}d\,n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{3}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where in the first step we replaced the operator norm with the Frobenius norm, and used triangle inequality; in the fifth line we used that
\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
for all
i\in[n]
(true because of (
C.13
)), and that jointly for all
i\neq j
we have
\left|z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}\right|/\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log d\right)
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}d)
since the
z_{i}
-s are independent sub-Gaussian vectors (since they are mean-0 and Lipschitz concentrated). The diagonal part of the second term on the RHS of (
C.12
) respects

\left\lVert\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}\left(\sum_{k\geq 3}D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}\right)-{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\left|\sum_{k\geq 3}\left(\mu_{k}^{(i)}\right)^{2}-\sum_{k\geq 3}\mu_{k}^{2}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.18)

because of (
C.16
). Lastly, notice that,

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{1}ZZ^{\top}D_{1}-\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left|u^{\top}D_{1}ZZ^{\top}D_{1}u-\mu_{1}^{2}u^{\top}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}u\right|

(C.19)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left|\left\|Z^{\top}D_{1}u\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}\left\|\frac{Z^{\top}}{\sqrt{2d}}u\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(\left\|Z^{\top}D_{1}u\right\|_{2}+\mu_{1}\left\|\frac{Z^{\top}}{\sqrt{2d}}u\right\|_{2}\right)\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(\left\|Z^{\top}D_{1}u-\mu_{1}\frac{Z^{\top}}{\sqrt{2d}}u\right\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert Z^{\top}D_{1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mu_{1}\left\lVert\frac{Z^{\top}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right)\left\lVert Z^{\top}D_{1}-\mu_{1}\frac{Z^{\top}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert D_{1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mu_{1}\frac{\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right)\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert D_{1}-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}.

By Lemma
C.1
, we have that
\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
, and since
\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
and
\mu_{1}^{(i)}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
for all
i\in[n]
(true because of (
C.13
) and (
C.14
) respectively), we have that
\left\lVert D_{1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{d}\right)
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{1}-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left|\frac{\mu_{1}^{(i)}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right|

(C.20)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\frac{1}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}\left(\left|\mu_{1}^{(i)}-\mu_{1}\right|+\mu_{1}\left|1-\frac{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2d}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right),

where the last step is a consequence of (
C.13
) and (
C.14
). Then, we have that (
C.19
) reads

\left\lVert D_{1}ZZ^{\top}D_{1}-\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.21)

A
standard application of the triangle inequality to (
C.17
), (
C.18
) and (
C.21
) gives

\left\lVert\left(D_{1}ZZ^{\top}D_{1}+\sum_{k\geq 3}D_{k}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)^{\circ k}D_{k}\right)-\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{3}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.22)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
(where we used
\mu_{2}=0
since
\phi
is odd), which readily gives the thesis when plugged in (
C.12
).
∎

Lemma C.3
.

We have that

\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.23)

\left\lVert\Phi\Phi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{pd}\right),

(C.24)

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)=\Omega\left(p\right),

(C.25)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

\Phi^{\top}
is a matrix with i.i.d. rows in the probability space of
V
. In particular, its
i
-th row takes the form

\left[\Phi^{\top}\right]_{i:}=\phi\left(ZV_{i:}\right)=\phi\left(ZV_{i:}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\phi\left(ZV_{i:}\right)\right],

(C.26)

where the last step holds since the (Gaussian) distribution of
V_{i:}
is symmetric and
\phi
is an odd function by Assumption
2
. Then, since
\sqrt{2d}\,V_{i:}
is a standard Gaussian (and hence Lipschitz concentrated) random vector, and
\phi
is a Lipschitz continuous function, we have that

\left\|\left[\Phi^{\top}\right]_{i:}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.27)

where the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
is meant on the probability space of
V
, and the second step holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}d\right)
over
Z
due to Lemma
C.1
. Conditioning on this high probability event,
\Phi^{\top}
is a
p\times n
matrix whose rows are i.i.d. mean-0 sub-Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, by Lemma B.7 in
[
6
]
, we have

\left\lVert\Phi^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{p}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.28)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}n)
over
V
, where the second step holds because
n=o(p)
.

For the second part of the proof, we again follow the argument in Lemma B.7 in
[
6
]
, which in turn exploits the discussion in Remark 5.40 in
[
58
]
, and conclude that

\left\lVert\Phi\Phi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{pn}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{pd}\right),

(C.29)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{3}n)
over
Z
and
V
.

For the last statement, Lemma
C.2
and Weyl’s inequality imply that, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)&\geq p\,\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)-\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]-p\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}-\left\lVert\Phi\Phi^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\\
&\geq p{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p\log^{3}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right)-\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{pd}\right)=\Omega(p),\end{split}

(C.30)

where the last step is true since
\tilde{\mu}\neq 0
, as
\phi
is non-linear by Assumption
2
.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
\tilde{\phi}(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot)-\mu_{1}(\cdot)
, and set

n^{\prime}=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\left\lfloor\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{d^{3/2}}{\log^{3}d}\right\rfloor\right).

(C.31)

Let
\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}
be
n^{\prime}
i.i.d. random variables sampled from a distribution respecting Assumption
4
, not necessarily with the same covariance as
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
, and independent from
V
. Then, if
\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times p}
is defined as the matrix containing
\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{i})
in its
i
-th row, we have that

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.32)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as Lemma C.8 in
[
8
]
, with the only difference that they work under their Assumption 1.2,
i.e.
that the data is normalized as
\left\|\hat{z}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{2d}
(in our notation). This difference, however, does not affect the result. We can in fact condition on the high probability event that all
\hat{z}_{i}
are such that
\left\|\hat{z}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
, which holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-cd\right)
by Lemma
C.1
, and proceed in the same way (as their Equation (C.78) now holds) until their Equation (C.81), which requires their Lemma C.7,
i.e.
that

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime}}{\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime}}}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right).

(C.33)

This holds also in our case, as it can be proven following the argument in (
C.17
) and (
C.18
), where now the
n
in the last line of (
C.17
) has to be replaced with
n^{\prime}
, making the RHS there being
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, as
n^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{3/2}}{\log^{3}d}\right)
by definition. Lastly, the normalization of the data is used one more time in their Equation (C.91), but it is not critical to obtain the result, as
\left\|\hat{z}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
is sufficient. We remark that Assumption 1.2 in
[
8
]
also requires the covariance of the distribution to be well-conditioned, which however is not required for the purposes of the above mentioned lemmas.
∎

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
\tilde{\phi}(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot)-\mu_{1}(\cdot)
, and let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be sampled from a distribution respecting Assumption
4
, not necessarily with the same covariance as
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
, and independent from
V
. Then we have that

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Vz)\tilde{\phi}(Vz)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}d+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.34)

with probability at least
1-2p^{2}\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The proof follows a similar path as the one in Lemma C.15 in
[
8
]
. In particular, set

n^{\prime}=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\left\lfloor\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{d^{3/2}}{\log^{3}d}\right\rfloor\right),\qquad N=p^{2}n^{\prime},

(C.35)

and let
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
be a matrix containing
\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{i})
in its
i
-th row, where every
\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is sampled independently from the same distribution of
z
.
Thus,
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}
can be seen as the vertical stacking of
p^{2}
matrices with size
n^{\prime}\times p
. All these matrices respect the hypotheses of Lemma
C.4
, and hence have their operator norm bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right)
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}d\right)
. Thus, performing a union bound over these
p^{2}
matrices, we get

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{2}\,p\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Np}{n^{\prime}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(N\log^{4}p+\frac{Np\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.36)

with probability at least
1-2p^{2}\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
and
\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Via the same argument used for the last statement of Lemma
C.1
, denoting with
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
the
k
-th row of
V
, we have that
\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
uniformly for every
k
with probability at least
1-2p\exp(-c_{2}d)
. Conditioning on such event, we have that each entry of
\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})
is sub-Gaussian (with uniformly bounded sub-Gaussian norm), since
\hat{z}_{1}
is sub-Gaussian (as it is mean-0 and Lipschitz concentrated) and
\tilde{\phi}
is a Lipschitz function. Thus, we have that each entry of
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}_{1}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right]
is
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
(see Proposition 2.5.2 in
[
59
]
), and therefore that
\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z_{1}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z_{1}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right]\right\|_{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right)
. Then, conditioning on the high probability event
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p/d}\right)
given by Lemma
C.1
, we have

\left\|\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\|\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})-\mathbb{E}_{z_{1}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z_{1}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{z}_{1})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{d}}+\sqrt{p}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.37)

where the second step holds because
\hat{z}_{1}
is Lipschitz concentrated and
\tilde{\phi}
is Lipschitz. Since the rows of
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}
are identically distributed, this also holds jointly for all other
\hat{z}_{i}
-s, for
i\in[N]
. Then,
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}/\sqrt{p}
is a matrix with independent sub-Gaussian rows, and by Theorem 5.39 in
[
58
]
(see their Remark 5.40 and Equation (5.25)), we have that

\frac{1}{p}\left\lVert\frac{\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Vz)\tilde{\phi}(Vz)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{N}}\right),

(C.38)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}p\right)
over
\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Vz)\tilde{\phi}(Vz)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Vz)\tilde{\phi}(Vz)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\frac{\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{N}

(C.39)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(p\sqrt{\frac{p}{N}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log^{4}p}{p}}+\sqrt{p}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

where the first step follows from the triangle inequality, the second step is a consequence of (
C.38
) and (
C.36
), and the third step follows from the definition of

.

Taking the intersection between the high probability events in (
C.36
), (
C.37
) and (
C.38
), the previous equation then holds with probability at least

. Also note that its LHS does not depend on

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
\tilde{\phi}(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot)-\mu_{1}(\cdot)

\tilde{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

\tilde{\phi}(Vz_{i})
in its

-th row. Then, we have

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
and

, where

Proof.

Note that
\tilde{\phi}

\phi
is Lipschitz by Assumption
2
). During all the proof, we condition on the event
\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
and
\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta\left(\sqrt{d}\right)

. During the proof we also use the shorthand

to denote a random vector such that

, it has the same distribution as the rows of
V
. This implies

\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Zv\right)\right\|_{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right),\qquad\left\|\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Zv\right)\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Zv\right)\right\|_{2}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.41)

and

\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|v\right\|_{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),\qquad\left\|\left\|v\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|v\right\|_{2}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.42)

where both sub-Gaussian norms are meant on the probability space of
v
, and where the very first equation follows from the discussion in Lemma C.3 in
[
6
]
.
Then, there exists an absolute constant
C_{1}
such that we jointly have

\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Zv\right)\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}

(C.43)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
over
v
.

Let
E_{k}
be the indicator defined on the high probability event above with respect to the random variable
v_{k}:=V_{:k}
(the
k
-th row of
V
),
i.e.

E_{k}:=\mathbf{1}\left(\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\textup{ and }\;\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Zv_{k}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{d}\right),

(C.44)

and we define
E\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as the diagonal matrix containing
E_{k}
in its
k
-th entry. Notice that we have
\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=0
with probability at least
1-2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
, and
\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right]\leq 2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}\left(I-E\right)V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert 1-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right]

(C.45)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{4}\right]^{1/4}\left(2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|\tilde{\Phi}\right\|^{4}_{F}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|V\right\|^{4}_{F}\right]^{1/4}\left(2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=o(1),

where the last step holds because of our initial conditioning on
Z
: the first two terms are the sum of finite powers of sub-Gaussian random variables (the entries of
\tilde{\Phi}
and
V
), and thus (see Proposition 2.5.2 in
[
59
]
) the first two factors in the third line of the previous equation will be
\mathcal{O}\left(p^{\alpha}\right)
for some finite
\alpha
, which gives the last line due to Assumption
3
.

As in Lemma
C.2
, we introduce the notation (for all
i\in[n]
)
\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}(\cdot)=\tilde{\phi}(\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}\cdot/\sqrt{2d})

a random vector such that
\sqrt{2d}v
is standard Gaussian (
i.e.
, distributed as the rows of
V
), we can write

\left[\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right]_{ij}=p\left[\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv)v^{\top}\right]\right]_{ij}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{2d}}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}\left(\frac{z_{i}^{\top}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}}\sqrt{2d}v\right)\left(e_{j}^{\top}\left(\sqrt{2d}v\right)\right)\right]=\frac{p}{\sqrt{2d}}\frac{{\tilde{\mu}}_{1}^{(i)}z_{i}^{\top}e_{j}}{\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}},

(C.46)

where
{\tilde{\mu}}_{1}^{(i)}
is the first Hermite coefficient of
\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}
. Then, denoting with
\tilde{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the diagonal matrix containing
{\tilde{\mu}}_{1}^{(i)}/\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}
in its
i
-th entry, we can write

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{2d}}\left\lVert\tilde{D}Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{p}{\sqrt{2d}}\left\lVert\tilde{D}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}.

(C.47)

Then, since we conditioned on
\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}=\Theta\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
for all
i\in[n]
, following the same argument as in (
C.14
), and since the first Hermite coefficient of

is 0 by definition, we have

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p}{\sqrt{d}}\,\frac{\log d}{d}\,\sqrt{d}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p\log d}{d}\right),

(C.48)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}\left(I-E\right)V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p\log d}{d}\right),

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
.

Let’s now look at

\tilde{\Phi}EV-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]=:\sum_{k=1}^{p}W_{k},

(C.50)

where we defined the shorthand

. (
C.50
) is the sum of
p

(in the probability space of
V
), such that

\displaystyle\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.51)

\displaystyle=2\sup_{v_{k}}\left\|\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})\right\|_{2}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}E_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}^{2}\sqrt{d},

because of (
C.44

where

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}v_{k}E_{k}\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.54)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{v_{k}}\left(E_{k}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

where the last step is a direct consequence of Lemma
C.2
, applied to
\tilde{\Phi}
instead of to
\Phi
, and holds with probability at least

over

in (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[W_{k}^{\top}W_{k}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[v_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{v_{k}}\left(E_{k}\left\|\tilde{\phi}(Zv_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

Then, plugging these last two equations in (

\displaystyle\leq\left(n+d\right)\exp\left(-\frac{p\log^{2}d/2}{C_{2}p+2C_{1}^{2}\sqrt{d}\sqrt{p}\log d/3}\right)

(C.56)

3

C.49

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}EV\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\log d+\frac{p\log d}{d}\right),

(C.57)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z,V
.
Then, since
E=I
with probability at least

(C.72)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{z^{\top}Z^{\top}}{2d}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+\left({\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p+n\lambda\right)I\right)^{-1}E\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+\left({\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p+n\lambda\right)I+E\right)^{-1}G\right|

\displaystyle\leq\log d\left\|\frac{p}{2d}Z^{\top}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+\left({\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p+n\lambda\right)I\right)^{-1}E\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+\left({\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p+n\lambda\right)I+E\right)^{-1}G\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\log d\,\frac{p}{2d}\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{1}{{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p}\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)}\left\|G\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log d\,\frac{p}{d}\,\sqrt{d}\,\frac{1}{p}\,p\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{p}}+\frac{\log^{3}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\,\frac{1}{p}\,\sqrt{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log d\sqrt{\frac{d}{p}}+\frac{\log^{4}d}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Here, the second step holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}d\right)
since
z
is sub-Gaussian and independent from everything else; the fourth step is a consequence of Lemma
C.1
, (
C.70
), Lemma
C.3
, and
\left\|G\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
(see the argument prior to (
C.60
)), and as a whole holds with probability
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}d\right)
over
Z
,
G
, and
V
.

Note that the second term in the LHS of (
C.72
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{z^{\top}Z^{\top}}{2d}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{ZZ^{\top}}{2d}+\left({\tilde{\mu}}^{2}p+n\lambda\right)I\right)^{-1}G

\displaystyle=z^{\top}Z^{\top}\left(ZZ^{\top}+n\left(\frac{2{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}d}{\mu_{1}^{2}n}+\frac{2d}{\mu_{1}^{2}p}\lambda\right)I\right)^{-1}G

(C.73)

\displaystyle=z^{\top}\left(Z^{\top}Z+n\left(\frac{2{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}d}{\mu_{1}^{2}n}+\frac{2d}{\mu_{1}^{2}p}\lambda\right)I\right)^{-1}Z^{\top}G

\displaystyle=f_{\textup{LR}}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\tilde{\lambda}),z),

where the second line is due to the classical identity
A^{\top}\left(AA^{\top}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}=\left(A^{\top}A+\kappa I\right)^{-1}A^{\top}
, and the third line uses the definition in (
4.1
), with
\hat{\theta}_{\textup{LR}}(\tilde{\lambda})
defined in (
4.4
) and

\tilde{\lambda}=\frac{2{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}d}{\mu_{1}^{2}n}+\frac{2d}{\mu_{1}^{2}p}\lambda.

(C.74)

Furthermore, the first term of the LHS of (
C.72
) satisfies

\left|\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{z^{\top}Z^{\top}}{2d}\left(\Phi\Phi^{\top}+n\lambda I\right)^{-1}G-f_{\textup{RF}}(\hat{\theta}_{\textup{RF}}(\lambda),z)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{1/4}\log d}{p^{1/4}}+\frac{\log^{3/2}d}{d^{1/8}}\right),

(C.75)

with probability
1-C_{1}\sqrt{d}\log^{2}d/\sqrt{p}-C_{1}\log^{3}d/d^{1/4}
over

,
G
,
V
and
z
, due to Lemma
C.7
. Thus, an application of the triangle inequality together with (
C.72
), (
C.73
) and (

) gives the desired result.

Appendix D
Features Composed as
z=x+y

In this work, we consider the data to be composed as
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
. However, some of the high level results we obtain can be recovered for settings where the features are composed differently. As an example, let us consider the model

z=x+y,\qquad g=x^{\top}\theta^{*}+\varepsilon.

(D.1)

Then, according to (
3.3
), we have

\mathcal{C}(\hat{\theta})=\mathrm{Cov}\left((\tilde{x}+y)^{\top}\hat{\theta},x^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)=\hat{\theta}^{\top}\Sigma_{yx}\theta^{*},

(D.2)

which provides a quantity that could be studied again via the analysis in
[
19
]
. In this new setup, we remark that the full data covariance takes the form

\Sigma_{zz}=\Sigma_{xx}+\Sigma_{yy}+\Sigma_{xy}+\Sigma_{yx}.

(D.3)

In a nutshell, we expect the analysis for this setting to provide a qualitative behavior similar to that unveiled by the case
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
. In fact, the experiments on Color-MNIST (which does not strictly follow the model
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
, as the color overlaps with the core feature pattern as in the model
z=x+y
) suggest that our conclusions hold beyond the setting of “orthogonal features”. We further remark that in the setting
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
the optimal solution
\hat{\theta}=\theta^{*}
gives
\mathcal{C}=0
, while this is not necessarily the case in the setting
z=x+y
.

Appendix E
Proof of (
3.4
): connection with out-of-distribution loss

Let
\tilde{x}
and
[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
be sampled independently from
\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
respectively. For simplicity, assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})^{2}\right]=1
and
\mathbb{E}\left[f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right]=0
. Thus, for the quadratic loss, we readily get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x},y}\left[\left(f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])-f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2-2\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x},y}\left[f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right]

(E.1)

\displaystyle=2-2\mathrm{Cov}\left(f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y]),f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right).

Denoting with
S
the covariance matrix of the three random variables
f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y])
,
f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})
, and
f^{*}_{x}(x)
, we have

S=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c c c@{}}1&\rho&\mathcal{C}\\
\rho&1&0\\
\mathcal{C}&0&1\end{array}\right),

(E.2)

where we introduced
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Cov}\left(f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y]),f^{*}_{x}(x)\right)
and
\rho=\mathrm{Cov}\left(f(\hat{\theta},[\tilde{x}^{\top},y]),f^{*}_{x}(\tilde{x})\right)
. Since
S
is p.s.d., its determinant has to be non-negative, hence

1-\rho^{2}-\mathcal{C}^{2}\geq 0,

(E.3)

which, when plugged in (
E.1
), gives (
3.4
).

This bound suggests the close connection between
\mathcal{C}
and the out-of-distribution test loss. In Figure
6
, we repeat the same experiments of Figures
3
-
4
and report in black the out-of-distribution test loss. The plots clearly show that
\mathcal{C}
and the out-of-distribution test loss follow a similar trend, for both linear regression and random features.

Appendix F
Experimental details

All the plots in the figures report the average over 10 independent trials, with a shaded area describing a confidence interval of 1 standard deviation. For the Gaussian and Color-MNIST datasets, every iteration involves re-generating (or re-coloring) the data, while for the CIFAR-10 dataset the randomness comes from the model and the training algorithm.

Synthetic Gaussian data generation.

This follows the same model across all the numerical experiments presented in the paper. In particular, we fix
d=400
and set
\Sigma_{xx}=I
.
\Sigma_{yy}
is a diagonal matrix, such that its first entry equals
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)
and all the other entries equal
\left(d-\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)\right)/(d-1)
. In this way,
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Sigma)=2d
. Then, we set the off-diagonal blocks
\Sigma_{xy}
and
\Sigma_{yx}
to the same diagonal matrix, so that

\Sigma_{xy}=\Sigma_{yx}=(\Sigma_{yy}-\beta I)^{1/2},

(F.1)

which implies that the Schur complement
S^{\Sigma}_{x}=\beta I
and, therefore,
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(S^{\Sigma}_{x}\right)=\beta
. To conclude, we set the ground truth
\theta^{*}_{x}=e_{1}
,
i.e.
, the first element of the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This design choice is motivated by our interest in capturing the role of
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)
and to have an easy control on the Schur complement
S^{\Sigma}_{x}
(which is therefore chosen to be proportional to the identity).

Unless differently stated in the figure,
n=2000
,
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)=2
,
\beta=0.5
, and
\lambda=1
. Furthermore, to generate the labels, we add an independent noise with variance
\sigma^{2}=0.25
, and we subtract this quantity from the test loss, so that the optimal predictor
\theta^{*}
has a test loss equal to 0.

When we use an RF model, on every dataset, unless differently stated in the figure, we use
\tanh
as activation function, with
p=20000
neurons.

Binary color MNIST.

This dataset is graphically shown in Figure
1
. To generate it, we take a subset of the MNIST training dataset (
n=1000
samples as default, unless differently specified) made only of zeros and ones. Then, for every training image, we color the white portion in blue (red) with probability
(1+\alpha)/2
if the digit is a zero (one), and red (blue) otherwise. For the test set, we proceed in the same way, but setting
\alpha=0
, to make the core feature (the digit) effectively independent from the spurious one (the color). For all the experiments, we set
\beta=1-\alpha^{2}=0.25
.

CIFAR-10.

For the experiments on CIFAR-10, we implicitly suppose that the middle
22\times 22
square contains the core, predictive feature
x
. Thus, we sum to all the channels of the outer region white noise with increasing variance, and we later clamp the pixels to ensure their value is between 0 and 1. Increasing the variance of the noise, this progressively makes the outer portion being dominated by random noise, thus reducing its value of
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\Sigma_{yy}\right)/\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\Sigma_{yy})
when estimating the covariance on the perturbed training set. At test time, we take the images from the CIFAR-10 test set, and we add the same level of noise. To compute
\mathcal{C}
, we create an out-of-distribution dataset where the core features are randomly permuted across different backgrounds. We always consider the subset of boats and trucks, which contains
n=10000
images.

2-layer neural network.

In the experiments shown in Figure
5
