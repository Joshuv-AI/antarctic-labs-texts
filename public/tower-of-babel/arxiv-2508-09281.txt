Pattern-based Knowledge Component Extraction from Student Code Using Representation Learning
Muntasir Hoq, Griffin Pitts, Andrew Lan, Peter Brusilovsky, Bita Akram

Abstract
. Effective personalized learning in computer science education depends on accurately modeling what students know and what they need to learn. While Knowledge Components (KCs) provide a foundation for such modeling, automated KC extraction from student code is inherently challenging due to insufficient explainability of discovered KCs and the open-endedness of programming problems with significant structural variability across student solutions and complex interactions among programming concepts. In this work, we propose a novel, explainable framework for automated KC discovery through pattern-based KCs : recurring structural patterns within student code that capture the specific programming patterns and language constructs that students must master. Toward this, we train a Variational Autoencoder to generate important representative patterns from student code guided by an explainable, attention-based code representation model that identifies important correct and incorrect pattern implementations from student code. These patterns are then clustered to form pattern-based KCs. We evaluate our KCs using two well-established methods informed by Cognitive Science: learning curve analysis and Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT). Experimental results demonstrate meaningful learning trajectories and significant improvements in DKT predictive performance over traditional KT methods. This work advances knowledge modeling in CS education by providing an automated, scalable, and explainable framework for identifying granular code patterns and algorithmic constructs, essential for student learning.



1.
Introduction

In educational research,
Knowledge Components
(KCs) represent discrete, learnable units of knowledge or skill that learners must acquire to master a domain
(
Koedinger et al. 2012a
)
. The identification of KCs forms the foundation for understanding how students learn, enabling systems to track knowledge acquisition, adapt instructional strategies, and provide targeted support based on individual learning needs. Applications of KC modeling have proven effective in domains such as mathematics and chemistry, where problem-solving paths are constrained and the granularity of KCs is predefined
(
Rau 2017
;
Ozyurt et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024a
)
.

In computer science (CS) education, KC extraction plays a central role in modeling student learning of programming concepts, such as loops, conditionals, recursion, or data structures
(
Koedinger et al. 2012a
)
. With the growth of large-scale programming courses and online learning platforms, automated KC extraction from code could enable scalable, real-time knowledge tracing, targeted practice recommendations, and adaptive feedback, capabilities that are particularly valuable in introductory programming (CS1) contexts
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
. Manual KC specification, common in cognitive tutors and intelligent tutoring systems, is often costly and subjective. In programming education specifically, manually annotating KCs in student code has become infeasible due to the scale and wide range of student solutions
(
Rivers et al. 2016
)
. This challenge has motivated research on automated KC discovery across educational domains.

Automated approaches to KC discovery have been explored using various methods, including statistical analysis of student performance, clustering of learning trajectories, and, more recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) to infer conceptual structures directly from instructional content or student work
(
Tan and Kim 2024
;
Moore et al. 2024
;
Ozyurt et al. 2024
)
. While these automated methods have demonstrated success in structured problem-solving domains, automating KC extraction in the programming domain presents unique challenges. First, the open-ended nature of programming allows for numerous correct implementations. Students potentially solve the same problem with substantial variability in structure and implementation strategies, such as recursion vs. iteration or modular decomposition vs. inline logic. Additionally, the granularity of KCs in programming remains undefined; while low-level constructs like loops and conditionals can be considered fine-grained KCs, high-level KCs often emerge from combinations of these primitives, reflecting more complex problem-solving strategies or conceptual understanding
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, practical challenges such as syntax errors, incomplete submissions, and unconventional coding styles often create a noisy environment where standardized KC mapping becomes extremely difficult. To address these challenges, recent work has explored techniques based on deep learning and Abstract Syntax Trees (AST)
(
Shi et al. 2024b
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
, as well as LLMs
(
Niousha et al. 2025
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
to infer latent knowledge representations from student code. However, these approaches still face challenges, including a lack of explainability and limited granularity of identified KCs, often capturing only superficial programming concepts such as the mere presence of loops or conditions rather than deeper conceptual understanding.

In this work, we propose an explainable framework for automated KC extraction from student programming submissions. Unlike traditional KCs, which are skill- or curriculum-aligned and difficult to define in programming, our approach defines KCs as structural patterns, which we introduce as the
pattern-based KCs
. These pattern-based KCs are recurring and semantically meaningful subtree patterns extracted from the ASTs of student code, reflecting the important programming patterns and algorithmic constructs necessary for correctly solving a programming problem. Our framework begins with an explainable attention-based model, the Subtree-based Attention Neural Network (SANN)
(
Hoq et al. 2023
)
, which is trained to predict code correctness while assigning attention weights to AST subtrees
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
. These subtrees are then normalized to remove literal and identifier-specific variance and passed through a variational autoencoder (VAE), augmented to generate important representative patterns from the code. The VAE encodes structurally similar subtree patterns into a shared latent space. To obtain stable and explainable representations of KCs, we cluster the encoded representations using K-means, treating each cluster centroid as a representative pattern-based KC. We evaluate our KC model through its alignment with learning theory via student learning curves to examine student mastery trajectories over different KCs
(
Rivers et al. 2016
)
and its utility in improving prediction performance of students’ future success via training a Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) model
(
Duan et al. 2025
)
. Experimental results show that our approach yields meaningful learning trajectories and significantly improves DKT predictive performance over traditional KT methods.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Formalizing
pattern-based KCs
as a new conceptual framework for representing knowledge in CS education.

•

Developing an explainable, data-driven framework enabling scalable and explainable KC extraction from student code.

•

Demonstrating the effectiveness of the extracted KCs through verifying their alignment with established learning theory via learning curve analysis, and showing significant improvements in predictive accuracy for student performance modeling using DKT.

This work contributes a data-driven, scalable and explainable approach to knowledge modeling in programming education, offering a bridge between structural code analysis and cognitive modeling of student learning. By identifying pattern-based KCs directly from student programs, our framework provides actionable insights for researchers and educators aiming to support individualized instruction and better track how students acquire programming skills, and can be used to provide adaptive support further.

2.
Related Work

Understanding how learners acquire and apply specific skills is a central goal of educational research, and Knowledge Components (KCs) provide a structured way to represent this learning. In this section, we review prior work on the concept of KCs and existing methods for their automated extraction, with a focus on programming education.

2.1.
Knowledge Component

The concept of Knowledge Components (KCs) is foundational in educational research, particularly within cognitive science and intelligent tutoring systems. KCs are defined as discrete units of knowledge or skills that a learner must acquire to perform a task successfully. They encompass a range of cognitive elements, including concepts, principles, facts, and procedures
(
Koedinger et al. 2012a
;
Rivers et al. 2016
)
.

The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction Framework
(
Koedinger et al. 2012a
)
further elaborates on KCs by linking them to instructional events and learning outcomes. According to this framework, KCs are the mental constructs that mediate between instructional activities and observable learning behaviors. This perspective underscores the importance of accurately identifying and modeling KCs to enhance instructional design, learning analytics, and develop Intelligent Tutoring Systems
(
Corbett et al. 1997
)
. In programming education, defining KCs and determining their appropriate level of granularity is inherently challenging, as they can span from fine-grained concepts, e.g., loop initialization, to complex interactions among constructs such as nested conditionals within loops, encompassing control structures, data manipulation, and algorithmic strategies. Accurately modeling these components is crucial for developing adaptive learning systems that can provide insights into student knowledge tracing
(
Liang et al. 2022
)
, help provide personalized feedback, recommend worked examples, and support to learners
(
Abdelrahman et al. 2023
)
.

2.2.
Knowledge Component Extraction

Automated extraction of KCs from student code has gained significant attention, aiming to mitigate the labor-intensive nature of manual annotation and to scale personalized education.

Deep learning approaches have been employed to model student learning and extract KCs by analyzing code submissions. These AST-based methods leverage the structural representation of code to assign KCs
(
Shi et al. 2024b
;
Filgueira and Garijo 2022
;
Rivers et al. 2016
)
. For instance, the KC-Finder model integrated learning curve theory into its architecture to identify KCs that align with students’ learning progression
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
. Although these models can assign KCs, they often suffer from explainability issues, making it challenging to understand the rationale behind KC assignments. Moreover, these approaches may struggle with the semantic understanding of code, especially when different code structures achieve the same functionality, and understanding the granularity and hierarchical nature of different KCs could be challenging.

LLMs have recently been explored for KC extraction due to their ability to understand programming code and generate human-like text. Studies have demonstrated that LLMs can identify KCs from code and problem descriptions
(
Niousha et al. 2025
;
Niousha et al. 2024
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
. Despite their potential, LLMs can produce overgeneralized KCs, i.e., different conditional structures can not be identified effectively due to a lack of KC taxonomy, or even irrelevant KCs. While manual KC tagging by experts might ensure high accuracy, it is impractical for large-scale applications due to the significant time and effort required
(
Shi et al. 2024b
)
. Moreover, inconsistencies among experts can lead to variability in KC definitions, affecting the reliability of the annotations. While automated methods for KC extraction have advanced, challenges remain in balancing accuracy, explainability, and scalability. Our work aims to address these issues by introducing a pattern-based approach that leverages attention mechanisms and clustering techniques to identify meaningful KCs from student code submissions.

4.
Methodology

Extracting actionable KCs from student code submissions requires
both
(i)
fine-grained, explainable
signals that pinpoint which parts of a program matter for correctness and (ii)
context-aware abstraction
that groups structurally distinct yet semantically related implementations under the same conceptual pattern. SANN
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
excels at the first requirement: its hierarchical AST processing and attention scores reveal
which
subtrees drive a prediction.
However, SANN computes attention locally and independently; it neither models long-range dependencies among high-attention subtrees nor learns a latent space where structurally similar subtrees cluster together.
We leverage a VAE to complement these gaps. By reconstructing sequences of high-attention subtrees, the VAE (i) injects broader
context
, capturing how salient patterns interact within the complete program, and (ii) regularizes them into a continuous latent manifold that is well-suited for clustering similar patterns into distinct pattern-based KCs.

4.1.
Identifying Important Patterns from Code

To identify structurally significant patterns within student programming submissions, we leverage a modified version of the Subtree-based Attention Neural Network (SANN) model from a previous study
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
, an explainable architecture designed for learning from the hierarchical structure of code and identifying logical errors from incorrect implementations. SANN has been previously validated across a variety of code analysis tasks, including program algorithm classification, detecting LLM-generated code, and recommending relevant worked examples
(
Hoq et al. 2023
;
Hoq et al. 2024b
;
Hoq et al. 2025a
)
. In this work, we utilize the attention mechanism of SANN to identify the most informative subtrees—substructures derived from ASTs, that are responsible for determining the correctness of student code submissions. The SANN architecture begins by extracting all possible subtrees from the AST of a program, where different hierarchies of subtrees are extracted.
Each subtree is encoded using a two-way embedding strategy: a node-based and a subtree-based embedding. These two representations are combined and passed through a time-distributed dense layer to form fixed-length subtree vectors. Once all subtree vectors are computed, they are fed into an attention network that calculates the importance of each subtree in the context of the prediction task, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.

Architecture of the SANN model with the two-way embedding approach and the attention neural network.

Originally, SANN employed a softmax-based attention mechanism that prioritized a single dominant subtree
(
Hoq et al. 2023
)
. While this approach was effective for highlighting a key subtree, it was limited in capturing multiple relevant components, especially in cases where an incorrect submission contained multiple logical errors. To overcome this limitation, the modified version of SANN
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
replaces the softmax with a sigmoid activation, allowing attention weights to be computed independently for each subtree. This enables the model to assign high attention to multiple subtrees without enforcing a single dominant focus. An additional entropy regularization term is introduced in the loss function to encourage the model to assign focused attention to fewer subtrees, thereby reducing false positives when identifying influential code segments. By training the SANN model to predict the binary correctness label of student submissions (correct or incorrect), we extract attention weights from the intermediate layers. These attention scores indicate the relative importance of each subtree in influencing the model’s prediction. For correct submissions, subtrees with the highest attention weights typically represent core programming patterns necessary for solving the problem correctly, such as correctly implemented loops, conditionals, or a combination of multiple concepts. Conversely, in incorrect submissions, the most highly attended subtrees are those that contain logical errors or misconceptions, offering insight into why the submission failed
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
.

4.2.
Learning Context-Aware Representations

After identifying important structural patterns in student programs using the SANN model, we aim to transform these patterns into generalizable representations that abstract away surface-level variability such as specific variable names, literals, or function identifiers. These transformations are critical because SANN is trained to predict correctness, which can cause its learned subtree vectors to become overly sensitive to small variations in literals or identifiers that do not affect the underlying structure or conceptual knowledge, and can add noise for KC discovery
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
. To address this, we apply a subtree normalization step, wherein all identifier tokens, such as variable names, constants, and function labels, are replaced with generic placeholders. This abstraction allows us to focus on the structural and semantic essence of the code rather than superficial syntactic differences.

To learn a continuous and robust context-aware latent representation of these normalized subtrees, we design a variational autoencoder (VAE) model
(
Higgins et al. 2017
)
that encodes the subtree sequences into a low-dimensional latent space and attempts to reconstruct the important subtrees from the input sequence. The goal of this model is to ensure that semantically similar subtree structures are mapped to neighboring regions in the latent space, facilitating their grouping into representative pattern-based KCs. Crucially, the VAE is trained only on subtrees from correct submissions, ensuring that the latent space reflects valid, pedagogically meaningful programming structures. Figure
2
shows a schematic architecture of the VAE model.

Figure 2
.
Model architecture of the VAE model.
Architecture of the VAE model.

The model begins by separately embedding each subtree along two dimensions—the sequence of AST nodes within the subtree and the subtree identity itself. The node sequence is passed through a time-distributed embedding layer, followed by positional encoding, which helps preserve the relative ordering of nodes within each subtree. This is essential for capturing nuanced structural differences, especially when similar logic is implemented with different nesting or sequencing. The embedded node sequences are then fed into a time-distributed LSTM layer, which compresses each node-level sequence into a fixed-length vector representation.

Parallel to this, each subtree identifier is passed through an embedding layer, and the resulting vectors are concatenated with the output of the node-level LSTM to produce composite subtree embeddings. These combined vectors are then processed by a time-distributed dense layer with a non-linear activation to generate the final subtree representations. To focus the model on the most relevant structural patterns, we incorporate the attention weights extracted from SANN and apply them as multiplicative scalars to the corresponding subtree vectors. This step ensures that subtrees deemed more influential by SANN’s attention mechanism have a stronger impact during encoding.

The weighted sequence of subtree vectors is passed through an LSTM layer to capture the sequential order of subtrees within the code, followed by dropout regularization. The output is then projected to two vectors representing the mean,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and log-variance,
\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
, of the latent distribution. We use the reparameterization trick to draw a latent vector,
\mathbf{z}
, from this distribution:

\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad\text{where }\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)

The KL divergence loss is computed to regularize the latent space and encourage its distribution to stay close to a standard Gaussian:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\log\sigma_{j}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2}-\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)

The decoder reconstructs the original subtree sequence by repeating the latent vector across the sequence dimension and passing it through a second LSTM layer, followed by a dense output layer with softmax activation over the subtree vocabulary. The reconstruction loss is calculated using categorical cross-entropy between the predicted and true subtree tokens:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(\hat{y}_{t}\mid\mathbf{z})

The total VAE loss used during training is a weighted combination of reconstruction and KL divergence losses:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}

where
\beta
is a tunable parameter that controls the contribution of the KL term.

The trained VAE model serves multiple roles within our framework. Because SANN’s attention mechanism evaluates each subtree in isolation, it lacks an explicit notion of how multiple salient subtrees interact; the VAE fills this gap by reconstructing the
entire
high-attention sequence, thereby injecting the broader program context that SANN alone cannot capture. First, it allows us to generate the representative subtree patterns of the important subtrees from a given sequence of correct subtrees of a code, effectively acting as a denoising compressor that captures only the most essential aspects of program structure. Second, this design and training setup of the model facilitates the analysis of incorrect code submissions to understand which of the correct subtree patterns are incorrectly implemented. During inference, subtree sequences from incorrect submissions are passed through the model, where subtrees with high attention weights, identified by SANN, are the sources of logical errors
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
. This enables the model to suggest corrected structural equivalents for erroneous code segments by leveraging both the local context of the faulty subtrees and the global structure of the overall subtree sequences within the code. This mapping facilitates the construction of learning curves based on KC mastery to evaluate KC models
(
Rivers et al. 2016
)
. Therefore, VAE enables us to build a representation model of structural KCs that generalizes across surface variations while preserving the conceptual integrity of the programming constructs.

4.3.
Clustering Pattern-Based KCs

Following the generation of latent subtree representations through the VAE, we focus on aggregating structurally similar subtrees into representative pattern-based KCs. These pattern-based KCs serve as abstractions of the minimal functional substructures essential for solving a programming problem, independent of surface-level syntactic differences. To form these clusters, we utilize the encoded subtree vectors produced by the encoder portion of the VAE, which embed normalized high-attention subtrees derived from correct student submissions.

Before initiating the clustering process, we apply a filtering mechanism to remove anomalous student behaviors that may confound the interpretation of learning trajectories. In line with prior work
(
Hoq et al. 2024a
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
, we observe that certain students exhibit suspicious learning patterns, such as frequent failures on simpler early problems followed by sudden and consistent success on more advanced problems without intermediate improvement. These patterns, which may indicate instances of cheating or code sharing, can distort the statistical distribution of KCs and lead to erroneous conclusions. Therefore, we exclude submissions that match these anomalous trends to preserve the integrity of the KC discovery process.

Afterward, we isolate the subtree vectors corresponding to correct code submissions, which are expected to contain structurally sound and semantically valid programming constructs. We apply the K-means clustering algorithm to these vectors to group subtrees based on their similarity in the learned latent space. The clustering process is guided by the assumption that subtrees with similar latent encodings encode similar underlying knowledge, even if their syntactic representations differ. To determine the appropriate number of clusters, we employ the elbow method, a commonly used heuristic that identifies the point of diminishing returns in the variance explained by additional clusters. This ensures that the number of clusters balances representation fidelity and interpretability, preventing over-fragmentation of the knowledge space.

Each resulting cluster represents a distinct pattern-based KC, and the centroid of each cluster is treated as its canonical representative. These centroids form the basis of our KC model and act as anchors for evaluating all other subtrees. By assigning every high-attention subtree from both correct and incorrect submissions to its closest cluster center, we effectively map each subtree pattern to a representative pattern-based KC. This enables a consistent, explainable framework for tracking student learning, where the presence or incorrect student implementation of a pattern-based KC can be measured across submissions, and the actual form of the code pattern responsible for the resultant pattern-based KC can be traced back through the pipeline. Through this framework, we enable a scalable and explainable pipeline for automated knowledge modeling in programming education, grounded in structurally meaningful code representations.

4.4.
Knowledge Component Evaluation

To assess the validity and educational relevance of the extracted pattern-based KCs, we employ two well-established evaluation strategies informed by Cognitive Science: learning curve analysis
(
Rivers et al. 2016
)
and Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)
(
Duan et al. 2025
)
. These methods enable us to evaluate both the instructional soundness and predictive utility of pattern-based KCs.

4.4.1.
Learning Curve Analysis

Learning curve analysis provides a principled framework for evaluating whether a candidate KC behaves in accordance with expected student learning dynamics. Grounded in the power law of practice
(
Newell and Rosenbloom 1981
)
, a KC is theoretically expected to exhibit a decreasing error rate as learners receive more opportunities to apply it
(
Cen et al. 2006
)
.

Consistent with prior work in programming education
(
Rivers et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
, we define an
opportunity
as a student’s first submission attempt on a programming problem that exercises a particular pattern-based KC. This choice follows conventional practice in educational data mining, which recommends using first attempts to avoid confounding effects of feedback or guessing in later submissions. For each pattern-based KC, we compute the average error rate across increasing opportunity counts and analyze the shape of the learning curve. KCs exhibiting a downward error trend with increasing practice are considered
valid
, while flat or erratic curves suggest
no-learning
or
still-learning
categories
(
Rivers et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
. This form of analysis allows us to examine whether individual pattern-based KCs correspond to learnable units of programming knowledge and to categorize them into distinct learning curve types such as “good learning”, “still learning”, or “no learning”. These curve profiles guide both theoretical validation and instructional interpretation of each KC.

4.4.2.
Deep Knowledge Tracing with Pattern-based KCs

To evaluate the effectiveness of the extracted pattern-based KCs in predicting future student performance, we integrate them into the original DKT framework
(
Piech et al. 2015
)
. DKT models capture a student’s evolving mastery by representing learning as a sequence of interaction events and predicting the probability of success on future tasks.

Let
S=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{T}\}
represent a sequence of
T
student interactions over time. Each interaction
x_{t}
corresponds to the student’s attempt on a problem at time
t
, consisting of a problem identifier
q_{t}\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}
and a correctness label
a_{t}\in\{0,1\}
, where
M
is the total number of unique problems. Following the original DKT formulation, each interaction is encoded as a one-hot vector of size
2M
. Specifically, the element at index
q_{t}+M\cdot(1-a_{t})
is set to 1, indicating whether the student answered the problem correctly or incorrectly, and all other elements are set to 0.

In our enhanced model (KC-DKT), the input is extended by incorporating the pattern-based KC mastery vector associated with each submission. This is a binary vector of length
K
, where
K
is the number of pattern-based KCs. For each submission, this vector encodes the presence (1) or absence/incorrect implementation (0) of each pattern-based KC in the student’s solution. The final input
\tilde{x}_{t}
to the DKT model at time
t
is constructed by concatenating the original DKT problem-correctness encoding with the KC vector:

\tilde{x}_{t}=[x_{t}^{\text{DKT}}\parallel x_{t}^{\text{KC}}]

where
x_{t}^{\text{DKT}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
,
x_{t}^{\text{KC}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, and
\parallel
denotes vector concatenation. The architecture of our KC-DKT is shown in Figure

\{\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2},\dots,\tilde{x}_{T}\}
and outputs a sequence
\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{T}\}
, where each
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
contains the predicted probability of correctly solving each of the

q_{t+1}
is obtained from the corresponding entry in

. The model is trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function and evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), measuring its ability to distinguish between correct and incorrect future submissions. This KC-DKT model not only maintains the sequential modeling strengths of the original DKT but also leverages explainable and pedagogically meaningful representations of student knowledge. The integration of structural knowledge patterns enables a deeper understanding of how students acquire and apply programming concepts over time.

By evaluating both learning curve trajectories and DKT predictive performance, we demonstrate that the extracted pattern-based KCs fulfill the dual criteria of educational plausibility and modeling utility, consistent with principles of the Knowledge-Learning-Instruction framework
(
Koedinger et al. 2012a
)
. This comprehensive evaluation provides confidence that the discovered pattern-based KCs reflect students’ learning and can be used for adaptive instruction and learning environments.

5.
Experiments

To extract meaningful structural representations from student code, we first trained the modified SANN model from
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
to classify code submissions as correct or incorrect. The full dataset, consisting of
18{,}787
correct and
28{,}977
incorrect Java submissions across
50
problems, was split into training, validation, and test sets using an 80:10:10 stratified split to preserve label proportions due to the inherent class imbalance. The model was trained using a code embedding size of
128
with a maximum of
100
nodes in a subtree and a
100
subtree sequence in a code, over a maximum of
100
epochs with an early stopping patience of
20
, using Adamax optimizer with a learning rate of
0.001
. To ensure that the entropy regularization component guided the attention mechanism without disrupting classification performance, we set its weight to a minimal value of
3\times 10^{-5}
. The model achieved high scores across all evaluation metrics: accuracy (
0.87
), precision (
0.86
), recall (
0.88
), and F1-score (
0.87
). These results validate the effectiveness of the modified SANN model in capturing the semantics of student code for correctness prediction. Upon successful training, we extracted the intermediate attention weights and associated subtrees from the model, which formed the basis for the VAE model training.

We selected subtrees with high attention weights (above a
20\%
threshold). These high-attention subtrees represent the most influential substructures for prediction, following the original study of this model
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
. From this filtering, we obtained a total of
78,647
important subtrees from correct submissions and
132,658
important subtrees with logical errors from incorrect submissions. For representation learning, we processed the subtrees by normalizing all the identifier tokens and trained the VAE model exclusively on all the normalized subtrees from correct submissions, and the output labels were only the important subtrees. This design choice aims to encode structurally similar, valid, and pedagogically meaningful patterns and understand the overall correct code architecture. The dataset of correct subtrees was partitioned into a 60:20:20 split for training, validation, and testing, respectively. The VAE was configured with a subtree embedding size of
64
, a latent dimension of
128
, and intermediate dense layers equal in size to the embedding layer. A dropout rate of
0.2
was applied throughout to prevent overfitting. The model was trained over a maximum of
30
epochs with an early stopping patience of
10
, using the Adamax optimizer with a learning rate of
0.001
and a
\beta
-VAE formulation with a KL divergence scaling factor
\beta=1\times 10^{-2}
, balancing reconstruction fidelity and latent space regularization. The trained VAE achieved a test reconstruction accuracy of
95\%
when measured across all tokens, including padding tokens (unimportant subtrees were considered padding in the output labels). However, when focusing only on the actual important subtree token sequences (excluding padding), the reconstruction accuracy was
10\%
. While this may appear lower compared to typical autoencoder tasks, perfect reconstruction of the input subtrees was not the primary objective. Instead, the VAE was designed to learn a latent embedding space where structurally similar subtrees are mapped to nearby regions, enabling generalization across minor syntactic variations, guided by
\beta
-VAE principles
(
Higgins et al. 2017
)
, where a controlled trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and latent space regularization is maintained to facilitate structural generalization. From a total of
11,953
unique subtree patterns in the data, the VAE reconstructed
106
unique representative subtree outputs, demonstrating its ability to compress and abstract over fine-grained structural variations. To validate that the predicted subtrees are semantically meaningful and not the result of arbitrary alignment, we developed a naive baseline: subtrees from the predicted group were randomly assigned to input subtrees. Both the input and predicted subtrees using our approach and the randomized baseline were embedded using the CodeT5 model
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, and their similarity was computed using cosine similarity. Our proposed approach achieved a significantly higher mean similarity score of 0.96 (standard deviation 0.03) compared to the randomized baseline’s mean similarity of 0.85 (standard deviation 0.04) between the input and predicted representative subtrees. A statistical test confirmed that the difference between the two distributions was significant with a
p
-
value
of
0.0001
.

To form explainable and reusable units of knowledge, we clustered the VAE-generated subtree vectors into distinct pattern-based KCs using the K-means clustering algorithm. This stage focuses solely on high-attention subtrees from correct submissions, as these subtrees encode valid patterns. The elbow method was employed to empirically determine the optimal number of clusters, and
k
was set to
50
, balancing model granularity with interpretability. Each subtree vector was assigned to the nearest cluster center, and the centroid of each cluster was designated as the representative pattern for that cluster, thereby defining a set of
50
pattern-based KCs. These clusters were then used to map both correct and incorrect subtrees to their closest KC representation, enabling downstream analyses of student performance at the KC level. Therefore, our whole pipeline resulted in a set of explainable, structurally coherent, and pedagogically grounded KCs extracted directly from student code. Table

6.1.
Learning Curve Analysis

To evaluate the extracted pattern-based KCs, we perform a learning curve analysis to assess whether these KCs exhibit trends consistent with learning theory
(
Shi et al. 2023
;
Rivers et al. 2016
)
. A learning curve reflects a student’s improvement over repeated opportunities to practice a specific KC, typically showing a decline in error rate as mastery increases. Our goal is to examine whether the pattern-based KCs extracted from student code display this expected learning trajectory.

Following prior work in knowledge tracing
(
Selent et al. 2016
;
Badrinath et al. 2021
)
, we restrict our analysis to the
first submission
of each student for every problem. This decision is based on several technical and pedagogical factors. First, students are most likely to engage in an authentic knowledge application during their initial attempt before receiving feedback or trying to debug. Subsequent submissions often involve partial rewrites, trial-and-error debugging, or copy-editing behavior that may not reflect changes in conceptual understanding. Furthermore, repeated incorrect submissions may lead to disengagement or superficial retries (e.g., “submit spamming”), which introduces significant noise in mastery estimation
(
Baker 2011
)
.
Unlike trajectory-based models that track student learning across problems, our learning curves emphasize per-KC performance trends over aggregated student populations
(
Rivers et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
. For each pattern-based KC, we compute the average error rate of student submissions across sequential “opportunities”—the number of times a student encounters a code containing a particular pattern-based KC. We observe that the learning curves fall into three qualitatively distinct categories.

The first category, which we denote as
good learning
, includes
35
pattern-based KC clusters (out of
50
) containing patterns that demonstrate a clear downward trend in error rates. The learning curves exhibit the expected behavior of a steady decline in error rates, indicating that students gradually acquire mastery over these patterns through exposure. One possible reason for this trend is that the problems in the course were grouped into
5
coherent assignments (
10
problems in each assignment), with each assignment containing similar conceptual foci. Thus, repetition of similar substructures across problems within the same assignment likely contributed to the successful transfer and consolidation of knowledge (mean number of problems per KC for this category:
20
). Figure
4
illustrates the learning curves for two such pattern-based KCs.

The second category, which we refer to as
high error/still learning
, includes
10
pattern-based KCs. These clusters do not display the ideal monotonic decline typically associated with ideal learning curves. Instead, error rates start high and fluctuate in early opportunities before eventually decreasing. This trend reflects the high difficulty and recurrence of these patterns across problems. Many of these KCs involve complex conditional structures, such as nested conditionals, complex Boolean expressions, and method invocation. These constructs appear throughout the curriculum (mean number of problems per KC for this category:
30
), and the variability in contextual difficulty from problem to problem may cause students to regress temporarily in performance despite prior exposure. Additionally, the assumption of the “no-forgetting” model commonly used in traditional knowledge tracing does not hold strictly in open-ended domains, such as programming, where increased problem complexity can mask prior mastery and make students slip. First, these KCs may co-occur with other complex KCs in the same code, making it difficult to isolate their individual effects on correctness. Second, due to the lack of ground truth KC-to-error mapping in the dataset, we cannot determine whether an incorrect submission is attributable to a specific KC. Third, since the order of student problem attempts is not standardized in the CodeWorkout dataset and many students can skip problems, the aggregated error rate across opportunities may be confounded by heterogeneous problem difficulty. This learning curve analysis reveals critical insights into student understanding of programming patterns. In contrast to traditional knowledge tracing over predefined skills, our structural KC approach captures variations in mastery over emergent code structures. These findings underscore the value of explainable, data-driven KC discovery methods in CS education. By identifying the structural patterns with which students repeatedly struggle, educators can design more targeted instructional interventions or recommend personalized practice problems and worked examples. Such KC-informed adaptations hold promise for improving learning outcomes in large-scale, open-ended programming environments.

6.2.
KC Model Comparison and Ablation Study

To further evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed pattern-based KC model, we assessed its ability to explain and predict student performance within the learning curve fitting framework of the Additive Factors Model (AFM)
(
Cen et al. 2006
;
Koedinger et al. 2012b
)
. The AFM was fit to the corresponding student–problem–opportunity data. The model estimates the probability of a correct response as a function of KC-specific intercepts and learning rates. Following previous research
(
Shi et al. 2023
;
Alencar et al. 2025
)
, we compared the performance of our KC model against two common baselines: the single-KC model, which treats all submissions as practicing the same KC, and the item-KC model, which assigns a distinct KC to each unique problem, and computed model fit and predictive performance using three standard metrics: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). Lower values of AIC and BIC indicate better model fit, with BIC applying a stronger penalty for model complexity to discourage overfitting, and RMSE, on the other hand, captures the average prediction error and reflects the model’s ability to estimate student correctness accurately.

Along with these experiments, we conducted an ablation study to isolate the contribution of our representation and clustering pipeline by introducing two additional baselines. First, we replaced the VAE model with a pretrained programming language model, CodeT5
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
. We fine-tuned the CodeT5-base model (220M parameters) using the same training procedure as the original VAE and used the fine-tuned CodeT5’s encoder output instead of the VAE embeddings in the clustering process. Second, we removed the VAE entirely and directly used the SANN-generated embeddings of the subtrees to form the KC clusters. Table
2
suggests that our model consistently outperformed the baselines across all three metrics, achieving lower AIC and BIC values, indicating better model fit with appropriate complexity, and a lower RMSE, demonstrating improved predictive accuracy of student performance. These results suggest that our approach yields a more accurate and effective representation of students’ underlying KCs compared to traditional skill attribution strategies.

6.3.
Deep Knowledge Tracing

To evaluate the educational utility of our extracted pattern-based KCs, we integrated them into a DKT framework to assess their effectiveness in modeling and predicting student learning over time. Unlike previous approaches that incorporated raw code vectors as input to the DKT model
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
;
Shi et al. 2022

(
Piech et al. 2015

50

Our enhanced KC-DKT model was trained to predict whether the student would succeed on the next attempt based on their KC state history. To make the evaluation fair, we split the dataset at the student level using an 80:10:10 ratio for training, validation, and testing, ensuring no overlap between students across splits. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Keras Tuner. The best performing configuration used an LSTM layer with
512

7.
Discussion

Knowledge Components (KCs) are fundamental units
of knowledge that students must learn and apply to solve problems. Accurate identification and modeling of KCs are crucial for understanding student learning processes, predicting performance, identifying struggles, and providing targeted instructional support. However, manual extraction of KCs is labor-intensive
and may not capture variations in student approaches, especially in domains such as programming, where there are multiple solution paths
(
Shi et al. 2024b
)
.

In this work, we introduced
pattern-based KCs
and proposed an automated, explainable, and data-driven approach to extract pattern-based KCs from student code submissions. We define pattern-based KCs as the recurring structural patterns within student code that capture the specific programming patterns and constructs that students must master to successfully solve programming problems. Using the explainable SANN model, our method identifies important AST substructures that correspond to essential programming patterns. These substructures were then converted to representative patterns by the VAE model and clustered to form pattern-based KCs, capturing both granular and complex coding constructs. We evaluated the effectiveness of these KCs through learning curve analysis, showing student learning with a decreasing trend in error rate, and by integrating them into the DKT model, showing significant improvement of over
13
% performance gain, demonstrating their educational relevance and predictive utility.

Our approach offers several advantages over traditional methods. Firstly, it improves explainability; the use of SANN allows for the visualization of attention weights on specific code structures, enabling educators to understand and validate the extracted KCs. Moreover, the original code pattern can be traced back through the framework from the resultant pattern-based KC if we want to examine what the student actually implemented. This transparency contrasts with existing deep learning models for KC extraction, which often operate as black boxes and annotate code with unexplainable KCs, limiting their applicability in educational settings. Secondly, the hierarchical clustering of AST substructures captures a spectrum of programming patterns, from simple granular patterns to complex logic, reflecting the diverse ways students solve problems. This granularity supports scalable and adaptable pattern-based KC discovery across various programming tasks.

The implications of our findings are important for adaptive learning systems. By accurately modeling student mastery at the KC level, our approach facilitates real-time feedback on specific coding patterns, enabling personalized learning approaches
(
Corbett et al. 1997
)
. For example, if a student consistently struggles with a particular loop construct, the system can provide targeted exercises or resources associated with that pattern to address this gap
(
Abdelrahman et al. 2023
)
. Pattern-based KCs can also inform content recommendation engines, ensuring that students receive problems
(
Baker et al. 2006
)
or worked examples
(
Hosseini et al. 2020
)
that align with their current knowledge and learning needs. Our work bridges the gap between automated KC extraction and pedagogical applicability, offering an explainable, scalable, and data-driven effective solution for modeling student learning in CS education.

8.
Limitations and Future Work

Although our work demonstrates the effectiveness of pattern-based KC extraction using AST structures and explainable attention-based modeling, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the dataset used in this work consists of Java programming submissions from a CS1 course, where most solutions are relatively short (10–20 lines of code). However, such brevity is typical of early programming curricula and appropriate for studying foundational skills
(
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
.

Second, our approach is based on the successful parsing of code into ASTs, which excludes uncompilable submissions. Although this removal is necessary and follows previous work
(
Shi et al. 2023
;
Hoq et al. 2025b
)
, it results in the loss of valuable data, particularly data that could be associated with important misconceptions or conceptual gaps. Future work should explore syntactic-error-resilient parsers or embedding models that can tolerate and learn from uncompilable code fragments. Moreover, during our evaluation, a small subset (less than
1
%) of code submissions was identified as incomplete, lacking one or more essential components required to fulfill the problem specification. These instances pose a unique challenge for KC analysis, as it is difficult to determine whether missing components reflect conceptual misunderstanding, student carelessness, or strategic partial submissions (e.g., incremental debugging). Our current framework, which relies on structural patterns, does not account for such contextual nuances. In future iterations, we aim to integrate semantic program components or correct functional constructs necessary to solve a problem in the framework to better identify and handle incomplete or underspecified submissions.

In addition, in our evaluation process, we followed the standard practice of knowledge tracing and learning curve analysis by focusing exclusively on students’ first submission attempts. Although this choice reduces noise caused by feedback-driven debugging, it introduces a limitation: we cannot learn from repeated practice opportunities for KCs across multiple attempts. As noted in
(
Baker 2011
)
, students can submit multiple times not to refine their understanding but to guess or experiment, making it challenging to infer true learning intent from later attempts. Modeling and analyzing post-first-submission behaviors, particularly distinguishing between genuine skill refinement and surface-level debugging, remains a rich direction for future work.

Another important direction for future work should be the generation of natural language descriptions for the extracted pattern-based KCs. Currently, KC representations exist as structural subtrees, which may not be directly interpretable by novice learners or instructors. By leveraging the natural language generation capabilities of large language models, we aim to automatically generate human-readable explanations or summaries of each KC. This would further enhance the accessibility, pedagogical utility, and feedback generation potential of our framework in real educational settings. Additionally, we intend to incorporate our extracted pattern-based KCs into a recommendation system for CS1 classrooms that supports adaptive instruction by recommending worked examples or new practice problems targeted at KCs where students demonstrate difficulty. Such an integration would allow our framework to not only model student knowledge but also actively support learning in real-time educational systems.

9.
Conclusion

In this work, we presented an automated and explainable framework for extracting pattern-based KCs from student programming submissions with the integration of a VAE model with SANN and clustering.
We evaluated the resulting pattern-based KCs through learning curve analysis and an improved DKT model, demonstrating that they exhibit both educational plausibility and predictive utility. Our approach advances the state of knowledge modeling in CS education by offering scalable, explainable, and structurally grounded representations of student understanding.
