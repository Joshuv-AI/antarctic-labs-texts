SynComs should be rigorously defined, not renamed
Authors: Miaoxiao Wang, Yong Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Koch et al. propose replacing “synthetic microbial communities (SynComs)” with “defined microbial communities”. We argue that this substitution obscures the design-driven logic of SynCom research, and that the field needs clearer conceptual classification rather than a broader renaming of SynComs.

Koch et al. argue that the term “synthetic microbial communities (SynComs)” should be replaced by “defined microbial communities” to avoid ambiguity and negative connotations1. We agree that the rapid expansion of SynCom research has led to terminological inconsistency. The increasing popularity of SynComs has been accompanied by inconsistent use of the term across articles, reviews, and even textbooks. In some cases, SynCom has been used to describe any artificially assembled microbial consortium, regardless of whether the system was designed to test ecological principles, predict community behavior, or simply formulate a multi-strain product. This conceptual dilution deserves attention.

However, we disagree that the solution is to replace SynCom with “defined microbial community”. This proposal places existing conceptual ambiguities into a broader terminological container without clarifying the logical relationships among related concepts in the field of microbial ecology and community engineering. We are concerned that such a replacement may inadvertently hinder the development of the field, especially for newcomers who may adopt terms uncritically rather than considering the design logic and scientific purpose behind their model systems.

SynComs are more than defined communities

First, we would like to clarify the meaning of “synthetic”. Koch et al. frame synthetic biology largely in terms of genetic modification. The authors note that some so-called SynComs are composed of naturally occurring, non-genetically modified microorganisms, and therefore argue that the term synthetic is inappropriate because synthetic biology typically involves the genetic modification of microorganisms. However, we argue that modern synthetic biology is not defined solely by genetic engineering. The term “synthetic” does not necessarily imply genetic modification; rather, it refers to the deliberate assembly of a composite system from defined building blocks, such as microbial species or strains. In this sense, a SynCom can be synthetic because its community structure is intentionally constructed from the bottom up, regardless of whether its members are genetically modified.

In addition, we argue that the intellectual core of synthetic biology has shifted toward rational design, modular construction, quantitative characterization, and predictive frameworks. Indeed, many SynCom studies using naturally occurring microorganisms are motivated by precisely these goals. For example, after isolating microorganisms, many studies examine pairwise interactions among them, build models such as generalized Lotka–Volterra models, consumer–resource models, or genome-scale metabolic models to predict the outcomes of different assemblages, rationally assemble SynComs based on these predictions, test their performance, and learn principles that can guide further SynCom construction2–4. This process generally follows the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) cycle, a standard framework in synthetic biology for the systematic development and optimization of biological systems5. Many recent perspectives have suggested that the DBTL cycle should become an important future direction for building and analyzing SynComs6–8.

Therefore, we argue that replacing “synthetic” with “defined” may erase some of the most distinctive scientific logic of SynComs. “Defined” refers mainly to compositional knowledge, that is, the members of a community are known, isolated, preserved, and reproducibly combined. This is an important property, but it does not capture the intellectual core of SynCom research. SynComs are not merely microbial mixtures with known membership. At their best, they are rationally designed systems, based on the properties of individual species, interactions, and modules, built to test hypotheses, identify causal mechanisms, develop predictive principles of community dynamics, and enable controllable community function7,9,10. Notably, we argue that containing genetically modified microorganisms is not a strict criterion for SynComs.

Clarification of terminology in community engineering

Instead of terminological substitution, we propose that a more sustainable solution is to rigorously define and classify all relevant concepts involved in community engineering (Box 1). Figure 1 illustrates the relationship among these key concepts. Specifically, we argue that “defined microbial community” and “synthetic microbial community” are not competing terms, and that SynComs should meet higher standards than “defined microbial communities”. “Defined microbial community” can be used in any scenario in which the community has a known composition and is reproducible and standardizable. “Synthetic microbial community” should add further requirements to this baseline; that is, the community should be rationally designed, its dynamics and functions should be quantitatively characterized, and studies using it should generate predictive insights for community engineering more broadly. In ecological terminology, a community generally refers to an assemblage of populations from different species11. We therefore suggest considering the term “synthetic microbial consortium” (SynCon) to describe a microbial system comprising genetically modified variants of a single species, where appropriate, for greater terminological precision. Although several publications also referred to such systems as SynComs12–14, we suggest that SynCon is more appropriate in an ecological context. We think that SynCon is a broader term that can refer to deliberately assembled microbial systems composed of either multiple species or different engineered variants within a single species (Box 1).

Donor natural community

A natural microbial community selected as the source of isolated strains, a whole-community inoculum, or the starting material for top-down modification. A donor natural community may serve as a source of strains for the bottom-up construction, a starting material for top-down engineering, or a donor for community transplantation1,16,17.

Engineered microbial community

An umbrella term for any microbial community that is deliberately manipulated to achieve a scientific or practical objective. It includes both bottom-up communities assembled from isolated members and top-down communities modified from natural microbiomes6,8.

Bottom-up engineering

The deliberate construction of a microbial system by isolating, combining, and controlling individual strains or species6,18.

Defined microbial community

A deliberately assembled community composed of different microbial species that are known, and its composition can be reproducibly reconstructed and standardized1,19.

Synthetic microbial community (SynCom)

A defined microbial community that is rationally assembled to investigate causal mechanisms or achieve predictable community functions, ideally supported by quantitative characterization of its members and interactions, design principles, and experimentally testable predictions7,9,18.

Synthetic microbial consortium (SynCon)

A deliberately constructed microbial consortium composed of functionally distinct microbial members. Unlike “community”, “consortium” does not necessarily require multiple species and is therefore more appropriate for systems composed of different engineered genotypes of one species20.

Top-down engineering

The modification of a donor natural community without first isolating and reassembling its individual members, aiming to shift community composition and function to a desired outcome6,8.

Modified natural microbial community

A community derived from a donor natural community through top-down engineering, whose composition is altered relative to the donor community but is not necessarily fully defined or reconstructable from individually characterized isolates1.

Enrichment

A top-down procedure in which a natural community is repeatedly cultivated under controlled environmental or nutritional conditions that favor microorganisms performing a desired function21,22.

Artificial selection of microbial communities

A top-down procedure in which replicate communities are evaluated for a desired community-level phenotype, and the best-performing communities are propagated to subsequent selection cycles23,24.

Directed evolution of microbial communities

An iterative process that combines the generation or maintenance of heritable community variation with selection for a target community-level function. Directed evolution exploits repeated variation–selection–propagation cycles to improve community performance and may involve evolutionary changes within member populations as well as ecological changes in community composition25,26.

Transplantation

The transfer of an intact or minimally processed microbial community from a donor environment into a recipient environment1,17.

Recipient natural community

The resident microbial community into which an engineered community or a donor natural community is introduced1,17.

Amended microbial community

The community that results after a recipient natural community has been supplemented with an exogenous engineered community or a donor natural community1.

Naturalness bias should not guide terminology

Koch et al. also argue that “synthetic” may evoke negative public connotations because of naturalness bias. We agree that this is a valid concern in commercial communication, where public acceptance and regulatory clarity are important. However, the assumption that “natural is better” is itself problematic. Naturalness bias has been linked to science skepticism, which involves distrust of scientific evidence and the rejection of conclusions supported by scientific consensus15. Scientific terminology therefore should not be shaped primarily by accommodating the misconception that “natural” is inherently better than “synthetic”. On the contrary, academic discourse should help correct this bias. If scientists avoid the term “synthetic” simply because it may be viewed unfavorably by the public, this may inadvertently reinforce the very misunderstanding that science seeks to overcome. Many foundational terms in biology, including artificial selection, genetic engineering, and synthetic biology, have required careful explanation rather than avoidance. The same should apply here. The appropriate response to naturalness bias is transparent communication: explaining that many SynComs consist of natural isolates, and that “synthetic” refers to deliberate design and assembly rather than artificial or unsafe organisms.

More broadly, this discussion should not be framed as a simple opposition between “natural” and “engineered”. Both nature-based and engineered interventions can be viewed as different forms of human action aimed at improving human well-being, and neither is inherently superior or risk-free. In the context of sustainability, the key question is therefore not whether an intervention is described as natural or synthetic, but whether its potential ecological and environmental consequences have been carefully considered before implementation. Retaining the term “synthetic” can thus be compatible with sustainable microbiome engineering.

Clearer definitions, not broader renaming

Terminology matters because it shapes how a field thinks. We fully agree with Koch et al. that the inconsistent use of SynCom creates confusion within and between fields, and that replacing SynCom with “defined microbial community” may facilitate some industrial applications. However, it does not solve the deeper problem of conceptual ambiguity. We argue that the field needs clearer definitions, not a retreat from the term synthetic. SynComs should be defined more rigorously, not renamed more broadly. Given the importance of terminology for both scientific communication and public understanding, the establishment of a dedicated international committee or professional working group to evaluate and standardize terminology is highly desirable. Such a body could coordinate community-wide discussions, issue consensus recommendations, and promote consistent usage across publications, conferences, educational materials, and regulatory documents.
