Approaching-unity PLQY and high stretchability in polymer emitters via molecular spacers
Authors: Glingna Wang, Wei Liu, Zhiming Zhang, Cheng Zhang, Benjamin T. Diroll, Naisong Shan, Yang Li, Mohammed Syed Nurul Azam Azmir, Xiaodan Gu, Sihong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stretchable organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) hold great promise for wearable displays and optical biointerfaces, yet progress is hindered by the intrinsic trade-off between mechanical stretchability and high emission efficiency. Here, we present a broadly applicable strategy to enhance both stretchability and light-emitting performance in thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) polymers through the incorporation of optoelectronically inert small-molecule plasticizers. Using dioctyl phthalate (DOP) as a model additive, we show that plasticizers function as molecular spacers, expanding free volume to suppress triplet exciton quenching while facilitating stress-dissipative chain mobility. The resulting composites achieve approaching-unity photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), stretchability beyond 110% strain, and improved electroluminescent efficiency, with external quantum efficiency (EQE), reaching 12.6% in rigid devices and 3.05% in fully stretchable OLEDs. This strategy is effective across a range of TADF polymers, demonstrating plasticizer engineering as a simple, scalable design principle for intrinsically stretchable optoelectronic materials.

Introduction

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) have revolutionized modern display and lighting technologies, offering advantages such as high brightness, wide viewing angles, fast response, low driving voltages, and low power consumption. Recent advancements are extending these devices into mechanically soft and deformable form factors—most notably stretchable OLEDs, which emerge as critical components for next-generation wearable and bio-integrated applications1–8, including on-skin displays, sensors9, imagers10, and optical stimulators11. Such applications demand emissive materials that achieve high stretchability together with high electroluminescent (EL) efficiency, which requires new design strategies for OLED emitters.

Recently, thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) polymers have emerged as promising candidates for stretchable OLEDs due to their capability in achieving high efficiency from harnessing both singlet and triplet excitons to realize theoretical 100% internal quantum efficiency (IQE)12. This marks a clear advantage compared to fluorescent emitters13–19, which are limited to 25% IQE. Compared to phosphorescent emitters20,21, TADF designs also do not require heavy metals, thereby offering higher biocompatibility. Recently, stretchable designs for TADF systems have been realized through molecular engineering of TADF polymers to increase the polymer-chain flexibility. However, the emitter efficiency is still lower than that of the state-of-the-art TADF polymers.

To further increase the light-emitting efficiency of stretchable TADF emitters while realizing high stretchability, we propose that the packing structure of polymer chains plays a critical role. High efficiency in TADF emitters requires the suppression of triplet-triplet annihilation22–25, which can be achieved by increasing the packing distance between TADF units to reduce local triplet concentrations. Simultaneously, greater packing distance between polymer chains improves chain mobility, thereby enhancing mechanical ductility and stretchability26–28. We therefore hypothesize that physically increasing the packing distance in TADF polymers can simultaneously improve both electroluminescent performance and mechanical compliance. Compared to strategies involving new molecular designs19,29–31, this physical approach offers the advantages of simplicity and broad applicability across various TADF polymer systems.

Here, based on this hypothesis, we propose a universal design strategy of using small-molecule plasticizers that have been widely employed for commodity polymers to increase the packing distance of TADF polymers (Fig. 1a), so as to both enhance efficiency by suppressing triplet concentration quench (Fig. 1b) and improve stretchability by increasing chain mobility (Fig. 1c). We utilize two types of phthalate plasticizers of different molecular sizes (Fig. 1a), dioctyl phthalate (DOP) and dimethyl phthalate (DMP), to demonstrate this design strategy on five different TADF polymers with rigid mechanical properties. The larger-size plasticizer—DOP— increased the TADF polymer’s PLQY by as high as 60% to almost 100%, and the increase of OLED EQE by up to 12.6%, alongside the increase of the stretchability to 110% strain. When this strategy was applied to the EML, the performance of fully stretchable OLED devices also improved to a similar extent. The simplicity of this method will allow it to be applied to other TADF polymer designs to achieve even higher efficiency and broaden the optical properties of stretchable OLEDs. Results

Design of molecular spacers

Plasticizers have been broadly used as processing additives for commodity polymers to enhance flexibility, reduce brittleness, and improve processability by lowering the glass transition temperature. Over the years, a wide variety of plasticizers have been developed, including phthalates, adipates, citrates, and bio-based alternatives32,33. For different polymer systems, the choice of the plasticizer must ensure good miscibility to achieve uniform dispersion. Because TADF polymers are constructed by π-conjugated structures, we reason that the plasticizers for implementing our design strategy should also contain aromatic units. Phthalate structures meet this requirement by having a benzene ring. To compare the effect of plasticizer molecular size34, we selected two representative phthalates for this study: DOP, with longer side chains and a larger molecular size, and DMP, with shorter side chains and a smaller molecular size. Notably, both plasticizers possess large energy band gaps and thus do not interfere with the intrinsic energy levels of the TADF polymer (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each plasticizer was co-dissolved with a selected TADF polymer at controlled weight ratios in chlorobenzene. These mixed solutions were subsequently spin-coated to form emissive thin films for device fabrication and characterization.

Enhancement of PL and EL properties

The effect of phthalate plasticizers on PL and EL performance was first evaluated in a representative TADF polymer with decent performance—PDKCE (Fig. 2a)35. For thin films prepared by spin-coating, the gradual increase of the DOP amount from 0 (i.e., neat PDKCE film), to 12.5%, and then to 25% (all asweight ratio relative to PDKCE) led to an increase in PLQY from 60% to 97%, corresponding to 165% of the initial value (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 2). Upon further increasing the DOP:PDKCE weight ratio to 50% and 75%, the PLQY is mostly saturated, with only minor further increases. Using time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements (Fig. 2c) to separate the prompt fluorescence (PF) and the delayed fluorescence (DF) in the PLQY, we can find that the increase mainly comes from the DF. This indicates that DOP’s molecular spacing effect could primarily serve to suppress triplet-exciton quenching. In comparison, blending the smaller phthalate plasticizer DMP into PDKCE films results in a maximum PLQY increase of only 16% relative to the neat polymer (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 3–5). This confirms the importance of molecular size in this phenomenon. It is hypothesized that plasticizers reduce aggregation among TADF moieties, thereby suppressing concentration-driven quenching of triplet excitons. To verify this, we began by measuring the PLQY of PDKCE in solution using chlorobenzene as the solvent. As shown in Fig. 2e, concentration quenching is evident from the decrease in the PLQY at the higher PDKCE concentration, starting from an approaching-unity PLQY at 0.08 mg/mL. With the addition of DOP at a weight ratio of 75% to PDKCE, the PLQY exhibited a much less pronounced decrease with increasing PDKCE concentration. This confirms DOP’s effect in suppressing concentration quenching. In comparison, using the smaller-molecule plasticizer DMP led to a weaker suppression effect, which agrees with the trend observed in thin-film PLQY testing (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To validate that this effect is mainly associated with the delayed fluorescence from triplet excitons, we further tested the effect of these plasticizers on Super Yellow (SY), a fluorescent polymer with little triplet emission. In both solid-state thin films and chlorobenzene solutions of different SY concentrations (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 8), the incorporation of DOP and DMP had no measurable impact on the fluorescent polymer’s PLQY under all testing conditions. Therefore, these results further support the conclusion that the primary effect of the phthalate plasticizers is to suppress concentration-driven quenching of triplet emissions.

The impact of plasticizer incorporation on EL performance was subsequently evaluated using a standard OLED device structure (Fig. 2g). As shown in Fig. 2h, the incorporation of DOP in PDKCE EML films has little influence on the current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics compared to the pristine polymer, indicating that charge transport remains largely unaffected by the presence of the additive, as further validated by hole-only device measurements (Supplementary Fig 9). At the same time, the luminance increases substantially at higher DOP concentrations (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 11). As a result, the maximum EQE increased from 9.3% for the neat PDKCE to 12.6% for the EML with DOP at a weight ratio of 75% relative to PDKCE, which corresponds to a 35.5% increase (Fig. 2i). It should be noted that the efficiency enhancement does not originate from difference in EML thicknesses, but from the higher PLQY of the corresponding EMLs (Supplementary Fig. 10). The comparison with previously reported representative intrinsically stretchable EMLs is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The electroluminescence spectra remain invariant across plasticizer weight ratios (Fig. 2i, inset; Supplementary Fig. 12), confirming that the emissive states and energy transfer mechanisms are unaltered by the incorporation of DOP (Supplementary Fig. 13). Collectively, these results underscore that spatial dilution via phthalate additives can enhance electroluminescence efficiency without compromising charge transport or photophysical features.

Enhancement of mechanical stretchability

The effect of DOP on the mechanical properties of PDKCE thin films was then investigated. As shown in Fig. 3a, the addition of DOP reduces the Young’s modulus substantially, from 447 MPa to 13.2 MPa at the highest DOP weight ratio of 75% relative to PDKCE, as evidenced by both wrinkling (Fig. 3a) and direct float-on-water tensile (Supplementary Fig. 14) measurements. Concurrently, the stretchability improves dramatically, with the crack-onset strain (COS) increasing from 5% to 110% (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 15). A similar improvement in COS is observed with DMP, indicating that both plasticizers can enhance mechanical compliance despite their differences in molecular size (Supplementary Figs. 16–18). These effects are likely to stem from the typical plasticizing behavior of phthalates, which increases polymer chain dynamics by weakening interchain packing, as reflected by the reduced modulus and increased stretchability, consistent with increased free volume in the polymer network. This reduced interchain packing can be observed through rheological measurements of PDKCE solutions in chlorobenzene. With increasing DOP weight ratio (Fig. 3c), a gradual decrease in viscosity at low shear rate is observed, which indicates decreased interactions between polymer chains. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) shows that both pristine and DOP-plasticized PDKCE films are amorphous (Supplementary Fig. 19). Notably, the composite films lose miscibility and show obvious microscale phase separations at plasticizer blending ratios higher than 100 wt.% (Supplementary Fig. 20). During stretching to 100% strain, the PLQY of DOP-PDKCE (75%) thin films remains stable at an approaching-unity level (Fig. 3d), demonstrating that the emission properties are maintained under mechanical deformation. Consistently, PL decay measurements under applied strain reveal that exciton dynamics remain unchanged (Fig. 3e).

Fully stretchable OLEDs

A fully stretchable OLED was then fabricated using the DOP-plasticized EML. The device structure (Fig. 4a) comprises a silver nanowire (AgNW) anode, a stretchable PEDOT:PSS_PFI (Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate)-perfluorinated ionomer) hole transport layer (HTL), a DOP:PDKCE (75 wt.%) EML, a PEIE_PFN-Br electron transport layer (ETL), and a liquid metal eutectic gallium-indium (EGaIn) cathode (Supplementary Fig. 22). The sheet resistance of the EGaIn electrodes stayed fairly stable under different strains in the operational range of the fully stretchable OLEDs (Supplementary Fig. 23). The device exhibits a low turn-on voltage of 3.5 V (Fig. 4c), making it compatible with commercial battery operation, and emits light only from the AgNW side, aligning with typical display requirements. At the initial state, the EQEmax is ca. 3.0% (Fig. 4e). During stretching to 60% strain, the devices exhibited stable luminance and current density (Fig. 4b, c, and f). The EQEmax showed less than a 15% decrease (Fig. 4g). To show the benefit of DOP to the performance of fully stretchable OLED devices, we further carried out comparisons with another group of devices made with neat PDKCE EMLs. At the initial state, the current density is 58% lower than that of devices with PDKCE: DOP emitters at 6 V, while the luminance is 32% lower (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 24). The resulting EQEmax is only about 1.5%, which is half of the PDKCE:DOP devices. During stretching, the decrease in the EL performance is much more pronounced (Fig. 4e, f, and g). Further expanding the EL performance comparison to three additional DOP ratios—12.5%, 25%, and 50%—revealed a clear increasing trend in both luminance and EQE with higher DOP content (Supplementary Fig. 25). This comparison unequivocally shows the dual effects of DOP in enhancing both the EL performance and stretchability of fully stretchable OLED devices.

Broad applicability to different TADF polymers

To assess the broad applicability of the plasticizer-induced enhancements in both luminescence performance and stretchability, we applied DOP blending to four additional TADF polymers (Fig. 5a): PTLA36, PTT37, PDKCM, and PDKCP19. For all tested TADF polymer thin films, PLQY increases with increasing DOP content. At the highest tested DOP amount of 75 wt.%, the PLQY reaches 146–183% of the neat polymer values, indicating substantial enhancement (Fig. 5b). Notably, despite disparate backbones and frontier-orbital energies, all four polymers show similar PLQY–versus-loading trends, consistent with an electronically inert, spacing-driven mechanism rather than host–guest energy-level matching (Supplementary Fig. 26; Supplementary Table 2). When DMP is used as the plasticizer, the PLQY increases to a lesser extent, further validating the molecular size effect underlying this phenomenon in these polymers (Supplementary Figs. 27–33). In parallel, the films with this highest DOP weight ratio (75 wt.%) also exhibit much higher stretchability than the neat polymer films, as evidenced by the substantially decreased crack sizes and densities at 50% or 75% strain (Fig. 5c). Discussion

In summary, for the development of high-performance, stretchable TADF polymer emitters, this work shows that reducing interchain packing by incorporating commodity plasticizers as molecular spacers can enhance both stretchability and light-emitting efficiency. Using a commercial plasticizer, DOP, we created TADF emitters with a PLQY approaching 100%, an OLED EQE of 12.6%, and a stretchability exceeding 110% strain. Looking ahead, the broad applicability of this approach positions it as a complementary strategy to ongoing efforts in the design and synthesis of new TADF polymer structures, enabling the development of high-performance, stretchable emitters with diverse optical properties.

Methods

Materials

Lithium fluoride (LiF) was purchased from Xi’an Polymer Light Technology. Poly[(9,9-bis(3′-((N,N-dimethyl)-N-ethylammonium)-propyl)−2,7-fluorene)-alt-2,7-(9,9-dioctylfluorene)] (PFN-Br) was purchased from 1-Material. Polyethylenimine, 80% ethoxylated solution (PEIE), and Nafion perfluorinated resin (PFI) solution (527084-25 ml, 5 wt% in a mixture of lower aliphatic alcohols and water) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without changing the concentration, unless mentioned. PEDOT:PSS solution (Clevios P VP CH 8000, 2.4–3.0 wt.% in water) was purchased from Heraeus Deutschland and used without changing the concentration, unless mentioned. SEBS H1221 with a volume fraction of poly(ethylene-co-butylene) = 88% was provided by Asahi Kasei. Dow Corning SYLGARD 184 silicone elastomer clear kit was used to prepare polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) with different base/crosslinker mix ratios. Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU; Elastollan 1185 A) was provided by BASF. AgNWs (AW045) as suspensions in water were purchased from Zhejiang Kechuang Advanced Materials. Gallium (Ga) and indium (In) for liquid metal were purchased from Amazon. Patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrates were purchased from Ossila. Plasticizers DOP (Di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate), DMP (Dimethyl phthalate), Triethyl citrate, and Bisphenol A (4,4′-isopropylidenediphenol) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. SY (Super Yellow) and RED (Red Light-Emitting Spiro-Copolymer; Sigma-Aldrich, Product No. 900447).) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Other general reagents and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich or Fisher Scientific. PDKCE35, PDKCM19, PDKCP19, PTLA (P(TMP-l-AcBPAc))36 and PTT (PNB-TAc-TRZ-5)37 are previously reported polymers and were prepared following literature procedures as cited. 1H NMR spectra for the synthesized polymers (PDKCE/PDKCM/PDKCP) are provided in Supplementary Figs. 34–36; characterization for PTLA and PTT are available in the corresponding literature.

General characterization

Absorption of plasticizers was measured using a Shimadzu UV-3600 Plus ultraviolet–visible– near-infrared spectrophotometer. The PL spectra were measured with a Horiba spectrofluorometer, Fluorolog 3 instrument. Time-resolved emission experiments were performed on samples loaded into an evacuated nitrogen cryostat. Time-resolved emission was performed by exciting the sample with ultraviolet pulses prepared by directing the output of a 35-fs Ti: sapphire laser into an optical parametric amplifier, with emission monitored using a streak camera. The PLQY of thin film was measured with an LSM Series high-power light-emitting diode (310 nm, Ocean Optics) as the light source and a fiber integration sphere (FOIS-1) coupled with a QE Pro spectrometer (Ocean Optics) as the spectrometer. The samples were held on a homemade stage to allow the light source to excite them, and the emitted light was collected with the integration sphere. The PLQY of the solution was measured under nitrogen (air-free) conditions using an FLS1000 spectrometer equipped with an integrating sphere (F-M101), with the solution placed in a quartz cuvette and excited at a 310 nm pulsed Xenon lamp. Static GIWAXS measurements were performed at beamline 11-BM of National Synchrotron Light Source II, Brookhaven National Laboratory. The beam energy is 10 keV. The samples were tilted at an incident angle of 0.14° with respect to the incident beam and exposed for 10 seconds for each scan. The samples were placed under helium protection.

Surface treatment for the substrates (Si wafer, glass or quartz)

Hexadecyltrimethoxysilane (HTS)-functionalized substrates were prepared as follows. A pre-cleaned substrate was treated with O₂ plasma (150 W) for 2 min and then placed in a vacuum oven at room temperature, where it was exposed to HTS vapor overnight to allow surface functionalization. The substrate was then sonicated in toluene for 5 min, followed by thorough cleaning and drying before use.

Characterization of stretchable TADF polymer thin films

The TADF polymer was dissolved in chlorobenzene (CB) solution at 60 °C overnight to ensure complete dissolution. A predetermined solid state weight ratio of plasticizer was then introduced into the polymer solution, followed by vigorous vortex mixing to homogenize the blend. The thin films were prepared by spin-coating the polymer solution in CB (8mg/ml) onto HTS-modified Si substrates (HTS-Si) at 2000 rpm, followed by annealing at 110 °C for 20 min. This leads to film thicknesses of around 45–100 nm, depending on the plasticizer blending ratio. The films were transferred to PDMS stamps to apply different strains and then transferred to SEBS-coated Si substrates for characterization. The other parameters for making the films are the same as our stretchable polymers. For the time-resolved emission measurement, the films were transferred to SEBS-coated quartz substrates.

Grazing-incidence wide-angle x-ray scattering

Samples for GIWAXS measurements were prepared on plasma-treated Si substrates by spin coating. Static GIWAXS measurements were performed at beamline 11-BM of National Synchrotron Light Source II, Brookhaven National Laboratory. The beam energy is 10 keV. The samples were tilted at an incident angle of 0.14° with respect to the incident beam and exposed for 10 seconds for each scan. The samples were placed under helium protection.

Fabrication and testing of conventional rigid OLEDs

The ITO-coated glasses were first cleaned with 1 vol% Hellmanex solution, isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and deionized water, and then dried and further treated with O2 plasma (150 W, 10 min). The PEDOT:PSS_PFI mixture solution in IPA (PEDOT:PSS:PFI = 1:1:3 (v:v:v)) was spin-coated at 4500 rpm for 1 min and then annealed at 130 °C for 30 min as the hole injection layer/hole transporting layer. Then, TADF EMLs were spin-coated in a glovebox from the polymer solutions of 8 mg/ml in CB. The spin-coating condition is 2000 rpm for 30 s, which is followed by annealing at 110 °C for 10 min. Next, thermal evaporations were carried out to sequentially deposit TPBi as the electron transporting layer, LiF as the electron injection layer, and Al as the cathode, with the corresponding deposition rates of 1.0, 0.1, and 10.0 Å/s, respectively. The J–V–L measurements were carried out at room temperature in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. A Keithley 2450 source meter and fiber integration sphere (FOIS-1) coupled with a QE Pro spectrometer (Ocean Optics) were used for the measurements. The OLED devices were tested on top of the integrating sphere, where only forward light emission can be collected. The absolute OLED emission was calibrated by a standard visible–near-infrared light source (HL-3P-INT-CAL plus, Ocean Optics). EQE of the device is computed from the ratio of emitted photon flux to injected electron flux using the integrating-sphere spectral radiant flux and the source current (Keithley 2450 SourceMeter). Fabrication of stretchable semitransparent electrodes

AgNW electrode was fabricated as follows: AgNW solution (AW045, 1 wt.% in water) was first vortexed for 10 min to remove big aggregations and then diluted in IPA (1:19 by volume), followed by 30 s bath sonication. Next, the solution was spray-coated on a cleaned OTS-Si substrate at 130 °C until the target sheet resistance of around 20 Ω/sq was reached. The pattern was made with Kapton tape masks. After removing the masks, the AgNW on OTS was washed with deionized water to remove the surfactant, and then dried at 110 °C for 5 min. Next, a TPU solution (20 mg/ml in tetrahydrofuran (THF)) was spin-coated onto AgNW at 3000 rpm for 30 s. After drying at 110 °C for 20 min, AgNW covered by TPU was treated with O2 plasma (150 W) for 1 min. Then, the PDMS mixture (base:crosslinker ratio of 15:1) was drop-casted onto TPU and subsequently degassed under a vacuum and cured at 80 °C for 4 h. After delamination from the OTS-Si substrate, the AgNW_TPU/PDMS electrode was ready for the fabrication of fully stretchable OLEDs.

Fabrication of stretchable cathodes based on liquid metal

GaIn liquid metal (LM) electrodes were fabricated as follows. LM was first dispersed in a methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK) solution (1 g/10 mL) and sonicated at 50% power for 15 s to break up large aggregates. The dispersion was then centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded, and the LM was washed twice with isopropanol (IPA) to remove residual MIBK. The resulting LM dispersion was diluted in 20–30 mL of IPA and spray-coated onto a glass substrate covered with Teflon tape at 130 °C until the target sheet resistance of approximately 0.5 Ω/sq was reached. The patterning was defined using Kapton tape masks, which were removed after deposition. The LM electrodes were then dried at 110 °C for 5 min before being encapsulated. A PDMS mixture (base: crosslinker ratio of 15:1) was drop-cast onto the LM electrodes and subsequently degassed under vacuum before curing at 80 °C for 4 h. After delamination from the Teflon tape substrate, the LM/PDMS electrode was ready for use in the fabrication of fully stretchable OLEDs.

Fabrication of fully stretchable OLED devices

The separately prepared AgNW_TPU/PDMS electrode was laminated onto an OTS-treated glass substrate (OTS-glass) with the AgNW side facing up, and the electrode was treated with O₂ plasma (150 W) for 10 s to improve wettability. The PEDOT: PSS_PFI mixture solution in IPA (PEDOT:PSS:PFI = 1:1:3 (v:v:v)) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 1 min and annealed at 130 °C for 30 min, forming a 110 nm film. A PDKCE solution (8 mg/ml in CB) blended with different plasticizer concentrations was then spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 110 °C for 10 min, yielding a 50−100 nm emissive layer. Next, a PEIE_PFN-Br solution (total 0.25 wt% in methanol at a weight ratio of 1:1) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 110 °C for 10 min, forming a 20 nm electron injection/transporting layer. The samples were then released from the Teflon-glass substrate. A separately prepared LM/PDMS electrode was gently laminated onto the cathode. Finally, the device was heated at 80 °C for 5 min to enhance interlayer adhesion. The performed measurements were the same as those for the rigid devices.
