Allograft Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Reconstruction Using Patellar Suture Anchors and Stepwise Medial Imbrication
Authors: Mark M. Kodsy, Tony Ezzat, Simon Yacoub, Lambert Li, Jeremy Adelstein, Bhargavi Maheshwer, Michael J. Salata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatellar instability commonly affects adolescents and young athletes and is associated with injury to the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL). MPFL reconstruction is a well‐established treatment for recurrent instability; however, complications related to graft tensioning, fixation, and incomplete restoration of medial soft‐tissue restraints persist. We describe a surgical technique for allograft MPFL reconstruction that emphasizes stepwise imbrication of the medial knee layers, anatomic femoral tunnel placement, and all‐suture fixation to the patella. This approach restores both the static restraint provided by the MPFL graft and the dynamic stabilizing effect of the medial soft tissues, creating a “bumper effect” analogous to principles of shoulder stabilization. This technique provides a thoughtful and durable method for MPFL reconstruction and may be performed as an isolated procedure or in conjunction with adjunctive bony or cartilage procedures.

This video shows an allograft medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction with deliberate, sequential imbrication of the medial knee layers in a patient with recurrent lateral patellar instability of the left knee. The patient is positioned supine, and a thigh‐level tourniquet is applied. Diagnostic arthroscopy is performed using a 30° arthroscope through the anterolateral portal to evaluate articular cartilage, patellar tracking and tilt, and trochlear morphology. Following arthroscopy, a medial patellar incision is created to expose the medial soft tissues. Medial knee layers 1 (vastus medialis oblique and sartorial fascia), 2 (native medial patellofemoral ligament and medial retinaculum), and 3 (synovium) are identified, dissected, and tagged to allow controlled, sequential imbrication. A semitendinosus allograft is then secured to the proximal two‐thirds of the patella using all‐suture anchors, avoiding patellar bone tunnels. Femoral tunnel localization is performed under fluoroscopic guidance with provisional placement of a 2.4 mm Beath pin at Schöttle's point. Graft isometry is assessed dynamically prior to definitive tunnel preparation. The graft is passed between layers 2 and 3 and into the femoral tunnel, incorporating a rescue stitch to assist with graft passage and provide a contingency option for lateral femoral cortical fixation if needed. Femoral fixation is achieved with an interference screw with the knee positioned at approximately 30° of flexion. Sequential imbrication of medial knee layers 3, 2, and 1 is then performed, including advancement of the vastus medialis oblique to enhance dynamic medial stabilization. Final assessment shows restoration of medial restraint and stable patellar tracking through the knee range of motion. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70087.

Patellofemoral instability occurs in adolescents and young athletes and accounts for a meaningful proportion of acute knee injuries. 1 , 2 Incidence rates are highest in adolescents, reaching 23.2 per 100,000 person‐years, with recurrence rates after a first‐time dislocation ranging from 15% to over 50%. 1 , 3 , 4 The mechanism of injury involves a noncontact twisting motion with valgus overload, resulting in lateral patellar displacement. 2 , 3 Although isolated dislocation may be treated with conservative management, recurrent instability can be functionally limiting and associated with cartilage injury and increased risk of patellofemoral osteoarthritis. 5

Patellar stability rests on osseous anatomy and soft‐tissue restraints. During the first 30° of knee flexion, when the patella is not fully engaged in the trochlea, the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary restraint against lateral translation. 6 The MPFL spans from the superomedial patella to the medial femoral epicondyle and contributes up to 60% of resisting force against lateral dislocation. 1 Rupture of the MPFL occurs in most acute dislocations, with some series reporting rates as high as 98%. 6 Recent literature has emphasized reconstruction of the broader medial patellofemoral complex, rather than isolated graft reconstruction alone, to better restore native soft tissue restraint and patellar tracking. 7

Evaluation of suspected patellofemoral instability begins with a thorough history and physical examination. Radiographs may display trochlear dysplasia or patella alta, while advanced imaging with computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging can assess MPFL integrity, associated osteochondral injury, and tibial tubercle‐trochlear groove distance. 8 , 9 Risk factors for recurrent instability include trochlear dysplasia, tibial tubercle‐trochlear groove distance >20 mm, patella alta, and ligamentous laxity. 10

MPFL reconstruction remains a cornerstone of operative management, either performed in isolation or combined with adjunctive bony procedures such as tibial tubercle osteotomy or trochleoplasty. 1 , 3 Numerous reconstruction techniques have been described, using autograft, allograft, or synthetic materials. Although outcomes are generally favorable with low recurrence rates, complications such as graft overtensioning, patellar fracture from bone tunnels, and nonanatomic femoral fixation remain concerns. 6 Contemporary techniques describe modifications aimed at mitigating these complications, including the use of all‐suture patellar fixation, anatomic tunnel placement, and reinforcement of medial soft tissues to supplement graft restraint. 11 , 12

The purpose of this article is to describe a thoughtful approach to MPFL reconstruction. The individual patient in this presentation did not reveal findings of chondral damage requiring intervention; however, a strength of the presented technique includes the ability to intervene on cartilage lesions during this stabilizing procedure (Figure 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The incision for exposure of the medial aspect of the patella and dissection of the medial knee layers is marked in line with the anteromedial portal. We additionally mark an incision for later femoral drilling for the allograft centered over the posterior aspect of the medial femoral epicondyle, extending proximally (Figure 2, Video 1). The “saddle” for the femoral tunnel is found superior and posterior to the epicondyle.

(A) Intraoperative clinical photograph showing supine positioning of the patient with the left lower extremity elevated on BoneFoam orthopedic ramp (Corcoran, MN) to assist with lateral fluoroscopic imaging (red star). A thigh‐level nonsterile tourniquet is applied (blue star). Additional soft “bump” is placed under the ipsilateral hip to assist with internal rotation of the lower extremity (not pictured). (B) Preincision photograph illustrating planned surgical incisions. A medial incision for the patella (white star) is marked approximately 4 cm in length and in line with the standard anteromedial portal. A secondary incision for lateral bone tunnel is marked posterior to the medial femoral epicondyle and extended proximally (yellow arrow). The “saddle” for the femoral tunnel is superior and posterior to the medial epicondyle.

Diagnostic arthroscopy is initiated with a 30° arthroscopic camera entering the anterolateral portal. As aforementioned, this individual patient did not have magnetic resonance imaging findings consistent with severe chondral injury or loose bodies but did have mild fraying of a subcentimeter region of the medial patellar facet (Figure 3). The limb is exsanguinated, and the tourniquet is inflated after the knee is flexed to relax the quadriceps complex. A 4 cm longitudinal incision is made sharply through skin and subcutaneous tissue along the medial aspect of the patella. Using dissecting scissors, layer 1, which includes sartorial fascia and vastus medialis oblique, is identified, along with layer 2, which includes the native MPFL, patellar retinaculum, and capsule. Layers 1 and 2 are tagged with a 1 Vicryl (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) suture. Layer 3, comprised of synovium, is typically seen bulging from recent arthroscopy and incised sharply and tagged with a 1 Vicryl suture.

(A) Intraoperative arthroscopic images of the left knee as viewed from the anterolateral portal with 30° arthroscope in both (A,B). The undersurface of the patella is demarcated by “P” in both images. There is evidence of mild, partial‐thickness chondral injury to the medial patellar facet (red arrow). (B) Arthroscopic visualization (anterolateral portal of left knee) of trochlear dysplasia (“T” in (B)) with relative lateral patellar tilt, confirming anatomic contributors to patellar instability. (P, patella; T, trochlea.)

A rongeur and curette are used to create a bleeding bony surface for all‐suture anchors into the patella, located at midline and proximal two‐thirds of the patella. Two 2.8 mm Q‐FIX suture anchors (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) are drilled across the patella and deployed. The strands of the superior anchor are separated, and 1 end is shortened. A free needle is used on the longer strand to pass through the semitendinosus tendon allograft (BioCleanse, Alachua, FL) 3 times. The suture is tied to itself 3 times with figure 4 knots. This allows for the graft to be shuttled to the patella by pulling on the short end of the strand. This strand is then tied to itself around the graft, followed by the other colored suture in a similar fashion to secure the graft to the patella. This process is repeated for the inferior suture anchor.

We move to the medial side of the knee where the previous incision was marked over the posterior medial epicondyle and incise through skin and subcutaneous tissue sharply. A plane is developed toward medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle, at which point the saddle between these landmarks can be palpated. The 2.4 mm Beath pin (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) is provisionally placed in the saddle region, 9 mm proximal and 5 mm posterior to the medial epicondyle. Positioning at Schöttle's point is confirmed with fluoroscopy (Figure 4). The pin is advanced in a proximal direction to avoid the intercondylar notch and through the skin of the lateral thigh. At this juncture, graft tension can be checked by tying the graft to the pin and flexing the knee. If the graft is too tight with flexion (“high and tight”), the pin should be repositioned distally. In contrast, if the graft is too loose with flexion (“low and loose”), the pin should be moved proximally. A cannulated reamer matching the graft size is used to ream over the Beath pin to a depth of 50 mm.

Intraoperative lateral fluoroscopic image of the left knee showing placement of a 2.4 mm Beath pin (white star) is visualized and Schöttle's point (white circle). Fluoroscopic confirmation of anatomic femoral tunnel positioning is performed prior to advancement to ensure appropriate graft isometry during knee range of motion.

Now, tensioning of medial‐sided soft tissues begins with imbrication of layer 3. This is accomplished by passing the more inferior‐based sutures from the suture anchors from deep to superficial and tied (Figure 5). We turn back to the allograft and tie a “rescue stitch” ETHIBOND*EXCEL (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) halfway between the graft loop (Figure 6). The graft is passed between layers 2 and 3 into the medial wound. The suture is passed through the Beath pin eyelet, and the pin is pulled through the lateral side of the knee, allowing the graft to seat in the tunnel as the wire is pulled. The knee is placed over a bump at 30° of flexion, and the 7 × 25 mm BIOSURE Regenesorb interference screw (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) is advanced over the 1.2 mm guidewire (Figure 7).

Final tensioning of the medial soft tissues is completed similar to the previous imbrication of layer 3. The remaining sutures from the anchors are passed medially through layer 2 followed by layer 1 and tied, avoiding knots directly over patellar tissue to avoid future irritation (Figure 8). As a technical note, tensioning of layer 1 constitutes a vastus medialis oblique advancement which provides a mechanical advantage to prevent lateral subluxation of the patella during quadriceps contraction. The remaining subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed in layers. The use of skin staples provides a safeguard against wound complications, especially in a population of patients with ligamentous laxity in a high‐tensile region. Postoperative rehabilitation is stratified into 4 phases with full weight‐bearing beginning immediately (Figure 9).

(A) Intraoperative photograph depicting imbrication of layer 2 (blue arrow) which begins with passing suture from lateral to medial from both the superior and inferior suture anchors in the patella. (B) Imbrication of layer 1 (white arrow) is performed, which constitutes a VMO advancement for mechanical, dynamic advantage when the quadriceps complex is activated. (VMO, vastus medialis oblique.)

Summary of our 4‐phase postoperative rehabilitation protocol and return to sport for patients undergoing MPFL reconstruction with a focus on early range of motion. E‐STIM is incorporated to facilitate quadriceps activation during early recovery. (E‐STIM, electrical muscle stimulation; MPFL, medial patellofemoral ligament.)

DISCUSSION

A host of proposed techniques for the management of patellar instability with MPFL reconstruction exists. Although the use of all‐suture anchors has grown for graft fixation into the patella for ease of application and potential reduced risk of patellar fracture compared with traditional bone tunnel techniques, we are not aware of a published technique highlighting the importance of tensioning the remainder of medial‐sided tissue after these typically violent dislocation events. 13 Recently described techniques report double‐bundle and multilimb MPFL reconstruction and underscore growing interest in restoring anatomic restraint patterns but typically focus on graft configuration rather than deliberate, layered soft tissue imbrication. 14

The disadvantages of the proposed technique include over‐tensioning and failure at the all‐suture anchor and interference screw. Advantages include (1) intraoperative assessment of tensioning and adjustment of Beath pin, (2) supplementing medial tissue tensioning with sequential imbrication of layers 1 through 3 mirroring the “bumper effect” obtained from a Bankart repair of the shoulder, and (3) use of rescue suture that permits alternative lateral femoral cortical fixation if interference screw fixation proves inadequate (Tables 1 and 2). The sequential soft tissue tensioning and careful handling of the synovial layer in particular mirrors that of the previously described modified Langenskiöld reconstruction. 15 , 16 The authors have found the above technique as a thoughtful, reproducible, and durable approach to MPFL reconstruction with or without concurrent tibial tubercle osteotomy or cartilage preservation.
