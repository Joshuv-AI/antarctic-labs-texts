Error exponent of activated non-signaling assisted classical-quantum channel coding
Aadil Oufkir, Marco Tomamichel, Mario Berta

Abstract
We provide a tight asymptotic characterization of the error exponent for classical-quantum channel coding assisted by activated non-signaling correlations. Namely, we find that the optimal exponent — also called reliability function — is equal to the well-known sphere packing bound, which can be written as a single-letter formula optimized over Petz-Rényi divergences. Remarkably, there is no critical rate and as such our characterization remains tight for arbitrarily low rates below the capacity. On the achievability side, we further extend our results to fully quantum channels. Our proofs rely on semi-definite program duality and a dual representation of the Petz-Rényi divergences via Young inequalities.

Introduction

Shannon
[
44
]
showed that the asymptotic transmission rate of classical information through a noisy channel can be characterized by a single number
C
, called the capacity. In fact, the capacity demarcates a transition point in the following sense: On the one hand, it is possible to send a number of messages

with a rate

n

,
43
,
15
]
. On the other hand, it is not possible to send information with a rate
r=\frac{1}{n}\log M>C
above the capacity without incurring an error probability going to one exponentially in
n

[
19
]
. The notion of capacity was later generalized by Holevo
[
25
]
to coding over channels with classical input and quantum output, so-called classical-quantum channels. Interestingly, the capacity of classical and classical-quantum channels remains the same when assistance via shared randomness, or quantum entanglement is available (see, e.g.,
[
28
]
). These additional resources can be seen as particular cases of non-signaling assistance, which is assistance by devices shared between the sender and receiver that both parties can interact with, but that cannot by themselves be used for communication. In fact, the one-shot meta-converse of Polyanskiy, Poor, and Verdú
[
41
]
and Hayashi
[
21
]
exactly corresponds to the setting of non-signaling assisted strategies
[
31
]
. In contrast, for classical-quantum channels, the meta-converse cannot be achieved with the help of plain non-signaling correlations, but rather requires the additional help of a single-bit of perfect communication
[
50
]
.

Indeed, in the setting of classical-quantum channels, the meta-converse and the setting of non-signaling assistance provide two different semi-definite programming (SDP) relaxations for determining the optimal error probability for coding over classical-quantum channels. Understanding the relationship between these programs, and more generally, the impact of additional resources on higher-order refinements to Holevo’s capacity theorem, can offer fundamental information-theoretic insights. First, converse expansions of the meta-converse immediately imply converses to plain, shared randomness, and entanglement-assisted codes. Second, following the rounding results of
[
3
,
18
]
for the success probability for coding, achievability expansions of the meta-converse can be translated into achievability bounds for plain codes (see also
[
48
]
). This then gives deeper insights about the type of quantum Rényi divergences to find in formulas for higher order capacity refinements — which in turn allows to analyze the tightness of existing bounds on the error probability (including the necessity of critical rates and alike).

In the following, we focus on a particular higher-order refinement: the error exponent, also known as the reliability function. It governs the decay of the error probability of sending information with a fixed rate below the capacity over many identical copies of the channel. We study the classical-quantum error exponents for the case of non-signaling assisted codes as well as for the meta-converse. These two are exactly the same for classical channels and as such our work can be seen as a generalization of Polyanskiy’s classical findings
[
40
]
.

We will next give an overview of our results. The precise definitions of the involved quantities are given in the subsequent section.

2.2
Plain coding error probability

Given a classical-quantum channel
\mathcal{W}
that maps an input
x
from a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
to a quantum state
W_{x}
, the coding problem is to transmit classical messages through an encoding, decoding scheme. More precisely, let
M
be the number of the messages to be transmitted. An encoder is a mapping from the set of integers between
1
and
M
to elements in the alphabet
\mathcal{X}
or equivalently a subset
\mathcal{E}=\{x(1),\dots,x(M)\}\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
constituted of elements from the alphabet
\mathcal{X}
. A decoder is given by a POVM
\mathcal{D}=\{\Lambda_{x(1)},\dots,\Lambda_{x(M)}\}
(a set of positive semi-definite matrices that sum to identity). A code is given by the encoding codebook and the decoding measurement
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
. The error probability of transmitting the
m^{\text{th}}
message

is

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x(m),\mathcal{W}):=1-\mathrm{Tr}\left[W_{x(m)}\Lambda_{x(m)}\right]

(19)

and the plain average error probability is then given by the expression

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\mathcal{C},M,\mathcal{W}):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varepsilon(x(m))=1-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[W_{x(m)}\Lambda_{x(m)}\right].

(20)

The optimal plain error probability is given by optimizing over all possible codes
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\varepsilon(M,\mathcal{W}):=\inf_{\mathcal{C}}\varepsilon(\mathcal{C},M,\mathcal{W}).

(21)

In Section
3
, we describe a possible relaxation of the optimization (
21
) obtained by allowing the sender and receiver to share non-signaling correlations.

With
n
uses of the (iid) channel
\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
, transmitting a number of messages with a rate
r
corresponds to
M=e^{nr}
. A message
m\in[M]
is encoded to a codeword
x_{1}^{n}(m)=x_{1}(m)\cdots x_{n}(m)
and sent through the iid channel
\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
. The decoder receives the state
W^{\otimes n}_{x_{1}^{n}(m)}=W_{x_{1}(m)}\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{x_{n}(m)}
and performs the measurement
\{\Lambda_{x_{1}^{n}(m^{\prime})}\}_{x_{1}^{n}(m^{\prime})}
.
The average error probability takes the form

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\mathcal{C},M,\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})=1-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left[W^{\otimes n}_{x_{1}^{n}(m)}\Lambda_{x_{1}^{n}(m)}\right].

(22)

It is known that to send messages with a rate strictly smaller than the capacity of a channel the error probability can be exponentially small. More precisely, for a given classical-quantum channel
\mathcal{W}
with capacity
C(\mathcal{W})
, the error probability of sending

messages (where

) with

\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
, has the form

\displaystyle\varepsilon(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})=e^{-nE(r)+o(n)}

(23)

for some function

called error exponent or reliability function.

Meta-converse and non-signaling coding error probability

The starting point for our derivations is the meta-converse for the one-shot coding error probability
[
41
,
32
]
. Recall that for a classical-quantum channel
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
and a probability distribution
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we define
p\circ\mathcal{W}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)|x\rangle\!\langle x|\otimes W_{x}
and write
p=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)|x\rangle\!\langle x|
. Then, the meta-converse error probability is

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})

\displaystyle:=\inf\Big\{\varepsilon:\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})}D_{H}^{\varepsilon}(p\circ\mathcal{W}\|p\otimes\sigma)\geq\log M\Big\}

(24)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})}\inf_{0\preccurlyeq O\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}}\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[p\circ\mathcal{W}(\mathbb{I}-O)\right]\;\Big|\;\mathrm{Tr}\left[p\otimes\sigma O\right]\leq\tfrac{1}{M}\Big\}.

(25)

Classically, it is known
[
31
,
50
]
that the meta-converse corresponds to the setting where the sender and the receiver share non-signaling (NS) correlations. A NS-assisted code corresponds to a super-channel which is non-signaling from the encoder to the decoder and vice-versa. More precisely, a non-signaling channel between Alice and Bob whose input systems are

and output systems are

has a positive semi-definite Choi matrix

satisfying (e.g.,
[
14
]
)

\displaystyle J_{A_{i}B_{i}B_{o}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}\otimes J_{B_{i}B_{o}},

\displaystyle(A\nrightarrow B),

(26)

\displaystyle J_{A_{i}A_{o}B_{i}}

\displaystyle=J_{A_{i}A_{o}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B_{i}},

\displaystyle(B\nrightarrow A).

(27)

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of non-signaling super-channels. The optimal non-signaling success probability of a classical quantum-channel
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
for sending
M
messages is

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(M,\mathcal{W}):=\sup_{\Pi\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bra{m}\Pi(\mathcal{W})(|m\rangle\!\langle m|)\ket{m},

(28)

where
\Pi(\mathcal{W})
is the effective channel. It is known that this success probability is the solution of the following semi-definite program (SDP)
[
32
,
50
,
18
]

\begin{split}1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(M,\mathcal{W})=\sup_{\Lambda,p}&\quad\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{x}W_{x}\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Lambda_{x}=\mathbb{I}\\
&\quad 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{x}\preccurlyeq p(x)\mathbb{I}\qquad\forall{x\in\mathcal{X}}\\
&\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)=M.\end{split}

(29)

As remarked by
[
50
]
, this program differ from the meta-converse program
(
25
) in the constraint

that should be
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Lambda_{x}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}
in the latter (see (

) later on). Note that we always have the inequality
\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})\leq\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(M,\mathcal{W})
. Moreover,

]
relates these two apparently different programs using the notion of activated capacity. More precisely, they show in

that allowing a perfect communication of a single bit using the channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}(\cdot):=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\bra{i}(\cdot)\ket{i}|i\rangle\!\langle i|

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})=\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(2M,\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})\leq\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(2M,\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})\leq\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W}),

where the first equality follows from

. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})=\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(2M,\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2}).

Note that for classical channels we have

]

\displaystyle E^{\rm{ANS}}(r):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2}).

\displaystyle E^{\rm{ANS}}(r)=E^{\rm{MC}}(r):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}).

Hence, it suffices to focus on the meta-converse for our derivations of the activated non-signaling error exponents. The meta-converse program (

\begin{split}1-\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})=\sup_{\Lambda,p}&\quad\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{x}W_{x}\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Lambda_{x}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I}\\
&\quad 0\preccurlyeq\Lambda_{x}\preccurlyeq p(x)\mathbb{I}\qquad\forall{x\in\mathcal{X}}\\
&\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)=M.\end{split}

4
Non-signaling assisted error exponents

4.1
Main result

In Section

In particular, we defined the plain error exponent as the decaying rate of the optimal error probability for sending information with rate below the capacity. Since non-signaling assisted codes are at least as powerful as their plain counterpart, the error probability should also decay exponentially with the number of channel uses below capacity

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})=\exp(-nE^{\rm{NS}}(r)+o(n)).

Motivated by the meta-converse, we consider the activated non-signaling error probability as defined in Section

2

5
On activation and the need for a critical rate

5.1
Approach

In this section we prove an achievability bound on the non-signaling error probability without activation. In contrast to the setting where activation is allowed (Proposition
4.2
), we are only able to get the error exponent termed with the Petz Rényi divergence under some assumptions:
(a)
coding over channels with positive non-signaling assisted zero-error capacity or
(b)
coding over general channels above a certain critical rate. The first observation is that if we can use a sub-linear number of copies of the channel to send noiselessly one bit then activation is not needed for error exponents as it can be simulated without incurring a significant loss in the rate transmission. We discuss this observation in more details in Section
5.2
. The second attempt to go beyond the activation assumption uses a rounding result on the non-activated non-signalling error probability, as well as an inequality between Petz and sandwiched Rényi divergences (Section
5.3
).

5.2
Coding over channels with positive zero-error capacity

The non-signaling capability
M^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W})
of a classical-quantum channel
\mathcal{W}
is the largest number of messages that can be transmitted through a channel with zero-error and when non-signaling correlations are available
[
13
]
. The non-signaling zero-error classical capacity is then defined as the optimal rate of the transmitted information over iid channels with non-signaling strategies achieving zero-error

C^{\rm{NS}}_{0}(\mathcal{W})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M^{\rm{NS}}(\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}).

(74)

The zero-error non-signalling assisted capacity is pleasantly quantified by
C^{\rm{NS}}_{0}(\mathcal{W})=C_{0}(\mathcal{W})

[
13
]
. In this section, we consider a channel
\mathcal{W}
with positive zero-error classical capacity and show that, in this case, activation is not needed when considering non-signaling error exponents.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a classical-quantum channel with
C_{0}(\mathcal{W})>0
, and
r\geq 0
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{NS}}(r)=E^{\rm{ANS}}(r).

(75)

Proof.

If
\mathcal{W}
is a channel with positive zero-error capacity then there is

such that

which means
n_{0}
copies of the channel
\mathcal{W}
, assisted by non-signaling correlations, can be used to transmit one bit of information with zero error. Now, for an integer
n\geq n_{0}

, (see (

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n-n_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{nr}}\sum_{m=1}^{e^{nr}}\bra{m}\Pi^{n}(\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n-n_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})(|m\rangle\!\langle m|)\ket{m}.

(76)

We want to construct a super channel
\widetilde{\Pi}^{n}
that takes into input only iid copies of
\mathcal{W}

\mathcal{I}_{2}
. To this end, we use

\mathcal{W}
to simulate
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, we call this non-signaling super channel

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{e^{nr}}\sum_{m=1}^{e^{nr}}\bra{m}\Pi^{n}\circ(\mathrm{id}^{n-n_{0}}\otimes\Pi^{n_{0}}_{\rm{sim}})(\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})(|m\rangle\!\langle m|)\ket{m}

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{nr}}\sum_{m=1}^{e^{nr}}\bra{m}\Pi^{n}(\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n-n_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})(|m\rangle\!\langle m|)\ket{m}

(78)

\displaystyle=1-\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n-n_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2}).

(79)

Hence, we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})\leq\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})\leq\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n-n_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2}).

(80)

From Proposition
4.2

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(e^{nr},\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n})

Non-signaling assisted quantum error exponents

In Section

that gives the dual formulation of the meta-converse and activated non-signaling error probabilities in the quantum setting.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}
be a quantum channel with Choi matrix

\displaystyle(J_{\mathcal{W}})_{RB}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{|A|}\ket{i}\bra{j}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}(\ket{i}\bra{j}_{A}).

(118)

The one-shot meta-converse and activated non-signaling error probabilities satisfy

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm{NS}}(2M,\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{2})=\varepsilon^{\rm{MC}}(M,\mathcal{W})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\sup_{Z_{B}\succcurlyeq 0}\Big\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sqrt{\rho_{R}}(J_{\mathcal{W}})_{RB}\sqrt{\rho_{R}}\wedge M\rho_{R}\otimes Z_{B}\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[Z_{B}\right]\Big\}.

(119)

Using this formulation, we obtain the achievability bound of the activated non-signalling error probability in the quantum setting.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{W}_{A\rightarrow B}
be a quantum channel with Choi matrix
(J_{\mathcal{W}})_{RB}
,
r\geq 0
, and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\rm{ANS}}(r)\geq\sup_{\rho_{R}\in\mathcal{S}(R)}\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\mathcal{S}(B)}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\Big(D_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\rho_{R}}(J_{\mathcal{W}})_{RB}\sqrt{\rho_{R}}\|\rho_{R}\otimes\sigma_{B})-r\Big).

(120)

The proof is similar to the proofs of Propositions
3.1
&
4.2
, and is included in Appendix
E
for completeness. Similar to Section
5
, one could achieve the same performance as in Proposition
6.2
without activation for channels with positive zero-error non-signaling classical capacity or above a critical rate. On the other hand, the converse result (sphere-packing bound) is hard to generalize to the fully quantum setting even for plain coding. The main obstacle for this generalization is the minimization over arbitrary quantum input that can be non product. This is similar to the difficulty faced in the problem of quantum channel discrimination with adaptive strategies (see, e.g.,
[

Conclusion

We demonstrated that the (activated) non-signaling error exponents for coding over classical-quantum channels are determined by the sphere packing bound with Petz-Rényi divergence for all rates, analogous to the classical case. Moreover we showed that activation is not needed for channels with positive zero-error non-signaling capacity, channels outputting pure states, and for all channels above a critical rate. Future research directions include
(a)
investigating the necessity of activation for optimal non-signaling error exponents,

(b)
generalizing the sphere packing bound to the fully quantum setting, and
(c)
designing rounding protocols similar to the ones in

]
on channel simulation, or
[

Method of types

Let
n

\mathcal{X}
be a finite alphabet of size
|\mathcal{X}|

be an element of
\mathcal{X}^{n}

(121)

A type

\mathcal{X}
of the form

T=\Big\{\frac{n_{x}}{n}\Big\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\text{ where }n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}n_{x}=n.

(122)

The set of types of alphabet
\mathcal{X}
of length

is denoted
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathcal{X})

(123)

This simple bound can be proved using the simple observation that each

in (

x_{1}^{n}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
has type
T
and write

we have
{n(x|x_{1}^{n})}={n}T_{x}
. A probability distribution

(124)

Let

be a type and

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim T}\left[\var(v^{x}\|p^{x})\right]}{\tfrac{4}{n}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim T}\left[\var(v^{x}\|p^{x})\right]}=\frac{1}{4}.

Appendix D
Reverse Petz-sandwiched Rényi inequality

Here, we prove the full version of Lemma
5.4
along with new inequalities.

Lemma D.1

(Reformulated Lemma
5.4
)
.

We have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma),

\displaystyle\forall\rho\succcurlyeq 0,\,\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}),\,\forall\alpha\in(0,1],

(185)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma),

\displaystyle\forall\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}),\,\forall\sigma\succcurlyeq 0,\,\forall\alpha\in[0,2].

(186)

In particular, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

(187)

recovering the reverse inequality of
[
26
]
.

The inequality (
186
) was proven for
\alpha\in(1,2)
in
[
27
, Corollary 3.6]
and then extended to
\alpha\in[0,2)
in
[
51
, Proposition 11]
. In the following we provide a self contained proof.

Proof.

To prove
\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)
we can use
[
47
, Proposition 4.12]

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma^{\otimes n}}(\rho^{\otimes n})\|\sigma^{\otimes n})

(188)

and prove that the inequality
\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)
holds for commuting
[\rho,\sigma]=0
. In the case where
[\rho,\sigma]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2-\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\right]

(189)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]

(190)

so in case of
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(\rho^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha(2-\alpha)}\sigma^{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right]\leq\left(\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha(2-\alpha)}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma\right]^{(1-\alpha)^{2}}

(191)

which is true by Hölder’s inequality.

We move to prove
\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)
. We make cases depending on whether
\alpha\in(0,1)
or
\alpha\in(1,2)
. Note that for
\alpha=1
we have
\widetilde{D}_{1}(\rho\|\sigma)=D(\rho\|\sigma)=D_{1}(\rho\|\sigma)
.

Case
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

From the definitions of the sandwiched and Petz Rényi divergences, we have that

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right],

(192)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2-\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}.

(193)

Let
A=\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
and
B=\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
. Let
p=2
,
q=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}
and

. We have
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{r}
so by the generalized Hölder’s inequality
[
6
]
we have

\displaystyle\|AB\|_{r}\leq\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{q}.

(194)

Observe that

\displaystyle\|AB\|_{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(AB^{2}A^{\dagger})^{\frac{r}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

(195)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}})^{2}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}.

(196)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\|A\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(AA^{\dagger})^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[AA^{\dagger}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(197)

\displaystyle\|B\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(BB^{\dagger})^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}})^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\right]\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}=1.

(198)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{q}\geq\|AB\|_{r}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}.

(199)

Therefore by taking the logarithm in both sides then multiplying by

we obtain the desired inequality

\displaystyle{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]\leq-\frac{2-\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]=\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma).

(200)

Case
\alpha\in(1,2)
.

From the definitions of the sandwiched and Petz Rényi divergences, we have that

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right],

(201)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}.

(202)

Let
A=\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
B=\rho^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}
. Let
p=\frac{2}{2-\alpha}
,
q=\frac{2}{\alpha-1}
and
r=2
we have that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{r}
so
by the generalized Hölder’s inequality
[
6
]
we have

\displaystyle\|AB\|_{r}\leq\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{q}.

(203)

Observe that

\displaystyle\|AB\|_{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(ABB^{\dagger}A^{\dagger})^{\frac{r}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

(204)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}})^{2}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{2}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(205)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\|A\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(AA^{\dagger})^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}},

(206)

\displaystyle\|B\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(BB^{\dagger})^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\rho^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\right]\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\right]\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}=1.

(207)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left[\big(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right]\right)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{q}\geq\|AB\|_{r}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(208)

Therefore by taking the logarithm in both sides then multiplying by

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right]=D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma).

(209)

∎

Lemma F.2
.

We have for

\displaystyle\sup_{s\geq 0}s^{1-\alpha}c-s=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}c^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

We apply this inequality with

.

Lemma F.3
.

Let
X
be a compact convex set. Let
G
be a finite group acting on
X
. Let
f
be a convex real valued function on
X
. If
f
is invariant under the action of
G
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall x\in X,\forall g\in G:f(g\cdot x)=f(x),

(233)

f
achieves its infimum at a point invariant under the action of
G

Proof.

Let
x^{\star}
such that
\inf_{x}f(x)=f(x^{\star})
. By the convexity of

we have that:

\displaystyle f(x^{\star})\leq f\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}g\cdot x^{\star}\right)\leq\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f\left(g\cdot x^{\star}\right)=f(x^{\star}).

(234)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{x}f(x)=f(x^{\star})=f\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}g\cdot x^{\star}\right)

(235)

and
\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}g\cdot x^{\star}
is invariant under the action of
G
.
