Quantitative analysis of imaging characteristics in lung adenocarcinoma in situ using artificial intelligence
Authors: Wensong Shi, Yuzhui Hu, Yulun Yang, Yinsen Song, Guotao Chang, He Qian, Zhengpan Wei, Liang Gao, Yingli Sun, Ming Li, Hang Yi, Sikai Wu, Kun Wang, Yousheng Mao, Siyuan Ai, Liang Zhao, Huiyu Zheng, Xiangnan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the rising incidence of pulmonary nodules (PNs), lung adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) is a critical early stage of lung cancer, necessitating accurate diagnosis for early intervention. This study applies artificial intelligence (AI) for quantitative imaging analysis to differentiate AIS from atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), aiming to enhance clinical diagnosis and prevent misdiagnosis.MethodsThe study analyzed 1215 PNs with confirmed AAH, AIS, and MIA from six centers using the Shukun AI diagnostic module. Parameters evaluated included demographic data and various CT imaging metrics to identify indicators for clinical application, focusing on the mean CT value's predictive value.ResultsSignificant differences were found in several parameters between AAH and AIS, with nodule mass showing the highest predictive value. When comparing AIS to MIA, total nodule volume was the best predictor, followed by the maximum CT value.ConclusionThe mean CT value has limited discriminative power for AIS diagnosis. Instead, the maximum CT value and maximum 3D diameter are recommended for clinical differentiation. Nodule mass and volume of solid components are strong indicators for differentiating AIS from AAH and MIA, respectively.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality and incidence worldwide. 1 Targeted interventions, coupled with enhanced early detection and treatment modalities, are pivotal in reducing cancer‐related deaths. The increased utilization of low‐dose chest CT scans in routine health assessments has elevated the detection of pulmonary nodules (PNs). While not all PNs are malignant, all advanced lung cancers originate from nodules. Early‐stage lung cancer prognosis is markedly superior to that of later stages. Screening for lung nodules can facilitate the early identification of suspicious early‐stage lung cancer, thereby enabling timely treatment and the reduction of lung cancer mortality. However, distinguishing benign from malignant nodules and determining the appropriate timing for intervention present significant clinical challenges, with the risk of both overtreatment and delayed diagnosis.

Ground‐glass nodules (GGNs), a subset of PNs, are increasingly prevalent, particularly among younger individuals. Persistent GGNs are often classified as noninflammatory in clinical practice. Given the distinct growth patterns of ground‐glass opacity (GGO)‐like lung adenocarcinomas compared to solid tumors, 2 , 3 and the presence of an “indolent phase,” clinical decision‐making for lung adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) demands meticulous consideration. Deciding the frequency of follow‐up examinations, the timing of intervention, and the choice of intervention methods is especially complex in young patients. A surgical biopsy confirming lung AIS may result in curing a patient but also labeling them as a “lung cancer survivor,” potentially impacting their life trajectory.

Lung AIS marks a critical juncture in lung cancer development, classified as stage 0 lung cancer, and represents the initial transition from normal to neoplastic cells. Accurate clinical diagnosis of lung AIS is essential for early detection and treatment, preventing tumor progression due to oversights and misdiagnoses, and enabling personalized clinical decision‐making tailored to its “indolent growth” characteristics. Variability in manual measurements, blood vessel traversal, and the presence of air bronchogram signs, traditionally based on subjective experience, can lead to inconsistencies among physicians. This study leverages artificial intelligence (AI) for a quantitative and precise analysis of the imaging characteristics of lung nodules from six centers, pathologically confirmed postoperatively as lung AIS. The objective is to enhance understanding of lung AIS and inform precise clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

From May 2012 to April 2024, a retrospective analysis was performed on a cohort study of 1215 PNs, postoperatively confirmed as atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), lung AIS, and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), collected from six medical centers (distribution and time span of nodules within parenthesis): Zhengzhou People's Hospital (624 nodules; November 2017 to November 2023), Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (276 nodules; August 2023 to April 2024), The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (173 nodules; June 2023 to December 2023), Huadong Hospital of Shanghai (135 nodules; May 2012 to September 2018), Beijing Liangxiang Hospital (2 nodules; May 2017 to December 2023), and The Ninth People's Hospital of Zhengzhou (5 nodules; November 2017 to January 2024). Chest CT imaging was primarily performed using one of the eight CT systems: Somatom Definition, NeuViz 16Classic, Philips Brilliance 16, Philips IQon Spectral CT, Philips Healthcare, GE Discovery CT750 HD, Somatom Force, and Somatom Definition AS. The images of nodules were acquired by multirow (64 rows) spiral CT (at least 16 rows) with a slice thickness of less than or equal to 1.5 mm. The DICOM CT images are imported into the Shukun Artificial Intelligence Full Chest Diagnostic Module for in‐depth quantitative recognition and analysis. The Shukun Artificial Intelligence workstation we use plays an important role in the quantitative analysis of PNs. The Lung Nodule Intelligent Auxiliary Analysis System developed by Shukun Technology leverages computational vision and deep learning methodologies to perform swift intelligent detection and identification of lung nodules from plain chest CT scans. The system is capable of delineating suspected nodules, precisely measuring their dimensions and morphology, and generating automated diagnostic imaging data. It presents physicians with a clear and intuitive display of intelligent lung nodule analysis outcomes on its user interface. The process of quantitative analysis is depicted in Figure 1.

Examination methods

During the examination, patients were placed in a supine position and instructed to hold their breath. CT scans were performed using the equipment from each institution, with the scanning range extending from the lung apex to the upper abdomen. After the scans were completed, the data were collected and 1215 lesions were independently confirmed on thin‐section lung window images by two or more attending physicians. Any discrepancies were resolved through consultation.

Statistical methods

Data organization and statistical analysis were conducted using R version 4.3.0. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The t‐test and chi‐square test were applied to compare differences between continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using SPSS 23.0. For all data results, a p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

General data

This study encompassed a total of 1062 individuals from six medical centers, with a detailed distribution of PNs as follows: Zhengzhou People's Hospital contributed 539 individuals with 624 nodules, categorized as AAH (37), AIS (272), and MIA (314); the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences provided data from 232 individuals with AAH (5), AIS (125), and MIA (147); Huadong Hospital reported on 130 individuals with AAH (1), AIS (64), and MIA (70); The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University included 155 individuals with AIS (42) and MIA (70); Beijing Liangxiang Hospital and The Ninth People's Hospital of Zhengzhou contributed the least, with two and four individuals, respectively, and corresponding nodule counts.

The gender distribution was nearly balanced, with 324 males and 738 females. Participant ages varied from 20 to 79 years, yielding an average age of 54.12 ± 11.68 years. Spatial distribution of the 1215 nodules included 432 in the right upper lobe, 69 in the right middle lobe, 227 in the right lower lobe, 312 in the left upper lobe, and 125 in the left lower lobe.

Specific to lung AIS, there were 506 cases: 138 males and 368 females, with a median age of 54 years (range, 18 to 78 years). The AIS nodules were distributed across lobes as follows: 168 in the right upper lobe, 30 in the right middle lobe, 94 in the right lower lobe, 124 in the left upper lobe, and 70 in the left lower lobe.

However, statistically significant differences were found in maximum CT value, CT standard deviation, entropy, energy, maximum slice area, maximum 3D diameter, and nodule mass (p < 0.05). The value of maximum 3D diameter and maximum CT value in differentiating AAH, AIS, and MIA

Previous data indicated that the mean CT value did not show a statistically significant difference in the differentiation between AAH and AIS, but demonstrated a difference between AIS and MIA, thus not recommending the mean CT value for the identification of AIS. Both the maximum CT value and the maximum 3D diameter showed consistent discriminative performance in differentiating among the three. In the differentiation between AAH and AIS, the maximum CT value had the optimal predictive performance at the Youden index maximum (0.43379) with a cutoff value of −493.5 HU, yielding a sensitivity of 88.735% and a specificity of 38.636%; the maximum 3D diameter had a cutoff value of 7.155 mm at the Youden index maximum (0.43379), with a sensitivity of 72.925% and a specificity of 70.455%. In the differentiation between AIS and MIA, the maximum CT value had the optimal predictive performance at the Youden index maximum (0.28005) with a cutoff value of −87.5 HU, yielding a sensitivity of 51.128% and a specificity of 76.877%; the maximum 3D diameter had a cutoff value of 10.455 mm at the Youden index maximum (0.24061), with a sensitivity of 51.729% and a specificity of 72.332%. DISCUSSION

Carcinoma in situ, synonymous with intraepithelial neoplasia, is defined by atypical cellular proliferation confined to the mucosal or epidermal epithelium. It is distinguished by the absence of invasion into the underlying dermis or metastasis, thereby representing a “pre‐invasive cancer.” In 2021, a joint classification by the IASLC, ATS, and ERS redefined “bronchioalveolar carcinoma” as “adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS),” emphasizing its noninvasive nature. The WHO's 2021 lung tumor classification delineated AIS by the presence of alveolar wall‐lining adenocarcinoma cells without stromal, vascular, or pleural invasion, and specified a maximum diameter of ≤3 cm.This reclassification highlights the clinical significance of differentiating AIS, influencing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. The WHO criteria aid in precision medicine, potentially enhancing patient prognoses. Continued research into AIS's molecular and genetic profiles could yield novel therapeutic targets and prognostic markers.

Chest CT scanning is pivotal in lung nodule detection, with GGO nodules often indicative of AIS. AIS is typically visualized as pure GGO with a uniform, cloud‐like pattern and thin, well‐defined margins, ≤3 cm in diameter. The presence of fine (≤2 mm) peripheral blood vessels exhibiting a branching pattern within the nodule, termed the “tumor microvascular CT imaging sign,” is diagnostically suggestive of AIS. The diagnostic principle for lung carcinoma in situ on imaging involves the identification of incidental, persistent pure GGO nodules in conjunction with the tumor microvascular sign.

The size and CT values of the lung nodule play a significant role in the diagnosis and assessment of the invasiveness of lung carcinoma in situ. In the diagnostic and follow‐up process, the value of the maximum 3D diameter is superior to that of the maximum diameter on a single transverse section, and 3D CT values are also superior to 2D CT values, 4 , 5 , 6 facilitating optimal clinical decision‐making. The data used in this article are all 3D data. In this study, the maximum 3D diameter of 506 lung carcinoma in situ cases was 9.35 ± 3.61 mm, with the maximum CT value being −234.69 ± 208.44 HU, and the average CT value being −594.11 ± 123.88 HU. Li's study 7 reported an average CT value of −590.27 ± 127.93 HU and an average size of 9.19 ± 3.14 mm for 68 cases of AAH/AIS. Fang's study 8 reported an average CT value of −631.75 ± 54.38 and a 3D average longitudinal diameter of 8.44 ± 2.03 mm for 55 cases of AAH/AIS.

AIS of the lung must be differentiated from AAH and MIA. The mean CT value is frequently utilized for distinguishing AIS from other pulmonary lesions 9 , 10 , 11 and is a key indicator to monitor changes during follow‐up periods, with some studies suggesting its superiority over lesion size. 9 AAH presents as pure GGNs, which are typically round or oval, devoid of solid components, and lack spiculated edges or pleural retraction. There are no fine blood vessels entering the periphery or interconnecting within the nodule. The diameter is often less than 5 mm, with an average CT value around −600 HU. In this study, the average CT value for 44 cases of AAH was −624.14 ± 117.24 HU. However, after univariate analysis of variance, no statistically significant difference was found between the average CT values of AAH and AIS. There is a statistically significant difference in the maximum CT values, with AAH at −346.43 ± 234.02 HU and AIS at −234.69 ± 208.44 HU, indicating that the discriminative value of the maximum CT value is superior to that of the average CT value. The maximum 3D diameter of AAH in this cohort was 7.15 ± 2.91 mm, which was statistically different from AIS at 9.35 ± 3.60 mm. Additionally, there were statistically significant differences in standard deviation, entropy, energy, maximum cross‐sectional area, and nodule mass between the two groups. The ROC curve analysis revealed that the discriminative value of nodules for AAH and AIS, from strongest to weakest, was in the order of nodule mass, energy, maximum 3D diameter, surface area, entropy, standard deviation, and maximum CT value, with nodule mass showing the best discriminative value, AUC 0.742 (95% CI, 0.658–0.827). Contrary to previous studies, the maximum 3D diameter in this study [AUC 0.733 (95% CI, 0.651–0.814)] showed a higher discriminative value than the maximum CT value [AUC 0.651 (95% CI, 0.556–0.746)], which may be related to the precision of AI in measurement.

MIA is defined as a solitary small adenocarcinoma that predominantly exhibits a lepidic growth pattern with an invasive focus of ≤5 mm. If, during follow‐up, a solid component appears within a lesion that was previously classified as AIS, and the maximum diameter of the solid change is ≤5 mm, it suggests a possible progression of AIS to MIA. In this cohort, the volume of the solid component in AIS was (31.21 ± 93.04) mm3, which is statistically different from that in MIA, (109.02 ± 229.87) mm3. This difference in volume and proportion indicates an evolution of the lung nodule's solid component as AIS progresses to MIA.

The average age of patients with AIS was (52.34 ± 11.42) years, and for MIA, it was (55.13 ± 11.70) years, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups. This age difference suggests a trend of AIS progressing to MIA with increasing age. There is also a statistically significant difference in the maximum CT values between AIS and MIA, similar to the distinction between AAH and AIS.

The average CT value shows a statistically significant difference in the differentiation between AIS and MIA. However, the ROC curve analysis indicates that the maximum CT value, with an AUC of 0.695 (95% CI, 0.665–0.725), is superior to the average CT value, with an AUC of 0.620 (95% CI, 0.588–0.652), suggesting that the maximum CT value is more advantageous in the differential diagnosis of in situ carcinoma.

The maximum 3D diameters of AIS and MIA were (9.35 ± 3.61) mm and (11.73 ± 5.03) mm, respectively, with a statistically significant difference, providing a basis for the preliminary clinical differentiation of the two based on diameter, which is also beneficial for follow‐up decisions regarding such GGNs. Other statistically significant differences were observed in CT median, CT standard deviation, entropy, compactness, sphericity, energy, maximum cross‐sectional area, nodule mass, and total nodule volume. When these factors were included in the ROC curve analysis, the discriminative power for differentiating AIS from MIA, from highest to lowest, was as follows: solid component volume, maximum CT value, energy, CT standard deviation, nodule mass, 3D CTR, maximum 3D diameter, total nodule volume, maximum cross‐sectional area, entropy, average CT value, median CT value, and age. Among these, the solid component volume showed the best discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.715 (95% CI, 0.685–0.744).

It is essential to recognize that GGO lung cancer presents distinct characteristics from traditional lung cancer. When considering lung AIS in clinical practice, there is still considerable debate on how to manage these patients. It is important to avoid treating early‐stage lesions as if they were advanced‐stage diseases. The metaphor of not “catching the bad guys in kindergarten” highlights the need to avoid overtreatment. Additionally, it is crucial to balance the goal of not “curing one lung cancer while creating a lung cancer survivor,” which implies the importance of considering the long‐term implications of treatment on the patient's quality of life. The psychological state of the patient must be taken into account, and medical intervention plans should be tailored to each patient's specific situation. Decisions should be made in a shared manner between the patient and the medical team, with the goal of maximizing individual benefits while minimizing risks. 12

In summary, the management of lung AIS requires a nuanced approach that considers the unique characteristics of the disease, the patient's individual circumstances, and the potential long‐term effects of treatment. By integrating clinical expertise with technological advancements such as AI, and by adopting a patient‐centered, multidisciplinary strategy, healthcare providers can deliver more precise and personalized care for patients with lung AIS.

LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

This study has certain limitations. The differences in image acquisition conditions across various centers may have a certain impact on the precision of the results. The exclusion of characteristics such as family history of cancer, smoking history, imaging morphological features, and blood test biomarkers may also affect the precision of the outcomes, necessitating further enrichment from related research.

CONCLUSION

The increasing use of low‐dose chest CT for health screenings, coupled with a growing emphasis on health, has led to the identification of a large population with PNs. AIS is classified as stage 0 lung cancer, and accurate diagnosis of AIS is crucial for the early detection of lung cancer. For GGNs with a diameter of approximately 7 mm, a maximum CT value of −234 HU, and an average CT value of −594 HU, and when imaging suggests the presence of small internal blood vessels, clinicians should consider the possibility of lung AIS and differentiate it from AAH and MIA to facilitate the development of clinical follow‐up strategies.

Given the accessibility of current clinical data, this article recommends the use of maximum 3D diameter and maximum CT value for the differentiation of lung AIS. With the advancement of AI in the field of pulmonary surgery, the acquisition of nodule mass and volume data will become easier, which will undoubtedly enhance the precision of AIS diagnosis.
