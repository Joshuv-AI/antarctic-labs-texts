Evaluating hepatitis B screening, prevalence, vaccination coverage, and linkage to care in Abuja, Nigeria: insights from a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ogechukwu MaryAnn Akabuike, Mabel Kamweli Aworh, Nkiruka Lynda Uzoebo, John Erwat, Onyinyechi Agukwe, Kingsley Ngong, Amos Dangana, Kenneth Enwerem, Idris Nasir Abdullahi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) is a major global health threat, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, where Nigeria has a prevalence exceeding 8%. Despite the availability of effective vaccines, inadequate coverage and lack of awareness have resulted in high rates of chronic infections and HBV-attributable liver disease. The study aimed to raise awareness of HBV, enroll participants for HBV screening, determine HBV prevalence across various communities, vaccinate negative cases and link positive cases to care.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 16 districts of Abuja from April 2022 to March 2023. Participants aged 18 and over were screened for hepatitis B, and those testing negative were offered on-site vaccination. Blood samples were collected and tested using HBV immunochromatographic tests and real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) for HBV DNA quantification. Data were collected through interviews and analyzed using R software, with descriptive statistics calculated for continuous and categorical variables. Associations between socio-demographic factors and hepatitis B/vaccine status were assessed using the Chi-square test of independence.ResultsOut of 3,245 individuals screened, 141 (4.3%) tested positive for HBV. The highest prevalence was observed in the 20–39 age group (5.9%), with males showing a higher infection rate (5.4%) compared to females (3.7%) [p = 0.02]. The mean age of HBV-positive individuals was significantly lower (30.8 years) than those negative for HBV (36.2 years) [p = < 0.001]. Among 2,506 participants who consented to vaccination, 2,488 received the first dose (99.3%), 1,834 the second dose (73.2%), and 1,100 the third dose (43.9%). Vaccination uptake declined with each subsequent dose, but more older participants completed the third dose. A similar pattern of HBV prevalence was observed across gender and age groups.ConclusionThe study reveals a 4.3% prevalence of hepatitis B, with the highest infection rate among individuals aged 20–39 years, and males showing a higher prevalence than females. Although vaccination uptake was high for the first dose, adherence declined for subsequent doses. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions, particularly among younger adults and males, to improve awareness and vaccination completion. Enhanced community engagement and sustained vaccination efforts are crucial for reducing HBV transmission and achieving better coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-21017-3.

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a hepatotropic virus that can cause life-threatening liver damage, representing a significant global health concern. HBV is a leading cause of liver disease worldwide, with over 248 million people living with chronic HBV infection globally [1]. The global prevalence of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) is estimated at 3.61%, with the highest rates in the Western Pacific (5.26%), and Africa (8.83%) [1]. Nigeria, in Sub-Saharan Africa, is hyper-endemic for HBV infection [2], with an average prevalence rate of 13.6%, according to a meta-analysis [3].

Despite the availability of a highly effective and affordable recombinant DNA vaccine since 1982, Nigeria’s vaccination programs face challenges including inadequate government funding and widespread community misconceptions [4]. As a result, vaccination coverage remains low [5], with only 41% of Nigerians were vaccinated against HBV in 2013, according to UNICEF and WHO [6]. Chronic HBV significantly increases the risk of childhood hepatic failure, cirrhosis and liver cancer [3, 7] with Nigeria having one of the highest rates of HBV-attributable cancer in West Africa, estimated at 2.6 to < 5.1 cases per 100,000 persons [8].

The lack of affordable diagnostics, including specialized immunoassays and nucleic acid tests, poses significant barriers to HBV elimination in Nigeria. High out-of-pocket costs further exacerbate the issue, making HBV a critical public health threat. Perinatal transmission is the primary route in high-endemic regions [6], where at least 8% of the population are chronic HBV carriers. Approximately 90% of perinatally infected individuals, 30% of those infected in early childhood, and 6% of those infected after age five develop chronic HBV [8]. Among adults, transmission typically occurs through infected blood, body fluids, inadequately sterilized instruments with unscreened blood transfusions and traditional practices, such as local circumcisions playing a significant role [7]. Horizontal transmission, including household contact is also prevalent in endemic regions [9].

HBV is recognized as an occupational health hazard for healthcare practitioners [5, 9], yet understanding of HBV remains limited in Nigeria, particularly regarding adult vaccination coverage. The asymptomatic nature of chronic hepatitis B until its advanced stages, the lack of curative therapy, and limited disease awareness contribute to low rates of surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment [10].

Nigeria’s National Guidelines for the Prevention, Care, and Treatment of Viral Hepatitis B & C, align with the global goal to eliminate viral hepatitis by 2030, as endorsed by the United Nations during the 59th World Health Assembly in 2016 [11]. This target has spurred vaccination campaigns by various organizations, emphasizing the importance of vaccination which offers 90–100% protection when the full vaccination schedule is completed [12].

In response, the Remedium Plus Foundation, a non-profit, affiliated with the Society of St. Vincent De Paul at St. Benedict Catholic Church in Lokogoma, Abuja, Nigeria, initiated a campaign in districts of Abuja. This study aimed to raise awareness, screen for HBV, determine its prevalence, and provide immunological and nucleic acid testing for those who tested positive, and their referral to care.

Methods

Study area

A cross-sectional study was conducted in sixteen randomly selected districts of Abuja: Lokogoma, Dogongada, Kabusa, Sherriti, Ndako, Utako, Gwagwalada, Kubwa, Waru, Gwarinpa, Apo Mechanic, Asokoro, Wuse, Garki, and Dagba - Area 1. The study spanned from April 2022 to March 2023. Abuja, the capital city of Nigeria, is located in the central part of the country within the Federal Capital Territory (FCT). It became the capital in 1991 and has since experienced rapid population growth, with the metro area population estimated to exceed 4 million by 2024 [13]. The city’s landscape is characterized by savanna vegetation and is underlain by crystalline rocks, including granites and gneisses.

Study population

The study targeted residents of selected districts in Abuja, including those who were working or schooling outside the district but were present during the screening and vaccination period. Eligible participants were individuals aged 18 years and older who provided written informed consent. Exclusions were made for individuals under the age of 18 and those who did not provide written informed consent.

Awareness campaign

Effective community engagement played a vital role in the awareness campaign during the course of the study. Information was disseminated through various channels, including religious organizations, community gate keepers, social media platforms, and estate communication networks. Health talks were conducted, providing the community with essential knowledge and encouraging active participation, as well as increased awareness of Hepatitis B.

However, the final sample of 3,245 respondents does not reflect the total population exposed to the awareness campaign; instead, it represents those who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate after outreach efforts. We acknowledge that some individuals may have been informed but chose not to participate due to personal, socio-economic, or logistical reasons, which aligns with observed health-seeking behaviors in the study area.

Data collection method

Trained field workers conducted face-to-face interviews, the data collected in this study comprised socio-demographic information, hepatitis B screening results, and vaccination status. Socio-demographic data with variables such as age, gender, which provided context for analyzing associations between factors and hepatitis B status or vaccine uptake. Hepatitis B data included results from HBV immunochromatographic tests, sourced from ECOTEST, for initial screening and HBV DNA quantification using RT-PCR for confirming infection. PCR kits was sourced from RoboGene ®, Analytika Jena, Jena, Germany.

Briefly, the study participants had their thumbs swabbed with 70% ethanol to ensure aseptic conditions. A lab technician then swiftly pricked each participant’s finger using a sterile lancet, and the blood sample was promptly applied to RDT, which was pre-labeled with a unique numerical identifier for tracking. The results were interpreted following the manufacturer’s instructions. All used needles were safely discarded in a sharp’s container, while the RDT kits and other consumables were disposed of in a designated waste bag to maintain proper biohazard protocols. These tests meet the World Health Organization’s “ASSURED” criteria: Affordable, Sensitive, Specific, User-friendly, Rapid and Robust, Equipment-free, and Deliverable to end-users [14].

Immunochromatographic test

The HBV immunochromatographic test was used for all participants that met the inclusion criteria. It employs two assay techniques to detect HBV markers in serum samples. The direct antigen-antibody-antigen “Sandwich” method is used to identify HBsAg, HBsAb, HBcAg, and HBeAg. This technique involves immunochromatographic strips containing gold nanoparticles pre-coated with monoclonal antibodies specific to these antigens. These tests work on the principle of antigen-antibody reactions, which, in the presence of a chromogen, produce a visible reaction after 15 min. The results are interpreted according to the kit manufacturer’s instructions. For detecting HBcAb, an indirect antigen-antibody-antibody assay method was used [14].

Specimen collection and analysis for confirmatory testing of HBV positive cases

A 3 mL blood sample was collected from each participant that tested positive, using standard operating procedure. The blood was carefully transferred into sterile, plain sample containers, with each tube appropriately labeled with the participant’s identification number. The serum was separated by first allowing the blood to clot at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min. After separation, the serum was transferred into serum aliquot containers and stored at -20 °C until laboratory analysis. All stored samples were analyzed in batches within seven days of collection.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

The HBV Real-Time Polymerase chain Reaction (RT-PCR) method involves the use of fluorescent probes to detect and quantify HBV DNA. Details of RT-PCR have been described elsewhere [15]. In summary, DNA was extracted from 100 μL of serum using the SMI test EX-R and D (Sumitomo Metal Industries, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, DNA was purified and suspended in 20 mL of distilled water. A 10- μL aliquot of this solution was used for RT-PCR, performed with a PCR core reagent kit (RoboGene ®, Analytika Jena, Germany) and an ABI 7700 sequence detector system (Perkin Elmer, Foster City, Calif. The thermal cycling conditions involved an initial UNG activation at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by Taq Gold activation and UNG inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min, and 53 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 20 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Quantification of HBV DNA was conducted as a blind test, with real-time data acquisition and analysis [15].

By utilizing RDTs for initial screening and follow-up confirmatory tests for positive cases, we ensured a robust and reliable approach to identifying HBV infection among the study participants. This method allowed for timely identification and appropriate referral for further medical care and intervention [16].

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using R statistical software version 4.4.1. The study included a total of 3,245 respondents, with data collected on variables such as age, sex, hepatitis B status, and the number of vaccine doses administered. Descriptive statistics were computed, including mean and standard deviation, as well as median and interquartile range for continuous variables. For categorical variables, frequency and percentage distributions were calculated. To assess associations between socio-demographic factors and hepatitis B status or vaccine status, the Chi-square test of independence was employed.

Results

Out of 3,245 individuals screened, 2,025 (62.4%) were females, 1,941 (59.8%) were aged 29–39 years (Table 1). The mean age of respondents was significantly lower among those who tested positive for hepatitis B (30.8 years) compared to those who tested negative (36.2 years) [p = < 0.001]. Of those screened, 141 (4.3%) tested positive for HBV. The highest prevalence of hepatitis B was observed in the 20–39 years age group (5.9%), followed by the 0–19 years age group, which includes only individuals aged 18 and 19, as the inclusion criteria specified participants aged 18 years and above. The least prevalence was found in the 60 + years age group. Additionally, the prevalence of hepatitis B was significantly higher among male respondents (5.4%) compared to female respondents (3.7%) [p = 0.02].

Discussion

This study reveals important sociodemographic differences that require attention and offers crucial insights into the epidemiology of HBV in the study area. The study recorded a prevalence of 4.3%. In comparison, a similar study conducted in Lagos State, Nigeria, screened 4,862 individuals and reported a prevalence rate of 2.1%, with 100 positive cases [17]. Other studies in Nigeria have reported varying prevalence rates of HBV. For instance, in the North-East region, 8.2% prevalence was observed among pregnant women [18], while a lower prevalence of 2.5% was reported among blood donors in South-West Nigeria [19]. Although another study in North West Nigeria reported a much higher prevalence of 15.1% among pregnant women [20].

These regional differences highlight the impact of specific risk factors and population characteristics on HBV transmission. Our study area included localities close to internally displaced persons (IDP) camps, which are known to have high-risk populations, including sex workers. These camps often have higher rates of HBV due to increased exposure to risk factors such as unprotected sex and limited access to healthcare services. This proximity likely contributed to the increased prevalence rate observed in our study compared to Lagos, where the population distribution and risk factors may differ [17]. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and vaccination programs in high-risk areas, including those near IDP camps, to effectively control the spread of HBV.

A similar study conducted in Senegal found a significantly higher HBV prevalence of 13.6% among healthcare workers [21]. This elevated prevalence may be attributed to the increased risk healthcare workers face due to their occupational exposure to infections, placing them in a higher-risk category compared to the general population. Frequent contact with blood and body fluids, as well as needle stick injuries, are potential contributors to the heightened vulnerability of healthcare workers to HBV infection [21].

The age group 20–39 had the highest participation rate, followed by the 40–59 age group in the present study. The Lagos study had a median age of 33 years, with most positive cases being between 21 and 30 years old [17]. Our results are similar to those of another study among pregnant women in North East Nigeria where the highest HBV seropositivity was observed in the age group 21–25 years [18]. This higher participation in the younger age group could be attributed to several factors, including increased exposure to risk behaviors such as multiple sexual partners, a history of blood transfusions, surgeries, or hospitalizations, and traditional practices like tattoos, piercings, scarification, and circumcision [22, 23]. Additionally, this age group is more likely to be active on social media and other platforms where information about hepatitis B is disseminated. Other risk factors, such as drug use, unprotected sex, alcohol abuse, and injuries from sharp objects, are also more prevalent among individuals in this age range [22, 23].

According to gender distribution, there were more female respondents than male respondents in the present study. The Lagos study had predominantly male HBV positive cases [17]. Our results are in contrast to the observation among blood donors where more males tested positive for HBV [14]. This could be because women are inherently more likely than men to engage in health-seeking behaviors due to their innate maternal instinct, most pregnant women are checked for HBV as a requirement for antenatal registration, which makes it easier for them to show up for follow-up or future screening when opportunity arises.

Another study in Egypt shares a similar finding, the frequency of HBV in men and women found that men were more likely to contract the virus (3.75%) than women (2.2%) [24]. The observed gender gap could potentially be explained by variations in risk behaviors, including high rates of unprotected sexual activity and occupational exposures in men. Males continued to test positive for HBV after the age of 50, according to our findings, which may be related to persistent risky behavior or delayed onset of symptoms compared to females.

Nigeria, a West African country, has a lower HBV vaccination rate compared to many other Sub-Saharan African nations [25, 26]. During the study, all individuals present on the designated screening days, who met our inclusion criteria were enrolled, regardless of sex, socio-demographic characteristics, or risk factors. Those who tested negative were immediately administered the first dose of the HBV vaccine, while those testing positive were counseled and linked to care. However, only about 30 out of 141 positive cases were successfully linked to care due to financial constraints. A lack of funding posed a significant barrier, limiting the reach of vaccinations and reducing the scope of outreach efforts, which may have affected the identification of additional cases. This disparity underscores the importance of targeted education and screening programs, particularly in regions with varying HBV prevalence rates, to improve the detection and management of infections [25].

The need to intensify efforts towards achieving widespread immunization coverage, especially among the adult population, is critical [26]. In this study, a key outcome was the vaccination of 80.2% of participants with the first dose. Nearly all participants who tested negative for HBV in the study area received their first dose of the hepatitis B vaccine. However, the percentage of individuals receiving the second and third doses gradually decreased, with 59.1% receiving the second dose and only 35.4% completing the third dose. In our study area, retention for the second and third doses of hepatitis B vaccination was notably affected by behavioral, socio-economic, and lifestyle factors. A prevailing belief that “what you don’t know won’t kill you” influenced attitudes toward vaccine uptake, as many participants perceived themselves as better off without the vaccine if they were not currently sick. This mindset, observed in a study by Agha S, et al. [27] indicates that awareness alone may be insufficient to drive completion of multi-dose vaccination schedules. There is need to put into cognizance, these findings in subsequent studies; Nigerians place greater value on hope than fear when it comes to vaccination acceptance, they also believe that having fun is more significant than worrying about contracting the illness, and that receiving approval from friends and family is more potent than their rejection. According to these results, highlighting the advantages of living a longer life after vaccination is more likely to be successful than escalating Nigerians’ anxiety about viral infections such as COVID 19 and HBV [27, 28].

Socio-economic barriers also played a significant role, particularly in districts with higher poverty rates, such as Sherriti. Here, many respondents cited transportation costs and the need to prioritize daily income-generating activities (e.g., going to market) over returning for follow-up vaccinations. The impact of such financial constraints contrasts with higher compliance rates observed in wealthier districts like Asokoro, where almost all participants completed the three-dose regimen. In Asokoro, higher education levels, economic stability, and greater exposure to health information likely contributed to increased adherence to vaccination schedules.

Given these findings, tailored retention strategies are essential to ensure complete vaccination coverage.

In contrast, another study reported low user acceptance of the HBV vaccine, particularly among men who have sex with men, where only 17.9% received the first dose, 12.2% the second, and 5.9% the full series [29]. People who inject drugs and transgender women/sex workers demonstrated higher levels of acceptance for the first dose (66.7% and 53.3%, respectively), but completion rates significantly dropped for subsequent doses (24.4% and 26.7% for the second dose, and 6.7% and 0% for the third) [29].

Interestingly, older adults in this study were more likely to complete the third dose the HBV vaccine, possibly due to increased health awareness as they age. Our results are at variance with a similar study done in the United States where vaccination adherence rates for the third dose were lowest among the youngest adults, aged 18–39 years [30]. Analysis of vaccination status by location revealed that most eligible participants in all areas received their first dose. This is consistent with findings of the American study where vaccination adherence rates were observed to be lowest among those living in a neighborhood with a median household income [30].

This disparity underscores the importance of enhanced follow-up and ensuring the completion of the immunization schedule for full protection against HBV. To prevent new infections, and also bring the spread of the HBV to a halt, Nigeria urgently needs to intensify efforts in vaccination efforts, including continuous education and awareness campaigns focused on young adults and high-risk groups, to curb HBV transmission.

Limitations

The study’s limitations included low vaccination coverage due to participants perception towards vaccination, lifestyle and inadequate follow-up due to financial constraints, which also restricted access to care for positive cases. Only 30 out of 141 positive cases were successfully referred for additional management, indicating that paucity of funds was a significant obstacle to linking HBV-positive people to care. This research emphasizes how important it is to provide free or heavily subsidized healthcare services for managing HBV, especially for vulnerable groups living in low-income environments. We could not calculate the positive predictive value because not all samples were subjected to PCR. We were able to carry out PCR only on the positive cases because of limited resources.

The absence of a structured questionnaire for data collection may have impacted data quality, and gender disparity along with a higher prevalence in certain age groups, were noted. Excluding individuals under 18 years limited insights into younger populations, and potential selection bias may have affected the accuracy of the analysis. Additionally, the study’s limited geographic scope may restrict the generalizability of its findings. These challenges highlight barriers to achieving the global goal of eradicating hepatitis B by 2030, emphasizing gaps in immunization programs and awareness efforts amongst young adults.

Conclusions

This study underscores important sociodemographic factors influencing HBV epidemiology in Abuja and provides critical insights into community health behaviors. The findings highlight the role of effective community engagement in increasing awareness and promoting participation in HBV screenings. However, barriers such as financial constraints, logistical challenges, and incomplete vaccination schedules emerged as significant hurdles to achieving comprehensive care.

To address these challenges, there is a pressing need for improved follow-up systems, enhanced education efforts, and innovative solutions such as mobile or community-based vaccination units. These interventions can help reduce transportation barriers, encourage full vaccination, adherence, and shift perceptions around preventive healthcare. Financial incentives and affordable healthcare services would further support these efforts, ensuring better access to care for underserved populations.

Expanding HBV screening and immunization programs across the FCT and Nigeria, particularly among adults, is essential to achieving broader protection and improved health. Addressing these gaps through targeted, community-centered approaches is critical to advancing progress towards the global goal of eradicating hepatitis by 2030.
