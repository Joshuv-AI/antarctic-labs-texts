Televisits for Parkinson’s disease: benefits and barriers
Authors: V. Canoro, C. Giordano, M. Rotolo, P. Barone, R. Erro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Televisits are a valuable tool for Parkinson’s disease (PD) follow-up; however, their implementation is hindered by a fragmented evidence base. This narrative review synthesizes 41 articles (31 observational, 10 comparative) spanning three decades, categorizing findings into three domains: feasibility, clinical outcomes, and efficiency. Results indicate high feasibility with visit completion frequently exceeding 70% and high user satisfaction primarily due to reduced travel burden and costs. Clinical outcomes appear comparable to in-person care, yet evidence on efficiency remains inconclusive. Current literature suffers from methodological limitations, including small sample sizes, U.S. geographic bias, and a lack of standardized outcome taxonomies. Notably, we identify a recurring “augmentation bias” in comparative trial designs: experimental arms frequently receive more clinical attention than controls. This suggests that reported benefits may derive from increased care frequency rather than the specific care delivery modality, questioning the cost-effectiveness of such resource-heavy models in routine practice. Furthermore, barriers regarding the digital divide and concerns about international privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR/HIPAA) remain unresolved. In conclusion, televisits should be integrated into validated hybrid frameworks rather than serving as standalone replacements. To advance the field, research must pivot from basic feasibility toward establishing standardized outcome measures and proving long-term economic sustainability in real-world clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00702-026-03238-5.

Introduction

The global raise in chronic diseases calls for adequate efforts to counteract this trend and mitigate their impact on both the individuals and the society (GBD 2023 Disease and Injury and Risk Factor Collaborators 2025).

Parkinson’s Disease (PD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, is a prototypical chronic condition: its incidence increases with aging and leads to growing disability, high healthcare costs, and caregiver burden (Kruse et al. 2021; Chaudhuri et al. 2024). Its prevalence has more than doubled in less than two decades (Dorsey et al. 2018) and is expected to further increase in the near future (GBD 2016 Parkinson’s Disease Collaborators 2018; Su et al. 2025). Although a typical disease course of PD can be recognized, some features, such as the rate of progression, the pattern of symptoms and their severity, can vary greatly between individuals, thus determining the need for a tailored therapy (Kalia and Lang 2015). Regular assessments are therefore required in order to address treatment needs (Tanner et al. 2014), with specialized neurological care having been shown to provide significant benefits, to reduce the risk of complications such as falls, hospitalization and mortality, and in turn to lower healthcare and societal costs (Schootman et al. 2011). However, a high percentage of people with PD (pwPD) do not receive adequate and specific care (Pearson et al. 2023). The uneven distribution of dedicated specialist centers, the progressive physical limitations and additional travel difficulties frequently hinder access to care, especially for people in remote or underserved regions and for members of social minorities (including nursing home residents) (Dorsey et al. 2010).

A potential solution to overcome some of the aforementioned barriers is represented by telemedicine, defined as the delivery of health care services at a distance, by using information and communications technologies (ICTs) (WHO 2021).

In the last decades, the spread of digital consumer technologies has paved the way for the development of many telemedicine services as a complement to traditional care, for diagnosis, treatment and management of different diseases (Bashshur et al. 2014). Nevertheless, concerns about privacy, effectiveness in delivering high-quality care, and maintaining the patient-physician relationship have slowed their widespread adoption (Scott and Mars 2015). A significant acceleration occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the need to comply with social distancing restrictions required the use of alternatives to in-person care (Greiwe 2022). For many diseases - including PD - this consisted of switching from traditional outpatient visits to synchronous (e.g., phone- and/or video-calls) and/or asynchronous (e.g., email exchanges) remote communication with physicians (Podlewska and van Wamelen 2022). This offered the opportunity to build trust and improve acceptance in the routine use of digital technologies in the medical field (Mammen et al. 2018; Mayela et al. 2022; Ogawa et al. 2022; Ghourchian et al. 2022; Magriço et al. 2023).

In the management of PD, the use of televisits is supported by a strong clinical rationale. Since follow-up of pwPD primarily relies on visual assessment and interview with the subject and/or caregiver, it can easily be conducted remotely via video-calls, only forgoing some elements of the clinical assessment such as the muscle tone and the pull-test (Shalash et al. 2021). Furthermore, evidence suggests that observing patients within their home environment provides a more ecologically valid assessment, offering clinicians a more accurate reflection of pwPD’s daily functional status (Tarolli et al. 2020).

These principles have been supported by preliminary small-scale studies which mainly anticipated the feasibility of televisits and reported high satisfaction from users also due to reduced travel burden and costs (Hubble et al. 1993; Samii et al. 2006; Dorsey et al. 2010; Marzinzik et al. 2012; Abdolahi et al. 2013; Qiang and Marras 2015; Hanson et al. 2019). Subsequent comparative studies have further confirmed and deepened this evidence (Dorsey et al. 2013, 2016; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; van der Kolk et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; van den Bergh et al. 2021; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b). However, to the best of our knowledge, the results of this growing body of research have yet to be addressed in a cohesive and systematic manner.

A previous literature review on the broader topic of “telemedicine in movement disorders” summarized results into themes (i.e. feasibility, satisfaction, and efficacy) (Houston et al. 2022). However, although the majority of the examined studies were on PD, it only included articles published up to 2020, therefore missing a substantial segment of literature published during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, we believe that specific attention should be paid to each individual service (i.e. televisit), to precisely evaluate its properties across the domains of feasibility, clinical outcomes, and efficiency.

The current work aims therefore to provide a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence to explore the clinical benefits, inherent constraints, and future role of televisits in the management of pwPD, identifying critical areas to address for their fully implementing in the standard care of PD (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2017).

Methods

A PubMed search was conducted on December 1st, 2025, with no date filter, by using the following Boolean combinations: (Parkinson’s disease OR PD) AND (remote visits OR telemedicine OR virtual consultation OR teleconsultation OR video visit) AND (on-site visits OR in-person visits OR in-office visits OR standard care OR traditional care OR usual care). As various terms are utilized in the literature to describe this specific telemedicine service, we opted for broader and more historically established keywords in our Boolean strings to maximize sensitivity and avoid missing relevant records; subsequently, we adopted the term “televisit” throughout this manuscript as a specific and unambiguous label. A second broader PubMed search was conducted on the same day by using the combination keywords “Parkinson” AND “Telemedicine” in order to retrieve additional records missed during the first search.

Retrieved articles were screened by two authors (V.C. and R.E.) independently and based on the title and abstract they were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) Original research articles; (2) targeting pwPD; (3) included televisits as intervention; and (4) article written in English. Articles were excluded when: (1) focusing on other interventions including neurorehabilitation, speech and language therapy; neuropsychological evaluation, etc.; (2) focusing on telemonitoring with the use of wearable sensors and/or dedicated technological tools, which is not considered equivalent to a regular clinical visit, either remote or in-person (van den Bergh et al. 2021; Falkenburger et al. 2025). Duplicated items were removed.

Included articles were subsequently assessed through full-text evaluation, their reference lists being checked to potentially include additional articles not retrieved through the electronic search, and stratified into: (1) articles evaluating televisits through single-arm or cross-sectional observational designs (e.g., feasibility pilot studies, validation studies, and surveys) and (2) articles providing a formal comparison between televisits and in-person visits.

Given the nature of this narrative review (e.g., not providing quantitative outputs beyond main descriptive statistics), data from the included articles were collected using a standardized template broadly including the following information: enrolled population, study description, outcomes, results, caveats. Data extraction was made by a single author (V.C.) to ensure consistency in collecting relevant data to highlight benefits and barriers of televisits for pwPD.

To provide a structured overview of the literature, extracted data were categorized into key outcome domains adapted from established frameworks for feasibility studies (Bowen et al. 2009) and telemedicine assessment (Kidholm et al. 2012). Accordingly, outcome domains included: (1) feasibility (sub-categorized into: implementation, acceptability, and validity); (2) Clinical Outcomes (labeled as ‘efficacy’ and ‘safety’); and (3) efficiency (cost and time analysis).

To evaluate the methodological quality and risk of bias of the included primary literature, we employed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools (Barker et al. 2023). Given the methodological heterogeneity of the articles, the specific JBI checklist appropriate for each study design was applied (e.g., the JBI tool for Randomized Controlled Trials, for Cohort Studies, etc.) (Munn et al. 2020; Barker et al. 2026). To provide a standardized and condensed quantitative measure across different designs, we adopted a binary scoring system: 1 point was assigned for each “Yes” response to the JBI criteria, while 0 points were assigned for “No”, “Unclear”, or “Not applicable”. The final quality score for each study was calculated as the sum of the achieved points divided by the total number of applicable items in the respective checklist (excluding ‘Not Applicable’ responses from the denominator), expressed as a fraction/percentage (see Supplementary Table 1).

Results

A total of 1573 articles were retrieved from the PubMed searches. Of these, 1537 were removed; after adding 4 more records identified from citation searching, 41 articles were included in the final review (Fig. 1).

All of these evaluated components of the feasibility domain [15 (48.4%) assessed implementation, 20 (64.5%) focused on acceptability and 6 (19.4%) on validity]; 6 (19.4%) studies examined Clinical Outcomes (i.e. Efficacy) and 6 (19.4%) Efficiency. Notably, six studies (19.4%) specifically evaluated the use of televisits as an instrument for research (Tanner et al. 2014; Barbour et al. 2016; Durner et al. 2017; Deeb et al. 2021; Shivkumar et al. 2021; Bendig et al. 2022).

Overall, the feasibility of telemedicine visits was mostly reported to be very high with retention/completion rates ranging from 72% to 96% of scheduled visits. However, two studies from Brazil reported lower values of feasibility (e.g. about 53% via phone-consultation; about 38–41% via video-consultation) (Santos et al. 2022; Teixeira-Dos-Santos et al. 2023). Validity of telemedicine was also demonstrated to be very high, with good to optimal correlation/agreement between outcomes obtained through televisits as compared to those on records (e.g. obtained in prior in-person visits). The majority of studies assessing patients’ attitudes in joining telemedicine (either for usual care or research) reported reasonable level of interest (e.g., > 60%) (Shprecher et al. 2012; Dorsey et al. 2015a; Spear et al. 2019; Myers et al. 2022; Virmani et al. 2022), but these figures could have been inflated by sampling bias (e.g. recruitment performed by digital means and/or targeting tech-savvy samples) and prior experience. In fact, in one study interest in telemedicine dropped to about 50% among non-users (Qiang and Marras 2015). Patients’ satisfaction was generally good, reported reasons being cost and travel time savings as well as improved access to care. However, when specifically asked to state their preference over in-person visits, 30–40% of patients preferred telemedicine (Xu et al. 2022; Joo et al. 2024), about 40% preferred in-person visits (Joo et al. 2024) and about 45% preferred hybrid models (Magriço et al. 2023). Studies that explored barriers to join telemedicine indicated that up to 70% of pwPD might have limited access to technology (Shivkumar et al. 2021), that about 50% of pwPD might be resistant to join telemedicine services (Shivkumar et al. 2021), and that technical issues (including failed attempts) had occurred in 13.3% to 75% of remote consultations (Shalash et al. 2020; Lima et al. 2022). This is indirectly mirrored by the fact that studies reporting more favourable outcomes included patients with good access to technology (as per inclusion criterion or as reflected by recruitment via digital means) or provided the necessary technology, including broadband internet modems, to the recruited pwPD (Durner et al. 2017; Myers et al. 2021, 2022; Xu et al. 2022; Teixeira-Dos-Santos et al. 2023; Joo et al. 2024). Additional concerns reported by pwPD in one study included lack of hands-on care (69%) and intimacy (43%) (Spear et al. 2019).

Studies formally comparing remote visits with the standard of care

A total of 10 comparative studies, published between 2010 and 2025, were included (Table 2). Of these, 7 originated from the U.S.A. (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Tarolli et al. 2020; Fleisher et al. 2022), while the remainder comprised single studies from Spain (Cubo et al. 2025) and Japan (Sekimoto et al. 2019), along with one international multicenter study (García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) (Fig. 2).

Seven of the included articles were randomized controlled trials (RCTs); the remaining three consisted of a crossover study (Sekimoto et al. 2019), a nonrandomized controlled trial (Fleisher et al. 2022), and a prospective observational sub-study of a larger phase-III RCT (Biglan et al. 2017) in which teleconsultations are compared with in-person visits conducted shortly thereafter on the same individuals (Tarolli et al. 2020).

Study duration ranged from six months (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Heldman et al. 2017; Cubo et al. 2025) to one year (Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b), and sample size ranged from 10 to 209 pwPD. Except for one study that used an online campaign for subjects’ recruitment (Beck et al. 2017), participants were always enrolled directly from the clinic (Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) or care facilities (Dorsey et al. 2010).

In terms of intervention, the experimental group received either: only televisits (n = 3) (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016); televisits in addition to usual care (n = 4) (Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b); or remote visits combined with a telemonitoring service (n = 1) (Heldman et al. 2017) or extensive remote/hybrid program (n = 2) (Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025). While most of the televisits were conducted with the subjects connected from their home (Dorsey et al. 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b), the others took place in clinical settings (e.g. nursing home, general practitioner office) or facilities (Dorsey et al. 2010; Wilkinson et al. 2016). The control groups received a “usual care” with only in-person visits, although there were some variations in the delivery [i.e. performed by general practitioner or local neurologist (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Beck et al. 2017) or movement disorder specialist (Dorsey et al. 2010; Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b); or with precise and rigid scheduling (Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) or scheduling according to clinical needs (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013), etc.].

Primary outcomes were distributed as follows: feasibility (n = 6), encompassing the sub-domains of implementation (n = 4) and acceptability (n = 2); clinical outcomes (n = 5), assessing efficacy (n = 4) and safety (n = 1); and efficiency (n = 2). Only one study focused on the use of televisits for research purposes. (Tarolli et al. 2020)

Comparative studies consistently supported the feasibility of the remote visits by demonstrating high implementation rate (with completion rates generally exceeding 80–90%) (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Beck et al. 2017; Tarolli et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) and high acceptability among patients (Dorsey et al. 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) and clinicians (Cubo et al. 2025; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b), often driven by the reduced travel burden and costs (Wilkinson et al. 2016; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019). Furthermore, the only study assessing validity showed good overall correlation between remote and in-person motor ratings. (Tarolli et al. 2020)

Clinical outcomes were comparable between modalities: motor assessments (Dorsey et al. 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019), quality-of-life (QoL) measures (Dorsey et al. 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Fleisher et al. 2022), and patterns of healthcare utilization and therapeutic adjustments (Wilkinson et al. 2016; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) showed no meaningful differences between remote and usual in-person care. However, in most studies this comparability was inferred from non-significant superiority tests (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025) rather than formal non-inferiority analyses (García-Bustillo et al. 2025b); in some cases the analysis suggested the superiority of remote visits over in-person care in maintaining or improving QoL and motor scores (Dorsey et al. 2010; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025).

Discussion

Interest in introducing telemedicine into the management of PD has grown when modern technologies became more consumer-friendly, that is, since the early Internet era (Hubble et al. 1993). From then on, digital devices have become increasingly pervasive and ubiquitous, especially in more industrialized countries, although many barriers persist that make their use highly variable across the social spectrum (Van Dijk Jan 2012; Saeed and Masters 2021).

Despite these limitations, the body of evidence accumulated over the past three decades suggests that remote neurological assessments — although less suited for diagnostic purposes due to the impossibility of performing a complete neurological examination (Louis et al. 2002) — are highly suitable for follow-up visits. Having long outgrown its pioneering label, telemedicine for pwPD is now increasingly integrated into healthcare delivery (van den Bergh et al. 2021) and is generally well received by users and healthcare providers (Wilkinson et al. 2016; Mammen et al. 2018; Hanson et al. 2019; Cubo et al. 2025).

Overall, the synthesis of the 41 articles included in this review provides a clear evaluation of the core properties of televisits across our three domains of interest. First, the literature robustly supports the feasibility of remote care, highlighting strong patient retention and widespread acceptability, largely facilitated by the convenience of remote access. Second, from a clinical perspective, remote follow-ups appear to safely maintain care standards, yielding motor and quality-of-life evaluations that are broadly comparable with those obtained in conventional office settings. Finally, the domain of efficiency presents a more complex picture: while direct personal savings for patients are evident, the broader economic impact and resource allocation for healthcare systems remain unresolved.

The generalizability of the findings from the present literature was found to be unsatisfactory for a number of reasons, starting from the small sample sizes - with more than 43% (13 out of 30) of the included single arm studies (Hubble et al. 1993; Samii et al. 2006; Kondraske and Stewart 2008; Venkataraman et al. 2014; Dorsey et al. 2015a; Barbour et al. 2016; Durner et al. 2017; Cabrera-Martos et al. 2019; Shalash et al. 2020; Evans et al. 2020; Deeb et al. 2021; Virmani et al. 2022; Bendig et al. 2022) and 70% (7 out of 10) of the comparative ones (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016; Heldman et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020; Cubo et al. 2025) enrolling less than 100 pwPD. Moreover, many of these studies were performed during or short after the COVID-19 pandemic (Shalash et al. 2020; Cilia et al. 2020; Deeb et al. 2021; Myers et al. 2021, 2022; Shivkumar et al. 2021; Mayela et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2022; Ruggiero et al. 2022; Lima et al. 2022; Teixeira-Dos-Santos et al. 2023), when the lack of alternatives could have influenced some of the reported outcomes, including pwPD’s attitudes and satisfaction. A further consequence of the studies being conducted during the pandemic lies in the use of different modalities that were rapidly implemented to ensure continuity of care, including audio-only visits (e.g., over the phone) (Cilia et al. 2020; Evans et al. 2020; Magriço et al. 2023), which do not allow for a proper physical examination of pwPD.

Further caution is required when interpreting these findings as a whole, since the design of the included studies as well as the definition of the outcomes exhibit substantial differences.

A first layer of heterogeneity lies in the selection and definition of the outcome measures, which suffer from a lack of a common taxonomy — a gap that prompted our methodological choice to reclassify outcomes by adapting the Bowen and MAST frameworks to ensure comparability (Bowen et al. 2009; Kidholm et al. 2012).

This ambiguity manifests in two main ways: the use of different terms for the same theoretical concept (e.g., practical utility instead of efficacy) (García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) or, conversely, the application of the same term for different ends, depending on the study design (e.g., for instance one study aimed to assess “feasibility”, although QoL was in fact actually assessed as a measure of efficacy) (Sekimoto et al. 2019). Furthermore, different metrics were used across studies to infer the same outcome. This variability — when observed from a chronological perspective — could also reflect the historical evolution and the increasing maturity of telemedicine practices. For instance, measures of feasibility (the most frequent endpoint) evolved from a simple proportion of completed visits in earlier studies (Dorsey et al. 2013, 2016; Beck et al. 2017; Tarolli et al. 2020) to a multidimensional construct in more recent research. As an example, in one study feasibility was a combined outcome including adherence, retention, technical burden, and administrative workload (García-Bustillo et al. 2025b) — all of which explore the implementation component, according to Bowen’s classification (Bowen et al. 2009). It is also worth noting that, except for the latter example which adopted a comparative framework for these metrics, feasibility was typically analyzed using a descriptive/non-comparative approach (i.e., reporting completion rates only for the intervention arm), even within RCTs (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Beck et al. 2017; Tarolli et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b).

A further level of heterogeneity was identified in the design of the comparative studies with only a minority of them comparing the two management models in their pure form (i.e. televisits vs. standard in-person care) and with the same dosing (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Wilkinson et al. 2016). For instance, one trial adopted a hybrid substitution model, where remote visits replaced specific in-person follow-up visits while maintaining the same total visit frequency between arms (García-Bustillo et al. 2025b), whereas in most other cases, the comparison was strictly additive, meaning that the intervention arm received extra care compared to the control. This occurred either through a “hybrid” regimen consisting of extra televisits added on top of the usual care, effectively increasing the total number of contacts (Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Tarolli et al. 2020), or through an “extensive” telemedicine model in which televisits were provided along other services such as telemonitoring with wearable sensors and/or multidisciplinary care (Heldman et al. 2017; Cubo et al. 2025), therefore limiting to draw firm conclusions about the efficiency of televisits in comparison with the standard of care.

Compounding this issue is the lack of uniformity in the meaning of “usual care” which was provided by different medical figures with uneven expertise level. This disparity, which further reflects the heterogeneity of real-world PD care, has likely introduced a bias in studies where the comparison was made between non-specialized in-office care and highly specialized remote care (Dorsey et al. 2013; Beck et al. 2017).

Overall, these flaws could have generated an “augmentation bias” in favour of the study arm receiving more frequent or more specialized clinical services, which should be carefully considered when interpreting findings, especially regarding measures of efficacy, feasibility (particularly in terms of the acceptability component), and efficiency. Accordingly, it is challenging to decouple the inherent benefits of the remote modality from those of intensified care. Improved clinical outcomes may stem primarily from enhanced oversight, while higher acceptability might reflect the value of increased clinical attention rather than a genuine preference for the digital medium. In addition, we identified a frequent methodological error: several superiority trials (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Heldman et al. 2017; Beck et al. 2017; Sekimoto et al. 2019; Fleisher et al. 2022; Cubo et al. 2025) transitioned to claims of non-inferiority based only on an absence of statistical significance. Without a dedicated non-inferiority framework or sufficient power, such inferences regarding clinical equivalence remain statistically unsupported. Moreover, the lack of significant differences between standard- and remote-care might suggest that the increased number of contacts does not necessarily improve clinical outcomes, potentially revealing the inefficiency of the additive service in some setting.

The difficulty in isolating the specific value of televisits extends beyond methodological limitations and into the broader economic evaluation of these interventions, as a measure of their efficiency. While patient savings in terms of travel expenses are well-established, evidence regarding cost-effectiveness from the payer’s perspective (i.e., health systems) remains scarce and conflicting. A paradigmatic example of this discrepancy is found in the cost-utility analysis by Cubo et al. (Cubo et al. 2025): while telemedicine proved efficient for managing specific symptoms (e.g., depression, freezing), it failed to generate a net gain in Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) compared to standard care, thus delivering a “non-cost-effective” result, in view of the higher upfront investment required for hardware, training, and technical support (Cubo et al. 2025). Economic comparisons across these studies are confounded by the disparate technologies utilized for televisits. These ranged from costly, dedicated software (Dorsey et al. 2013) to ubiquitous consumer-grade applications such as FaceTime or WhatsApp (Cabrera-Martos et al. 2019; Evans et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025b). The use of the latter further raises significant data-security concerns, as these tools often lack the robust identity verification and safeguarding protocols required for protected health information (HIPAA 1996; Protection of Personal Information Act 2013; GDPR 2016). Moreover, their lack of integration with official electronic health records might create fragmented care. Future research must ultimately demonstrate a measurable impact on the long-term healthcare burden using ‘hard’ metrics such as hospitalization and mortality rates (Groeneveld 2018). This necessitates extending follow-up periods beyond the current sub-12-month standard (Dorsey et al. 2010, 2013; Heldman et al. 2017; Cubo et al. 2025), as short-term designs are inherently unable to capture sustained economic benefits.

Another key limitation of the current literature is its lack of generalizability, stemming primarily from a significant geographic bias. The existing research is predominantly U.S.-based, with sparse representation from resource-limited regions (Kondraske and Stewart 2008; Barbour et al. 2016; Evans et al. 2020; García-Bustillo et al. 2025a). Potential sampling biases are further evidenced by the disproportionate inclusion of white, highly educated, male patients in the early-to-moderate stages of Parkinson’s disease. Such demographics fail to represent more vulnerable cohorts that were underrepresented (Dorsey et al. 2013; Deeb et al. 2021; Saeed and Masters 2021; Xu et al. 2022). Furthermore, recruitment strategies relying on digital means (e.g., email or online surveys) (Dorsey et al. 2015b; Beck et al. 2017; Spear et al. 2019; Myers et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2022; Ruggiero et al. 2022) inherently target ‘digitally-ready’ populations, further marginalizing those with limited technological access or proficiency (Canoro et al. 2025). Notably, a recent multinational trial (García-Bustillo et al. 2025a) demonstrated that high retention rates in digitally advanced countries, like South Korea, do not translate globally. In developing regions such as Egypt and Uruguay, retention dropped to zero, highlighting that the feasibility of remote interventions is fundamentally contingent on local infrastructure rather than being a universal constant.

Furthermore, the findings of this study must be considered in light of the limitations inherent to our narrative review methodology. First, the literature search was constrained to a single electronic database (PubMed) and restricted to articles written in English, which may have led to the inadvertent omission of relevant evidence published in other repositories or in different languages. Second, although the initial screening of the retrieved records was conducted independently by two authors, the subsequent data extraction was performed by a single author, which could theoretically introduce extraction bias. Finally, the substantial clinical, methodological, and taxonomic heterogeneity characterizing the primary literature precluded the execution of a formal quantitative meta-analysis, mandating a narrative synthesis that inherently carries a degree of interpretative subjectivity.

This interpretative challenge is further compounded by the variable methodological rigor of the included primary literature, as clearly reflected in our formal quality assessment. As detailed in Supplementary Table 1, while several randomized controlled trials achieved high methodological standards (Quality Scores > 75%)—thus providing robust validity regarding the efficacy of telehealth interventions—many observational and cross-sectional studies exhibited lower scores. These lower ratings were primarily driven by inherent selection biases, such as the overrepresentation of tech-savvy populations, and a frequent failure to adequately identify or control for confounding factors. Consequently, the highly positive findings concerning patient satisfaction and overall feasibility derived from these specific cohorts must be weighted accordingly and interpreted with caution.

In sum, while televisits seem to offer undeniable advantages by enhancing accessibility and enabling a more personalized approach to PD care, there remain knowledge gaps and significant barriers that currently hinder their routine adoption. Table 3 delineates these barriers, categorized according to the outcome classification utilized throughout this review, and proposes potential mitigation strategies. Some of these advocate for a transition toward flexible, hybrid frameworks, an integrated approach that has not been adequately explored in the current literature, likely due to organizational complexities and the challenge of embedding adaptive modalities into rigid study protocols. Ultimately, the collection of real-world data on the integration of televisits into routine PD care may be required to bridge this evidence gap.

Identified barriers and proposed mitigation strategies, categorized for outcome domain

Infrastructure:

Lack of high-speed internet access or hardware devices, particularly in rural areas or among lower socioeconomic groups.

Technical instability:

Connection drops and software glitches disrupting the clinical workflow.

Access policies:

Promulgate policies for equitable digital infrastructure; provision of necessary hardware within structured programs.

Backup protocols:

Establish “low-tech” backup plans (e.g., telephone calls) to prevent visit cancellation.

Digital literacy:

Difficulty in independent use of technology due to age, low education level or cognitive decline.

Relational concerns:

Patient preference for face-to-face contact due to perceived lack of intimacy or thoroughness in televisits.

Facilitation:

Involve caregivers as “digital facilitators” to manage the technical setup; adopt simplified, senior-friendly interfaces.

Interaction optimization:

Prioritize video over audio-only to maintain visual engagement; train staff in remote communication skills; schedule periodic in-person follow-up visits.

Incomplete physical assessment:

Inability to accurately assess rigidity (tone) or postural instability (pull test) leads to modified/incomplete MDS-UPDRS scores.

Hybrid model:

Adopt validated “remote-specific” scales (e.g., mUPDRS) for routine monitoring, but revert to in-person visits when complete staging is required.

Generalizability & Complexity:

Current evidence of non-inferiority is largely restricted to stable patients; data on efficacy for unstable disease stages or complex fluctuations is lacking.

Evidence-Based Selection:

Prioritize televisits for routine follow-up where efficacy is proven (stable patients); maintain in-person standard of care for complex/unstable phases where efficacy is unproven.

Dependence on Supervision:

Review data show comparable safety profiles only when supervision is guaranteed; independent remote assessment is not validated for safety.

Mandatory Protocol:

Ensure caregiver presence as a mandatory requirement for patients with postural instability.

Economic viability:

High upfront costs and lack of reimbursement parity reduce cost-effectiveness for providers.

Inefficiency of “Additive” models (Augmentation Bias):

Telemedicine often increases workload and costs when used as an “add-on” rather than a replacement for standard care.

Sustainability:

Advocate for adequate reimbursement policies to cover technology and training costs.

Strategic substitution:

Shift from additive to substitutive models (replacing routine office visits when appropriate) to ensure economic sustainability.

MDS-UPDRS movement disorders society - unified Parkinson’s disease rating scale, mUPDRS modified version of the UPDRS, which excludes items of muscle tone and postural stability
