The influence of social capital and evidence-based orientation on psychiatrists’ attitudes toward antipsychotic polypharmacy in schizophrenia
Authors: Lagreula Juliette, Lorant Vincent, Dalleur Olivia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite showing no superiority over monotherapy, antipsychotic polypharmacy (APP) is used in up to half of schizophrenia patients. However, no standardized instrument currently exists to systematically evaluate clinicians’ attitudes toward APP and the role of social capital, underscoring the need for such a tool. This study presents the development of a scale designed to measure psychiatrists’ attitudes toward APP. It further investigates factors associated with more evidence-based medicine (EBM) attitudes, including social capital, general EBM orientation, and demographics. The Knowledge and Attitude scale toward Antipsychotic Polypharmacy in Schizophrenia (KAAPS) was developed and pre-tested. The questionnaire also incorporated measures of professional social capital, the Evidence-Based Practice Attitude Scale (Aaron et al., 2004), and demographic variables. The web-based survey was distributed to 1618 Belgian psychiatrists and psychiatry residents. Psychometric characteristics of the KAAPS were assessed to ensure validity and factors potentially associated with the KAAPS score were tested. Among 152 complete respondents, the mean KAAPS score was 55.8 out of 102 (SD = 13.7), indicating a tendency toward evidence-based knowledge and attitudes. Factors associated with lower evidence-based attitudes included higher professional social capital (β= -0.254, p < 0.01), working in non-multidisciplinary settings (β= -9.1435, p < 0.001), belonging to the Dutch-speaking community (β= -5.133, p = 0.036) and higher APP prescription rates (β= -0.194, p < 0.001). Practicing cognitive behavioural therapy (β = 6.366, p < 0.01) was associated with higher KAAPS scores, whereas intrinsic EBM orientation showed no significant association (β = 0.231, p = 0.083). Applied across diverse populations or countries, the KAAPS scale may help identify clinical circumstances in which antipsychotic polypharmacy is most frequently considered appropriate, thereby informing targeted educational interventions and supporting the evaluation of implementation strategies. Incorporating social network dynamics into these approaches, given the influence of social capital on psychiatrists’ attitudes toward antipsychotic polypharmacy, may further contribute to reducing inappropriate prescribing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31446-8.

Introduction

Up to half of patients with schizophrenia are treated with antipsychotic polypharmacy (APP), defined as the concurrent use of two or more antipsychotics1,2, despite evidence showing no superiority of efficacy compared to monotherapy3,4. APP induces more side effects, worsens global functioning, reduces treatment-adherence and increases the risk of relapse, all of which raises healthcare costs, making its widespread use problematic5–9. Clinical prescribing guidelines for schizophrenia, based on this evidence, recommend antipsychotic monotherapy as the standard of care10,11. Various factors may explain the recourse to APP, including attempts to further reduce symptoms or to manage comorbid conditions12,13.

Clinical practice should be based on the best available evidence, applied to the individuality of each patient. However, research findings are not always translated into practice, and many treatments used in routine care are not supported by strong evidence of efficacy or effectiveness14. The growing volume of medical literature, combined with the limited time available to clinicians, makes it difficult to keep up to date. Consequently, clinicians often rely on discussions with colleagues to stay informed about the latest innovations or to assist in interpreting new evidence, particularly when scientific findings are complex or controversial15. Moreover, the psychiatric field has benefitted from high-quality evidence later than other fields, such as oncology, which may have delayed the adoption of innovations such as new guidelines or novel pharmacological treatments16.

Social influence can shape attitudes and prescribing behaviour, and depends on the social capital of each clinician17. The influence of colleagues has been recognised by psychiatrists themselves as a factor affecting their decision to prescribe APP18. Two main theories have been proposed to explain how the clinician networks exert this influence. The first, supported by Coleman et al.19, suggests that the more socially connected a clinician is (i.e., the wider their network), the more likely they are to adopt innovations. In contrast, the second theory, known as the theory of structural holes, argues that the structure of the network is more important than its size, and that the denser the network, the less new information can penetrate it17. This may be because cohesive groups are effective at exerting normative pressure to conform, while new information from outside the network must first overcome these barriers. According to this theory, clinicians with more contacts outside the network are more likely to encounter and adopt innovation, whereas those most socially integrated within the network are less likely to do so17. Nevertheless, given the influence of social capital on clinical practice, it is important to assess its impact on attitudes toward antipsychotic polypharmacy.

Another important factor to consider is the individual differences among clinicians, particularly their intrinsic orientation toward evidence-based medicine (EBM)14.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined psychiatrists’ evidence-based attitudes and social capital using standardized methods to explain the use of suboptimal prescribing practices such as antipsychotic polypharmacy. Understanding these factors is important given the high prevalence of APP worldwide, as such insights could inform the development of targeted interventions. The high prevalence observed in Belgium2 provides an opportunity to investigate these dimensions.

Our study aims to : (1) Identify psychiatrists’ attitudes toward the use of antipsychotic polypharmacy in the treatment of schizophrenia, (2) Examine factors (e.g. social capital, demographics, intrinsic orientation toward EBM) associated with more evidence-based attitudes toward APP.

Methods

Development of the knowledge and attitude scale toward antipsychotic polypharmacy in schizophrenia (KAAPS)

The KAAPS scale was developed in several steps. First, the most frequent justifications for APP were identified in the literature12,13,20–23. We chose to focus on APP not involving clozapine, as recent data suggest that clozapine augmentation with aripiprazole may reduce psychiatric re-hospitalisations3. Clozapine, unlike other antipsychotics, is effective despite blocking less than the normally required 60% of D2 receptors21,24, and it also modulates glutamatergic transmission25. Clozapine is reserved for the treatment of refractory illness, and clozapine augmentation can only be used in situations of ultra-resistance (i.e. resistance to clozapine)10,11. Because of these distinct mechanisms of action and indications, we chose to study the most common form of APP, namely the use of two or more non-clozapine antipsychotics.

The robustness of evidence for each justification for APP was assessed using clinical guidelines or high-quality meta-analyses, where appropriate. An initial list of 16 items was developed. Cognitive testing with a panel of six experts (i.e., psychiatrists and clinical pharmacists) was conducted to identify misunderstandings and significant problems, thereby ensuring content validity26. Some items were modified accordingly, and one item was added, resulting in a 17-item scale. In the final KAAPS, each response was rated on a 7-point bipolar Likert scale (0 = Don’t know, 1 = Totally agree, 2 = Agree, 3 = Rather agree, 4 = Rather disagree, 5 = Disagree and 6 = Strongly disagree). The total KAAPS score was calculated as the sum of all items, with reverse coding applied where appropriate. Some items were reversed to avoid the “yea saying”, i.e., the tendency to agree more than disagree27.

The psychometric properties of the scale were assessed, and tests performed are described in Sect. 2.5. (Statistical analysis). The score ranges from 0 to 102, with the highest score indicating the most evidence-based attitude toward APP.

The French and Dutch versions of the scale are shown in the supplementary materials (eQuestionnaire (1) KAAPS scale- French version, eQuestionnaire (2) KAAPS scale- Dutch version).

Factors potentially associated with the KAAPS score

The Resource Generator for Psychiatrists (RG-Psy), an 11-item instrument scored on a 6-point Likert scale (range : 0–72), was used to measure the social capital of psychiatrists and its potential association with the KAAPS score; i.e., psychiatrists’ attitudes toward antipsychotic polypharmacy in schizophrenia28. The RG-Psy consists of a fixed list of resources representing a concrete collection of social capital in the professional field of psychiatry. It assesses the strength of the links through which different resources are accessed for various purposes in the psychiatric practice. The development and validation of this scale have been described previously28.

The Evidence-Based Practice Attitude Scale (EBPAS), a 15-item instrument scored on a 5-point Likert scale (range : 0–60), aims to measure the four dimensions that explain attitudes toward evidence-based medicine (EBM) in general among mental health providers14. These four dimensions cover the appeal of innovations (i.e., the influence of attractiveness depending on how the information is presented), the requirement by authorities or supervisors and clinician’s response to these requirements, general openness, and divergence14. Divergence appears when the difference between current practice and a new one is perceived as coming from another culture (e.g., the culture of research)14. Innovations are less likely to spread when it has to cross organisational or professional boundaries, such as recommendations coming from researchers to practicing clinicians, which may hamper diffusion depending on the personality of each clinician29. The EBPAS was another factor tested for its potential association with the KAAPS total score.

Finally, demographic data and individual factors were also collected to account for their potential co-influence on the KAAPS score. These include age, gender, primary spoken language (i.e., Dutch or French), psychotherapy orientation (i.e. type of psychotherapy most often used), practice setting, and years of practice (eTable 1 in Supplementary materials). Predictor selection was guided by prior literature review and theorical relevance.

Study population and data collection

Psychiatrists and residents in psychiatry (i.e., physicians currently in postgraduate training to become psychiatrists) working in Belgium were eligible for the survey. The public list of the Belgian federal agency for health, food chain safety and environment, which records all psychiatrists licensed to work in Belgium, was used to identify the population of interest. Residents in psychiatry were contacted through the head of the department of psychiatry at each Belgian medical school.

This cross-sectional survey was distributed between April and July 2022 as an online-self completion questionnaire, available in French and Dutch and accessible through a link sent by email.

Statistical analysis

Structural validity of the KAAPS scale was assessed by conducting an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which aims to determine the theoretical construct of the scale, i.e., the presence of subscales30,31. A Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and a Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed to confirm the appropriateness of conducting an EFA, where a KMO ≥ 0.6 indicates sampling adequacy and a p-value < 0.05 in the Bartlett’s test rejects the hypothesis of independence between variables31. EFA with a maximum likelihood for factor extraction was used as data are normally distributed30 and a scree test was conducted to determine the number of factors to retain30,32,33. Promax rotation was employed as the different dimensions of EBM were expected to be correlated, and items with loadings ≥ 0.3 after rotation for one factor were grouped with the corresponding factor32,33. Internal consistency was tested with Cronbach’s alpha for each subscale and for the total KAAPS scale, as well as with item-total correlation (ITC)27. Cronbach’s alpha between 0.7 and 0.9 indicates reliable consistency, and items with an ITC above 0.4 were kept27,34. Test-retest reliability was also ensured with a purposive sample of 8 respondents who completed the survey twice at one-week intervals, enabling the calculation of an intra-class correlation (ICC) where an ICC > 0.7 is considered reliable34.

The sum of item responses on the KAAPS scale was the dependent variable. Items were rated so that higher scores reflected a more evidence-based attitude. Potential predictors of the KAAPS score, identified through the literature and theoretical relevance, were initially included in a multiple linear regression model. The model was refined through backward stepwise elimination based on the Akaike Information Criterion. The final model retained only predictors selected through this process, with statistical significance defined as p-values < 0.05. This approach ensured a balance between theoretical justification and statistical robustness, minimizing the risk of arbitrary predictor selection. Casewise deletion was used for missing values, which accounted for only 4.6% of cases and were restricted to peripheral variables. This approach is recommended for low levels of missingness, and a sensitivity analysis confirmed that imputation decreased model fit. Model assumptions and validity were assessed through standard diagnostic procedures. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5 considered acceptable). Influential observations were identified using Cook’s distance (> 1) and DFBETAs (> 1). Normality of residuals was assessed visually and through statistical tests, and residual plots were examined for heteroscedasticity and non-linearity. The final model met all regression assumptions and demonstrated strong stability. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.0.3.

Ethics

This study was approved by the ethics committee of UCLouvain (No. 2022/08FEV/055) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participation was voluntary, and each respondent provided informed consent before completing the questionnaire. All responses were anonymous.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

After excluding ineligible psychiatrists (eFigure 1 in the Supplement), a final population of 1618 psychiatrists and residents in psychiatry was contacted. A total of 196 psychiatrists responded to the survey (response rate: 12.1%), but 44 questionnaires were excluded due to markedly incomplete data (i.e. only demographic data available), resulting in the analysis of 152 questionnaires.

Respondents were predominantly men (59.2%, n = 90), evenly distributed between the language communities (i.e. French-speaking: 52%, Dutch-speaking: 48%) with an average of 23.7 (SD = 15.5) years of practice. Psychiatrists’ characteristics are summarised in Table 1.

Psychometric characteristics and validation of the KAAPS scale

The KMO test (0.77) and the Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.0001) indicated that the data were suitable for conducting an EFA. Additionally, the sample size was appropriate, with at least 10 subjects per variable (for samples below 200)32. The scree plot suggested a five-factors model (i.e. five subscales). After rotation, every item loaded above 0.3 on one factor, and there was no cross-loading (Fig. 1). All items were retained, as they all have an ITC above 0.4, demonstrating good correlation with the other items. Internal consistency was great for the total KAAPS scale (0.89) and also good for each subscale (Table 2). Finally, the ICC of the KAAPS was above 0.7 (ICC = 0.72), indicating a good test-retest reliability.

Psychiatrists’ knowledge and attitude toward antipsychotic polypharmacy (KAAPS scale)

The KAAPS total score followed a normal distribution, with a mean of 55.8 (SD = 13.7) on the 0–102 scale. The majority of clinicians (62.5%) had a score above 51, indicating a more evidence-based knowledge and attitude.

Each response was scored on a 7-point bipolar Likert scale, and coded from 0 to 6. The most commonly known or accepted evidence for APP included “increased risk of side-effects” (4.7 ± 1.3), “increased risk of serious adverse events” (4.5 ± 1.3), and “reduced treatment-adherence” (4.3 ± 1.5). The least known or accepted evidence was that APP was inappropriate for patient with a dual diagnosis of schizophrenia and substance use disorder (2.5 ± 1.5), and for patients with a dual diagnosis of schizophrenia and intellectual disability (2.6 ± 1.6), as well as after an inadequate response to monotherapy (2.9 ± 1.4) (Table 2). The mean response regarding the use of APP for sleep disorders (3.0 ± 1.3) and the combination of different pharmacological profiles (3.0 ± 1.1) suggests that most psychiatrists are uncertain about these justifications for use (Table 2).

Factors associated with an evidence-based attitude toward APP

Psychiatrists with higher professional social capital (β= −0.254, p < 0.01) were significantly less likely to have an evidence-based attitude toward APP, judging APP less problematic in several situations. In addition, working in a non-multidisciplinary setting (i.e. private practice, primary mental health services, prisons) (β=−9.1435, p < 0.001), belonging to the Dutch-speaking community (β=−5.133, p = 0.036) and prescribing a higher percentage of APP (β=−0.194, p < 0.001) also decreased the evidence-based attitude toward APP.

Conversely, practising cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (β = 6.366, p < 0.01) increased the odds of adopting evidence-based opinions toward APP, i.e., finding APP less scientifically sound.

Finally, the EBPAS score did not significantly explain the KAAPS score (β = 0.231, p = 0.083) (Table 3).

Discussion

Responses to the KAAPS scale, which exhibits robust psychometric properties, indicate that a slight majority of psychiatrists hold an evidence-based attitude toward APP. However, while most psychiatrists clearly acknowledge the potential harmful effects of APP and its negative impact on treatment adherence, a significant proportion still considers its use appropriate in specific clinical contexts, particularly in cases of dual diagnosis such as intellectual disability or substance use disorder.

In complex clinical scenarios, psychiatrists may justify APP by targeting distinct symptom domains. For instance, up to 4.4% of patients with intellectual disability also present with schizophrenia35. In such dual diagnosis cases, clinicians may prescribe one antipsychotic for psychotic symptoms and another, generally more sedating agent, for aggressive or challenging behaviours associated with intellectual disability36–40. Similarly, patients with comorbid schizophrenia and SUD present significant treatment challenges. Indeed, SUD diminishes antipsychotic efficacy, resulting in more severe positive symptoms, reduced treatment adherence, and poorer overall illness trajectory41,42. Although evidence regarding the optimal choice of initial antipsychotic for patients with SUD remains limited, some studies suggest that clozapine and risperidone may offer advantages in reducing substance misuse and craving, respectively43. Consequently, psychiatrists may use APP to simultaneously address psychotic symptoms and substance-related complications or to compensate for reduced apparent efficacy. These scenarios illustrate how perceived clinical complexity can influence prescribing practices, even in the absence of robust supporting evidence. Clinical guidelines recommend following standard schizophrenia treatment principles for patients with comorbid intellectual disability or SUD, which includes avoiding antipsychotic polypharmacy (APP) whenever possible. This recommendation is particularly critical given the elevated risk of motor side effects and other adverse outcomes in these populations11,39,42,44.

Psychiatrists with greater professional social capital were significantly less likely to have an evidence-based attitude toward APP (β= −0.225, p = 0.013). According to diffusion of innovation theory, new practices such as evidence-based medicine are more likely to spread through interpersonal networks rather than through top-down dissemination of clinical guidelines (i.e. authority mandates)16. However, the presence of structural holes suggests that psychiatrists tend to socialise around shared opinions. This pattern leads the most socially integrated clinicians to reinforce prevailing attitudes within their network, thereby reducing the penetration of new evidence or practices from external sources16. The relationship between social capital and EBM adoption is therefore context-dependent. In settings where evidence-based prescribing and deprescribing practices are already well-established practices within the psychiatric community, higher social capital facilitates the adoption and reinforcement of these practices through network effects. Conversely, in contexts where EBM prescribing and deprescribing remain novel or contested innovations, dense network cohesion may impede their adoption16. Indeed, in such situations, strong network cohesion may hinder the adoption of practices perceived as externally imposed or divergent from established group norms29. This appears to be the case in our sample, where APP may be normalised by local norms rather than scientific evidence.

Furthermore, psychiatrists working in non-multidisciplinary settings (i.e., private practice, primary mental health services, prisons) were also less likely to adopt an evidence-based attitude towards APP (β=−9.338, p < 0.001). This finding corroborates the social network hypothesis outlined above. Psychiatrists working in isolated settings have fewer interactions with clinicians outside their established professional network and may therefore be less exposed to innovations emerging from other networks. Conversely, those working in multidisciplinary settings have greater opportunities to encounter diverse perspectives and evidence-based practices through weak ties (i.e., connections that bridge structural holes and facilitate the diffusion of innovations across network boundaries). Additionally, working in a multidisciplinary setting itself reflects a more evidence-based approach to mental health treatment. Psychiatrists working in such environments are therefore more likely to adopt an evidence-based stance when prescribing antipsychotics for schizophrenia.

The practice of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) was significantly associated with a more evidence-based attitude toward APP. CBT is the psychotherapeutic approach with the strongest evidence base for efficacy across multiple mental disorders45–47. Psychiatrists who incorporate CBT into their practice may therefore exhibit a consistent evidence-based orientation that influences both their psychotherapeutic and pharmacotherapeutic decision-making.

Finally, the EBPAS score, reflecting general EBM orientation, was not significantly correlated with KAAPS scores. This suggests that, in this population, social capital exerts a stronger influence on prescribing attitudes than individual EBM orientation.

The KAAPS scale, when applied across diverse populations or international contexts, may help identify clinical scenarios in which antipsychotic polypharmacy is commonly considered appropriate. Focusing educational interventions on these specific scenarios may promote evidence-based prescribing. Moreover, the KAAPS provides a structured tool to monitor the effectiveness of such educational sessions. To reduce ‘echo’ effects and bridge gaps, early-career psychiatrists should be encouraged to engage with clinicians and researchers outside their immediate network. Local opinion leaders could also play a role in disseminating evidence-based practices through lectures or informal educational sessions, though their effectiveness may depend on context and complementary interventions. Using tools like KAAPS and RG-Psy to assess local contexts could further guide tailored implementation strategies48.

Although the KAAPS demonstrated good psychometric characteristics and survey respondents provided a representative sample of Belgian psychiatrists (i.e. covering the three administrative regions of Belgium, linguistic communities, and workplace settings), certain limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. The theoretical construct of the KAAPS scale was identified through exploratory factor analysis, as no clear prior hypothesis regarding its underlying structure was available. However, the absence of cross-validation through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on an independent sample limits the strength of the construct validity evidence. Collecting such a validation sample was not feasible within the scope of the current study, therefore, future studies should conduct CFA validation on new data to confirm the factor structure. In addition, the test-retest subsample was relatively small (n = 8) and, although it offers preliminary evidence of temporal stability, confirmation with a larger sample is needed. In addition, two factors contain only two items, which may affect their long-term stability despite strong factor loadings. Future studies should evaluate whether these factors would benefit from additional items or whether their parsimonious structure optimally captures these specific constructs. The response rate of 12.1% may raise concerns about generalisability. However, lower response rates are typical in physician surveys due to time constraints49,50, with psychiatrists exhibiting the lowest participation rates among medical specialists51. While financial incentives have been shown to increase physician response rates51,52, we opted not to use them in this study. Nevertheless, recent evidence suggests that low response rates do not inherently introduce bias, nor do higher rates guarantee unbiased results. Instead, comparing respondents’ and non-respondents’ characteristics is a more valid approach to assessing potential bias53,54. In this survey, non-respondents were demographically similar to respondents, mitigating concerns about selection bias (eTable 3). Finally, cross-sectional design of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships.

Conclusion

The application of the KAAPS scale across diverse populations and countries may facilitate the identification of clinical circumstances in which antipsychotic polypharmacy is most frequently regarded as appropriate. Directing educational interventions toward these specific scenarios could improve adherence to evidence-based prescribing practices. Additionally, the KAAPS provides a systematic tool for evaluating and monitoring the effectiveness of implementation strategies. Given the demonstrated impact of social capital on psychiatrists’ attitudes toward antipsychotic polypharmacy, integrating social network dynamics into implementation approaches may further contribute to reducing inappropriate prescribing.
