High-performance hydrogen detection of nanoarrays based on plasmonic enhancement mechanism
Authors: Xuhui Zhang, Liang Guo, Xinran Wei, Qiang Liu, Yuzhang Liang, Junsheng Wang, Wei Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to the demand for hydrogen safety monitoring, this study proposes an optical hydrogen sensor based on surface plasmon resonance enhancement. Traditional palladium film optical sensors have problems such as weak response and susceptibility to interference. This design innovatively uses low refractive index magnesium fluoride as the substrate and combines it with a periodic palladium nanopore array structure. Finite-difference time-domain simulation shows that the structure exhibits a significant redshift in the reflection spectrum after hydrogen adsorption and an increase in reflectivity, with a sensitivity superior to that of traditional planar palladium films. The nanopore array enhances the optical response through local field enhancement and surface lattice resonance coupling. The magnesium fluoride substrate, with low optical loss and excellent light transmittance, further optimizes the plasmon resonance performance. This study provides a new approach for the development of highly sensitive, fast-response, and intrinsically safe hydrogen sensors.Keywords: Hydrogen sensors, Palladium nanopore arrays, Surface plasmon resonance, Magnesium fluoride substrate, Optical gas detection.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

With the global imperative to achieve carbon neutrality, hydrogen energy, as a clean and highly efficient secondary energy carrier, is currently undergoing an unprecedented wave of development1. From the commercial deployment of hydrogen fuel cell vehicles to the execution of large-scale green hydrogen smelting projects, the construction of hydrogen energy infrastructure has created a critical demand for hydrogen sensors that are safe, reliable, and capable of real-time monitoring2. Hydrogen, being colorless and odorless, is highly flammable and explosive (with an explosion limit of 4–75%) and prone to leakage due to its small molecular weight and high diffusion coefficient. In facilities such as hydrogen production plants, hydrogen refueling stations, hydrogen transmission pipelines, and confined spaces (e.g., the compartments of hydrogen vehicles), hydrogen can accumulate to dangerous concentrations within seconds to tens of seconds following a leak. Consequently, the advancement of hydrogen sensing technology—characterized by high sensitivity, rapid response, strong anti-interference capabilities, and intrinsic safety—is of paramount importance for ensuring the secure operation of the entire hydrogen energy industry chain3.

Currently, commercial hydrogen sensors predominantly utilize electrical principles for detection, categorized into three main types: resistive, field-effect transistor, and electrochemical sensors4–6. Among these, palladium (Pd)-based resistive sensors are prevalent due to their relatively low cost and well-established technology. These sensors operate by detecting the resistance change that occurs when palladium absorbs hydrogen to form palladium hydride (PdHx). However, significant bottleneck issues have emerged during practical applications. Electrical sensors are susceptible to electromagnetic interference (EMI), raising concerns about their stability in complex industrial environments. Furthermore, the inherent reliance on electrical contact restricts their suitability for use in hazardous areas prone to flammability and explosion. To address the aforementioned limitations, researchers have focused on the optical sensing approach. By leveraging the reversible changes in the optical constant (complex refractive index) of palladium during hydrogenation reactions, this method converts concentration information into modulated optical signals such as transmittance, reflectance, or interference spectra, enabling precise measurements7,8. This technique offers distinct advantages, including intrinsic safety (no risk of electric sparks), immunity to electromagnetic interference, and the capability for long-distance remote sensing. However, traditional palladium film-based optical sensors are still limited by a critical performance issue: the weak optical response at the nanoscale. Furthermore, the low signal strength renders these sensors susceptible to ambient light noise or fluctuations within the optical system. Although enhancement strategies based on Fabry-Perot interference can improve sensitivity, they remain highly sensitive to minor mechanical vibrations, making them unsuitable for demanding engineering environments.

The emergence of surface plasmon technology has introduced transformative opportunities for addressing the performance limitations of traditional optical hydrogen sensors. Surface plasmons represent a non-radiative electromagnetic mode resulting from the collective oscillation of free electrons at the interface between a metal and a medium. Their ability to constrain optical fields and enhance near-field effects at the nanoscale surpasses the conventional diffraction limit. Although palladium is considered a weak plasma material compared to other noble metals such as gold and silver, significant local surface plasmon resonance can be achieved through precise nanostructure engineering. When the characteristic dimensions of the designed palladium nanostructures fall within the range of tens to hundreds of nanometers, the resonant frequency can be tuned to the visible-near-infrared spectrum. Theoretical analyses indicate that the formant shift generated by optimized nano-plasmonic structures can be 10 to 100 times greater than that of planar films9. Moreover, the substantial specific surface area provided by nanostructures markedly enhances the kinetics of hydrogen diffusion and mass transport, enabling early warning sensors capable of achieving second-level response times.

In response to the aforementioned challenges, this study proposes an innovative device architecture: employing low refractive index magnesium fluoride (MgF2) as the substrate and integrating it with a periodically ordered palladium nanopore array to construct an enhanced plasmonic optical hydrogen sensor. The refractive index of MgF₂ in the visible-near-infrared region is significantly lower than that of conventional glass or oxides, effectively reducing the light absorption and scattering correction of the substrate. This allows for greater localization of electromagnetic energy within the “hot spot area” at the edges of the palladium nanopores. Compared to discrete nanoparticles, the self-supporting ordered Pd nanoporous structure can effectively distribute hydrogen absorption stress and suppress structural peristaltic fusion via physical anchoring effects, thereby providing superior cycling stability. Furthermore, the lattice resonance coupling effect induced by periodic nanoporous arrays, such as surface lattice resonance (SLR), can substantially narrow the linewidth and enhance refractive index sensitivity. This solution has established a robust technical platform for the research and development of the next generation of high-performance optical hydrogen safety sensors, addressing the core requirements of the future hydrogen economy for sensors that are highly sensitive, rapid in response, and intrinsically safe.

This study mainly utilized the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method to obtain the reflection spectrum and electric field distribution diagrams. In the numerical calculation, the refractive index of the MgF2 substrate was set as a constant value of 1.3910. The dielectric constant of metallic palladium was modeled using the Drude-Lorentz model11. The dielectric constant of palladium hydride is derived from the experimental data of Palm12. The refractive index data was measured on a hydrogenated Pd film prepared under conditions of high hydrogen partial pressure (7 bar H2), room temperature, 200 nm film thickness, electron beam evaporation and annealing (350 °C), which is close to the optical constants of saturated β-phase palladium hydride. The x and y directions of the FDTD region are set as the periodic arrangement directions of the structure, and the region precisely includes one period in both directions. The z direction is the normal direction of the structure, which includes a sufficiently thick substrate and a sufficiently large space above the structure. In the calculation, a fine grid of 2 × 2 × 1 nm3 is adopted in the x, y, and z directions, and the time step is set to 1000 fs to meet the Courant stability condition. A plane wave with a wavelength range of 400–1100 nm and polarization in the x direction is incidental along the −z direction onto the surface of the structure. Due to the symmetry of the studied structure, anti-symmetric boundary conditions are set in the x direction, symmetric boundary conditions in the y direction, and perfect matching layer (PML) boundary conditions in the z direction, with the number of layers set to 12. The background refractive index is set to 1.

These findings indicate that the optical dielectric constant of the nanoporous structure undergoes a substantial alteration upon hydrogen adsorption, transitioning from Pd to PdHx. The resonant wavelength is taken from the wavelength corresponding to the lowest point of reflectance in the reflection spectrum. In this study, λp corresponds to the location of the reflection peak or Wood anomaly, while λD corresponds to the position of the local reflection valley induced by the momentum matching between the surface plasmon and the grating wave vector13.

According to the aforementioned formula, the two reflection peaks located at λP1 = 437 nm and λP2 = 609 nm can be ascribed to the Wood anomaly effect at the (1, 0) order Pd/MgF2 interface and the (1, 0) order Pd/air interface, respectively. The reflection valleys at λD1 = 501 nm and λD2 = 770 nm correspond to the surface plasmon resonances of the (1, 1) order Pd/MgF2 and the (1, 0) order Pd/MgF2, respectively. Unlike the Wood anomaly, λD is determined by and highly sensitive to the change of ε1. Therefore, when Pd → PdHx, the wavelength and line shape of λD2 change significantly, while the position of λD1 remains basically unchanged. It is worth noting that, in comparison with noble metals such as gold and silver, the plasmonic effect of metallic palladium is relatively weaker14–16. This is primarily attributed to its higher light absorption coefficient and larger imaginary part of the dielectric constant, which result in a reduced quality factor of resonance and suppress the extraordinary optical transmission phenomenon commonly observed in other metal nanoporous arrays17. It should be emphasized that the reduced quality factor of this resonant material property does not weaken the hydrogenation effect. During the Pd → PdHx process, it is accompanied by a significant change. When combined with MgF2, the material change can be efficiently converted into a distinct positional and reflection intensity difference without the need to increase the linewidth as a trade-off.

Results and discussion

To further validate the resonance mechanism, the electric field distribution of the nanoporous cross-sections at various resonance wavelengths was systematically analyzed. As depicted in Fig. 3, the findings reveal a localized electric field enhancement region at the interfaces between Pd/MgF2 and Pd/air. This enhancement arises from the coupling of surface plasmon resonance and periodic structures. At the Pd/MgF2 interface, the electric field enhancement is predominantly governed by surface plasmon resonance, whereas at the Pd/air interface, the Wood anomaly further modulates the electric field distribution. This detailed electric field distribution not only confirms the excitation of surface plasmon resonance but also provides a robust foundation for the aforementioned physical mechanism.

It can be observed from the existing data that, due to the distinct properties of reflection peak/valley characteristics within nanopores, their dynamic variations exhibit significant differences. Previous studies have demonstrated that the dielectric constant of palladium increases by approximately 20% in a hydrogen atmosphere12,18,19. This results in an increase in as defined in Eq. (1) and λp(i, j) shifts towards longer wavelengths. Furthermore, owing to variations in resonance indices (i, j), the changes in the dielectric constants of palladium and palladium hydride are more pronounced in the infrared region compared to the visible light region12. Conversely, the wavelength shifts at λp1 and λp2 associated with the Wood anomaly remain relatively minor. As indicated by Eq. (2), these shifts are independent of the optical dielectric constant of the palladium/palladium hydride film.

When the palladium film nanopore array structure is exposed to a hydrogen environment, the enhancement of these peaks and valleys occurs because of the attenuation of the local electric field at the resonant wavelength. Hydrogen absorption induces a reduction in the negative dispersion factor ε2/ε1 (where ε1 and ε2 represent the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constants of palladium and palladium hydride films, respectively)12,20, thereby diminishing the plasmonic properties of the palladium nanoparticle array. Simulation results indicate that higher light absorption (corresponding to lower reflectivity) is observed at the Wood anomaly, whereas higher reflectivity is detected at the resonance valley. Notably, the reflectance at λD1 does not conform to this trend (the reflectance decreases at the formant peak during hydrogenation), which can be attributed to the anomalous proximity of this resonance to the Wood anomaly.

It is evident from Fig. 4 that as the diameter D increases from 200 nm, the wavelength position of the reflection valley D1 shifts, and the reflectance in specific wavelength ranges decreases significantly. For instance, at D = 200 nm, the reflection valley D2 is located at approximately λ = 757 nm. As the diameter increases (e.g., D = 250 nm), the reflection valley undergoes a redshift. However, as the radius continues to increase, for instance, when D = 375 nm, the characteristic valley D2 shows a trend of blue shift again. Meanwhile, the increase in the opening size leads to an increase in the fill factor, a decrease in the overall reflection, and the depth of the valley first increases and then tends to flatten. As the aperture size increases, the equivalent refractive effect at the interface strengthens, causing the resonance to redshift initially; when the aperture size continues to increase, resulting in a decrease in the metal duty cycle, weakened mode confinement, and increased radiation leakage, the equivalent propagation constant decreases and changes the interference conditions with the background, pulling the spectral valley position back to a shorter wavelength band, thus causing the resonance to blueshift. The competition between these two effects forms a non-monotonic change of first redshift and then blueshift21. Additionally, an increase in hole diameter enhances the filling factor (the ratio of the holes’ area to the total area), allowing more light to pass through the holes rather than being reflected. Consequently, this leads to a reduction in reflectivity. Furthermore, an excessively large hole diameter may strengthen the local plasma mode while weakening the influence of periodic resonance, thereby causing a further decrease in reflectivity for certain wavelengths and forming deeper valleys22. The results indicate that the peak position varies with changes in diameter. In hydrogen gas sensing applications, selecting an appropriate pore diameter can maintain a balance between sensitivity and selectivity, whereas an overly large pore diameter may compromise spectral selectivity.

The variation in the thickness of the palladium film exerts a substantial modulating effect on the characteristics of the reflection spectrum. As shown in Fig. 4, when the thickness of the palladium film on the surface increases from 15 to 50 nm, three significant changes occur in the spectral morphology: First, the overall total reflectance increases, especially at the characteristic valley D1. Second, the characteristic valleys D1 and D2 narrow as the thickness increases. Third, the position of the valley D2 shifts towards shorter wavelengths (blue shift) as the thickness continuously increases, and the valley depth is most pronounced at a medium thickness (about 30–35 nm), while it slightly decreases when the thickness is further increased to 40–50 nm. This phenomenon can be attributed to the suppression of electromagnetic field penetration and leakage by the thick film: although palladium has strong absorption, in the current structure, the increase in thickness reduces the penetration depth of the field in the metal, weakens the transmission through the channels, and increases the reflection coefficient at the metal/medium interface, thus more incident energy is reflected rather than transmitted, resulting in an increase in the baseline reflectance with thickness. At the same time, a blue shift of the reflection valleys can be observed. This is also due to the change in the resonance conditions caused by the thickness variation: as the film thickness increases, the proportion of the “sampling” of the surrounding medium by the surface plasmon decreases, and the equivalent dielectric constant experienced decreases, thereby increasing the equivalent resonance frequency and causing the resonance wavelength to shift towards shorter wavelengths (blue shift)17. Combining Fig. 4, the intensity of the peaks shows a trend of increasing and slightly narrowing with the increase in thickness, which is most significant at around 30–35 nm. When the thickness is further increased to 40–50 nm, the peak/valley contrast slightly decreases due to the influence of Ohmic loss and radiation damping23. In hydrogen gas sensing applications, employing a thinner palladium film may facilitate a faster response due to the shorter hydrogen diffusion path. Conversely, a thicker palladium film provides significant spectral contrast, aiding in the detection of subtle variations in hydrogen concentration24. Therefore, in practical implementations, an optimal balance must be achieved between these two factors.

To further validate the application and sensing capabilities of palladium film nanopore arrays in hydrogen detection, this study systematically compared the reflection spectra before hydrogenation (Pd) and after hydrogenation (PdHx). As shown in Fig. 5, the spectral characteristics after hydrogenation (PdHx) exhibit a significant shift in the position of the reflection valley. For instance, the reflection valley prior to hydrogenation (Dip 2, λ = 780 nm) shifts to 795 nm post-hydrogenation. This redshift phenomenon can be attributed to the alteration in the dielectric constant of PdHx. Specifically, when metallic palladium absorbs hydrogen to form PdHx, its lattice expands, leading to a reduction in the dielectric constant and a consequent shift toward longer wavelengths at the surface plasmon resonance wavelength25. In addition, the reflection intensity decreases after hydrogenation. The main reason for this is that the extinction coefficient of PdHx is lower than that of metallic Pd. The metal loss and radiation return are weakened, and the change in the real part of the refractive index reduces the impedance mismatch at the metal/medium interface, allowing more incident energy to pass through or couple into the resonant channel rather than being reflected24. Furthermore, the approximately 20 nm wavelength shift of the reflection valley and the roughly 5% change in reflectance suggest that this palladium film nanopore array exhibits relatively high sensitivity to hydrogen.

As shown in Fig. 6, when the period P increases within the range of 400–700 nm, both typical spectral features shift towards the long-wave band, and the reflection intensity increases with the increase of P (the area ratio of the holes within one period decreases with the increase of P, and the metal coverage increases). More importantly, the displacement of the valley D2 on the long-wave side almost linearly changes with P, while the displacement amplitude of the valley D1 on the short-wave side is relatively small and the linewidth is wider. This trend is consistent with the nature of the two types of modes we observed in Fig. 3b (electric field distribution): D2 is an interface-propagating lattice-coupled resonance, and D1 is mainly dominated by the Wood anomaly. Unless otherwise specified, the remaining research in this paper uses a fixed period of P = 500 nm. This value can keep D2 within the wavelength range of 700–850 nm while maintaining a sufficient wavelength interval from the Wood anomaly on the substrate side, thereby avoiding the broadening of the linewidth caused by modal mixing and matching the self-assembled template in the subsequent experiments.

In this study, magnesium fluoride (MgF2) was employed as the substrate material, and a comparative analysis was performed with silicon dioxide (SiO2), which is traditionally used in most studies. Figure 7 illustrates the reflection spectra of palladium film nanopore arrays deposited on MgF2 and SiO2 substrates in both pre-hydrogenation (Pd) and post-hydrogenation (PdHx) states. As shown in Fig. 7, the choice of substrate material significantly influences the characteristics of the reflection spectrum. Prior to hydrogenation, the reflection valley of the MgF2 substrate (λ = 772 nm) exhibits a blue shift (21 nm) relative to that of the SiO2 substrate (λ = 793 nm). Additionally, the peak reflectance of the MgF2 substrate surpasses that of the SiO2 substrate. This disparity arises from the differing dielectric constants of the two substrates. The refractive index of MgF2 is lower than that of SiO2, leading to a modification of the effective dielectric environment at the interface between the palladium film and the substrate, thereby modulating the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) conditions26. Specifically, the reduced refractive index of the MgF2 substrate decreases the resonant wavelength of SPR, shifting the reflection valley towards shorter wavelengths while enhancing the resonant intensity and resulting in a higher peak reflectance27. After hydrogenation, the reflection valley (λ = 820 nm) of the MgF2 substrate still demonstrates a blue shift trend (24 nm) compared to the SiO2 substrate (λ = 844 nm). The influence of the substrate material on the wavelength change of SPR induced by hydrogenation remains relatively minor. However, the reflectance change of the MgF2 substrate is considerably greater than that of the SiO2 substrate, suggesting that the MgF2 substrate exhibits higher sensitivity to the spectral response induced by hydrogen. This enhanced sensitivity can be attributed to the exceptionally low optical loss (extinction coefficient) and superior optical transparency of the MgF2 substrate, which facilitates more efficient SPR coupling at the interface between the palladium film and the substrate. Consequently, this amplifies the impact of the dielectric constant change of PdHx on the reflection spectrum24. Furthermore, the chemical stability of MgF2 may minimize interfacial reactions between the substrate and the palladium film, thus enhancing the long-term stability of the sensor26.

Conclusion

This study addresses the limitations of traditional hydrogen sensors, including susceptibility to electromagnetic interference, the potential for electric spark generation, and weak optical response. A novel plasmonic optical sensor is proposed, based on an ordered palladium nanopore array fabricated on a magnesium fluoride (MgF2) substrate. The design minimizes light loss by utilizing a low-refractive-index MgF2 substrate (compared to SiO2), and incorporates a periodic palladium nanopore structure (pore diameter: 300 nm; period: 500 nm) to achieve localized electromagnetic field enhancement. Upon hydrogen absorption, the reflection spectrum exhibits a 19 nm redshift at 770 nm, accompanied by an 11% increase in reflectivity (from 0.35 to 0.46). Parameter optimization reveals that reducing the palladium film thickness (from 30 to 10 nm) accelerates hydrogen diffusion but diminishes spectral contrast. This sensor integrates intrinsic safety, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and remote monitoring capabilities, offering a high-performance solution for safety monitoring in hydrogen energy infrastructure.
