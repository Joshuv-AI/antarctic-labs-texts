Plant spatial compartmentalization buffers bacteriome structure and function under antibiotic stress
Authors: Caryn Kgokonyane Lenonyane, Karabo Tsholo, Lesego Getrude Molale-Tom, Cornelius Carlos Bezuidenhout, Oluwaseyi Samuel Olanrewaju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agricultural antibiotic contamination poses increasing threats to crop productivity and ecosystem stability through disruption of the plant-associated microbiome. While antibiotic impacts on bulk soil and rhizosphere communities are documented, the extent to which spatial compartmentalization across the plant-soil continuum buffers these effects remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated how compartment-specific selective pressures influence bacterial community assembly, functional resilience, and interaction networks under antibiotic stress. Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) was grown under five treatments in a completely randomized greenhouse design: T1 (sulfamethoxazole [SMX], 3 mg kg⁻¹ + manure + plant), T2 (trimethoprim [TMP], 3 mg kg⁻¹ + manure + plant), T3 (manure + plant, antibiotic-free control), T4 (manure only, plant-free control), and T5 (soil only, negative control). Bacterial communities were profiled across bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere compartments using full-length 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Spatial compartmentalization emerged as the primary driver of bacteriome structure and functional potential, surpassing antibiotic treatment effects across all analytical approaches. PERMANOVA revealed significant compartment-driven community structuring (R² = 0.189, P = 0.001), while treatment effects were non-significant (R² = 0.145, P = 0.116). Endosphere communities exhibited substantially lower alpha diversity than bulk soil and rhizosphere (P = 0.0001), with significant treatment × compartment interactions (P = 0.007). Antibiotic treatments selectively enriched xenobiotic degradation (P = 0.042) and secondary metabolism functions, particularly in bulk soil, without systematically increasing pathogen-associated or resistance-related functions. Network analysis revealed reduced bacterial connectivity under antibiotic pressure, yet cooperative interactions dominated across all treatments. Compositional differential abundance testing (ALDEx2) detected no significantly altered taxa for primary antibiotic contrasts (T1 vs. T3, T2 vs. T3), indicating context-driven rather than antibiotic-driven compositional changes. Functional diversity was significantly structured by compartment (Shannon P = 0.0017; richness P = 0.0039), while core plant-beneficial functions remained stable across treatments, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.8) restricted to antibiotic degradation and secondary metabolism pathways. Our findings demonstrate that plant-microbe spatial structuring provides an ecological buffer that maintains core bacteriome functions against pharmaceutical disturbance, preserving plant-beneficial capabilities despite compositional shifts. The selective enrichment of antibiotic degradation pathways suggests potential for microbiome-assisted mitigation of pharmaceutical residues in agricultural systems. These results provide insights for developing compartment-specific microbiome management strategies that integrate with One Health approaches to enhance agricultural resilience under increasing pharmaceutical pressure in agroecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46797-z.

Introduction

The impact of antibiotics on the plant microbiome has garnered increasing attention due to its significance for food security and human health1–3. Antibiotics, while critical for controlling pathogenic bacteria, can disrupt the balance of plant-associated microbiota, leading to considerable implications in agriculture and broader ecological contexts. Understanding these interactions is vital for addressing the emerging concerns about antibiotic resistance and its impacts on food production systems. Antibiotics can alter the composition of the plant microbiome, influencing both beneficial and harmful microbial communities. For instance, some rhizosphere microbes act as natural biocontrol agents against phytopathogens, producing antibiotic metabolites that directly suppress pathogen populations4. However, the introduction of antibiotics can compromise the natural biotic interactions essential for plant health, potentially permitting the proliferation of phytopathogens that would otherwise be kept in check5. Moreover, the rhizosphere microbiome composition has profound implications for nutrient cycling, which is critical for plant growth. The disruption of these microbial communities by antibiotics, among others, leads to a reduced capacity for nitrogen fixation, as specific beneficial bacteria important in nitrogen fixation are diminished under antibiotic pressure6. Similar disruptions affect bacteria responsible for phosphorus mobilization, sulfur cycling, and other essential nutrient transformations. This diminishment jeopardizes the plant’s nutritional intake and could alter the growth rates and overall health of plants, thus impacting agricultural yield and food security. The concern also extends to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) in agricultural environments. Antibiotic use in agriculture, particularly through practices such as manure fertilization and wastewater irrigation, contributes significantly to the emergence and spread of ARB7. These resistant strains can threaten plant health and enter the human food chain, posing a risk to human health and complicating the treatment of infections caused by these resistant pathogens8,9. Beyond compositional changes, antibiotics may alter microbial interaction networks that underpin community stability and ecosystem functioning, requiring network-based approaches to understand community-level responses.

Antibiotic contamination in agricultural soils is an emerging global concern10,11, threatening crop productivity12, soil health13, and public safety through the persistence and spread of antibiotic resistance genes14. While antibiotic impacts on bulk soil and rhizosphere microbiomes have been reported15,16, the extent to which these disturbances affect the full plant-soil continuum, including the plant endosphere, remains poorly understood. Spatial compartmentalization, through the distinct niches of bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere, may act as an ecological buffer that maintains key microbial functions under disturbance. Yet, the mechanisms and extent of such buffering, particularly under pharmaceutical stress, remain largely unknown. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for predicting microbiome resilience and for developing microbiome-based strategies that align with sustainable agriculture and One Health goals. Within the One Health framework, an integrated approach recognizing the interconnected health of humans, animals, and ecosystems, which emphasizes that antimicrobial resistance emerging in agricultural environments can propagate to human pathogens through food chains, water systems, and horizontal gene transfer17.

The persistence and abundance of antibiotic-resistant genes (ARGs) in the soil microbiome can significantly impact both agricultural productivity and human health18. The global distribution of ARGs in diverse agricultural settings raises concerns regarding their implications for microbial community dynamics in soil, directly relating to plant health and agricultural outputs19,20. The study of wastewater treatment plants has revealed substantial reservoirs of ARGs, contributing further to the environmental persistence of these resistance traits21,22. In terms of microbial resilience, certain plant-associated bacteria can recover from antibiotic exposure, potentially restoring beneficial microbiota that aid in plant health and productivity. However, this recovery can vary significantly based on baseline microbiota composition, as highlighted in the studies by Rashidi, et al.23 and Lavelle, et al.24 who discussed how individual microbial communities can exhibit different resilience patterns following antibiotic perturbation. This resilience emphasizes the need for understanding specific microbial interactions and their susceptibility to antibiotics to refine agricultural practices aimed at sustaining food security. However, the extent to which spatial compartmentalization within plant-soil systems may buffer against antibiotic impacts remains unclear, with different compartments potentially exhibiting varying degrees of resilience to pharmaceutical stress.

Plant-associated microbiome exhibits strong spatial organization across bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere compartments, each characterized by distinct selective pressures and physicochemical conditions. For example, the rhizosphere is characterized by elevated carbon availability from root exudates, lower oxygen tension, and altered pH compared to bulk soil25,26, while the endosphere represents a nutrient-rich but immune-surveilled niche requiring specific colonization traits27,28. Understanding how this compartmentalization interacts with antibiotic pressure requires comprehensive analytical approaches, including community profiling, interaction network analysis, machine learning classification, and functional prediction. While 16 S rRNA-based functional prediction cannot replace direct gene detection, it provides valuable insights into community-level functional potential and has been successfully validated for assessing environmental impacts on agricultural microbiomes29,30. We note that while antibiotic resistance is a key concern motivating this work, our 16 S rRNA-based approach infers resistance-associated functional potential through taxonomic-functional associations rather than direct ARG detection, a limitation we address explicitly in our study design and interpretation.

Given the critical role of plant-associated microbiome in agriculture, understanding the effects of antibiotics on the microbiome is essential for predicting ecosystem stability and managing antibiotic resistance risks. Recent studies have explored antibiotic impacts on soil microbial communities15,31,32, yet the extent to which plant spatial compartmentalization influences microbiome resilience under antibiotic stress remains unclear. Unlike some studies, which typically assessed bulk soil or rhizosphere bacteria communities separately in plant stress33,34, our study simultaneously evaluates the bacteriome responses across bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere compartments to antibiotic exposure. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) assess how antibiotic treatments alter bacterial diversity, composition, and co-occurrence networks; (2) evaluate differences in bacterial communities between bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere compartments; (3) investigate the extent of plant-mediated bacteriome assembly in response to antibiotics; (4) examine the influence of manure amendments on microbial ecology; and (5) determine how antibiotic exposure impacts the predicted functional potential of bacterial communities, focusing on resistance mechanisms, pathogen-related functions, and plant-beneficial activities. Sulfamethoxazole (SMX) and trimethoprim (TMP) were selected as model antibiotics based on several criteria: (1) their status as WHO Essential Medicines commonly prescribed globally for human and veterinary infections; (2) frequent detection in agricultural soils receiving manure amendments, with reported environmental concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 15 mg/kg35,36; (3) their distinct but related mechanisms targeting sequential steps in bacterial folate biosynthesis (SMX inhibits dihydropteroate synthase; TMP inhibits dihydrofolate reductase); and (4) their environmental persistence and documented contributions to antibiotic resistance selection in agroecosystems11. The concentration of 3 mg/kg used in this study represents an environmentally relevant exposure level within the range commonly detected in manure-amended soils.

We hypothesized that: (1) plant spatial compartmentalization (bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere) imposes stronger selective pressure on bacterial community structure than antibiotic exposure at environmentally relevant concentrations, as evidenced by greater variance explained by compartment versus treatment in ordination and PERMANOVA analyses; (2) if compartmentalization provides ecological buffering, then core plant-beneficial functions (plant growth promotion, biocontrol) will be maintained across antibiotic treatments despite compositional shifts, with small effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.5) for these functional categories; and (3) antibiotic exposure will selectively enrich taxa with degradation or resistance capabilities, manifesting as increased relative abundance of xenobiotic metabolism pathways and reduced network modularity. These hypotheses are testable against their null alternatives and can be accepted or rejected based on the multivariate statistical framework employed.

Methods

Plant cultivation and experimental design

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) seedlings were grown in three media types: vermiculite, coco-perlite mixture, and 1:1 compost-red soil blend. Seedlings received TriPart Hydro Coco nutrients (General Hydroponics) at increasing concentrations: 0.5 mL/L (pH 6.2, EC 0.5) for root development, 1 mL/L (pH 6.2, EC 1.2) after first true leaf emergence, and 14.4 mL/L Grow + 12.8 mL/L Micro + 4.8 mL/L Bloom (pH 6.2, EC 1.4) during active growth phase.

A completely randomized design with five treatments and three biological replicates (n = 15, total 45 pots) was employed: T1, sulfamethoxazole (SMX, 3 mg kg⁻¹) with manure and plants; T2, trimethoprim (TMP, 3 mg kg⁻¹) with manure and plants; T3, manure with plants (antibiotic-free control); T4, soil and manure alone (plant-free control); T5, soil alone (negative control). Antibiotic concentrations of 3 mg kg⁻¹ for SMX and TMP were selected based on environmentally relevant concentrations, which are between 0.1 and 10 mg/kg35,36. These concentrations represent realistic exposure scenarios and allow assessment of ecologically relevant microbiome responses. Each pot contained 5 kg of a 1:1 compost-soil mixture and 3 mg of manure, where applicable. Antibiotics were applied as soil amendments before planting, with SMX and TMP dissolved in acetone and thoroughly mixed into the soil in the pots where antibiotic treatments are required. Pots were pre-incubated for 48 h before planting and irrigated daily with 1 L of distilled water. The experiment was terminated after 60 days.

Sample collection

About 10 g of bulk soil was collected from each pot close to the side of the pot and far from the plant roots. For rhizosphere soil (approximately 2–5 g) was obtained by gently shaking roots to remove loosely adhering soil, followed by collection of tightly adhering soil particles, followed by the collection of tightly adhering soil particles within 1–2 mm of root surfaces. Leaf samples underwent surface sterilization (70% ethanol for 3 min, 10% calcium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by three sterile water rinses) to remove epiphytic microorganisms. All samples were collected in sterile bags, transported at 4 °C, and stored at -80 °C until processing.

DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Plant tissue DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Plant Pro Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 80 mg tissue was homogenized with 500 µL Solution CD1 using TissueLyser III (24 Hz, 2 × 30 min), processed through lysate clarification steps, and eluted in 80 µL Buffer EB. Soil DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen). Up to 250 mg of soil was homogenized with 800 µL Solution CD1 using TissueLyser III (24 Hz, 2 × 30 min), processed through the manufacturer’s protocol, and eluted in 80 µL Buffer C6. The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’)37 in 25 µL reactions containing 1 µL each primer, 12.5 µL MasterMix, 8.5 µL nuclease-free water, and 2 µL template DNA. PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplification was confirmed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide staining.

Libraries were prepared using the Native Barcode Expansion Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 400 ng PCR amplicons underwent end-repair, native barcoding, and adapter ligation. Samples were cleaned using AMPure XP beads and quantified using Qubit fluorometry. Full-length 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) due to its capability to produce long-read sequences covering the entire 16 S gene, improving taxonomic resolution. Sequencing was performed on R9.4.1 flow cells using MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) for 72 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequence data analysis

Raw sequenced read files were analyzed using NanoASV38. NanoASV is a conda environment, snakemake-based workflow that processes full-length SSU rRNA (16 S/18S) amplicons acquired with ONT. Sequences were grouped based on their barcode identifiers and subsequently filtered using Chopper v0.9.039 with specific parameters “- -quality 10, - -minlength 1400, - -maxlength 1700” to remove low-quality reads. Porechop v0.2.4 (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop) was used to identify and remove sequencing adapters from the filtered sequences. The filtered fastq reads were subsampled to 50,000 sequences per barcode and then aligned with minimap2 v2.2840 against the SILVA138.2 database41. Unmapped reads, secondary alignments, and supplementary alignments were removed using samtools v1.2142 to only keep correctly mapped reads. NanoASV uses a vsearch-based clustering step with vsearch v2.29.143 specifically for unassigned sequences. To account for the high error rate associated with ONT data, vsearch was set to use a low clustering similarity threshold “- -id 0.7 “. Unknown clusters with a total abundance lower than 5 were excluded from the final results. Resulting sequences were pooled and aligned with MAFFT v7.52544. FastTree v2.1.1145 was used to construct a phylogenetic tree. The phyloseq object was created from assigned and unknown clusters, the abundance table, the taxonomy table, and the phylogenetic tree. Sequences belonging to Eukaryota, Chloroplast, and Mitochondria were removed from the phyloseq object. We acknowledge the absence of extraction blanks, PCR blanks, and mock communities, which prevents definitive exclusion of contamination artifacts, particularly in low-biomass endosphere samples; accordingly, we could not apply prevalence/frequency-based decontamination (e.g., decontam). In the absence of sequenced negatives, we took the following precautions: (1) DNA extractions were performed under sterile conditions in a dedicated clean workspace46,47 with UV-sterilized surfaces; (2) host organellar sequences (Chloroplast, Mitochondria) were computationally removed; (3) we report compartment-specific sequencing depths (bulk soil median ~ 8,000 reads; rhizosphere ~ 9,000 reads; endosphere ~ 300 reads) to contextualize biomass differences; and (4) we restrict primary conclusions to higher-biomass bulk soil and rhizosphere samples and present all endosphere findings as exploratory observations requiring independent validation.

Functional analysis

FAPROTAX database48 with custom functional mappings (Supplementary Table S1). FAPROTAX database coverage is limited for environmental bacteria, and functional predictions carry inherent uncertainty when based solely on taxonomic identity. Therefore, predictions represent potential functional capabilities rather than active gene expression or confirmed functional traits. Custom mappings were developed to specifically target agriculturally relevant functions, including antibiotic production, antibiotic degradation, plant growth promotion, biocontrol, and resistance-associated bacterial taxa. To address the specific objectives of this study, we developed functional mappings for key bacterial genera known to be associated with antibiotic resistance mechanisms and human pathogenic potential. These mappings were based on an extensive literature review and established genomic databases linking specific taxa (e.g., Enterobacter, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas) to functional capabilities, including antibiotic resistance potential, human pathogen-associated functions (gastroenteritis, pneumonia, opportunistic pathogens), and plant-beneficial functions. The custom mappings were integrated with existing FAPROTAX annotations using a many-to-many relationship structure, allowing individual taxa to be associated with multiple functional categories. Resistance potential was inferred through taxonomic-functional associations rather than direct gene detection. This approach identifies bacterial taxa with established associations to antibiotic resistance mechanisms based on genomic and functional databases. We emphasize that this methodology provides estimates of community-level resistance potential and taxonomic indicators of selective pressure rather than quantitative resistance gene abundance. Taxa classified as having “antibiotic resistance potential” included genera with well-documented resistance capabilities in the literature, such as members of Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and other taxa known to harbor multiple resistance mechanisms. Similarly, human pathogen potential was assessed based on taxonomic associations with known pathogenic capabilities. Categories included general human pathogens, gastroenteritis-associated pathogens, pneumonia-associated pathogens, and opportunistic pathogens. This approach identifies shifts in community composition toward taxa with documented pathogenic potential rather than detecting specific virulence factors. Functional abundance was calculated by summing the relative abundances of all ASVs assigned to each functional category. To account for substantial variation in sequencing depth across compartments (particularly low-biomass endosphere samples), relative abundance transformations were applied within samples rather than rarefaction, preserving all samples and maintaining experimental design integrity. Raw functional counts were converted to relative abundances within each sample to enable cross-sample comparisons while accounting for sequencing depth differences to reduce technical noise while preserving biological signal.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.4.1, utilizing packages like phyloseq49 and vegan50 for core analysis, while dplyr and ggplot251 were used for data processing and visualization. We analyzed the sequencing depth across compartments and noted considerable differences among bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endosphere samples. Following recommendations by McMurdie and Holmes52 for handling uneven sequencing depth, relative abundance transformations were applied within samples rather than rarefaction, preserving all samples and maintaining experimental design integrity. Sequencing depth analysis revealed substantial variation across compartments, with endosphere samples exhibiting consistently lower read counts (median ~ 300 reads) compared to bulk soil (~ 8,000 reads) and rhizosphere (~ 9,000 reads) samples (Supplementary Figs. S1A and S1C). This bimodal distribution in sequencing depth (Supplementary Fig. S1B) reflects inherent differences in microbial biomass between plant-internal and soil-associated environments. Despite these differences, rarefaction curves approached asymptotes for most samples (Supplementary Fig. S1D), indicating adequate sequencing depth for diversity estimation across compartments.

Taxonomic diversity and community analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test were utilized to evaluate whether the phyloseq item meets the assumptions for ANOVA before proceeding with further analysis. Statistical significance was evaluated using ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests from the stats package, followed by Tukey HSD and Dunn’s tests for post-hoc comparisons, respectively. The phyloseq package49 was employed to compute observed ASV richness, Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, and the Chao1 estimate.

Communities were compared using Bray-Curtis and Jaccard dissimilarities and UniFrac/weighted UniFrac53–56; ordination used PCoA and dbRDA/capscale on Hellinger-transformed abundances57, and dbRDA significance was assessed using 9,999 permutations, and adjusted R² (R²adj) values were reported while PERMANOVA and PERMDISP used 999 permutations. Group differences were tested with PERMANOVA and homogeneity of multivariate dispersion with PERMDISP, and location effects interpreted alongside dispersion tests58,59. Variance partitioning used vegan’s varpart. Community-assembly processes were inferred with the βNTI/RCbray​ two-step framework: ∣βNTI∣>2 indicates variable/homogeneous selection; otherwise RCbray​>0.95 or <–0.95 indicates dispersal limitation or homogenizing dispersal; remaining cases are undominated following Stegen, et al.60, Stegen, et al.61, and the quantitative process estimates framework of Ning, et al.62.

Core microbiome membership was defined using prevalence-abundance thresholds (≥ 50% prevalence within a group and ≥ 0.01% relative abundance), following established protocols for plant root microbiome studies63. To assess robustness, we conducted sensitivity analyses using more conservative thresholds (0.1% and 1% relative abundance). Core membership patterns remained qualitatively consistent across thresholds (Supplementary Fig. S2). The relatively permissive 0.01% threshold was selected to capture ecologically relevant low-abundance taxa that may contribute to community function despite their rarity, particularly in the species-poor endosphere compartment where stringent thresholds would eliminate most taxa from consideration.

Network analysis

To infer putative taxon-taxon associations while accounting for compositionality, we constructed correlation-based co-occurrence networks using a SparCC framework64. Within each treatment network, taxa were filtered to retain features present in ≥ 3% of samples; correlations were then estimated on log-transformed relative abundances with small-value replacement for zeros, and edges were retained at |r| ≥ 0.2 with nominal P ≤ 0.25 to yield sparse, interpretable graphs. As an internal validation, we repeated network inference using Pearson correlations on centered log-ratio (CLR) transformed data, retaining edges at |r| ≥ 0.3 and P ≤ 0.10, and confirmed that the main qualitative treatment-level patterns were consistent. In addition, network robustness was assessed by systematically varying the correlation strength cutoff (|r| from 0.1 to 0.5) and statistical significance threshold (p from 0.01 to 0.3) used to filter SparCC-derived pairwise associations, yielding 25 parameter combinations per treatment. Treatment ranking consistency across all combinations was quantified using pairwise Spearman rank correlations of network density values (Supplementary Fig. S3). Networks were visualized using igraph65; edge color denotes association sign (positive/negative) and edge width scales with |r|. Hub taxa were defined as the top 5% by composite centrality (mean of normalized degree, betweenness, and closeness). Highly connected taxa in microbial co-occurrence networks are considered ecologically important because their removal disproportionately reduces network connectivity and destabilizes community structure, and their central positions reflect potential roles as mediators of community-wide responses to environmental perturbation66,67.

Differential abundance and classification analysis

Differential abundance analysis was performed using ANCOM-BC68 and ALDEx2, and DESeq269. Correction for false discovery rate (FDR) was applied (α < 0.05).

Functional diversity and statistical testing

Functional diversity indices were calculated using the vegan package, including Shannon diversity (H’), Simpson diversity (1-D), functional richness (number of functions present), and evenness (J’ = H’/log(S)). Treatment and compartment effects on both taxonomic and functional data were evaluated using non-parametric approaches due to non-normal distributions typical of microbiome data. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to assess overall group differences, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for post-hoc comparisons when significant effects were detected.

All p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with α < 0.05 considered significant. Beyond statistical significance, biological relevance was assessed using Cohen’s d effect sizes to quantify the magnitude of treatment effects relative to the control (T3). Effect sizes were calculated by comparing mean abundances between each treatment and the control, with pooled standard deviations used as the standardization factor. Effect sizes were classified as negligible (|d| < 0.2), small (0.2 ≤ |d| < 0.5), medium (0.5 ≤ |d| < 0.8), or large (|d| ≥ 0.8) following conventional interpretations.

Exploratory functional profiling

To explore potential functional implications of antibiotic exposure, the relative abundances of FAPROTAX-predicted functions related to antibiotic resistance potential and human pathogen-associated capabilities were extracted and compared descriptively across treatments and compartments. These predictions are based on taxonomic associations with cultured relatives and do not represent confirmed gene content or functional activity in the sampled communities48. Putative functional profiles were visualized using clustered heatmaps with hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and complete linkage. Log₁₀-transformed relative abundances were used to improve visualization of patterns across the full range of putative functional abundances.

Results

Bacterial diversity and community composition in the rhizosphere, endosphere, and bulk soil in response to antibiotic treatment

From the analysis, bacterial alpha diversity is depleted in both treatments T4 (No plant) and T5 (Soil only), with treatment T3 (Control) showing the highest diversity, followed by T2 (TMP) and T1(SMX) (Fig. 1A). However, no statistically significant differences were found among all treatments (p = 0.962) (Supplementary Table S2). In the compartments, bacterial alpha-diversity is understandably depleted in the endosphere compared to both rhizosphere and bulk soil; however, the impact of the compartments on the diversity was still significant (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S2). Interaction effect between the treatments and the compartments on the alpha diversity based on the Chao1 index showed statistical significance (p = 0.007) with higher diversity being observed in both rhizosphere and bulk soil compartments compared to the endosphere (Fig. 1C). Specifically, interaction effect between T1 and each compartment showed high alpha diversity in both bulk soil and the endosphere but low in the rhizosphere, and T2 and T3 showed high diversity in bulk soil and the rhizosphere but low in the endosphere.

However, when grouped by treatment, T3, which has a manured plant but no antibiotic treatment, was generally enriched in alpha diversity than T1, which is treated with SMX, and T2, which is treated with TMP (Fig. 1A). T4, which has only manure but no antibiotics treatment and no plants, has similar diversity to T5, which is just soil (Fig. 1A). In addition, bacterial diversity, including the observed species richness, Shannon’s diversity index, and Simpson’s diversity index, shows similar patterns to the Chao1 index for treatments, compartments, and their interaction effects (Supplementary Figs. S4-S6, Supplementary Table S2).

On Bray-Curtis, communities differed mainly by compartment (R² = 0.189, P = 0.001) with a smaller treatment effect (R² = 0.145, P = 0.116) (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations) representing a weak effect. Across Jaccard, UniFrac and weighted UniFrac, compartment explained R² = 0.133–0.174 (P = 0.001), whereas treatment explained R² = 0.124–0.145 (P = 0.020–0.299) (Supplementary Table S3), both metrics showing weak effect. PERMDISP indicated heterogeneity of dispersion for compartments (P = 0.001–0.008) and treatments (P = 0.001–0.016) in at least one distance, so centroid shifts are interpreted cautiously. Constrained ordination (dbRDA on Hellinger-Bray) yielded P = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ with adj. R² = 0.162 (Supplementary Table S3), showing a weak but statistically significant effect, and consistent with strong compartmental habitat filtering. Null-model partitioning of pairwise turnover (βNTI + RC_bray) indicated dispersal limitation, drift/undominated and homogenizing dispersal each contributed ~ 20% of cases, consistent with mixed stochastic-dispersal dynamics under the applied antibiotic levels. Other beta diversity metrics show similar pattern to that observed in the Bray-Curtis metric (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The bacterial classes Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacilli were enriched in all treatments in the rhizosphere, endosphere, and bulk soil compartments (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S4). Specifically, for T1(SMX) treatment, Alphaproteobacteria (bulk soil), Gammaproteobacteria (endosphere), and Alphaproteobacteria (rhizosphere) were the most abundant with relative abundance percentages of 33.4%, 37.7%, and 39%, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). For T2 (TMP) treatment, Alphaproteobacteria (bulk soil), Gammaproteobacteria (endosphere), and Alphaproteobacteria (rhizosphere) were the most abundant, with percentages of 28.9%, 29.1%, and 25.8%, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). However, in the endosphere compartment of the T3 (control) treatment, Gammaproteobacteria abundance was more notable, having 82.9% of the total class abundance (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S4). The relative abundance of Microbacterium immunditiarum was much richer for all treatments in the bulk soil (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S5). A similar pattern was observed in the rhizosphere for T2 (TMP) and T3 (control), with the same species being more dominant; however, for T1 (SMX), Acidocella aminolytica was more dominant with an abundance of 4.1% (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S5).

Most core taxa show similar abundance patterns across all treatments (Fig. 2C), while Microbacterium shows the highest relative abundance (~ 0.02) and is most prominent in the T5 treatment. However, Bacillus, Sphingopyxis, Streptomyces, and Croceibacterium are the core taxa across all treatments. The core microbiome appears largely resilient to antibiotic treatments T1 (SMX) and T2 (TMP) (Fig. 2C).

When looking at the number of species in the two main treatments, T1 (SMX) and T2 (TMP) (Fig. 2D and E), T1 had communities that were very compartmentalized and had a lot of one species. Microbacterium immunditiarium made up about 85% of the bulk soil, while Dyella sp. ECB15 made up about 65% of the rhizosphere (Fig. 2D). The T2 treatment had communities that were more balanced and varied in all areas. In bulk soil, the relative abundance of Microbacterium immunditiarium dropped by about 35% compared to T1 (Fig. 2D).

Using a prevalence-based definition (≥ 50% of samples within a group and ≥ 0.01% per-sample abundance), we showed clear compartment- and treatment-specific cores (Fig. 3). To characterize treatment-specific restructuring of microbial association patterns while accounting for compositionality, we constructed correlation-based co-occurrence networks using the SparCC framework on log-transformed relative abundances, retaining edges at |r| ≥ 0.2 with P ≤ 0.25. Networks are shown with node size proportional to degree centrality, and hub taxa highlighted as the top 5% by composite centrality (mean of normalized degree, betweenness, and closeness). Edges represent pairwise correlations estimated by SparCC, with color indicating association sign (positive/negative) and thickness proportional to absolute association strength (Fig. 4A-C).

Antibiotic-specific network reorganization was evident as SMX treatment produced networks dominated by Chryseolinea, Lysobacter, and Cellvibrio as central hubs (Fig. 4A), while TMP exposure resulted in Bacillus, Microbacterium, Pseudomonas, and Sphingopyxis emerging as key network connectors (Fig. 4B). The untreated control displayed increased modularity with Chryseolinea and several unclassified lineages serving as more distributed hubs (Fig. 4C).

The robustness of network topology patterns was confirmed through sensitivity analysis across 25 combinations of correlation strength (|r| = 0.1–0.5) and statistical significance (p = 0.01–0.3) thresholds. While absolute network metrics varied with threshold stringency (e.g., edge counts ranged from ~ 6.2 to ~ 18.7 million), treatment-level rankings for network density remained highly consistent (mean Spearman ρ = 0.833). Functional diversity patterns across plant compartments

Functional prediction analysis revealed pronounced compartment-driven patterns in microbial community functional diversity (Fig. 7A). Shannon and observed richness diversity metrics demonstrated significant variation across plant compartments, with the most striking differences observed between endosphere and bulk soil/rhizosphere communities. Shannon diversity showed the strongest compartment effect (P = 0.0017), with endosphere samples exhibiting substantially lower functional diversity (median ~ 4.7) compared to both bulk soil (median ~ 6.2) and rhizosphere (median ~ 5.8) environments (Fig. 7A). This pattern was consistently observed across functional richness (P = 0.0039), where endosphere communities harbored approximately 300–400 fewer functional categories than bulk soil communities, and evenness metrics (P = 0.016), indicating that endosphere functional communities are not only less diverse but also more dominated by specific functional groups, although not significant.

The rhizosphere consistently displayed intermediate functional diversity values between bulk soil and endosphere across all measured indices (Fig. 7A). Simpson diversity, while showing a less pronounced and non-significant pattern (P = 0.08), reinforced the hierarchical organization of functional diversity from bulk soil (highest) through rhizosphere (intermediate) to endosphere (lowest). Multi-metric assessment of functional diversity confirmed this hierarchical organization from bulk soil through rhizosphere to endosphere (Supplementary Fig. S9D).

Treatment and compartment effects on key functional categories

Analysis of specific functional categories revealed complex interactions between treatment and compartment effects, with different functions showing distinct response patterns (Fig. 7B). Antibiotic degradation functions demonstrated non-significant treatment-dependent variation (P = 0.102 for treatment effects), with both SMX and TMP treatments although they showed enhanced degradation potential compared to controls across all compartments. This enhancement was most pronounced in bulk soil samples (Fig. 7B). The compartment-specific response patterns (P = 0.066) indicated that while all compartments showed some capacity for antibiotic degradation, the magnitude of response to antibiotic pressure was not significant across plant-associated environments.

Antibiotic production functions exhibited strong but non-significant compartment specificity (P = 0.17) with bulk soil communities consistently showing the highest production potential across all treatments (Fig. 7B). Treatment effects on antibiotic production were evident but non-significant (P = 0.95).

Plant growth promotion functions showed distinct compartment-dependent patterns (P = 0.22) with rhizosphere communities exhibiting the highest growth promotion potential across all treatments (Fig. 7B). But treatment effects on plant growth promotion were moderate and non-significant (P = 0.72), suggesting that while antibiotic treatments may influence the abundance of growth-promoting bacteria, the overall functional capacity is maintained across treatments. Biocontrol functions followed similar non-significant patterns (P = 0.066 for compartment effects). Detailed functional profiling across all categories confirmed these compartment-driven patterns while revealing treatment-specific metabolic enrichments (Supplementary Figs. S9A-C). The consistency of these patterns across both abundance-based and presence/absence-based analyses indicates robust functional restructuring rather than simple abundance shifts (Supplementary Figs. S9B-C).

Organic compound degradation capabilities demonstrated significant treatment effects (P = 0.042) with enhanced degradation potential observed in antibiotic-treated samples, particularly in bulk soil environments (Fig. 7B). Compartment effects were however not significant (P = 0.424), with bulk soil showing the highest degradation potential, followed by rhizosphere and then endosphere communities. In addition, post-hoc pairwise comparisons, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction, showed that no individual treatment pairs differed significantly for any functional category (Supplementary Table S7), indicating that the significant overall treatment effect observed for organic compound degradation (P = 0.042) reflects a diffuse pattern across multiple treatments rather than a discrete shift driven by a single antibiotic exposure.

Effect size quantification of biological significance

Large effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.8) were predominantly observed for antibiotic production and organic compound degradation functions in both SMX and TMP treatments compared to the plant-manure control (Fig. 8A) while medium effect sizes (0.5 ≤ d < 0.8) were observed for biocontrol and secondary metabolite biosynthesis functions, indicating substantial but moderate impacts on these plant-beneficial capabilities (Fig. 8A). The robustness of these functional patterns was validated through multiple analytical approaches (Supplementary Fig. S9), with predicted pathogen potential analysis showing sporadic rather than systematic enrichment across treatments (Supplementary Fig. S9G).

Functional profiling and community clustering

Hierarchical clustering analysis of comprehensive functional profiles revealed clear treatment-specific signatures in the community functional architecture (Fig. 8B). SMX and TMP treatments formed distinct clusters separate from all control treatments. The functional heatmap revealed complex patterns of function-specific responses across the treatment-compartment matrix (Fig. 8B). Plant-associated samples (treatments containing plants) consistently showed enrichment of plant growth promotion and biocontrol functions regardless of antibiotic treatment, indicating that plant-microbe functional relationships are maintained even under antibiotic stress. However, the intensity of these plant-beneficial functions was modulated by antibiotic treatment, with treated samples showing altered expression patterns. The clustering also revealed that compartment-specific functional signatures were maintained across treatments, with endosphere samples clustering together regardless of treatment. Comprehensive pathway analysis confirmed that while antibiotic treatments altered the relative abundance of specific functional groups, core ecosystem services, including nitrogen cycling, phosphate solubilization, and plant hormone production, maintained consistent presence across treatments (Supplementary Fig. S9H). This functional resilience was particularly evident in the maintenance of plant-beneficial functions despite significant enrichment of degradation pathways in antibiotic-treated samples (Supplementary Figs. S9E-F).

Discussion

Our findings clearly establish spatial compartmentalization as the dominant influence shaping plant bacteriome structure and functionality under antibiotic stress. Compartmentalization appears to buffer plant-associated bacterial communities from antibiotic-induced disruptions by selectively preserving critical functions such as plant growth promotion and biocontrol. This ecological insight underscores the importance of compartment-specific microbiome management strategies in agricultural systems to maintain beneficial microbiome functions under antibiotic exposure. Soil microbes can be important in plant antibiotic stress tolerance70. Improving our understanding of plant-associated community variations during exposure to antibiotic stress can help improve plants’ resilience through targeted microbiome manipulation. This study aimed to understand bacterial community shifts during exposure to two antibiotics, SMX and TMP, in the lettuce plant. We focused on the subset of the bacterial community that was likely to be active in the presence of antibiotic treatment by comparing unplanted and planted rhizosphere, endosphere, and bulk soils of antibiotic-treated plants to non-treated plants. These comparisons were critical to identify plant-mediated responses to antibiotic treatment, bacterial community variation in different plant compartments in the presence of antibiotic treatment, which otherwise can be challenging to explore, as antibiotic stress will alter soil microbial communities and make many taxa dormant71–73. Importantly, because low-biomass samples are susceptible to reagent and cross-sample contamination and we did not include sequenced negatives, we refrain from strong conclusions for the endosphere and therefore base our primary interpretations on bulk soil and rhizosphere.

While treatments T1 (SMX) and T2 (TMP) differ from T3 (control) solely in antibiotic application, with soil type, manure amendment, plant presence, and all abiotic greenhouse conditions (temperature, humidity, irrigation) held constant, the correlative nature of this study precludes definitive causal attribution of observed community differences to antibiotic exposure alone. Unmeasured variables such as fine-scale spatial heterogeneity within the greenhouse or stochastic variation among biological replicates may contribute to the observed patterns. Accordingly, the treatment-level differences reported here should be interpreted as associations consistent with antibiotic-mediated selective pressure rather than confirmed causal effects.

Dominance of spatial compartmentalization on microbial community diversity and composition

Our findings demonstrate that plant compartmentalization is the principal factor affecting bacterial community composition, consistent with previous studies that emphasize notable selection pressures within different compartments74,75. This pattern is consistently observed across diversity metrics, differential abundance analysis, and machine learning classification findings in this study. The clear distinction between bulk soil and endosphere communities highlights the plant’s selective recruitment and filtration mechanisms, favoring certain bacterial species capable of endophytic colonization while excluding others. Such a mechanism has been reported in various studies76,77. The findings regarding taxonomic composition suggest that the endosphere harbors a more specialized community compared to the diverse microbiome of bulk soil, thereby supporting the concept of the endosphere as a distinct ecological niche characterized by specific selective pressures, including biotic and abiotic stresses78,79. Distinct plant compartments, such as the rhizosphere and endosphere, provide unique physicochemical conditions (e.g., pH and nutrient availability) that drive microbial variations80,81. The stability of these compartments influences microbial diversity, consistent with previous studies82,83. The internal heterogeneity between compartments (e.g., rhizosphere vs. endosphere) has a greater impact on microbial composition. This pattern aligns with the established understanding of plant-microbe interactions, in which host plants actively shape their microbial communities via various mechanisms, including immunological responses and metabolite production84. The hierarchical organization from bulk soil through rhizosphere to the endosphere reflects increasingly selective plant-mediated recruitment, with the counterintuitive finding that plant presence (T3) enhanced overall diversity compared to plant-free controls (T4, T5), suggesting that plant-microbe interactions promote rather than constrain community complexity.

The presence of a stable soil-associated core across treatments suggests ecological resilience and provides candidate bioindicators for monitoring community state and data quality across experiments and sites. Treatment-linked shifts within this conserved set imply that interventions modulate the relative abundance of shared core members rather than whole replacement, pointing to dose/condition-responsive taxa that could be followed longitudinally. The absence of a 50%-prevalence core in the endosphere highlights strong host selection; practically, it cautions against extrapolating “universal” plant-internal cores and suggests that endosphere targets may be genotype-, time-, or tissue-specific, requiring either a lower prevalence threshold or higher replication to detect consistent members. Genera repeatedly entering the core with appreciable abundance (Microbacterium, Chryseolinea, Dyella, Devosia, Cellvibrio) can be prioritized candidates for isolation and functional assays (e.g., carbon turnover, nutrient mobilization, or plant-interaction traits) and could inform inoculant design or management strategies that aim to shift communities without destabilizing the broader soil microbiome. In addition, the “core-band” view separates ubiquity (prevalence) from dominance (abundance) (Fig. 3), clarifying why some highly abundant taxa are not core (sporadic blooms) and why some core members are modest in abundance but persistent.

It is important to note that while treatments T1 and T2 differ from T3 in antibiotic application, the greenhouse experimental design controlled for abiotic variables (temperature, humidity, soil moisture) across all treatments. Critically, all plant-related treatments (T1, T2, and T3) share the same soil type, manure amendment, and plant presence, ensuring that antibiotic application is the only variable differing between antibiotic treatments and their direct control. Variance partitioning confirmed that spatial compartment explained more variation in bacteriome structure than treatment (Supplementary Table S3), further supporting that antibiotic effects are secondary to plant compartment effects.

Antibiotic-induced selective pressures on microbiome structure and networks

The predominance of positive associations across all networks suggests that positive co-occurrence patterns and potential niche overlap or shared environmental preferences dominate competitive exclusion in the plant microbiome, consistent with recent findings that positive interactions support high microbial diversity in plant-associated communities67,85. However, antibiotic exposure fundamentally altered network architecture: while the untreated control exhibited a modular, distributed structure characteristic of stable plant microbiomes, both SMX and TMP treatments collapsed this organization into more centralized topologies dependent on fewer hub taxa. This shift represents a critical transition from resilient, compartmentalized communities toward fragile networks vulnerable to cascading failures. The emergence of antibiotic-resistant genera like Lysobacter, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus as central nodes reflects selection for metabolically versatile taxa capable of exploiting resources released by antibiotic-sensitive organisms, while simultaneously providing resistance-conferring metabolites to associated community members. Notably, SMX and TMP selected for distinct hub taxa despite both being folate pathway inhibitors, suggesting that even chemically related antibiotics can drive divergent community assembly patterns. These findings reveal that sub-lethal antibiotic concentrations act as powerful selective forces that restructure the foundational interaction networks governing plant microbiome function, with potentially severe consequences for agricultural sustainability as centralized microbial networks may compromise both pathogen resistance and plant-growth promotion while facilitating the spread of antibiotic resistance genes through highly connected hub organisms.

The observed shift from modular to centralized network architecture under antibiotic exposure carries important ecological implications for community resilience. Modular networks, characterized by densely connected subgroups with sparse inter-module connections, are generally considered more stable because perturbations remain contained within modules66. The transition toward centralized topology in antibiotic-treated soils, with increased reliance on hub taxa, may render communities more vulnerable to secondary disturbances especially if hub taxa are themselves affected by environmental stressors. The emergence of antibiotic-associated hub taxa (e.g., Lysobacter in SMX treatment, Bacillus in TMP treatment) suggests that antibiotic exposure restructures ecological interaction networks around taxa with resistance or degradation capabilities, potentially creating keystone vulnerabilities in agricultural microbiomes.

It is important to note that correlation-based co-occurrence networks represent statistical associations that may arise from shared environmental preferences, similar physiological requirements, or indirect ecological interactions, rather than direct positive associations. Experimental validation would be required to confirm the ecological nature of these associations. Nevertheless, the consistent shift from modular to centralized network architectures under antibiotic stress indicates fundamental restructuring of community organization with potential implications for ecosystem resilience. In addition, positive co-occurrences in correlation-based networks may arise from shared environmental preferences, similar niche requirements, or indirect associations mediated by unmeasured factors, rather than direct mutualistic interactions. Accordingly, these patterns represent statistical associations that generate testable hypotheses for experimental validation of proposed ecological mechanisms, rather than confirmed biological interactions.

An important methodological consideration for correlation-based network inference is the sensitivity of network topology to the choice of filtering thresholds. Our sensitivity analysis demonstrated that while the absolute number of edges and network density expectedly increased with more permissive thresholds, the relative differences between treatments were preserved. The statistical significance threshold (p-value cutoff) exerted a stronger influence on network size than the correlation strength cutoff, reflecting the iterative sparsification inherent to the SparCC algorithm, which preferentially retains stronger associations during the estimation process. Crucially, T2 (TMP) consistently produced the least dense, most modular network regardless of threshold choice (density rank 3.0 across all 25 parameter combinations), reinforcing the conclusion that trimethoprim exerted a more disruptive effect on microbial co-occurrence structure than sulfamethoxazole. These findings support the reliability of the network patterns reported in Fig. 4 and suggest that the ecological interpretations drawn from network topology are robust to reasonable methodological variation.

Functional resilience and ecological implications of antibiotic exposure

Functional analysis revealed that while antibiotic treatments created statistically significant shifts in predicted community capabilities (Supplementary Fig. S9A), effect size analysis demonstrated that most changes were biologically negligible, with large effects (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.8) observed only for core metabolic functions like organic compound degradation. Importantly, pathogen-associated and resistance-related functions showed small effect sizes despite statistical significance, indicating that the predicted human health risks may be less consequential than taxonomic shifts alone would suggest. The maintenance of plant-beneficial functions (growth promotion, biocontrol) across treatments, combined with enhanced antibiotic degradation capabilities, suggests that plant-associated communities possess functional resilience mechanisms that buffer against antibiotic disruption while selectively adapting metabolic capabilities. This resilience was evident across multiple functional categories, with stable plant-beneficial capabilities maintained across treatments (Supplementary Fig. S9H). Microbial diversity promotes ecosystem functioning86,87, and high microbial richness is important for ecosystem functioning and health88. However, in our study, we find that greater bacterial diversity and/or richness do not always imply beneficial outcomes, as perturbations cause prevalence in potentially undesired taxa (e.g., pathogens). Therefore, it is important to accurately evaluate both taxonomic and functional biodiversity for an in-depth understanding of the impacts of the presence of antibiotics on the structure and functions of soil and plant microbial communities. Taxonomic diversity alone might lead to a false assumption that increased biodiversity implies benefits in terms of ecosystem functioning. In particular, we highlight that, besides the taxonomic monitoring of soil microbes, a scheme for controlling functional groups could help conceive actions targeting soil management and potentially creating a safe environment for humans. However, any such scheme emanating from FAPROTAX-based approach would first require validation through direct gene-based approaches. The compartment-specific distribution of functional capabilities, with the rhizosphere showing the highest functional diversity and pathogenic potential, underscores the critical role of this interface in mediating both beneficial plant-microbe interactions and potentially harmful pathogen accumulation. These functional shifts, while less pronounced than the taxonomic compartmentalization effects we observed, reveal that antibiotic pressure may have more subtle but potentially more consequential impacts on ecosystem services and human health risks than compositional changes alone would suggest, highlighting the importance of functional assessments in evaluating the environmental and public health implications of agricultural antibiotic use. Albeit with its own limitations, the systematic assessment of potential microbial functional groups would, in addition, allow a quick screening of potential microbial functions.

Our findings have important implications for achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), and SDG 15 (Life on Land), by demonstrating how spatial compartmentalization within agricultural microbiomes can buffer ecosystem functionality against antibiotic stress. This compartment-driven resilience-maintained plant growth promotion and biocontrol potential even when network complexity and connectivity were reduced under antibiotic pressure. From an applied perspective, these results highlight opportunities for strategic manipulation of compartment-specific microbiomes through microbial inoculants, organic amendments, or precision soil-plant management to enhance resilience to pharmaceutical stress without compromising crop yield. The selective enrichment of xenobiotic degradation pathways suggests potential for microbiome-assisted bioremediation, reducing environmental antibiotic persistence while sustaining ecosystem services. The absence of systematic amplification of pathogen-associated or resistance-related functions under antibiotic exposure provides preliminary descriptive evidence relevant to agricultural use scenarios, though direct gene-based confirmation is needed before drawing risk-related conclusions. But the detection of resistant taxa in plant interiors underscores the need for multi-omics surveillance and field-scale trials. By integrating structure-function analyses with a One Health framework, this work advances microbiome-based approaches for sustainable agriculture, ecosystem stability, and public health protection.

Study limitations and future research directions

Because no negative or mock controls were sequenced, low-level reagent or cross-sample contamination, particularly in endosphere samples, cannot be definitively excluded; these results should be interpreted cautiously, and future work should include sequenced blanks and statistical decontamination consistent with low-biomass best practices46,47. The functional analysis approach provides insights into microbial community functional potential based on taxonomic composition rather than direct measurement of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), virulence factors, or metabolic pathways. Categories such as antibiotic resistance associations and pathogen associations reflect the presence of taxa whose cultured relatives possess these traits in the literature but are not confirmed gene content in our specific samples. Horizontal gene transfer, gene loss, and strain-level variation mean that taxonomic identity is an imperfect proxy for functional capacity89,90. While this method has been validated in previous studies and provides valuable comparative information, future studies should consider direct functional gene analysis (e.g., qPCR, metagenomics) to validate these predictions. The approach is particularly appropriate for identifying relative changes in functional potential between treatments and for preliminary risk assessment in agricultural systems. Beyond the functional prediction constraints mentioned, this greenhouse study may not fully represent field conditions where factors such as weather, soil heterogeneity, and agricultural practices create additional complexity. The 60-day exposure period, while sufficient to detect community shifts, may not capture long-term adaptation or seasonal dynamics relevant to agricultural systems. Our analysis was limited to two antibiotics and a single soil type and crop species, potentially limiting generalizability across diverse agricultural contexts. In addition, 16 S rRNA sequencing cannot distinguish metabolically active from dormant or dead cells, and the substantial differences in sequencing depth between compartments (particularly low-biomass endosphere samples) may affect comparative analyses despite relative abundance normalization. The FAPROTAX database has limited coverage of environmental bacteria, and predictions of human pathogen potential carry substantial uncertainty when based solely on taxonomic identity. The correlative nature of this study precludes causal inferences about antibiotic effects, and network analyses represent statistical associations rather than functional interactions. Effect size interpretations, while providing biological context, apply standard thresholds that may not reflect ecological relevance in microbiome systems. Finally, our human health risk assessments are speculative and require validation through direct pathogen detection and risk modelling studies. We acknowledge that the current design cannot fully disentangle potential manure-antibiotic interaction effects. However, the comparison of T1/T2 against T3 (the manure + plant control) isolates antibiotic-specific effects, while variance partitioning analysis confirmed that compartmentalization explained more variance than treatment effects. Another limitation is the absence of a combined SMX-TMP treatment. These antibiotics are clinically co-administered as co-trimoxazole due to their synergistic activity against bacterial folate biosynthesis. The individual treatments tested here may underestimate the microbiome impacts of real-world co-contamination scenarios. However, testing individual antibiotics allowed isolation of compound-specific selective pressures, revealing antibiotic-specific hub taxa (e.g., Lysobacter centrality in SMX networks versus Bacillus centrality in TMP networks). Future studies should include SMX-TMP combinations to capture synergistic impacts representative of environmental co-contamination.

Our results are important for agriculture management when it comes to antibiotic exposure. The ability of plant spatial compartmentalization to act as a buffer suggests that microbiomes connected to plants in manure-amended systems may be able to handle environmentally relevant antibiotic concentrations without significantly affecting their beneficial functions, as long as they are exposed to levels below those that inhibit them. The observed network simplification due to antibiotic pressure suggests that the community is less resilient, which could put long-term functional stability at risk when there are repeated or ongoing contamination events. The targeted improvement of xenobiotic breakdown pathways in bulk soil that has been treated with antibiotics shows that native microbial communities have natural adaptive skills that could be used in management measures that support natural attenuation. Furthermore, the fact that microbial responses are different in different compartments shows how important it is for tracking and intervention methods to consider spatial differences along the soil-root continuum instead of relying only on bulk soil assessments. These management-relevant insights, while derived from a controlled greenhouse system, provide a framework for designing field-scale studies for evaluating microbiome resilience under realistic agricultural antibiotic contamination regimes.

Conclusion

This study tested three hypotheses regarding the relative influence of plant spatial compartmentalization versus antibiotic exposure on lettuce-associated bacterial communities. Our first hypothesis that compartmentalization would impose stronger selective pressure than antibiotic exposure, was supported. PERMANOVA revealed that compartments explained substantially more community variance than antibiotic treatments, indicating that plant-mediated niche differentiation represents the dominant structuring force even under antibiotic perturbation. Our second hypothesis that compartmentalization provides ecological buffering to maintain core beneficial functions, was partially supported. Core microbiome analysis identified 26 taxa consistently present across treatments, including plant growth-promoting genera (Bacillus, Streptomyces, Lysobacter) whose persistence suggests functional resilience. However, taxonomic shifts toward resistance-associated taxa were detected under antibiotic treatment based on FAPROTAX exploratory predictions, suggesting that the functional landscape may not be entirely buffered from antibiotic pressure. These taxonomy-based inferences require validation through gene-based functional profiling. From our third hypothesis that antibiotic exposure would enrich resistance-associated taxa while reducing network connectivity and shifting topology, network analysis revealed that antibiotic treatments (T1 [SMX], T2 [TMP]) exhibited reduced modularity and altered hub taxa compared to controls, with treatment-specific taxa emerging (Lysobacter under SMX; Bacillus under TMP). Taxa with documented antibiotic degradation and resistance associations showed increased predicted relative abundance under antibiotic exposure based on FAPROTAX assignments.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that sub-inhibitory antibiotic concentrations representative of agricultural soil contamination produce detectable but compartment-modulated effects on plant microbiome structure and co-occurrence networks. The persistence of core plant-beneficial taxa alongside predicted enrichment of resistance-associated organisms highlights the complex trade-offs inherent in antibiotic-contaminated agricultural systems and underscores the need for integrated management strategies within a One Health framework.
