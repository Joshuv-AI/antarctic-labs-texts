Deep zero problems and the HRT conjecture Thanks: Liu is the corresponding author. Thanks: Research is partially supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China, grant numbers 12571136, 12001089, and 12271328.
Yufei Li, Zeguang Liu, Kehe Zhu

Abstract
. We investigate a “deep zero problem” proposed by Hedenmalm. We show that there is a natural connection between Hedenmalm’s problem and the classical HRT conjecture in time-frequency analysis. This connection allows us to show that Hedenmalm’s problem 5.2 in [ 5 ] as well as some of its natural analogs have affirmative answers.

30H20, 42C15, 47B38.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a domain (connected, open, and nonempty set) in the complex
plane
\mathbb{C}
and let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,3,\cdots\}
denote the set of all nonnegative
integers. The classical
identity theorem
states that if
f
is analytic on

\Omega
and if either
f^{(j)}(a)=0
for some point
a\in\Omega
and all

j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
or
f(a_{j})=0
for a sequence
\{a_{j}\}
in
\Omega

that has an accumulation point in
\Omega
, then
f
is identically
0
on

\Omega
.

There are two natural directions in which we can attempt to extend these identity
theorems (or uniqueness theorems). The first concerns the condition that

f(a_{j})=0
for a sequence
\{a_{j}\}
in
\Omega
, where
\{a_{j}\}
does not
necessarily have an accumulation point in
\Omega
. There can be additional
assumptions on
f
and
\{a_{j}\}
which would imply that
f
is identically
0
.
The most prominent example is the following: if
f\in H^{\infty}
, the space of all
bounded analytic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
in
\mathbb{C}
, and if

\sum(1-|a_{j}|)=\infty
, then the condition that
f(a_{j})=0
for all
j
(counting
multiplicity) implies
f=0
. More generally, for any given space
X
of analytic
functions on
\Omega
, we can seek conditions on
\{a_{j}\}
such that for any

f\in X
the condition
f(a_{j})=0
for all
j
(counting multiplicity) implies
f=0
.
This problem of characterizing zero sequences for
X
has only been solved for
a very small number of spaces. The problem remains open for many classical
function spaces, including the Bergman space of
\mathbb{D}
.

The second direction concerns the condition
f^{(j)}(a)=0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
where
a
is some fixed point in
\Omega
. Again, we can require
f

to belong to a certain analytic function space
X
. The following problem has
been studied in the literature. Let
a
and
b
be two points in

\Omega
, let
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{0}
. Find
additional assumptions (on
f
,
\mathcal{E}_{0}
,
a
, and
b
) such that the condition

\begin{cases}f^{(j)}(a)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{0},\\
f^{(j)}(b)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{1},\end{cases}

implies that
f=0
. See
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
8
,
14
]

for example.

More generally, if
d\geq 2
and if

(i)

T_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq d-1
, are bounded linear operators on
X
,

(ii)

\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq d-1
, are disjoint subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(iii)

a_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq d-1
, are points in
\Omega
,

we may ask to find additional assumptions under which the condition

\begin{cases}(T_{0}f)^{(j)}(a_{0})=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{0},\\
(T_{1}f)^{(j)}(a_{1})=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{1},\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
(T_{d-1}f)^{(j)}(a_{d-1})=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{d-1},\end{cases}

implies that
f=0
. Following Hedenmalm
[
5
]
, we will call all
these “deep zero problems”.

Of particular interest is the case when

\mathcal{E}_{0}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{E}_{d-1}=\mathbb{N}_{0}.

For example,

\mathcal{E}_{k}=\{k+dj:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},\quad 0\leq k\leq d-1,

give such a decomposition of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. When
d=2
,
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is the set of all
even integers in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, while
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is the set of all odd integers in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
If
\Omega
is “sufficiently symmetric” and the space
X
is “sufficiently
regular”, we may be able to move points around in
\Omega

(translations for example) and so can adjust the operators involved and
assume that
a_{0}=a_{1}=\cdots=a_{d-1}
.

Hedenmalm’s paper
[
5
]
, as well as ours here, focuses on the case
where
X
is the Fock space
F^{2}
of the complex plane and the operators
T

on
F^{2}
are Weyl unitary operators (weighted translation operators). Recall that

F^{2}
consists of all entire functions
f
on
\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|f(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{G}(z)<+\infty,

where
\mathrm{d}\mu_{G}(z)=e^{-|z|^{2}}\mathrm{d}A(z)
is the Gaussian
measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Here
\mathrm{d}A(z)=\pi^{-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y
,
with
z=x+iy
, is the normalized area measure. For any point
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
the Weyl operator
U_{\alpha}:F^{2}\to F^{2}
is defined by

U_{\alpha}f(z)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}-\overline{\alpha}z}f(z+\alpha).

It is well known and easy to check that
U_{\alpha}
is a unitary operator with

U_{\alpha}^{-1}=U_{\alpha}^{*}=U_{-\alpha}
. For background information
on the Fock space and Weyl unitary operators, see
[
15
]
for example.

One of the results proved in
[
5
]
is the following.

Theorem A
.

Suppose
f\in F^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}
is the set of even integers in
\mathbb{N}_{0}

(or the set of odd integers in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
). If for some
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
we
have
f^{(j)}(0)=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{E}
and

(U_{\beta}f)^{(j)}(0)=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{E}^{c}
, then
f=0
.

Similar results were also proved in
[
12
]
for Hardy, Bergman,
and Dirichlet spaces of the unit disc.

In the same paper
[
5
]
, Hedenmalm also raised the following
question, phrased in terms of the general setup above.

Problem B
.

Let
f\in F^{2}
and let
\mathcal{E}_{k}=\{k+4j:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
for
0\leq k\leq 3
. If there
exists a point
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\begin{cases}f^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{0},\\
(U_{\beta}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{1},\\
(U_{\beta}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{2},\\
(U_{\beta}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{3},\end{cases}

(1)

does it follow that
f=0
?

Note that any entire function
f
on
\mathbb{C}
has a Taylor expansion

f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\,z^{j}.

With the sets
\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq 3
, defined in Problem B, it is easy
to see that the condition
f^{(j)}(0)=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
if and only if

f(z)+f(iz)+f(-z)+f(-iz)=0.

Similarly, the condition
f^{(j)}(0)=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\mathcal{E}_{3}

if and only if
f(iz)=f(z)
. Thus Problem B can be restated in terms of
functional equations as follows.

Problem B
′
.

Let
f\in F^{2}
. If there exists some
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\begin{cases}U_{\beta}f(iz)=U_{\beta}f(z),\\
f(z)+f(iz)+f(-z)+f(-iz)=0,\end{cases}

does it follow that
f=0
?

The purpose of this note is to show that Problem B has a positive answer.
In fact, we will prove the following.

Main Theorem
.

Problem B as well as the corresponding problems for
d=3
and
d=6

all have affirmative answers.

Our approach is to explore a certain connection between deep zero problems
for the Fock space and the so-called HRT conjecture in time-frequency analysis.
Although the HRT conjecture is still open, a known special case will enable us
to prove the theorem above. It is natural for us to conjecture that the analog
of Problem B for any positive integer
d>1
has an affirmative answer.

2.
The HRT Conjecture

The following is a core open problem in time-frequency analysis, which
is called the
Linear Independence Conjecture
or sometimes the

HRT Conjecture
. See
[
6
,
7
]
for example. As usual,
we use
\mathbb{R}
to denote the real number line.

Conjecture C
.

If
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}x)\setminus\{0\}
and
\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}

are distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then the functions

\displaystyle g_{k}(x)=e^{2\pi ib_{k}x}g(x-a_{k}),\quad 1\leq k\leq N,

are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}x)
.

We are actually going to use the Fock space version of the HRT conjecture.
Recall that (see
[
15
,
16
]
for example) the Bargmann
transform is the integral operator defined by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}g)(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-izx+\frac{1}{2}z^{2}-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

It is well known that
\mathbf{B}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R},dx)\rightarrow F^{2}
is a unitary
transformation.

For functions
g
and
g_{k}
in Conjecture C, it is well known (see

[
16
]
for example) and easy to check directly that

(\mathbf{B}g_{k})(z)=e^{i\pi a_{k}b_{k}}(U_{\lambda_{k}}f)(z),

where

f=\mathbf{B}g\quad{\rm and}\quad\lambda_{k}=-2\pi b_{k}-ia_{k}/2

for
1\leq k\leq N
.

Thus the HRT conjecture can be stated in terms of
the Fock space as follows.

Conjecture C
′
.

If
\lambda_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq N
, are distinct points in
\mathbb{C}
and

f\in F^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, then the functions
U_{\lambda_{k}}f
,

1\leq k\leq N
, are linearly independent in
F^{2}
.

Although the HRT conjecture remains open, there has been a considerable
amount of work in the literature concerning the problem. In the following
theorem we collect a few partial results relevant to our work here.

Theorem D
.

The HRT conjecture holds true

(a)

if
N\leq 3
,

(b)

or if
N=4
and, among the four points in
\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{4}
,
two lie on one line and the other two lie on a second parallel line,

(c)

or if

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}\\
b_{k}\end{pmatrix}:1\leq k\leq N\right\}\subset A\mathbb{Z}^{2}+\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}n\\
m\end{pmatrix}:n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is the
ordinary integer lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
A
is an invertible
2\times 2
matrix,
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Part (a) follows from Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
, part (b) follows from
Theorem 1.4 of
[
2
]
, and part (c) follows from Proposition 1.3
of
[
11
]
. See
[
7
,
10
,
13
]
and references
therein for further information and results about the HRT conjecture.

We will call the set
A\mathbb{Z}^{2}+\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}
a regular
lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Similarly, we can also talk about regular lattices in
\mathbb{C}
,
which are sets of the form
\mathbb{Z}e_{1}+\mathbb{Z}e_{2}+e
, where
e
is any complex
number, and
e_{1},e_{2}
are any two
\mathbb{R}
-independent complex numbers.

It is easy to see that the mapping

z=-2\pi y-ix/2

(see earlier remarks about the Bargmann transform) from the real
Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
has the following properties:

(i)

It maps straight lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to straight lines in
\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

It maps parallel lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to parellel lines in
\mathbb{C}
.

(iii)

It maps any regular lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to a regular lattice in
\mathbb{C}
.

Therefore, a corresponding version of Theorem D also holds for the Fock
space version of the HRT conjecture when points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are replaced
by points in
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then (the Fock
space version of) the HRT conjecture is valid for the points

\lambda_{k}=\beta e^{2k\pi i/d},\qquad 0\leq k\leq d-1.

Proof.

The case
d=1
is trivial. The cases
d=2
and
3
follow from part (a)
or part (c) of Theorem D. It is clear that the case
d=4
follows from
part (b) or part (c) of Theorem D.

Less obvious is the case
d=6
, which we show also follows from
part (c) of Theorem D. In fact, with

\omega=e^{2\pi i/6}=e^{\pi i/3},

we have
\lambda_{k}=\beta\omega^{k}
for
0\leq k\leq 5
, and

\omega^{0}=1,\ \omega^{1}=\omega,\ \omega^{2}=\omega-1,\ \omega^{3}=-1,\ \omega^{4}=-\omega,\ \omega^{5}=1-\omega.

This shows that, for every
0\leq k\leq 5
, we have

-\beta\omega^{k}=-\beta(m_{k}+n_{k}\omega),\qquad m_{k},n_{k}\in\{-1,0,1\}.

It follows that the points in
\{1,\omega,\cdots,\omega^{5}\}
belong to
the regular lattice
\mathbb{Z}+\omega\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathbb{C}
(see picture below).
Consequently, the points in
\{\beta e^{2k\pi i/6}:0\leq k\leq 5\}
belong
to the regular lattice
\beta(\mathbb{Z}+\omega\mathbb{Z})
. This completes the proof
of the theorem.

For any integer

\omega_{k}=\exp(2k\pi i/d)

It is very tempting for us to guess that they must always belong to

We will need some algebraic preliminaries to prove this.

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of all positive integers. Recall that the
Euler function
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by

\phi(n)=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}^{k_{j}-1}(p_{j}-1),&n>1,\\
1,&n=1,\end{cases}

where

n=p_{1}^{k_{1}}p_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}},\qquad p_{1}<\cdots<p_{r},

is the unique prime factorization for
n>1
. See
[
9
, pp. 95-96]
.

Lemma 2
.

\phi(n)
is even for all
n>2
.

Proof.

If
n
contains an odd prime factor
p
, then
\phi(n)
includes
the factor
p-1
, which is even. If
n
contains no odd prime factors,
then
n=2^{k}
with
k\geq 2
, and in this case
\phi(n)=2^{k-1}

is also even.
∎

Lemma 3
.

The solutions to the equation
\phi(n)=2
are
n=3,4,6
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\phi(n)=2
for
n=3,4,6
. On the other hand, if
n

is any solution of
\phi(n)=2
, then
n>1
and

n=2^{k_{1}}3^{k_{2}}5^{k_{3}}\cdots
, where each
k_{j}\geq 0

and not all are zero. If
n
has a prime factor
p\geq 5
, then

\phi(n)\geq 4
. Therefore,
k_{j}=0
for all
j\geq 3
and and we
must have
n=2^{k_{1}}3^{k_{2}}
.

(a)

If
k_{1}=0
, then
2=\phi(n)=\phi(3^{k_{2}})=3^{k_{2}-1}(3-1)
,
which gives
k_{2}=1
and
n=3
.

(b)

If
k_{2}=0
, then
2=\phi(n)=\phi(2^{k_{1}})=2^{k_{1}-1}(2-1)
,
which gives
k_{1}=2
and
n=4
.

(c)

If
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are both nonzero, then

2=\phi(n)=\phi(2^{k_{1}}3^{k_{2}})=2^{k_{1}-1}3^{k_{2}-1}\cdot 2
,
which gives
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
and
n=6
.

This proves the desired result.
∎

\mathbb{F}
be a subfield of another field
\mathbb{E}
. Following

[

[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]

Theorem E
.

[

Let

be a positive integer and
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}

[\mathbb{Q}(\omega):\mathbb{Q}]=\phi(d),

where
\mathbb{Q}

, the

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
. Then the
following are equivalent.

(a)

The points

\lambda_{k}=\omega^{k}=e^{2k\pi i/d},\qquad 0\leq k\leq d-1,

belong to a regular lattice on the complex plane.

(b)

n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
.

Proof.

It is obvious that the points in
\{\omega^{k}:0\leq k\leq d-1\}

belong to a regular lattice in
\mathbb{C}
when
d\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
By Theorem
1
, this is also true when
d=6
.

Next we fix
d>4
and assume that

\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d-1}\}
belong
to a regular lattice

L=\mathbb{Z}e_{1}+\mathbb{Z}e_{2}+e

in the complex plane, where
e\in\mathbb{C}
and
e_{1},e_{2}
are two
complex numbers that are linearly independent with respect
to
\mathbb{R}
.

For
1\leq k\leq d-1
we write

v_{k}=\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{0}=n_{k1}e_{1}+n_{k2}e_{2},

where
n_{k1}
and
n_{k2}
are integers. Then for
k\geq 2

we can write

\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{11}&n_{12}\\
n_{k1}&n_{k2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}\\
e_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

With respect to the real numbers,
v_{1}
and
v_{k}
are linearly
independent, and
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are linearly independent. It
follows that each of the above

is invertible. In particular, we have

\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}\\
e_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{11}&n_{12}\\

v_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus for

we can write

\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{11}&n_{12}\\

n_{21}&n_{22}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\

Elementary linear algebra tells us that the inverse of an invertible

v_{k}
,
k\geq 3
, is a linear combination of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
with

are both contained in

. This along with

and Theorem E shows that
\phi(d)=2
. An
application of Lemma

then gives

.

3.
A deep zero problem

In this section we use the HRT conjecture to study a certain deep
zero problem for the Fock space. We begin with some preliminary
results about composition operators on the Fock space.

Given an integer
d>1
, we again let
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
.
Consider the function
\varphi(z)=\omega z
. Obviously,
\varphi

is just a rotation of the complex plane about the origin, so it induces
a unitary composition operator
C_{\varphi}
on the Fock space,

C_{\varphi}f(z)=f\circ\varphi(z)=f(\omega z),\qquad f\in F^{2}.

It is clear that any function

f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}z^{j}

in
F^{2}
admits a unique decomposition

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}f_{k}(z),

where

f_{k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{k+jd}z^{k+jd},\qquad 0\leq k\leq d-1.

For
0\leq k\leq d-1
we define the projection operator
P_{k}:F^{2}\to F^{2}
by

P_{k}f=f_{k}
. It is clear that the decomposition of
f
above is orthogonal,
that is, we actually have

I=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{d-1}P_{k},

where
I
is the identity operator on
F^{2}
.

Lemma 5
.

With the notation above, we have
C_{\varphi}P_{k}=\omega^{k}P_{k}
and

P_{k}f(z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}f(\omega^{m}z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}C_{\varphi}^{m}f(z)

for
0\leq k\leq d-1
and
f\in F^{2}
.

Proof.

For any
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
in
F^{2}
we have

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}P_{k}f(z)

\displaystyle=C_{\varphi}f_{k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{k+dj}(\omega z)^{k+dj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{k+dj}\omega^{k+dj}z^{k+dj}=\omega^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{k+dj}z^{k+dj}

\displaystyle=\omega^{k}f_{k}(z)=\omega^{k}P_{k}f(z).

Thus
C_{\varphi}P_{k}=\omega^{k}P_{k}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}f(\omega^{m}z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}C^{m}_{\varphi}f(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}C^{m}_{\varphi}P_{n}f(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-km}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\omega^{mn}P_{n}f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}P_{n}f(z)\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}(\omega^{n-k})^{m}

\displaystyle=dP_{k}f(z)+\sum_{n=0,n\not=k}^{d-1}\frac{1-\omega^{d(n-k)}}{1-\omega^{n-k}}P_{n}f(z)

\displaystyle=dP_{k}f(z).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following lemma establishes the commutation relation between
the Weyl operator
U_{\alpha}
and the composition operator

C_{\varphi}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
d>1
,
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
,
\varphi(z)=\omega z
, and

\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}U_{\beta}f=U_{\beta\omega^{-1}}C_{\varphi}f,\quad f\in F^{2}.

Proof.

For any
f\in F^{2}
we have

\displaystyle(C_{\varphi}U_{\beta}f)(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta|^{2}-\bar{\beta}\omega z}f(\omega z+\beta)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta|^{2}-\bar{\beta}\omega z}f(\omega(z+\omega^{-1}\beta))

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta|^{2}-\bar{\beta}\omega z}(C_{\varphi}f)(z+\omega^{-1}\beta)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta\omega^{-1}|^{2}-\overline{\beta\omega^{-1}}z}(C_{\varphi}f)(z+\omega^{-1}\beta)

\displaystyle=(U_{\beta\omega^{-1}}C_{\varphi}f)(z).

This completes the proof.
∎

The main result of this section is the following. As a consequence of it,
we will obtain Hedenmalm’s Theorem A as a special case, answer
Hedenmalm’s Problem B affirmatively, and derive a couple of additional
new results.

Theorem 7
.

Let
d>1
be an integer,
\beta_{k}=\beta\in\mathbb{C}
for
k=1,\ldots,d-1
, and

\mathcal{E}_{k}=\{k+dj:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
for
0\leq k\leq d-1
. If Conjecture C
′

holds for
N=d
and
\lambda_{k}=-\beta\omega^{-k+1}
,
0\leq k\leq d-1
,
where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/d}
, then the deep zero problem

\begin{cases}f^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{0},\\
(U_{\beta_{1}}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{1},\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
(U_{\beta_{d-1}}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{d-1},\end{cases}

(2)

for
f\in F^{2}
has an affirmative answer, that is, the condition
above implies
f=0
.

Proof.

Let
h=U_{\beta}f
. The conditions in (
2
) are the same as

\begin{cases}(U_{-\beta}h)^{(j)}(0)=(U_{\beta}^{-1}h)^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{0},\\[8.0pt]
h^{(j)}(0)=0,&j\in\mathcal{E}_{k},1\leq k\leq d-1,\end{cases}

which imply that
P_{0}U_{-\beta}h=0
and
h_{k}=P_{k}h=0
for

1\leq k\leq d-1
. Write
h=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}h_{k}
and apply
Lemmas
5
and
6
. We obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=P_{0}U_{-\beta}h=P_{0}U_{-\beta}h_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}C_{\varphi}^{m}U_{-\beta}h_{0}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}U_{-\beta\omega^{-m}}C_{\varphi}^{m}h_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}U_{-\beta\omega^{-m}}h_{0}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}U_{-\beta\omega^{-m}}h.

If Conjecture C
′
holds for
N=d
and the points
\lambda_{k}=-\beta\omega^{-k}
,
0\leq k\leq N-1
, then we must have
h=0
,
and so
f=0
. This proves the desired result.
∎

The following result is now a consequence of Theorem
1
,
Theorem D, and Theorem
7
. The case
d=2
yields
Theorem A, the case
d=4
gives an affirmative answer to
Problem B, and the cases
d=3
and
d=6
are something
that has not been considered before.

Corollary 8
.

For
2\leq d\leq 4
or
d=6
let
\beta_{0}=0
and

\beta_{1}=\cdots=\beta_{d-1}=\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. If
f\in F^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle(U_{\beta_{j}}f)^{(j)}(0)=0,~~\textnormal{for each}~j\in\mathcal{E}_{k}~\textnormal{and}~k=0,\ldots,d-1,

then
f=0
.

Proof.

If
\beta=0
, then the assumptions imply that all Taylor coefficients of
f

at the origin are
0
, so
f=0
. The case
\beta\neq 0
follows from
Theorem
1
, part (c) of Theorem D, and Theorem
7
.
∎

We conclude the paper with the following problem, a natural special case
of the HRT conjecture.

Problem 9
.

For any positive integer
d
let
\lambda_{k}=e^{2k\pi i/d}
,
0\leq k\leq d-1
.
Is the HRT conjecture true for the points in
\{\lambda_{k}\}
, that is, if

f\in F^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, are the functions in
\{U_{\lambda_{k}}f\}

linearly independent?

Although the full HRT conjecture remains open and seems to be a very
hard problem, we feel that this special case may not be that difficult.
Its solution together with our Theorem
7
will then yield a nice
result about deep zero problems. By the previous section,
Problem
9
for the case
d\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
has an affirmative
answer, so the interesting remaining cases are
d=5
and
d>6
,
that is when the roots of unity no longer lie in a regular lattice but
we suspect that the HRT conjecture may still be true.
