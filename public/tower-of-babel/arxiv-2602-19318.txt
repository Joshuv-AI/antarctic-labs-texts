Dualizable abelian fibrations
Davesh Maulik, Junliang Shen, Qizheng Yin

Abstract
. In his proof of the fundamental lemma of the Langlands program, Ngô initiated the study of the decomposition theorem for abelian fibrations. When an abelian fibration admits a duality structure, the decomposition theorem and the perverse filtration on cohomology exhibit rich structures. The purpose of these notes is to describe a framework for dualizable abelian fibrations and to discuss some recent progress and applications.



0.
Introduction

Throughout, we work over the complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
, and all Chow groups and (relative) Chow motives are taken with
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients.

An abelian fibration is a proper surjective morphism
f:M\to B
between nonsingular varieties such that
f
has equidimensional fibers and a general fiber is an abelian variety. Elliptic surfaces provide first examples of abelian fibrations, whose geometry and topology have been studied intensively since the foundational work of Kodaira and Néron in the 1960s.

In higher dimension, a complete classification of singular fibers analogous to that for elliptic surfaces is no longer possible. Instead, to help understand the topology of the fibration, a natural object to study is the (derived) pushforward

(1)

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M}\in D^{b}_{c}(B)

lying in the bounded derived category of constructible sheaves. By the decomposition theorem of Beilinson, Bernstein, Deligne, and Gabber
[
9
]
, the object (
1
) splits into shifted
semisimple perverse sheaves
,
i.e.
, direct sums of simple perverse sheaves which are the building blocks of the abelian category
\mathrm{Perv}(B)\subset D^{b}_{c}(B)
of perverse sheaves. Every simple perverse sheaf is constructed from an irreducible subvariety
Z\subset B
together with an irreducible local system on a dense open subset of
Z
. The subvariety
Z
that appeared as above is called a
support
of the morphism
f:M\to B
. In general, it is challenging to determine all the supports and the local systems associated with the decomposition theorem for (
1
).

Over 15 years ago, Ngô
[
35
]
observed that the decomposition theorem becomes more tractable for a large class of abelian fibrations, which he called
\delta
-regular weak abelian fibrations
. Ngô showed for such fibrations that remarkably limited geometric information suffices to determine
all
the supports and local systems appearing in the decomposition theorem for (
1
). Then Ngô applied this tool to the Hitchin system associated with a reductive group, from which he deduced the fundamental lemma of the Langlands program. A crucial geometric ingredient in Ngô’s argument is the use of an action of a commutative
B
-group scheme
P
on the total space
M
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\;\curvearrowright\;}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

The existence of such a group action is the main assumption for a
\delta
-regular weak abelian fibration, and it plays a key role in determining the supports and the local systems. We refer to
[
35
, Section 7]
,
[
36
]
,
[
41
, Section 1]
, and
[
27
, Section 1]
for more detailed discussions. Besides the proof of the fundamental lemma, Ngô’s techniques for the decomposition theorem of abelian fibrations have been applied to solving a number of open questions and conjectures, see
e.g.

[
15
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
31
,
32
]
.

Recently, it has been observed that if the abelian fibration
f:M\to B
admits a
dual fibration

f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
satisfying certain nice properties, the object (
1
) exhibits richer structures. We highlight three directions in which the dualizable abelian fibration structure proves particularly powerful.

(a)

Locating tautological classes in perverse filtrations:
it was used in
[
29
]
to give a proof of the
P=W
conjecture
[
12
]
for the Hitchin system, in
[
29
,
39
]
to give a proof of the
P=C
conjecture
[
24
,
23
]
in the free range, and in
[
18
]
to locate the Chern classes in the perverse filtration as conjectured in
[
4
]
.

(b)

Constructing motivic decompositions:
it was used to verify the motivic decomposition conjecture of Corti–Hanamura for various abelian fibrations, including compactified Jacobian fibrations for integral locally planar curves
[
29
]
, and Hitchin systems
[
30
]
.

(c)

Analyzing multiplicative structures:
it was used in
[
29
]
to prove that the perverse filtrations for many abelian fibrations are multiplicative with respect to the cup-product, generalizing a result of Oblomkov–Yun for certain compactified Jacobians
[
37
]
; furthermore, the multiplicativity was used to construct the intrinsic cohomology ring of the universal Jacobian — a ring structure on the universal fine compactified Jacobian which is independent of the choice of a natural compactification
[
5
]
.

Roughly, an abelian fibration
f:M\to B
is called
dualizable
, if there is a dual fibration
f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
whose general fiber is the dual abelian variety of the corresponding fiber of the original abelian fibration,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
,
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\vee}}

and there is an object
{\mathcal{P}}
on the relative product
M^{\vee}\times_{B}M
which behaves like the Poincaré line bundle associated with an abelian scheme and its dual. The key observation in
[
29
]
is that the correspondences induced by
{\mathcal{P}}
, which are called the
Fourier transforms
, characterize both the decomposition theorem for (
1
) and the associated perverse filtration. In particular, it suggests that even if we are only interested in the given abelian fibration
f:M\to B
, it is advantageous to consider its dual.

We note that the structure of a dualizable abelian fibration is expected to enhance Ngô’s group scheme action in the following sense: the fibration
f:M\to B
is a partial compactification of (a torsor under) the group
P\to B
; a dual fibration
f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
should then be a partial compactification of (a torsor under) the dual group
P^{\vee}\to B
. Furthermore, the property of
\delta
-regularity, which is a key ingredient in Ngô’s theory, is a main geometric ingredient for the desired properties of the object
{\mathcal{P}}
for dualizable abelian fibrations
[
29
]
; see also the proof of Theorem
2.9
below. We refer to
[
2
,
21
,
22
]
for relevant discussions regarding the group scheme
P
and dual fibrations.

In Section
1
, we review the work of Beauville
[
7
]
and Deninger–Murre
[
17
]
for abelian schemes. This provides the main motivation for, and serves as a guiding example of, dualizable abelian fibrations. In Sections
2
and

, we discuss the definition, main results, and applications of dualizable abelian fibrations. The theory of dualizable abelian fibrations is presented largely following
[
29

with minor modifications. Most results discussed in this paper are drawn from
[

,

,

,

1.
Abelian schemes

1.1.
Overview

Let
f:A\to B
be an abelian scheme of relative dimension
g
over a nonsingular base
B
. It admits a natural dual

(2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\vee}}

with
A^{\vee}
given by the identity component of the relative Picard
A^{\vee}:=\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(A)
. The dual
A^{\vee}
is a moduli space relative over
B
with a universal family given by the normalized Poincaré line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathrm{Pic}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A)
. The main purpose of this section is to explain that the dual (
2
) and the normalized Poincaré line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}
yield the following structural results:

(a)

The Fourier transform induced by
{\mathcal{P}}
yields a motivic decomposition for
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}
; this decomposition is nowadays known as the Beauville decomposition.

(b)

The algebra objects
(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-],\cup)
and
(Rf^{\vee}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A^{\vee}}[-],\ast)
induced by the cup-product on
A
and the convolution product on
A^{\vee}
are isomorphic via the Fourier transform of (a).

(c)

The natural tautological classes are homogeneous with respect to the Beauville decomposition; this is a toy model of the
P=C
phenomenon.

All the results in this section are due to Beauville
[
7
]
and Deninger–Murre
[
17
]
.

1.2.
Fourier transforms

The normalized Poincaré line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathrm{Pic}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A)
induces a Fourier–Mukai transform

\mathrm{FM}_{\mathcal{P}}:D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(A^{\vee})\xrightarrow{\simeq}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(A)

whose inverse is given by the Fourier–Mukai kernel

{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}:={\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}\otimes p_{A}^{*}\omega_{A/B}[g]\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(A\times_{B}A).

Here
p_{A}:A\times_{B}A^{\vee}\to A^{\vee}
is the natural projection and
\omega_{A/B}
is the relative canonical bundle.

We set

\mathfrak{F}=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{F}_{i}:={\mathrm{ch}}({\mathcal{P}})\in\mathrm{CH}^{*}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A),\quad\mathfrak{F}_{i}:={\mathrm{ch}}_{i}({\mathcal{P}})\in\mathrm{CH}^{i}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A).

We view
\mathfrak{F}
as a mixed-degree Chow correspondence between
A^{\vee}
and
A
, whose inverse is defined via Riemann–Roch:

\mathfrak{F}^{-1}=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\in\mathrm{CH}^{*}(A\times_{B}A^{\vee}),\quad\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\in\mathrm{CH}^{i}(A\times_{B}A^{\vee}).

The correspondences
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
are known as the
Fourier transforms
.

We have a tautological expression of the relative diagonal classes

(3)

[\Delta_{A/B}]=\mathfrak{F}\circ\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\in\mathrm{CH}^{g}(A\times_{B}A),\quad[\Delta_{A^{\vee}/B}]=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\circ\mathfrak{F}\in\mathrm{CH}^{g}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A^{\vee})

where
\circ
stands for the composition of correspondences.

The following
Fourier vanishing
result plays a key role in the theory of Fourier transforms for abelian schemes.

Proposition 1.1

(Fourier vanishing)
.

For
i+j\neq 2g
, we have

\mathfrak{F}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{j}=0\in\mathrm{CH}^{i+j-g}(A\times_{B}A),\quad\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{j}=0\in\mathrm{CH}^{i+j-g}(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A^{\vee}).

Proof.

Considering the degree
k\neq 2g
part of the second identity of (
3
) gives

(4)

0=\sum_{i+j=k}\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{j}.

Now for an integer
N
, we consider the “multiplication by
N
” map
[N]:A^{\vee}\to A^{\vee}
. The normalized Poincaré line bundle interacts with this map as

\left([N]\times\mathrm{id}\right)^{*}{\mathcal{P}}\simeq{\mathcal{P}}^{\otimes N}\in\mathrm{Pic}\left(A^{\vee}\times A\right).

In particular, the map
[N]\times\mathrm{id}:A^{\vee}\times A^{\vee}\to A^{\vee}\times A^{\vee}
satisfies

([N]\times\mathrm{id})^{*}\left(\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{j}\right)=N^{j}\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{j}

Consequently, we apply the pullback along
([N]\times\mathrm{id})^{*}
to (
4
) for all
N
, yielding the second identity of Proposition
1.1
. The first identity follows by a parallel argument.
∎

1.3.
Applications

We discuss some applications of the duality (
2
) and the Fourier transforms
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
.

The decomposition theorem for the smooth morphism
f:A\to B
reads

(5)

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}R^{i}f_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-i]

where each summand on the right-hand side is a (semisimple) local system.

The first application of the Fourier transform package is the construction of the Beauville decomposition — a canonical motivic lifting of (
5
) where the projectors are explicitly given by
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Motivic decomposition)
.

There is a decomposition of the relative Chow motive

h(A)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}h_{i}(A)

compatible with the cup-product. The projector
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
for each summand
h_{i}(A)=(A,\mathfrak{q}_{i},0)
is given by

\mathfrak{q}_{i}:=\mathfrak{F}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{2g-i}\in\mathrm{CH}^{g}(A\times_{B}A).

Moreover, the sheaf-theoretic specialization yields a canonical multiplicative decomposition

(6)

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}R^{i}f_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-i],

i.e.
, the isomorphism (
6
) is compatible with the cup-product.

The motivic decomposition follows directly from the first identity of (
3
), where the Fourier vanishing of Proposition
1.1
is used to prove that the correspondences
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
indeed form orthogonal projectors. The multiplicativity is deduced by showing the compatibility of the motivic decomposition and the “multiplication by
N
” map, which was already implicitly used in the proof of Proposition
1.1
. In particular, consider the “multiplication by
N
” correspondence

[N]^{*}:Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}\to Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A};

the decomposition (
6
) is recovered by the decomposition into eigenspaces of this correspondence with each local system
R^{i}f_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}
the eigenspace corresponding to
N^{i}
.

Similarly, we consider the decomposition into eigenspaces for the dual fibration

(7)

Rf^{\vee}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A^{\vee}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}R^{i}f^{\vee}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A^{\vee}}[-i].

The decompositions (
6
) and (
7
) further yield multiplicative decompositions of the cohomology

H^{*}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}H^{*}_{(i)}(A,{\mathbb{Q}}),\quad H^{*}(A^{\vee},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}H^{*}_{(i)}(A^{\vee},{\mathbb{Q}})

which split the Leray filtrations for
f:A\to B
and
f^{\vee}:A^{\vee}\to B
respectively.

Theorem 1.3

(Fourier stability)
.

The Fourier transforms

(8)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Rf^{\vee}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A^{\vee}}[-]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}}

respect the decompositions (
6
) and (

). Moreover, the only nontrivial correspondences restricting to the direct sum components are

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{i}f_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-i]}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}_{2g-i}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}_{i}}

In particular, the

is characterized by the Fourier transform:

H_{(i)}^{*}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})=\mathrm{Im}\left(\mathfrak{F}_{i}:H^{*}(A^{\vee},{\mathbb{Q}})\to H^{*}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})\right).

In fact, Fourier vanishing implies that the motivic decompositions for

with respect to the group structure. This yields a

whose restrictions to the summands are

Theorem 1.4

(Convolution)
.

The Fourier transforms induce an isomorphism of algebra objects

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{A}[-],\cup\right)}

which enhances (

We note that Theorem

is a

, under the Fourier–Mukai transforms, intertwines with the convolution structure

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathrm{mult}}\in\mathrm{Coh}\left(A^{\vee}\times_{B}A^{\vee}\times_{B}A^{\vee}\right).

1.4.
A toy model for
P=C

The
P=C
phenomenon is a match of two structures from different origins for certain moduli spaces of sheaves which admit abelian fibrations. More precisely, it asserts that the location of a tautological class in the perverse filtration associated with the abelian fibration is determined exactly by its Chern grading — the latter being defined intrinsically without reference to the abelian fibration. The
P=W
conjecture of de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini
[
12
]
for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
can be deduced from
P=C
for the moduli space of stable Higgs bundles; see
[
14
, Section 0.3]
. Indeed, all known proofs
[
28
,
19
,
29
]
of
P=W
are obtained through proving
P=C
. More recently, the
P=C
phenomenon has been discovered to hold for moduli spaces other than those in non-abelian Hodge theory
[
24
,
23
]
. In this section, we explain through a toy model that
P=C
is a consequence of the Fourier transform package.

Let
C\to B
be a family of nonsingular curves lying on a nonsingular surface
S
. For convenience, we assume that
S
is projective and the family
C\to B
admits a section
\iota:B\hookrightarrow C
.
1
1

1

These assumptions are only for the convenience of the discussion and can be removed; see
[
29
, Sections 4 and 5]
.

The relative Jacobian

f:J_{C}\to B

which parameterizes degree
0
line bundles on the fibers is a self-dual abelian fibration

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J_{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J_{C}=J_{C}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\vee}=f}

with
{\mathcal{P}}
the normalized Poincaré line bundle. The relative Jacobian
J_{C}
can be viewed as moduli space of
1
-dimensional torsion sheaves on
S
whose universal sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
S\times J_{C}
can be compared with
{\mathcal{P}}
via the Abel–Jacobi map
\mathrm{AJ}:C\to J_{C}
induced by the section
\iota:B\hookrightarrow C
. More precisely, let
\mathrm{ev}:C\to S
be the evaluation map; then Riemann–Roch implies that there is a normalization of the Chern character such that

\widehat{\mathrm{ch}}({\mathcal{F}})=(\mathrm{ev}\times\mathrm{id}_{J_{C}})_{*}(\mathrm{AJ}\times\mathrm{id}_{J_{C}})^{*}\mathrm{ch}({\mathcal{P}})=(\mathrm{ev}\times\mathrm{id}_{J_{C}})_{*}(\mathrm{AJ}\times\mathrm{id}_{J_{C}})^{*}\mathfrak{F}.

Define the tautological classes

c_{k}(\gamma):=\int_{\gamma}\widehat{\mathrm{ch}}_{k}({\mathcal{F}})\in H^{*}(J_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}),\quad\gamma\in H^{*}(S,{\mathbb{Q}}).

Theorem 1.5

(
P=C
)
.

We have

\prod_{i=1}^{s}c_{k_{i}}(\gamma_{i})\in H^{*}_{(k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{s})}(J_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

In particular, the location of each tautological class in the perverse filtration, which is equivalent to the Leray filtration for
f:J_{C}\to B
, is determined by the Chern grading.

The proof is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.3
. More precisely, the projection formula implies

c_{k}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{F}_{k}(\mathrm{ev}^{*}\mathrm{AJ}_{*}\gamma)

which lies in
H^{*}_{(k)}(J_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
by (
9
); then the theorem follows from the multiplicativity of the decomposition
H^{*}(J_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i}H^{*}_{(i)}(J_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
.

We see in this case that the Fourier transform explains the
P=C
phenomenon. Furthermore, it clarifies why normalizations are required for the tautological classes
[
12
,
24
,
23
]
: from the perspective of the Fourier transform, the normalizations arise from comparing a universal family with respect to the surface, to the normalized Poincaré line bundle with respect to the dual fibration.

2.
Dualizable abelian fibrations: definition and examples

2.1.
What is our purpose?

Before introducing the (rather technical!) definition of dualizable abelian fibrations, we first discuss informally the underlying motivation.

In Section
1
, we reviewed results for abelian schemes established by Beauville and Deninger–Murre over 30 years ago. A natural question is: which structures of abelian schemes can be
generalized
to abelian fibrations with singular fibers? The examples of abelian fibrations we have in mind include:

(a)

Hitchin systems.
They are crucial in the
P=W
conjecture, and serve as our main motivating example.

(b)

Compactified Jacobians.
The topology of compactified Jacobians has deep connections to knot invariants
[
37
,
38
,
41
]
and the universal Abel–Jacobi theory
[
3
,
6
]
.

(c)

Lagrangian fibrations.
They play a key role in the study of compact hyper-Kähler varieties.

First, we should not expect that results parallel to Section
1
hold for abelian fibrations when there are singular fibers. For example, the following theorem shows that the Beauville decomposition cannot exist even we include the “mildest” singular fibers.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

For
g\geq 3
, there exists a family
C\to B
of curves of arithmetic genus
g
with at worst one simple node, such that the total space of the relative compactified Jacobian
\overline{J}_{C}
is nonsingular, and the perverse filtration associated with
f:\overline{J}_{C}\to B
does not admit any multiplicative splitting. Consequently, no isomorphism given by the decomposition theorem

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{C}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)},\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}:={{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}H^{i+\dim B}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{C}})[-i-\dim B]\in D^{b}_{c}(B)

is multiplicative.

The failure of the Beauville decomposition may be explained by the fact that there is no appropriate extension of the “multiplication by
N
” structure over the singular fibers. Instead, we may hope to build our theory on a duality

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\vee}}

with a pair of objects
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
on the relative product
M^{\vee}\times_{B}M
that generalize the Poincaré line bundles. In many interesting examples, the existence of such a Poincaré sheaf was established by Arinkin
[
1
]
.

The Poincaré objects
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
still yield Fourier transforms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M}[-]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Rf^{\vee}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M^{\vee}}[-].}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{F}}

However, the Fourier transform is not expected to satisfy Theorem
1.4
with some convolution product defined on
M^{\vee}
. This was illustrated in the following example.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

For
g\geq 4
, there exist two birational abelian fibrations
f_{1}:M_{1}\to B
and
f_{2}:M_{2}\to B
given by fine compactified Jacobians associated with stable curves of genus
g
, which share a common dual
M^{\vee}\to B
,
i.e.
, there exist Poincaré sheaves
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
inducing derived equivalences

\mathrm{FM}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{i}}:\mathrm{}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M^{\vee})\xrightarrow{\simeq}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M_{i}),\quad i=1,2,

but whose cohomology rings are not isomorphic as
H^{*}(B.{\mathbb{Q}})
-algebras:

\left(H^{*}(M_{1},{\mathbb{Q}}),\cup\right)\not\simeq\left(H^{*}(M_{2},{\mathbb{Q}}),\cup\right).

In particular, Theorem
2.2
shows that there is no well-defined convolution product on
M^{\vee}
which is matched with the cup-product for both duals
M_{1},M_{2}
through a Fourier transform.

The two counterexamples above suggest that a theory of Fourier transforms for abelian fibrations (which should cover the abelian fibrations considered in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
), if it exists, is considerably subtler than the corresponding theory for abelian schemes. We aim to single out a class of abelian fibrations that admit dual fibrations, for which the resulting duality enjoys favorable properties, while still including a sufficiently wide class of interesting geometric examples. We introduce this class of abelian fibration in the next section, which we call
dualizable abelian fibrations
.

2.2.
Dualizable abelian fibrations

We begin by carefully defining what we mean by
abelian fibrations
. We say that
f:M\to B
is an abelian fibration of relative dimension
g
, if

(a)

M,B
are nonsingular,

(b)

f
is proper and flat, and

(c)

f:M\to B
is generically an abelian scheme of relative dimension
g

More precisely, the condition (c) says that there is a Zariski open subset
U\subset B
over which
f_{U}:M_{U}\to U
is an abelian scheme of relative dimension
g
. In applications, we sometimes consider the case where
f_{U}:M_{U}\to U
is a torsor under an abelian scheme; the framework can be adapted accordingly to accommodate this situation; see Section
3.4
.

In the following, we list the axioms for an abelian fibration of relative dimension
g
to be
dualizable
; clearly these axioms are modeled on the corresponding properties of abelian schemes that we discussed in Section
1
.

Axiom A: Dual abelian fibration exists.
There exists an abelian fibration

such that there is a Zariski open subset

over which we have dual abelian schemes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{U}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{U}^{\vee}=\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(M_{U})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
.
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{U}^{\vee}}

Axiom B: Full support.
Both

and

have full support. More precisely, every (shifted) simple perverse sheaf that appears in the decomposition theorem for either
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M}

Remark 2.3
.

Axiom B suggests that it may be better to restrict ourselves to

Axiom C: Poincaré line bundle extends.
By Axiom A, we have the normalized Poincaré line bundle

We require that there is an extension

{\mathcal{P}}\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}\left(M^{\vee}\times_{B}M\right)

of the Poincaré line bundle, such that there exists
{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M\times_{B}M^{\vee})

Here

In other words, Axiom C asserts that the Fourier–Mukai duality for (

Axiom C+: Poincaré line bundle extends nicely.
The objects

(12)

{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{P}}^{\otimes N}\in D\mathrm{Coh}(M^{\vee}\times_{B}M^{\vee}),\quad{\mathcal{P}}^{\otimes N}\circ{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\in D\mathrm{Coh}(M\times_{B}M)

and

respectively for infinitely many integers

Remark 2.4
.

We note that the objects (
12

not
be bounded, as
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
are not assumed to be

on the possibly singular variety

. Nevertheless, the codimension of their support is still well-defined. Over the abelian scheme locus
U
, the support of (

.

Axiom D: Convolution extends nicely.
Let

be the relative small diagonal in

is supported in codimension

is the only component of pure codimension

Remark 2.5
.

By definition, the Fourier–Mukai transform associated with
{\mathcal{K}}
defines a convolution product on
D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M^{\vee})
which intertwines the tensor product on
D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M)
; over the abelian scheme locus
U

{\mathcal{K}}
is exactly the structure sheaf of the graph
\Gamma_{\mathrm{mult}}
of the multiplication map for the abelian scheme
M^{\vee}_{U}\to U
.

Definition 2.6

(Dualizable abelian fibration)
.

We say that

is a dualizable abelian fibration of relative dimension
g
, if it is an abelian fibration of relative dimension
g
satisfying Axioms A, B, C, C+, D.

Remark 2.7
.

Although the group structures of

cannot be extended over the singular fibers, Axioms C+ can be viewed as “poor man’s substitute” for the compatibility between the “multiplication by

for abelian schemes, and Axiom D can be viewed as “poor man’s substitute” for the existence of a multiplication map (

3.
Dualizable abelian fibrations: results and applications

3.1.
Decomposition theorem

Since our main goal is to understand the decomposition theorem
[
9
]
for dualizable abelian fibrations, we begin with a brief discussion of the relevant general terminology.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a proper and flat morphism between nonsingular varieties. Assume that each fiber is of dimension
g
. The decomposition theorem reads

(14)

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)},\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}:={{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}H^{i+\dim Y}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})[-i-\dim Y]\in D^{b}_{c}(Y).

The decomposition theorem is not canonical; what remain canonical are the perverse truncation functors
{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq\bullet}(-),{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\geq\bullet}(-)
applied to the pushforward complex
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}
. Setting

P_{i}H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}):=\mathrm{Im}\left(H^{*}(Y,{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq i+\dim Y}Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})\to H^{*}(Y,Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})=H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\right),

we obtain the perverse filtration

P_{0}H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{1}H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\subset\cdots\subset P_{2g}H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})=H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})

and the canonical identification

\mathrm{Gr}_{i}^{P}H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})=H^{*}(B,{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}).

The cup-product on
X
yields the cup-product on the pushforward complex,

(15)

\cup:Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}\otimes Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}\to Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}.

We say that
f:X\to Y
admits a
multiplicative decomposition theorem
if there exists an isomorphism (
14
) which interacts with the cup-product (
15
) as

\cup:{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{(j)}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{(i+j)}.

By Theorem
1.2
, an abelian scheme admits a multiplicative decomposition theorem; this was generalized to any smooth abelian fibration in
[
5
, Appendix A]
. In contrast, Theorem
2.1
shows that compactified Jacobian fibrations
f:\overline{J}_{C}\to B
associated with nodal curves may not admit a multiplicative decomposition theorem.

In general, multiplicative decomposition theorems are rare. For example, most projective bundles
{\mathbb{P}}(V)\to Y
associated with a vector bundle
V\to Y
do not admit a multiplicative decomposition theorem; this is because the Leray filtration (which is equivalent to perverse filtration in this case) does not admit a multiplicative splitting for most projective bundles
{\mathbb{P}}(V)
.

A weaker condition is the
multiplicativity of the perverse truncation functors
,
i.e.
,

(16)

\cup:{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq i+\dim Y}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})\otimes{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq j+\dim Y}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})\to{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq i+j+\dim Y}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}),\quad i,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Of course, if
f:X\to Y
admits a multiplicative decomposition theorem, it has multiplicative perverse truncation functors; but the latter is much weaker. For example, any smooth and proper map
f:X\to Y
has multiplicative perverse truncation functors. But a general proper flat map between nonsingular varieties may fail to have multiplicative perverse truncation functors; see
[
11
, Exercise 5.6.8]
and the discussion in
[
10
]
.

If (
16
) holds for
f:X\to Y
, then the cup-product still yields a cup-product on the summands:

\overline{\cup}:{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{(j)}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{(i+j)},

which we call the
induced cup-product
. In general, if
f:X\to Y
does not admit a multiplicative decomposition theorem, the algebra object

(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)},\overline{\cup})

does not recover the algebra object

(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X},\cup).

The first algebra object is a degeneration of the second algebra object.

Finally, we say that
f:X\to Y
admits a
motivic decomposition
, if there exists an isomorphism of relative Chow motives

h(X)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}h_{i}(X),\quad h(X)=(X,[\Delta_{X/Y}],0),\,\,h_{i}(X)=(X,\mathfrak{q}_{i},0)

whose sheaf-theoretic specialization recovers an isomorphism (
14
). In other words, there exists an orthogonal decomposition of the relative diagonal class

[\Delta_{X/Y}]=\sum_{i=0}^{2g}\mathfrak{q}_{i}\in\mathrm{CH}_{\dim X}(X\times_{Y}X)

whose associated (self-)correspondences on
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}
recover a decomposition (
14
). The motivic decomposition conjecture of Corti–Hanamura
[
16
]
predicts that a motivic decomposition always exists.

Conjecture 3.1

(Motivic decomposition conjecture
[
16
]
)
.

Any proper
f:X\to Y
between nonsingular varieties admits a motivic decomposition.

Theorem
1.2
shows that the conjecture holds for abelian schemes with the summands
h_{i}(X)
constructed from the Fourier transforms.

3.3.
Applications

We discuss several applications of the theory of dualizable abelian fibrations.

Motivic decompositions.
The results above allow us to prove Conjecture
3.1
for many abelian fibrations, including all the examples given in Section
2.3
. Furthermore, we obtain the following result which at a first glance is not a direct consequence of Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
30
]
)
.

Let

h:\mathrm{Higgs}_{n,d}\to{\mathcal{A}}

be the Hitchin system associated with a curve
\Sigma
of genus
g\geq 2
, and coprime rank
n
and degree
d
. Then Conjecture
3.1
holds for
h:\mathrm{Higgs}_{n,d}\to{\mathcal{A}}
.

As we mentioned in Section
2.3
, the Hitchin system above is not a dualizable abelian fibration. The proof of Theorem
3.5
is obtained by reducing it to the case of parabolic Hitchin system
f_{d}:M^{\eta}_{n,d}\to B
using Springer theory and nearby/vanishing cycle techniques. The latter is dualizable by Theorem
2.12
whose motivic decomposition is given by Theorem
3.2
(a).

Multiplicativity.

Let
C_{0}
be an integral locally planar curve, and let
\overline{J}_{C_{0}}
be its compactified Jacobian; it is in general a very singular variety. Maulik–Yun
[
32
]
introduced a perverse filtration on the cohomology

P_{0}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{1}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset\cdots\subset H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}}).

This perverse filtration is conjectured to have deep connections to knot invariants associated with the singular points of
X

[
38
,
41
]
, Using techniques in representation theory, Oblomkov–Yun proved in
[
37
]
that the perverse filtration is multiplicative with respect to the cup-product for rational curves with a singular point given by

x^{p}-y^{q},\quad\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)=1.

The theory of dualizable abelian fibrations yields a proof of the multiplicativity for any integral locally planar curve
C_{0}
; see
[
29
, Theorem 0.7]
.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
29
]
)
.

The perverse filtration
P_{\bullet}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}})
is multiplicative,
i.e.
,

\cup:P_{i}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}})\times P_{j}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}})\to P_{i+j}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C_{0}},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Recall that the perverse filtration of Maulik–Yun is defined as follows. By embedding the curve
C_{0}
into an appropriate family
C\to B
as a fiber over a point
0\in B
, we obtain the Cartesian diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{J}_{C_{0}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{J}_{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

The perverse filtration for the fiber over
0\in B
is then defined by the perverse truncation functor applied to
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{C}}
. Therefore, Theorem
3.6
can be deduced from Theorem
2.9
and Theorem
3.2
(b).

Perverse vs. Chern.
A key step in the proof of Theorem
3.2
is the characterization (
19
) of the perverse truncation functor using the Fourier transform. As a consequence, we obtain the following; see
[
29
, Theorem 0.1]
.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let
f:M\to B
be a dualizable abelian fibration with a dual fibration
f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
. Then we have

\mathrm{Im}\left(\mathfrak{F}_{i}:H^{*}(M^{\vee},{\mathbb{Q}})\to H^{*}(M,{\mathbb{Q}})\right)\subset P_{i}H^{*}(M,{\mathbb{Q}}).

The reader may compare Theorem
3.7
with (
9
).

By a similar argument as in Section
1.4
, Theorem
3.7
combined with Theorem
3.3
(a) leads to
P=C
type results:

(a)

Applying Theorem
3.7
to the parabolic Hitchin system
f_{d}:M^{\eta}_{n,d}\to B
yields a proof of the
P=W
conjecture
[
12
,
28
,
19
]
; see
[
29
, Proof of Theorem 0.4]
.

(b)

Applying Theorem
3.7
to the Le Potier moduli space of
1
-dimensional semistable sheaves on a del Pezzo surface yields a proof of the
P=C
conjecture
[
24
,
23
]
for low degree cohomology; see
[
29
, Theorem 0.6]
and
[
39
, Theorems 0.3 and 0.6]
.

Remark 3.8
.

More accurately, in both (a, b) above, we apply a variant of Theorem
3.9
; see the discussions in the paragraph after Theorem
2.12
and Section
3.4
.

Intrinsic cohomology rings.
Theorem
3.3
(b) can be applied in the following interesting way: if we have two abelian fibrations
f_{1}:M_{1}\to B
and
f_{2}:M_{2}\to B
which share a common dual
f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
, then we must have an isomorphism of the bigraded
H^{*}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
-algebras:

\left(\bigoplus_{i,d}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{i}H^{d}(M_{1},{\mathbb{Q}}),\overline{\cup}\right)\simeq\left(\bigoplus_{i,d}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{i}H^{d}(M_{2},{\mathbb{Q}}),\overline{\cup}\right);

this is because both sides are isomorphic to the convolution algebra on the associated graded cohomology of
M^{\vee}
given by the class
[\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{mult}}]
. This leads to the following theorem proven in
[
5
, Theorem 0.6]
, which constructs the
intrinsic cohomology ring
of the universal compactified Jacobians.

Theorem 3.9

(
[
5
]
)
.

Assume
n\geq 1
. Let
f^{\phi}:\overline{J}^{\phi}_{g,n}\to\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{g,n}
be the universal fine compactified Jacobian associated with a nondegenerate stability condition
\phi
. Then the induced associated graded cohomology ring

\left(\bigoplus_{i,d}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{i}H^{d}(\overline{J}^{\phi}_{g,n},{\mathbb{Q}}),~~~\overline{\cup}\right),

as a bigraded
H^{*}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
-algebra, does not depend on the nondegenerate stability condition
\phi
.

As discussed above, Theorem
3.9
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.3
(b) and Theorem
2.11
. On the other hand, it was proven in
[
5
, Theorem 0.5]
that the cohomology ring

\left(H^{*}(\overline{J}^{\phi}_{g,1},{\mathbb{Q}}),\cup\right)

is sensitive to
\phi
as long as
g\geq 4
; these are exactly the examples in Theorem
2.2
.

The results above suggest that the associated graded cohomology ring behaves “better” than the usual cohomology ring for dualizable abelian fibrations.

3.4.
Variants

To apply the theory of dualizable abelian fibrations, some variants are considered.

Torsors.
A very common situation is that the nonsingular fibers of an abelian fibration do not form an abelian scheme, but rather a torsor under an abelian scheme. Such cases were studied in
[
29
, Section 4]
,
[
30
]
, and
[
5
, Section 1]
, where the framework of dualizable abelian fibrations continues to apply with only minor modifications.

Smaller support.
The full support assumption in Axiom B is only needed to verify that

(
\ast
)

\textup{the correspondence \eqref{P_cycle} realizes the perverse truncation functor}.

Therefore, if there are other methods to verify
(\ast)
, Axiom B can be removed. For example, if the structure of the support is not so complicated, one may try to verify (
\ast
) for the general point of
every
support. Alternatively, nearby/vanishing cycle techniques were applied in
[
30
, Section 4]
to verify (
\ast
) for certain Lagrangian fibrations.

Enhanced Axiom C+,

In all the examples in Section
2.3
, the following enhanced version of Axiom C+ is satisfied:

Assume that
f:M\to B
and
f^{\vee}:M^{\vee}\to B
given by Axiom A are
\delta
-regular weak abelian fibrations in the sense of Ngô
[
35
]
with the commutative group schemes
P\to B
and
P^{\vee}\to B
. For any
P
-equivariant object
{\mathcal{F}}\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M)
and
P^{\vee}
-equivariant object
{\mathcal{G}}\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(M^{\vee})
, the objects

{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\circ\left(p^{*}{\mathcal{F}}\otimes{\mathcal{P}}^{\otimes N}\otimes q^{*}{\mathcal{G}}\right),\quad\left(p^{*}{\mathcal{F}}\otimes{\mathcal{P}}^{\otimes N}\otimes q^{*}{\mathcal{G}}\right)\circ{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}

have support of codimension
\geq g
for infinitely many integers
N\geq 1
. Here
p,q
are the natural projections from
M^{\vee}\times_{B}M
to
M^{\vee}
and
M
respectively.

The proof of
[
18
, Theorem 0.1]
gives the following theorem.

Theorem 3.10

(Ghosh
[
18
]
)
.

Assume that
f:M\to B
is a dualizable abelian fibration satisfying the enhanced Axiom C+. Then the Chern classes of the tangent bundle
T_{M}
satisfy

c_{i}(T_{M})\in P_{i}H^{2i}(M,{\mathbb{Q}}).

We refer to
[
18
]
for more details, which also proved some motivic versions of Theorem
3.10
.

3.5.
Lagrangian fibrations

We conclude the discussion with Lagrangian fibrations. They form a distinguished class among all abelian fibrations and, from the perspective of the decomposition theorem, exhibit behavior that is closest to that of abelian schemes.

We say that
f:M\to B
is a Lagrangian fibration if

(a)

both
M,B
are nonsingular,

(b)

f
is proper and flat, and

(b)

a general fiber
F
is an abelian variety of half dimension, such that there is a holomorphic symplectic form
\sigma
on
M
whose restriction to
F
vanishes.

We note that if
M
is a projective irreducible symplectic manifold and
0<\dim B<\dim M
, then
f
is automatically a Lagrangian fibration
[
25
]
. For our definition of Lagrangian fibrations, we do
not
assume that
M
is projective or proper.

Conjecture 3.11

(
[
4
,
40
]
)
.

Let
f:M\to B
be a Lagrangian fibration. The following hold.

(a)

The morphism
f:M\to B
admits a multiplicative decomposition theorem

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)},\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{(i)}:={{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}H^{i+\dim B}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{M})[-i-\dim B]\in D^{b}_{c}(B)

(b)

Let

H^{*}(M,{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i}H_{(i)}^{*}(M,{\mathbb{Q}})

be the cohomological decomposition given by an isomorphism in (a). Then

c_{i}(T_{M})\in H^{2i}_{(i)}(M,{\mathbb{Q}}).

If Conjecture
3.1
holds, then the perverse filtration for
f:M\to B
admits a multiplicative splitting of the perverse filtration as in (b). This was known when
M
is a projective irreducible symplectic manifold by
[
42
, Appendix A]
, or when
f:M\to B
is the Hitchin system, where it follows from the
P=W
conjecture; see
[
14
, Sections 0.3]
.

The reader may compare Theorem
3.10
and Conjecture
3.11
(b).

Example 3.12
.

For an abelian scheme
f:A\to B
, the Chern classes of
T_{A}
are pulled back from the base
B
. Therefore we clearly have

c_{i}(T_{A})\in P_{0}H^{2i}(A,{\mathbb{Q}}).

Conjecture
3.11
(b) predicts that, if
f:A\to B
is Lagrangian, then

c_{i}(T_{A})=0,\quad i>0.

This indeed holds by the Mumford relations for the Hodge bundle
[
34
, Corollary 5.3]
.

Finally, we note that Conjecture
3.11
is expected to admit a motivic enhancement: there exists a multiplicative motivic decomposition

h(M)=\bigoplus_{i}h_{i}(M),\quad\cup:h_{i}(M)\times h_{j}(M)\to h_{i+j}(M)

in the category of relative Chow motives over
B
, and the Chern classes satisfy

c_{i}(T_{M})\in\mathrm{CH}^{i}(h_{i}(M)).

We refer to
[
4
, Section 3]
for further discussions on connections to conjectures of Beauville and Voisin
[
8
,
43
,
44
]
.
