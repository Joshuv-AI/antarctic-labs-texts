Averaging Dynamics and Wong-Zakai approximations for a Fast-Slow Navier-Stokes System Driven by fractional Brownian Motion
Eliseo Luongo, Francesco Triggiano

Abstract
. We study a slow-fast system of coupled two- and three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations in which the fast component is perturbed by an additive fractional Brownian noise with Hurst parameter ℋ > 1 3 \mathcal{H}>\frac{1}{3} . The system is analyzed using rough path theory, and the limiting behaviour strongly depends on the value of ℋ \mathcal{H} . We prove convergence in law of the slow component to a Navier–Stokes system with an additional Itô-Stokes drift when ℋ < 1 2 \mathcal{H}<\frac{1}{2} . In contrast, for ℋ ∈ ( 1 2 , 1 ) \mathcal{H}\in(\frac{1}{2},1) , the limit equation features only a transport noise driven by a rough path.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, slow-fast systems associated with fluid equations have attracted considerable attention; see, for instance,
[
23
,
24
,
15
,
13
]
. The paradigmatic example to have in mind is provided by the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations on the three-dimensional torus, given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}du^{\epsilon}=\nu\Delta u^{\epsilon}dt-(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla u^{\epsilon}dt+\nabla p^{\epsilon}\,dt,\\
dv^{\epsilon}=\left(\nu\Delta v^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{-1}Cv^{\epsilon}-(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla v^{\epsilon}+\nabla q^{\epsilon}\right)\,dt+\epsilon^{-1}dW_{t},\\
\nabla\cdot u^{\epsilon}=0,\quad\nabla\cdot v^{\epsilon}=0,\end{cases}

where
W
is an infinite-dimensional Brownian motion satisfying suitable spatial regularity assumptions,
\epsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter governing the separation of scales between the
large
scales
u^{\epsilon}
and the
small
scales
v^{\epsilon}
, and
C
is a suitable damping operator. For simplicity, we restrict this heuristic discussion to the case
C=-I
. In the aforementioned works it was shown that, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the large-scale component
u^{\epsilon}
converges in distribution to a process
u
, which is a weak solution to the following stochastic Navier–Stokes equations:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}du=\left(\nu\Delta u-u\cdot\nabla u-\overline{r}\cdot\nabla u\right)dt+d\nabla p-\circ dW\cdot\nabla u,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{cases}

where
\overline{r}
is a deterministic vector field, referred to in the literature as the Itô-Stokes drift (cf.
[
47
]
,
[
14
]
), and is related to the spatial covariance structure of the noise
W
. Moreover,
p
is a semimartingale, often called the turbulent pressure, and the stochastic integral is understood in the Stratonovich sense.

The interest in results of this type is twofold. On the one hand, they are relevant for stochastic model reduction techniques in climate dynamics; see, for example,
[
46
,
27
,
28
]
. On the other hand, they provide a rigorous justification for the emergence of transport-type noise in fluid equations such as (
1.2
). Alternative approaches to those developed in
[
15
]
can be found in
[
38
,
47
]
. Over the last decade, starting from
[
19
]
, models like (
1.2
) have received increasing attention due to their regularizing effects (cf.
[
5
,
21
,
10
]
and the references therein), as well as their ability to capture key features of turbulent flows, including enhanced and anomalous dissipation phenomena (see for istance
[
40
,
18
,
45
,
51
,
16
]
) and dynamo effects (cf.
[
8
,
7
]
).

From a modeling perspective, however, it is important to account for non-Markovian noise with memory effects when describing turbulent fluids; see, for instance,
[
3
,
2
,
26
,
17
,
44
]
. This has led to a growing interest in the analysis of equations of the form (
1.2
), where the Brownian motion

[

,
37
,
11
,
50
,
32
,
25
,

]
. Nevertheless, as pointed out in
[

). A rigorous analysis of the original system when the driving noise
W
is replaced by a fractional Brownian motion
W^{\mathcal{H}}
with Hurst parameter
\mathcal{H}
is still missing. Indeed, all the aforementioned works crucially rely on the Markovian nature of
W
and on the availability of Itô-type formulas for the system (
1.1
).

Furthermore, although a substantial body of literature on slow–fast systems driven by fractional noise has recently emerged (see, for instance,
[

,
34
,
49
,
48
,
42
]
and the survey
[

), to the best of our knowledge none of these works addresses the specific issues discussed above. The present article initiates the study of this gap, see
Theorem 1.1
below for details.

Before presenting our main contributions, we briefly discuss the origin of the terms appearing in (
1.1
), as their justification plays a central role in our framework. We start from the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\overline{u}&=\nu\Delta\overline{u}-\overline{u}\cdot\nabla\overline{u}+\nabla\overline{p},\\
\nabla\cdot\overline{u}&=0.\end{cases}

We decompose the initial condition into large and small scales according to a suitable scale-separation rule,

\displaystyle\overline{u}(0)=u^{\epsilon}(0)+v^{\epsilon}(0),

where
\epsilon>0
is a parameter characterizing the separation of scales. Assuming that this separation persists for positive times, we introduce two velocity fields
u^{\epsilon}(t)
and
v^{\epsilon}(t)
, describing the evolution of the large- and small-scale components, respectively. Their dynamics are then governed by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}+(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla u^{\epsilon}+\nabla p^{\epsilon}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla v^{\epsilon}+\nabla q^{\epsilon}&=0,\\
\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=0.\end{cases}

At this stage, system (
1.3
) does not include either noise or damping at the level of the small scales. The introduction of an additive noise term in the small-scale equation is motivated by instabilities induced by boundary irregularities or internal obstacles, which are typically not explicitly modeled in mathematical formulations based on toroidal geometries or smooth bounded domains. We refer to
[
22
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed discussion. However, the presence of additive noise alone leads, via Itô’s formula, to an
infinite
mean energy injection into the system as
\epsilon\to 0
. To counterbalance this effect, an additional damping operator must be introduced in the small-scale equation. This modification yields precisely the structure of (
1.1
), leaving only the scaling of the noise and damping terms to be justified.

The fact that the noise and damping operators scale in the same way can be motivated in at least two different manners in the literature for systems driven by standard Brownian motion. The first argument, inspired by Wong-Zakai-type approximations, is that this scaling leads the small-scale dynamics to approximate white noise. More precisely, consider the process

dw^{\epsilon}_{t}=-\epsilon^{-1}w^{\epsilon}_{t}\,dt+dW_{t}.

It, formally, satisfies

(1.4)

\displaystyle w^{\epsilon}_{t}=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}\,dW_{s}\rightarrow dW_{t}

because, upon integrating the equation for
w^{\epsilon}
in time, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{t}=\epsilon\,w^{\epsilon}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}w^{\epsilon}_{s}\,ds\approx\int_{0}^{t}w^{\epsilon}_{s}\,ds.

This heuristic can be made rigorous using, for instance, rough path techniques; see
[
31
]
. Moreover, this argument is robust with respect to the choice of the Hurst parameter and therefore applies equally when
W
is replaced by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\mathcal{H}
.

A second motivation is provided by multiscale analysis; see
[
23
, Section 2]
for the case
\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
Appendix A
for its extension to the general case considered here. In contrast to the Wong–Zakai argument, this approach crucially depends on the self-similarity properties of fractional Brownian motion, which vary with the Hurst parameter. As a consequence, from this perspective the scaling
\epsilon^{-1}
in (
1.1
) is not universal. For general values of
\mathcal{H}
, the multiscale analysis instead suggests the scaling
\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mathcal{H}}
. In particular, the Wong–Zakai and multiscale scalings coincide only in the classical Brownian case
\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

In view of the discussion above, our primary objective is to rigorously analyze the asymptotic behavior, as
\epsilon\to 0
, of the system

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}du^{\epsilon}=\nu\Delta u^{\epsilon}dt-(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla u^{\epsilon}dt+\nabla p^{\epsilon}\,dt,\\
dv^{\epsilon}=\left(\nu\Delta v^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{-1}Cv^{\epsilon}-(u^{\epsilon}+v^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla v^{\epsilon}+\nabla q^{\epsilon}\right)\,dt+\epsilon^{-\alpha}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{t},\\
\nabla\cdot u^{\epsilon}=0,\quad\nabla\cdot v^{\epsilon}=0,\end{cases}

focusing on the interaction between the
large
- and
small
-scale components under stochastic fractional perturbations. Here
\nu>0
,
p^{\epsilon}
and
q^{\epsilon}
are pressure fields,
u^{\epsilon},v^{\epsilon}\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the large- and small-scale velocity fields, respectively, and
W^{\mathcal{H}}
is a divergence-free,
\mathcal{H}
-fractional Wiener process on
[L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})]^{d}
. The noise is spatially colored, with covariance operator
Q
, and has Hurst parameter
\mathcal{H}\in\left(\tfrac{1}{3},1\right)
. The operator
C
is linear. Precise assumptions on
C
and
Q
are given in
subsection 2.2
; here we only mention that
C
and
Q
are required to commute. Solutions to (
1.5
) are interpreted in the sense of unbounded rough drivers; see
[
4
]
and the recent review
[
35
]
. This notion is recalled in
subsection 2.3
.

As discussed above, there are in principle two relevant choices for the parameter
\alpha\in\{1,\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}\}
, corresponding to different noise intensities. For technical reasons (see the discussion following
Theorem 1.1
), for each fixed value of
\mathcal{H}
we are only able to treat one of these two regimes. Specifically, we work with the choice
\alpha=1\wedge(\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H})
, which yields a Wong–Zakai regime when
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
and a multiscale regime when
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
. As already noted, the two regimes coincide in the critical case
\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and this fact has important consequences for the structure of the limiting equation.

When
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
, we prove that
u^{\epsilon}
converges in law to a weak solution of the Young-type PDE with transport noise

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}du=\left(\nu\Delta u-u\cdot\nabla u\right)dt+d\nabla p-d(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\nabla u,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0.\end{cases}

In this Wong–Zakai regime,
v^{\epsilon}
converges, as expected, to a fractional white noise. However, in contrast to
[
15
,
13
]
, no Itô–Stokes drift appears in the limit. This absence is not due to isotropy assumptions on the covariance
Q
, as in
[
23
,
24
]
, but rather to the specific scaling of the noise.

On the other hand, when
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the noise intensity is too weak to yield convergence to fractional white noise. Nevertheless, the quadratic nonlinearity
v^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla v^{\epsilon}
still produces a macroscopic effect in the limit dynamics. In this case, we show that
u^{\epsilon}
converges in law to a weak solution of the deterministic PDE with an additional transport term,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u-\overline{r}\cdot\nabla u-u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{cases}

where
\overline{r}
denotes the Itô–Stokes drift and depends explicitly on
C
,
Q
, and
\mathcal{H}
; see
3.4
below.

It appears that only in the case
\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, where the Wong–Zakai and multiscale regimes coincide, do both effects, transport noise and Itô–Stokes drift, simultaneously emerge in the limiting dynamics, as observed in
[
15
,
13
]
.

Finally, solutions to (
1.6
) (respectively, (
1.7
)) are understood in the sense of
2.4
(respectively,
2.5
).

1.1.
Methodologies and Main Results

Similarly to
[
13
]
, we introduce the decomposition

\displaystyle v^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon}.

After projecting onto divergence-free vector fields via the Leray projector
\Pi
, denoting by
A
the Stokes operator
A:=\Pi\Delta
, and by
b(\cdot,\cdot)
the Navier–Stokes nonlinearity as rigorously defined in
subsection 2.1
, the components evolve according to the following system:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}=\nu Au^{\epsilon}-b(u^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon},u^{\epsilon}),\\
&dw^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-1}Cw^{\epsilon}dt+\epsilon^{-\mathcal{H}}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{t},\\
&\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}r^{\epsilon}&=\epsilon^{-1}Cr^{\epsilon}+\nu A(\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon})\\
&-b(u^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon},\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon}),\end{aligned}\\
&w^{\epsilon}(0)=0,\quad r^{\epsilon}(0)=v_{0}^{\epsilon}.\end{cases}

Solutions to this system are understood in the sense of
2.3
. We do not provide the details of the construction of solutions for fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, as these follow from classical arguments. In particular, the rescaled Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
w^{\epsilon}
exists on any probability space supporting the
Q
-fractional Wiener process
W^{\mathcal{H}}
, is uniquely determined, and is measurable with respect to
W^{\mathcal{H}}
. Moreover, for each fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a probabilistically and analytically weak solution to (
1.8
). That is, for every
\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exist a probability space
(\Omega_{\epsilon},\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon},\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon})
, a
Q
-fractional Wiener process
W^{\epsilon,\mathcal{H}}
, and processes
(u^{\epsilon},r^{\epsilon},w^{\epsilon})
solving (
1.8
) in the analytically weak sense. The proof relies on a Galerkin approximation combined with stochastic compactness arguments. In dimension
d=2
, where uniqueness holds as in the deterministic case, probabilistically weak existence can in fact be strengthened to probabilistically strong existence: as for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process alone, the triple
(u^{\epsilon},w^{\epsilon},r^{\epsilon})
exists on any probability space supporting the
Q
-fractional Wiener process
W^{\mathcal{H}}
.

As already mentioned, it is necessary to reformulate the system within the framework of rough path theory. This is carried out in
subsection 2.3
and
section 4
, and allows us to control the singular term
b(\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon},u^{\epsilon})
by introducing the process

\displaystyle y^{\epsilon}_{t}=\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}\int_{0}^{t}w^{\epsilon}_{s}ds

together with its canonical lifts. Here, in contrast to
[
13
]
, we cannot rely on Itô-type formulas to characterize the lifts, and instead must appeal to the theory of Gaussian rough paths (see
[
30
,
29
]
), especially in the case
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
. As already discussed, when
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
no Itô–Stokes drift appears in the limit, whereas for
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
this drift is the only surviving contribution. This dichotomy stems from the convergence properties of
r^{\epsilon}
, which ultimately rely on an ergodic-type result for
\int_{0}^{T}b(w^{\epsilon}_{s},w^{\epsilon}_{s}),ds
. While the Markovian structure makes this argument relatively standard when
\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}
(cf.
[
13
, Section 5]
), in the fractional case we must instead rely on ad hoc computations exploiting the explicit structure of the Navier–Stokes nonlinearity; see
section 3
. With these preliminaries in place, we can now state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in(\frac{1}{3},1)
, and let the initial data
u_{0}^{\epsilon},v_{0}^{\epsilon}
be divergence-free, of zero mean, and such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\|u_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}+\|\sqrt{\epsilon}v_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}<+\infty.

Assume that the operators
Q
and
C
satisfy the conditions stated in
subsection 2.2
. Then there exist probabilistically weak rough path solutions
(u^{\epsilon},(-C)^{-1}dW^{\mathcal{H},\epsilon})
to (
1.8
) that converge in law to a probabilistically weak rough path solution of (
1.6
) in the case
\alpha=1
and
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
, and to a probabilistically weak rough path solution of (
1.7
) in the case
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 1.2
.

In dimension
d=2
, thanks to the uniqueness of both the prelimit and the limit equations, the above statement can be strengthened. Under the same assumptions as in
Theorem 1.1
, there exist probabilistically strong rough path solutions
(u^{\epsilon},(-C)^{-1}dW^{\mathcal{H}})
to (
1.8
) that converge in probability to the unique weak rough path solution of (
1.6
) in the case
\alpha=1
and
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
, and of (
1.7
) in the case
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Note that even in the case
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}
, the limit object is, in principle, genuinely stochastic and supported on (possibly non-unique) weak solutions of (

).

We now discuss why the complementary regimes appear to be out of reach. In the case
\mathcal{H}>\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}>1
, neglecting the additional Navier–Stokes terms yields that the small scales satisfy

\displaystyle v^{\epsilon}_{t}=\epsilon^{-(\mathcal{H}-1/2)}\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\epsilon^{-1}C(t-s)}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{s}.

In view of (
1.4
), this leads to

\displaystyle v^{\epsilon}_{t}\approx\epsilon^{-(\mathcal{H}-1/2)}(-C)^{-1}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}\rightarrow+\infty,

showing that the small-scale component diverges.
The case
\mathcal{H}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=1
is more delicate. Here, the main difficulty is the lack of control of the quadratic nonlinearity in the equation for the small scales
v^{\epsilon}
. Nevertheless, for particularly simple choices of the noise, namely

\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x)=f(x)W^{\mathcal{H},1}_{t},

where
f
is divergence-free, of zero mean, and satisfies
f\cdot\nabla f=0
1
1

1

For instance, this holds when
f(x)=a_{l}\sin(l\cdot x),\ l,\ a_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ a_{l}\perp l
.
and

is a real-valued fractional Brownian motion, we can still establish the following result.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\alpha=1
,
\mathcal{H}\in\left(\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
, and let the initial data
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
and
v_{0}^{\epsilon}
be divergence-free, of zero mean, and satisfy

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\|u_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}+\|\sqrt{\epsilon}v_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}<+\infty.

Assume that
W^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x)=f(x)W^{\mathcal{H},1}_{t}
, with

and

satisfy the conditions stated in

. Then there exist probabilistically weak rough path solutions

We conclude this subsection by mentioning the recent preprint
[
42
]
, which appeared during the final stages of preparation of this work. There, the authors study a fast–slow Navier–Stokes system similar to (

), with

, focusing on the Wong–Zakai regime. While the slow dynamics coincide with ours, the fast dynamics differ substantially, being governed in

\displaystyle dv^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-1}Cv^{\epsilon}dt+\epsilon^{-1}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Let
H
be the subspace of

composed by zero mean, divergence-free vector fields, where

denotes the d-dimensional torus and

.

We denote by
\Pi
the Leray-Hopf projector of

\Pi=\mathcal{I}-\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\dvg
and by

and is well-known (see for example

We further denote by

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{n}}:=\langle(-A)^{n/2}v,(-A)^{n/2}v\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard scalar product in

.

In the following, we will denote by
b
the Navier-Stokes non-linearity, namely

We conclude this subsection by specifying the formalism of the rough path theory.

Any continuous function
\omega:\Delta_{T}:=\{(s,t):0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}\to\mathbb{R}
s.t.
\omega(t,t)=0
and super-additive, i.e
\omega(s,u)+\omega(u,t)\leq\omega(s,t)

For
p>0

, a continuous
1
-index map

-variation if

(2.1)

\|f\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{p}:=\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\Pi}\sum_{t_{i}\in\mathbf{P}}\|f(t_{i},t_{i+1})\|_{V}^{p}<\infty,

where
\Pi
denotes the partitions
\mathbf{P}:=\{t_{0}:=0,\dots,t_{N}:=T\}
of
[0,T]
, while
f_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}:=f(t_{i+1})-f(t_{i})
. Analogously, a
2
-index function
f:\Delta_{T}\to V
has finite
p
-variation if (
2.1
) holds with
f_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}:=f(t_{i},t_{i+1})
.
Let us recall that

\omega_{f}(s,t):=\|f\|_{p\textup{-var},[s,t]}^{p}
is a control. Whenever we want to highlight the Banach space under consideration, we will denote the
p
-variation seminorm as
\|f\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T],V}.

We consider the Polish space
C^{p\textup{-var}}(V)
(resp.
C^{p\textup{-var}}_{2}(V)
) given by the closure of smooth 1-index functions (resp. 2-index functions) with respect to the seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{p\textup{-var}}
.
Moreover, we denote by

the space of
2
-index map

\{I_{k}\}_{k}
of
[0,T]
where

has finite
p
-variation on any

.

For any Hilbert space
H
, let

be equipped with the standard Hilbert space structure.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
p\in[N,N+1)
for
N\in\{1,2\}
,
a
p
-rough path is a function

2.2.
Small Scales Assumptions

Regarding the noise structure, let

2.3.
Rough path formulation

In order to simplify the notation, now on we set

As already mentioned in

Remark 2.6

In

This suggests a slightly different notion of solution, where the rough integral is not interpreted in the sense of Davie and, in particular, through the URD formalism. \displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(s-r)}\left(\int_{1/\lambda}^{(s-r)^{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-2}\left(e^{\lambda z}-e^{-\lambda z}\right)dz\right)^{2}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(s-r)}\left(\int_{(s-r)^{\gamma}}^{s-r}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-2}\left(e^{\lambda z}-e^{-\lambda z}\right)dz\right)^{2}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(s-r)}\left(\int_{0}^{1/\lambda}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-1}dz\right)^{2}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(s-r)}e^{2\lambda(s-r)^{\gamma}}\left(\int_{1/\lambda}^{(s-r)^{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-2}\right)^{2}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(s-r)}|s-r|^{4\gamma(\mathcal{H}-1)}\left(\int_{(s-r)^{\gamma}}^{s-r}e^{\lambda z}dz\right)^{2}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{t}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}+1}}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{(r+1)\wedge t}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(1-\gamma)(s-r-1)}dsdr

(3.28)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}|s-r|^{4\gamma(\mathcal{H}-1)}\bm{1}_{\{s-r>1\}}dsdr\lesssim\frac{t}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}+1}}+\frac{t^{1+\theta}}{\lambda^{4}}.

Combining (
3.1
), (
3.1
) and (
3.1
) we obtain

(3.29)

\displaystyle K_{3,1,1}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{t}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}}}+\frac{t^{1+\theta}}{\lambda^{4}}.

Not surprisingly, also the analysis of
K_{3,1,2}
is similar to that of
K_{1,1,2}
. Indeed, by trivial estimates and change of variables we have

\displaystyle K_{3,1,2}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\int_{t/2}^{t}dr\int_{0}^{t-r}ds(1-e^{-2\lambda s})^{2}|s|^{4\mathcal{H}-4}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\int_{0}^{t/2}dr\int_{0}^{r}ds(1-e^{-2\lambda s})^{2}|s|^{4\mathcal{H}-4}ds

which corresponds exactly to (
3.1
), therefore by the very same computations we have

(3.30)

\displaystyle K_{3,1,2}(t)\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}+2}}+\frac{t}{\lambda^{4\mathcal{H}+1}}+\frac{t^{1+\theta}}{\lambda^{1+4\mathcal{H}-\theta}}.

Combining (
3.14
) with (
3.15
),(
3.16
),(
3.20
),(
3.1
),(
3.24
),(
3.25
),(
3.29
),(
3.30
) completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Infinite-dimensional case

In this section we treat the infinite-dimensional framework. Let
w^{\epsilon}
the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dw^{\epsilon}&=\epsilon^{-1}Cw^{\epsilon}dt+\epsilon^{-\mathcal{H}}dW^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}\\
w^{\epsilon}(0)&=0.\end{cases}

Under the conditions on
Q
and
C
described in
subsection 2.1
,
w^{\epsilon}
is well-defined and moreover the following holds:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in(\frac{1}{4},1)
, for each
q\geq 2

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1),\ t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{q}]\leq C_{T,q}.

Proof.

Due to the assumptions,
C
generates a strongly continuous semigroup

\displaystyle S(t)=\sum_{i}e^{\lambda_{i}t}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}

on
L^{2}
with infinitesimal generator
C
. Since the
W^{\mathcal{H},i}
are independent, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}(t-s)}\sigma_{i}e_{i}dW^{\mathcal{H},i}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left\|e_{i}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}(t-s)}dW^{\mathcal{H},i}_{s}\|^{2}\right]

Based on (
3.1
),
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}(t-s)}dW^{\mathcal{H},i}_{s}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}z}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-1}dz+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}(2t-z)}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-1}dz\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}t}\frac{\left(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{i}|t\right)^{2\mathcal{H}}}{2\left(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{i}|\right)^{2\mathcal{H}}}+\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-z}\|z\|^{2\mathcal{H}-1}dz}{(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{i}|)^{2\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{i}|)^{2\mathcal{H}}}.

Since we assumed
\sup\lambda_{i}<0
we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon^{-1}\lambda_{i}(t-s)}dW^{\mathcal{H},i}_{s}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{2\mathcal{H}}

and therefore for each
t\in[0,T],\ \epsilon\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left\|e_{i}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}<+\infty

and the claim follows due to Gaussianity.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

As the proof show it is enough to ask

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\|\lambda_{i}\|^{2\mathcal{H}}}\left\|e_{i}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}<+\infty,

so if the operator
C
gives some regularization we get less stringent restrictions. However since our main goal is to treat the case
C=-I
we do not stress this and not use any regularization coming from the semigroup generated by
C
that ultimately reduces only in less stringent assumption on the decay of the
\sigma_{i}
’s.

Next we show, similarly to equation (
3.1
), the validity of the following

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]
denoting by

\displaystyle\overline{r}=\int_{L^{2}}(-C)^{-1}b(r,r)d\mu(r),\quad\begin{cases}dw&=Cwdt+dW_{t}\\
w(0)&=0,\end{cases}

where
\mu
is the centered Gaussian measure on
L^{2}
with covariance operator

\displaystyle\overline{Q}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}}{2|\lambda_{i}|^{2\mathcal{H}}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}s^{2\mathcal{H}}ds

then it holds

(3.31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}(-C)^{-1}b(w_{s},w_{s})ds\rightarrow\overline{r}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;H).

Proof.

In order to ease the notation, let us call
X^{i}_{t}=\langle w_{t},e_{i}\rangle
, due to the independence of the
W^{\mathcal{H},i}
’s each
X^{i}
is Gaussian and they are independent if
i\neq j
. We can therefore write the left hand side of (
3.31
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{e_{k}}{|\lambda_{k}|}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{T}\langle b(e_{i},e_{j}),e_{k}\rangle X^{i}_{s}X^{j}_{s}ds\right),

while, calling
\overline{\sigma}_{i}:=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{2|\lambda_{i}|^{2\mathcal{H}}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}s^{2\mathcal{H}}ds
,
\overline{r}
satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{r}=\sum_{i,k\geq 1}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\frac{e_{k}}{|\lambda_{k}|}.

Therefore the claim corresponds to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{a^{k}_{T}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }T\rightarrow+\infty,

where

\displaystyle a^{K}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i,j\geq 1}X^{i}_{s}X^{j}_{s}\langle b(e_{i},e_{j}),e_{k}\rangle ds-\sum_{i\geq 1}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\right\|^{2}\right].

Obviously it holds

\displaystyle a_{T}^{K}

\displaystyle=I_{T}^{1}+I_{T}^{2}+I_{T}^{3},

\displaystyle I_{T}^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\geq 1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\langle b(e_{i},e_{j}),e_{k}\rangle\langle b(e_{i^{\prime}},e_{j^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{i}_{s}X^{j}_{s}X^{i^{\prime}}_{r}X^{j^{\prime}}_{r}\right]dsdr,

\displaystyle I_{T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\right)^{2},

\displaystyle I_{T}^{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}\overline{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\langle b(e_{i^{\prime}},e_{i^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{i}_{s}|^{2}\right]ds.

Therefore equation (
3.4
) implies

\displaystyle\|2I^{2}_{T}+I^{3}_{T}\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}}{|\lambda_{i}|^{2\mathcal{H}+1}}\langle b(e_{i^{\prime}},e_{i^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\langle b(e_{i^{\prime}},e_{i^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle=:\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,T).

Hence

\displaystyle|a^{K}_{T}|

\displaystyle\leq|I^{1}_{T}-I^{2}_{T}|+\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,T).

Exploiting Gaussianity and independence of the
X^{i}
we furthermore have

\displaystyle I^{1}_{T}=I^{1,1}_{T}+\mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T),\quad\text{where:}

\displaystyle I^{1,1}_{T}=\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{0}^{T}\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}^{i}|^{2}\right]ds\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T)=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}\frac{\langle b(e_{i},e_{i^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle\left(\langle b(e_{i},e_{i^{\prime}}),e_{k}\rangle+\langle b(e_{i^{\prime}},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle\right)}{T^{2}}\\
\ \times\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{i}_{s}X^{i}_{r}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X^{i^{\prime}}_{s}X^{i^{\prime}}_{r}\right]dsdr.\end{aligned}

Once more, by equation (
3.4
) and assumption (
2.5
) we have

\displaystyle\|I^{1,1}_{T}-I^{2}_{T}\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}|\langle b(e_{i},e_{i}),e_{k}\rangle|\right)^{2}=:\mathcal{R}_{3}(k,T).

So far we showed

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{a^{k}_{T}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,T)+\mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T)+\mathcal{R}_{3}(k,T).

Moreover the convergence of
\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,T)+\mathcal{R}_{3}(k,T)
can be showed easily. Indeed since
e_{k}
is a complete orthonormal system of
L^{2}
and
\xi>3
it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,T)+\mathcal{R}_{3}(k,T)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\left\|\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}b(e_{i},e_{i})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left\|e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{H^{\xi}}\right)^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{T}.

The convergence of
\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T)
corresponds to the analysis of
\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{s}J^{2}_{2}(s,r)drds
in the proof of
3.1
. Indeed, again since
e_{k}
is an orthonormal system of
L^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T)\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}

\displaystyle\left(\left\|b(e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime})\right\|^{2}+\left\|b(e_{i}^{\prime},e_{i})\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{s}\left\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i}_{s}X^{i}_{r}]\right\|\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i^{\prime}}_{s}X^{i^{\prime}}_{r}]\|drds\right).

Now, as shown in the proof of
3.1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{s}\left\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i}_{s}X^{i}_{r}]\right\|\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i^{\prime}}_{s}X^{i^{\prime}}_{r}]\|drds

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\left\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i}_{s}X^{i}_{r}]\right\|^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}+\frac{\left\|\mathbb{E}[X^{i^{\prime}}_{s}X^{i^{\prime}}_{r}]\right\|^{2}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{4}}drds

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\mathcal{R}(T)T^{2},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{T^{2}}+\frac{1}{T^{\theta(1-\theta)}}\quad&\text{if }\mathcal{H}\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})\\
\frac{1}{T}\quad&\text{if }\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}.

for each
0<\theta<2\mathcal{H}<1
. Let us stress that the hidden constant above depends on
\theta.
However once fixed a choice of
\theta,
then we have that
\mathcal{R}(T)\rightarrow 0
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{R}_{2}(k,T)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{R}(T)\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\geq 1}\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\left\|e_{i}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\left\|e_{i}^{\prime}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }T\rightarrow+\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Unbounded rough drivers convergence

Building on
subsection 2.3
, our goal is to analyze the convergence of the canonical rough path lift of

(4.1)

y^{\epsilon}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\epsilon^{-\alpha+\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}_{r}\,dr

as
\epsilon
tends to zero.
Since lower Hurst parameters entail a rougher fBm and hence a more intricate rough path structure, this analysis becomes more delicate when
\mathcal{H}<1/2
. We therefore begin with the simpler regime
\mathcal{H}>1/2
, adopting the strategy of
[
13
]
. The more involved case will instead rely on specific properties of Gaussian rough paths (see
[
30
,
29
]
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}>1/2
,
\alpha=1
. Then for
\epsilon\to 0
,
(y_{t})_{t}
converges to
(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
in
L^{q}(\Omega,C^{p\textup{-var}}(H^{\xi}))
for any
q\in[2,\infty)
and
p\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}}
.

Moreover, the following map

\epsilon\in(0,1)\mapsto y^{\epsilon}\in L^{q}(\Omega,C^{p\textup{-var}}(H^{\xi}))

is Holder-continuous and it holds that

(4.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\|y^{\epsilon}\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{q}\right]\lesssim 1.

Proof.

First step: Convergence.

Let us highlight the following two properties:

(1)

By the self-similarity of fBm, the process
z_{t}=w^{\epsilon}_{\epsilon t}
satisfies the equation
dz_{t}=Cz_{t}\,dt+\overline{W}^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
, where
\overline{W}^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
is distributed as
W^{\mathcal{H}}
.

(2)

Since
\alpha=1
, then

(4.3)

y^{\epsilon}_{t}=(-C)^{-1}[W_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}-\epsilon^{\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}_{t}].

Therefore, by applying
3.2
, we obtain that

(4.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}_{s,t}-(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s,t}\|^{2}_{H^{\xi}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|w^{\epsilon}_{s,t}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]\epsilon^{2\mathcal{H}}\lesssim\epsilon^{2\mathcal{H}}.

Now, we aim at showing

(4.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}_{s,t}-(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s,t}\|^{2}_{H^{\xi}}\right]\lesssim|t-s|^{2\mathcal{H}}.

For
r\geq 0
denote
\overline{z}^{s}_{r}:=z_{r+s}-z_{s}
. Then,
\overline{z}^{s}_{r}
solves an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SPDE with additional time-independent random forcing:

\overline{z}^{s}_{r}=\int_{0}^{r}C\,\overline{z}^{s}_{u}\,du+Cz_{s}r+W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s+r}-W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s}=\int_{0}^{r}C\,\overline{z}^{s}_{u}\,du+Cz_{s}r+\overline{W}^{\mathcal{H},s}_{r},

where
\overline{W}^{\mathcal{H},s}_{r}:=W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s+r}-W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s}
has the same law as
W^{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
. Therefore by exploiting the assumptions on
C
and equation (
3.1
), we have that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|(-C)^{-1}\overline{z}^{s}_{r}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k}\left|\int_{0}^{r}e^{\lambda_{k}(r-u)}du\right|^{2}\lambda_{k}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle e_{k},Cz_{s}\rangle^{2}\right]\|e_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\xi}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{0}^{r}e^{\lambda_{k}(r-v)}\sigma_{k}e_{k}\,d\overline{W}^{\mathcal{H},s,k}_{v}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{-2}|1-e^{\lambda_{k}r}|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle e_{k},z_{s}\rangle^{2}\right]\|e_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\xi}}^{2}+r^{2\mathcal{H}}\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{2\mathcal{H}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|z_{s}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]\right).

Then, equation (
4.5
) follows from

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|(-C)^{-1}\epsilon^{\mathcal{H}}w^{\epsilon}_{s,t}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]\lesssim\epsilon^{2\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|(-C)^{-1}\overline{z}_{\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}^{\epsilon^{-1}s}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]\lesssim\left(t-s\right)^{2\mathcal{H}}.

By interpolating equation (
4.4
) with equation (
4.5
), and using the Gaussian property, we obtain for any
\chi\in(0,1)

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}_{s,t}-(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}_{s,t}\|^{q}_{H^{\xi}}\right]^{1/q}\lesssim\epsilon^{\mathcal{H}\chi}(t-s)^{\mathcal{H}(1-\chi)},

that implies convergence by means of Kolmogorov’s theorem
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Second step: Proof of equation (
4.2
).

Notice that by integration by parts and by imposing
W^{k,\mathcal{H}}\equiv 0
on
(-\infty,0]
, we have that

\displaystyle y^{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\sigma_{k}e_{k}}{|\lambda_{k}|}(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{k}|)\int_{0}^{t}W^{k,\mathcal{H}}_{r}e^{\lambda_{k}\epsilon^{-1}(t-r)}dr

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\sigma_{k}e_{k}}{|\lambda_{k}|}(\phi^{\epsilon}_{k}\ast W^{k,\mathcal{H}})(t)=:\sum_{k}\frac{\sigma_{k}e_{k}}{|\lambda_{k}|}W^{\epsilon,k,\mathcal{H}}_{t},

where
\phi^{\epsilon}_{k}(r)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,T]}(r)e^{r\lambda_{k}\epsilon^{-1}}|\lambda_{k}|\epsilon^{-1}.

For
\epsilon<\delta
, it holds

W^{\epsilon,k,\mathcal{H}}_{t}-W^{\delta,k,\mathcal{H}}_{t}=z^{\delta,k}_{t}-z^{\epsilon,k}_{t}=:v^{\epsilon,\delta,k}_{t},

where
z^{\epsilon,k}_{t}
solves

dz^{\epsilon,k}_{t}=\lambda_{k}\epsilon^{-1}z_{t}^{\epsilon,k}dt+dW^{k,\mathcal{H}}_{t},

with
0
as starting condition. While,
v^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}
solves

dv^{\epsilon,\delta,k}_{t}=\lambda_{k}\delta^{-1}v^{\epsilon,\delta,k}_{t}dt+\lambda_{k}z^{\epsilon,k}_{t}(\delta^{-1}-\epsilon^{-1})dt.

Therefore,

(4.10)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}_{s,t}-y^{\delta}_{s,t}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{k}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(v^{\epsilon,\delta,k}_{s,t})^{2}\right]

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(v^{\epsilon,\delta,k}_{s,t})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{2}\epsilon^{-4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{s}^{t}e^{\lambda_{k}\delta^{-1}(t-r)}\lambda_{k}z_{r}^{\epsilon,k}dr\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+(\delta-\epsilon)^{2}\epsilon^{-4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{s}e^{\lambda_{k}\delta^{-1}(t-r)}-e^{\lambda_{k}\delta^{-1}(s-r)}\lambda_{k}z_{r}^{\epsilon,k}dr\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{2}\epsilon^{-4}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|z_{t}^{\epsilon,k}|^{2}\right]\delta^{2}\left(1-e^{\lambda_{k}\delta^{-1}(t-s)}\right)^{2}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{2}\epsilon^{-4}\lambda_{k}^{2}(t-s)^{2},

where, thanks to equation (
3.1
),

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|z_{t}^{\epsilon,k}|^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{\epsilon^{2\mathcal{H}}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{2\mathcal{H}}}\log\left(1+T(\epsilon^{-1}|\lambda_{k}|)^{\frac{1-\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{H}}}\right)\lesssim 1.

By equation (
4.10
), equation (
4
) and by applying Kolmogorov continuity theorem with respect to the temporal variable, we end up with

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}-y^{\delta}\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{q}\right]^{1/q}\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)\epsilon^{-2}.

At last, let
y^{0}=(-C)^{-1}W^{\mathcal{H}}
. Then for
\epsilon,\delta\in[0,1]
, we have that

(1)

if
2\epsilon<\delta
, equation (
4.8
) implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}-y^{\delta}\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{q}\right]^{1/q}\lesssim\epsilon^{\chi\mathcal{H}}+\delta^{\chi\mathcal{H}}\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{\chi\mathcal{H}};

(2)

if
\delta\in(\epsilon,2\epsilon)
and
\epsilon\leq(\delta-\epsilon)^{\nu}
, equation (
4.8
) implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}-y^{\delta}\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{q}\right]^{1/q}\lesssim\epsilon^{\chi\mathcal{H}}\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{\xi\mathcal{H}\nu};

(3)

if
\delta\in(\epsilon,2\epsilon)
and
\epsilon>(\delta-\epsilon)^{\nu}
, thanks to equation (
4.12
) we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{\epsilon}-y^{\delta}\|_{p\textup{-var},[0,T]}^{q}\right]^{1/q}\lesssim\epsilon^{-2}(\delta-\epsilon)\lesssim(\delta-\epsilon)^{1-2\nu}.

Therefore, the claim follows from properly choosing
\nu
and applying the Kolmogorov theorem w.r.t. the
\epsilon
parameter.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+\int_{0}^{T}\left\|r^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\gamma}}^{2}ds\right)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim 1

\displaystyle\lesssim 1

uniformly in

We split this section in two parts. In the first one we do compactness in space, therefore we focus on (

5.1.
Space Compactness

For
\epsilon>0
the integral appearing in equation (

) are standard Lebesgue integral and moreover the solutions have been obtained by Galerkin approximation. Therefore it follows immediately that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|u^{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}+2\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla u^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|^{2}ds\leq 2\left\|u_{0}\right\|^{2}.

Now we want to estimate

. This analysis depends to the case we are considering
\mathcal{H}>1/2
or
\mathcal{H}<1/2.

Case
\mathcal{H}<\frac{1}{2},\ \alpha=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}
.

Our solutions are obtained by Galerkin approximation therefore, by (
5.4
), Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities they satisfy the following relation

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{d\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}}{dt}+2\epsilon\left\|\nabla r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}+\left\|C^{1/2}r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon^{1/2}\langle b(u+\epsilon^{-1/2}w^{\epsilon}+r^{\epsilon},w^{\epsilon}),r^{\epsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\left\|\nabla r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\nabla w^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}+\delta\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+C_{\delta}\epsilon\left\|u_{0}\right\|^{2}\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+C_{\delta}\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}

\displaystyle+\epsilon C_{\delta}\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}-2\epsilon^{1/2}\langle\nabla w^{\epsilon},\nabla r^{\epsilon}\rangle.

Hence, by selecting
\delta

(5.5)

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{d\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}}{dt}+\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|_{H^{\gamma}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}+\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\epsilon\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}\left\|w^{\epsilon}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}.

Therefore Grönwall’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\epsilon\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|r^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\epsilon\left\|r_{0}^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}+C_{1}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}+\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}ds\right)e^{C_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}ds}.

Plugging this information in (
5.5

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\|r^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\gamma}}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\left(\epsilon\left\|r_{0}^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}+C_{1}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}+\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}ds\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1+e^{C_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}ds}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}ds\right).

In particular, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+\int_{0}^{T}\left\|r^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\gamma}}^{2}ds\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{e^{C_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}ds}}{2}\right)\bm{1}_{\left\{e^{C_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|w^{\epsilon}_{s}\right\|_{H^{\xi}}^{2}ds}\leq 1\right\}}\right]

Appendix A
Multiscale Model

Here we briefly recall, and adapt to our fractional framework, the arguments of
[
23
, Section 2]
.

We consider systems with three time scales. This statement should be understood as follows. There is a small time scale
\mathcal{T}_{s}
at which one observes variations and fluctuations of the velocity fields. There is an intermediate time scale
\mathcal{T}_{m}
at which these fluctuations appear random, though not yet as white noise; rather, they behave as random processes with a typical time of variation of order
\mathcal{T}_{s}
, which is small compared to
\mathcal{T}_{m}
. Finally, there is a large time scale
\mathcal{T}_{l}
at which the cumulative effect of the small-scale fluctuations becomes non-negligible. We assume that these time scales satisfy the relation

(A.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{T}_{s}}{\mathcal{T}_{m}}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{m}}{\mathcal{T}_{l}}.

As discussed in
[
23
]
, an ideal example of this situation is provided by atmospheric flows over a large region, restricted to the lower atmospheric layer interacting with ground irregularities such as hills and mountains. We idealize such a fluid by means of the three-dimensional Euler equations with forcing, written in velocity form as

(A.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=f,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{cases}

where
f
represents the generation of small-scale perturbations induced by ground irregularities, as discussed for instance in
[
22
, Chapter 5]
. For long-time investigations, it would of course be necessary to include additional realistic terms, such as a small viscous dissipation
\nu\Delta u
with
\nu\ll 1
, in order to dissipate the energy injected by
f
, as in the model (
1.5
). However, such refinements are not essential here, since our focus is solely on the scaling of the noise coefficients.

A.1.
Human Scale: Seconds

The human scale corresponds to observations made at distances measured in meters and over time intervals of seconds. Accordingly, we set
\mathcal{T}_{s}=1,\mathrm{s}
. The velocity field
u(t,x)
is measured in meters per second. We decompose the initial condition into large- and small-scale components according to some reasonable criterion,

\displaystyle u(0)=u_{l}(0)+u_{s}(0).

The small-scale component describes wind fluctuations over spatial distances of
1
–
10
m, while the large-scale component captures structures of regional extent, ranging from
10
to
1000
km. Motivated by (
A.2
), we split the evolution of the velocity field into the dynamics of the large and small scales as

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{l}+(u_{s}+u_{l})\cdot\nabla u_{l}+\nabla p_{l}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}u_{s}+(u_{s}+u_{l})\cdot\nabla u_{s}+\nabla p_{s}&=f,\\
\nabla\cdot u_{s}=\nabla\cdot u_{l}=0,\end{cases}

reflecting the assumption that the forcing
f
primarily generates small-scale structures. We stress that (
A.3
) is a modeling assumption and cannot be rigorously derived from (
A.2
).

A.2.
Intermediate scale: Minutes

If the same atmospheric flow is observed by a recording device with a temporal resolution of minutes, that is,
\mathcal{T}_{m}=1,\mathrm{min}
, the small-scale fluctuations described above appear random, while retaining the same spatial scale of
1
–
10
m. This motivates the modeling assumption that small scales can be described by a stochastic equation with memory, see also
[
46
,
26
]
. This leads to the system

(A.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{l}+(u_{s}+u_{l})\cdot\nabla u_{l}+\nabla p_{l}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}u_{s}+(u_{s}+u_{l})\cdot\nabla u_{s}+\nabla p_{s}&=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{m}}u_{s}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{m}^{\mathcal{H}}}\dot{W}^{\mathcal{H}},\\
\nabla\cdot u_{s}=\nabla\cdot u_{l}=0.\end{cases}

Neglecting the Navier–Stokes dynamics at small scales, the process

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{s}(t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{m}^{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{(t-r)}{\mathcal{T}_{m}}}dW^{H}_{r}

returns to equilibrium over a time of order
\mathcal{T}_{m}
, and its intensity is independent of
\mathcal{T}_{m}
.

A.3.
Regional scale: Hours

We now consider the same system, namely the lower atmospheric layer over a large region, observed at the regional scale, for instance by a satellite. Time is measured in hours,
\mathcal{T}_{l}=1,\mathrm{h}
, and spatial units range from
10
to
1000
km, in contrast to the meter-scale resolution relevant at the human and intermediate levels. These are typical scales for weather prediction.

We rescale (
A.4
) accordingly. Variables at the intermediate scale are denoted by
(t,x)
, while those at the regional scale are denoted by
(T,X)
. Since minutes and hours differ by a factor of
60
, we set

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}=60,\quad t=\varepsilon^{-1}T.

Similarly, we assume that spatial variables are related by

\displaystyle x=\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}X.

If
u
denotes the velocity at the intermediate scale and
U
the corresponding velocity at the regional scale, then

\displaystyle U(T,X)=\varepsilon_{x}\varepsilon^{-1}u\left(t\varepsilon^{-1},x\varepsilon^{-1}_{x}\right).

In these new coordinates, system (
A.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{T}U_{l}+(U_{s}+U_{l})\cdot\nabla_{X}U_{l}+\nabla_{X}P_{l}&=0,\\
\partial_{T}U_{s}+(U_{s}+U_{l})\cdot\nabla_{X}U_{s}+\nabla_{X}P_{s}&=-\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}}{\mathcal{T}_{m}}U_{s}+\frac{\varepsilon_{x}\varepsilon^{-2}}{\mathcal{T}_{m}^{\mathcal{H}}}\dot{W}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{x}}\right),\\
\nabla\cdot U_{s}=\nabla\cdot U_{l}=0.\end{cases}

The scaling condition (
A.1
), together with the scaling properties of fractional Brownian motion, implies
\mathcal{T}_{m}=\varepsilon
and yields

(A.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{T}U_{l}+(U_{s}+U_{l})\cdot\nabla_{X}U_{l}+\nabla_{X}P_{l}&=0,\\
\partial_{T}U_{s}+(U_{s}+U_{l})\cdot\nabla_{X}U_{s}+\nabla_{X}P_{s}&=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}U_{s}+\frac{\varepsilon_{x}}{\varepsilon^{1+2\mathcal{H}}}\dot{\tilde{W}}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(T,\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{x}}\right),\\
\nabla\cdot U_{s}=\nabla\cdot U_{l}=0,\end{cases}

for a new fractional Brownian motion
\tilde{W}^{\mathcal{H}}
. Up to the redefinition
\epsilon=\varepsilon^{2}
, system (
A.5
) exactly corresponds to (
1.8
) with the choice
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{H}
.
