Enhancing therapeutic decisions during robot-assisted gait therapy: current challenges and development of a novel app-based therapy protocol to address them
Authors: Florian van Dellen, Tabea Aurich, Rob Labruyère
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapists’ decisions on influencing patient behavior during robot-assisted gait therapy directly impact clinical practice and, thus, therapeutic efficacy. These manipulations include device parameter adjustments and direct interactions with patients. Little research has been done on how therapists accomplish this task, and knowledge about what constitutes good decisions is scarce. The scarce knowledge is partly due to missing tools to document these decisions. Understanding how therapists assess behavior, manipulate patient behavior, and re-assess behavior for potential improvement could lead to better therapy outcomes.MethodsWe developed a novel mobile application to document therapeutic decisions based on expert knowledge and literature findings. Each documented manipulation consisted of three parts: The evaluation of the patient behavior (goal-oriented and compensatory), the manipulation by the therapist (direct interaction or device interaction), and the re-evaluation of the behavior concerning the consequences of this manipulation. The resulting application was pilot-tested with 10 patients in 5 sessions and 7 therapists in a real-world clinical setting.ResultsTherapists selected more than two therapy goals per session for 50% of the training sessions. The results show that 92% of the manipulations were direct interactions and 8% device interactions. Sixteen percent of the direct interactions targeted the selected goal and 43% compensatory movements. The type of manipulation had a statistically significant impact on the rate of improvement at re-evaluation, with direct interactions targeting compensatory movements having the best rate of improvement (48%) and the parameter adjustments having the lowest rate of improvement (25%).DiscussionThe app developed is a novel approach to documenting therapists’ decisions. The data collected in the pilot study revealed several challenges in the decision-making process that therapists can currently only address based on their experience, including goal structure, the relative importance of goal-directed movements versus compensatory patterns, and the type of manipulation taken. Quantitative data provided by the app has the potential to objectify and improve these decisions and could support a transfer of successful treatment approaches between therapists and clinics. In conclusion, the developed mobile application tracks therapists’ clinical reasoning during robot-assisted gait therapy and provides data that can be used to promote evidence-based decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03242-x.Keywords: Decision support systems, Digital health, Rehabilitation, Neurologic gait disorders, Therapeutic strategies

Background

With a prevalence of up to 60%, mobility deficits are very common in patients with neurological diagnoses [1]. As such, improving mobility is one of the major rehabilitation goals. Traditionally, physical therapy plays an important role in achieving these goals. However, gait training requires a lot of physical effort by the therapists to secure and support the patients. Robots and other technologies are capable of taking over some of the manual work from the therapists [2, 3]. This prospect is why researchers, clinicians and industry have worked on these technologies for over 20 years. In that time, significant advances were made in understanding the prerequisites for successful therapy. Task-oriented practice is generally considered the best way to make functional gains [4, 5]. Often mentioned requirements for therapy success are also a high number of repetitions and active participation by the patients [6, 7]. Despite these advances, it is still not fully understood how robots best facilitate the therapy process. Although there is evidence that robot-assisted therapy can be effective, this form of therapy is still being discussed controversially [8, 9].

The widely accepted International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework contains different domains, including participation, activities, body functions and structures [10]. Within this framework, walking is assigned to the activity domain. However, in a recent article, Putrino and Krakauer argue that one reason for the heterogeneous evidence could be that walking in a robot is very different from walking in daily life. Consequently, robots might be better suited to reduce impairments on the level of body functions instead of directly improving gait related activities of daily living and should be evaluated as such [11]. A simple analogy would be that training in running will likely contribute to becoming a better football player, but it is by no means sufficient. Therefore, at most, small effects can be expected when the effect of training running is evaluated with football match performance. We generally agree with this argumentation, which also has important implications for the therapy process itself. Instead of walking being a single goal of robot-assisted therapy in the activity domain, this type of therapy should also consider goals on the level of body functions. The relevance of this paradigm becomes apparent when considering that patients sharing similar walking abilities (i.e., able to cover similar distances in comparable amounts of time) can substantially vary in their functional impairments. Such functional impairments include limited selectivity, coordination, strength deficits or spasticity of muscles or muscle groups. These functional differences might also pose different requirements for a device and its control strategy for robot-assisted gait therapy. Therefore, it is also unsurprising that several reviews failed to identify a single superior control strategy for robot-assisted gait therapy (RAGT) [12–14].

In general, there are two approaches to how the field of rehabilitation robotics can deal with this clinical heterogeneity to optimize therapy outcomes. On the one hand, hard- and software components are developed that can be better adapted to the functional impairments of individual patients. For example, Sommerhalder et al. have developed a flexible control framework to adapt upper extremity robots better to patients’ needs [15]. However, the flexibility comes at the cost of increased complexity, leading to many possible choices for a certain patient. On the other hand, the efficacy of existing devices or less complex devices could be improved by a smarter usage. In any of the two cases, users have to value the different options they have for a given patient and choose the best setting. This task is common for many different problems in the medical domain and is called clinical reasoning [16]. Clinical reasoning depends on various factors, such as therapists’ knowledge and experience, and it also includes the clinical and patient context [16]. Technical knowledge poses a particular challenge for therapists, who generally receive little to very limited education in that domain.

The field has responded to the lack of technical knowledge with attempts to increase automatization, e.g., by introducing assist-as-needed controllers [17]. These controllers relieve the therapists of optimizing certain parameters. Still, the current generation often uses a single cost function (i.e., they attribute the same value to the parameter independent of the patient characteristics). In addition, they neglect certain external influences, e.g., direct interactions between the therapist and the patient. This is far from clinical reality, where substantial variation can be found in how therapists value the available parameter settings even for the same patient [18]. One reason for this observation could be that the value therapists attribute to certain options depends on the specific goals on the level of body functions, of which there can be multiple per patient (i.e., unstable trunk and weakness of the legs). At the same time, it is known that direct interactions between therapists and patients substantially contribute to the behavior [19]. Combining direct interactions with device interactions might allow for multiple options leading to a similar outcome. Consequently, the automatization of the robots can be rarely found outside of a research context. Nevertheless, a better understanding of how therapists value the options a device offers and how this contributes to the therapy success would be important information both for developing new devices and features and for a better usage of existing ones. So far there is very limited evidence on clinical decision making with robots. Two qualitative studies have both investigated how therapists see the use of robots in general but only superficially touched instructions and parameter settings [20, 21].

In general, assigning a value to several options based on some context information to maximize therapy success would be an ideal task for supervised machine learning. Therefore, it is unsurprising that several attempts have been made in this direction. For example, a study by Kuo and colleagues showed that outcomes are related to the parameter settings as an average of whole sessions [22]. In another project, the clinics participating in the ARTIC network [23] collected step-by-step data on parameter settings of patients training with the robot-assisted gait trainer Lokomat (Hocoma, a DIH brand, Volketswil, Switzerland) on a large scale. Neither of the two attempts resulted in clinical recommendations. This can be pinned down to two major reasons: (a) The data collected did not contain any information about direct interactions between the therapists and the patients. (b) In both studies, it is impossible to extract information on the therapy content and the reasons why therapists selected it. In other words, the question of whether choosing a certain set of parameters is always useful or only in a certain context cannot be solved. Consequently, the data are incomplete and any sophisticated machine learning technique will lead to poor results (commonly called the “garbage in, garbage out” principle). With the currently used tools for data collection, the necessary features are inaccessible.

The primary goal of this work was, therefore, to develop a novel tool for comprehensive data collection during robot-assisted gait therapy. We identified that, for minimal interference with routine clinical workflows, a mobile application would be best suited to capture direct interactions in addition to adaptations of the parameter settings and the underlying reasons. The resulting application was then tested in a real-world clinical setting with pediatric patients with neurological gait disorders.

Methods

The project can be divided into three parts: (a) analysis of clinical practice, (b) application design and (c) pilot implementation. Firstly, the current clinical practice was analyzed to identify the necessary features of the database. Secondly, a mobile application was designed to collect these data to allow for a seamless integration into the clinical workflow of therapists. Thirdly, the app was used in 5 sessions of Lokomat therapy with 10 pediatric patients in a real-world clinical setting to test the feasibility of the data collection and demonstrate the informational content. The following sections describe each part in detail.

Part 1 – analysis of clinical practice

We invited experienced RAGT therapists from 4 different clinics and three different countries to an expert panel and discussed how they perform robot-assisted therapy. In order to be able to speak freely, independent of their employer, they were guaranteed confidentiality on their identity before the discussion. The discussion was conducted and recorded online using Zoom (Zoom Video Communications Inc., San José, California, US) in English by the authors FvD and TA. Questions were designed to cover two main topics: (a) the tasks therapists face during RAGT and (b) factors that help or hinder therapists in completing these tasks. The video recording was transcribed verbatim by FvD. Descriptive thematic analysis was used to analyze the content [24]. First, FvD and TA coded the transcript individually to ensure coding consistency. Then, they agreed on a common code book in a joint discussion. Subthemes and themes were identified and discussed using an inductive approach, i.e., they were generated by merging various codes. The results and formal evidence from the literature were then fused into a framework that contains the most relevant aspects of RAGT in terms of context, therapeutic manipulations and consequences.

The thematic content analysis of the discussion with the therapists revealed four themes, i.e., four main tasks therapists are engaged in during RAGT. These are patient selection, selection of functional goals, device interactions, and direct patient interactions. The four tasks depended on four different factors to different degrees: therapist-dependent, patient-dependent, device-dependent and environment-dependent.

A closer look at the tasks reveals a temporal hierarchy. While the patient selection and the overall set of functional goals can remain constant over longer periods and can span multiple therapy sessions, the device interactions and direct patient interactions change more frequently and vary within single sessions. This hierarchy also applies to the factors influencing these tasks. For example, therapists mentioned that the selection of patients depends on their diagnosis and results of clinical assessments, but also operational considerations like staff resources and device availabilities that warrant prioritizing patients. Similarly, therapy goals rely greatly on the patient’s participation, activity goals, and functional impairments. The corresponding data to these factors, like diagnoses or assessment results, are often readily available from electronic patient records and were therefore not considered in the framework for the app. Instead, the framework focused on more short-term decisions within a single session, which were the device interactions, i.e., parameter adjustments and direct patient interactions. Here, therapists mentioned that they generally evaluate the patient’s behavior concerning the specific functional goals they are working on. At the same time, they also consider any possible compensatory behaviors that might not be directly related to the functional goals. They then decide to provide direct feedback to the patient or adapt the device parameters. Finally, they assess whether their inputs led to the desired changes or if new/other inputs are necessary. The therapists iteratively go through this process during therapy. A visualization of this process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Framework of the workflow during robot-assisted gait therapy: Therapists constantly evaluate the movement state based on patient-specific goals on the ICF level of body functions. If necessary, they manipulate the behavior with a device interaction (change of parameter settings) or a direct interaction (haptic guidance, verbal feedback or visual cues). Part 2 – application design

Based on the framework from Part 1, a schema for a database was developed to capture the relevant states of the framework. The database was implemented with ObjectBox (Version 2.4.1, ObjectBox Ltd, Berkhamsted, UK). All data was stored locally without network access to ensure data protection. Then, a mobile application was developed around that database to collect the data with Flutter Version 1.0.0 (Google, Mountain View, US), allowing the app to be deployed on iOS and Android. The app was tested by 7 therapists and improved based on their feedback. The final version was then used for the pilot implementation.

The database consists of 5 separate objects that refer to each other. The structure is depicted in Fig. 2. The backbone of the database forms an object that directly maps the framework by splitting it into three parts, which is named “Therapy transaction”. First, the therapists need to evaluate the current context (Evaluation) and then decide how to manipulate patient behavior (either direct interaction or device interaction, termed manipulation). Finally, they need to evaluate the consequences of their manipulation (Re-evaluation). The relevant aspects of this workflow are reflected in the attributes of the object (Fig. 2). Each transaction is linked to a “Session” and a “Goal”. Each session is additionally linked to a “Patient” and a “Therapist”. A user interface was designed around this database schema.

Database structure. The database consists of 5 objects. The core is the therapy transactions that map the framework to the database. Each therapy transaction refers to exactly one session and goal (1), but each goal and each session can appear in many therapy transactions (∞). Similarly, each session refers to exactly one patient and one therapist, but patients and therapists appear in many sessions

After starting the app, users arrive at the homepage to select a therapist and a patient from the database (see Fig. 3 - A). Users can navigate to the corresponding page via a plus sign (B&C) to create new therapists or patients. When the choice of the therapist and patient is confirmed, a new session is added to the database and users are redirected to select a first therapy goal from the database (D). To add new goals, users can again navigate to the corresponding page via a plus sign (E). Once a goal is selected by clicking on it, the user is redirected to a new page to enter the evaluation of the patient and the manipulation by the therapist (F). The evaluation consists of closed-ended questions (yes/no) to check whether the behavior matches with the goal (Q1) and whether compensatory movements are present (Q2). If compensatory patterns were present, therapists can select the relevant ones by tapping the respective body part. An initial set of goals and relevant compensatory behavior movements were determined in a workshop together with seven therapists of the Swiss Children’s Rehab. Throughout the session, the app allows changing the functional goal, automatically changing Q1 accordingly. Users can select a device interaction or audio record a direct interaction with the microphone (F). Once a manipulation has been recorded, the app automatically redirects users to the re-evaluation page, which asks the therapist to re-evaluate Q1 and Q2 to assess changes to the behavior (G). The transaction is then added to the database and users are directed back to start a new transaction (F). A session ends by pressing the “home” button, redirecting users to P1.

Throughout the design phase, feedback from 7 therapists testing the app during therapy was collected, and the app was adapted accordingly before moving on to the pilot implementation. This included, for example, additional compensatory behaviors and additional features like the possibility to select multiple compensatory behaviors at once and the possibility to filter the goals for an easier search. For the pilot implementation, the design was frozen (see: https://github.com/flvde/RAGT-therapy-app).

Part 3 - Pilot implementation

The goal of the pilot implementation was to test the feasibility of using the app in a routine clinical environment and to demonstrate the informational content of the data collected. The implementation took place at the Swiss Children’s Rehab (Affoltern am Albis, Switzerland) with a LokomatPro. Ten in- and outpatients younger than 18 years were recruited by convenience sampling, i.e. all children assigned to Lokomat therapy were asked to participate in the order of their assignment. Since the goal was not to draw generalizable conclusions, but to show what kind of data can be obtained no special inclusion criteria were defined. The only inclusion criteria was that they were participating in Lokomat therapy at the time of their enrollment. All participants and their legal representatives filled out an informed consent. The study was assessed by the ethics committee of the Canton Zurich, which confirmed that it did not fall under the Human Research Act (Req. 2023–01421). All study procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

To characterize the patients, we collected their diagnoses, age, and overall walking ability with the Gillette Functional Assessment Questionnaire (GFAQ) [25] (see Supplement 1). Therapists used the app during five therapy sessions per participant without any further restrictions. Each session lasted 45 minutes and included donning and doffing of the device. Before using the app for the first time, all therapists received a short introduction and could use a test profile to familiarize themselves with the app. After each session, therapists were asked to fill out the NASA-TLX as a proxy for the effort using the app [26]. It is a well-established assessment quantifying the task load in 6 dimensions: Mental demand, temporal demand, physical demand, effort, performance and frustration. Each dimension was normalized to values from 0, minimal task load, to 100, maximal task load, and an average over all dimensions, as well as the standard deviation, was calculated.

The collected data were analyzed to evaluate the potential of the information collected with the app. The device interactions tracked were fused with the device logs downloaded directly from the robot to obtain the nature of the changes via corresponding timestamps. The recorded direct interactions were verbatim transcribed and labeled with the target of the interaction. To get a general overview of the informational content, we calculated summary statistics of the transactions overall, then for the evaluation, manipulations and their relationship and finally, the re-evaluation individually.

The number of goals per participant and the proportion of occurrence for the different goals and compensatory behaviors were calculated to analyze the evaluation phase. For the manipulations, the proportions of direct and device interactions were calculated per participant. The direct interactions were further split into three categories: (a) direct interactions where the target of the interaction directly matched the goal (goal-oriented direct interaction), (b) direct interactions where the target of the interaction matched a compensatory behavior (compensation-oriented direct interaction) and (c) other direct interactions, e.g., an instruction targeting knee extension to support the actual goal of an increased step length. This categorization allowed to investigate the relationship between the evaluation and the manipulations by calculating the proportion of manipulations explainable with the therapy goal or compensatory behavior per participant. Please note that this was impossible for device interactions as they do not contain information on the target per se.

Finally, the rate of manipulations leading to an improvement was calculated for the re-evaluation. The improvement was split into two distinct groups: (a) Improvements directly related to the goal of the transactions (goal-oriented improvement) and (b) improvements reducing compensatory behavior. If a manipulation improved both, they were categorized as goal-oriented improvements. The sum of these two rates is the overall improvement. The rates were calculated separately for each patient and manipulation type (goal-oriented direct interaction, compensation-oriented direct interaction, other direct interaction and device interaction), allowing statements like “35% of the compensation-oriented direct interactions led to an improvement for ID05”.

Based on the statements of the expert therapists and literature, we developed two hypotheses to test the influence of (a) the evaluation phase on the manipulations and (b) the influence of the manipulations on the improvement. These hypotheses were: (a) The proportion of explainable direct interactions would be significantly higher using both the therapy goals and the compensatory behavior as explanatory variables compared to only one of them. This hypothesis was motivated by the statements of the expert therapists during the analysis phase. (b) Direct interactions are associated with a higher proportion of improvements than device interactions. This hypothesis was motivated by a previous study with healthy participants showing that device adaptions had only a modulatory influence while instructions were the main drivers of movement adaptions [19].

Two linear mixed models were fitted to the data to test these hypotheses [27]. Random intercepts were allowed for the different participants in both models. This choice was made because it cannot be assumed that the improvement is independent of the patients. For model (A), the proportion of explained interactions was used as the dependent variable, and the manipulation category (goal-oriented, compensation-oriented, both) was used for the explanation as the independent variable. For model (B), the proportion of transactions with an overall improvement was used as the dependent variable and the manipulation category (goal-oriented, compensation-oriented, other, parameter setting) as the independent variable. The alpha level was set to 0.05%.

Discussion

We developed a comprehensive app that allows to evaluate the complex interaction between therapist, robot and patient and demonstrated that with the app, it is feasible to collect meaningful data during routine therapy.

The data collected during the pilot implementation showed considerable variability in the therapy content and the choices made by the therapists. This data supports the relevance and design of our framework and aligns well with what the expert therapists reported. It is noteworthy that there was some variability even within individual sessions. The data offer relevant insights even with the limited number of patients and sessions. The following sections will, therefore, outline the challenges associated with these findings and how the larger-scale data collected with our app could help to address them.

Goals

A total of 20 distinct goals were worked on for the 10 patients. In 50% of the sessions, more than two of these functional goals were selected by the therapists. This corresponds to the complexity of gait pathologies resulting from a combination of multiple functional deficits [28]. The challenge for therapists is to prioritize which goal to focus on constantly. However, the influence of the different goals on the therapy outcome is currently still unknown. Here, the data collected with our app could be used to investigate whether more therapeutic manipulations focusing on a specific goal lead to a greater improvement in the respective outcome, i.e., whether goal-specific dose-response relationships exist. There is also a temporal component that needs to be considered. It may be beneficial to work on a single goal for a longer period to achieve high intensities or to constantly switch between goals to make the therapy less boring and to target contextual interference [29]. This temporal structure was very difficult to analyze with existing tools, but data collected with the app makes it possible to study these effects in a real-world context.

Compensatory behavior

Our data showed that 43% of direct interactions were directly related to the selected goal, and another 15% were related to compensatory behavior. This clearly shows that the therapists considered both elements when designing the therapy content. However, this also poses a further challenge for the therapists. They have to balance between compensatory patterns and therapy goals. For example, patients might be given very little support to work on a primary goal with high intensity but at the expense of more compensatory behavior, or they might opt for more support with lower intensity but less compensatory behavior. The extent to which compensatory movements should be allowed is still an open question [30]. In addition, the data collected with the app could also provide valuable insights into the circumstances under which certain compensatory behaviors occur. This information could be valuable not only for therapists but also for device development.

Manipulations

Another interesting observation was that 92% of all manipulations by the therapists were direct interactions. This observation reflects the philosophy of the therapists in the concrete setting. It is in line with the key assumption motivating the development of the app, namely that robot-assisted gait therapy consists of much more than guided movements. Interestingly, ID04 and ID05 had a higher proportion of parameter adaptions. Looking at the individual data, it seems that ID05 spent a lot of time working on cardiovascular endurance, and many of the parameter changes were associated with gait speed. ID04 was working a lot on the knee extension during stance phase and body weight support was changed more frequently. Data collected with the app could help find more such patterns and determine if they are consistent across multiple patients. Most of the direct interactions were used to verbally relay information on the expected behavior to the patients in a few cases with additional haptic or visual guidance by the therapists (see Supplement A). In principle, relaying such information could also be done via exergames or other computerized feedback. However, the Lokomat used during the pilot implementation offered only limited possibilities and therapists rarely used them during our pilot implementation. A potential explanation for this observation could be that in contrast to the therapists, these games and feedback strategies do not account for any compensatory behavior, which could explain the reluctance of therapists to use them. In addition, the exergames could also be not specific enough to trigger directed responses the therapists expect [19]. Here, data collected with the app could also help to identify which forms of direct interactions are more frequently used and more successful than others in changing the behavior, which would be valuable knowledge for therapists but could also help to improve computerized feedback and exergames. The statistical analysis confirmed our hypothesis that both goal-oriented and compensatory behavior are important in manipulating patient behavior. However, the goal and compensatory behavior alone could not explain 42% of the instructions. This indicates that there are relationships that are not covered by the evaluation. Since the data collected stems from a pediatric population, one potential aspect could be that some complex instructions were simplified to adapt them to the cognitive level of the patients. One example is that the goal “Knee extension terminal swing” was often relayed as “Take a long step!” (see Fig. 6). The goal and the instruction are closely related but not equivalent because, technically, taking a longer step does not require a better knee extension. Participants could also achieve this through the hip joint. Data collected with the app could help to identify such relationships between goals and direct interactions. Especially step length was often a target of direct interactions, but it was not the primary goal (see Fig. 6). One reason might be that focusing on other goals often came at the cost of a decreased step length and therapist tried to counteract this effect.

Improvements

The results showed that around 49% of the compensation-oriented direct interactions improved the immediate behavior during therapy. For the goal-oriented movements, this percentage dropped to 36% (although this drop was non-significant). Device interactions only led to an improvement of 24%. This agrees with previous findings showing that specific instructions better influenced behavior than parameter adaptions alone [19]. However, the large variability between patients and their limited number does not allow definitive conclusions. Considering that, for most of the participants, some manipulations were effective and some not, therapists have to rely on their experience to optimize the interactions with the patient to influence the patient’s behavior in a desired way. Here, data from the app could help to identify manipulations that are more successful in a certain context, e.g., certain direct interactions that work only under certain device settings. This could lower the number of trial-and-error manipulations necessary to identify what works and boost the efficiency of clinical reasoning. Thereby, the effective therapy time of patients could be increased.

Task load

In general, therapists coped well with the app during therapy. Compared to the literature, the overall task load was around the 25th percentile of most tasks [31]. On average, the domains, performance, effort and temporal demand, contributed most to the task load. One interesting observation is that the performance domain indicates it was not possible to perfectly document therapy. There are several reasons for this. On the one hand, during therapy with children, there is a strong psychological component that does not directly relate to the therapy content, e.g., the patient getting distracted. This could be especially pronounced in children. On the other hand, the app per se is a standardized workflow that does not perfectly cover all eventualities. However, this standardization was necessary to streamline the app workflow. More versatile data entry options would be possible, but this would come at the cost of a more complex user interface and, thus, a higher time demand for using it. As the possibility to use the app during routine therapy was a key requirement and the time demand was already one of the other important drivers of the task load, the current application likely sits at a sweet spot between complexity and demand for the therapist. Although fully automated data collection would be one option, this would come at the cost of some information loss. One example of such an informational loss is that the goal that the therapists pursue is not always a 1:1 mapping of the manipulation they take, as was shown by the data collected for this study (see Fig. 6).

Implications

Overall, the pilot implementation successfully highlighted many open questions regarding the use of robot-assisted therapy with data. In the absence of standardized procedures, which coincides with a very limited evidence landscape of how robot-assisted gait therapy should be designed [32], therapists’ clinical reasoning depends on experience-based heuristics and a knowledge transfer from more traditional therapy forms. The results of the pilot implementation also highlight the diversity between patients and the therapy content they receive. We argue that the heterogeneous results of clinical trials might, to some extent, be a direct consequence of these two factors. With the app, we provide researchers and clinicians with new ways to document the content of this therapy type and to improve it by tackling some of the discussed challenges.

Due to the structured database, collected data could be easily shared between centers, leading to sufficiently large amounts of data that can be used to study and assist therapists during robot-assisted gait therapy. While the database structure ensures that data could be efficiently shared, it is flexible enough to be adapted to user needs. Additional objects or fields can be added without impacting the existing data structure. One example would be to add the device as an object to compare how the usage changes for different devices or to compare new features added by an updated firmware.

Nevertheless, some improvements could be made to the current version, which would be advantageous. Firstly, the current version was not directly connected to the device. Thus, the device interactions were not directly recorded, but the device log and the app data were fused based on timestamps. A direct integration would eliminate this processing step and could prevent data errors due to erroneous synchronization. Secondly, the voice recordings collected during the therapies were manually transcribed during the data collection stage. This was necessary because therapists spoke a Swiss German dialect for which no good speech-to-text models were available at the time of the analysis. However, with the current leaps in improving large language models, automatic transcriptions with good accuracy are possible for many languages. Thirdly, the data were currently stored offline on the device, so frequent downloads were necessary to prevent potential data loss. This choice was made for privacy reasons in the context of this study. Still, Objectbox natively supports synchronization with servers, which would be beneficial for a large-scale implementation during routine therapy.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge that data from only 10 patients in the context of pediatric rehabilitation were collected during the pilot implementation. Thus, the data strongly mirror this context. This impedes the generalizability of the results. Choosing this population with its complexity and varying severities was a deliberate decision to test the framework under challenging conditions for the therapists. For the NASA-TLX therapists were quite confident for most therapies that they did a good job in documenting. Future studies intending to use data obtained with the app could take such questions into account to get an estimate for the “uncertainty” of the data. During the discussions with experts to develop the framework, one expert therapist working with adults who suffered from a stroke was involved, and she did not report a deviating workflow. Thus, it is likely that similar challenges also exist in other populations. Furthermore, the 7 therapists using the app had a variable experience. Consequenty, it is possible that some of the variability in therapy content seen stems from a variable experience. Further studies should evaluate how the experience influences the therapy content. Since the purpose of the current study was mainly to highlight what kind of information can be obtained with the app and its potential for further research, this does not have a direct impact on the conclusions of the current work.In addition, the app was only tested in the context of one device and the data apparently reflect this. While other devices might pose other challenges and be suitable for different goals, the framework is general enough to be used for any kind of therapeutic device for gait rehabilitation and to compare them. Fine-tuning the app to specific settings could help make the workflow slightly more intuitive and improve handling, but it should be considered that this makes it harder to compare data from different settings. Finally, the data collected relies on the therapist judgment. This can be subjective and is an important limitation, but the therapist is also the gold standard for decisions taken during therapy and key for high quality. Consequently, it makes sense to include the therapists’ decisions in the equation. Relying on the therapist judgment poses the advantage that, besides the mobile phone with the app, no additional equipment is necessary and collecting data is feasible without expensive measurement equipment. Nevertheless, future studies relating the therapist judgment during evaluation/re-evaluation to objective measurements would be an interesting addition.

Conclusion

This work presented the development and pilot implementation of a mobile application to provide insights into clinical reasoning during robot-assisted gait therapy. The data collected during the pilot implementation provide unique insights into challenges that were previously only anecdotally accessible, and to solve these challenges, therapists have to rely on their experience. The analysis highlights the substantial variability in specific functional therapy goals, the therapeutic manipulations based on these goals and the impact of these manipulations on behavior. The data collected with the app could provide a way to support therapists in their use of the devices and explore the potential for further automation. This would offer the opportunity to support clinical reasoning with evidence and could help to increase the effectiveness of this form of therapy.
