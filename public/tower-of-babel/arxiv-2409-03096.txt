Coxeter groups and Billey-Postnikov decompositions
Suho Oh, Edward Richmond

Abstract
. In this chapter, we give an overview of Billey-Postnikov (BP) decompositions which have become an important tool for understanding the geometry and combinatorics of Schubert varieties. BP decompositions are factorizations of Coxeter group elements with many nice properties in relation to Bruhat partial order. They have played an important role in the classification and enumeration of smooth Schubert varieties. They have also been used in the study of inversion hyperplane arrangements and permutation pattern avoidance. We survey many of these applications.



1.
Introduction

Coxeter groups and their combinatorics play a vital role in the study of Lie groups, flag varieties, and Schubert varieties. Of particular importance are the length function and Bruhat partial order (also called Bruhat-Chevalley order) on a Coxeter group. Geometrically, the length of an element equals the dimension of the corresponding Schubert variety, and the Bruhat order gives closure relations of Schubert cells in a flag variety. Let
W
be a Coxeter group, and for any
w\in W
, we define the Poincaré polynomial

P_{w}(q):=\sum_{u\leq w}q^{\ell(w)}

where
\ell:W\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denotes the length function and
\leq
denotes Bruhat order. If
W
is the Weyl group of a complex reductive Lie group, then
P_{w}(q^{2})
is the topological Poincaré polynomial with respect to singular cohomology of the corresponding Schubert variety
X(w)
. The central problem we address is to characterize when the polynomial
P_{w}(q)
factors in some nice or natural way.

When
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is the permutation group, Gasharov proved in
[
20
]
that the polynomial
P_{w}(q)
is a product of factors of the form
(1+q+\cdots+q^{r})
if and only if
w
avoids the permutation patterns 3412 and 4231.
This is a generalization of the well known result that the Poincaré series on the full permutation group is

(1)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\ell(w)}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+q+\cdots+q^{k}).

Chevalley
[
15
]
and Solomon
[
43
]
prove that the Poincaré series of any finite Coxeter group has a similar factorization as in Equation (
1
), so we can ask if there is an analogue of Gasharov’s factorization theorem for elements of other groups. In
[
29
]
, Lakshmibai and Sandya prove that a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
avoiding the patterns 3412 and 4231 is equivalent to the Schubert variety
X(w)
being smooth, and hence Gasharov’s factorization theorem is a property of “smooth” permutations. As it turn out, nice Poincaré polynomial factorizations hold for not only smooth permutations, but “smooth” elements of any Coxeter group of finite Lie-type. We summarize these properties of (rationally) smooth elements in Section
3
. Of critical importance to these results is the notion of what is now called a Billey-Postnikov (BP) decomposition. BP decompositions are certain factorizations of group elements with many nice properties in relation to intervals in Bruhat order. The name “BP” comes from the paper
[
5
]
where Billey and Postnikov use these decompositions to give a root-theoretic criterion for the rational smoothness of Schubert varieties. However, the idea of these decompositions dates back to earlier works such as
[
6
]
and
[
20
]
. BP decompositions have had numerous applications and have appeared in the study of fiber bundle structures of Schubert varieties
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
3
]
, inversion hyperplane arrangements
[
33
,
35
,
41
,
32
]
and permutation pattern avoidance
[
2
,
19
,
3
]
. The purpose of this chapter is to provide an overview of BP decompositions and their applications.

We structure this chapter as follows. In Section
2
, we review the basic properities of Coxeter groups and define BP decompositions. In Section
3
we given an overview of the how BP decompositions are used to study rationally smooth elements of Coxeter groups. In Section
4
, we discuss the applications of BP decompositions to inversion hyperplane arrangments. In Section
5
we state and prove how BP decomposition correspond to fiber bundle structures on Schubert varieties. In Section
6
, we look at iterated BP decompositions and how they are modeled by staircase diagrams. One application of staircase diagrams is that they can used to enumerate smooth and rationally smooth Schubert varieties. In Section
7
, we focus on permutation groups and discuss how BP decompositions are connected to permutation pattern avoidance. Finally, in Section
8
we state some open questions and possible future directions for the study of BP decompositions.

Our hope is that this chapter will provide readers insights on the nature of BP decompositions and how they are applied. Since this is a survey article, many statements will be given without proof. If a proof is not provided, then we will either have brief outlines of the proof or have references provided. Many results and concepts will have illustrating examples.

2.
Background on Coxeter groups

We review several foundational properties of Coxeter groups. For more details, we refer readers to
[
9
]
. Let
W
be a Coxeter group with simple generating set
S
. In other words,
S
is a finite set and
W
is the group generated by
S
where for any
s,t\in S
, we have a relation

(st)^{m_{st}}=e

for some
m_{st}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\cup\{\infty\}
where
m_{st}=1
if and only if
s=t
. We say an expression of
w\in W
in the simple generators

w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}

is
reduced
if
w
cannot be expressed in fewer generators. The length of any reduced expression is unqiue, so we define the function
\ell:W\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
which maps
w\in W
to the length of any reduced expression. We call the value
\ell(w)
the
length
of
w
.

Let
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
be a reduced expression. We say that
u\leq w
in the
Bruhat order
if there exists a subsequence
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j})\subseteq(1,\ldots,k)
such that
u=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{j}}
is a reduced expression for
u
. We remark that this definition is known as the sub-word property as Bruhat order has other equivalent definitions (see
[
9
, Theorem 2.2.2]
).

One particularly important family of Coxeter groups are the permutation groups on integers
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We will use the notation
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
when focusing on permutations. As a Coxeter group,
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
has a simple generating set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
where
s_{i}
corresponds the simple transposition that swaps
i
and
(i+1)
. These generators satisfy the Coxeter relations

s_{i}^{2}=(s_{i}s_{j})^{2}=(s_{i}s_{i+1})^{3}=e\quad\text{for all $|i-j|>1$.}

The permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is referred to as the Coxeter group of type
A_{n-1}
.

2.1.
Quotients and parabolic decompositions

In this section, we discuss parabolic quotients of Coxeter groups.
First, we say that a product
w=xy
is a
reduced factorization
if
\ell(w)=\ell(x)+\ell(y)
. Let
J\subseteq S
and let
W_{J}
denote the subgroup of
W
generated by the set
J
. Subgroups of form
W_{J}
are called
parabolic subgroups
of
W
. Each left coset
wW_{J}
has a unique representative of minimal length. The set of minimal coset representatives can be defined as

W^{J}:=\{w\in W\ |\ ws>w\text{ for all }s\in J\}.

The next proposition is from
[
9
, Proposition 2.4.4]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
J\subseteq S
. Then the following hold:

(1)

Every
w\in W
has a unique factorization
w=vu
such that
v\in W^{J}
and
u\in W_{J}
.

(2)

The decomposition
w=vu
is a reduced factorization. In other words,

\ell(w)=\ell(v)+\ell(u).

We call the decomposition
w=vu
in Proposition
2.3
the
parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
. We remark that each
w\in W
also has a “left-sided” parabolic decomposition
w=uv
where
v
denotes a minimal length representative of the right coset
W_{J}w
. If needed, we denote this set of minimal length representative by
{}^{J}W
. However, the convention we take is that parabolic decompositions will be “right-sided” decompositions
w=vu
where
v
is the minimal element of
wW_{J}
and
u\in W_{J}
.

One consequence of Proposition
2.3
is that the coset decomposition of the group

W=\bigsqcup_{v\in W^{J}}vW_{J}

respects length in the sense that

\sum_{w\in W}q^{\ell(w)}=\left(\sum_{v\in W^{J}}q^{\ell(v)}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{u\in W_{J}}q^{\ell(u)}\right).

It is natural to ask if the analogous coset decomposition of the interval

[e,w]=\bigsqcup_{v\in W^{J}}[e,w]\cap vW_{J}

gives a similar factorization of the Poincaré polynomial
P_{w}(q)
. To make this question precise, we define relative Bruhat intervals and relative Poincaré polynomials. For any
J\subset S
and Bruhat interval
[u,v]
, define the
Bruhat interval relative to
J
as

[u,v]^{J}:=[u,v]\cap W^{J}.

For any
v\in W^{J}
, we define the
Poincaré polynomial relative to
J
as

P^{J}_{v}(q):=\sum_{z\in[e,v]^{J}}q^{\ell(z)}.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
J\subseteq S
. We say the parabolic decomposition
w=vu
such that
v\in W^{J}
and
u\in W_{J}
is a
Billey-Postinkov (BP) decomposition with respect to
J
if the Poincaré polynomial factors as

P_{w}(q)=P_{v}^{J}(q)\cdot P_{u}(q).

Proposition
2.3
implies that
w=vu
is a BP decomposition with respect to
J
if and only if there is a graded poset isomorphism:

[e,v]^{J}\times[e,u]\simeq[e,w]

where
(v^{\prime},u^{\prime})\mapsto v^{\prime}u^{\prime}
.

2.2.
Characterizing BP decompositions

Our next goal is to list a several combinatorial characterizations of a BP decomposition and prove they are equivalent. For any
w\in W
, define the
support of
w
as the set

S(w):=\{s\in S\ |\ s\leq w\}.

The support of
w
can be viewed as the set of generators needed to make any reduced expression of
w
. We also define the
left and right descent sets
of
w
as

D_{L}(w):=\{s\in S\ |\ \ell(sw)\leq\ell(w)\}

and

D_{R}(w):=\{s\in S\ |\ \ell(ws)\leq\ell(w)\}.

These decent sets can be thought of as the set of generators appearing on the left (respectively right) of some reduced expression of
w
. Observe that
D_{L}(w)=D_{R}(w^{-1})
. For example, if
w=s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}s_{3}\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}
,
then

S(w)=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\},\quad D_{L}(w)=\{s_{1},s_{2}\},\quad\text{and}\quad D_{R}(w)=\{s_{1},s_{3}\}.

The following characterization theorem appears in
[
37
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
J\subset S
and let
w=vu
be a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

w=vu
is a BP decomposition.

(2)

The map
[e,v]^{J}\times[e,u]\rightarrow[e,w]
given by
(v^{\prime},u^{\prime})\mapsto v^{\prime}u^{\prime}
is bijective.

(3)

u
is maximal in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}.

(4)

S(v)\cap J\subseteq D_{L}(u).

Proof.

We prove the theorem by showing the equivalencies:
(1)\leftrightarrow(2)
,
(2)\leftrightarrow(3)
, and
(3)\leftrightarrow(4)
.

Proof of
(1)\leftrightarrow(2)
: By Proposition
2.3
, the multiplication map given in part (2) is length preserving and injective. Hence, part (2) says the interval
[e,w]
decomposes as a product of posets
[e,v]^{J}\times[e,u]
. This implies that part (1) is equivalent to part (2).

Proof of
(2)\leftrightarrow(3)
: We first assume the multiplication map is surjective (and hence bijective). Then
[e,u]=[e,w]\cap W_{J}
and hence
u
must be the maximal element in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
. Conversely, assume
u
is maximal in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
and let
x\in[e,w]
. Let
x=v^{\prime}u^{\prime}
denote the parabolic decomposition of
x
with respect to
J
. By
[
9
, Proposition 2.5.1]
, since
x\leq w
, we have
v^{\prime}\leq v
. But
u
is maximal in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
and hence
u^{\prime}\leq u
. Thus the multiplication map is surjective.

Proof of
(3)\leftrightarrow(4)
: First suppose that
u
is maximal in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
and
s\in S(v)\cap J
. Then
su\in[e,w]\cap W_{J}
and by the maximality of
u
, we must have
su\leq u
. Hence
s\in D_{L}(u)
. Conversely, suppose that
S(v)\cap J\subseteq D_{L}(u)
and hence we can write a reduced factorization
u=u_{0}u^{\prime}
where
u_{0}
is the maximal element in
W_{S(v)\cap J}.
Let
x\in[e,w]\cap W_{J}
. Since
x\leq w=vu=(vu_{0})u^{\prime}
, we can write a reduced factorization
x=u_{1}u_{2}
where
u_{1},u_{2}\in W_{J}
with
u_{1}\leq vu_{0}
and
u_{2}\leq u^{\prime}
. In particular, we have
u_{1}\in W_{S(v)\cap J}
and hence
x=u_{1}u_{2}\leq u_{0}u^{\prime}=u
. Thus
u
is maximal in
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
.
∎

While parts (3) and (4) of Theorem
2.7
seem like less conventional ways to describe a BP decomposition, we will see in the subsequent sections these characterizations are very useful when working with BP decompositions.

3.
Rationally smooth elements of Coxeter groups

In this section, we discuss BP-decompositions and “rationally smooth” elements of Coxeter groups.

Definition 3.1
.

We say
w\in W
is
rationally smooth
if the coefficients of Poincaré polynomial

P_{w}(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell(w)}a_{i}q^{i}

satisfy

for all

P_{w}(q)
is a palindromic polynomial.

Similarly, we say
v\in W^{J}
is

if
P_{v}^{J}(q)
is a palindromic polynomial.

For example,
w=s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}

w=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}s_{1}

is not rationally smooth. Note that rational smoothness and rational smoothness with respect to

do not imply each other.

well-defined for elements in any Coxeter group, even if there is no corresponding Schubert variety. Any smooth variety is rationally smooth for topological reasons, however the converse is not true. If

3.1.
BP decompositions of rationally smooth elements

The next theorem connects BP decompositions to rationally smooth permutations and is a rephrasing of results due to Gasharov in
[
20
]
and, independently, due to Lascoux in
[
30
]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

. Then
w
is smooth if and only if either
w
or
w^{-1}
has a BP decomposition
vu
with respect to
J=S\setminus\{s_{n-1}\}
where

P_{w}(q)=(1+q+\cdots+q^{\ell(v)})\cdot P_{u}(q)

and
u\in W_{J}\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
is smooth.

In Theorem
3.3
, the relative interval
[e,v]^{J}
is a chain and hence relative Poincaré polynomial

P^{J}_{v}(q)=1+q+\cdots+q^{\ell(v)}.

Hence

is rationally smooth with respect to
J
. Polynomials of this form will come up frequently, so we use
q
-integer notation

[r]_{q}:=1+q+\cdots+q^{r-1}

for

. Since

, the reverse implication of Theorem
3.3
follows from the fact products of

-integers are palindromic polynomials. In Section

, we provide a new proof of the forward direction of Theorem
3.3
using “split-pattern” avoidance which was developed by Alland and Richmond in
[
2
]
to describe BP decompositions in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in one-line notation.

The following is a generalization of Theorem

to Coxeter groups of finite Lie-type.

In this section, we focus on the permutation group

4.1.
Hyperplane arrangement of a Coxeter group

Each finite Coxeter group
W
is naturally associated with a hyperplane arrangement through a root system. Let
\Phi
be a finite collection of non-zero vectors in some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For each
\alpha\in\Phi
, we define the reflection
s_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

s_{\alpha}(x):=x-\frac{2(\alpha,x)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.

We say
\Phi
is a
root system
of
W
if the following hold:

(1)

\Phi\cap\mathbb{R}\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}
for each
\alpha\in\Phi
,

(2)

s_{\alpha}(\Phi)\subseteq\Phi
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
, and

(3)

W\simeq\langle s_{\alpha}\ |\ \alpha\in\Phi\rangle
.

The vectors
\alpha\in\Phi
are called
roots
. Let
S
denote the set of simple generators of
W
. For each
s\in S
, there is a unique root (up to sign)
\alpha_{s}
which satisfies the condition that
s(\alpha_{s})=-\alpha_{s}
. We define a set of
simple roots

\Delta:=\{\alpha_{s}\ |\ s\in S\}

by selecting
\pm\alpha_{s}
that all lie on one side of a suitably generic, but fixed hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It can be shown that each
\alpha\in\Phi
is either a totally positive or totally negative linear combination of simple roots, so we can decompose

\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-}

into collections of positive and negative roots with respect to
\Delta
. For more on root systems of Coxeter groups, see
[
24
, Chapter 1]
or
[
9
, Chapter 4]
.

To each
\alpha\in\Phi
, there is the corresponding hyperplane given by

H_{\alpha}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (\alpha,x)=0\}.

Note that
H_{\alpha}=H_{-\alpha}
and that if
x\in H_{\alpha}
, then
s_{\alpha}(x)=x
. The collection of hyperplanes

\mathcal{A}_{W}:=\{H_{\alpha}\ |\ \alpha\in\Phi^{+}\}

is called the
Coxeter arrangement of
W
. Since each
H_{\alpha}
contains the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{A}_{W}
is an example of a central hyperplane arrangement.

For each element
w\in W
, we can take a subset of hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}_{W}
corresponding to the inversions of
w
.
We define the
inversion set
of
w
as the set of positive roots

\Phi_{w}:=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\ |\ w(\alpha)\in\Phi^{-}\}.

and the
inversion hyperplane arrangement

\mathcal{A}_{w}:=\{H_{\alpha}\ |\ \alpha\in\Phi_{w}\}.

If
w_{0}
denotes the longest element in
W
, then
\mathcal{A}_{w_{0}}=\mathcal{A}_{W}
. For permutations, the root-theoretic inversion set
\Phi_{w}
corresponds the usual inversion set of pairs:

\{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}\ |\ i<j\ \text{and}\ w(i)>w(j)\}.

Consider the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vectors space with coordinate basis
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. The group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the standard permutation action the coordinate basis elements. The set of vectors

\Phi=\{x_{i}-x_{j}\ |\ i\neq j\}

is a root system of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with positive roots
\Phi^{+}=\{x_{i}-x_{j}\ |\ i>j\}
and simple roots
\Delta=\{x_{i}-x_{i+1}\ |\ 1\leq i<n\}
. If
\alpha=x_{i}-x_{j}
, then the hyperplane
H_{\alpha}
is defined by the equation
x_{i}=x_{j}
. The Coxeter arrangement of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is

\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\{x_{i}=x_{j}\ |\ i\neq j\}.

For any
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have the inversion arrangement

\mathcal{A}_{w}=\{x_{i}=x_{j}\ |\ i<j\ \text{and}\ w(i)>w(j)\}.

Note that the hyperplane
\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
is invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Hence we can realize
\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
as a hyperplane arrangement in

\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}/(\sum_{i}x_{i}=0).

This reduction will help us visualize hyperplane arrangements in examples.

For any inversion arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{w}
, Let
r_{0}
denote the
fundamental chamber
which is defined as the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
(\alpha,x)>0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi_{w}
. We define the
distance enumerating polynomial
on
\mathcal{A}_{w}
as

R_{w}(q):=\sum_{r}q^{d(r_{0},r)}

where the sum
is over all chambers of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{w}
and
d(r_{0},r)
is the minimum number of hyperplanes separating

r_{0}
and
r
.

4.2.
Inversion arrangements for permutations

In this section, we provide a proof of Theorem
4.3
for permutations. The main strategy is to show that
R_{w}(q)
follows the exact same decomposition as
P_{w}(q)
in Corollary
3.7
.

For an undirected graph
G
on vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the
graphical arrangement
,
\mathcal{A}_{G}
is the hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with hyperplanes
x_{i}=x_{j}
for all edges
(i,j)
of
G
. In the case
G
is a complete graph on
n
vertices, we get the Coxeter arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
.

Given a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we define its
inversion graph

G_{w}
as an undirected graph on vertices
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and edges
(i,j)
whenever we have
i<j
and
w(i)>w(j)
. Note that the inversion arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{w}
is the graphical arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{G_{w}}
.

An
acyclic orientation
on
G
is an assignment of directions to the edges of
G
so that no directed cycles are formed. It is easy to see that the regions of
\mathcal{A}_{G}
are in bijection with acyclic orientations of
G
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{O}
is an acyclic orientation of
G
, then we interpret each directed edge
i\rightarrow j
as
x_{i}<x_{j}
. This corresponds to choosing a side of each hyperplane of
\mathcal{A}_{G}
, hence uniquely defining a region in
\mathcal{A}_{G}
.

From this observation, the distance enumerating polynomial
R_{w}(q)
can be described in terms of acyclic orientations of the graph
G_{w}
. For an acyclic orientation
\mathcal{O}
, let
\text{des}(\mathcal{O})
be the number of edges oriented oriented as
i\rightarrow j
in
\mathcal{O}
where
i>j
(so this corresponds to a descent of
w
). We define

R_{G}(q):=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}q^{\text{des}(\mathcal{O})}.

It can be shown that
R_{G_{w}}(q)=R_{w}(q)
. A
clique
of
G
is a subgraph of
G
such that it is isomorphic to a complete graph. Given a graph
G
and a vertex
k
of
G
, let
G\setminus k
denote the graph obtained by deleting
k
and its adjacent edges in
G
. The following lemma is from
[
10
]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that a graph
G
on vertex set
[n]
has a vertex
k
that satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

4.3.
The general case

We now consider the case when
W
is a Coxeter group of finite Lie type.
As with permutations, the main idea is to use BP-decompositions to show that the polynomials
P_{w}(q)
and
R_{w}(q)
follow the same recursion. In type
A
, the analysis was simpler since we could always choose
v
so it is the maximal length element of
W_{S(v)}\cap W^{J}
, in the context of Theorem
3.4
.

For other types there are more cases of
v
to consider, so the proof is more technical. Despite already having covered the type
A
case, the examples we use in this section used to illustrate the ideas will also come from the type
A
for simplicity. We will be utilizing Theorem
3.4
as our main tool for decomposing the polynomials. The results in this section are due to Mcalmon, Oh, and Yoo in
[
32
]
and Oh and Yoo in
[
35
]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a central hyperplane arrangement with a fixed fundamental chamber
r_{0}
and let
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
denote the set of chambers of
\mathcal{A}
. We define a poset structure on
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
generated by the covering relations
r_{1}<r_{2}
if chamber
r_{1}
is adjacent to chamber
r_{2}
and
d(r_{1},r_{0})=d(r_{2},r_{0})-1
.

If
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is some subarrangement of
\mathcal{A}
and
r\in Q_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
, we define the induced subposet
Q_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},r}
to be the subposet of
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
obtained by restricting to the chambers of
\mathcal{A}
contained in
r
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
uniform
with respect to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
if for all chambers
r
of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, the induced subposets
Q_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},r}
are all isomorphic. In this case, we use
Q_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
to denote the poset.

We consider the inversion arrangement of

Let

is the longest element of

Proof.

Each chamber of

is indexed by an element

where

and

We use BP decompositions to develop some tools needed for the recursion on

when

is rationally smooth. Let
J\subseteq S
and suppose we have a BP decomposition

with respect to

. By Theorem

part (4), we have that

. In particular, we can write a reduced factorization

where

is the longest element of

. Theorem

implies that if

is rationally smooth, then either
w
or

has Grassmannian BP decomposition with respect to some

of the form

v\cdot(u_{S(v)\cap J}\cdot u^{\prime})

For notational simplicity, let

. Given such a decomposition, we decompose

\mathcal{A}_{w}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}

where

\mathcal{A}_{2}:=\mathcal{A}_{w}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{u},\quad\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\mathcal{A}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}_{0}:=(u^{\prime})^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
r
be some chamber inside
\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Let
r^{\prime}
be the chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
that contains
r
. Then the poset
Q_{\mathcal{A}_{w},\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{0},r}
is isomorphic to
Q_{\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{A}_{0},r^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Once a chamber
r^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is fixed, we will show that any chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}
contained in
r^{\prime}
intersects every chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{0}
contained in
r^{\prime}
. In order to show this, we can freely add more hyperplanes to
\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. So we may assume that
u=u_{I\cap J}u^{\prime}
is the longest element of
W_{J}
and
v
is the longest element of
W^{J}
.

From Lemma
4.8
, each chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is now indexed with a permutation of
W_{I\cap J}
. Fix a chamber
r_{x}
labeled with a permutation
x\in W_{I\cap J}
. Each chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}
contained in
r_{x}
is labeled with a permutation
zx
where
z\in W^{J}
. Each chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{0}
contained in
r_{x}
is labeled with a permutation
xy
where
y^{-1}\in W^{I\cap J}\cap W_{J}
. For any such chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{0}
, their intersection will be the chamber of
\mathcal{A}
that is labeled by
zxy\in W
.

Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be two different chambers of
\mathcal{A}
contained in
r
. They are separated by a hyperplane in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. For
i=1,2
, let

be the chamber of
\mathcal{A}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}
that contains

. Then

and

are different chambers, since they are separated by the hyperplane that separates

and
r_{2}
. If
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are adjacent, then
r_{1}^{\prime}
and
r_{2}^{\prime}
are adjacent. If
r_{1}^{\prime}
and
r_{2}^{\prime}
are adjacent but

and

are not, it means there is a hyperplane of

that separates

and

. But that contradicts the fact that

and

are both contained in the same chamber of

. We conclude that

and

are adjacent if and only if

and

are adjacent.
∎

Corollary 4.11

Suppose we have a decomposition

as in Proposition

Corollary

allows us to consider only the case where

is the longest element of some

.

Let

be the set of simple roots that appear in a reduced word of

if it is the maximal element of

is a chain poset. Notice that in Theorem

Proposition 4.12
.

Suppose we have a decomposition
w=v(u_{I\cap J}u^{\prime})
as in Proposition
4.9
.

If
v
is a locally-maximal element or a local chain, then
P_{u}(q)=R_{u}(q)
implies
P_{w}(q)=R_{w}(q)
.

Proof.

From corollary
4.11
, it is enough to show
\mathcal{A}_{vu_{I\cap J}}
is uniform with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}
and
R_{vu_{I\cap J}}(q)=P_{vu_{I\cap J}}(q)
.

If
v
is the longest element of
W^{J}
, then

is the longest element of
W_{I}
. In this case, the proposition follows from Lemma
4.8
.

When
W_{I}^{I\cap J}
is a chain, let
v^{\prime}
denote the longest element of
W_{I}^{I\cap J}
. Then
w^{\prime}:=v^{\prime}u_{I\cap J}
is the longest element of
W_{I}
. From Lemma
4.8
, we have that
R_{u_{I\cap J}}(q)=P_{u_{I\cap J}}(q)
and
R_{v^{\prime}u_{I\cap J}}(q)=P_{v^{\prime}u_{I\cap J}}(q)
. For each chamber
r
of
\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}
, the poset
Q(\mathcal{A}_{w^{\prime}},\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}},u)
is a chain of length
\ell(v^{\prime})
. In particular, every hyperplane of

intersects the interior of the chamber

.

When we go from
\mathcal{A}_{w^{\prime}=v^{\prime}u_{I\cap J}}
to
\mathcal{A}_{vu_{I\cap J}}
, we remove some hyperplanes from
\mathcal{A}_{w^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}
. For each chamber
r
of
\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}
, the poset

is a chain of length

minus the number of hyperplanes removed. Hence
\mathcal{A}_{vu_{I\cap J}}
is uniform with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{u_{I\cap J}}
. Moreover, we have

R_{vu_{I\cap J}}(q)=(1+\cdots+q^{\ell(v)})\cdot R_{u_{I\cap J}(q)}.

The proposition now follows from Lemma
4.8
.
∎

Lastly we analyze two special examples each coming from Coxeter groups of type

. We start with type

Example 4.13

Let
W
be a Coxeter group of type

. Let

where

is the longest element of

is a BP decomposition and

The root system of type

lies in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and the hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{w}
is the union of

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{u}=\{x_{1}=0,\

\displaystyle x_{2}=0,\ x_{3}=0,\ x_{2}-x_{1}=0,\ x_{3}-x_{2}=0,

\displaystyle x_{3}-x_{1}=0,\ x_{1}+x_{2}=0,\ x_{1}+x_{3}=0,\ x_{2}+x_{3}=0\}

\displaystyle\{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=x_{4},\ -x_{1}-x_{2}+x_{3}=x_{4},\ -x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}=x_{4},\ x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}=x_{4}\}.

Pick any chamber

of
\mathcal{A}_{u}
and an arbitrary interior point

. Consider the line

obtained from

to

Now we consider the case of

with the leaf

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
W
be a type

and let

. Let

where

is the longest element of

and

The root system of type
B_{n}
lies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the hyperplane arrangement

consists of hyperplanes defined by the following equations:

(1)

,

(2)

, and

(3)

The hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{w}
is obtained from
\mathcal{A}_{u}

Pick any chamber

and arbitrary interior point

, the order in which we cross the hyperplanes of

Hence
\mathcal{A}_{w}
is uniform with respect to

. Moreover, the poset

We are now ready to prove Theorem

. First note that

is always palindromic by definition. So if

Theorem 4.15

Let

be a Coxeter group of finite Lie-type and let

be rationally smooth. Then

.

Proof.

We use induction on
|S(w)|
. First, if
w=s\in S
, then
R_{w}(q)=P_{w}(q)=1+q
. By Theorem

, either

or

has a Grassmannian BP decomposition

is a locally-maximal element or is in a local chain or is in special cases of types

or

. In the first two cases, that is, when

and Lemma

∎

When

, Slofstra gives an explicit description of these exponents. For other interesting results in inversion arrangements, we recommend that the reader take a look at

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,

.

). For any subset
J\subseteq S
, let
W_{J}
the parabolic subgroup of
W
generated by
J
and let
P_{J}:=BW_{J}B
denote the corresponding parabolic subgroup of
G
. The coset space
G/P_{J}
is called a
partial flag variety
of
G
and it is a smooth complex protective homogeneous space. If
J=\emptyset
, then
P_{\emptyset}=B
and
G/B
is called the
complete or full flag variety
of
G
.

Consider the natural projection between flag varieties

\pi:G/B\rightarrow G/P_{J}

given by
\pi(gB)=gP_{J}
. It is not hard to see that the fibers of the this map are

\pi^{-1}(gP_{J})=gP_{J}/B

and that the map
\pi
gives a
(P_{J}/B)
-fiber bundle structure on the flag variety
G/B
with base
G/P_{J}
. For any
w\in W^{J}
, we define the
Schubert variety
as the closure of the
B
-orbit (i.e. Schubert cell)

X^{J}(w):=\overline{BwP_{J}/P_{J}}.

If
J=\emptyset
, then
P_{\emptyset}=B
and we will denote
X(w):=X^{\emptyset}(w)
.
It is well known that if
w\in W^{J}
, then

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(X^{J}(w))=\ell(w)

and Bruhat order describes the closure relations on Schubert cells

X^{J}(w)=\bigsqcup_{w^{\prime}\in[e,w]^{J}}Bw^{\prime}P_{J}/P_{J}.

In this section, we consider all Schubert varieties and do not restrict to the cases of smooth or rationally smooth. If
w=vu
is a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
, then
u\in P_{J}
and hence
\pi
restricts to a projection between Schubert varieties

\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v).

The question we address is when does the map
\pi
induce a fiber bundle structure on
X(w)
? As we will see, the generic fibers of this map are isomorphic to the Schubert variety
X(u)
, however, unlike for
G/B
, the map
\pi
restricted to a Schubert variety may not fiber bundle. The following theorem is a geometric realization of BP-decompositions and is proved by Richmond and Slofstra in
[
37
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
w=vu
be a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The decomposition
w=vu
is a
BP
decomposition with respect to
J
.

(2)

The projection
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
is a Zarisky-locally trivial with fiber
X(u).

Our goal is to give a detailed proof Theorem
5.1
following
[
37
]
. First, we need several important lemmas about Schubert varieties. One key property needed in the proof of these results is the following well-known relation for double
B
-orbits for BN-pairs (or Tits systems).

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
s\in S
and
u\in W
, we have

BsB\cdot BuB=\begin{cases}BsuB&\text{if $s\notin D_{L}(u)$}\\
BuB\cup BsuB&\text{if $s\in D_{L}(u)$}\\
\end{cases}

If
xP_{J}\in X^{J}(v)
, then we can write
xP_{J}=b_{0}v_{0}P_{J}
for some
b_{0}\in B
and
v_{0}\in[e,v]^{J}
. The next lemma (
[
37
, Lemma 4.6]
) describes the fibers of the map
\pi
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
w=vu
be the parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
and
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v).
Let
xP_{J}\in X^{J}(v)
and write
x=b_{0}v_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in B
and
v_{0}\in[e,v]^{J}
. Then

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=x\bigcup Bu^{\prime}B/B

where the union is over all
u^{\prime}\in W_{J}
such that
v_{0}u^{\prime}\leq w
.

Proof.

We first look at the fiber over
xP_{J}
of the map
\pi:G/B\rightarrow G/P_{J}.
Note that

P_{J}=BW_{J}B=\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\in W_{J}}BuB

and hence the fiber of
xP_{J}
in the full flag variety
G/B
is

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=b_{0}v_{0}\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\in W_{J}}BuB/B.

Restricting the map
\pi
to the Schubert variety
X(w)
gives

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=\left(b_{0}v_{0}\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\in W_{J}}Bu^{\prime}B/B\right)\cap\left(\bigcup_{w^{\prime}\leq w}Bw^{\prime}B/B\right).

Since
v_{0}\in W^{J}
and
u^{\prime}\in W_{J}
, Lemma
5.2
implies
b_{0}v_{0}Bu^{\prime}B\subseteq Bv_{0}u^{\prime}B
. Hence

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=b_{0}v_{0}\bigcup Bu^{\prime}B/B

where the union is over all
u^{\prime}\in W_{J}
such that
v_{0}u^{\prime}\leq w.

∎

The next lemma is from
[
37
, Proposition 4.7]
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
w=vu
be a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The decomposition
w=vu
is a BP decomposition.

(2)

The fibers of the map
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
are isomorphic to
X(u)
.

(3)

The fibers of the map
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
are equidimensional.

Proof.

Clearly part (2) implies part (3), so we focus on showing part (1) implies part (2) and part (3) implies part (1). Let
xP_{J}\in X^{J}(v)
and write
x=b_{0}v_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in B
and
v_{0}\in[e,v]^{J}
. If
w=vu
is a BP decomposition, then Theorem
2.7
part (3) implies that
v_{0}u^{\prime}\leq w
if and only if
u^{\prime}\leq u
. Lemma
5.3
implies that the fiber

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=b_{0}v_{0}\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\leq u}Bu^{\prime}B/B=b_{0}v_{0}X(u)

and hence all fibers of
\pi
are isomorphic to
X(u)
.

Now suppose all the fibers of
\pi
are the same dimension. Then Lemma
5.3
implies the fiber over the identity is
\pi^{-1}(eP_{J})=X^{J}(u^{\prime})
where
u^{\prime}
denotes the maximal element of
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
. Similarly, we have the fiber over
vP_{J}
is
\pi^{-1}(vP_{J})=vX^{J}(u)
. Since the fibers are equidimensional, we have
\ell(u^{\prime})=\ell(u)
. But
u\leq u^{\prime}
and hence
u=u^{\prime}
. Thus
w=vu
is a BP decomposition.
∎

What remains to be proved is that when
w=vu
is a BP-decomposition, then the map
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
is locally trivial and hence a
X(u)
-fiber bundle. We first need the following lemma which is proved in
[
37
, Lemma 4.8]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
v\in W^{J}
and let
I=S(v)
denote the support set of
v
. Let
G_{I}\subseteq G
denote the Levi subgroup of
P_{I}
. Let
P_{I,J}:=G_{I}\cap P_{J}
and
B_{I}:=G_{I}\cap B
denote the corresponding Borel and parabolic subgroups of
G_{I}
.

Then the inclusion
i:G_{I}/P_{I,J}\hookrightarrow G/P_{J}
induces an isomorphism

i:X_{I}^{I\cap J}(v)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)

where the Schubert variety

X_{I}^{I\cap J}(v):=\overline{B_{I}vP_{I,J}/P_{I,J}}\subseteq G_{I}/P_{I,J}.

We can now prove the main theorem of the section.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

First observe that if
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
is a locally-trivial fiber bundle, then the fibers are equidimensional and hence
w=vu
is a BP-decomposition by Lemma
5.4
.

Now suppose that
w=vu
is a BP-decomposition and let
I=S(v)
. Lemma
5.4
implies the fibers of the map
\pi
are all isomorphic to
X(u)
and hence we only need to show local triviality. Lemma
5.5
states that the inclusion
i:G_{I}/P_{I,J}\hookrightarrow G/P_{J}
restricts to an isomorphism
i:X_{I}^{I\cap J}(v)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
. The map
G_{I}\rightarrow G_{I}/P_{I,J}
is locally trivial and thus has local sections. Hence for any
x\in X^{J}(v)
, there exists a Zariski open neighborhood
U_{x}\subseteq X^{J}(v)
with a local section
s:U_{x}\rightarrow G_{I}\subseteq G
. Define the multiplication map

m:U_{x}\times X(u)\rightarrow G/B

by
m(x^{\prime},y):=s(x^{\prime})\cdot y
. We claim that the image of
m
lies in the Schubert variety
X(w)
. Let
x^{\prime}\in U_{x}\subseteq X^{J}(v)
and hence
x^{\prime}\in Bv_{0}P_{J}
for some
v_{0}\leq v
. Thus we can write
s(x^{\prime})=b_{0}v_{0}p_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in B_{I}:=G_{I}\cap B
and
p_{0}\in P_{I,J}
. Since
w=vu
is a BP-decomposition, Theorem
2.7
implies that
I\cap J\subseteq D_{L}(u)
. Since
P_{I,J}\subseteq P_{I\cap J}=BW_{I\cap J}B
, Lemma
5.2
implies
p_{0}X(u)=X(u)
. Hence

m(x^{\prime},X(u))=b_{0}v_{0}p_{0}X(u)=b_{0}v_{0}X(u)\subseteq X(v_{0}u)\subseteq X(w).

Consider the commuting diagram:

\textstyle{U_{x}\times X(u)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m}
\textstyle{X(w)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{U_{x}\ \ \ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X^{J}(v)}

and note that the map
m
identifies
(x^{\prime},X(u))
with the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x^{\prime})
. For any
z\in\pi^{-1}(U_{x})
, let
g_{z}:=s(\pi(z))\in G_{I}
. Then
z\mapsto(\pi(z),g_{z}^{-1}z)
maps
\pi^{-1}(U_{x})
to

and is, in fact, the inverse of

. This implies the map

is an algebraic isomorphism and hence
\pi
is locally trivial.

∎

One consequence of Theorem
5.1
, is the following cohomological interpretation of BP decompositions. For any variety
X
, let
H^{*}(X)
denote its singular cohomology with complex coefficients.

Corollary 5.6
.

The decomposition
w=vu
is a BP decomposition with respect to
J
if and only if

(2)

H^{*}(X(w)\simeq H^{*}(X^{J}(v))\otimes H^{*}(X^{*}(u))

as
H^{*}(X^{J}(v))
-modules.

Proof.

If
w=vu
is a BP decomposition, then Equation (
2
) follows from Theorem
5.1
and the Leray-Hirsch theorem. Conversely, recall that

P_{w}(q^{2})=\sum_{i}^{2\ell(w)}\dim(H^{i}(X(w))\,q^{i}\quad\text{and}\quad P^{J}_{v}(q^{2})=\sum_{i}^{2\ell(v)}\dim(H^{i}(X^{J}(v))\,q^{i}.

If Equation (
2
) holds, then
w=vu
is a BP decomposition since
P_{w}(q)=P^{J}_{v}(q)\cdot P_{u}(q)
.
∎

We give some remarks about the fibers if
\pi
when
w=vu
is not necessarily a BP decomposition. The union describing general fibers in Lemma
5.3
is taken over all
u^{\prime}
such that
v_{0}u^{\prime}\in[e,w]\cap v_{0}W_{J}
. It is not difficult to see that this collection forms a lower order ideal in
W_{J}
. In
[
31
,
27
,
34
]
, it is independently shown that these lower order ideals have unique maximal elements and hence are intervals in
W_{J}
. This leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
w=vu
be a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
and
\pi:X(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v).
Let
xP_{J}\in X^{J}(v)
and write
x=b_{0}v_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in B
and
v_{0}\in[e,v]^{J}
. Then

\pi^{-1}(xP_{J})=b_{0}v_{0}X(u_{0})

where
u_{0}
is the unique maximal element of the set
v_{0}^{-1}([e,w]\cap v_{0}W_{J})
.

Moreover, if
u^{\prime}
denotes the maximal element of the set
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
, then
u\leq u_{0}\leq u^{\prime}
. If
w=vu
is a BP decomposition, then
u=u_{0}=u^{\prime}
.

Example 5.8
.

Let
G=\SL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
. Geometrically, we have

G/B=\{V_{\bullet}=(V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset V_{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{4})\ |\ \dim V_{i}=i\}.

Let
E_{\bullet}
denote the flag corresponding to
eB
and
w=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}s_{2}s_{1}
. Then

X(w)=\{V_{\bullet}\ |\ \dim(V_{2}\cap E_{2})\geq 1\}.

We consider the geometric analogues of Examples
2.5
and
2.6
.

First, if
J=\{s_{1},s_{3}\}
, then
\pi(V_{\bullet})=V_{2}
and

w=vu=(s_{1}s_{3}s_{2})(s_{3}s_{1})

is a BP decomposition with respect to
J
as in Example
2.5
. In particular, the Schubert variety

X^{J}(v)=\{V_{2}\ |\ \dim(V_{2}\cap E_{2})\geq 1\}

and the fibre over
V_{2}
in the projection
\pi:X^{\emptyset}(w)\rightarrow X^{J}(v)
is

\pi^{-1}(V_{2})=\{(V_{1},V_{3})\ |\ V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset V_{3}\}\cong X(u)\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}\times\mathbb{CP}^{1}.

If
J=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
, then
\pi(V_{\bullet})=V_{3}
and

w=vu=(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3})(s_{2}s_{1})

is not a BP decomposition as in Example
2.6
. The fiber over

is given by

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(V_{3})

\displaystyle=\{(V_{1},V_{2})\ |\ V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset V_{3}\text{ and }\dim(V_{2}\cap E_{2})\geq 1\}

\displaystyle\cong\begin{cases}X(s_{2}s_{1})&\text{if}\quad\dim(V_{3}\cap E_{2})=1\\
X(s_{1}s_{2}s_{1})&\text{if}\quad E_{2}\subset V_{3}\end{cases}

Note that the fibres are not equidimensional.

Remark 5.9
.

Combinatorially, Corollary
5.7
says that if
w=vu
is a parabolic decomposition with respect to
J
and
u^{\prime}
denotes the maximal element of
[e,w]\cap W_{J}
, then for every
v_{0}\in[e,v]^{J}
, the coset interval

[e,w]\cap v_{0}W_{J}\simeq[e,u_{0}]

for some

. At the extremes, we have

[e,w]\cap W_{J}\simeq[e,u^{\prime}]\quad\text{and}\quad[e,w]\cap vW_{J}\simeq[e,u].

5.1.
Relative BP decompositions

We finish this section by stating a relative version of Theorem
5.1
. In this case, we have two parabolic subgroups
P_{J}\subseteq P_{K}\subseteq G
corresponding to subsets
J\subseteq K\subseteq S
. Consider the projection

\pi:G/P_{J}\rightarrow G/P_{K}

and we can ask the question: when does the map
\pi
induced a fiber bundle structure when restricted to the Schubert variety
X^{J}(w)
? To answer to this equation, we define the relative version of a BP decomposition.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
J\subseteq K\subseteq S
and
w\in W^{J}
. Let
w=vu
denote the parabolic decomposition with respect to
K
. We say
w=vu
is a
BP decomposition with respect to
(J,K)
if the Poincare polynomial factors

P_{w}^{J}(q)=P_{v}^{K}(q)\cdot P_{u}^{J}(q).

Note that if
J=\emptyset
, then this is the usual BP decomposition of
w
with respect to
K
. We remark that relative BP decompositions are characterized by a similar list of conditions to those given in Theorem
2.7
. See
[
37
, Proposition 4.2]
for a precise statement.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
w\in W^{J}
and let
w=vu
be a parabolic decomposition with respect to
(J,K)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The decomposition
w=vu
is a
BP
decomposition with respect to
(J,K)
.

(2)

The projection
\pi:X^{J}(w)\rightarrow X^{K}(v)
is a Zarisky-locally trivial with fiber
X^{J}(u).

Theorem
5.11
is proved in
[
37
]
and the proof is very similar to that of Theorem
5.1
.

Remark 5.12
.

Theorems
5.1
and
5.11
hold for the much larger class of Kac-Moody Schubert varieties. Kac-Moody groups are infinite dimensional generalizations of Lie groups and include the family of affine Lie groups. Their Weyl groups are (not necessarily finite) crystalographic Coxeter groups. While the flag varieties of Kac-Moody groups are also infinite dimensional, their Schubert varieties are finite dimensional. For more on Kac-Moody flag varieties and their Schubert varieties see
[
28
]
.

6.
Iterated BP decompositions and staircase diagrams

In this section, we discuss iterations of BP decompositions for Coxeter groups of finite type. In particular, if
(W,S)
is a Coxeter system and
J\subseteq S
, then each subgroup
W_{J}
has a unique longest element we denote by
u_{J}
. We begin with the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

We say a factorization

w=v_{n}v_{n-1}\cdots v_{1}

is an
iterated BP decomposition
if
(v_{i+1})(v_{i}\cdots v_{1})
is a BP decomposition for each
1<i<n
.

By Theorem
5.1
, iterated BP decompositions correspond to iterated fiber bundle structures on Schubert varieties.

6.1.
Staircase diagrams

In this section we combinatorially characterize iterated BP decompositions by objects called
labelled staircase diagrams
. Staircase diagrams are certain partially ordered sets over a given graph and were introduced by Richmond and Slofstra in
[
38
]
with the goal of developing a combinatorial framework to study iterated BP decompositions. We focus on staircase diagrams over the Coxeter graph of a Coxeter group. The Coxeter graph is simply the Coxeter diagram of
W
without the edge labels and we denote this graph by
\Gamma_{W}
(See Figure

\Gamma_{W}
is a graph with vertex set
S
and edge set

. Note that the Coxeter groups of types
A_{n}
and

all have the same underlying Coxeter graph.

Before stating the definition of a staircase diagram, we need some terminology. Given

is

is an edge in

is

is connected. If

is a collection of subsets of

and

In other words,
\mathcal{D}_{s}

that contain

Let

B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}

(3)

Proof.

Part (1) follows from the fact that

is the intersection of

is a chain.

For part (2), select

For part (3), if

Definition 6.4

.

We denote a labeled staircase diagram by the pair
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)
.

Example 6.5
.

The function
\lambda:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow W

is a labelling of
\mathcal{D}
. This labelling is called the maximal labelling of

Note that while staircase diagrams of type

The definition of a labelling is compatible with the dual of staircase diagram. For any labeled staircase diagram
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)
, define the inverse labelling

\lambda^{-1}:\flip(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow W

\lambda^{-1}(B):=\lambda(B)^{-1}

\lambda(B)
. These coset representatives play an important role in the next definition, so for any labelled staircase diagram
(\mathcal{D},\Lambda)
and

\overline{\lambda}(B):=\lambda(B)u_{J_{R}(B)}.

Given a labeled staircase diagram

6.3.
Complete BP decompositions

In this section we discuss a special class of decompositions called complete BP decompositions. We start with the following definition which was introduced in Section
3
.

Definition 6.10
.

A BP composition
w=vu
with respect to
J
is a
Grassmannian BP decomposition
if
|J|=|S(w)|-1
. In other words,
J
is maximal proper subset of
S(w)
.

Geometrically, Grassmannian BP decompositions correspond to projections
\pi:G/B\rightarrow G/P
where
P
is taken to be a maximal parabolic. In the classical type
A
setting this partial flag variety
G/P
corresponds to a Grassmannian variety. If
w=vu
is a Grassmannian BP decomposition, then Theorem
5.1
implies the Schubert variety
X(w)
is an
X(u)
-fiber bundle over the Grassmannian Schubert variety
X^{J}(v)
. Note that the decompositions that arise in Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
are Grassmannian BP decompositions.

Definition 6.11
.

Let
n=|S(w)|
. We say

w=v_{n}v_{n-1}\cdots v_{1}

is a
complete BP decomposition
if
(v_{i+1})(v_{i}\cdots v_{1})
is a Grassmannian BP decomposition for each
1\leq i<n
.

Complete BP decompositions are iterated BP decompositions where the number of non-trivial factors is maximized in the sense that each iteration adds exactly one additional generator to the support set of
w
. For example, in type
A_{3}
, we have that

w=(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3})(s_{1}s_{2})(s_{1})

is a complete BP decomposition of the longest element. Note that these decompositions are not unique. For
w
above, the decomposition

w=(s_{2}s_{1}s_{3}s_{2})(s_{1})(s_{3})

is also a complete BP decomposition. The goal of this section is to classify which elements
w\in W
that have complete BP decompositions. The key to this classification is the notion of nearly-maximal elements.

Definition 6.12
.

We say an element
w\in W
is
nearly-maximal
if there is a Grassmannian BP decomposition
w=vu
such that
S(u)\subset S(v)
.

Furthermore, we say a labelled staircase diagram
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)
is
nearly-maximal
if each
B\in\mathcal{D}
,
\lambda(B)
is nearly-maximal.

If
w=vu
is nearly-maximal, then

S(u)\subseteq S(v)\cap J\subseteq D_{L}(u)

and hence
S(u)=D_{L}(u)
. This implies that
u
is the maximal element of
W_{J}
. Geometrically, this corresponds to the fiber
X(u)
being isomorphic to the flag variety
P_{J}/B
. Not all Grassmannian BP decompositions satisfy the nearly-maximal condition. For example, in type
A_{4}
,

w=(s_{1}s_{2})(s_{1}s_{3}s_{4})

is Grassmannian BP decomposition with respect to
J=\{s_{1},s_{3},s_{4}\}
, but
w
is not nearly maximal. Note that the maximal labelling of a staircase diagram is nearly-maximal. The importance of nearly-maximal labelings is that they can used to construct complete BP decompositions. In fact, this construction will yield the following bijection:

Theorem 6.13
.

Let
W
be a Coxeter group. Then the map
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)\mapsto\Lambda(\mathcal{D})
defines a bijection between staircase diagrams over
W
with a nearly-maximal labelling
\lambda
, and elements of
W
with a complete BP decomposition.

Outline of the proof of Theorem
6.13
.

First note by Theorem
6.9
and Definition
6.12
, if
\lambda
is a nearly maximal labelling of
\mathcal{D}
, then
\Lambda(\mathcal{D})
has a complete BP-decomposition and thus the map
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)\mapsto\Lambda(\mathcal{D})
is well defined.

To show that the map is injective, suppose we have two nearly-maximal labelled staircase diagrams
(\mathcal{D}_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
(\mathcal{D}_{2},\lambda_{2})
such that
\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{1})=\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{2})
. Choose
s\in S
such that
\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{i})=vu
is a BP decomposition with respect to
J=S\setminus\{s\}
. It can be shown that
B:=S(v)
is a maximal block of
\mathcal{D}_{i}
and hence, by induction on the number of blocks,
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
. To show that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, note that, by Lemma
6.8
part (1), the parabolic decomposition of
\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{i})^{-1}
with respect to
B
is given by

\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{i})^{-1}=v^{\prime}\cdot\lambda_{i}(B)^{-1}

for some
v^{\prime}
and thus
\lambda_{1}(B)=\lambda_{2}(B)
. We also have

J_{R}(B,\mathcal{D}_{i})=B\cap S(\mathcal{D}_{i}\setminus\{B\})=B\cap S(\lambda_{i}(B)\cdot\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{i}))

and hence
J_{R}(B,\mathcal{D}_{1})=J_{R}(B,\mathcal{D}_{2})
.
This implies
\overline{\lambda}_{1}(B)=\overline{\lambda}_{2}(B)
. Since
\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{1})=\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{2})
and
\overline{\lambda}_{1}(B)=\overline{\lambda}_{2}(B)
, we have that the induced labelling on lower order ideals satisfies
\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{1}\setminus\{B\})=\Lambda(\mathcal{D}_{2}\setminus\{B\})
. By induction on
|\mathcal{D}_{i}|
, we have that the labellings
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

To show that the map is surjective, suppose
x\in W
has a complete BP decomposition
x=v_{n}\cdots v_{1}
. By induction, suppose that
(\mathcal{D},\lambda)
is a nearly-maximal labelled staircase diagram such that
\Lambda(\mathcal{D})=v_{n-1}\cdots v_{1}
. Define the staircase diagram

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}:=\mathcal{D}^{0}\cup\{S(v_{n})\}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{D}^{0}:=\{B\in\mathcal{D}\ |\ B\nsubseteq S(v_{n})\}

with the added covering relations
\max(\mathcal{D}^{0}_{s})\prec S(v_{n})
for every
s\in S(\mathcal{D}^{0})
contained in, or adjacent to
S(v_{n})
. It can be shown that
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
satisfies Definition
6.2
of staircase diagram. Finally, define the labelling
\tilde{\lambda}:\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\rightarrow W
by

\tilde{\lambda}(B):=\begin{cases}\lambda(B)&\text{if $B\in\mathcal{D}^{0}$}\\
v_{n}\cdot u_{S(v_{n})\cap S(\mathcal{D})}&\text{if $B=S(v_{n})$}.\end{cases}

Again, it can shown that
\tilde{\lambda}
is a nearly maximal labelling of
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\Lambda(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\tilde{\lambda})=x
. This completes the proof.
∎

If

\mathcal{D}

P_{v}^{J}(v)=[\ell(v)+1]_{q}
. This completes the proof.
∎

7.2.
Related results on pattern avoidance

In this section by state two analogues of the following theorem which summarizes various characterizations of smooth permutations.

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

w
avoids
3412
and
4231
.

(2)

X(w)
is an iterated fiber-bundle of Grassmannian varieties.

(3)

The interval
[e,w]
is rank symmetric.

Theorem
7.10
follows from the combined works of Lakshmibai-Sandhya
[
29
]
, Ryan
[
40
]
, and Carrell
[
13
]
. Note that Theorem
7.2
can be viewed as analogue of the equivalence of parts (1) and part (2) in Theorem
7.10
where we replace part (2) with an iterated fiber-bundle of Grassmannian Schubert varieties. Each Grassmannian has a co-dimension one Schubert variety which is unique in the sense that, as a Weil divisor, it generates the Picard group of the Grassmannian. We call this variety a Grassmannian Schubert divisor. The following theorem is another analogue of the equivalence of parts (1) and (2) and is proved by Azam in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

w
avoids
3412,52341,52431,
and
53241
.

(2)

X(w)
is an iterated fiber bundle of Grassmannian varieties or Grassmannian Schubert divisors.

The class of permutations in Theorem
7.11
is larger than the smooth class of permutations, but within the class of permutations that have complete BP decompositions. Note that Grassmannian Schubert divisors are almost always singular varieties. One consequence of Theorem
7.11
is that the generating function for permutations that avoid
3412,52341,52431,
and
53241
can be calculated using labelled staircase diagrams. This calculation uses “Catalan type” objects similar to those used to prove Theorem
6.18
. For more details see
[
3
]
.

The next theorem is a analogue of the equivalence of parts (1) and (3) in Theorem
7.10
. Given a poset
P
, the dual poset
P^{*}
is obtained by reversing the partial order. We say
P
is
self-dual
if
P\simeq P^{*}
as posets. It is easy to check that any graded self-dual poset is rank symmetric. However the converse may not be true. The next theorem is proved by Gaetz and Gao in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 7.12
.

Let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

w
avoids
3412,4231,34521,45321,54123,
and
54312
.

(2)

The interval
[e,w]
is self-dual (as a poset).

The authors refer to permutations characterized in Theorem
7.12
as “polished” permutations since the condition of self-duality on the interval
[e,w]
is sufficient for smoothness, but not necessary.

7.3.
Affine permutations

In this section we discuss applications of BP decompositions to the group of affine permutations denoted
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. An
affine permutation
is a bijection
w:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that

(1)

w(i+n)=w(i)+n
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(i)=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
.

Note that a regular permutation extends to an affine permutation by applying part (1) above to the one-line notation sequence
w(1)\cdots w(n)
.
Similarly, any affine permutation is uniquely determined by the “window” of values

\cdots w(-1),w(0),[w(1),w(2),\cdots,w(n)],w(n+1),w(n+2),\cdots

by the same extension. For example
[4,2,3,1]
,
[8,1,-2,3]
, and
[-7,7,6,4]
are all examples of affine permutations in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{4}
.

The group of affine permutations is an infinite Coxeter group with generating set
S=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
and Coxeter graph:

s_{1}
s_{2}
\cdots
s_{n-1}
s_{0}

Affine permutations are referred to as Coxeter groups of affine type
A
. Note that all maximal parabolic subgroups of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are isomorphic to the finite permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

As with finite permutations, (rational) smoothness is closely tied to pattern avoidance and was studied by Billey and Crites in
[
4
]
. We say an affine permutation
w

contains the (finite) pattern

u\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
if there is a sequence
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k})
such that
w(i_{1})\cdots w(i_{k})
has the same relative order as
u
. Note that the sequence
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k})
does not necessarily have to be contained in the integers
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
w
does not contain
u
, we say it
avoids the pattern

u
. The following thoerem if proved by Billey and Crites in
[
4
]

Theorem 7.13
.

The affine permutation
w\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is rationally smooth if and only if one of the following hold:

(1)

w
avoids the patterns
3412
and
4231
or

(2)

w
is a twisted spiral permutation (see
[
4
, Section 2.5]
).

It is shown by Mitchell in
[
8
]
, that if
w
is a twisted spiral permutation, then the Schubert variety
X(w)
is not smooth. Hence smooth is not equivalent to rationally smooth for affine permutations. One technical result used to prove Theorem
7.13
is the following analogue of Theorem
3.4
.

Proposition 7.14
.

If
w\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
avoids
3412
and
4231
, then either
w
or
w^{-1}
has a Grassmannian BP decomposition
vu
where both
v
and
u
belong to proper parabolic subgroups of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

Billey and Crites show that, for the BP decomposition
vu
found in Proposition
7.14
, the Poincaré polynomial
P_{v}^{J}(q)
is a
q
-binomial and hence palindromic. They also show that
u
is a smooth (finite) permutation. So Theorem
3.5
implies
P_{w}(q)
is palindromic.
The next theorem was partially conjectured in
[
4
]
and proved by Richmond and Slofstra in
[
39
]
.

Theorem 7.15
.

Let
w\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

X(w)
is smooth.

(2)

w
avoids the patterns
3412
and
4231
.

(3)

w
has a Grassmannian BP decomposition
vu
with respect to some
J
where both
v
and
u
belong to proper parabolic subgroups of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
Furthermore
v
is the maximal element of
W_{S(v)}\cap W^{J}
and
u
is a smooth permutation in the
W_{S(u)}
.

The proof of Theorem
7.15
is similar to the proof of Theorem
6.15
. We remark that part (3) of Theorem
7.15
implies that for an affine permutation
w\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, the affine Schubert variety
X(w)
is smooth if and only if it is an iterated fiber bundle of Grassmannian varieties.

Theorem
7.15
also implies an analogue of Corollary
6.17
on staircase diagrams of affine type
A
which we state below. Since
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is an infinite Coxeter groups, we say a staircase diagram is
spherical
if for each
B\in\mathcal{D}

is a finite Coxeter group. The next statement is from

Corollary 7.16
.

The maximal labelling gives a bijection between spherical staircase diagrams over the Coxeter graph of

and smooth affine permutations in

8.
Future directions

We state some open questions and possible future directions for the study of Coxeter groups in relation to BP-decompositions.

Question 8.1
.

While rational smoothness for Coxeter groups of finite Lie type have been extensively studied. Characterizations of rationally smooth elements for arbitrary Coxeter groups are relatively unknown. For example, if
w
is rationally smooth, does
w
have a Grassmannian BP decomposition? Does Theorem
4.3
hold for inversion hyperplane arrangements of rationally smooth elements in arbitrary Coxeter groups? We remark that Richmond and Slofstra study rationally smooth elements in Coxeter groups that avoid certain rank 3 parabolic subgroups in
[
36

.

Question 8.3
.

Let
w=vu

,

, and
