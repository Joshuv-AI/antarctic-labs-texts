Resolving uncertainty in satellite-derived shorelines of a reef-lined beach using high spatiotemporal resolution topographic surveys
Authors: Anna B. Mikkelsen, Joel C. Nicolow, Kyle D. Murray, Sean Vitousek, Richelle U. Moskvichev, Tiffany R. Anderson, Charles H. Fletcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Automated satellite-derived shoreline (SDS) detection is increasingly used for coastal monitoring, yet few high spatiotemporal resolution datasets exist for detailed error analysis. This study compares SDS from Landsat 7, Landsat 8, Sentinel-2, and PlanetScope against 3.5 years of weekly orthomosaic and digital elevation model surveys of a 500 m reef-lined beach in Waikīkī, Hawai‘i (n = 128 surveys). We evaluated error mitigation strategies including image co-registration, outlier removal, and water-level corrections (tides and wave set-up). SDS accuracy improved substantially after these corrections, with root mean square errors (RMSE) ranging from 2.83 m (PlanetScope) to 5.54 m (Sentinel-2). Our analysis showed that satellite-derived waterlines (SDWs) do not consistently align with the same geomorphic feature (e.g., waterline, low water mark). Mid-resolution satellites aligned with the waterline in only ~ 50% of cases, whereas higher-resolution PlanetScope imagery (3.7 m) aligned consistently in 97% of cases. These findings reveal that SDS can represent varying shoreline proxies, introducing an under-recognized uncertainty that higher-resolution imagery can help resolve. We demonstrate that high-resolution imagery and robust error mitigation are useful for improving the reliability of automated shoreline detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54336-z.

Introduction

Shorelines represent a dynamic interface between land and sea, making them significant indicators of environmental change, coastal stability, and human vulnerability. Effective monitoring can help mitigate issues related to rising sea levels, coastal development, and intensified storminess, which exacerbate erosion and threaten approximately 896 million people who reside in low-lying areas 1–4. Recent advancements in satellite remote sensing have transformed coastal monitoring, enabling large-scale assessments of shoreline dynamics 5–7. These datasets inform projections of future shoreline hazards and guide management strategies amidst increasing risks to coastal populations and environments 7–9. Studying uncertainties in these methodologies can help refine techniques, characterize errors, and improve global coastal monitoring efforts.

Frameworks that use satellites for coastal monitoring leverage publicly available imagery, such as those from Landsat, offering a historical record of shoreline positions spanning the last 30 to 40 years for nearly any coastal location 10–12. The introduction of Sentinel-2 in 2015 (Sentinel-2a) adds higher spatial and temporal resolution to the fleet of open-access satellite monitoring 13. These missions provide multispectral data including short-wave infrared (SWIR) bands. Along with open-access data, the commercial constellation PlanetScope, launched in 2015, now comprises 180 CubeSats. These satellites are capable of imaging Earth’s landmasses daily at a resolution of 3.7 m, offering even more detailed and frequent observations of shoreline changes, though with lower spectral resolution 14. Image hosting platforms such as Google Earth Engine (GEE) have made satellite imagery more easily accessible15 and open-source tools like CoastSat have simplified the process of extracting shoreline indicators 10,12. These advancements, combined with accessible computing power, allow coastal scientists to derive shoreline positions globally, facilitating studies of global beach trends, seasonal and basin-scale patterns, and supporting the development and application of shoreline forecast models 6,7,11,16–21.

Despite these advancements, systematic and random errors remain in satellite-derived shorelines (SDS) 22–24. These errors stem from factors such as poor georeferencing, water-level variability (including tides, wave set-up [the increase in mean water level due to breaking waves], and wave swash [the oscillation of water up and down the beach face]), poor image quality (i.e., clouds, haze, or unfocused images), and low image resolution. Efforts to evaluate SDS accuracy rely on comparisons with ground truth data, typically obtained through costly and labor-intensive field monitoring limited to accessible coastlines 22,25,26. Limited field data makes detailed error analysis challenging, and there are still significant opportunities for refinement, as highlighted by Vos et al. (2023)[22]. These include improving water-level corrections, co-registration to improve image alignment, and developing a deeper understanding of shoreline indicators recognized within SDS frameworks. This includes considering alternative indicators like the vegetation line proposed by Muir et al. (2024) 27 and precisely identifying which shoreline features are automatically detected in each satellite image - an important step to bridge SDS with in situ elevation data.

Most SDS algorithms detect a satellite-derived waterline (SDW), which is typically assumed to represent the instantaneous waterline at the time of image capture 12,28–30. However, it remains unclear what physical feature is actually being detected 24,31. SDWs can vary with beach morphology, image resolution, and thresholding techniques, and may not consistently align with a specific tidal stage or geomorphic contour 24,31. Despite this ambiguity, SDWs are typically corrected to a vertical datum (e.g., local mean sea level [LMSL]) using estimated tide and wave set-up values and subsequently interpreted as satellite-derived shorelines (SDS). This translation assumes that the detected feature corresponds reliably to the waterline. Uncertainties in SDW elevation, combined with erroneous total water level or slope estimates, can introduce error in the corrected shoreline position 24,31. These inconsistencies in the detection of shoreline features across temporally varying images of the same location introduce uncertainty that current frameworks are not accounting for (as they often lack concurrent elevation data). To address potential errors from discrepancies in detected shoreline features and to explore possible improvements through various corrections, we use high-resolution digital elevation models (DEMs; 0.5 m) and image orthomosaics (0.035 m). These were derived from small Unoccupied Aerial Systems (sUAS) collected nearly concurrently with SDS, as described in the Data and Methods overview. We automatically detect shorelines from satellite imagery of Waikīkī, Hawai‘i, using two frameworks (Fig. 1): The open-source framework CoastSat for Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery and a new tool, CoastVision, for PlanetScope imagery (Fig. 2).

CoastSat is a widely used python-based framework designed to map the instantaneous waterline and shoreline in Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery for any region in the world 12. It leverages Google Earth Engine’s (GEE) application programming interface (API) 15, to retrieve imagery. Relevant bands from Sentinel-2 imagery are up-sampled to 10 m, while Landsat imagery is pansharpened to 15 m. CoastSat, as well as most other SDS frameworks, rely on a water spectral index to enhance contrast between open water and land features to effectively discern the waterline. CoastSat uses the modified normalized difference water index (MNDWI) derived from green and short-wave infrared (SWIR) bands. The location of the detected waterline is determined by an adaptive threshold on the MNDWI image, sometimes guided by a pre-trained pixel classification model, which classifies each pixel into ‘sand’, ‘water’, ‘whitewash’, or ‘other land’.

In addition to CoastSat, this study uses CoastVision to process PlanetScope imagery 32, which provides higher resolution compared to Landsat and Sentinel-2. While CoastVision follows a similar framework, it segments the imagery directly into ‘land’ and ‘water’ (rather than subdivide water into unbroken and broken water, and land into sediment and ‘other’, as CoastSat) and derives the waterline using marching squares, which evenly delineates the waterlines between these two classes. To address potential errors from misclassified pixels, it applies a binary opening morphological filter 33 to remove small objects and reduce noise.

CoastSat has been benchmarked against ground truth data around the world, showing that root mean square error (RMSE) - the cross-shore difference between in situ shorelines and SDS measurements collected within 10 days - is smaller than the associated pixel size 21,24,28,34–36. However, these validation efforts remain spatially sparse, limiting generalizability of performance across all coastal settings. Additionally, sites that have a larger ground truth sample size (or longer time-series) can obtain more stable and representative RMSE estimates by reducing influence of outliers or variability present in smaller samples. Past comparisons between sites show highest RMSE values Truc Vert, France, a high-energy, low-sloping beach, whereas the sandy beach at Duck, North Carolina, USA, which experiences little coastal change shows the lowest RMSE values 22. Notably, past studies demonstrate despite a three-fold difference in native spatial resolution between Landsat (30 m) and Sentinel-2 (10 m), RMSE values are quite similar, ranging from 8.1 to 10.6 m for the beaches at Duck and Narrabeen (Australia) 22. Studies that use high-resolution imagery from PlanetScope (3.7 m resolution) improved RMSE values to between 3.5 m and 5.2 m for these same beaches 37. This study is limited to a single reef-fringed site, which restricts the ability to generalize our findings across diverse coastal settings and leaves many sources of variability - such as geomorphic differences, hydrodynamic conditions, and image acquisition - uncontrolled. However, it leverages a temporally dense, high-resolution ground-truth dataset to isolate relative contributions of error sources. By systematically comparing shorelines from Landsat 7 and Landsat 8, Sentinel-2, and PlanetScope, this study provides new insight into the uncertainty and consistency in shoreline feature identification of SDS in a previously understudied coastal environment.

We compare automatically derived SDS from three satellite missions to a high spatiotemporal resolution coastal monitoring dataset of a reef-fringed beach in Waikīkī, Hawai‘i (Figs. 1 and 2). This analysis provides a first look at the role of geomorphic features (low-water line versus waterline versus high water line) in SDS and how they vary with image resolution and ocean conditions. Data and methods overview

We use a dataset of 128 weekly DEMs and orthomosaics collected over 3.5 years as ground-truth 38. The mean sea level (MSL) contour derived from each DEM is used as ground-truth shoreline position. UAS-derived DEMs are uncertain in the swash zone, where water motion inhibits point cloud reconstruction. As a result, all points below 0.6 m above MSL were removed. The resulting data gap is filled by supplementing the point cloud with surveyed locations along the beach toe (approximating the low water mark). A continuous DEM surface is interpolated from both sUAS and beach toe points. This introduces uncertainty in the foreshore, as no true elevation data is available. To quantify this, we compare the DEM against 107 independent survey points of the foreshore, yielding a mean vertical bias of 5.2 cm (standard deviation [STD] = 7.3 cm; mean absolute error [MAE] = 7.8 cm). These uncertainties are important when interpreting validation results, as this corresponds to approximately 0.43 m seaward bias of ground-truth shoreline (based on an average beach slope of 0.12), and a corresponding horizontal MAE of 0.65 m Therefore, SDS improvements exceeding ~ 0.65 m are likely meaningful relative to DEM uncertainty, whereas smaller improvements may fall within the expected noise of the DEM.

Ground-truth shorelines are compared to automatically extracted satellite shorelines derived from Landsat 7, Landsat 8, and Sentinel-2 imagery using CoastSat, and from PlanetScope imagery using CoastVision (Fig. 2). Each survey also produces a high-resolution image mosaic. To determine which geomorphic feature each SDS most closely aligns with, we overlay the SDS onto a high-resolution orthomosaic captured within three days of the satellite image. Each shoreline is then manually classified into one of three well-established shoreline features (Fig. 1d) as defined by Boak and Turner (2005) 39.

First, we identify the low-water mark feature (roughly corresponding to the beach toe) by a color transition from sand to water at the foreshore base that is often easily discernible in aerial imagery in Hawai‘i [40].Second, we consider the waterline, which is also identified manually based on color contrasts. Instantaneous waterlines are typically the expected shoreline feature when using automated satellite algorithms because of the marked contrast in near infrared bands between water and land.Third, we consider the ‘previous high tide high water line’ (hereafter referred to as ‘high water line’) approximately equal to the seaward-most berm crest or the high water line, which can be identified in high-resolution imagery by a color/texture contrast between smooth and footprinted sand or wet and dry sand.

Over 3.5 years, a total of 511 quality checked shorelines were automatically derived: 68 from Landsat 7 and Landsat 8, 152 from Sentinel-2, and 291 from PlanetScope, totaling 9574 valid data points across 19 transects. All three satellite missions are sun-synchronous and capture images consistently between 9 am and noon local time (Hawaiʻi Standard Time; Fig. 3a). Field surveys were conducted earlier in the day, around 7 am (before the surveyed beach becomes overly crowded), leading to same-day satellite-survey matches (n = 58) occurring within a time window of 2 to 5 h, with an average time difference of 3:45 h. Tidal differences for the same-day matches have an average of approximately 0 m, indicating an even distribution around rising and falling tides, with minimum and maximum values of -0.49 m and 0.40 m, respectively. Water level variations of this magnitude, within the 2–5 h same-day timeframe, can influence wave run-up and swash processes, and cause relatively mild morphological adjustments to the beach profile, such as landward or seaward shifts in the beach toe position.

Matches that are between one and ten days apart (n = 418) have an average tide difference of 0.04 m, ranging from − 0.60 to 0.54 m (Fig. 3d), and are more likely to include differences in wave conditions. To account for more substantial morphological changes that might occur with longer time lags, we follow Vos et al. (2023) [22] and use a linear interpolation between the two nearest survey dates to compare ground truth to SDS when the satellite-survey time difference is three to ten days apart (n = 189). Consistent with their approach, we directly compare satellite-derived shorelines to surveys when the time difference is less than 3 days, which is also the limit for visually evaluating shoreline geomorphic features. Figure 3 panels b and c show water-level and wave heights at the time of each image, demonstrating variable ocean conditions throughout our sampling period. It also reveals a bias towards lower tide levels during summer months, as surveys occurred in the morning and summer high tides occur in the afternoon.

Results

Satellite-derived shoreline accuracy for a reef-lined beach

Shoreline positional accuracy was evaluated on a SDS dataset with outliers manually removed by visual inspection, against ground-truth data consisting of MSL shorelines derived from weekly DEMs. We investigated SDS error associated with the time lag between the satellite and survey acquisitions and found SDS measurement to be accurate up until a 10-day lag, likely due to the low-variability nature of the coastline; this may not apply to more dynamic shorelines (refer to Methods 3.3 for details). The RMSE presented throughout this paper represents the error along 19 cross-shore transects (Fig. 1). For the CoastSat-derived dataset, comparison of the uncorrected satellite-derived waterlines with ground-truth MSL shorelines resulted in RMSEs of 5.6 m for Landsat 7 and Landsat 8 (Table 1; Fig. 4). Sentinel-2 yielded a marginally greater RMSE of 5.9 m, while PlanetScope imagery (processed using CoastVision) resulted in an RMSE of 4.8 m. Surprisingly, Sentinel-2 exhibits similar variability and RMSE compared to Landsat imagery, despite the improved resolution. CoastSat-derived shorelines (Landsat 7, Landsat 8, and Sentinel-2)show a seaward bias while CoastVision (PlanetScope) shows a landward bias.

Satellite image preprocessing steps included image co-registration and training a site-specific sand/water pixel classification model in CoastSat. Co-registration improved the accuracy of Sentinel-2-derived shorelines significantly, reducing RMSE by 2.1 m (26%; from 8.0 to 5.9 m) and standard deviation by 1.9 m (25%; from 7.8 to 5.9 m) with no other corrections (Table 1; Figure S1). Smaller improvements were found for PlanetScope-derived shorelines with the same co-registration process, resulting in no difference in RMSE and a reduction of 0.1 m (5%; from 3.3 to 3.2 m) in standard deviation. Using a classification model trained on local imagery significantly reduced an average seaward bias of CoastSat-derived shorelines from 5.2 m to 0.5 m for all shorelines, thereby improving overall RMSE. Although the standard deviation of CoastSat-derived shorelines remained constant regardless of classification model (Table S1), the locally trained model classified a significantly larger area as sand, particularly for Landsat imagery. For Landsat, it identified an average of 273 sand pixels per image, compared to just 0–46 pixels with the default model (out of approximately 3705 total pixels). This enabled CoastSat to apply an MNDWI threshold based on classification output in 43 out of 46 Landsat images, compared to relying solely on a global threshold with the default model. For Sentinel-2, the default model averaged 252 sand pixels per image, while the local model averaged 524 (out of approximately 9180 pixels). In both cases when using our local model, the beach was more accurately characterized with sand pixels, while additional misclassified sand pixels appeared around buildings.

Removing outliers proved to be a useful component for improving accuracy. From automatically extracted shorelines, manual outlier removal reduced RMSE; with reductions of 0.9 m (14%) for Landsat 7, 0.5 m (8%) for Landsat 8, 2.2 m (21%) for Sentinel-2, and 3.6 m (43%) for PlanetScope (Table 1). The intention behind using manual outlier detection was to assess errors of SDS purely due to differences in image properties and SDS framework without the influence of outliers. These manually reviewed shorelines are used for all subsequent analysis. For a fully automated workflow, we also tested an outlier removal process using a median filter, excluding all shoreline points located more than 20 m from the transect median (based on total shoreline variability; Methods 4.3). This resulted in reasonable improvements of RMSE ranging from 0.4 m (6%) for Landsat 7 to 2 m (27%) for PlanetScope. Although this median-based approach was fairly effective for low-variability shorelines in Hawai‘i[20] , it may not generalize well to more dynamic coastal settings. Notably, the higher improvement for PlanetScope suggests a greater occurrence of extreme outliers compared to Landsat, potentially due to differences in shoreline detection methods or PlanetScope’s higher revisit time.

Figure 4 also shows a tail of landward-biased points in narrow beach sections for Landsat 7 and Landsat 8, and Sentinel-2. These points correspond to the western end of the beach (Fig. 1c-d), where automated shoreline extraction smoothed across the groin and misidentified the eroded and recessed beach on the opposite side as part of the continuous shoreline. This resulted in landward shifts of the detected shoreline relative to the in situ survey, contributing to the observed error cluster.

Improvements from water-level corrections

We find that tidal corrections reduce RMSE, but wave set-up corrections do not consistently improve RMSE across all satellite missions. After outlier removal, tidal corrections based on tide gauge measurements reduced RMSE by 0.2, 0.4, and 0.4 m for Landsat 7, Landsat 8, and co-registered Sentinel-2 derived shorelines, respectively (Table 1; Fig. 4). For PlanetScope, RMSE is reduced by 0.5 m and the standard deviation by 0.6 m. Improvements from tidal corrections are comparable when using sea level data from a nearby tide gauge versus the FES2014 global tide model 46. In all cases, tide gauge data yielded slightly better results, reducing RMSE by approximately 0.1 m more than the global model.

Wave set-up — the elevated water level due to wave breaking — is corrected for by shifting shoreline positions seaward (back toward the MSL datum) to account for the elevated water levels during image capture. Set-up corrections make no significant improvements for Landsat and Sentinel-2 derived shorelines, since it further increased the existing seaward bias, while reducing standard deviation by ~ 0.1 m (Fig. 4a). In contrast, PlanetScope shorelines exhibited an initial landward bias in shoreline position, and set-up corrections improve shoreline accuracy by reducing this bias (from -3.4 m to -1.1 m) and improving RMSE from 4.2 m to 2.8 m, while the overall variability remained the same (2.6 m).

Variability in foreshore beach slope

DEM-derived slopes were calculated as linear fits between -0.2 m and 0.3 m MSL along each transect, averaged across all DEMs. Slopes ranged from 0.075 to 0.125, with most values clustering around 0.12 in central and eastern segments (Fig. 5). In contrast, westernmost transects showed greater variability and were generally flatter, averaging around 0.08. Slope estimates from CoastSat.slope showed much greater alongshore variability than DEM-derived slopes, ranging from 0.05 to 0.2 (Fig. 5). CoastSat.slope performs best when the ratio of tidal range to beach slope (TR/tanβ) exceeds 10 [44] . For Waikīkī, using a DEM-derived slope of 0.12 and a spring tidal range of 1 m, this ratio is approximately 8.3 - below the recommended threshold, and incurring a normalized mean absolute error of 25% [44]. Despite being outside the recommended operating range, CoastSat-derived slopes offered estimates of alongshore variation, though they showed an opposite trend compared to the DEM, indicating steeper gradients in the western end of the beach and gentler slopes in the central region. However, shoreline RMSE is negligibly different whether CoastSat- and DEM-derived slopes are used for water level corrections. PlanetScope and Sentinel-2 showed ~ 0.1 m lower RMSE with DEM slopes, while Landsat had ~ 0.1 m lower RMSE with CoastSat slopes (Table S1).

We also compare a third measure of beach slope from profile data obtained at Waikīkī, consisting of 18 transects, each with 15 repeat measurements between 2012 and 2015. The mean foreshore slope across all survey profiles is 0.13, which is slightly steeper than the DEM-derived slope, but shows a similar alongshore trend, with a slight increase in steepness in the westernmost end (Fig. 5).

Satellite shoreline elevation from DEMs

To further investigate improvements from water-level corrections, we derived elevation values at each intersection of the SDS and transects using nearly concurrent sUAS-derived DEMs taken within three days of satellite image acquisition (n = 343). Ideally, if no errors were present, all shoreline contour elevations would be equal to the shoreline datum they were corrected to (in this case MSL). Figure 6 shows the elevation values before and after water-level correction. Elevation values before water-level correction reveal a long ‘tail’ of high-elevation landward data points. This tail is reduced after water-level corrections for all satellites; modestly for Landsat and Sentinel-2 and more substantially for PlanetScope. Additionally, the standard deviation of errors reduces when correcting for wave set-up compared to corrections for tide only (Table 1). This highlights that even though the bias increases and the overall RMSE worsens, correcting for wave set-up reduces the number of onshore biased points. When comparing satellite shoreline elevation to ocean conditions, we observe a weak trend of higher elevation contours associated with higher water-levels (R2 = 0.32) but no clear relationship between satellite shoreline elevation and wave heights (Fig. 7). This indicates that factors beyond ocean conditions, such as time-varying slope or variability in the feature identified as the waterline, may also influence the location and elevation of SDS.

Geomorphic features identified by satellite-derived waterlines

Satellite-derived waterlines are intended to represent the “instantaneous waterline” visible at the time of image capture. To evaluate this assumption, each SDW was compared to a high-resolution orthomosaic to identify the geomorphic feature it most closely aligns with. Due to the lack of a truly synchronous reference, such as a ground-based or aerial image captured at the exact moment of satellite overpass, these comparisons are inherently approximate. Each SDW was classified as aligning with one of three shoreline features: low water mark, waterline, or high water line. Although the drone imagery provides high spatial resolution, the time offset (ranging from hours to days) introduces uncertainty. Thus, the comparison reflects our best effort to approximate variability in what feature the SDW most closely resembles.

Of 343 SDWs that occurred within three days of a survey, 273 (80%) represented the waterline - the feature expected in SDS frameworks. The low water mark was detected in 58 cases (17%), and high water line in 9 cases (3%) (Fig. 8a). Landsat-derived SDWs are divided between the waterline (n = 23) and the low water mark (n = 16), with no high water lines detected. Sentinel-2 SDWs represented all three features: 40 aligned with the low water mark, 41 with the waterline, and 5 with the high water line. PlanetScope derived SDWs predominantly aligned with the waterline (97%, n = 209) with only 2 low water marks and 4 high water lines. These data reveal a much greater inconsistency in mid-resolution imagery (Landsat and Sentinel-2), where only 59% and 48% of SDWs are aligned with the expected “waterline zone”, approximately the location of the waterline, here defined as the narrow swash zone subject to small short-term excursions. In contrast, markedly higher consistency is observed with high-resolution satellite imagery from PlanetScope.

Comparing geomorphic feature classification to ocean conditions at the time of each satellite image indicates a tendency for low water marks to be identified more frequently during low water-levels and still ocean conditions, while high water lines are more frequent during higher water-levels or larger waves, though the difference is not statistically significant (Fig. S2). Even after corrections for tide and wave set-up, low water marks remained biased seaward and high water lines remained biased landward, compared to waterlines (Fig. 8b).

Discussion

This study evaluated the accuracy of SDS for a reef-fringed beach, assessed the effectiveness of error mitigation (e.g., water-level correction, co-registration) specific to this environment, and examined the consistency with which SDWs align with geomorphic shoreline features. Each of these is discussed in detail below.

Performance of CoastSat and CoastVision for shoreline detection of a reef-lined beach

We compared SDS from CoastSat and CoastVision to in situ data for a reef-lined beach, building on previous validation efforts of CoastSat. Both SDS frameworks were specifically tuned for Hawai‘i beaches, which partly contributed to RMSE values at Waikīkī (5.3 m for Landsat and 5.5 m for Sentinel-2) that compare favorably with those reported in the literature. However, comparing performance across sites is inherently difficult, as no two coastal settings are alike and multiple sources of uncertainty combine into a single RMSE value. Other studies report RMSEs at Columbia River littoral cell (Washington/Oregon), USA (22.4 m) [35] ; Duck (North Carolina), USA (7.6 m), Torrey Pines City Beach (California), USA (12.4 m), Narrabeen Beach, Australia (8.1 m), and for beaches at Truc Vert, France (24.8 m)[22] ; and Slapton, UK (14.0 m) and Perranporth, UK (22.2 m)[24].For PlanetScope imagery, CoastVision achieved an RMSE of 2.8 m with optimal processing (co-registration, tide, and set-up corrections). This is comparable to the pixel size (3.7 m) and slightly lower than other studies of shoreline detection on PlanetScope imagery, such as at Narrabeen, Australia (3.5 m RMSE 37) and Duck (North Carolina), USA (5.1 m RMSE and 5.8 m root mean square deviation [RMSD] 31).

These relatively low RMSE values likely reflect three contributing factors: (1) frameworks were optimized for this specific location and outliers were manually removed, (2) a large sample size and densely spaced transects produced a high n which could lower RMSE by minimizing influence of individual large outliers, and (3) shorelines at Waikīkī exhibit relatively low natural variability - an effect also noted by Vos et al. (2019)[12], who found that less variable coastlines tend to yield lower errors in SDS detection.

For example, beaches at both Waikīkī and Duck (North Carolina) are microtidal and exhibit low short-term variability 47. Waikīkī, which had the lowest RMSE, showed the least shoreline change, likely due to its reef-lined setting and limited water-level fluctuations. The survey data from Waikīkī show average weekly changes of only 1.0 m per transect and a standard deviation of 3.1 m across all 128 surveys – both of which are lower than the RMSE of CoastSat-derived shorelines. However, over the span of observations, which includes construction of a groin and beach renourishment in 2021, total shoreline positional range (i.e., maximum minus minimum position) exceeded 8 m for all transects and reached up to 24 m for the most dynamic transect. This is detectable by SDS frameworks with RMSE values around 5 m, such as CoastSat. Use of PlanetScope, with its higher temporal and spatial resolution, may allow for detection of shorter-term variability and improve shoreline accuracy.

A specific limitation of CoastSat was observed in the western section of Waikīkī, where a groin led the algorithm to smooth across the structure, misplacing the shoreline and introducing a landward bias. This highlights potential issues when applying SDS frameworks near coastal infrastructure, pocket beaches, or narrow natural headlands.

Obtainable improvements with water-level corrections in SDS frameworks

Water-level variability remains a site-specific source of systematic (tide, set-up) and random (swash) errors in SDS, influenced by ocean conditions and coastal geomorphology. Correcting for water-level is a standardized step in most SDS frameworks that derive shorelines from individual images, to ensure that shorelines are suitable for analysis of both trends and variability. Some studies show that yearly image composites or masking out high or low tide can effectively obtain suitable long-term trends 6,11,48,] but this limits the higher temporal monitoring capabilities for SDS. It remains important to acknowledge water-level variability and identify effective correction methods for specific coastal regions.

Using a time-averaged slope, our results support the consensus that tidal corrections improve SDS accuracy across various coasts, although the improvements for a microtidal reef-fringed coast are modest compared to other sites 22,28,35,37. In contrast, wave set-up corrections produced mixed results. Studies on high-energy, low-sloping beaches highlight wave-induced water-levels as critical sources of error 23. Other studies find only moderate or negligible improvement compared to tide-only corrections 35,37. In terms of accuracy metrics, this study shows modest improvements for PlanetScope shorelines with wave set-up corrections by reducing a landward bias (from -3.35 m to -1.09 m). In contrast, set-up correction increased RMSE for CoastSat shorelines. Analyzing the shoreline contour elevation highlights a new nuance to set-up corrections: For all satellites, set-up corrections notably reduced the number of high-elevation landward data points (Fig. 6), even for CoastSat-derived shorelines, despite reduced accuracy metrics. While this study focuses on one microtidal beach, these findings highlight the importance of both wave set-up corrections and proxy-datum bias adjustments, which account for the preferential detection of different shoreline features (e.g., low water line vs. waterline). Notably, these two factors may be interrelated — large wave conditions can generate extensive swash zones, increasing the likelihood that the detected shoreline corresponds to a specific swash phase rather than a consistent tidal datum. Addressing both sources of error can help ensure consistent shoreline contours.

As water-level corrections depend on accurate local ocean data and beach slope, we briefly compared different data sources for waves, water-level, and beach slope. For wave data, we compared a nearby buoy with (1) model output of Simulated WAves Nearshore (SWAN) and (2) global reanalysis data from ERA-5 (Figs. S3 & S4). While SWAN data has been verified for the coast of Hawaiʻi 49, we found that global reanalysis models are unsuitable for SDS corrections at Waikīkī, as they drastically overestimate wave height and neglect the island’s sheltering effects during winter swells (Fig. S4a). This indicates that using global wave models for SDS processing, especially for islands with variable wave climates, may not be appropriate. Local tide gauge data and the global tidal model AVISO FES2014 are comparable, but the tide model lacks resolution of sea-level anomalies 50, leading to minor underestimation/errors in water-level throughout the study period. Although these differences can be significant, particularly in the summer months (Fig. S4c), AVISO FES2014 appears to be suitable for tidal corrections for these micro-tidal environments when no nearby tide gauge is available.

The final component to water-level corrections is foreshore beach slope, which is difficult to obtain without consistent in situ monitoring. Comparing slopes obtained from sUAS-derived DEMs, CoastSat.slope, and 2012–2015 GPS profile data, we observed differing alongshore patterns between methods: For example, CoastSat reports steep slopes (0.18–0.2), at the beach’s western end, while DEMs and profile data show more gradual slopes (~ 0.08). Visual observations confirm this area shows high temporal variability, with steeper slopes and small erosional scarps forming during storms. These findings align with Graffin et al. (2024)[35], who found that CoastSat tends to report steeper slopes compared to in situ data. Our results suggest that in regions with significant slope variability, CoastSat.slope may tend to report steeper slopes, resulting in smaller horizontal adjustment when correcting SDW.

Overall, slope variability leads to only small differences in the effectiveness of tidal corrections at Waikīkī. Observed (DEM-derived) beach slopes ranged from 0.025 to 0.16, with the lowest values occurring near groin structures at beach edges (Fig. S5). This relatively wide range in slope suggests that in environments with larger tidal amplitudes, incorporating time-varying slope estimates could substantially affect water-level corrections. While previous studies have similarly reported minimal differences between time-varying and time-averaged slope approaches, these findings are based on only two beaches, and with slope data obtained with multiday time-lags (Narrabeen, Australia and Duck, USA) and each study used slightly different methods 23,36,37. Brown et al. (2025)[31] used concurrent light detection and ranging (lidar)-derived slope and found improvements using time-varying slopes over average slope. They also found that the use of only tide correction combined with an averaged slope yielded the largest error out of all combinations (time-varying slope and various calculations of swash and run-up) 31. As such, the generalizability of these results remains uncertain. For this microtidal reef-lined beach, we find that slope data had a relatively minor influence on SDS accuracy compared to other improvements – most notably outlier removal, which led to the largest improvement in RMSE and R2 (Fig. S6) followed by co-registration.

Do satellite-derived waterlines represent a consistent geomorphic feature?

Finally, this study provides a first evaluation of how consistently SDWs align with coastal features (low water mark versus water line versus high water line) and how this varies with image resolution and ocean conditions. Comparisons to geomorphic features are approximations and should not be interpreted as definitive mappings, given the temporal mismatch between satellite overpass and ground-truth imagery, as well as the dynamic nature of coastal environments. In many cases, SDWs do not clearly correspond to traditional geomorphic features. Despite this uncertainty, results demonstrate a clear difference in consistency of SDW placement across satellite sources. Mid-resolution imagery (Landsat 7, Landsat 8, and Sentinel-2) shows considerable variability, with only about 50% of SDWs aligning with the expected “waterline zone” i.e., the waterline including a narrow swash zone subject to small short-term excursions. In contrast, high-resolution imagery from PlanetScope produces markedly more consistent placements, suggesting higher reliability for shoreline detection at finer spatial scales. This analysis compares SDWs to surveys within three days; however, we observe similar trends using a six-day window, with waterline detections of 58% and 55% for Landsat 7 and Landsat 8, 49% and 48% for Sentinel-2, and 97% and 96% for PlanetScope, respectively. Although this analysis is based on a single beach, the findings align with observations by Konstantinou et al. (2023)24, who noted that CoastSat tends to capture the downrush limit on reflective beaches but misidentifies inland features on dissipative beaches due to pooling. Waikīkī, with its relatively steep, reflective morphology, appears consistent with this pattern: most SDWs fall near the low water mark or waterline.

For accurate monitoring of a specific contour, such as 0 m MSL, variability in SDW consistency affects how water-level corrections are applied. At Waikīkī, nearly half of CoastSat-derived waterlines align with the low water mark (i.e. close to low tide level) (Fig. 8). In these cases, applying wave set-up corrections does not improve accuracy relative to 0 m MSL. However, when the detected shoreline aligns with the high water line, as in a smaller subset of Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope images, set-up corrections help align the SDW to a fixed datum such as MSL. In this microtidal, steep beach environment, most shorelines are detected at the instantaneous waterline or low water line, making tidal corrections useful, but limiting the benefits of wave set-up adjustments.

Placements of SDWs also vary depending on thresholding techniques 31 and manually-labelled training data used for classification models. CoastVision is sensitive to the location of the land–water interface in the training data, whereas CoastSat is more influenced by the chosen thresholding method. For example, Brown et al. (2025) [31] compared Otsu and Weighted Peaks thresholding applied to water indices with Sentinel and PlanetScope imagery and found that Otsu consistently placed SDWs more seaward. In our study, using a locally trained classification model that tends to classify more sand pixels resulted in SDWs being placed more landward and closer to mean sea level, as this model more often informed the MNDWI threshold. However, differences in classification model did not affect consistency (e.g., STD; Table S1), indicating that while processing choices can shift the absolute position of the SDW (i.e., bias), small tweaks do not necessarily change precision (i.e. consistency) of the SDS framework.

This distinction between bias and consistency highlights a broader point: SDWs may not correspond to a fixed geomorphic feature but instead reflect a reproducible, internally consistent proxy tied to the sensor, algorithm, and processing choices. In this sense, SDWs function more as floating reference lines, potentially ambiguous in terms of geomorphic meaning, but valuable for capturing shoreline change when methods are held constant. Notably, higher-resolution imagery improves precision, as shown by the smaller spread of PlanetScope shorelines compared to Landsat or Sentinel-2. This interpretation suggests that traditional geomorphic vocabulary may not fully capture the nature of SDWs, and it may not always be possible - or appropriate - to describe them using traditional shoreline definitions. A clearer conceptual and terminological bridge could better connect SDW-derived products with legacy coastal datasets and frameworks. Image properties and research opportunities

While this study focused on the impact of spatial resolution and SDS framework, other sensor- and image processing-related factors also influence shoreline detection accuracy and proxy consistency. These include differences in spectral resolution, sensor design, and image pre-processing. Future studies could evaluate how sensor types (e.g., the whiskbroom scanner of Landsat 7 ETM+ versus the pushbroom scanner of Landsat 8, Sentinel-2, and PlanetScope), and spectral properties (band centers, widths, and resolution) influence shoreline detection. For example, PlanetScope-0 (Dove-Classic) has broader, overlapping bands, whereas newer PlanetScope sensors (Dove-R, SuperDove) feature narrower, non-overlapping bands more comparable to Sentinel-2 51.

All imagery in this study was processed to top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance, as this is the established approach within CoastSat[12] which avoids potential ocean-specific artifacts introduced by surface reflectance (SR) products 52,53. However, uncorrected atmospheric effects, especially under humid or hazy conditions, could reduce the reliability of spectral thresholds. Future studies may benefit from evaluating SR products as processing pipelines develop.

Image processing techniques such as pansharpening and spectral unmixing could be further explored. Spectral unmixing, which estimates fractional pixel composition (e.g., percent water or sand cover) has been successfully applied to mid-resolution sensors such as Landsat for waterbody and shoreline detection 54–58. These techniques may aid in sub-pixel detection rather than relying on a subpixel contouring methodology. However, its application to high-resolution sensors such as PlanetScope remains limited. Hyperspectral imagery, which provides finer spectral resolution, may further improve unmixing accuracy by better distinguishing between mixed surface materials along complex shorelines 57,58.

Finally, high-resolution imagery (40–60 cm; e.g., WorldView-2, QuickBird-2) has been used for shoreline detection 59–61, but it remains unclear whether continued increases in spatial resolution yield proportional gains in shoreline accuracy and consistency. At very high resolution, factors such as geolocation error and feature ambiguity (e.g., run-up, wet sand) may limit further improvements. While our results demonstrate that higher-resolution imagery improves RMSE and the consistency of shoreline proxy selection, additional research can clarify whether spatial resolution is the dominant source of positional uncertainty and if further improvements can be obtained through improved spectral resolution or image processing. Alternative approaches, such as video or short-interval imagery from satellites, may help resolve some of these uncertainties by capturing short-term variability in shoreline position and water level.

Conclusion

Evaluating the accuracy and uncertainty of satellite shoreline analyses can benefit coastal monitoring efforts. This study introduces CoastVision and evaluates its performance alongside CoastSat through a comparison with 3 years of ground and aerial surveys at a reef-fringed beach in Waikīkī, Hawai‘i. Our results show high accuracy across all satellite missions compared to prior studies, which likely reflects site-specific tuning, manually removed outliers, and relatively low shoreline variability at this location. The best-performing combination of processing steps yielded RMSEs of 5.41 m (Landsat 7), 5.19 m (Landsat 8), 5.54 m (Sentinel-2), and 2.83 m (PlanetScope).

Error mitigation strategies included outlier removal, image co-registration, site-specific classification model training, and water-level corrections for tides (based on tide gauge and tide model) and wave set-up. All satellite mission data were improved by manually removing outliers, with the higher resolution data showing the largest reductions in RMSE: 3.6 m (PlanetScope), 2.2 m (Sentinel-2), 0.9 m (Landsat 8), and 0.5 m (Landsat 7). Co-registering Sentinel-2 imagery yielded a significant improvement, reducing RMSE by 2.1 m (26%), while co-registering PlanetScope data showed negligible improvement.

We find mixed results in the improvement of shoreline accuracy when applying water-level corrections (tidal versus wave set-up). Notably, tidal corrections reduced SDS errors across all satellite platforms, where RMSE was reduced by 0.2 to 0.5 m; tide gauge data, which includes local water-level anomalies, provided a minor improvement over the FES2014 global tide model. By contrast, wave set-up corrections actually increased both the seaward bias and RMSE in Landsat and Sentinel-2 SDS, but showed substantial improvement for PlanetScope (in conjunction with CoastVision processing), reducing a mean landward bias by 2.3 m and improving RMSE from 4.2 to 2.8 m.

We also provide the first evaluation of how consistently SDWs align with geomorphic features such as the waterline or low water mark. While there is significant uncertainty due to temporal mismatch, results show a clear difference in geomorphic detection consistency across sensors. Mid-resolution imagery (e.g., Landsat, Sentinel-2) shows higher variability, with only 59% and 48% of SDWs aligning with the expected waterline zone for Landsat and Sentinel-2, respectively. In contrast, high-resolution imagery from PlanetScope produces more consistent placements (97% waterline), suggesting higher reliability for shoreline detection at finer spatial scales. However, SDWs do not always correspond neatly to traditional shoreline definitions, and a conceptual and terminological bridge could help align SDW-derived products with legacy coastal datasets and frameworks.

Given the complex interplay of factors influencing SDS accuracy, such as wave conditions, tidal range, beach slope, and geomorphic variability, it remains unclear how well specific pre- and post-processing steps translate across diverse coastal settings. Our findings show that unresolved uncertainties in SDS remain. Investigating geomorphic feature identification at other sites can help determine whether the biases observed here persist under different pixel resolutions and oceanographic or geophysical conditions. This study also underscores the usefulness of ongoing refinement in satellite data processing, standardization of corrections and uncertainty quantification, and the importance of high spatiotemporal resolution surveys for ground truthing.

Methods

Site

This study focuses on the beach at Kahaloa (hereafter referred to as Kahaloa beach), also known as the “Royal Hawaiian Beach” or “Waikīkī Beach” in Waikīkī, Hawai‘i [45] . Unlike the large, uninterrupted sandy shorelines of continental beaches, Kahaloa beach is a relatively short, 520 m crescent-shaped beach bounded by terminal groins 45,62. The beach is fronted by a shallow, gently-sloping reef platform characterized by a complex nearshore bathymetry that plays a significant role in shaping nearshore wave energy dissipation and sediment transport patterns. The sand is carbonate, with grain sizes ranging from 0.29 mm to 0.40 mm, though this varies due to engineering projects 63. Kahaloa beach is an engineered shoreline, that has undergone significant coastal development since the 1890s, including sand nourishment, reef dredging, and the construction of groins and seawalls to retain sand 62. Chronic erosion necessitates regular interventions to maintain beach width and protect backshore coastal infrastructure.

Kahaloa beach is located on a microtidal coastline with tidal ranges up to 1 m and seasonally varying wave climate 64. Summer swells from the Southern Ocean bring the largest waves of the year with significant wave heights of 1–5 m, whereas locally-generated trade-wind waves are smaller, have shorter periods, and occur year-round. While the beach is sheltered from large winter north swells by the island, it is periodically impacted by Kona storms or occasional tropical cyclones.

In situ surveys

Between April 2018 and July 2021, 128 near-weekly ground and aerial surveys were conducted of Kahaloa beach. For technical details, refer to Mikkelsen et al. (2024) 38. Below we include a brief description of survey and data processing.

For each survey, imagery with 80% side and front overlap was captured using a sUAS (DJI Phantom 4 Pro v1 and v2) at an altitude of 120 m above ground using flight mapping software DroneDeploy (v3.17 https://www.dronedeploy.com). The sUAS imagery was georeferenced using seven ground control points (GCPs) spaced about 100 m apart alongshore. GCP positions were surveyed using a rod-mounted prism and a Leica TS16 Robotic Total Station with millimeter-level accuracy. In addition, points were collected every 5–15 m along the seaward edge of the foreshore, marked by the top of the beach toe, which is approximately equal to the low water mark (LWM) datum 40. Each survey was conducted around sunrise, to capture the beach before it became crowded (e.g. people, beach umbrellas, canoes), but irrespective of tide level and wave conditions. Photogrammetric processing was performed using Agisoft Metashape (v1.6 https://www.agisoftmetashape.com [formerly Photoscan]) in accordance with established U.S. Geological Survey protocols to produce a dense point cloud and an image orthomosaic 65. Noise and artifacts were removed from the dense point cloud, and ground points identified using Rapidlasso’s LAStools (https://rapidlasso.de, 2019 release). Point clouds were cropped to a standardized extent and merged with manually collected beach toe points to enable smooth interpolation across the wave-affected foreshore. A 0.5 m DEM was generated using natural neighbor interpolation and smoothed with a 5 m radius circular filter to remove residual elevation anomalies. Shoreline vectors were generated at MSL on the resulting DEMs. Data used here, including the shoreline contours, are available on Figshare 38. To assess vertical uncertainty in the foreshore zone, we compared the DEM to 107 independent survey points below 0.6 m elevation. The comparison yielded a mean vertical bias of + 5.2 cm and a STD of 7.3 cm. Given an average foreshore slope of 0.12, this vertical offset corresponds to a seaward horizontal bias of approximately 0.43 m in shoreline position.

Satellite-derived shorelines

To derive shorelines from satellite imagery, two different frameworks were employed: (1) CoastSat, a well-established open-source package compatible with Landsat 5, 7, 8, and 9, and Sentinel-2 imagery 12; and (2) CoastVision, a toolset developed specifically for PlanetScope imagery. Both frameworks were applied to the same region of interest (ROI) with a hand-annotated reference shoreline and the timeframe April 2018 to July 2021. In the following text we provide descriptions of each framework and methodologies for comparison against ground-truth data.

CoastSat

CoastSat is a Python-based open-source tool designed to map the instantaneous waterline within a satellite image across any region worldwide. It retrieves relevant Landsat 5–9 and Sentinel-2 images via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) API 15 for specified regions and time frames of interest. CoastSat processes Landsat imagery by upsampling its top-of-atmosphere (TOA) multispectral bands (Blue, Green, Red, Near-Infrared [NIR]) from 30 m to 15 m using pansharpening for Landsat 7, 8, and 9, and bilinear interpolation for Landsat 5. Similarly, Sentinel-2 imagery, with a native resolution of 10 m for Blue, Green, Red, and NIR bands, undergoes upsampling of its Short-Wave Infrared 1 (SWIR1) band from 20 to 10 m per pixel.

To delineate the land/water interface, each pixel in the image is classified as ‘sand’, ‘water’, ‘whitewash’, or ‘other land’. The shoreline position is identified along a threshold of the odified normalized difference water index (MNDWI). This index, derived from the green and short-wave infrared (SWIR) bands, enhances contrast between open water and land features. The threshold for shoreline delineation is determined in one of two ways, depending on how many sand pixels are identified. If less than 50 sand pixels are identified, it applies a simple MNDWI thresholding, or if more than 50 sand pixels are identified, the Otsu threshold that maximizes the distinction between ‘sand’ and ‘water’ is used to determine the waterline position 66. Lastly, the marching squares algorithm [69] is applied with the threshold to obtain sub-pixel waterline detection.

CoastSat integrates a pre-trained multilayer perceptron classification model trained on imagery from five silicate beaches on the east coast of Australia. We use CoastSat’s built-in feature to train a new multilayer perceptron classification model specifically for Hawai‘i using 58 images from eight carbonate beaches on O‘ahu. The dataset included clear images as well as those with light shadows and haze, reflecting common conditions in Hawai‘i. When annotating training data, the distinction between ‘sand’ and ‘water’ was drawn at the instantaneous waterline, to the best of the annotator’s ability. The locally trained model eliminated a significant seaward bias observed in other SDS studies 28,29,68 ,although no changes were observed in the overall standard deviation between the two models (Table S1).

CoastVision

CoastVision was developed to extract shorelines from PlanetScope imagery, supplementing those obtained by CoastSat from Landsat and Sentinel-2. After this project began, an extension of CoastSat, called CoastSat.PlanetScope, was developed to process PlanetScope imagery similarly to CoastSat. The main distinction between CoastSat.PlanetScope and the CoastVision approach developed here lies in their shoreline detection methods: CoastSat.PlanetScope applies a threshold to a water index (NIR minus Blue), whereas CoastVision delineates the shoreline directly from the classified image. For Hawai‘i’s beaches, we found that direct contouring provided more reliable results, while the threshold-based method sometimes introduced errors around reefs, and wet sand, though not quantified for this study. Like CoastSat and CoastSat.PlanetScope, CoastVision is a Python-based tool that adopts an overall similar architecture to CoastSat including image download, processing, shoreline extraction, and transect intersections. It uses PlanetScope’s API 32 to automate downloads with user-specified criteria such as ROI, date-range, and maximum cloud cover threshold. It processes PlanetScope’s ‘Basic Analytic 4 band Scene’ data (PS2.SD and PSB.SD instruments) that are scaled to top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance, for consistency with CoastSat. The ground sampling distance of these images ranges from 3.7 to 4.1 m but they are automatically resampled to 3 m, which which may reduce sharpness at land-water boundaries.

To process each image, CoastVision classifies each pixel into either ‘land’ or ‘water’. The shoreline is directly delineated between these two classes without reliance on a water-index thresholding approach. The classification model is a logistic regression that uses 12 input features, which include band values, calculated indices (e.g., NDVI and MNDWI), and the standard deviation (texture) of values within a two-pixel radius of each pixel (refer to Table S2). The classifier is trained on a wide variety of shoreline images across Hawai‘i with variable conditions. Pixels in the training data were manually labeled as either land or water, with the division made at the instantaneous waterline.

To minimize errors from misclassified pixels, a binary opening morphological filter 33 is applied. Small objects are removed, and users can specify a minimum land size. A kernel of 3 × 3 pixels effectively minimized speckles of misclassified pixels for beaches in Hawai‘i but may not generalize well to other more dynamic coastlines. Similar to CoastSat, CoastVision employs the marching squares algorithm 69 to delineate the shoreline.

In addition to increased spatial resolution, PlanetScope offers near daily coverage starting around 2016, compared to Landsat and Sentinel-2’s sixteen- and five-day revisit time, respectively. This combination allows for detecting long-term changes from Landsat starting in 1990 alongside more frequent observations from PlanetScope. PlanetScope imagery was provided by Planet Labs PBC (https://www.planet.com/) under a research license. Daily, 3 m resolution PlanetScope imagery was obtained for Waikīkī, Oʻahu between April 2018 and August 2021. A GitHub link to CoastVision is provided under Code availability.

Error mitigation approaches

Co-registration

To minimize spatial variability between images, we performed co-registration (image-to-image matching) using the open-source package AROSICS 70 on Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope images. AROSICS automates co-registration using phase correlation in the frequency domain to achieve sub-pixel corrections. A clear, cloud free, focused PlanetScope image was selected as reference for co-registration for both PlanetScope and Sentinel-2, which works for this relatively short timeframe of study. For a longer timeseries analysis, one might consider updating the reference image at a set interval. We used a grid size of 50 following the methodologies of Doherty et al. (2022) [37].

This small beach is backed by high-rise buildings and fronted by a visible reef beneath shallow, clear water. We briefly tested co-registration improvements by masking high-rises, which introduce relief displacement, and by masking ocean pixels, as the water column may obscure the location of underwater features. However, neither mask improved accuracy compared to ground-truth shorelines. In another study, this same co-registration approach was integrated into the CoastSat.PlanetScope framework, though it was not reported whether this improved shoreline detection accuracy 37. Overall, co-registration appears to be an important step to improve accuracy of Sentinel-2 shorelines.

Sources of water-level data

Water-level and wave data were obtained from multiple sources to assess the impact of modeled vs. measured ocean data on SDS accuracy. Water-levels were obtained from: (1) Honolulu tide gauge (station ID 1612340) 42, and (2) global tide model AVISO Finite Element Solution 2014 (FES2014) 71, from a grid located approximately 1 mile offshore. AVISO FES2014 uses 34 tidal constituents to provide an estimate of tidal height at a spatial resolution of 1/16 degrees (~ 6.5 km at Waikīkī). In contrast, Honolulu tide gauge, operational since 1905, captures local water-level anomalies in addition to tidal variations.

Wave data, including significant wave height and peak wave period, were collected from: (1) a PacIOOS wave buoy located on the south shore of O‘ahu (CDIP buoy #233) providing hourly measurements, (2) a local nearshore wave model, SWAN (Simulating WAves Nearshore), provided by PacIOOS at a resolution of 0.5 km [43], and (3) The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) global reanalysis ERA-5 (ECMWF Reanalysis version 5), available at a resolution of 0.5 degrees (~ 51.9 km at Waikīkī) 72.

Foreshore beach slope

The foreshore beach slope was derived from three different sources (Fig. 5). First, from sUAS-derived DEMs we used a time-averaged beach slope. We assume a linear slope between -0.2 m and 0.3 m above MSL along each cross-shore transect. DEMs derived from sUAS have uncertainties, particularly in the swash zone, where wave action adds noise to the photogrammetry process. These data were processed by including manually collected points at the seaward edge of the beach, interpolating across the foreshore and applying a mean 5 m radius circular filter, which showed good agreement with accuracy assessments 38. However, these smoothing approaches may dampen alongshore variability in slope.

The second source of slope values are from data collected between 2012 and 2015, comprising 18 transects at the study site with 15 repeat measurements 45. For each transect, we calculated a linear beach slope between the top of the beach toe and the berm crest, which were noted in the datasheets. If either point was missing, slope was not calculated.

Lastly, foreshore slopes were derived using the open-source package CoastSat.slope 44. CoastSat.slope uses water-level and shoreline variability to estimate the foreshore slope that reduces variability related to tides. It performs well in low-sloping macro tidal environments, but its accuracy decreases in regions with small tidal ranges and steep beaches, where horizontal shoreline shifts are minimal. We used CoastSat-derived shorelines from Landsat (2000–2022) and a water-level time series from Honolulu tide gauge with CoastSat.slope to obtain a single time-averaged slope for each transect.

Outlier removal

All accuracy metrics in this paper are based on data with outliers manually removed. Outliers in SDS may arise from haze, clouds or cloud shadows, building shadows, misclassified pixels, inland water bodies or water pools on the beach, white wash, or shallow reefs emerging out of the water, and represent a persistent challenge for automated SDS workflows. Before any manual or automated review, CoastSat and CoastVision automatically filter usable images based on georeferencing error thresholds, cloud cover, and area coverage. For 3.5 years of data at Waikīkī, 1,437 images were available across all sensors, of which392 images (27%) were automatically discarded, leaving 1,045 images from which shorelines were contoured. We manually reviewed these shorelines and removed an additional 534 (38% of total;Landsat 7: 11 of 40 [28%], Landsat 8:6 of 45 [13%], Sentinel-2: 121 of 273 [44%], PlanetScope: 396 of 687 [56%]) yielding 511 accepted shorelines. Of the 273 Sentinel-2 images contoured, 232 were successfully co-registered; the remainder were excluded due to co-registration errors or insufficient image overlap.

Manual outlier removal allowed us to compare frameworks and sensors without confounding errors caused by outliers. Scaling SDS requires some form of automated outlier detection. We tested a simple median filter that excludes shoreline positions falling more than a set distance from the transect’s median location. Survey data indicates a maximum of 30 m differences across any transect over the period of study, so we used the transect median ± 20 m, allowing for a 40 m total variability.

The median filter accepted the majority of manually flagged outliers for Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope (80 of 121 and 310 of 396 respectively), resulting in RMSE remaining 1.3 m and 1.1 m higher than manual review for these sensors (Table S1). However, the filter still improved overall performance metrics by eliminating the most extreme values. Performance was better for Landsat, where fewer outliers were present and RMSE values from automated filtering were within 0.1–0.5 m of manual review. The median filter has been applied to other beaches in Hawai‘i [20], and may generalize to similarly low-variability shorelines. However, it is unlikely to be effective at sites with high shoreline variability or lacking in situ data to guide threshold selection. In such cases, visual inspection of SDS overlays or more adaptive statistical filters (e.g., interquartile range) may offer better alternatives 16.
