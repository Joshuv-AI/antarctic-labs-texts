Role of the S1P/S1PR axis in inflammation and structural damage in psoriatic arthritis: a cross-sectional study with clinical, biochemical and imaging correlation
Authors: Dolores Ruiz-Montesinos, Dolores Mendoza-Mendoza, Jesús Bocio Núñez, Carmen Dominguez-Quesada, Maria Luisa Serrano-Guijo, María Ángeles Vázquez-Gámez, Miguel Ángel Rico, Miguel Ángel Colmenero, José Javier Perez-Venegas, María José Montoya-García, Mercè Giner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease with a complex clinical spectrum. Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and its receptor (S1PR) are key lipid mediators involved in immunity and bone remodelling. This study evaluates the inter-relationship between serum levels of S1P and S1PR and clinical activity, radiological findings and different stages of PsA.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted including 64 subjects: 21 healthy controls and 43 patients with PsA: 16 with recent-onset PsA and 27 with established PsA (EPsA). Clinical variables such as disease activity in PsA (DAPSA), bone mineral density (BMD), Trabecular Bone Score, 3D-Shaper and inflammatory activity by Doppler ultrasound and MRI were assessed. S1P and S1PR levels were measured by ELISA.ResultsS1P levels were significantly higher in the PsA group compared with controls (p=0.013), with the highest values observed in the EPsA subgroup (p=0.009). Although no significant correlation was observed with the DAPSA Score, S1PR levels were significantly higher in patients with Achilles enthesitis detected by ultrasound (p=0.046) and in those with bone proliferation/structural damage (p=0.03). Patients with EPsA showed significantly higher cortical volumetric BMD compared with controls (p=0.027).ConclusionsThe S1P/S1PR axis is dysregulated in PsA and is associated with disease progression. Its correlation with ultrasound findings of enthesitis and new bone formation suggests that S1P acts as a critical mediator in the osteoimmunology of the enthesis, positioning it as a potential biomarker of structural damage and a novel therapeutic target.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory arthropathy associated with psoriasis (Ps). Classified within the spectrum of spondyloarthritis (axSpA), it shares a common pathophysiological origin centred on enthesis involvement. This condition presents with a broad clinical spectrum that includes peripheral and axial joint manifestations, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin and nail lesions, uveitis, a strong genetic and immunological association with inflammatory bowel disease, and multiple comorbidities.1 2

Although PsA was previously regarded as a relatively mild disease, it is now recognised to have a significant impact on patients’ quality of life due to systemic inflammation, social stigma and associated comorbidities. The relationship between Ps and PsA is well established, with Ps preceding the onset of arthritis in most patients.1 Geographical variability exists in incidence and prevalence, with estimates of 3–23.1 cases per 100 000 inhabitants reported in Europe and North America, and onset typically occurring between the third and fifth decades of life. The incorporation of the CASPAR classification criteria has influenced the reported prevalence of PsA.3–5

The aetiopathogenesis of PsA is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers such as infections, trauma and alterations in the microbiome, as well as dysregulated innate and adaptive immune responses.6 7 T lymphocytes and other immune cells play central roles in perpetuating inflammation. Activation of dendritic cells and macrophages contributes to the release of cytokines such as interleukin (IL) 1, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-23. Biomechanical stress at the enthesis can stimulate IL-23 release, promoting the activation of Th17 cells and subsequent production of inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-17 and IL-22.8

Sphingolipids are essential components of mammalian cell membranes and serve as precursors for key signalling molecules, including ceramide, sphingosine and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P). S1P signalling through its receptor S1PR1 regulates T cell development, promotes synoviocyte proliferation, enhances inflammatory cytokine expression, and contributes to osteoclastogenesis and bone homoeostasis. Notably, S1P supports both cell proliferation and survival.9 10

In inflammatory conditions, proinflammatory cytokines increase S1P production, influencing bone remodelling by attracting osteoblasts and modulating osteoclast differentiation.10–12 S1P also plays a role in sustaining chronic inflammation by regulating lymphocyte trafficking and amplifying IL-17 production in chondrocytes and osteoblasts. Serum concentrations of S1P in humans typically range from 200 nM to 900 nM, and its assessment may offer additional predictive value in PsA.13

A deep understanding of the aetiopathogenic mechanisms of PsA is crucial for the development of more effective and personalised therapeutic strategies that may improve the prognosis and quality of life of affected patients. There is growing evidence that S1P metabolism is dysregulated in PsA and that S1P may contribute to pathological ossification of the enthesis. Therefore, our objective is to determine the inter-relationship between serum S1P levels and its receptor S1PR at the different stages of PsA, correlating them with clinical and radiological indices. Consequently, inhibition of S1P metabolism could represent a novel therapeutic approach for treating abnormal ossification.

Material and methods

This was a cross-sectional observational study. Subjects were recruited consecutively and randomly from the Rheumatology Unit and the Internal Medicine Unit at Virgen Macarena University Hospital (Seville) between September 2019 and December 2021. All patients and controls provided written informed consent prior to participation.

The sample size was determined for a comparative study involving three independent groups. For the calculation, mean S1P values from previous studies with similar variability were assumed. The planned statistical analysis was a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a significance level of α=0.05 and a statistical power of 80%.

The sample size calculation was performed using the G*Power V.3.1 software (University of Düsseldorf, Germany), indicating that a total of 15 participants per group, for a total of 45 participants, would be required. This sample size provides power well above the minimum required and ensures adequate stability of the results

Eighty-nine patients with active plaque-type Ps and 21 sex-matched and age-matched healthy controls were included. PsA was classified using the CASPAR criteria.4 Subjects were allocated into two groups: patients with PsA (TPsA) and health controls (C). Patients with PsA were further subdivided into two groups: recent-onset PsA (RPsA), defined as a disease duration of <12 months from onset of articular symptoms, and established PsA (EPsA), defined as disease duration >12 months with evidence of osteoproliferation and/or erosion.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, absence of conditions interfering with sphingosine or ligand measurement, and no contraindications for low-field extremity MRI (0.2 T). All patients followed a structured evaluation protocol (figure 1). A clinical questionnaire and imaging studies were performed to assess bone mineral density (BMD), bone microarchitecture by 3D-Shaper and Trabecular Bone Score (TBS), MRI and Doppler ultrasound.

Clinical questionnaire

Variables collected included age, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), health habits (smoking, alcohol consumption), physical activity (sedentary <2 hours/week; light 2–6 hours/week; moderate >6 hours/week), current and previous pharmacological treatments, and comorbidities.

Biochemical measurements

Peripheral blood samples were collected before initiating treatment in both patients and controls. After centrifugation, serum was stored at −80 °C. High-sensitivity C reactive protein (CRP) was measured using an ADVIA 2400 autoanalyser (Siemens). S1P levels were quantified by ELISA (Echelon BI, USA) and S1PR by ELISA (Biomatik, USA).

BMD and bone microarchitecture

Lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck and total hip BMD were measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry using a Hologic Discovery W densitometer with APEX V.3.1.1 software. In vivo coefficients of variation were 2.4% (femoral neck), 1.1% (total hip) and 2.5% (lumbar spine).

DXA-based 3D modelling of hip scans was performed using 3D-SHAPER (cortical and trabecular bone from DXA; DXA: Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry) software V.2.7, providing integral volumetric BMD (vBMD), cortical and trabecular vBMD, surface BMD (sBMD) and cortical thickness at each vertex of the 3D femoral model.

Trabecular Bone Score (TBS) was assessed using TBS iNsight software (V.3.0.3.0) from lumbar spine DXA images. TBS values were categorised as: normal (≥1.31), partially degraded (1.23–1.31) or degraded (≤1.23).

MRI

MRI was performed using a 0.2 Tesla system (Esaote, Italy) of the dominant hand, the right foot (or symptomatic foot) and the right heel (or symptomatic heel), assessing the Achilles enthesis and plantar fascia.

Images were scored according to Psoriatic Arthritis Magnetic Resonance Imaging Score (PsAMRIS) criteria for erosions and bone marrow oedema, recorded as 0=absent and 1=present, noting location (hand, foot, calcaneus).14 Enthesitis was defined by Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) as the presence of bone oedema, bursitis or soft tissue changes with STIR (short-tau inversion recovery) hyperintensity at the enthesis.15 16

Doppler ultrasound

Ultrasound (LOGIQ F8, GE) of the dominant hand (from distal radioulnar to distal interphalangeal joints), right or symptomatic foot (MTP joints: metatarsophalangeal), and right or symptomatic heel was performed.

Synovitis (grayscale and power Doppler), tenosynovitis and enthesitis were recorded according to OMERACT criteria.17 Active synovitis was defined as PD≥2. Achilles and plantar fascia enthesitis were evaluated per the Madrid Sonographic Enthesitis Index scoring system.18

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were summarised as mean±SD or median (min–max), qualitative variables as percentages. Associations between qualitative variables were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests; when assumptions were not met, Monte Carlo simulations or exact tests were used. Quantitative variables were compared using Student’s t-test or ANOVA when normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk), and Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests otherwise. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman coefficients, with 95% CIs. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.28.0 (IBM, 2021).

BMI was significantly higher in patients with PsA (TPsA: 30.1 kg/m²; EPsA: 30.5 kg/m²) compared with controls (20.6 kg/m²; p<0.05). In the subgroup with EPsA, significant differences were observed in S1P and S1PR1 levels according to BMI, with S1P showing an increase from overweight to obesity and a decline in morbid obesity, while S1PR1 progressively decreased as BMI increased (p=0.027 and p=0.048, respectively). In contrast, in early PSA no significant differences were identified in either marker (p>0.15). In the control group, S1P remained stable across BMI categories and S1PR1 (p=0.091). These results may suggest that the effect of BMI on S1P and its receptor depends on the clinical stage of the disease.

Smoking and alcohol consumption were more frequent among patients, particularly in the TPsA group (27.9% smokers; 7.1% alcohol use; p<0.05). Physical activity differed significantly: controls exhibited greater sedentary behaviour, whereas patients with PsA engaged more frequently in light or moderate activity (p<0.05).

Biochemical measurements were similar between groups, with no statistically significant differences.

Regarding treatment, 74.4% of patients with TPsA were receiving DMARD therapy. In both RPsA and EPsA (p=0.089), methotrexate was the most commonly used drug. None of the patients with RPsA were taking biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), whereas 11% of patients with EPsA were receiving a bDMARD (p=0.24), most frequently adalimumab (p=0.17). Steroid use was noted in 16.3% of patients with TPsA, with a median prednisone dose of 18 mg (range 5–30 mg), with no significant differences between subgroups in median dose (p=0.41) (table 1).

Imaging assessment of disease activity

All patients with PsA underwent MRI and Doppler ultrasound to assess inflammatory activity and structural damage. Over half of the TPsA group showed ultrasound-detected inflammatory activity, most frequently in the hands. Tenosynovitis and synovitis in the hands were the most common inflammatory findings on both ultrasound and MRI.

When comparing subgroups, the percentage of patients with inflammatory activity on ultrasound and MRI was similar between RPsA and EPsA. Erosion was present in both subgroups, although bone proliferation was more common in EPsA, where Achilles enthesophytes were also more frequently found (online supplemental table 1).

S1P and S1PR levels in study populations

Serum S1P levels were significantly higher in the TPsA group compared with controls (p=0.013). S1PR levels were slightly higher in TPsA but did not reach statistical significance (p=0.196) (figure 3).

S1P (A) and S1PR (B) levels in the control group versus total, recent and established PsA. (*p<0.05; **p<0.01). PsA, psoriatic arthritis; S1P, sphingosine-1-phosphate; S1PR, S1P receptor.

A trend towards increasing S1P levels with disease duration was observed, with patients with EPsA showing the highest concentrations, statistically significant when compared with controls (p=0.009). RPsA levels of S1P and S1PR did not differ significantly from controls (p=0.78), although values tended to be higher (figure 3).

On the other hand, in the EPsA group, there were 17 patients receiving biological therapy, with a mean S1P level of 3.25±1.36, and 10 patients not treated with biologics, with a mean S1P level of 3.43±0.93 (p=0.514). S1PR levels in the biologic-treated group were 2.54±1.26, whereas in the non-treated group they were 3.58±1.36 (p=0.045).

Correlation between clinical activity (DAPSA) and S1P/S1PR levels

To assess the usefulness of S1P and S1PR1 as markers of disease activity, patients were grouped according to DAPSA scores. No significant correlations were found between biomarker levels and disease activity, either in the total TPsA group or within subgroups.

Correlation between imaging findings (MRI and ultrasound) and S1P/S1PR levels

When comparing S1P/S1PR levels with MRI-detected bone oedema, S1PR levels tended to be higher in patients with oedema, although not significantly. Similarly, a non-significant upward trend in S1PR levels was noted in patients with synovitis and/or tenosynovitis. Ultrasound findings showed similar S1P/S1PR levels regardless of inflammation type or location, with no subgroup differences.

However, a significant association was observed between S1PR levels and Achilles enthesitis detected by ultrasound in the TPsA group (p=0.046) (figure 4).

Discussion

The findings of this study reinforce the role of S1P and its receptor in the pathophysiology of PsA, particularly regarding pathological ossification and enthesitis. Our results show that patients with PsA exhibit higher serum S1P levels than controls, with levels increasing in parallel with disease duration and being highest in those with established disease. These observations align with previous studies and demonstrate dysregulation of S1P metabolism in chronic inflammatory disorders, including PsA.9 19–21

The interaction between the immune system and bone remodelling, framed within the concept of osteoimmunology, is well established. Several studies have highlighted the involvement of S1P and its receptor S1PR in this interplay. The S1P–S1PR1 interaction regulates bone remodelling, promoting both osteoclastogenesis and osteogenesis.21–23 Furthermore, S1PR is expressed on lymphocytes, playing a key role in immune cell trafficking.21 The S1P–S1PR1 signalling pathway likely contributes to continuous bone turnover under physiological conditions and may drive pathological bone remodelling, manifesting as both new bone formation and erosion, during inflammation.13

Few studies have explored the role of the S1P/S1PR axis in axSpA. In psoriatic disease, biomechanical stress and inflammation at the enthesis may elevate S1P and S1PR expression, enhancing the recruitment of inflammatory cells and altering the osteoblast–osteoclast balance.

It is important to note that patients with PsA in our cohort exhibited significantly higher BMI compared with controls. Obesity is known to be associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and alterations in sphingolipid metabolism, including increased circulating S1P levels.23 Therefore, we cannot exclude that part of the elevation in S1P observed in the PsA group may be partially influenced by metabolic status rather than the disease itself. The findings indicate that BMI modulates the S1P/S1PR1 axis only in EPsA, where the increase in S1P together with the reduction in S1PR1 suggests a chronic inflammatory environment that may drive receptor dysregulation. The absence of any effect in early PsA suggests that these alterations require a sustained inflammatory state to become apparent. In controls, the stability of both markers, along with the lack of differences compared with the overall PsA group, supports that these changes cannot be explained by BMI alone nor by the mere presence of PsA, but rather by the interaction between obesity and the established phase of the disease. Overall, the results point to a disruption of the S1P/S1PR1 axis that depends on both BMI and the clinical stage.

Although we did not observe significant correlations between S1P/S1PR levels and clinical activity assessed by DAPSA, we did identify an upward trend in patients with greater inflammatory activity on MRI and ultrasound. These findings suggest that serum S1P levels may better reflect subclinical or structural inflammation rather than clinical disease indices. This is consistent with recent investigations highlighting the utility of serum biomarkers, such as inflammatory proteins (CRP, ESR, IL-6, TNF-α), markers of joint destruction (COMP, RANKL, MMP-3) and metabolites (oxidised phospholipids), in evaluating inflammatory activity.8 24

Imaging remains essential for evaluating enthesitis. In this study, both low-field MRI and ultrasound were used to assess joint and entheseal inflammation. The prevalence of hand erosions in our early PsA cohort (18.5%) is consistent with recent studies reporting erosions in approximately 17%–25% of newly diagnosed, untreated patients.25 Given the moderate-to-high disease activity observed in our sample, this finding is biologically plausible, as higher baseline DAPSA scores predict structural damage progression. Thus, our results fall within the expected range for early PsA and reflect the inflammatory burden at disease onset.26

On the other hand, our results showed that S1PR levels were higher in patients with ultrasound-confirmed Achilles enthesitis compared with those without. Unlike synovitis in the hands, enthesitis involves tendinous structures of the feet and Achilles tendon, where mechanical stress and cytokine release may stimulate S1P production by chondrocytes and osteoblasts, perpetuating inflammation. This observation was also supported by MRI findings and is consistent with previous reports.9

Data on osteoporosis in PsA are limited, and most studies have not identified significant differences between patients with PsA and controls. Moreover, BMD has generally not been correlated with disease activity.27–30

Our findings regarding structural damage indicate that patients with bone proliferation exhibit higher S1PR levels. This suggests a possible link between S1PR expression and increased osteoblast-driven RANKL production, which may enhance osteoclast precursor recruitment, as these cells express S1P receptors. Such findings corroborate reports from in vitro and in vivo studies, as well as research in patients with periodontitis, demonstrating that inflammatory conditions stimulate macrophage–stromal interactions, activate sphingosine-1-kinase and subsequently increase S1PR1 activity. This cascade enhances osteoclastogenesis and bone loss through the S1PR1–RANKL axis.31

From a therapeutic perspective, these results support the possibility of targeting the S1P/S1PR axis as a novel approach to PsA treatment. S1P pathway inhibitors are already being investigated in other autoimmune diseases, and their application in PsA may provide a strategy to slow structural progression, particularly in patients with active enthesopathy.10 32

The main limitations of this study include the sample size and the heterogeneity of prior antirheumatic treatments, which may affect biomarker expression. We also did not evaluate S1P levels in synovial fluid or at the enthesis, although such measurements, if they yielded positive findings, would not be practical as clinical biomarkers. The strength of this study lies in the association of S1P/S1PR biomarkers with MRI and ultrasound parameters, validated tools in early diagnosis and prognosis. Their sensitivity to early changes such as tenosynovitis and bone marrow oedema strengthens the clinical value of our biomarker correlations.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that serum S1P and S1PR levels are associated with structural features of PsA, particularly enthesitis and ossification, rather than with conventional clinical activity indices. These findings emphasise the importance of integrating molecular biomarkers with imaging techniques for a comprehensive assessment of PsA and highlight novel therapeutic targets for disease modification.
