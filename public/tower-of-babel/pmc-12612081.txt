Advances and prospects of respiratory mucosal vaccines: mechanisms, technologies, and clinical applications
Authors: Jiaxuan Li, Guangshang Wu, Zhiwei Huang, Jutao Hu, Xiaotian Tie, Hao Wu, Zhiyi Wang, Keda Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory mucosal immunity constitutes a critical barrier against pathogen invasion and has emerged as a promising target for vaccine development. This review outlines current understanding of its mechanisms, recent technological advances, and clinical progress. Compared with conventional vaccines, mucosal vaccines more effectively elicit localized and systemic immune responses. Key challenges and future directions are discussed, highlighting their potential in controlling emerging respiratory infections through improved immunological protection.Subject terms: Vaccines, Recombinant vaccine, Cellular immunity, Humoral immunity, Immune tolerance, Vaccines, Infection, Influenza virus, Viral infection, Adjuvants, Inactivated vaccines, Live attenuated vaccines, Protein vaccines, Influenza virus, Adaptive immunity, Innate immunity, Infection, Viral infection, Influenza virus, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

Respiratory infections remain one of the major global public health threats. According to the World Health Organization, respiratory diseases—such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and lower respiratory tract infections—rank among the leading causes of death, accounting for approximately 2.5 million deaths in 20191, underscoring their lethality. The COVID-19 pandemic has further demonstrated the high pathogenicity of respiratory pathogens, with around 776 million confirmed cases and 7 million deaths reported globally by the end of 20242.

Vaccination is one of the most effective means of preventing infections caused by respiratory pathogens. Traditional vaccines are mostly administered intramuscularly, which poses certain limitations. While the lower respiratory tract is mainly protected by serum IgG antibodies, the upper respiratory tract relies on secretory IgA (SIgA) antibodies. However, vaccines delivered via intramuscular or intradermal routes typically elicit IgG responses but fail to effectively induce mucosal IgA responses3.

Mucosal vaccines have emerged as a frontier in vaccine development due to their capacity to induce localized immune responses at mucosal barriers during the early stages of infection, thereby preventing pathogen adherence and invasion while also activating systemic immunity4. Unlike conventional intramuscular vaccines, which primarily elicit systemic responses with limited mucosal protection5, mucosal vaccines stimulate the production of SIgA at the site of pathogen entry and engage the common mucosal immune system to promote the homing of activated immune cells to distant mucosal tissues, thereby conferring broader protection6. Intranasal vaccination, in particular, can induce the formation of tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs, such as CD69⁺CD103⁺), germinal center reactions, and memory B cells in the respiratory tract. These cells persist locally for at least six months and play a pivotal role in limiting viral replication and transmission7–9. Non-invasive delivery methods such as oral, intranasal, or sublingual administration not only avoid the discomfort and anxiety associated with injections but also significantly improve vaccine acceptance and compliance among recipients10. These features make mucosal vaccines particularly suitable for rapid deployment and large-scale immunization during infectious disease outbreaks. Therefore, the development of safe, effective, and broadly protective mucosal vaccines carries significant public health implications and represents a key trend in the evolution of vaccine technology.

Given the growing interest in mucosal vaccine technologies, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of respiratory mucosal vaccines, with a focus on candidates targeting the most significant viral pathogens. To ensure clarity and clinical relevance, we focus on three key respiratory viruses—influenza virus, SARS-CoV-2, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)—selected based on their global disease burden, established mucosal transmission routes, and the relatively advanced stage of vaccine development. Other respiratory viruses, such as parainfluenza virus and human metapneumovirus, are not covered in detail due to limited clinical data or their early-stage development status. By narrowing the scope, this review allows for a more in-depth analysis of representative vaccine strategies that reflect current scientific progress and translational potential.

Mechanisms of mucosal immunity in the respiratory tract

Physical and epithelial barriers

The respiratory tract is composed of a rich variety of cells, forming a continuous physical and immunological barrier (Fig. 1). Structural cell populations—including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal cells—together establish a defensive front that spans the entire airway and lung tissue11. One of the prominent features of the pulmonary mucosa is the widespread and persistent thin fluid layer on the airway lumen surface—mucus—secreted by goblet cells. Mucus primarily consists of mucins, cytokines, complement factors, antimicrobial peptides, SIgA, and commensal bacteria. This layer traps and neutralizes pathogens to prevent invasion and modulates viral entry by acting as a physical and biochemical filter. High-molecular-weight mucins enriched with sialylated O-glycans can serve as decoy receptors to trap influenza viruses and prevent membrane fusion12,13, although in some cases, mucin-virus interactions may facilitate infection by shielding virions or guiding them toward host cells14,15.

Schematic of the Mucosal Immune Mechanism in the Respiratory Tract.

Following mucosal vaccine uptake or pathogen invasion through the respiratory tract, antigens are recognized and processed by dendritic cells, which present them to T and B cells, initiating their activation and clonal expansion. B cells differentiate into plasma cells that produce IgA, IgG, or IgM antibodies, with secretory IgA (SigA) playing a central role in local mucosal defense. In some cases, molecular mimicry between microbial antigens and host proteins may lead to the generation of autoantibodies and the activation of autoreactive T cells. Concurrently, activated immune cells migrate to the mediastinal lymph nodes, where further priming and memory cell formation occur. These cells return to the infected lung through chemokine-mediated signaling to clear pathogens. The local immune response also promotes the formation of inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) and the accumulation of resident memory T cells (RAMD), which persist and self-renew to provide rapid protection upon reinfection. This integrated mucosal immune cascade contributes to both immediate pathogen clearance and long-term immune surveillance. (Created with BioRender.com).

Epithelial cells reinforce this barrier through tight junctions, adherens junctions, and desmosomes, and contribute to innate immunity by secreting mucins and antimicrobial peptides and by orchestrating ciliary movement for pathogen clearance16. They also activate signaling pathways that recruit phagocytes to infection sites17. In the lung parenchyma, epithelial cells form alveolar structures and collaborate with endothelial cells in gas exchange. Endothelial cells line pulmonary vessels and regulate immune cell trafficking, while mesenchymal cells maintain tissue integrity and secrete proinflammatory mediators during inflammation18.

Innate immune components and antigen uptake

The respiratory mucosa hosts abundant innate immune cells, including alveolar macrophages, unconventional T cells, and particularly, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs). ILCs are now recognized as key regulators of mucosal immunity and are categorized into ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3 subsets19. ILC1s secrete IFN-γ and participate in antiviral responses20; ILC2s produce IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, mediating anti-parasitic and allergic responses; ILC3s secrete IL-17 and IL-22, enhancing antibacterial defenses and tissue repair21,22. Through cytokine crosstalk, ILCs modulate dendritic cell (DC) activity and T cell priming, shaping the local immune landscape and vaccine responsiveness23,24.

Antigen uptake is coordinated through three main routes: (1) via microfold (M) cells that transport antigens to immune cells, such as mucosal DCs located beneath the epithelium25; (2) via epithelial cells that recycle antigens and deliver antibodies to the mucosal surface; and (3) through direct sampling by DCs that extend dendrites across the epithelium to capture antigens and migrate to draining lymph nodes26. There, DCs present antigens via MHC molecules, activate naïve T cells, and promote T follicular helper (Tfh) cell differentiation, which supports B cell maturation and high-affinity antibody production in mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT), such as inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT)27.

Adaptive immunity and mucosal memory

B cell activation depends on cooperation with Tfh cells in germinal centers28, where they undergo somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination before differentiating into plasma cells that secrete dimeric IgA (dIgA). dIgA is transported across epithelial cells by the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR), forming SIgA at the luminal surface29,30. SIgA neutralizes pathogens at mucosal entry points and prevents their adhesion and dissemination31. Notably, SIgA production is influenced by the surrounding microenvironment—cytokines secreted by glandular epithelial cells create niches that support long-term plasma cell survival32. However, mucosal immune responses, including SIgA levels, tend to wane significantly over time, which may impact the durability of protection induced by mucosal vaccines33.

Mucosal effector sites are enriched in the lamina propria and populated by lymphocytes activated in MALT. Upon antigen exposure, T and B cells migrate to these sites to carry out local immune functions. Among them, TRMs remain at the site of antigen entry and provide rapid, localized responses upon reinfection. TRMs respond faster than systemic memory T cells and play a pivotal role in mucosal defense34,35. Their differentiation and maintenance are regulated by cytokine signaling: IL-13 derived from ILC2 promotes TRM differentiation, while IL-17 from ILC3 supports their persistence in mucosal tissues36,37. The synergy between TRMs and systemic immune memory ensures a broad and lasting immune defense. Therefore, vaccine strategies that effectively induce TRMs offer a promising pathway for enhancing the efficacy of respiratory mucosal vaccines38.

An important factor influencing mucosal vaccine efficacy is the immunological distinction between antigen-naïve and antigen-experienced individuals. This difference is particularly relevant to MALT, where the strength and quality of immune activation depend heavily on prior antigen exposure27. In naïve individuals, such as infants, effective mucosal immunity requires full priming of the immune system, often resulting in weaker responses to initial vaccination. In contrast, experienced individuals can rapidly recall antigen-specific memory B and T cell responses, leading to stronger and faster mucosal immunity39. Studies have shown that intranasal vaccination induces significantly higher nasal IgA and IgG responses in individuals previously exposed to SARS-CoV-2 compared to naïve subjects40. These findings highlight the need to tailor mucosal vaccine strategies to different immune backgrounds—focusing on robust priming in naïve populations and effective boosting in experienced ones—to ensure optimal protection.

Biomarkers related to the evaluation of respiratory mucosal immunity

The clinical significance of various mucosal immune markers remains inconclusive. Immune components, such as neutralizing antibodies, Fc receptor-bound antibodies, memory B cell responses, and T cells, may all contribute to pulmonary protection41,42. In addition to circulating antibodies and systemic T and B cells, tissue-resident memory B and T cells identified in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid have been recognized as important contributors to lower respiratory tract immunity. This point was emphasized during the November 2022 symposium “Mucosal Vaccines for SARS-CoV-2: Scientific Gaps and Opportunities,” jointly organized by NIAID, CEPI, and the Gates Foundation43.

These findings underscore the growing importance of identifying reliable correlates of protection (CoP) for mucosal vaccines—defined as measurable immune parameters that statistically associate with clinical protection44. While systemic neutralizing antibodies are established CoPs for many parenteral vaccines, they may not accurately reflect immunity at mucosal sites45. Recent studies highlight SIgA and tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) as potential mucosal CoPs46,47. SIgA levels in the upper airways have been linked to reduced viral replication48,49, whereas Trm in lung tissue mediate long-term protection against pathogens like RSV and pertussis50,51. Validating such markers is essential not only for assessing protective efficacy in clinical trials but also for guiding immunological sampling strategies and informing the rational design of next-generation mucosal vaccines.

Development of respiratory mucosal vaccines

Advantages of respiratory mucosal vaccines

In the development of COVID-19 vaccines, researchers have adopted various platforms, including inactivated vaccines (such as those developed by Sinopharm and Sinovac), adenoviral vector vaccines (such as AstraZeneca), and mRNA vaccines (such as Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna). These vaccines have shown excellent efficacy in inducing systemic immune responses. Although these vaccines significantly reduce the risk of severe illness and hospitalization, their protection of the respiratory mucosa remains limited52, especially in blocking the initial infection and transmission of the virus. Consequently, the development of respiratory mucosal vaccines has regained strategic importance, reflecting a renewed appreciation for platforms that have been effectively used for decades—such as LAIV introduced in the 1960s and oral polio vaccines (OPV) that contributed to near-global eradication of wild poliovirus53,54. Building on these historical precedents, current efforts focus on mucosal vaccine strategies that aim to enhance localized immunity in the respiratory tract. Such vaccines can induce local immune responses and promote the production of specific IgA antibodies55, providing crucial support for enhancing mucosal barrier function and improving overall disease prevention efficiency. SIgA exists in a polymeric form on mucosal surfaces such as the respiratory tract, forming a localized immune barrier. Through an “immune exclusion” mechanism, it prevents pathogens from binding to cellular receptors, thereby interfering with their entry into host cells56. This mechanism not only helps prevent infection at the entry site but also offers new approaches for controlling other respiratory infectious diseases, such as influenza.

Challenges of respiratory mucosal vaccines

The physicochemical barriers of the mucosa significantly affect the uptake efficiency of vaccines. The tight junctions and ciliary movement of the mucosal epithelium not only hinder the effective absorption of vaccines but also lead to rapid clearance of the vaccine due to its short residence time on the mucosal surface57,58. Additionally, natural components in the mucus, such as antibodies, proteases, and nucleases, may degrade or destroy the vaccine, thereby weakening its efficacy. Therefore, in the design of vaccine carriers, it is essential to protect the antigen components effectively and prevent enzymatic or chemical degradation to ensure safe delivery to target cells59.

To further advance mucosal vaccines, it is necessary to consider the potential impacts of other immune mechanisms within the mucosal immune system, such as mucosal-induced immune tolerance (e.g., oral or nasal tolerance). These mechanisms play a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis with many orally encountered beneficial antigens, such as food and commensal microbiota60. When antigens are exposed at mucosal sites, immune tolerance is more likely to be induced rather than effective protective immunity, thus necessitating the selection of suitable vaccine formulations and adjuvants. Adjuvants, which enhance innate immunity and promote the induction of effector cell responses, play a key role in boosting mucosal immune responses61.

Moreover, the complexity of antigen recognition poses higher demands on vaccine design. Particle-based vaccine formulations are generally more effective than soluble antigens in eliciting protective immunity. Compared to soluble antigens, particulate antigens can remain in the mucosal layer 3–5 times longer, promoting sustained activation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs)62,63. However, antigen particles smaller than 40 nm may bypass local APC processing by entering the lymphatic circulation through paracellular pathways (with a fivefold increase in transport rate), thereby inducing immune tolerance27,62.

In addition to physicochemical and immunological challenges, safety concerns remain a key consideration in the development of respiratory mucosal vaccines. Intranasal vaccination, although advantageous in inducing local immunity, poses unique safety challenges due to the anatomical proximity of the nasal cavity to the central nervous system (CNS) via the olfactory bulb. This raises potential concerns regarding local adverse effects, such as nasal congestion and transient anosmia, as well as the theoretical risk of neurological events, emphasizing the need for stringent safety assessments during vaccine development64,65. Moreover, adenoviral vector-based vaccines—particularly those using human adenovirus type 5 (Ad5)—have been under close scrutiny in clinical trials. For example, phase Ib studies of Ad5-vectored respiratory mucosal vaccines have highlighted the importance of evaluating safety and immunogenicity profiles across diverse populations66. The safety of mucosal adjuvants also warrants careful consideration, as some formulations may elicit strong local immune activation or systemic effects. Recent studies have stressed the necessity for rigorous safety monitoring, long-term surveillance, and population-specific risk assessment when advancing mucosal vaccine platforms into clinical use67–69.

Therefore, to achieve the design goals of respiratory mucosal vaccines, it is imperative to innovate in both adjuvant development and delivery systems70. This includes developing specific adjuvants capable of enhancing local immune responses and designing safe and effective delivery systems to ensure that antigens reach their target sites efficiently.

Strategies for improving respiratory mucosal vaccines

Mucosal vaccine adjuvants

In vaccine formulations, adjuvants are defined as functional auxiliary components that can enhance antigen-induced immune responses (Table 1)71. They improve the immunogenicity of vaccines through multiple mechanisms and play a crucial role, particularly in subunit and inactivated vaccines72. Firstly, adjuvants can alter the physical properties of antigens, enabling their slow release and thereby prolonging their presence in the body73. This sustained-release effect helps continuously stimulate the immune system and maintain a long-lasting immune response74. Secondly, adjuvants can adsorb antigens, increasing their surface area and making them more easily taken up and processed by APCs, thus enhancing antigen uptake75 Adjuvants also significantly improve the efficiency of antigen presentation by activating and promoting the maturation of APCs76, enhancing both humoral and cellular immunity, and facilitating the differentiation of B and T cell memory77, thereby promoting the development of adaptive immunity. Moreover, they can induce the release of cytokines and chemokines, facilitating immune cell recruitment and further enhancing immune activation. However, despite their widespread application in systemic immunity, there is still a lack of safe and effective mucosal adjuvants, which limits the broader clinical application of mucosal vaccines78.

The heat-labile toxin (LT) of Escherichia coli and cholera toxin (CT), along with their variants, are among the most representative toxoid adjuvants79. These adjuvants enhance mucosal barrier permeability and promote the recruitment and activation of local APCs by binding to GM1 ganglioside receptors on the surface of most nucleated cells. Most importantly, when administered via mucosal routes, these adjuvants can stimulate antigen-specific IgA antibodies and long-lasting memory cells80. However, it is important to note that toxoid adjuvants have also raised safety concerns in clinical settings. For instance, a nasal influenza subunit vaccine (NasVax) developed by the Swiss company Berna Biotech, which included a mutated LT adjuvant (LTK63), was eventually withdrawn from the market. Although LTK63 exhibited low toxicity in animal models, its administration in humans was associated with a significantly higher incidence of Bell’s palsy compared to baseline levels81,82. LThαK is a detoxified derivative of heat-LT in which ADP-ribosyltransferase activity is suppressed. When delivered intranasally, LThαK does not translocate to the CNS, indicating a favorable safety profile83. Phase I (NCT03293732) and Phase II (NCT03784885) clinical trials have demonstrated that influenza vaccines adjuvanted with LThαK exhibited significant efficacy and good tolerability after two intranasal administrations. Another example is CTA1-DD, a derivative of CT that replaces its B subunit with a dimer of the D fragment from Staphylococcus aureus protein A. Preclinical studies have shown that vaccines using CTA1-DD as an adjuvant, when administered intranasally, can effectively induce antibody production and thereby block influenza virus infection and transmission84.

Some mucosal adjuvants exhibit dual characteristics as both immune stimulators and delivery systems, such as chitosan and its derivatives85. Chitosan is a natural derivative extracted from α-chitin and has become an ideal choice for mucosal adjuvants due to its mucosal adhesion and immune-stimulating abilities86. Chitosan-based nanoparticles not only prolong the retention time of antigens locally, but also enhance antigen absorption in the mucosa and protect the antigens from degradation in the mucosal environment. Additionally, chitosan can activate APCs via the cGAS-STING-dependent pathway, thereby inducing both innate and adaptive immune responses. Studies have shown that the use of chitosan as an adjuvant in the inactivated mutant diphtheria toxin CRM197 vaccine can generate high levels of neutralizing antibodies following nasal administration in humans87.

Glucans are a type of polysaccharide molecule that have been proven to effectively enhance vaccine immune responses, and are mainly classified into α-glucans and β-glucans. Studies have shown that glucans can increase serum antibody titers and promote the production of key cytokines, such as IL-2 and interferon (IFN)-γ88. In addition, Mirza et al. found that the internal structure of glucan particles can carry compounds like protein antigens and deliver them to macrophages and DCs, making glucans both adjuvants and targeted antigen delivery systems89. In mucosal immunity, oral administration of β-glucan significantly increases the number of intraepithelial lymphocytes and the levels of IFN-γ in the intestinal mucosa of mice, rapidly inducing T cell responses in the mucosa. The rapid response of mucosal immunity in the early stages of pathogen exposure helps prevent tissue infection. Currently, no significant toxicity or side effects have been found with β-glucan, although its different structures can induce varying immune effects90.

Polyethyleneimine (PEI) is a synthetic cationic polymer widely used in gene transduction, and it also demonstrates excellent potential as an intranasal vaccine adjuvant. However, due to safety concerns regarding its use in humans, the application of PEI as a mucosal adjuvant remains limited to experimental research. Studies have shown that intranasal vaccination with a receptor-binding domain (RBD) vaccine containing PEI as an adjuvant in mice can significantly induce high titers of neutralizing antibodies against SARS-CoV-291. Moreover, a single dose of subunit glycoprotein antigen adjuvanted with PEI, delivered intranasally, shows stronger antibody-mediated protection compared to traditional CT adjuvants92, providing sufficient defense against lethal doses of influenza virus and herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2). PEI can form nanoscale complexes with protein antigens, facilitating rapid uptake by APCs93. The adjuvant activity of PEI depends on the release of host double-stranded DNA and the activation of the Irf3 signaling pathway.

Immunostimulants activate cells in the innate immune system by targeting immune cells and their signaling pathways, especially promoting the activation of DCs and the initiation of adaptive immune responses. For example, adjuvants targeting M cells can facilitate antigen transport through the epithelial layer, while adjuvants targeting TLR and STING pathways can activate DCs and other innate immune cells, further enhancing the body’s immune response. Mucosal adjuvants can also effectively stimulate anti-tumor immunity94. Among various mucosal adjuvants, TLR agonizts and modified enterotoxins have shown significant effects in this regard and are relatively safe61. In recent years, many new insights have been gained in the field of adjuvant research. It is now possible to choose appropriate adjuvants, rather than relying on traditional ones, and by using these new-generation adjuvants in different combinations, reasonable strategies for the successful development of vaccines can be provided95.

The adjuvant dmLT, or more strictly LT (R192G/L211A), is an 84 kDa polymeric protein with an AB5 structure. It consists of an enzymatically active A subunit (28 kDa) and a pentameric B subunit (composed of five 11.5 kDa monomers) that are non-covalently bound80. Due to its immunostimulatory properties and broad cellular binding abilities, dmLT significantly enhances the cellular uptake of vaccine antigens, thereby boosting mucosal immunity. At the vaccination site, once the vaccine antigens are absorbed, epithelial cells secrete cytokines such as IL-8 and G-CSF, stimulating the recruitment and activation of DCs. These DCs process and present the antigens, while upregulating co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, and releasing cytokines like IL-1, IL-23, and IL-6. Activated DCs migrate to secondary lymphoid organs, promoting the differentiation of antigen-specific T cells and B cells, thereby enhancing the secretion of IgA and IgG antibodies. The dmLT adjuvant elicits a strong Th17 response specific to the vaccine antigens and induces IL-17, which primarily provides protection against infection at mucosal sites96. This is also crucial for germinal center formation and IgA secretion97. Studies have shown that dmLT plays a key role in inducing IL-1β and the Th17 response, making it an effective vaccine immunostimulant.

TLR ligand-based adjuvants enhance the immunoprotective effects of vaccines by activating the innate immune response. These adjuvants rely on the adapter protein MyD88 to stimulate APCs and B cells, promoting antibody production and inducing the formation of germinal centers, which are essential for the generation of antibody-secreting cells98. Common TLR ligand adjuvants include TLR9-dependent CpG DNA and TLR2-dependent Porin B, both of which effectively stimulate APCs via the MyD88 pathway. TLRs are expressed on various antigen-presenting cells, particularly macrophages, DCs, and B cells. Therefore, the main role of TLR agonizts as adjuvants is to activate these cells, linking innate and adaptive immune responses. Studies have reported that TLR4 agonizts can promote mucosal antibody production following vaccination with pathogenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) in a mouse model99. Currently, an oral SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (VXA-CoV2-1) is undergoing phase III clinical trials, using a double-stranded RNA adjuvant that primarily targets TLR3 to activate DCs. The adjuvant in this study not only expands the range of PRR targets but also provides a promising alternative to toxin-based adjuvants for oral vaccines100.

Bacterial second messengers, such as cyclic di-AMP (c-di-AMP) and cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP), are typical representatives of novel mucosal adjuvants. These molecules activate the STING pathway to produce type I interferons (IFN-I), which further trigger adaptive immune responses101. Studies have shown that combining recombinant Tc52 or its amino-terminal domain with c-di-AMP as an adjuvant for intranasal administration can effectively induce specific Th17 and Th1 immune responses, providing protection against Trypanosoma cruzi infection in mice102. A COVID-19 vaccine has successfully incorporated the spike protein antigen and the STING agonist 2′-3′ cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) into liposomes for intranasal immunization103. Additionally, a mixed adjuvant combining TLR agonizts (such as CpG (TLR9) and LP1569 (TLR2)) with c-di-GMP can induce the secretion of IL-17-producing TRMs after intranasal administration, offering long-lasting immune protection against Bordetella pertussis nasal colonization104.

Cytokines are widely used to enhance the immunogenicity of intranasal vaccines due to their crucial role in activating and regulating adaptive immune responses. For example, IL-15 has been used as an adjuvant in the development of an intranasal subunit vaccine against SARS-CoV-2 infection in rhesus macaques, significantly enhancing T cell immune responses105. Other cytokines, such as IL-12, IFN-I, IL-1 family, TNF family, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), are also commonly used as adjuvants in intranasal vaccines to help increase the intensity and breadth of immune responses106. Among them, IL-1β has been identified as a potent mucosal adjuvant capable of enhancing both local and systemic immunity. Its application has demonstrated safety and efficacy in various models, including influenza107, SARS-CoV-238, and mucosal tumor vaccines108. Recent findings suggest that the cytokine secretion profile of ILCs in adenoviral vector vaccines can be directionally modulated by neutralizing antibody complexes109, thereby enhancing vaccine efficacy. However, it is important to note that using excessively high doses or frequent administration of cytokine-containing vaccines may lead to overactivation of the immune system, resulting in adverse reactions, such as local inflammation or systemic responses. Therefore, careful control of the dosage is essential to ensure the safety of these cytokine adjuvants.

Saponins are a group of glycosides widely found in plants, recognized for their diverse biological activities and pharmacological effects, making them promising immunological adjuvants. By activating the mammalian immune system, saponins can induce the differentiation and maturation of neutrophils, macrophages, and DCs, while also promoting the production of cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-4110. Among the studied types of saponins, triterpene saponins stand out, especially QuilA and its derivative QS-21, extracted from soapbark. These saponins significantly activate Th1 immune responses and promote the expansion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and are widely used in the preparation of vaccines, particularly anticancer vaccines111. However, the high toxicity, hemolytic reactions, and poor aqueous stability of QuilA and QS-21 limit their widespread use. Therefore, researchers are focusing on finding more ideal saponin adjuvants from other natural sources. Studies have shown that ginsenoside Rg1 significantly enhances the immune response in mice against hepatitis B surface antigen through various mechanisms. These include promoting the secretion of IgG, IFN-γ, and IL-4, activating DCs, and inducing protective cellular immune responses against lymphoma. Ginsenoside Rg3 has been used as an adjunct in cancer therapy, not only enhancing the immunogenicity of vaccines but also reducing adverse reactions by inducing the production of IFN-γ and IL-2, thereby demonstrating significant synergistic effects112. Although saponins show good prospects in the field of adjuvants, their toxicity, hemolytic reactions, and aqueous instability remain significant obstacles to their application.

α-GalCer is a molecule that can activate invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells and has garnered attention as a potential nasal vaccine adjuvant. Its presentation process depends on the CD1d molecules on the surface of APCs113. Currently, α-GalCer has been widely used in the development of intranasal influenza vaccines, effectively inducing long-lasting and strong protective immune responses. To improve the solubility of the adjuvant, researchers have developed more water-soluble α-GalCer analogs, such as KBC-007 and KBC-009, which serve as safe and effective intranasal adjuvants for inactivated influenza virus vaccines114.

Mucosal vaccine delivery systems

The delivery system of mucosal vaccines plays a crucial role in vaccine development, particularly when delivering macromolecular antigens such as recombinant proteins and nucleic acid vaccines (Fig. 2). These antigens, due to their large molecular size, are difficult to directly cross the complex mucosal barriers. Therefore, the delivery system becomes an essential tool for transporting antigens to the appropriate location, thereby effectively inducing immune responses115. In the respiratory mucosa, antigens must be presented in particulate form to be effectively recognized by APCs, which then activate local and systemic immune responses. The delivery system not only protects the antigens from degradation by proteases, nucleases, and SIgA in mucus, but also prolongs the retention time of the antigens on the mucosal surface, thereby enhancing the vaccine’s immunogenicity. In recent years, mucosal adhesion-based nanoparticle (NP) delivery systems have attracted widespread attention in the field of mucosal vaccines.

Nanogels, as an important type of polymeric particle, offer strong biocompatibility and controllable antigen release capabilities due to their unique hydrated polymer structure. They can significantly enhance the immunogenicity of mucosal vaccines by improving the stability of the antigens116. ISCOMs (Immune Stimulating Complexes) are nanoparticles composed of antigens, cholesterol, phospholipids, and the saponin adjuvant Quil A. With a diameter of approximately 40 nanometers, ISCOMs are used to deliver vaccine antigens and efficiently target the immune system after transdermal injection or mucosal administration117. In mouse models and tumor antigen studies, ISCOM vaccines have been shown to strongly activate CD8 + T cell responses, providing protection in three different tumor models. This vaccine also promotes a Th1-type immune bias and can induce CD8 + T cell responses even in the absence of CD4 + T cell assistance118. Lipophilic ISCOMs containing Quil A can exert immune effects through both transdermal and mucosal routes. In guinea pig subcutaneous injection experiments using diphtheria toxin (DT) as an antigen, the ISCOM-based vaccine induced a more effective protective immune response against the diphtheria toxin compared to traditional aluminum-adjuvanted vaccines with the same dosage of DT119.

Various synthetic, semi-synthetic, and natural polymers have been widely applied in the preparation of NP vaccines and drug delivery systems120. These polymer nanoparticles typically have a size range of 1–100 nanometers, and due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, low toxicity, and the ability to adjust shape and size as needed, they have become ideal delivery carriers in vaccine development121. Based on these characteristics, the application of polymer nanoparticles in vaccine delivery systems has gained widespread attention, with significant progress in research on mucosal vaccines122. Chitosan, due to its good adhesion and permeability, has become one of the most promising natural polysaccharide polymers. Vaccines encapsulated in chitosan nanoparticles, such as inactivated infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) vaccines, have been administered via ocular and nasal routes in chickens, significantly inducing strong mucosal immune responses and increasing the levels of IFN-γ, IgA antibodies, and anti-IBV IgG antibodies123. Synthetic polymers such as N-2-hydroxypropyltrimethylammonium chitosan (N-2-HACC), PLGA, PEG, and polyacrylic acid (PAA) have also been used to prepare nanoparticles to protect antigens, extend their retention time at the target site, and achieve sustained release. PLGA is widely used in mucosal drug delivery due to its biodegradability and biocompatibility. Although PEG itself has weak mucosal adhesion, it can enhance adhesion by forming hydrogen bonds with glycosylated protein residues124. PAA significantly enhances mucosal adhesion through physical chain entanglement and ionic interactions, and its adhesion properties can be further enhanced by thiolation treatment125. Nanoparticles made from natural and synthetic polymers show great potential in vaccine delivery systems, providing new strategies and platforms to enhance immune efficacy.

Inorganic NPs have become important carriers in drug and vaccine delivery due to their non-toxicity126, excellent biocompatibility, and high stability. They possess unique physicochemical properties, such as high surface area, optical, and magnetic characteristics, and can enhance affinity for target cells or molecules through specific ligand modifications. Inorganic nanoparticles can effectively protect the encapsulated antigens or drugs from degradation and reduce drug dosage by controlling the release mechanism, thus lowering toxicity121. Common inorganic nanoparticles include silica nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), and metal oxide nanoparticles. Gold nanoparticles, due to their chemical inertness and non-toxicity, have become ideal vaccine carriers. Their size is controllable (2–150 nanometers), and they have significant advantages in intracellular delivery. Silver nanoparticles, known for their excellent antimicrobial properties127, have been widely used in medical, molecular imaging, and drug delivery applications, though there are currently no FDA-approved silver-based drug delivery nanocarriers. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles, with their large surface area, tunable size128, and excellent biocompatibility, show great potential in enhancing drug solubility and achieving sustained release. Metal oxide nanoparticles, such as Ag2O, ZnO, and TiO2, are widely used in various fields, including healthcare and cosmetics. The application of inorganic nanoparticles in vaccine delivery systems may provide new strategies and platforms to improve immune efficacy129.

Self-assembled particles represent a promising delivery strategy in mucosal vaccine design due to their structural stability and antigen presentation capabilities. For example, ferritin, a self-assembling protein nanoparticle, can display viral antigens in a repetitive and organized manner, enhancing B cell receptor cross-linking and immune activation130. SpyCatcher/SpyTag technology allows for modular and covalent attachment of antigens to carrier particles, offering flexibility in antigen display and rapid vaccine development131. Similarly, the I53-50A/B nanoparticle system forms stable icosahedral structures and can present multiple copies of antigens, improving immunogenicity and enabling multivalent vaccine design132. Their biocompatibility and adaptability make them ideal candidates for future respiratory mucosal vaccine formulations.

Existing respiratory mucosal vaccines

Respiratory mucosal vaccines against influenza virus

The concept of live attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIVs) can be traced back to the 1930s, when A. A. Smorodintsev and colleagues in the former Soviet Union developed the first intranasal influenza vaccines using attenuated viral strains. These vaccines were widely administered from the 1960s onward, and in 1987, the USSR became the first country in the world to license a trivalent cold-adapted LAIV53,133. The LAIV, administered via the nasal mucosa, was subsequently approved in the United States in 2003 as a trivalent formulation (LAIV3)134. With advancements in vaccine technology, LAIV4 (FluMist Quadrivalent, MedImmune) was approved by the US FDA in 2012 and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2013 (Table 2), further expanding the coverage of influenza vaccines135. Studies have shown that FluMist induces local IgA antibody levels that are twice as high as those induced by traditional inactivated influenza vaccines (such as the quadrivalent inactivated vaccine), and its systemic IgG antibody titers are also significantly increased, with protection rates generally exceeding 80%136. This local immune response gives it a significant advantage in preventing virus entry into the body, effectively reducing the occurrence of respiratory infections. In contrast, traditional influenza vaccines typically have protection rates between 60 and 70%136, with weaker induction of local immune responses. The vaccine performs particularly well in children; in a clinical study of the FluMist Quadrivalent vaccine, the geometric mean titer (GMT) of hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) antibodies significantly increased after vaccination, especially in children aged 2–8 years with no history of seasonal influenza vaccination. After receiving a second dose, HAI antibody titers increased fourfold137. In children, the live attenuated vaccine LAIV shows a prevention rate of up to 90% for culture-confirmed influenza138, and it has advantages over inactivated formulations in this age group, although this advantage diminishes with age. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of 3,242 community-dwelling elderly individuals with an average age of 69.5 years showed an overall efficacy of 42.3% (95% confidence interval: 21.6–57.8%) for LAIV against antigenically similar influenza viruses. The efficacy against A/H3N2 was 52.5% (95% confidence interval: 32.1–67.2%), but no significant protective effect was observed against type B viruses139. Another review noted that immune responses to LAIV in elderly individuals may be weakened due to pre-existing immune memory to similar viral strains, resulting in reduced vaccine effectiveness. In elderly populations, more systemic reactions, such as sore throat, runny nose, fatigue, headache, and muscle aches, may also occur140.

Nasovac-S is a trivalent LAIV developed by the Serum Institute of India (SIIL) based on the Russian master donor virus technology53. This vaccine is administered via nasal spray and can simulate the natural infection pathway, effectively inducing local immune responses in the respiratory mucosa, particularly the production of SIgA antibodies141. This mucosal immunity plays a key role in preventing viral entry into the mucosal barrier. Additionally, the vaccine can induce systemic immune responses, significantly enhancing HAI antibody levels and stimulating T cell responses, making it highly effective in preventing influenza virus infection. In a phase IV safety study conducted in India with 500 participants, approximately 20% reported mild local adverse reactions, such as nasal congestion and runny nose, while 23% of participants experienced mild to moderate systemic reactions, such as headaches and fever. These adverse reactions mostly resolved on their own within a few days. Nasovac-S demonstrated an overall protection rate of nearly 80% in multiple studies142, with particularly significant efficacy in the pediatric population. This trivalent live attenuated vaccine provides an effective means of seasonal influenza prevention and is suitable for individuals aged 2 years and older143.

In addition to cold-adapted and reassortant strategies, NS1 protein truncation or deletion has been developed as a rational attenuation method in LAIV design. For instance, a PR8-based influenza virus with truncation of the NS1 protein (retaining only the first 124 amino acids) demonstrated self-adjuvanting effects by failing to antagonize type I interferon, thereby enhancing CD8⁺ T cell responses and cross-protection against heterologous strains144. Another LAIV candidate based on the 2009 H1N1 virus, incorporating both NS1 truncation and PA-X deletion (NS1(1–126)-ΔPAX), showed improved immunogenicity and safety in preclinical models145. These findings support the use of NS1-modified backbones to enhance mucosal and systemic immunity, particularly against antigenically drifted influenza viruses146,147, and provide a foundation for their further development in multivalent or combination vaccine platforms.

NasVax, developed by Berna Biotech, is a nasal subunit influenza vaccine that employed LTK63, a genetically detoxified mutant of E. coli heat-LT. Although preclinical studies demonstrated promising immunogenicity, including strong mucosal SigA responses and systemic immunity, clinical trials were terminated due to a significantly increased incidence of Bell’s palsy82. This underscores the critical importance of rigorous safety evaluation for mucosal adjuvants in vaccine development.

CodaVax™-H1N1 is a live attenuated intranasal vaccine developed by Codagenix using codon deoptimization technology148. The vaccine was constructed by codon-deoptimizing the hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) genes of the A/California/07/2009 (H1N1) (CA07) strain, and then using in vitro DNA synthesis techniques to reconstruct the viral genome, resulting in an attenuated effect149.In animal experiments, CodaVax-H1N1 demonstrated significant attenuation and safety. In DBA/2 mice, even at a very low dose (100 or approximately 1 PFU), it was able to prevent lethal H1N1 infection. In BALB/c mice, 10³ PFU provided effective protection. In ferret models, CodaVax-H1N1 showed superior protective effects compared to existing approved LAIV and effectively suppressed viral replication at similarly low doses. Based on its outstanding performance in animal models, the vaccine has now entered Phase I/II clinical trials149.

M2SR (M2-deficient single-replication influenza virus) is a novel nasal spray influenza vaccine developed by the U.S. biotechnology company FluGen. It is based on the “single-replication” viral technology and lacks functional M2 protein (the ion channel protein of the influenza virus). This deficiency limits the virus to a single round of replication at the inoculation site, preventing it from spreading or transmitting, thus ensuring safety150,151.The vaccine aims to provide broader cross-protection, particularly against heterosubtypic influenza viruses. In animal models, the immune response induced by M2SR can target both the same subtype (e.g., H1N1) and heterosubtypic viruses (e.g., H3N2). For example, the H1N1 M2SR vaccine provided over 80% protection against H3N2 virus infection, while traditional inactivated vaccines only offer protection against the same subtype. In a Phase II trial of M2SR, a single dose (10⁹ TCID50) of M2SR induced neutralizing antibodies in 48% of participants against the matched H3N2 virus (e.g., A/Brisbane/10/2007), with a 17% reduction in infection rates compared to the placebo group (54% vs. 71%)152. Even when faced with antigenically drifted H3N2 variants (e.g., A/Belgium/4217/2015), participants who showed a more than two-fold increase in neutralizing antibody titers after receiving the M2SR vaccine still had a significantly lower risk of infection153. Additionally, M2SR co-administration with high-dose inactivated flu vaccine (Fluzone HD) enhanced immunogenicity in the elderly, with 48% of co-vaccinated individuals achieving seroconversion, significantly higher than the 31% in the inactivated vaccine-only group154. Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated that M2SR is well tolerated, with mild adverse events (e.g., nasal congestion, runny nose) occurring at rates similar to those in the placebo group152–154. Currently, the M2SR platform is being utilized to develop combination vaccines capable of simultaneously preventing both influenza and COVID-19155,156, offering a new strategy for addressing influenza virus mutations and the development of multi-pathogen vaccines.

Virus-like particles (VLPs) offer a safe and versatile platform for influenza vaccines, mimicking viral structures without containing genetic material157. Their stability allows insertion of foreign proteins like L20 without disrupting assembly158. VLPs expressing H1/H3 hemagglutinin can induce cross-protection against influenza B viruses159, while chimeric H1H3M2e5x VLPs elicit broad IgG responses against multiple subtypes, including H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1160. Targeting conserved HA stalk domains or including M2 and NA components enables protection against antigenically drifted strains161–163. Intranasal delivery enhances mucosal IgA responses and DC activation, likely involving MAIT cell signaling164,165. For elderly individuals, VLPs improve immunogenicity through multivalent antigen display and cytokine fusion strategies like IL-12166,167.

In summary, current intranasal influenza vaccines demonstrate the critical advantages of mucosal immunization. These platforms not only induce robust local and systemic immune responses but also offer improved protection in pediatric and elderly populations, enhanced safety profiles, and potential cross-subtype immunity. Their needle-free administration, capacity for mass production, and adaptability to multivalent or combination formulations position them as promising tools for both seasonal and pandemic influenza control.

Respiratory mucosal vaccines for SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19)

Adenoviruses are widely regarded as a safe and well-studied platform for antigen delivery168–170, and have achieved success in the development of COVID-19 vaccines171.AdCOVID is an intranasal COVID-19 vaccine developed by the U.S.-based company Altimmune, utilizing adenovirus vector technology (Ad5)172. Currently in the preclinical research stage, the vaccine is administered via nasal spray and aims to stimulate a localized immune response in the respiratory tract, particularly the production of SigA antibodies, to block the early entry of SARS-CoV-2. A single intranasal dose of AdCOVID has been shown to effectively induce robust systemic and mucosal immune responses against SARS-CoV-2.Studies have demonstrated that in C57BL/6 J and CD-1 mice, both specific IgG antibodies and respiratory tract-specific IgA antibodies remained at high levels for up to 180 days post-vaccination. In the high-dose group, neutralizing antibodies were detected in all C57BL/6 J mice (10/10) with a median titer of 563, and in 8 of 10 CD-1 mice with a median titer of 431, indicating effective neutralization of the wild-type SARS-CoV-2 virus173. Most notably, experiments using the K18-hACE2 mouse model revealed that all vaccinated mice were fully protected 28 days after immunization when challenged with a lethal dose of SARS-CoV-2.

Intranasal administration of Gam-COVID-Vac (Sputnik V) has been shown in animal models to induce durable and robust systemic and mucosal immune responses. In mice, a single dose sustained high levels of neutralizing antibodies, serum IgG, and strong T-cell responses for at least 180 days, while studies in non-human primates (e.g., common marmosets) confirmed pronounced immunogenicity with potent activation of mucosal IgA and cellular immunity174. Importantly, the intranasal formulation was officially registered for human use in Russia under the name Salnavac in April 2022, marking a critical step from preclinical research to real-world application. During booster immunization, intranasal adenoviral-vectored vaccines, such as Salnavac elicited consistent and long-lasting responses, with RBD-specific IgG and IgA avidity confirmed by biolayer interferometry, underscoring the high-affinity nature of serum antibodies175. In lethal SARS-CoV-2 challenge models, the intranasal Sputnik V vaccine provided complete protection against severe pulmonary pathology and mortality, including infection with Gamma and Delta variants, largely through preferential induction of mucosal IgA and T-cell responses, which reduced viral load and enhanced overall protection174. Although its efficacy was diminished against Omicron, protection against severe disease was still observed176. Collectively, these findings highlight intranasal adenoviral-vectored vaccines as promising candidates for mucosal immunization strategies, offering unique advantages in transmission blocking and non-invasive delivery, and underscoring their strategic value in the face of emerging variants177,178.

Convidecia Air (also known as Ad5-nCoV) is the world’s first approved respiratory mucosal COVID-19 vaccine. It uses a replication-deficient human adenovirus type 5 vector to deliver the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein gene179. Administered via aerosol inhalation through the respiratory mucosa, this vaccine not only enhances lung-specific IgG and IgA antibody levels, but also activates tissue-resident memory T cells, helping to block viral replication and transmission in the respiratory tract during the early stages of infection180. Studies have shown that in individuals who have completed two doses of inactivated vaccine (e.g., CoronaVac) as primary immunization, using Convidecia Air as a heterologous booster significantly increases the GMT of neutralizing antibodies compared to homologous boosting, with a more durable immune response181–183. For example, among people aged over 60 who had received only one dose of CoronaVac, those receiving a heterologous second dose with Convidecia had a GMT of 70.9 (95% CI: 49.5–101.7), significantly higher than those receiving a homologous second dose of CoronaVac at 9.3 (95% CI: 6.2–13.9)181. Another study showed that the aerosolized Ad5-nCoV induced a GMT of neutralizing antibodies of 2924.0 (95% CI: 2305.0–3542.0) 14 days after vaccination, which was 7.8 times higher than that of the inactivated vaccine184. Compared with protein subunit or inactivated vaccines, Convidecia Air induces a stronger and longer-lasting humoral immune response185. In response to the Omicron BA.4/5 variants, Convidecia Air also demonstrated good protective efficacy186, with a heterologous booster providing 51.6% protection against BA.5 infection, higher than the 48% protection from three doses of inactivated vaccine187. Regarding safety, the main adverse event reported was mild and transient headache (median onset time approximately 19 h, duration about 12 h)188, with no serious adverse events observed. The safety of the aerosolized formulation has been further confirmed in open-label studies180.

Bharat Biotech International Limited (BBIL) of India has developed an intranasal SARS-CoV-2 vaccine named BBV154, based on a chimpanzee adenoviral vector. This vaccine encodes a prefusion-stabilized spike protein, incorporating two proline substitution mutations in the S2 subunit189. These mutations help maintain the spike protein in its prefusion conformation, thereby enhancing the immune system’s ability to recognize the virus and increasing the vaccine-induced neutralizing antibody response. Intranasal administration of BBV154 induces strong mucosal and systemic humoral immunity, along with Th1 cell-mediated responses, and generates cross-neutralizing antibodies against Omicron variants190. In a pivotal phase III clinical trial, the intranasal BBV154 vaccine outperformed the traditional intramuscular vaccine Covaxin in multiple aspects. On day 42, the GMT of neutralizing antibodies against the wild-type SARS-CoV-2 was 768.5 in the BBV154 group, significantly higher than 531 in the Covaxin group, with a GMT ratio of 1.45 (95% CI: 1.11–1.88). Among baseline seronegative individuals, antibody titers increased 5234-fold and 3784-fold, respectively, indicating a stronger humoral immune response induced by BBV154. The vaccine also effectively induced mucosal immunity, with significant increases in SigA levels and the number of IgA plasmablasts observed by day 42. In terms of safety, BBV154 demonstrated good tolerability, with common adverse events occurring at a rate of 6.9%, unexpected adverse events at 1.2%, and no vaccine-related serious adverse events reported191.

ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 (also known as AZD1222) is a replication-deficient chimpanzee adenovirus-vectored COVID-19 vaccine developed jointly by the University of Oxford and AstraZeneca. It encodes the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein192,193. Animal studies have shown that intranasal administration of ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 can effectively prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection in hamsters and non-human primates (NHPs)194. A phase I clinical trial evaluated the safety and immunogenicity of the intranasal ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine, revealing good tolerability following nasal administration. When used as a booster (5 ×z 10^10 viral particles) in individuals previously immunized with two intramuscular doses of either ChAdOx1 or BNT162b2, the intranasal vaccine showed a favorable safety profile, with adverse event incidence as the primary endpoint195. However, its immunogenicity did not meet expectations. Although some recipients of the intranasal vaccine exhibited nasopharyngeal IgA levels comparable to those of convalescent individuals—indicating potential to induce local mucosal immunity—the overall immune response was inconsistent and exhibited significant interindividual variability. This suggests that further optimization of the formulation and delivery system of the current intranasal ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine is required to achieve more robust and consistent immune induction.

On December 2, 2022, the intranasal influenza virus-vectored COVID-19 vaccine (dNS1-RBD) was granted emergency use authorization. It is the world’s first mucosal COVID-19 vaccine whose safety and broad-spectrum efficacy have been validated in a phase III clinical trial. The vaccine is developed from a cold-adapted influenza A virus strain, with the non-structural protein 1 (NS1) gene deleted from its genome196, and the RBD gene of SARS-CoV-2 inserted via genetic recombination197. By expressing the RBD antigen of SARS-CoV-2, the vaccine induces both mucosal and systemic immune responses, activating innate immune responses in the nasal epithelium and local T cell responses198.A phase III clinical trial of this intranasal vaccine enrolled 31,038 volunteers aged 18 to 91 years. During the follow-up period of 15 days or more after the second dose (median follow-up of 161 days [IQR 111–189]), the overall vaccine efficacy against symptomatic, RT-PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection was 28.2% (95% CI: 3.4–46.6%). Its short-term efficacy within 15–90 days was 32.6% (95% CI: 8.2–50.5%). The efficacy was higher in participants who had not previously received a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (40.0%), compared to those with prior vaccination (23.5%), suggesting that dNS1-RBD may offer durable protection without significant decline over time199. Moreover, due to the influenza virus vector used, the vaccine can also provide cross-protection against H1N1 and H5N1 influenza viruses200.

NDV-HXP-S is a novel COVID-19 vaccine candidate developed by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. It utilizes Newcastle disease virus (NDV), a poultry virus non-pathogenic to humans, as a vector to express a prefusion-stabilized SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (HexaPro) modified with six proline mutations to enhance its stability and immunogenicity201,202. Designed to elicit a robust immune response, the vaccine can be mass-produced in embryonated chicken eggs using a process similar to that of traditional influenza vaccines. This enables low-cost production and storage under standard refrigeration conditions (2 °C–8 °C), making it well-suited for widespread use in low- and middle-income countries203, and significantly improving access and affordability of COVID-19 vaccines in these regions.An animal model study demonstrated that mucosal delivery of a multivalent NDV-HXP-S vaccine induced cross-reactive immune responses against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants. A trivalent live vaccine formulation, incorporating the Wuhan, Beta, and Delta strains, successfully elicited cross-neutralizing antibodies against the phylogenetically distinct Omicron variant, thereby broadening the vaccine’s immune coverage. Furthermore, this vaccine exhibited strong protective effects not only in unvaccinated animals but also as a booster, enhancing both systemic and mucosal immune responses in individuals previously immunized with mRNA vaccines204. In summary, the intranasally delivered multivalent NDV-HXP-S vaccine shows promising safety and broad-spectrum immunogenic potential, offering a viable strategy for combating the evolving SARS-CoV-2 variants. Currently, a phase I clinical trial of the intranasal NDV-HXP-S vaccine has been initiated, though the results have not yet been publicly released205. Notably, the Omicron variant primarily replicates in the upper respiratory tract and causes milder pulmonary pathology compared to the ancestral strain, which partially explains the reduced protective efficacy observed with current intramuscular SARS-CoV-2 vaccines against Omicron subvariants206. These features further underscore the need for vaccines that elicit strong mucosal immunity at the primary site of infection.

Some mucosal SARS-CoV-2 vaccine candidates have integrated next-generation adjuvants, such as TLR agonizts and cyclic dinucleotides (e.g., cGAMP, c-di-GMP), to further enhance mucosal imminfectious bronchitis virus (unity207. These agents activate PRRs like STING and TLR3/9, leading to robust type I interferon responses and the induction of IL-17-producing tissue-resident memory T cells207,208. PEI-based formulations have also demonstrated superior mucosal adjuvant effects in preclinical SARS-CoV-2 models91, eliciting strong neutralizing antibody responses and enhanced protection compared to conventional adjuvants. While still under evaluation in early-phase trials or preclinical models, such adjuvant strategies hold promise for improving the breadth and durability of mucosal vaccine responses against SARS-CoV-2 variants.

VLPs have shown strong potential as mucosal vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 by safely mimicking native viral structures and efficiently inducing nasal SigA, a key barrier against infection209,210. Elevated nasal SigA levels correlate with reduced Omicron infection risk209, and intranasal vaccination or breakthrough infections significantly enhance mucosal antibody responses and cross-neutralization against BA.1, BA.2, and BA.5211. VLPs expressing prefusion-stabilized spike protein (HexaPro-S) have demonstrated effective neutralization against BA.5 in clinical trials (e.g., AVX/COVID-12)212, with reduced post-challenge viral loads observed after prototype or BA.5-specific booster vaccination213. Compared to viral vector platforms, VLPs are non-replicating and genome-free, offering a favorable safety profile214,215, and their structural integrity supports stable antigen presentation216. Their flexibility to incorporate variant-specific antigens and potential for multivalent display make VLPs a promising strategy for next-generation mucosal vaccines against evolving SARS-CoV-2 variants214,217,218.

Collectively, these intranasal and aerosolized SARS-CoV-2 vaccines underscore the growing promise of mucosal immunization in eliciting both systemic and local immune responses, offering enhanced protection against infection, facilitating variant coverage, and addressing global vaccine accessibility through safe, non-invasive, and scalable platforms.

Mucosal vaccines against RSV

Live attenuated vaccines offer significant advantages in children219. Compared to intramuscular injection, intranasal administration is milder and more acceptable for children. This mucosal immune mechanism may play a key role in defending against RSV infection, as higher levels of RSV-specific IgA in the nasal cavity are generally associated with stronger protective effects220.

Currently, several pharmaceutical companies have adopted live attenuated vaccines as a strategy for RSV prevention. Among them, Sanofi’s RSVt vaccine candidate is the most advanced. This vaccine achieves attenuation by deleting the NS2 gene (ΔNS2) and introducing temperature-sensitive mutations (Δ1313/I1314L) in the polymerase gene. Phase I clinical trial data show that 90% of participants had detectable replication of the vaccine virus, indicating a certain level of infectivity. After vaccination, the GMT of serum neutralizing antibodies reached 1:64, demonstrating a clear immune response. The RSVt vaccine at a dose of 10⁶ PFU was well tolerated in RSV-seronegative children, with no serious adverse events reported219. In a Phase II clinical trial conducted in the United States, RSV-seronegative children aged 6–24 months also exhibited low virus shedding and good safety after vaccination221. A Phase III clinical trial (NCT06252285) evaluating the efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety of the RSVt vaccine in infants and young children is currently underway, and is expected to be completed in 2027222.

CodaVax-RSV is a live attenuated vaccine developed by Codagenix. Its attenuation mechanism is based on codon deoptimization, which involves systematically altering the synonymous codons in the viral genome to reduce protein expression efficiency in host cells, thereby weakening the virus’s replication capacity. This approach effectively reduces virulence while maintaining antigenic epitopes, thus achieving a balance between safety and immunogenicity223,224. Currently, the vaccine is undergoing a Phase I clinical trial (NCT04919109) involving RSV seropositive and seronegative children aged 2–5 years to assess its safety and immunogenicity225. The results of this study will provide key data to support further development of the vaccine in the pediatric population.

BLB201 is a chimeric viral vector vaccine developed by Blue Lake Biotechnology, using parainfluenza virus 5 (PIV5) as a vector to express the fusion (F) protein of RSV226. The first Phase I clinical trial of this vaccine was conducted in RSV seropositive adults aged 33–75 years. The results showed good safety with no serious adverse events, and most adverse reactions were mild. Approximately 17% of participants experienced viral shedding, 48% developed nasal RSV-specific IgA responses, and serum neutralizing antibody titers increased by ≥1.5-fold. In over 93% of participants, the proportions of RSV F-specific CD4+ and CD8 + T cells in peripheral blood also increased significantly, indicating strong cellular immunogenicity227. A Phase I/Iia clinical trial of BLB201 (NCT05655182) is currently being conducted in infants. Preliminary data show that within one month after vaccination, neutralizing antibody levels increased 3.6 to 57-fold from baseline.

Recent advances in mucosal vaccines have highlighted recombinant influenza virus vectors as dual-platform strategies for preventing both influenza and RSV infections. RSV/Flu-01E is based on an influenza vector attenuated by removing the C-terminal half of the NS1 protein (NS1_124) and expresses protective epitopes of the RSV fusion (F) protein within a truncated NS1 open reading frame228,229. Similar approaches, such as Flu/THSP, demonstrate the feasibility of influenza vectors for heterologous antigen delivery230. Intranasal administration of RSV/Flu-01E induces robust mucosal IgA and tissue-resident memory T cell responses231, while avoiding Th2-biased immunity and lung pathology seen with formalin-inactivated RSV vaccines232. Early clinical studies of intranasal RSV vaccines, including BLB201 and BARS13, support their safety and immunogenicity227,233. Recombinant LAIVs expressing RSV antigens, like chimeric HA-G constructs, also show no vaccine-enhanced disease234. Combined RSV and influenza vaccination strategies may provide synergistic protection, supporting dual-platform approaches such as RSV/Flu-01E235,236. Currently, RSV/Flu-01E is under evaluation in phase I (NCT05970744) and phase II (NCT06890429) clinical trials, which aim to assess its use as a safe and effective mucosal immunization strategy capable of targeting multiple respiratory pathogens through a single intranasal platform.

Summary and expectations of respiratory mucosal vaccines

Respiratory mucosal vaccines have demonstrated significant potential in inducing both local and systemic immune responses237. Notably, this local immune activation facilitates the formation of tissue-resident memory cells, enabling the host to rapidly mount specific immune responses upon re-exposure to pathogens. Such vaccines are expected to establish effective immune barriers in both the upper and lower respiratory tracts, thereby preventing self-infection and efficiently interrupting the transmission of respiratory pathogens among populations238. Furthermore, respiratory mucosal vaccines may induce cross-protective immunity, which is particularly valuable against rapidly mutating pathogens, thus enhancing overall vaccine efficacy.

In recent years, the field of vaccine development has undergone a major shift—from traditional whole-cell inactivated and attenuated vaccines to adjuvanted subunit vaccines and more advanced platforms such as viral vectors, RNA, and DNA vaccines239,240. This transition helps reduce the risk of excessive immune responses. Therefore, future efforts should focus on combining whole-cell antigens with effective mucosal adjuvants to improve vaccination outcomes.

Currently, the number of available respiratory mucosal vaccines on the market remains limited, highlighting the importance of developing alternative types of vaccines, such as recombinant subunit vaccines and viral vector vaccines241. This need is particularly pressing considering that the protection offered by certain vaccines—such as those against COVID-19—may be relatively short-lived. Extending the duration of vaccine-induced protection largely depends on improving the survival of tissue-resident memory cells within mucosal tissues242. This strategy not only enhances individual immune defenses but also supports large-scale immunization efforts. Therefore, the respiratory mucosa, as a highly promising route for vaccine delivery, urgently requires advanced formulation strategies to facilitate its development and widespread application100. This direction offers a critical pathway for future vaccine innovation and holds the potential to effectively address global public health challenges and improve human health outcomes243.
