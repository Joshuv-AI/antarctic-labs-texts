Central amygdala‐to‐pre‐Bötzinger complex neurotransmission is direct and inhibitory
Authors: Jeffrey Gu, Yae K Sugimura, Fusao Kato, Christopher A Del Negro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreathing behaviour is subject to emotional regulation, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate a direct relationship between the central amygdala, a major output hub of the limbic system associated with emotional brain function, and the brainstem pre‐Bötzinger complex, which generates the fundamental rhythm and pattern for breathing. The connection between these two sites is monosynaptic and inhibitory, involving GABAergic central amygdala neurons whose axonal projections act predominantly via ionotropic GABAA receptors to produce inhibitory postsynaptic currents in pre‐Bötzinger neurons. This pathway may provide a mechanism to inhibit breathing in the context of freezing to assess threats and plan defensive action. The existence of this pathway may further explain how epileptic seizures invading the amygdala cause long‐lasting apnea, which can be fatal. Although their ultimate importance awaits further behavioural tests, these results elucidate a link between emotional brain function and breathing, which underlies survival‐related behaviour in mammals and pertains to human anxiety disorders.Keywords: anxiety, central amygdala, defense, pre‐Bötzinger complex, respiration, SUDEP

INTRODUCTION

Breathing behaviour must be both robust and flexible: rhythmic pumping movements for physiological gas exchange are inexorable, but their frequency and amplitude adjust ventilation to adapt to changes in internal and external environments that affect physiological demand. Explaining how breathing can be stable, yet labile simultaneously remains an important objective for neuroscience because it is related to physiological and ethological brain function.

Emotion and respiration are fundamental functions that are essential to protect the animals from survival risks and satisfy oxygen demands, respectively. Recent lines of evidence revealed that these prosurvival functions are mutually and strongly connected with bidirectional influence. For example, emotional events that involve fear and anger strongly modulate respiration (Bagur et al., 2021; Folschweiller & Sauer, 2021; Jerath & Beveridge, 2020) and disciplined breathing changes mood and emotional state (Balban et al., 2023; Goheen et al., 2023; Kral et al., 2023; Lavretsky & Feldman, 2021; Zaccaro et al., 2018). Emotional brain function might regulate breathing via polysynaptic microcircuits that utilize neuropeptide or monoamine transmission (Homma & Masaoka, 2008; Doi & Ramirez, 2010). This type of mechanism would help coordinate gradual changes in respiratory activity and a slowly changing emotional state. However, it is also reported that breathing shows instantaneous responses to rapid emotional state changes, such as a fearful encounter with threats that evoke defensive behaviour, acute pain and sudden traumatic events that provide strong emotional traces. Such rapid breathing responses might enable the body to be prepared for risky, threatening events and execute protective behaviours. For acute emotional events to cause abrupt changes in the rhythm of breathing—or even cause breath holding—then one expects emotion‐related microcircuits to directly influence its source, the pre‐Bötzinger complex (preBötC) of the lower medulla (Ashhad et al., 2022; Del Negro et al., 2018; Ramirez & Baertsch, 2018a, 2018b; Smith et al., 1991). For such a function to be realized, one expects that the emotional circuits send commands to the network controlling breathing rhythm. The central amygdala (CeA), a core member of the amygdaloid complex particularly involved in sending outputs to influence behaviours associated with emotion (Fadok et al., 2018; LeDoux, 2000), is a potential candidate for such direct control of breathing by integrated emotional circuits. The neurons in the CeA send axons to various structures such as the periaqueductal grey and the dorsal vagal complex, where they form direct synaptic contacts (Li & Sheets, 2018), thus playing roles in emotion‐regulated behaviours, such as freezing (Viviani et al., 2011) or autonomic modulation (Huber et al., 2005). A recent identification of projections from the CeA‐to‐core preBötC rhythmogenic neurons, using single synapse‐spanning retrograde rabies virus tracing (Yang et al., 2020), provides support for the possibility of direct emotion‐to‐respiration modulation.

Here, we show neuroanatomical and physiological evidence for monosynaptic GABAergic projections from CeA neurons to preBötC interneurons, which could regulate the rhythm and pattern of breathing. CeA‐to‐preBötC connectivity might inhibit breathing in two relevant contexts: during threat assessment behaviour that involves attentive locomotor arrest (Roelofs, 2017; Tovote et al., 2016) and when epileptic seizures invade the amygdaloid complex and cause apnea without interoceptive alert (Dlouhy et al., 2015; Harmata et al., 2023; Rhone et al., 2020).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Jikei University where all the experiments were performed (Approval No. 2022‐088). All animal experiments conformed to the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments of the Science Council of Japan (2006) and which conform to the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Percie du Sert et al., 2020).

Mice

We used heterozygous and homozygous male and female Vgat‐ires‐cre mice (Jackson Laboratory [JAX] Slc32a1tm2(cre)Lowl/J, strain #016962, RRID:IMSR_JAX:016962) and male C57BL6J mice (JAX strain #000664, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664). Mice were housed in groups and maintained with a rodent diet and water available ad libitum with a 12 h:12 h light–dark cycle at 25°C.

Stereotaxic virus injections

Mice aged 7–13 weeks were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in 100% O2 for induction, followed by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of medetomidine hydrochloride (0.15 mg/kg, Zenoaq, Orion Corporation, Espoo, Finland), midazolam (2.0 mg/kg, Astellas, Tokyo, Japan) and butorphanol tartrate (2.5 mg/kg, Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan). Mice were kept motionless in a stereotaxic frame. We injected an adeno‐associated virus (AAV) (7 × 1012 GC/mL) for cre‐dependent channelrhodopsin‐2 (ChR2) expression (AAV5‐EF1α‐DIO‐hChR2[H134R]‐EYFP; 20298‐AAV5; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) into the right CeA of Vgat‐ires‐cre mice. We injected an AAV (6 × 1012 GC/mL) for ChR2 expression under the control of the human synapsin promoter (hSyn) (AAV5‐hSyn‐hChR2[H134R]‐EYFP; 26,973‐AAV5; Addgene) into the right CeA of C57BL6J mice. A suspension solution was injected slowly (80 nL/min) using a 2‐μL Hamilton syringe with a 30‐gauge needle. To avoid the spread of AAV solution along the tract, the needle was slowly removed 10 min after the injection. The coordinates for the CeA were 0.8–1.2 mm posterior to bregma, 2.6–3.0 mm lateral to the midline and 4.7–5.1 mm dorsoventral depth. Fluorescent microspheres (FluoSpheres; F8794; 0.04 μm, 565/580; Thermo Fisher Scientific Life Sciences, Waltham, MA, USA) were added to the suspension solution when using C57BL6J mice and the AAV5‐hSyn‐hChR2(H134R)‐EYFP to verify the centre of injection sites. Fluorescent microspheres were unnecessary to determine the centre of the injection sites in Vgat‐ires‐cre mice. We visualized the injection sites via post hoc histology (50‐μm thick sections) with a fluorescence microscope (BX63; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The mice recovered on a heating pad and were closely monitored postoperatively for signs of infection, pain or distress. We prepared 0.75% ropivacaine hydrochloride hydrate for topical application in case the mice showed a high grimace scale score or unusual behaviours such as head‐scratching or abnormal posture. However, none of these signs were observed.

In vitro slice preparations for electrophysiology

Mice were prepared for electrophysiological recordings 3–10 weeks after virus injection (Sugimura et al., 2016; Ting et al., 2014). The mice were anesthetized via isoflurane (5% in 100% O2) and then perfused transcardially with an ice‐cold cutting solution composed of (in mM): 2.5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 10 MgSO4, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 2 thiourea, 3 sodium pyruvate, 93 N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine, 20 HEPES (4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid), 12 N‐acetyl‐L‐cysteine, 25 D‐glucose, 5 L‐ascorbic acid and 30 NaHCO3, equilibrated with 95% O2–5% CO2 and titrated with HCl to a pH of 7.3–7.4 (290 mOsm/L). Mice were decapitated and their brain and neuraxis were dissected in cutting solution at ~4°C. We cut 300 μm‐thick transverse slices from a block of the brainstem fixed on the chamber of a vibratome (NeoLinearSlicer; Dosaka EM, Kyoto, Japan). The slices were first incubated in a holding chamber filled with cutting solution at 34°C for 10 min and then transferred to another holding chamber containing artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) composed of (in mM): 125 NaCl, 3 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 10 D‐glucose, 0.4 L‐ascorbic acid and 25 NaHCO3 (pH 7.3, bubbled with 95% O2–5% CO2, 300–310 mOsm/L) at room temperature (25°C) until recording. We also dissected the region of the brain containing the CeA, which we fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histological verification of the virus injection sites.

Patch‐clamp recording in the pre‐Bötzinger complex

Slices were secured using a grid of nylon fibres on the fixed stage of an upright microscope (BX51; Olympus), while being superfused continuously at a 2 mL/min with ACSF. Slices were visualized using oblique illumination and epifluorescent optics. Whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings were obtained from preBötC neurons found amid the enhanced yellow fluorescent protein (EYFP)‐expressing fibres in the ventrolateral region of the slice. Specifically, we selected neurons immediately ventral to the semi‐compact division of the nucleus ambiguus, which is colocated with the pre‐Bötzinger complex in the anterior–posterior axis, and lateral to the dorsal‐most border of the loop of the inferior olive, which are anatomical landmarks that demarcate the preBötC as documented in the mouse brain atlas (Paxinos & Franklin, 2019). Patch‐clamp electrodes were fabricated from borosilicate glass pipettes (1B120F‐4; World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA). Electrode tip resistances measured 4–8 MΩ with an internal solution containing (in mM): 136 CsCl, 10 HEPES hemisodium, 5 EGTA (ethylene glycol‐bis[β‐aminoethyl ether]‐N,N,N′,N′‐tetraacetic acid), 12 Na2‐phosphocreatine, 2 Mg‐ATP and 1 CaCl2 (pH 7.3; 305 mOsm/L). For all experiments using Vgat‐ires‐cre mice and some experiments using C57BL6J mice, QX‐314 (Lidocaine N‐ethyl bromide, 5 mM, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the internal solution.

Whole‐cell currents were recorded using a patch‐clamp amplifier (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA, USA), filtered at 2 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz and digitized via a 16‐bit analogue‐to‐digital converter for storage and analysis (PowerLab, ADInstruments, Colorado Springs, CO, USA). Relative changes in the series resistance were monitored by observing the decay time of the membrane current response to −5 mV step commands (50 ms duration) delivered before photostimulation protocols. All recordings were made at 21°C. Digitally captured images were overlaid by ImageJ software (Schindelin et al., 2012; Schneider et al., 2012) with modification only of the brightness and contrast.

Photostimulation

ChR2 was activated using a high‐power light‐emitting diode illumination system (470 nm, 5 ms duration and 167 mW/mm2 intensity, LEDD1B, Thorlabs, Inc. Newton, NJ, USA) controlled by a digital waveform generator (Master‐8, AMPI, Jerusalem, Israel). Photostimulation was performed every 10 s in pairs with a stimulation‐to‐stimulation interval of 100 ms. Photostimulation was delivered to the whole field through a 40×, 0.8 numerical aperture objective (LumPlan FL N; Olympus).

Drug application

We added tetrodotoxin citrate (TTX, 1 μM) (1069, Tocris, Bristol, UK), 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP, 100–200 μM) (A78403, Sigma) and then 1(S),9(R)‐(−)‐bicuculline methiodide (bicuculline, 10 μM) (14,343, Sigma) sequentially to the bathing solution whenever preBötC neurons exhibited postsynaptic currents in responses to photostimulation.

Immunohistochemistry

C57BL6J mice aged 14–15 weeks were injected with 200 nL cholera toxin subunit B alexa fluor 488 conjugate (CTb recombinant, C22841, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in the right preBötC (6.5 mm posterior to the bregma, 1.2 mm lateral to the midline and 5.2 mm dorsoventral from the brain surface) in the same manner as described above for AAV injections. Eleven days later, the mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in 100% O2 and transcardially perfused with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% PFA in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The brains were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA followed by cryoprotection with 30% sucrose at 4°C. Blocks of brain tissue isolating either the forebrain (containing the CeA) or the brainstem (containing the preBötC) were separately embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) and stored at −80°C.

A series of 30 μm‐thick transverse sections containing the CeA or the preBötC was prepared with a cryostat (CM1860, Leica Biosystems, Tokyo, Japan). Sections containing the CeA were stored in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.1% sodium azide (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) until immunostaining. We amplified CTb‐Alexa‐488 via immunostaining (Arima et al., 2019). Sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS, incubated in blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 and 3% normal donkey serum) for 1 h and then incubated overnight with a goat CTb antibody (1:10000, #703, List Labs, Campbell, CA, USA) and a rabbit monoclonal protein kinase C‐delta (PKCδ) antibody (1:1000, ab182126, Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) in blocking buffer. Subsequently, the sections were washed in PBS and incubated in blocking buffer containing donkey anti‐goat IgG (H + L) cross‐adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000, A11055, Invitrogen) and donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross‐adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 568 (1:1000, A10042, Invitrogen) for 4 h. After washing in PBS, the sections were stained with DAPI (1:500, D523, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA) and placed on glass slides and affixed with coverslips using Fluoromount‐G (SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL, USA). These sections were imaged on a confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Analysis for the number of CTb‐immunoreactive cells was performed from the images collected using the 10× objective. Z‐stacks were collected at 4.51‐μm steps and converted into maximum intensity z‐projections using ImageJ. Quantitative analysis was performed in the CeA identified according to the mouse brain atlas (Paxinos & Franklin, 2019). The region of interest (ROI) that corresponds to each CeA cell was generated from DAPI images by segmentation using Cellpose software (Stringer et al., 2021). The masks made by Cellpose were imported to ImageJ, and the area fraction for each ROI was measured in CTb images binarized by thresholding. The CTb‐positive cells were counted when the area fraction was over 30%.

Data and statistical analysis

Recorded membrane currents were analyzed offline with Igor 9 (WaveMetrics, Lake Oswego, OR, USA) using programmes written by F.K.

The latency of light‐evoked postsynaptic currents was calculated as the time between the onset of photostimulation and the beginning of onset of the light‐evoked postsynaptic currents. We evaluated the difference in latency between control ACSF versus ACSF with TTX and 4‐AP present via a Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistical test applied to the cumulative probability distributions of latency in each condition. To evaluate differences in latency between control ACSF versus ACSF with TTX and 4‐AP for all preBötC neurons recorded, we applied a paired t‐test to the set of mean latency values in both conditions. Throughout, we express group data using the mean ± standard deviation.

RESULTS

We hypothesized that a subset of CeA neurons projects to the preBötC as previously suggested (Yang et al., 2020). To test this, we injected the retrograde tracer cholera toxin B (CTb) into the preBötC of adult mice (Figure 1a). CTb infected the preBötC preferentially (Figure 1b), and 11 days later, we detected CTb‐labelled neurons in the ipsilateral CeA. The percentage of CTb‐labelled neurons in each CeA transverse section ranged from 1.95% to 3.76% of the DAPI‐positive cells (Figure 1c, n = 3), suggesting that only a small proportion of CeA neurons project to the preBötC. We found neurons with preBötC projections in the medial (CeM), lateral (CeL) and capsular (CeC) subregions of the CeA (Figure 1d), but not in the basolateral amygdala (BLA).

CeA neurons comprise subpopulations with distinct molecular profiles (Fadok et al., 2018). Neurons expressing PKCδ have been shown to play specific roles in the network associated with fear and threat learning, appetite suppression, anxiety and nociceptive signalling (Botta et al., 2015; Cai et al., 2014; Ciocchi et al., 2010; Haubensak et al., 2010;Wilson et al., 2019). To examine whether the CeA neurons projecting to the preBötC are members of this neuron group, we immunostained for PKCδ in slices prepared from the mice receiving previous intra‐preBötC CTb injections. The cells expressing PKCδ were found almost exclusively in the CeC and CeL, with negligible PKCδ expression in the CeM, in accordance with previous reports (Adke et al., 2021). Although we found neurons with retrograde CTb labelling in the CeM and CeL/CeC, with a larger number in the CeM as described above (Figure 1d), the CTb and PKCδ double‐positive cells were extremely rare in the CeM and CeL/CeC (Figure 1d, inset). These data indicate that preBötC‐projecting CeA neurons are unlikely to belong to the threat detection microcircuits of the CeA (Han et al., 2015; Haubensak et al., 2010; Sato et al., 2015), such as those receiving direct inputs from the parabrachial nucleus (Han et al., 2015; Sato et al., 2015; Wilson et al., 2019). Instead, the monosynaptic inhibitory signal from the CeA to preBötC that we identified would represent the integrated output from the CeA network (Sugimura et al., 2016).

Next, to probe the physiology of CeA‐to‐preBötC projections, we injected the CeA with AAV whose payload included genes for channelrhodopsin‐2 (ChR2) and EYFP (Figure 1e,f). ChR2 and EYFP expression were driven by a synapsin promoter in the AAV injected in C57BL6J mice and by Cre‐dependent recombination in the AAV injected in Vgat‐ires‐cre mice. After 3–10 weeks we detected EYFP diffusely in the preBötC, presumably because of its expression in axons and their terminals (Figure 1g). EYFP expression in the preBötC was strongest when the site of virus infection was specifically localized to the CeA. When the site of infection was primarily in the BLA, then EYFP expression in the preBötC was considerably diminished or absent altogether (Figure S1). These data suggest that CeA neurons selectively project to the preBötC, and that neurons from other subregions of the amygdala do not project to the preBötC.

Three to 10 weeks following AAV infection in the CeA, we performed patch‐clamp recordings from preBötC neurons in transverse slices expressing EYFP (Figure 2a) and exhibiting characteristic landmarks found in slices containing the preBötC, including the semi‐compact nucleus ambiguus and the loop of the inferior olive. We recorded 72 preBötC neurons in total, 31 preBötC neurons from C57BL6J mouse slices and 41 preBötC neurons from Vgat‐ires‐cre mouse slices. We held the membrane potential at −60 mV in voltage clamp, and we employed a patch‐pipette solution with a high concentration of caesium chloride to attenuate potassium currents and elevate the reversal potential of chloride to near 0 mV.

Photostimulation evoked postsynaptic currents in 26% of preBötC neurons recorded from C57BL6J mouse slices (n = 8 of 31 neurons) and 27% of preBötC neurons from Vgat‐ires‐cre mouse slices (n = 11 of 41 neurons). Successful recordings of light‐evoked postsynaptic current were made from a total of 17 animals (eight C57BL6J mice, nine Vgat‐ires‐cre mice). There was no recognizable difference between the two strains in terms of percentage of responsive neurons (p = 0.99, Fisher's Exact Test, n = 72 in a 2 × 2 contingency table where exact probability is computed).

We tested whether light‐evoked postsynaptic currents were genuine via surrogate waveform analysis. First, we computed a cycle‐triggered average, using the onset of blue light as the trigger point for averaging sweeps of putative postsynaptic current. Then, we compared that average waveform to a surrogate waveform generated from the same number of surrogate cycles as light‐triggered cycles (Figure 2b). Trigger points for the surrogate cycles were randomly assigned at any point in the recording, which tests the null hypothesis that no special postsynaptic event is linked to the onset of photostimulation. If the peak current from the average waveform exceeds the 95% confidence intervals of the peaks of the surrogate cycles, then postsynaptic current with a peak of that magnitude is unlikely to have occurred by random chance with a probability of less than 0.05. In that case, we considered the light‐evoked postsynaptic current to be genuine, and the recorded neuron was classified as a responder, that is, a preBötC neuron that receives a CeA projection.

Neurons that did not respond to photostimulation exhibited spontaneous postsynaptic currents and stable holding currents at baseline membrane potential, indicating those neurons receive synaptic inputs, but not from neurons with ChR2 expression. Those neurons, dubbed nonresponders, were deemed not to receive CeA projections rather than attribute the lack of light‐evoked postsynaptic current to physiological or technical shortcomings.

Light‐evoked responses included both inward (n = 18) and outward currents (n = 1). Postsynaptic inward currents developed in ~5 ms and decayed to baseline within 50 ms (Figure 2c). Postsynaptic outward currents exhibited slower kinetics, peaking within 20 ms and decaying to baseline within 150 ms (Figure 2d). Because most CeA neurons are GABAergic and ChR2 was driven by recombination in Vgat‐ires‐cre mice in 58% of the experiments (n = 11 out of 19), we posited that the postsynaptic inward currents were attributable to activation of GABAA receptors, whereas the postsynaptic outward current was mediated by GABAB receptors, whose target ion channel was at least partially insensitive to caesium.

preBötC neurons classified as responders exhibited some failures, that is, sweeps in which photostimulation did not evoke a detectable postsynaptic current. The failure rate measured 34.1% ± 28.2% (range: 0%–90%) in standard ACSF for all responders (n = 18 neurons from N = 8 C57BL6J mice and N = 8 Vgat‐ires‐cre mice). There was no consistent change in failure rate with tetrodotoxin (TTX) and 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP) present in the ACSF (see below).

Next, we examined whether the connections between CeA and preBötC neurons were monosynaptic using an established pharmacological protocol (Figure 2c). First, we added TTX (1 μM), which suppressed the light‐evoked postsynaptic current, thus suggesting an obligatory role for presynaptic action potentials. Nevertheless, adding a low dose of 4‐AP (100–200 μM) restored the light‐evoked postsynaptic currents, indicating that direct ChR2‐mediated depolarization of CeA‐to‐preBötC synaptic terminals was sufficient to trigger neurotransmitter release and evoke monosynaptic postsynaptic currents in preBötC neurons. If the CeA‐to‐preBötC link were disynaptic (or polysynaptic), then 4‐AP would not have recovered the light‐evoked postsynaptic current because it would have required spiking in the intervening neuron (or neurons) linking the ChR2‐expressing CeA terminal and the recorded preBötC neuron. We replicated this sequential drug application experiment in C57BL6J mice (n = 2) and Vgat‐ires‐cre mice (n = 3), which produced indistinguishable responses.

We calculated response latency as the time between the onset of photostimulation and the beginning of the light‐evoked postsynaptic inward current. The average latency in standard ACSF measured 4.7 ± 2.0 ms (n = 15), which is consistent with monosynaptic CeA‐to‐preBötC projections. The average latency increased to 9.6 ± 3.9 ms in the presence of TTX and 4‐AP (n = 5). This principle is clear in Figure 2c, where the light‐evoked postsynaptic current begins within the blue‐shaded photostimulation bout in standard ACSF (~3 ms), but its onset occurs outside of the blue‐shaded photostimulation bout in the presence of TTX and 4‐AP (~8 ms). Figure 2e quantifies these data in histograms of latency for a representative preBötC neuron. Figure 2f shows group data; the mean latency of light‐evoked postsynaptic inward currents in standard ACSF was significantly lower than latency in ACSF with TTX and 4‐AP (t = 3.766, dF = 4, p = 0.0197, paired t‐test, n = 5). The coefficient of variation (CV) of latency was not significantly different (t = 0.08797, dF = 4, p = 0.9341, paired t‐test, n = 5). The longer latency and greater variability of the evoked responses in the presence of TTX and 4‐AP are attributable to the slower and less synchronized light‐evoked depolarization of the presynaptic terminals expressing ChR2 compared to the highly synchronized large depolarization by the action potentials at preterminal axons triggered by light‐evoked ChR2‐mediated inward current (Sugimura et al., 2016).

Finally, the GABAA receptor competitive antagonist bicuculline (10 μM) abolished the light‐evoked postsynaptic inward current in the presence of TTX and 4‐AP (n = 5 slices from 2 C57BL6J and n = 3 Vgat‐ires‐cre mice), indicating this postsynaptic current is mediated by release of GABA from the CeA neuron axon terminals and subsequent activation of postsynaptic GABAA receptors on preBötC neurons. In one neuron, light stimulation gave rise to a slow and small outward current at a holding potential of −60 mV occurring in synchrony with the stimulation (Figure 2d), suggestive of a postsynaptic GABAB receptor‐mediated current. Its pharmacological properties were, however, not analyzed in detail because of its rarity. In most cases we would expect intracellular dialysis with caesium to block the potassium channels coupled to GABAB receptors. We mapped the recording location after each experiment, verifying the postsynaptic neurons, both responders and nonresponders, was in the preBötC (Figure 3).

All the recorded neurons with measurable light‐evoked inhibitory postsynaptic currents, that is, responders and nonresponders, were located within the preBötC.

DISCUSSION

Here, we show physiological evidence for a monosynaptic inhibitory projection from CeA neurons to interneurons in the pre‐Bötzinger complex. The existence of such a path was suggested by dramatic clinical observations in intractable epilepsy patients with indwelling cortical electrodes. Ictal electrical activities causing seizures originating in or invading the amygdala, as well as the intracerebral electrical stimulation of the amygdala, immediately stop breathing movements, leading to oxygen desaturation (Dlouhy et al., 2015; Lacuey et al., 2017; Nobis et al., 2018; Rhone et al., 2020). However, the neuronal connections underlying such amygdalar influence on breathing have not been identified until now.

The amygdala, or the amygdala complex, is a group of nuclei within the limbic system involved in a variety of prosurvival functions, such as memorizing and avoiding risks, as well as potentially harmful threats, on the basis of sensory information (Adolphs, 2010; Fadok et al., 2018). To realize these functions, the amygdala complex receives inputs from various structures carrying information about potential danger, such as the parabrachial nucleus (tissue damage and inflammation, that is, nociceptive information) (Palmiter, 2018), and the amygdala complex sends commands to various executive circuits involved in motor, autonomic and endocrine functions (Pessoa, 2010).

These series of commands emanating from the CeA activate or inactivate subsequent circuits, which finally optimize behavioural responses to increase the survival rate. Of these commands, the output from the CeA, a subnucleus mostly composed of GABAergic neurons, plays key roles in coordinating appropriate and optimized responses, such as freezing and bradycardia, in facing potential threats. In addition to such survival‐relevant functions, the output from the CeA also causes autonomic and endocrine dysregulation when it is aberrantly activated by ictal activity originating in the cortex or BLA and transmitted to the CeA through these cascades. In these situations, the activity of the final output from the amygdalar circuit arising from the CeA potently affect the autonomic and autonomous functions such as breathing and sympathetic‐parasympathetic regulation of organs (Viviani et al., 2011), which could be both physiological and pathological. As such, the present finding that there is a monosynaptic direct route from the CeA to preBötC neurons at the core of respiratory rhythmogenesis—without intermediary network modulation—constitutes a mechanism for these various inputs from cortical and subcortical structures to stop or modulate breathing with a 4.7 ms delay compared to other polysynaptic routes, which undergo substantial delay and modulations.

A multi‐synaptic microcircuit mechanism that could act in parallel to inhibit breathing includes a midline site within the raphe nuclei at the level of the facial cranial nucleus. The CeA projects to this site (Verner et al., 2008), and its activation via excitatory amino acid agonists causes respiratory depression up to and including apnea (Verner et al., 2004). Nevertheless, activation of this site would require an intervening local microcircuit to invert the GABAergic inhibitory signal from the CeA.

In an ethological context, consider an animal presented with a threatening stimulus (e.g., the shadow of a looming predator or its smell). As part of its defensive response, the animal orients to the stimulus, arrests limb and body movements and slows its heart rate (Hagenaars et al., 2014; Signoret‐Genest et al., 2023). The parasympathetic state of freezing overrides active motor systems (Roelofs, 2017). These actions confer at least two benefits: freezing—lack of movement—minimizes the potential of being detected and facilitates attentive assessment of the scenario, which maximizes the probability of plotting a course of action most likely to ensure survival (Roelofs, 2017; Rösler & Gamer, 2019).

Locomotor arrest in defensive behaviour involves projecting neurons of the CeA that disinhibit excitatory principal neurons of the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey (vlPAG) via local GABAergic interneurons. In turn, vlPAG neurons send their outputs to magnocellular neurons in the medulla with locomotor function (Ferreira‐Pinto et al., 2021; Tovote et al., 2016). It now appears that the CeA‐to‐preBötC projection we characterize, mediated largely through GABAA receptors, could enable a parallel command to the respiratory system that slows and regularizes breathing (from 4 to 12 Hz normally to less than 4 Hz during freezing) in the context of threat assessment behaviour.

There exists another microcircuit mechanism for locomotor freezing, which stops rather than decelerates breathing. That microcircuit does not involve the CeA, but rather the midbrain locomotor area, particularly the Chx10‐expressing glutamatergic neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus (Goñi‐Erro et al., 2023). A small number of Chx10‐expressing pedunculopontine neurons send axons to the preBötC (Caggiano et al., 2018; Goñi‐Erro et al., 2023), but this particular projection is a vanishingly small fraction of the total output of Chx10 pedunculopontine neurons. Further, because they are glutamatergic, the signal from Chx10‐expressing pedunculopontine neurons would have to be inverted at the level of the preBötC to inhibit breathing rhythmogenesis. It remains unclear whether and how Chx10‐pedunculopontine neurons communicate to the preBötC or another pneumotaxic site of the midbrain or medulla to stop breathing in behavioural contexts not necessarily related to threat or defense.

We conclude that the two freezing systems described above are separate microcircuits with different functions; the first involving the CeA relates to defense, but the latter path involving midbrain locomotor area may have nothing to do with defense but rather other behavioural exigencies that require attention.

The microcircuits above were characterized in rodents, primarily mice. Their applicability to understanding human behaviours depends on the correspondence between these mammals and their neuroanatomy. We certainly know that humans have both a CeA and a preBötC (Brierley et al., 2002; Sawada et al., 2022; Schwarzacher et al., 2011).

The CeA‐to‐preBötC link fits in with a proposed apnea‐induced anxiety model, wherein activation of the amygdala causes episodes of apnea. Because the arrest of breathing leads to hypercapnia (elevated carbon dioxide) and attendant panic, which is anxiogenic, the repetitive activation of this pathway has potentially morbid consequences that exacerbate the condition of anxiety through a feedback loop (Feinstein et al., 2022). There is a notion emerging in the popular nonscientific literature called ‘screen apnea’, which may be a milder manifestation of apnea‐induced anxiety. Ubiquitous hand‐held electronic devices regularly deliver information that could be considered threatening via social media, email and news feeds, whose effect may reduce or stop breathing (Gupta, 2023; Erikson, 2023; Robinson, 2020). Nevertheless, apnea‐induced anxiety is a working model (Feinstein et al., 2022), and the citations above are neither empirical nor peer‐reviewed studies but rather reflect anecdotal evidence of people experiencing hypoventilation without realizing it.

Let us return to patients with intractable epilepsy who recognize neither apnea nor dyspnea (air hunger) when the amygdala is invaded by seizure. That implicates the CeA‐to‐preBötC pathway we have characterized as a direct mechanism for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) where seizure‐related activity in the amygdala stops breathing yet fails to signal the mortal danger.

In human patients, activation of the lateral amygdala or BLA elicits apnea (Harmata et al., 2023; Rhone et al., 2020), and stimulation that spans the BLA resulted in persistent postictal apneas, which could be fatal (Harmata et al., 2023). Further, in a mouse model of SUDEP, amygdala lesions reduced seizure‐induced respiratory arrest (Marincovich et al., 2021).

In accordance with the widely accepted understanding that the CeA, particularly the CeM, serves as the output from the amygdala network, excitatory inputs from a broad array of regions—including the cortex, hypothalamus and cingulate and insular cortices (Swanson & Petrovich, 1998)—excite BLA neurons, which in turn excite CeA neurons. In addition, CeM neurons receive locally integrated inputs from neurons in the CeC and CeL, which themselves receive various excitatory signals associated with threats and danger from the brainstem nuclei (Gu et al., 2020; LeDoux, 2000). Following the integration of these signals, the CeA circuit determines the final output to various extra‐amygdalar target regions responsible for autonomic, endocrine and behavioural regulation (Fadok et al., 2018).

The present finding that GABAergic CeM neurons project monosynaptically to the preBötC provides a basis for understanding the mechanism through which epileptic activity invades the central pattern generator of respiratory rhythm from cortical and hippocampal networks, resulting in apnea. This projection may also be involved in the immediate inhibition of respiration in response to potential danger or sudden, shocking events.
