Translational tilings: structured or wild?
Rachel Greenfeld

Abstract
. The study of the structure of translational tilings has captivated mathematicians, scientists, and the general public for centuries and continues to thrive at the crossroads of analysis, combinatorics, dynamics, logic, number theory, and geometry. This vibrant field seeks to uncover the delicate divide between rigid structures and unpredictable, “wild” behaviors that arise when sets fill space by translations without gaps or overlaps. We provide an overview of this study and recent developments, highlighting its multidisciplinary nature and offering a glimpse into the process behind the results.

1.
Introduction

Tiling
a space means covering it without overlaps using a small number of different types of building blocks (often just one), which we refer to as
tiles
.
A simple example is a kitchen floor tiled with identical squares, neatly arranged next to each other. But tilings need not be so plain–they can be playful, like Escher’s lizard tiling, or even more surprising, with no patterns repeating, as in the aperiodic Penrose kite and dart or the recently discovered aperiodic hat tiling.

Beyond aesthetics and tile decoration, these examples reveal a crucial distinction in their underlying geometry: unlike the square tiling, which consists solely of translated copies of the tile, creating an Escher lizard tiling requires both translations and rotations of the lizard tile, and the hat tiling requires not only translations and rotations, but also reflections of the tile to perfectly cover the plane (cf. the

In this article, we exclusively explore the first, most basic case: tiling achieved through translations alone.

The study of tiling by translations has connections to parts of analysis, combinatorics, dynamics, algebra and number theory, as well as computability theory and, of course, geometry. It goes back to antiquity and has remained vibrant in the present through Hilbert’s eighteenth problem

The restriction of a tile’s allowed isometries to translations alone enforces a more rigid and structured behavior on the resulting tilings. Translational tilings (in Abelian groups) often have properties that more general tilings do not have.
However, it has turned out that these properties cease to hold when the dimension is sufficiently large. It appears that the extra freedom afforded by high dimension compensates for the rigidity imposed by restricting to translations.

More generally, there seems to be a mysterious divide between structured tiling problems, in which the tilings are well behaved, and wild tiling problems, where almost anything can happen; e.g.,

We provide an overview of the study of this mystery, including recent developments (to date) in this area, carried out by the authors and collaborators, focusing mostly on a project joint with Terence Tao.

We reveal some of the milestones in our study process–omitted from our published works yet crucial in the journey that led to our recent developments: how our thinking evolved, how failed attempts redirected us, and how these ultimately paved the way to our solutions.
Our main goal is to provide a window into our process, enabling other researchers to examine it and, we hope, build upon it to advance the field further.
Another objective is to convey the multidisciplinary aspect of our study process: how shifting perspectives, adopting different viewpoints, and combining tools from analysis, commutative algebra, combinatorics, dynamics, and logic eventually opened the gate to solving the periodic tiling conjecture.

2.
The domino problem: encoding wild behavior

In the early 1960s, H. Wang introduced a fundamental challenge known as
Wang’s domino problem

[
65
,
64
]
. This problem involves a finite set of square tiles, each with colored sides, also known as
Wang squares
. The goal is to determine whether an infinite plane can be completely covered by these tiles where the colors of all adjacent sides match. A successful arrangement of the entire plane is called
Wang tiling
; see Figure
2.1
for an example.

Figure 2.1

On the left, a set of 13 Wang square tiles (over five colors). On the right, a piece of a Wang tiling with the given squares.

Wang’s initial interest in this geometric problem was driven by his work on the decidability of first-order logic
\forall\exists\forall
formulas. He found that these formulas could be interpreted as domino problems, which raised a central question: Does there exist an algorithm that can, for an arbitrary set of Wang squares, determine in a finite number of steps if they admit a Wang tiling?

Wang observed that the domino problem would be algorithmically decidable

Logical decidability is stronger than algorithmic decidability: if a sentence is logically decidable, then it is also algorithmically decidable. Thus, a proof of the algorithmic undecidability of tilings immediately implies their logical undecidability.
See

if one could show that any finite set of Wang tiles that tiles the plane also has a tiling that is

The idea is that an algorithm, given any finite set of Wang squares, could systematically attempt to tile increasingly large finite regions. If this domino problem is not solvable (i.e., the set does not admit a Wang tiling), compactness guarantees that the obstacle will be detected after finitely many steps. If the set does admit a Wang tiling, then by assumption it admits a periodic tiling; hence, after finitely many steps the algorithm will detect a finite region satisfying periodic boundary conditions and can be tiled by the given squares, guaranteeing that the given set tiles the plane. This gave rise to the study of the
periodicity of tilings

A major breakthrough came with the proof that the fixed Wang tiling problem–determining if a finite set of Wang squares can create a tiling that includes a specific tile–is undecidable

2.2.
Tiling language.

In
[
22
]

that allowed us to establish the undecidability of translational tilings with as few as two tiles.
Heuristically, the guiding idea was to reduce the number of tiles at the expense of increasing the dimension of the group.
This language enables us to express certain first-order sentences as systems of
tiling equations
with
J

[
22

We then create a library of constraints, similar to a programmer’s collection of subroutines, expressible in our tiling language. This library is then be used to encode any domino problem as a tiling with only two tiles.

Note that a domino problem consists of a set of finitely many Wang squares

3.
Discrete translational monotilings

Let
G=(G,+)
be a finitely generated Abelian group, endowed with the discrete topology. Thus,
G
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times G_{0}
for some
d\geqslant 0
and a finite Abelian group
G_{0}
. We say that a finite set
F\subset G

tiles
G
by translations
if there exists a set
A\subset G
such that the translates

(3.1)

F+a=\{f+a\colon f\in F\},\quad a\in A

of
F
along
A
form a
partition
of
G
, i.e., for every point
g
in
G
there exists a
unique
pair
(f,a)
in
F\times A
such that
g=f+a
. We refer to such
F
as a
tile
of
G
and
A
as a
tiling of
G
by
F
, and say that the
tiling equation

F\oplus A=G
holds.
In other words,
F
tiles
G
by translations along
A
whenever the following two conditions are satisfied:

•

(packing)
Each point in
F+A
is covered exactly once by the sets (
3.1
), meaning that
F\oplus A
is well defined.

•

(covering)

F+A=G
.

We consider the
indeterminate tiling equation
:

(3.2)

F\oplus X=G,

where we view the finite set
F\subset G
as given data and the indeterminate variable
X
denotes an
unknown
subset of
G
. We define the
solution space
of the tiling equation
3.2
as

(3.3)

\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)\coloneqq\{A\subset G\colon F\oplus A=G\}.

Then, the given set
F
tiles
G
by translations if and only if the solution space (
3.3
) is nonempty.

Example 3.1

(Singleton tile)
.

Any singleton
\{x\}\subset G
is a translational tile as
\{x\}\oplus G=G
. Indeed, when translating
F
by every element in
A\coloneqq G
, every point of
G
is covered exactly once.
In fact, this is the only tiling of
G
by
\{x\}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tile}(\{x\};G)=\{G\}
. This is the simplest form of a translational tiling.

Example 3.2

(Non-tiling example)
.

The set
\{0,2,3\}
is not a translational tile of
\mathbb{Z}
, as the set
\operatorname{Tile}(\{0,2,3\};\mathbb{Z})
is empty. Indeed, suppose
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
were a tiling in
\operatorname{Tile}(\{0,2,3\};\mathbb{Z})
; by translation invariance, we can assume that
0\in A
. Then, by the covering assumption, there is
a
in
A\setminus\{0\}
such that

; hence
a\in\{-2,-1,1\}
. But this implies an overlap between the copies of
\{0,2,3\}
at

and

, contradicting the packing assumption.
This example demonstrates that not all finite sets can act as translational tiles.

Example 3.3

(Discrete square tile)
.

The discrete square
F=\{0,1\}^{2}
tiles
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
along

A_{a}\coloneqq\{(2n,2m+a(n))\colon n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\}\quad\text{as well as}\quad A^{a}\coloneqq\{(2n+a(m),2m)\colon n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\}

for every function

We are interested in the question of whether a given tiling equation (
3.2

\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)

3.1.
Elementary properties.

Some structural properties of the space
\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)
can be easily deduced from its definition.

•

Translation-invariance.
A fundamental property of tilings is that they are invariant under translation.
It follows immediately from the definition of tiling that
\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)=\operatorname{Tile}((F-x);G)
for every
x\in G
. In other words,
A
is a tiling of
G
by
F
if and only if
A+x
is a tiling of
G
by
F
for every
x\in G
. From a dynamical viewpoint, this property is useful.

•

Density.
Every tiling
A
in
\operatorname{Tile}(F;\mathbb{Z}^{d})
has a well-defined
asymptotic density
, which is equal to
\frac{1}{|F|}
; i.e., for every
A\in\operatorname{Tile}(F;\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|(A-x)\cap\{-N,\dots,N\}^{d}|}{(2N+1)^{d}}

exists and is equal uniformly to
\frac{1}{|F|}
(see, e.g.,
[
40
, Lemma 1.2]
for details). The notion of density provides a quantitative measure of a set’s distribution over its space–how spread out or clustered it is.
As such, this property imposes some degree of regularity on the structure of tilings by
F
.

•

Reflection-invariance.
By the commutativity of the group action, we have that
\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)=\operatorname{Tile}(-F;G)
, where
-F=\{-f\colon f\in F\}
(see, e.g.,
[
59
, Theorem 13]
or the proof of
[
19
, Theorem 1.2(i)]
for details). Since this property relies on the commutativity of the group action, it does not hold, e.g., when more tile isometries are allowed.

Translational monotilings have an additional algebraic structure that other tilings do not have, making them significantly more rigid. The rigid nature of translational monotilings gives rise to various structure problems.

3.2.
Different approaches and tools.

Equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence, the space
\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)
is compact and invariant under the action of the group
G
by translation;
thus, it is a
topological dynamical system
, which we can equip with a (Borel) probability measure to obtain a
probability space
, or with an ergodic measure to obtain an
ergodic system
. This gives rise to the use of probabilistic methods as well as ergodic theoretic methods to study the space
\operatorname{Tile}(F;G)
(see, e.g.,
[
6
,
24
]
).

Also, note that the tiling
F\oplus A=G
can be written in convolution form as

\mathds{1}_{F}*\mathds{1}_{A}(x)=\sum_{f\in F}\mathds{1}_{A}(x-f)=1,\quad\forall x\in G,

which looks tempting to analyze via the
Fourier transform
(see
[
40
]
,
[
41
, Section 2]
and the references therein). Taking distributional Fourier transform of
\mathds{1}_{F}*\mathds{1}_{A}=1
(as
\mathds{1}_{A}
is not necessarily a measure but rather a tempered distribution), we obtain the equation
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F}\cdot\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{A}=\delta_{0}
in frequency space (with
\delta_{0}
being the Dirac distribution), which provides significant structural information about
\mathds{1}_{A}
. In particular, we can deduce the necessary condition:

(3.4)

\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{A}\subset\{0\}\cup\mathcal{Z}(\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F})

for
F\oplus A=G
, where
\mathcal{Z}(\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F})
denotes the set of zeroes
\{\xi\in\widehat{G}\colon\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F}(\xi)=0\}
of the trigonometric polynomial
\mathcal{Z}(\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F})
(this condition becomes sufficient as well when
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{A}
is locally a measure). Thus, by analyzing the set
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F}
we can deduce substantial structural information about the tiling
A
.
For instance, by the continuity of the polynomial
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F}
and since
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{F}(0)=|F|
, the condition (
3.4
) implies a spectral gap in
\mathds{1}_{A}
, which is a powerful piece of data to use when analyzing the structure of the tiling

(see, e.g.,
[
6
,
44
,
42
]
).

The argument in the proof of Theorem

In our recent work

The proof of

6.
Discovery of wild translational monotilings

In the previous section, we saw that in
\mathbb{Z}
, virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
and also in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

is strong enough to imply the periodic tiling conjecture (Conjecture

\mathbb{Z}^{2}

6.1.
Reversible structure theorem in virtually-
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
spaces.

In Section

Theorem 6.1

(Reversible structure theorem in

Let

be a finite Abelian group and

be a finite subset of

such that for every

(i)

.

(ii)

For every

(6.1)

\mathds{1}_{F_{f}}*\mathds{1}_{A}=\sum_{v\in V}\mathds{1}_{E_{f,v}},

where for each

We then used this reversible statement to reduce Conjecture

6.2.

Given an integer

Example 6.2

Let

(Periodic coloring conjecture)

For any

6.3.
Failed van der Waerden-type attempt.

One can attempt to establish the periodic coloring conjecture

(and, in turn, the periodic tiling conjecture

in virtually-

For simplicity, assume

(r(n),g(m),h(n+m),\sigma((n,m)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N\mathbb{Z}^{2})))\in\Omega

for all

Suppose we could find such a progression with the additional requirement

. We could then define

for every

and

Therefore, if we could prove a strengthening of the van der Waerden theorem stating that for any finite coloring of the integers

6.4.
From aperiodic subshift to aperiodic Sudoku to aperiodic monotiling.

Let
p
be an odd prime. The function

in (

limit-periodic function

[

]
(in particular, it is an
almost periodic

Other than in the residue class

behaves as a clock: it is an affine map from
\mathbb{Z}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}
to
\Sigma_{p}
that increases by one every time the input advances by one; and inside this residue class (
0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
) but outside its subresidue class

,
f_{p}
is again an affine map from
p\mathbb{Z}\setminus p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
to
\Sigma_{p}
that increases by
1
every time the input advances by
1
; and so on (see
[
26

). Thus, the subshift
f_{p}
generates is a

.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{p}\subset(\Sigma_{p})^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the class of functions
g:\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma_{p}
that are either constant, or have a similar
p
-adic affine structure. Namely,
g\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
whenever there is a step
h\in\Sigma_{p}
such that outside of one coset
\Gamma_{1}
of
p\mathbb{Z}
,
g
is affine, with
g
increasing by
h
when the input advances by
1
; in
\Gamma_{1}
but outside of one coset
\Gamma_{2}\subset\Gamma_{1}
of

is again affine, and advancing by
h
when the input advances by
p
; and so on. This set is the
orbit closure
of
f_{p}
, in the sense that the functions
n\mapsto f_{p}(an+b)
for
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
form a dense subset of
\mathcal{S}_{p}
(which is translation and dilation invariant). Thus, it contains the subshift generated by
f_{p}
(see
[

), which is aperiodic in the sense that it is not empty and none of its points is periodic.

More generally, the only periodic elements of
\mathcal{S}_{p}
are the constant ones. Hence, to disprove Conjecture

3.8

-adic affine structure, which is non-periodic.

Fix

(ii)

Note that this set is of the form

6.5.
Undecidability of translational monotilings.

A proof of the periodic tiling conjecture implies the decidability of tilings (see Section
2
). The converse, however, is subtler: while the presence of an aperiodic tiling does not automatically lead to the undecidability of tilings, such an aperiodic construction is often the first step toward establishing an undecidability result. Motivated by this, once we had constructed the counterexample to Conjecture

. (See
[

for an exposition on this construction.)

Note that the structure of the solutions to our

By the undecidability of the domino problem, it follows that decorated
p_{1}\times p_{2}
-adic Sudoku puzzles are also undecidable; that is, there is no algorithm that, given a decorated
p_{1}\times p_{2}
-adic Sudoku puzzle, determines whether it admits a solution.

Using our tiling language, we further encode any decorated
p_{1}\times p_{2}
-adic Sudoku puzzle as a monotiling equation in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times G_{0}
, for a suitable finite Abelian group
G_{0}
depending on the given Sudoku puzzle. Consequently, since decorated
p_{1}\times p_{2}
-adic Sudoku puzzles are undecidable, we obtain the undecidability of translational monotilings in virtually-
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
spaces
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
. See Figure

Remark 6.4
.

Note that in our undecidability result
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
the group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times G_{0}
is not fixed; the finite Abelian group
G_{0}
is provided as part of the algorithm input. Indeed, the group
G_{0}
is determined by the decoration rules of the Sudoku, which is determined by the original domino problem. We reduce the number of tiles in the domino problem to one at the cost of allowing the group
G_{0}
to vary and to have an arbitrarily large rank.
Using
[
50
, Corollary 1.3]
, we can pull back our undecidability result for translational monotilings in virtually-
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
spaces to deduce the undecidability of translational monotilings in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
d
is not fixed, but is provided as part of the input and can be arbitrarily large. Thus, the following question remains open.

Question 6.5
.

Is there
d\geqslant 3
such that translational monotilings are undecidable in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
?

7.
Continuous translational monotilings

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded measurable set of positive measures. We say that
\Omega

tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations
if there exists a
tiling

T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that almost every point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is covered by
exactly
one of the sets
\Omega+t
,
t\in T
; i.e., these sets
partition

\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to null sets, or, equivalently

\Omega\oplus T=_{\mathrm{a.e.}}\mathbb{R}^{d}.

As before, we let
\operatorname{Tile}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of all the solutions to the indeterminate tiling equation
\Omega\oplus X=_{\mathrm{a.e.}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with unknown
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We study the structural behavior of this apace.

Example 7.1

(Hexagonal tiling)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a centrally symmetric hexagon. Then
\operatorname{Tile}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
consists of all the cosets of the lattice (i.e., a discrete co-compact subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) whose fundamental domain is
\Omega
.
This regular tiling appears in nature, e.g., in bee hives. See Figure
7.1
.

Figure 7.1
.
On the left–tiling by a symmetric hexagon, on the right–bee hive.

Example 7.2

(Cube tiling)
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional unit cube. Then clearly
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\in\operatorname{Tile}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a regular lattice tiling by
\Omega
. However, the space
\operatorname{Tile}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
becomes extremely complicated in high dimensions. In particular, Keller’s conjecture, which asserts that any cube tiling contains two cube translates sharing an entire facet
[
36
]
, turned out to be false in dimensions
d\geqslant 8

[
45
,
48
]
. This is an instance of the phenomenon demonstrated in this article: the higher the dimension, the worse the structure of tilings.

Continuous tilings share many properties with discrete tilings, such as translation and reflection invariance, and the density property (see Section
3
above).
However, a substantial difference is that there is no continuous analog of the dilation lemma
3.5
. Since this lemma plays a crucial role in our study of the structure of discrete tilings, we cannot adapt the discrete arguments to the continuous setup. As a result, new methods are needed to study the structure of continuous translational monotilings.

7.1.
Continuous periodic tiling conjecture.

We consider the following continuous analog of the discrete periodic tiling conjecture
3.8
.

Conjecture 7.3

(Continuous periodic tiling conjecture
[
27
,
44
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded measurable set of positive measures. Then the tiling equation
\Omega\oplus X=_{\mathrm{a.e.}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not aperiodic. In other words, if the space
\operatorname{Tile}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is nonempty then it must contain a
periodic
set
T
(i.e.,
T
is invariant under translations by some lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

In the absence of a continuous dilation lemma and the presence of irrational points, it is significantly more difficult to establish positive results toward Conjecture
7.3
than positive results toward Conjecture
3.8
.
The following partial supporting results are known:

•

Conjecture
7.3
holds for convex tiles in all dimensions
[
63
,
49
]
. In fact, it was shown that any convex set that tiles by translations admits a lattice tiling.

•

In
[
44
]
, by proving the rationality of translational monotilings in
\mathbb{R}
, it was shown that the continuous periodic tiling conjecture in
\mathbb{R}
can be reduced to the discrete periodic tiling conjecture in
\mathbb{Z}
. In turn, since Conjecture
3.8
is known to hold in
\mathbb{Z}
, this proves Conjecture
7.3
in
\mathbb{R}
.

•

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Conjecture
7.3
is known to hold for topological disks
[
2
,
14
,
37
,
38
]
. In was further shown that in this case all the tilings must be singly-periodic.

•

Recently, in
[
10
]
, we also established Conjecture
7.3
for rational polygonal sets, by modeling these tiles as discrete tiles and analyzing the internal structure of the earthquake components of their tilings.

7.2.
Continuous vs. discrete tilings.

It is known, due to
[
44
]
, that Conjecture
3.8
in
\mathbb{Z}
implies Conjecture
7.3
in
\mathbb{R}
. In dimensions
d\geqslant 2
, this reduction is not known. Thus, in particular, the continuous periodic tiling conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is still open, although the discrete periodic tiling conjecture in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is known to hold.

Question 7.4
.

Does Conjecture
7.3
hold in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
?

Discretizing continuous tiles turns out to be an extremely challenging task when the degree of irrational properties of the tiles increases.
Thus, for instance, discretizing polygonal sets with rational vertices is relatively simple, while discretizing polygonal sets assuming only rational slopes is significantly more subtle and discretizing polygonal sets with irrational slopes requires new tools, since in this case the slopes are not preserved under linear rational approximation and thus the combinatorial structure of the tiling need not be preserved.

On the other hand, in
[
23
, Section 2]
, by rigidly inflating a discrete tile into a continuous one, we showed that the converse reduction holds in all dimensions; that is, the continuous periodic tiling conjecture
7.3
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
implies the discrete periodic tiling conjecture
3.8
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for every
d\geqslant 1
.
Therefore, for a sufficiently large
d
, our counterexample in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
yields a construction of a counterexample in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
23
]
. Moreover, in
[
20
]
, by constructing a
folded bridge
to connectify the latter counterexample in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and obtain a
connected
counterexample in
\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
. This disproves a stronger version of Conjecture
7.3
in which the tiles are required to be open and connected, in all sufficiently high dimensions.
Indeed–the higher the dimension, the wilder the structure of the tilings.
