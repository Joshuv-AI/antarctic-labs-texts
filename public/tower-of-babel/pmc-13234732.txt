Stability of proton pump inhibitors for use in hypersensitivity evaluation
Authors: Troy Wanandy, Simon A. Handley, Rahul P. Patel, Supta Anwar, Thanh-Thao Adriana Le, Wun Yee Lau, Michael D. Wiese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerious adverse drug reactions, including anaphylaxis and hypersensitivity reactions to proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), have been reported and are expected to rise as a result of their widespread use and over-the-counter availability. Relevant physicochemical stability information of PPIs for use in drug hypersensitivity evaluation is virtually unavailable. Appropriate shelf life determination of PPIs for skin testing will improve patient safety and streamline allergy testing procedures.ObjectiveWe studied the physicochemical stability of PPIs in plastic syringes.MethodsPPIs’ chemical stability was determined as a function of percentage of initial concentration remaining by a validated stability-indicating high-performance liquid chromatography method. Physical stability was monitored for the presence of precipitation, haziness, and color change by visible spectrophotometry and pH shift.ResultsAt refrigerated temperature, esomeprazole and omeprazole at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL were stable for up to 7 days, while pantoprazole was stable for 7 and 2 days at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL, respectively. At room temperature, esomeprazole and omeprazole at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL, and pantoprazole at 4 mg/mL were stable for up to 2 days. Rabeprazole 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL were unstable at room temperature.ConclusionAseptically prepared 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL esomeprazole and omeprazole, and 40 mg/mL pantoprazole have a maximum shelf life of 7 days when stored at 2°C to 8°C. At room temperature, aseptically prepared 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL esomeprazole and omeprazole, and 4 mg/mL pantoprazole can be used within 2 days of preparation, while 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL rabeprazole should be prepared fresh and used within 24 hours.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), including esomeprazole, omeprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, and lansoprazole, are widely used for the treatment of acid-related gastrointestinal diseases and as gastric protectants. They are remarkably safe, with reported adverse drug reactions occurring in 1% to 3% of treated patients,1,2 although serious adverse reactions such as anaphylaxis and hypersensitivity reactions (HSR) have been reported.3,4

Bavbek et al1 have provided evidence-based recommendations for the investigation, diagnosis, and management of HSR to PPIs. Skin testing (ST) is useful in the diagnosis of IgE-mediated reactions to PPIs, with specificity and positive predictive value of 100%, while sensitivity and negative predictive value were 58.8-61.3% and 70.8-91.9%, respectively. Bavbek et al recommended ST as the first step in the evaluation of mild to moderate HSRs to PPIs, and they provided a recommendation of maximum nonirritating concentrations for skin prick test (SPT) and intradermal test (IDT), which are 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL for esomeprazole, omeprazole, and pantoprazole, 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL for rabeprazole, and 30 mg/mL and 1.5 mg/mL for lansoprazole, respectively.

Although generally safe, ST with PPIs can induce anaphylactic reactions.5 A prerequisite for reliable ST is ensuring standardized preparations, with known composition, potency, and stability.6 Little information on the stability of PPIs at the IDT concentration of 4 mg/mL is available,7,8 and no stability information is available at the concentrations required for SPT. Appropriate shelf life determination of PPIs for ST will improve patient safety and streamline allergy testing procedures.9 Therefore, we sought to determine the physicochemical stability of various PPIs at their recommended nonirritating concentrations (NIC) in polypropylene plastic syringes at refrigerated and room temperatures.

Results and discussion

The PPIs studied were esomeprazole (Juno Pharmaceuticals, Cremorne, Australia), omeprazole (Sandoz, North Sydney, Australia), pantoprazole (SunPharma, Macquarie Park, Australia), rabeprazole (Merck, Melbourne, Australia), and lansoprazole (Merck, Melbourne, Australia).

Stability samples were aseptically prepared in a horizontal laminar flow cabinet (Airstream Gen 3, Esco Micro, Singapore) by pharmacists specially trained in such preparation. Lyophilized sterile injectable forms of PPIs, except for rabeprazole and lansoprazole, which were prepared from a nonsterile powder, were reconstituted with sodium chloride 0.9% for injection (NS; InterPharma, Manly, Australia) or NS with 0.4% phenol (NSP, Greer Laboratories, Lenoir, NC) to produce isotonic solutions equivalent to the SPT NIC, which were then further diluted with NS or NSP to the NIC for IDT. The stability of PPIs in water for injection diluent was not investigated considering manufacturer’s recommendation, the hypotonic nature of water for injection, and its irritative potential.8,9

Preliminary investigations with NSP, which is commonly used by allergists to dilute allergenic extracts, led to precipitation of PPIs in less than 24 hours (see the Methods section in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org), and lansoprazole had poor solubility when prepared according to the recommended NIC. This finding was not unexpected, as lansoprazole has been reported to be insoluble in water and only sparingly soluble in ethanol,10 and our experience in preparing 30 mg/mL (SPT concentration) and 1.5 mg/mL (IDT concentration) of lansoprazole in water was in keeping with the report. As such, NSP and lansoprazole were not investigated further. For the remaining PPIs, triplicate samples were prepared for each of the concentrations studied. For rabeprazole, solutions at the SPT concentration were sterilized by passing them through a series of sterile Millex 5 μm and 0.22 μm syringe tip filters (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in subsequent order before further dilution to the IDT concentration. The prepared PPI solutions were packaged in sterile polypropylene plastic syringes (Becton Dickison, Temse, Belgium) and capped with Combi-Stopper (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany).

The stability samples were stored protected from light in either a temperature-monitored refrigerator set between 2°C and 8°C or at ambient room temperature (21-24°C). Aliquots of the stability samples were obtained on day 0 (baseline; within 3 hours of sample preparation), day 2 (after 48 hours of storage), and day 7 (after 168 hours of storage), then analyzed for chemical and physical stability.

The chemical stability of the PPIs was determined as a function of the percentage of the initial concentration remaining by using a validated stability-indicating high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method. A Dionex UltiMate HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Melbourne, Australia) consisting of high-pressure pumps, column oven, autosampler, and an ultraviolet (UV) detector were used. Instrument control was performed by Chromeleon v7.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) software. System eluents were as follows: (A) 0.1% aqueous phosphoric acid, and (B) 0.1% phosphoric acid in methanol. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min using the following gradient: (1) 30% B ramped to 70% B over 6 minutes, then stepped to 30% B for 3 minutes (flow rate, 0.6 mL/min). Prepared samples (50 μL) were injected onto a Gemini C18 column (Phenomenex, Lane Cove, Australia). Column temperature was maintained at 25°C. Detection was carried out at a UV wavelength of 330 nm, except for rabeprazole, which used a wavelength of 240 nm. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Chemical stability of the PPIs was calculated as the percentage (P) of the initial drug concentration remaining at each analyzed time point (Ct) relative to the baseline concentration (C0), where P = [(Ct/C0) × 100]. Detailed HPLC methods are provided in the Methods section in the Online Repository.

The physical stability of the PPIs was monitored for the presence of precipitation, haziness, color change, and pH shift. Precipitation or haziness in samples was observed with unaided vision under bright light against white and black backgrounds at each analyzed time point. A calibrated pH meter (HANNA Instruments, Melbourne, Australia) was used to measure the pH of each sample at each time point. A UV-visible spectrophotometer (Evolution One Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to scan for color changes in samples and blank control (consisting of ultrapure water) over the wavelength range of 400 to 800 nm at each time point. A total absorbance for area under the curve for each of the sample analyzed was calculated by subtracting the value obtained for the blank over the absorbance range analyzed.

Data analysis to generate descriptive statistical information was performed by Microsoft Excel v2302 (Microsoft, Redmond, Wash).

Quantitative results of the chemical and physical stability of the PPIs studied are summarized in Figs 1 and 2, and Tables I and II, respectively. Acceptance limits for the determination of physicochemical stability and shelf life determination of PPIs for ST followed the same criteria used previously,9 with chemical stability defined as a change of ±10% or less of initial concentration.11 At refrigerated temperature, esomeprazole and omeprazole at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL were physicochemically stable for up to 7 days. Considering the standard deviation (SD) observed, pantoprazole is conservatively determined to be physicochemically stable for 7 and 2 days at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL, respectively (Fig 1, C). Rabeprazole was chemically stable for 2 days at 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL, but a change in solution color (Table I) and evidence of precipitation were noted starting on day 2, presumably causing the large SD observed on the chemical stability analysis (Fig 1, D). At room temperature, esomeprazole and omeprazole at 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL, and pantoprazole at 4 mg/mL were physicochemically stable for up to 2 days. Limitations of this study were the relatively small sample size, the small number of time points, and the absence of microbiologic stability data to complement the physiochemical findings. However, microbiologic stability is largely dependent on the preparation’s conditions and are hardly transferrable from one institution to another.12 The 0.4% phenol in NSP functions as a microbiologic preservative, ensuring ST products protection from microbial contamination during preparation and storage, allowing for multiuse throughout their assigned shelf life. In the absence of an effective preservative and sterility testing for batch release, ST products prepared aseptically as small batches by trained staff in a laminar flow cabinet using sterile starting materials may be assigned an empirical shelf life of 4 days at room temperature or 10 days in the refrigerator12 and used for a single occasion only, with the maximum shelf life dictated by the physicochemical stability data.

In summary, this study shows that aseptically prepared 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL esomeprazole and omeprazole, and 40 mg/mL pantoprazole can be given a maximum shelf life of 7 days when stored protected from light at 2°C to 8°C, allowing preparation ahead of time. At room temperature and stored protected from light, aseptically prepared 40 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL esomeprazole and omeprazole, and 4 mg/mL pantoprazole can be used within 2 days of preparation, while 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL rabeprazole should be prepared fresh and used within 24 hours. This study also found that lansoprazole had poor solubility when prepared to the recommended NIC of 30 mg/mL and 1.5 mg/mL, and NSP is not a compatible diluent for diluting PPIs. Considering the absence of preservative in NS diluent, preparations of PPIs for HSR evaluation should be used for a single occasion only. Integrating the findings from this study into existing guidelines for PPI hypersensitivity evaluation1 will improve allergy testing procedures and the management of HSR to PPIs.Clinical implicationSolutions of the PPIs esomeprazole, omeprazole, and pantoprazole are physicochemically stable for between 2 to 7 days, supporting their preparation ahead of time and storage for use in drug hypersensitivity evaluation.
