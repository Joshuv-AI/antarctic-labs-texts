Persistent Local Systems of Periodic Spaces
Adam Onus, Primoz Skraba

Abstract
The topology of periodic spaces has attracted a lot of interest in recent years in order to study and classify crystalline structures and other large homogeneous data sets, such as the distribution of galaxies in cosmology. In practice, these objects are studied by taking a finite sample and introducing periodic boundary conditions, however this introduces and removes many subtle homological features. Here, build on the work of Onus and Robins [ 14 ] and Onus and Skraba [ 15 ] to investigate whether one can recover the (persistent) homology of a periodic cell complex K K from a finite quotient space G G of equivalence classes under translations. In particular, we search for a computationally friendly method to identify all “toroidal cycles” of G G which do not lift to cycles in K K . We show that all toroidal and non-toroidal cycles of G G of arbitrary homology degree can be completely classified for K K of arbitrary periodicity using the recently developed machinery of bisheaves and persistent local systems. In doing so, we also introduce a framework for a computationally viable persistence theory of periodic spaces. Finally, we outline algorithms for how to apply our results to real data, including a polynomial time algorithm for calculating the canonical persistent local system attributed to a given bisheaf.

Introduction

Spatially periodic topological spaces arise naturally in many areas of mathematics, physics, and materials science.
These spaces exhibit a repeating pattern or symmetry over regular intervals, making them important models for understanding phenomena such as the formation of (quasi)-crystals, repeating patterns in dynamical systems, and, more generally, systems with periodic boundary conditions
[
4
,
19
,
3
]
.
Periodic spaces also appear in the study of tiling patterns, lattices, and periodic functions
[
9
,
18
]

In this paper we study the (persistent) homology of periodic spaces.
As a motivating example, consider a periodic point set
\mathcal{P}
that arises from the
d

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Let

We can then examine the

Because

with periodic boundary conditions.
This practice is common in simulations in materials science, cosmology, and other fields, as it captures the relevant physics while minimising memory requirements.
For instance, complexes constructed in this way have recently improved machine-learning pipelines for perovskite design
[

]
.

On the one hand, periodicity allows a decomposition into repeating units; on the other, boundary effects introduce intermittent homological features that complicate computation.

Classifying toroidal cycles is essential: it reveals the true topology of the periodic point cloud and captures physically relevant properties.
For instance, the appearance of a toroidal 1-cycle, as opposed to a “true” 1-cycle, signals the merger of large connected components—features that are useful when comparing three-dimensional layered materials

This paper builds on earlier work on the homology of periodic cellular complexes, which treated only special cases—degree-0 and degree-1 homology
[
14
]
or one-dimensional periodicity
[

—and it contributes to the growing literature on topological and geometric properties of highly symmetric data.
Related work on periodic point clouds

introduced a complete and computationally efficient degree-0

Independently,

[

employed covering-space theory and monodromy to infer the ambient topology of non-Euclidean data.
Applying periodic boundary conditions in Figure

Finally,

Here, we extend the results of

]
by characterizing the (persistent) homology in all degrees of finite quotient spaces of periodic cellular complexes with arbitrary-dimensional periodicity.
This work is the first to apply the theory of

and their canonical invariants—

(PLSs) from

Bisheaves furnish a homology theory for maps to oriented manifolds and support richer structure than ordinary (co)sheaves; their associated PLSs can be computed efficiently using parallel algorithms.
By leveraging bisheaves and PLSs, we unite ideas from covering-space theory, monodromy, and scalable topological data analysis, establishing a comprehensive bridge between the (persistent) homology of a periodic space and that of its finite quotient.

Our main contributions are:

1.

a complete classification of toroidal cycles (e.g. the yellow “cycle” in Figure

2

2.

3.

These results show that bisheaves and PLSs constitute a practical computational toolkit for analysing periodic spaces.

We restrict ourselves to the case of embedded cellular complexes such as subsets of triangulations or cubical complexes and as a reminder we compute the bisheaves corresponding to the

(rather than more general maps).
To compute the bisheaves we need to be able to do the following operations:

•

compute homology (with representative cycles)

•

compute cohomology (with representative cocycles)

•

compute relative homology (with representative cycles)

•

compute cap products

These computations will be done over preimages
\pi^{-1}_{d}
of open sets of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Therefore, we first must construct a cellulation of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that all open sets are captured by open stars of the cellulation. This condition is equivalent to saying that sheaves/cosheaves/bisheaves are locally constant over the open star of every cell in the cellulation. This allows us to then use cellular homology computations for the operations above.

In the case where
G
is a cubical complex, this is trivial as any the projection will correspond to a
d
-face of the (
n

\mathbb{T}^{d}
is naturally an open (closed) cubical subcomplex of
G
.

If

is a simplicial complex, a constrained triangulation of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
must be computed, where each simplex maps to a sum of simplices in the triangulations of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Note that while the requirement of
G
being embedded is for convenience, the requirement that the cellulation of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
be embedded is necessary.
As a simple example in the case of a 1-periodic complex as in Figure
5
, a cellulation of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is given by projecting all the vertices to the corresponding coordinate. As any change in the complex must occur at a vertex, this is sufficient for our purposes. One dimension higher (from
\mathbb{T}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
), we show an example in Figure

. With sufficient subdivision, we can obtain a triangulation with the required properties — subdividing whenever two simplices which do not intersect in
G

\pi_{d}(G)
. To the best of our knowledge, there are no non-obvious ways to achieve this, with a better run time for example, and we leave it for future work to further investigate.

Once the cellulation of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, which we denote

, has been fixed, we must compute the simplices which lie in the preimage of each cell of
\mathcal{T}
. In the case of cubical complexes, this is again trivial. In the case of simplicial complexes, we must project each simplex

and find which simplices

With this information in hand, we can compute the bisheaves. For the sheaf, we must compute the relative homology (and later also the cohomology) of each open cell. Here we use the fact that if we were to subdivde
\mathcal{T}
, each open star in the subdivision would correspond to an open cell in
\mathcal{T}
– so we omit the subdivision. For each cell

\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}(\pi_{d}^{-1}(\mathbf{cl}(\mathbf{st}(\tau))),\pi_{d}^{-1}(\mathbf{cl}(\mathbf{st}(\tau)))-\pi^{-1}_{d}(\mathbf{st}(\tau)))

where
\mathbf{cl}(\cdot)
denotes the closure. Here by
\pi^{-1}_{d}(\tau)
we mean all open cells which intersect
\mathbf{st}(\tau)
in the projection. It is straightforward to check that this deform retracts to the preimage. This is well-defined and can be computed cellularly as we assume finiteness. The induced maps between preimages of cells are indued by chain maps which are restrictions. This includes all the required data for the sheaves.

For the cosheaves, we must compute the homology of
\mathrm{H}_{\bullet}(\pi^{-1}_{d})

\pi^{-1}_{d}
which is a subset of

\pi^{-1}_{d}

\mathcal{T}
, are well-defined. For
\tau_{1}\subset\tau_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
, observe that
\mathbf{st}(\tau_{2})\subset\mathbf{st}(\tau_{1})
.
It is straightforward to check that the maps
\pi^{-1}_{d}(\mathbf{st}(\tau_{2}))\hookrightarrow\pi^{-1}_{d}(\mathbf{st}(\tau_{1}))
are cellular between maximal subcomplexes of the subdivisions. Hence the cosheaf information can be computed cellularly.

This is illustrated in Figure
5
. On the left hand side, we see the preimage of an edge of the cellulation of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
shown in red along with its subdivision. The closure, used in the sheaf computation, is shown in yellow and orange. The maximum subcomplex, is given by the dark red points (and the edges connecting them). Thus, the homology of the preimage of this edge is of rank 2 (i.e. consists of 2 components). On the right hand side, we see the case for a preimage of a vertex. The maximum subcomplex is again shown in red, but is bigger, but the homology is again rank 2.

Finally, as the cap product is computed pointwise, we have all the information required to compute it.
As all (co)homological computation is computed on the chain space of the subdivision, the orderings will be consistent with the initial complex which ensures each cocycle we use for the cap products are consistent.
Taking the cap product of the relative homology group with the relative cohomology group yields a map to homology which is isomorphic to the homology group of the maximal subcomplex described above.
Note that from a practical perspective, it is simpler from an implementation standpoint to perform the calculations in the sheaf on the subdivision thereby making all the chain groups directly relatable (i.e. without any deformation retracts needed in the maps).

Example:

As a simple example, we describe how this for the case of a subcomplex of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
under a
\mathbb{Z}

\mathbb{S}^{1}
. As described above, the triangulation of

\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and connect them in order (of the required coordinate) with edges into a simple cycle.

We must first find the required preimages of the projection:

1.

For the preimage of the open star of a vertex, take the simplices whose projection contains the vertex. This is simply the test of an intersection between an interval (the projection of the simplex to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
) and a point (the vertex coordinate).

2.

For the preimage of an edge, it is similar but the test is for the intersection of two intervals.

3.

We observe that the open star of an edge is just the edge, while the open star of a vertex is the union of preimage of the vertex and its two adjacent edges.

We refer to these subsets of simplices for each vertex and edge in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by
X(\sigma)\subset G
where

is periodic complex, and
\sigma\in\{v,e\}
for vertices (

\mathbb{S}^{1}

1.

Take a subdivision
\mathrm{Sub}\;\mathbf{cl}(X(\sigma))

2.

To compute the relative homology/cohomology at
\sigma

To compute the cosheaf, construct

For both the sheaf and cosheaf, the inclusion restriction maps on the chain groups give the required morphisms and the standard simplicial cap product computation may be applied.

We make two further observations:

1.

These can be computed independently for each vertex and edge in the triangulation of
\mathbb{S}^{1}

2.

By working with subdivision, the chain groups in the sheaf and cosheaf are comparable, namely any chain in the sheaf can be restricted to a chain in the cosheaf.

3.

The main complexity lies in the dimensionality of the translation action rather than the ambient space – so in the case above, where the translation action is 1-dimensional, the consontruction is quite efficient.

We conclude this section with a summary of the main algorithmic difficulties when we consider higher dimensional translation actions:

•

computing a cellulation of the projection such that the preimage of each cell islocally constant;

•

finding the preimage of each cell.

.

Suppose

Moreover, suppose
\overline{F}
and

are
\mathbf{vect}
-(co)sheaves with vector spaces bounded by dimension

\omega
be the exponent of matrix multiplication complexity.

1.

Algorithm
1
computes the epification
\overline{E}\hookrightarrow\overline{F}
in
O\left(n(d+(c^{\omega}+C^{\omega})D^{1+\omega})\right)
.

2.

Algorithm
2
computes the monofication
\underline{F}\twoheadrightarrow\underline{M}
in
O\left(n(d+(c^{\omega}+C^{\omega})D^{1+\omega})\right)
.

Note that
d

refers to the algebraic dimension of a vector space.

Corollary 1
.

Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
combine to compute the isobisheafification of
\overline{\underline{F}}
in polynomial time.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

To show the complexity, we study the two while loops of each algorithm.
At the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
iteration of the first loop we may ignore cells of dimension greater than
d-j+1
, and in worst case scenario we must look at
n-j+1
cells.
Thus, there are at worst
d
total iterations and at most
n
cells saved in each iteration, so the first loop terminates in
O(nd)
.

Each iteration of the second loop must reduce the dimension of at least one one vector space of the (co)sheaf until it terminates.
In the worst case scenario, this means we must iterate through the second loop
nD
times – one for each dimension of each vector space.
Further, calculating intersections, kernels, images and preimages are all bounded by matrix multiplication time (c.f.
[
2
,
12
]
).
Each kernel, image and preimage is calculated from spaces of dimension bounded by dimension
D
, so these calculations have a complexity
O(D^{\omega})
.
Taking the intersection or linear sum of
k
such spaces (where
k\leq c
in one half of the loop and
k\leq C
in the other) will have computational complexity
O(k^{\omega}D^{\omega})
.
Thus the second loop contributes
O\left(n(c^{\omega}+C^{\omega})D^{1+\omega}\right)
.
Combining the two loops gives the desired complexity.
In particular, both algorithms must terminate.

Finally, we prove correctness of Algorithm
1
, as a dual argument completes the proof for Algorithm
2
.
By construction, Algorithm
1
must output an episheaf, as otherwise the second loop will never terminate.
Let
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}
be the maximal subepisheaf of
\overline{F}
.
By construction, the output
\overline{E}
of Algorithm
1
satisfies
\overline{E}\hookrightarrow\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}\hookrightarrow\overline{F}
.
Since we work over
\mathbf{vect}
, this means
\overline{E}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)
for each cell
\sigma
.
Conversely, we show by induction on
j
, the number of iterations on the second while loop of Algorithm
1
that
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{F}_{j}(\sigma)
for each
\sigma
, which completes the proof, since all maps of
\overline{E},\overline{F}_{j},\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}
are restrictions of maps of
\overline{F}
.
Indeed, when
j=0
we have
\overline{F}_{0}=\overline{F}
, and by definition of
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}
being a subepisheaf this means
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{F}_{0}(\sigma)
for each
\sigma
.
Now assume for some
r\geq 0
that
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{F}_{r}(\sigma)
for each
\sigma
.
Then

\overline{F}_{r+1}^{\prime}(\sigma)=\bigcap_{\tau:\sigma\leq\tau}\mathrm{im}\left(\overline{F}_{r}(\sigma\leq\tau)\right)=\bigcap_{\tau:\sigma\leq\tau}\overline{F}(\sigma\leq\tau)\left(\overline{F}_{r}(\tau)\right)\supseteq\bigcap_{\tau:\sigma\leq\tau}\overline{F}(\sigma\leq\tau)\left(\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\tau)\right)=\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)

where the containment comes from the assumption that
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}\hookrightarrow\overline{F}_{r}
and the final inequality holds because
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}
is an episheaf.
Hence
\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{F}^{\prime}_{r+1}(\sigma)
for each
\sigma
.
Likewise,

\overline{F}_{r+1}(\sigma)=\bigcap_{\tau:\tau\leq\sigma}\overline{F}(\tau\leq\sigma)^{-1}\left(\overline{F}_{r+1}^{\prime}(\tau)\right)\supseteq\bigcap_{\tau:\tau\leq\sigma}\overline{F}(\tau\leq\sigma)^{-1}\left(\overline{E}_{\mathrm{max}}(\tau)\right)=\overline{E}(\sigma)

where the final equality is true for all sheaves.
In particular, if

for each

In this section we outline how one can in practice extract the topological information of

5.1
Extracting the relevant data

Suppose we have calculated the

Consider the PLS

Conjecture 1
.

The eigenvalues of
M_{i}
must be roots of unity.

Now, having determined
M_{i}
, take a basis
\mathcal{B}_{v_{1}}

Appendix A
Existence of Persistent Local Systems

In order to prove the existence of persistence local systems (PLS’s) in arbitrary abelian categories, we first establish the existence of a maximal sub-episheaf.
We will show that given any two sub-episheaves there is a third sub-episheaf which is larger than both.
Then existence of a maximal episheaf follows by a standard application of Zorn’s Lemma on the poset category of sub-episheaves (c.f. Section 3 of
