Factors Associated With Exits From and Returns to Homelessness Among Older Homeless Adults: Results From the HOPE HOME Study
Authors: Cheyenne M. Garcia, Jennifer L. Evans, Dave Graham-Squire, Caitlin A. Carey, Karen A. Valle, Margot Kushel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Homelessness is dynamic; some individuals cycle between homelessness and housing. In a longitudinal study of 411 adults aged 50 and older who were homeless at study enrollment, we examined the prevalence of and factors associated with exits from and returns to homelessness. With a median 4 years of follow-up, 80% of participants exited homelessness into housing. Having a permanent rental subsidy was associated with retaining housing. Rental subsidies are an effective means to prevent recurring homelessness among older adults who have recently regained housing after a period of homelessness.

Introduction

Approximately 771,480 people experienced homelessness on a single night in the United States in 2024 (de Sousa et al., 2024). In the United States, the Homeless Emergency Assistance and Rapid Transition to Housing (HEARTH) Act defines someone as homeless if they lack a fixed residence, reside in a place not typically used for sleeping, are fleeing domestic violence, or are at imminent risk of losing housing within 14 days (“Homeless Emergency Assistance and Rapid Transition to Housing: Defining ‘Homeless,’” 2010). Homelessness is dynamic. For some, homelessness is a brief and isolated event, while others remain homeless for prolonged periods, and others transition between homelessness, housing, and institutions (Brown et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2016). Prior episodes of homelessness increase risk for future homelessness (Greer et al., 2016; Nilsson et al., 2019; Von Wachter et al., 2021). This study seeks to examine homelessness exits and reentries among older adults experiencing homelessness in a high cost of living area, where a large proportion experience unsheltered homelessness (Alameda County Housing & Homelessness Services, 2024).

Homelessness rates are higher in high-cost housing regions (Colburn & Aldern, 2022). The rates of homelessness in a community are associated with the disconnect between housing costs and the incomes of low-income households. Colburn and Aldern (2022) found that, within the 30 largest housing markets in the United States, housing markets with low vacancy rates and high rents are associated with greater rates of homelessness. A state-level analysis found that the cost of living index was the primary driver of homelessness rates; when the cost of living increases, so does the prevalence of homelessness (Heston, 2023). The United States Government Accountability Office found that a $100 increase in median rent was associated with a 9% increase in the estimated homelessness rates (Cackley, 2020). Those living in high-cost regions may face additional barriers to reentering or sustaining housing than those in low housing cost regions, such as a competitive rental market and limited affordable housing.

Older adults are a growing, yet understudied, homeless subsample with unique vulnerabilities to experiencing homelessness and challenges to regaining housing. The population experiencing homelessness is aging; cross-sectional studies find that half of single homeless adults are age 50 or older (Hahn et al., 2006; Kushel et al., 2023). Homelessness among older adults is expected to increase (Culhane et al., 2013; 2019). Over 40% of homeless adults over 50 years old first became homeless after the age of 50 (Brown et al., 2016; Kushel et al., 2023). Experiencing homelessness at an older age is associated with an increased risk of a longer duration of homelessness (Caton et al., 2005). Older adults may be less able to increase their incomes and have fewer options (e.g., Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) compliant housing, access to permanent rental vouchers) to sustain homelessness exits than younger adults (Heidkamp & Van Horn, 2008; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2017).

Despite the heterogeneous nature of homelessness, most nonintervention research is cross-sectional, limiting our understanding of homelessness exits or reentrances (Creech et al., 2015; Rowlands Snyder et al., 2021). Understanding the dynamics of homelessness exits and reentrances among older homeless adults living in high-cost regions will allow policymakers and program leaders to shape policies and programs to facilitate rapid and durable exits from homelessness among a high-risk population. Therefore, we analyzed up to 9 years of data from a longitudinal cohort study of older adults who were homeless at study entry in Oakland, California—a high-cost region with a high prevalence of unsheltered homelessness—to examine exits from homelessness to housing/institutions, returns to homelessness, and associated factors.

Background/Literature Review

We provide an overview of the current knowledge base regarding exits from homelessness into housing (i.e., returns to housing) as well as housing retention in the United States and other Western countries. We synthesize the evidence base for Housing First as a strategy to support returns to housing and housing stability.

Older Adults Experiencing Homelessness

Older adults experiencing homelessness have unique vulnerabilities to homelessness and require housing solutions that address these needs. Nearly 11.2 million older adults are cost-burdened (spending more than 30% of their income on housing), and only one in three older adults who qualify for a Housing Choice Voucher receives one (Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University, 2023; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2017; Center on Budget & Policy Priorities, n.d.). Older adults are more likely than younger adults to live on a fixed income, which limits their housing options and makes them more vulnerable to household income shocks or housing price increases, precipitating evictions and/or homelessness. Social security income or Supplemental Security Income (SSI) may no longer be sufficient to protect older adults from homelessness, particularly in high housing cost markets. Older adults are more likely to have mobility, functional, and cognitive impairments, which require accessible housing and additional services to remain housed. Both requirements limit older adults’ housing supply.

Homeless Management Information Systems

One of the main ways researchers examine homelessness exits in the United States is through analyzing administrative data, the largest of which are the Homeless Management Information Systems (HMIS). HMIS are maintained by local homelessness service providers to track the outcomes of people who receive federally funded homeless services (e.g., case management, shelters, and housing). While HMIS facilitates tracking long-term housing outcomes of people experiencing homelessness, HMIS data are limited (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, n.d.-c). In areas with a “right to shelter,” entrance into shelter is a reasonable proxy for the start of an episode of homelessness (An Act Further Regulating Assistance To Certain Needy Persons, 1983; Callahan v. Carey, 1979). In areas without a “right to shelter,” people who enter homelessness may not receive homelessness services immediately or at all. In 2024 point-in-time count data—wherein volunteers conduct a single-night count of people experiencing homelessness—36% of people (and 50% of adults) experiencing homelessness in the United States experienced unsheltered homelessness. In California, 66% of people were unsheltered (de Sousa et al., 2024). A representative cross-sectional study of adult homelessness using primary data collected from 2021 to 2022 found that 78% of adults experiencing homelessness in California spent most nights in the previous 6 months unsheltered (Kushel et al., 2023). Thus, HMIS data are limited in measuring homelessness entrances. Further, HMIS data on homelessness exits lack clear definitions and face other limitations, including incomplete information (e.g., large numbers of “refused” or “denied” responses) and inaccurate information (e.g., duplicate entries and multiple heads of household) (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, n.d.-b). Due to these limitations, HMIS data are not currently a reliable source to examine homelessness entrances and exits. These limitations highlight the need for studies using primary data collection with clear definitions of homelessness exits and reentrances.

Exits From Homelessness

Estimates from national data and limited funding for housing programs indicate that most people experiencing homelessness exit homelessness by self-resolving (i.e., finding market-rate housing on their own or moving in with friends or family without government housing support). While there is no official statistic documenting the number of people who enter and exit homelessness over the course of each year, some estimates show that millions of Americans experience homelessness every year (U.S. Interagency Council on Homelessness, n.d.). Yet each point-in-time count finds that fewer than a million people are experiencing homelessness on a single night (de Sousa et al., 2024). Government-funded housing programs for people experiencing homelessness include permanent supportive housing (PSH; subsidized housing with voluntary supportive services), transitional housing (time-limited [typically 24 months] housing with services), rental subsidies (programs where the government pays a portion of the rent), and rapid rehousing (limited-term housing subsidy with services). Only one in four adults and one in three adults age 65 and older who qualify for a Housing Choice Voucher, the most common rental subsidy in the United States, receives one (Center on Budget & Policy Priorities, n.d.; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2017). In 2022, homeless response systems had the capacity to place only 16% of those residing in shelters in permanent housing (Culhane et al., 2025). Given that the need is greater than the availability, it is impossible that most people could depend on these programs to end their homelessness (Colburn et al., 2025). These two pieces of evidence indicate that most people self-resolve homelessness.

Observational studies of housing outcomes in the United States find that many adults experiencing homelessness obtain housing. One study of 377 adults who had entered shelter for the first time found that by 18 months later, 81% had returned to housing. The most common ways to exit homelessness were to move in with family or friends (i.e., self-resolve) or to move into PSH. A smaller group exited to market-rate housing that they rent, with or without permanent subsidy (Caton et al., 2005). Another study of 250 adults aged 50 and older living in a shelter found that 41% had found housing in 12 months (Brown et al., 2015). The rates of returning to housing are associated with particular characteristics of the individual.

Research finds that there are individual factors associated with remaining homeless or regaining housing. A meta-analysis of 18 cohort studies examining exits from homelessness in Western countries (including the United States) found that female sex, higher educational attainment, and having a partner increased the odds of exiting homelessness. Relationship problems, psychotic disorders, and substance use problems reduced the odds of exiting homelessness (Nilsson et al., 2019). An arrest in the past year, a high level of physical manifestations of psychological distress, and having unmet care needs predicted housing instability (defined as less than 90 consecutive days independently housed or living in supportive housing) at a 2.5-year follow-up (Van Straaten et al., 2017). Individuals with substance use problems were less likely to reenter housing (Aubry et al., 2012). Given these associations, it is likely that different housing interventions are appropriate for different populations.

Multiple high-quality intervention studies found that PSH (subsidized housing with voluntary supportive services) offered on a Housing First basis succeeds in resolving homelessness rapidly and durably for people with chronic homelessness and significant behavioral health problems (Goering et al., 2014; Loubière et al., 2022; Montgomery et al., 2013; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2018; Raven et al., 2020; Tsemberis et al., 2012; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2023). Housing First is a philosophy that recognizes that housing is the root of stability. Housing services that operate on a Housing First basis do not require sobriety or engagement in treatment as a prerequisite to housing. The services offered in PSH include case management, substance use and mental health care, vocational services, and medical care, to people experiencing chronic homelessness (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2018; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2023). However, not all adults experiencing homelessness meet criteria for, or have access to, PSH (Colburn et al., 2025; Kushel et al., 2023). For people experiencing homelessness without significant behavioral problems, other less intensive housing interventions may be better options.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in families experiencing homelessness demonstrate that housing subsidies speed homelessness exits and decrease returns to homelessness. Compared to receiving rapid rehousing (limited-term housing subsidy with services), transitional housing, or usual care, a permanent rental subsidy was associated with the highest rate of housing and housing retention, and the lowest rates of homelessness at a 37-month follow-up (Gubits et al., 2016). Among veterans who received rapid rehousing services, a quarter of individuals lost housing within 2 years (Byrne et al., 2016). A longitudinal cohort study found that around 20% of formerly homeless individuals who regained housing subsequently lost it (Aubry et al., 2021). For individuals with low service needs, rental assistance without services may be sufficient. While some people will remain durably housed after receiving time-limited rental subsidies, those unable to increase their income (including many older adults) may not be able to remain housed after the subsidy ends.

Limitations of Prior Work

Our study addresses some limitations in the literature on exits from homelessness/returns to housing among adults, particularly older adults, experiencing homelessness. First, prior studies have short follow-up periods with follow-up at only one time point. Thus, they had limited ability to examine the dynamics of homelessness, including the frequency and timing of returns to homelessness. Second, most studies of housing retention among people who experienced homelessness—both experimental and observational—examine Housing First interventions (PSH). Housing First is one of many potential housing solutions to homelessness and is not available to most people experiencing homelessness due to limited funding and availability (Colburn et al., 2025; Culhane et al., 2025). Third, studies that focus on factors associated with regaining housing either report on only one type of housing (i.e., PSH) or do not report the types of housing outcomes; they typically report only whether a participant was housed or not housed. Fourth, studies examining factors associated with regaining or maintaining housing focus on younger populations, who face different obstacles when they regain housing and may be more likely to benefit from temporary housing solutions than older populations. One reason for this difference is that younger adults, compared to older adults, are more likely to be able to increase their income through job training and engagement in work (Heidkamp & Van Horn, 2008). Fifth, most studies that have tried to address similar concerns have relied on samples of people experiencing sheltered homelessness. Yet there is a growing unsheltered homeless population in the United States (de Sousa et al., 2024). People experiencing unsheltered homelessness in the United States are less connected to homeless services than those who are sheltered, including housing resources and other services to help people exit homelessness (Kushel et al., 2023; Richards & Kuhn, 2023). Lastly, studies that rely on administrative data, such as HMIS, that attempt to examine homelessness dynamics lack detailed and accurate data on homelessness exits and (in many cases) reentrances.

The Current Study

Our study fills existing gaps in the literature by examining exits from homelessness (i.e., returns to housing, housing outcomes) every 6 months. We track a range of housing outcomes—such as PSH, living with family or friends, living in market-rate rental housing, or living with a subsidy—and examine both housing attainment and retention. We include older adults who were homeless at baseline, whom we recruited from community settings including sheltered and unsheltered settings. We track the housing status of individuals, regardless of the length of their homelessness episode or whether it was their first episode, to examine the natural history of housing outcomes among older homeless adults.

Methods

Study Overview

The Health Outcomes of People Experiencing Homelessness in Older Middle agE (HOPE HOME) study is a longitudinal cohort study of health and life course events among adults aged 50 and over who were homeless at study entry. The University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Sample and Recruitment

Between July 2013 and June 2014, we used venue-based sampling to recruit 350 adults aged 50 and older who were experiencing homelessness in Oakland, CA (Wave 1) (Lee et al., 2016). We sampled from emergency shelters serving adults, free meal programs, a recycling center, and places where unsheltered adults lived. We constructed our sampling frame to approximate the source population and selected potential participants randomly at each recruitment site (Burnam & Koegel, 1988; Lee et al., 2016). From August 2017 to July 2018, we used the same strategy to recruit an additional 100 participants aged 53 and older (Wave 2).

To be eligible for HOPE HOME, participants had to meet age criteria, be homeless according to the federal HEARTH Act, speak English, and be able to give informed consent via a teach-back method (Dunn & Jeste, 2001; “Homeless Emergency Assistance and Rapid Transition to Housing: Defining ‘Homeless,’” 2010). We recruited people experiencing homelessness at the time of study recruitment, regardless of the length of their homelessness episode or whether it was their first episode. Participants remained in the study regardless of their housing status. We tracked housing status until 2022 regardless of when they entered the study (Supplemental Figure S1).

Study Design

Trained staff administered a structured enrollment interview and collected extensive contact information from participants. Contact information included where participants could be found between visits and contact information for others who would know where they were. Staff obtained permission to reach out to contacts if they could not find the participant. Staff conducted in-depth, structured interviews at baseline and every 6 months, with brief monthly check-ins in between, during which participants updated their contact information and confirmed their housing status. If participants missed two or more check-ins or any study visit, staff started “tracking” by calling contacts, visiting places the participant frequented, and searching relevant social service and online databases (e.g., HMIS, jail records) for information about participant whereabouts. We contacted participants’ contacts for information about their living situation and to remind participants to contact us. Participants received $25 for the screening and enrollment interview, $5 for monthly check-ins, and $20 for follow-up interviews.

Measures

Dependent Variable

Our primary dependent variable is housing status (homeless versus housed/in an institution) at each 6-month follow-up. We used data from all sources, but prioritized information that came from participants at 6-month follow-up visits. If participants missed a scheduled follow-up visit, we used housing data from the nearest check-in prior to the missed visit. If participants missed both follow-up and check-ins, we used data from secondary sources, such as contacts and administrative records. We dichotomized housing status as “homeless” or “housed/in an institution” in alignment with the HEARTH Act. We defined “homeless” as staying outside, in a vehicle, in a place not meant for human habitation (e.g., a tent, an abandoned building), a congregate or non-congregate shelter, an agency-paid short-term hotel or motel, and staying in housing where participants needed to leave within 2 weeks. For participants who missed a visit and were in jail, we classified participants as homeless or housed based on their status prior to their jail stay.

We defined “housed/in an institution” as living in an apartment/house with family or friends for at least 2 weeks, apartment/house/room that the participant owned or rented, transitional housing, PSH (subsidized housing with supportive services), institutional stays for at least 2 weeks (e.g., drug/alcohol detox program, board and care, and skilled nursing facility), or hotel/motel paid for by participant or family member where they had stayed for at least 2 weeks.

To assess housing stability, we examined returns to homelessness (defined as return to meeting HEARTH criteria) among participants who had obtained housing/an institutional placement during the study follow-up.

Independent Variables

We assessed time-constant characteristics (i.e., sociodemographics, veteran status, history of homelessness) at baseline. We assessed time-varying variables at baseline and each 6 month interview. For time-varying variables, we asked participants to report events in the prior 6 months (exceptions noted below).

Time-Constant Independent Variables

Sociodemographic Characteristics.

We assessed age, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, and the highest level of education. We categorized race/ethnicity as Black, White, Hispanic/Latine, and Multiracial/Other. We dichotomized educational attainment as graduated from high school or earned a General Educational Development (GED) certificate versus no high school diploma/GED.

Veteran Status.

We defined lifetime veteran status as active duty, reserves/National Guard, versus not.

History of Homelessness.

At baseline, we asked participants about the total duration of homelessness as an adult, and the duration of their current episode of homelessness (≤1 year versus >1 year). We asked participants about the age they were first homeless and categorized it within four age ranges: 18–25, 26–49, 50–59, and ≥60 years old.

Time-Varying Independent Variables

Residential History.

At each visit, we used a follow-back residential calendar to ask participants the location and duration of stays in their living environments, by night, over the prior 6 months (Tsemberis et al., 2007). Living locations included: homeless shelters, unsheltered places (including vehicles), housing belonging to family/friends, transitional housing, hotels or single-occupancy room units, rented rooms or apartments, owned homes, and institutions (e.g., prison, skilled nursing facilities, drug/alcohol detox programs). We categorized the number of nights participants spent unsheltered in the previous 6 months (≤30, 31–150, and >150 nights).

Permanent Housing Subsidies.

We asked housed participants whether they had received a housing subsidy or voucher in the prior 6 months or lived in public housing. We considered those in public housing as receiving a permanent (non-time-limited) subsidy. We categorized housed participants as not receiving a permanent rental subsidy (living in transitional housing, not reporting receiving a subsidy, not living in public housing or PSH), receiving a permanent rental subsidy (either a voucher or living in public housing), or living in PSH.

We determined receipt of a housing subsidy and whether those with subsidies lived in PSH using various methods to increase accuracy. First, we asked participants whether they received a subsidy by asking a set of questions about how they paid their rent. For instance, if they rented an apartment on the free market, we asked whether they received assistance from the government to pay their rent, such that they paid only a portion of their income. To understand whether a participant was living in PSH, we asked them whether they were living in housing for formerly homeless individuals, whether their rental payments were tied to their income, whether a nonprofit organization helped with their housing, and whether they had services in their housing. To spur memory, we provided participants with a list of names of local nonprofit PSH providers. As a final check, we compared participants’ addresses against a list of all project-based PSH and public housing in the area. If we found that a participant was in either PSH or public housing and they had not reported receiving a subsidy, we reclassified them to the correct group.

Other Factors Related to Housing.

We assessed employment status as currently working for pay or not. We asked participants whether they had experienced physical or sexual violence in the prior 6 months (any physical or sexual violence versus none) (Tong et al., 2021; World Health Organization [WHO], 2005). We asked participants if they had spent a night in jail and/or prison in the prior 6 months. We asked participants whether they had a case manager, defined as someone working at an agency who talked about or helped them get services. We asked participants whether they had any close friends or family members in whom they could confide, and if they had any close friends or family members who would lend money or with whom they could stay if needed (any versus none) (Gielen et al., 2001). We assessed community support by asking participants if they attended a place of worship, community center, or social meeting.

Health and Health-Related Behaviors.

We asked participants to report their health (poor/fair versus good/very good/excellent) (Ware et al., 1996). At baseline, we used questions from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to assess whether participants were ever diagnosed with the following conditions: hypertension, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, or diabetes (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2009). At each follow-up, we asked participants whether they had a new diagnosis since the last interview. Once participants reported a chronic illness, we considered them to have it from that point forward.

We used questions adapted from the Addiction Severity Index (ASI) to assess mental health in the past 6 months (McLellan et al., 1992). We asked participants if they had experienced: (a) serious depression, (b) serious anxiety/tension, (c) hallucinations, (d) trouble understanding, concentrating, or remembering, (e) trouble controlling violent behavior, (f) serious thoughts of suicide, (g) suicide attempts, or (h) prescription of medication for psychological or emotional problems. We assessed depressive symptoms in the past week using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) Scale (range 0–60; moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms defined as a score ≥22) (Radloff, 1977). To assess potential post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), we administered the Primary Care PTSD Screen (range 0–5; likely PTSD defined as a score ≥4) (Prins et al., 2004). We asked participants if they had been hospitalized for emotional/mental health problems in the last 6 months.

To assess alcohol use in the past 6 months, we administered the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) (Babor et al., 2001). We asked participants the frequency of their alcohol use: never, monthly or less, 2–4 times a month, 2–3 times a week, and 4+ times a week. We asked participants about the typical amount of alcohol they consumed during days they drank and considered those who reported having ≥ 6 drinks on one occasion at least monthly as binge drinkers (Babor et al., 2001). We measured illicit drug use (cocaine, methamphetamine, or non-prescription opioids) in the past 6 months using the World Health Organization Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (WHO-ASSIST; moderate-to-severe use defined as a score ≥4) (Humeniuk et al., 2010).

Cognitive and Functional Impairment.

We assessed functional status by asking participants whether they had difficulty performing any of the five activities of daily living (ADLs; bathing, dressing, eating, transferring, and toileting) (Katz, 1983). We assessed instrumental activities of daily living (iADLs) using the Brief Instrumental Functioning Scale, validated for homeless populations (Sullivan et al., 2001). iADLs are tasks that help people live independently such as cooking, cleaning, and using transportation. We defined ADL and iADL impairment as difficulty performing ≥1 ADLs or iADLs. We assessed cognition using the validated Modified Mini-Mental State Examination (3MS) (Teng & Chui, 1987). A licensed neuropsychologist trained staff to administer the 3MS and observed random interviews to ensure adherence to the protocol. We defined cognitive impairment as a score below the 7th percentile (i.e., 1.5 standard deviations below the reference cohort mean) or inability to complete the assessment (Teng & Chui, 1987).

Statistical Analysis

We included participants who had at least one follow-up visit or data on housing status at any time after the baseline interview. Our analytic sample includes 411 participants, or 91.3% of the study sample (Supplemental Figure S1). We completed a descriptive analysis to characterize the study sample overall and by housing status. We used the Kaplan-Meier method to describe the cumulative incidence of attaining housing and incidence of loss of housing after attainment. To assess potential bias due to differential loss to follow-up, we used Pearson x2 to compare distributions of selected demographic and behavioral variables between participants with follow-up and those with only baseline visits.

To identify predictors of attaining housing (i.e., exiting homelessness) and subsequent returns to homelessness, we used Cox regression models with time-dependent and fixed covariates. We defined time to attain housing as time from study enrollment to first observed episode of housing. Since participants were homeless at baseline, we left-truncated data. For this analysis, participants entered at baseline and remained until the first episode of housing. We censored participants at the last follow-up date or last known housing from secondary sources (i.e., data from tracking), whichever was later.

To analyze returns to homelessness amongst those who had regained housing, we defined time to return to homelessness as time from attaining housing to date of return to homelessness. For each analysis, we restructured the data to follow the intended 6-month follow-up interval and carried forward the previous value for any participants with missing housing and adjustment variables. We lagged the variable for case manager by one study visit. We performed all analyses in Stata 16 (StataCorp, 2019).

We assessed univariable associations between housing attainment and a priori independent variables. We included variables significant in univariable analyses (p < .10) in multivariable models. We explored age, duration of homelessness as an adult at baseline, race/ethnicity, and gender as potential confounders. We constructed multivariable models using an iterative backward selection procedure in which all variables that met the criteria above were included in a model and removed one at a time. We verified that hazards were proportional for time-dependent covariates by testing the interaction of visit time with each covariate and using episode splitting techniques. In the final model, we included variables that had a p value <.05. We adjusted all models for age, duration of adult homelessness at baseline, and calendar year of visit. We analyzed Schoenfeld residuals to assess the proportionality assumption and evaluated goodness of fit using the likelihood ratio test.

To account for potential biases due to differential follow-up for subjects recruited in Wave 1 vs. Wave 2, we performed a sensitivity analysis including only the first 36 months of interview data for each individual.

Results

Sample Description

Of 450 enrolled participants, we included 411 (91.3%) who had at least one follow-up interview (n = 392) or follow-up data on housing (n = 19) (Supplemental Figure S1). Included participants were more likely to be female, have good/very good/excellent health, and to be recruited in Wave 1 (Supplemental Table S1). We found no differences in inclusion based on age, race/ethnicity, education, employment or history of incarceration.

At baseline, participants had a median age of 58 (Interquartile Range (IQR): 54–61) and had spent a median of 3 years (IQR: 1–9) homeless as an adult. Many (68.2%) met temporal criteria for chronic homelessness. Most participants were male (74.5%) and Black (81.0%) (Table 1).

Housing

Participants were followed for a median of 4.0 years (IQR: 2.0–7.5 years; range: 0.5–8.0 years) for a total of 1920 years of observation time. Most participants (n = 327; 79.6%) exited homelessness (attained housing or received institutional placement) during follow-up. The median time from study entry to housing attainment was 1.5 years; the incidence was 39.3 (95% confidence interval (CI): 35.3–43.8) per 100 person-years (Supplemental Figure S2). Most who exited homelessness did so into apartments or rooms they rented or owned (n = 121; 37.0%) or with family or friends (n = 105; 32.1%); 11.6% entered transitional housing (Figure 1). Some participants (n = 31, 7.5%) exited homelessness into institutions, namely drug and alcohol detox programs (n = 12, 2.9%), board and care facilities (n = 8, 1.9%), and nursing homes (n = 7, 1.7%). Some participants (n = 68, 16.5%) received a permanent rental subsidy (including those living in public housing) and a few (n = 20, 4.9%) lived in PSH.

Among those who attained housing/an institutional placement, 52.6% (n = 172) subsequently experienced homelessness. The median time to returning to homelessness after exiting homelessness was 1.5 years with an incidence of 24.5 (95% CI: 21.1–28.5) per 100 person-years (Supplemental Figure S3). Rates of housing loss differed by type of housing. Participants who lived in PSH and those who lived in their own apartment or room had the lowest incidence rates of housing loss, 0.12 and 0.17, respectively (Supplemental Figure S4).

Factors Associated With Exiting Homelessness

The factors positively associated with exiting homelessness (attaining housing/an institutional placement) were having a case manager in the prior 6 months (Adjusted Hazard Ratio (AHR) = 2.04; 95% CI: 1.68–2.49) and having an ADL impairment (AHR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.04–1.50) (Table 2). Factors associated with a decreased likelihood of exiting homelessness were, in the prior 6 months, experiencing incarceration (AHR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.40–0.95), sexual or physical abuse (AHR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.52–0.92), or experiencing 31–150 nights or >150 nights of unsheltered homelessness (AHR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.94 and AHR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.48–0.83, respectively) compared to those who spent ≤30 nights unsheltered.

Factors Associated With Returns to Homelessness, Among Those Who Had Gained Housing/an Institutional Placement

Among those who exited homelessness (attained housing/an institutional placement), factors negatively associated with returns to homelessness (i.e., factors protective for retaining housing) were having a rental subsidy (AHR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.29–0.64) or living in PSH (AHR = 0.34; 95% CI 0.18–0.64; Table 3) (reference, no subsidy). Factors associated with increased returns to homelessness were moderate-to-severe amphetamine use (AHR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.04–2.68) and incarceration (AHR = 2.02; 95% CI: 1.18–3.47) in the prior 6 months.

In sensitivity analyses examining factors associated with exiting homelessness and returns to homelessness restricted to the first 36 months of follow-up, point estimates remained similar, with widened confidence intervals (Supplemental Tables S2 and S3). Tests for non-proportionality were not significant.

Discussion

In this study of older adults who were homeless in Oakland, CA at study enrollment and followed for a median of 4 years, 80% regained housing during study follow-up. This occurred despite the participants living in a high-cost housing area, with many meeting temporal criteria for chronic homelessness at study entry. This is consistent with other literature that found between 40 and 80% of people experiencing homelessness find housing within 2 years (Brown et al., 2015; Caton et al., 2005). These prior studies recruited people from homeless shelters (unlike ours). One (Caton et al., 2005) recruited participants early in an episode of homelessness, which doesn’t have the limitation of over-representing those with chronic homelessness. Despite their age and a high level of disability, most participants “self-resolved” their homelessness without the support of permanent rental subsidies or PSH, by moving into unsubsidized private market rental housing or in with family or friends. This is consistent with both researcher consensus and prior literature (Caton et al., 2005). Among our participants who regained housing, half returned to homelessness in a median of 1.5 years. Approximately half of those who regained rental housing received a permanent rental subsidy. Having a rental subsidy, whether through a Housing Choice Voucher, living in public housing, or living in PSH, protected them against returning to homelessness. This supports prior work showing that Housing First approaches and vouchers keep formerly homeless people housed (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2023; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2018; Gubits et al., 2016). These findings highlight the dynamic nature of homelessness, with exits to housing/institutions and returns to homelessness, and the precarity of housing returns for those who do so without a permanent subsidy.

Having a case manager was associated with regaining housing or an institutional placement. This aligns with prior work that found that case management is associated with better homelessness outcomes, such as less time homeless and greater housing stability (Weightman et al., 2023). Case managers play multiple roles in helping people access housing. People experiencing homelessness face barriers to navigating resources, including limited access to technology, limited literacy and knowledge of programs, limited transportation, and lack of necessary documentation, such as identification cards (Gillis & Singer, 1997). Many do not receive benefits for which they qualify. Homeless individuals may face barriers to obtaining housing, including prior evictions, poor credit histories, criminal records, lack of rental histories and employment, stigma against those experiencing homelessness, and discrimination based on race, experience of homelessness, or source of income. Case managers may help overcome these barriers for both housing and institutional placements by helping individuals identify opportunities and address barriers (National Health Care for the Homeless Council [NHCHC], 2016). While this was associated with participants regaining housing, this support did not prevent them from losing the housing and returning to homelessness.

Having a functional impairment was associated with regaining housing or an institutional placement, which is a novel finding. People with functional impairments may have received preference in the Coordinated Entry process, which local continuums of care use to determine prioritization for federally funded homelessness services. Coordinated Entry, required by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), assesses, prioritizes, and matches people experiencing homelessness to services, including housing (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2017). Alternatively, family members may have been more likely to offer housing to family members with functional impairment.

Participants experiencing longer durations of unsheltered homelessness were less likely to exit homelessness. Prior studies that examined housing outcomes often recruited from shelters. People experiencing unsheltered homelessness may have more difficulty accessing the technology and documents needed to regain housing than their sheltered counterparts. The difficult conditions of unsheltered homelessness, including inclement weather, thefts, and “sweeps,” (forced displacements of people experiencing unsheltered homelessness by police or local government agencies) can result in loss of documents and other disruptions, impeding exits to housing (Darrah-Okike et al., 2018). People experiencing unsheltered homelessness are more disconnected from services that provide support, such as case management or housing navigation; this is worsened by forced displacements (Kushel et al., 2023; Richards & Kuhn, 2023). Without access to hygiene facilities, people experiencing unsheltered homelessness may face more stigma during the rental process.

Recent experiences of victimization and incarceration were associated with decreased likelihood of exiting homelessness. This expands prior work that finds relationship problems are associated with a lower likelihood of regaining housing (Nilsson et al., 2019). Additionally, other research has found that prior incarceration is related to difficulty obtaining housing because of housing discrimination, prohibitive housing policies (i.e., individuals with certain types of felonies cannot live in public housing), and decreased income (Couloute & Kopf, 2018; Clark, 2007; U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, n.d.-a). Victimization may negatively impact the self-efficacy needed to find housing, isolate people from members of their social networks who could assist with housing and damage the victims’ financial stability (Sharp-Jeffs, 2015). People experiencing homelessness have a high risk of incarceration due to the criminalization of behaviors associated with homelessness (i.e., laws prohibiting sitting, lying, sleeping, and camping in public), the visibility of behaviors that may not be criminalized when done in private (such as drug use), and the inability of individuals experiencing homelessness to pay fines or post bail. Although we do not have data on the cause of incarceration in this study, older adults are less likely to commit violent crime (Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2021). Incarceration can interrupt rehousing by decreasing options for rental housing via discrimination against people with criminal records, decreasing access to family or friends, and presenting legal barriers to staying with others due to criminal records (National Housing Law Project, n.d.).

While some participants who regained housing received housing vouchers or moved into PSH, half of those who exited homelessness into housing or institutions reentered homelessness within a median of 1.5 years. Receiving a permanent rental subsidy (via a voucher or PSH) was associated with a lower likelihood of returning to homelessness. Our findings highlight the precarity of housing for older adults exiting homelessness and the need for permanent rental subsidies. In homeless families, permanent vouchers are associated with greater housing stability compared to no or time-limited rental support (Gubits et al., 2016). Older adults are less likely than younger adults to be able to increase their income through retraining or working additional hours, strategies that underlie the logic of time-limited rental subsidies (Heidkamp & Van Horn, 2008; Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University, 2023). This supports the need for more permanent rental subsidies for older adults. For those without permanent subsidies, there is a need for secondary prevention measures, including flexible funding to make up rental shortages and legal support to try to prevent returns to homelessness.

Problematic amphetamine use and recent incarceration were associated with an increased likelihood of returning to homelessness once housed or in an institutional placement. A recent arrest is a known risk factor for returning to homelessness (Van Straaten et al., 2017). Prior work on the role of substance use in housing retention is mixed. Studies of Housing First find that substance use is not associated with housing retention (Collins et al., 2013; Palepu et al., 2013). This is expected because Housing First programs are designed to support those with substance use and mental health disorders. Substance use may disrupt housing security in other types of housing through negative impacts on social relationships and employment; it is associated with remaining homeless (Aubry et al., 2012). However, our finding that amphetamine use, but not other types of substance use, was a risk factor for returns to homelessness among older adults rehoused in various types of housing and institutions is novel. Both incarceration and amphetamine use may result in missed rent payments, loss of work, or eviction (North et al., 2010).

Ten percent of participants exited homelessness into institutions. This is a novel finding since most studies have limited data on institutional stays. The most common institutional stay was drug or alcohol treatment facilities; these are time-limited and may not lead to permanent housing, particularly for those who relapse (Aubry et al., 2012). The second most common institution was skilled nursing facilities. In 2020 in California, 88% of nursing home patients were discharged within 3 months, likely corresponding to when their Medicare coverage lapsed (California Association of Health Facilities [CAHF], 2020; Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2022). While these institutional stays count as exits from homelessness (including in HMIS), our study finds they may not be durable.

Our paper has several limitations. We lack complete information on all participants, which may have biased the results. If those with missing data were more likely to remain homeless, we may have overestimated returns to housing. However, we used various sources to decrease data missingness. Participants may have underreported if their housing was PSH or whether they received a non-PSH permanent housing voucher because they may be unaware of the types of subsidies they receive (O’Brien, 2005). We used several strategies to reduce misclassification. However, misclassification would bias our findings to the null. Additionally, the association between case managers and exiting homelessness may represent effect–cause. Participants prioritized for housing assistance through Coordinated Entry may have been assigned a case manager.

By not recruiting people at the beginning of their first episode of homelessness, we oversampled those with longer homelessness episodes and more barriers to homelessness exits. This will lead to a lower proportion regaining housing, longer homelessness episodes, and more returns to homelessness after housing, leading us to underestimate the proportion who regained housing and overestimate returns to homelessness compared to all those who experience an episode of homelessness. Since we recruited people experiencing homelessness at baseline and calculated time to housing from study onset, their true duration of time spent homeless before regaining housing is longer than we noted.

Due to sample size limitations, we could not explore differences in housing outcomes between those with project-based versus tenant-based vouchers, nor those with subsidies alone versus those living in PSH, which combines subsidies with supportive services. Direct comparisons would be limited by confounding by indication. Coordinated entry systems, which prioritize people experiencing homelessness for housing interventions, prioritize those with significant disabilities (including substance use and mental health conditions) for PSH. These conditions are risk factors for exiting housing. While we can account for some of these differences based on measurable characteristics, we would not be able to account for all.

The rates of homelessness in a community are related to the disconnect between housing costs and incomes for low-income households. Individuals who live in extremely low-income households (i.e., those making less than 30% of the area median income) are at the highest risk of becoming homeless (National Alliance to End Homelessness, 2022). In California, the median household income in the 6 months prior to becoming homeless was $960 a month, far below the threshold to be extremely low-income (Kushel et al., 2023). We conducted our study in Oakland, CA between 2013 and 2022. In 2013, the median monthly rent for a studio apartment in Oakland was $1150; by 2021, it was $1800, an increase of 56% (Bursell & Fabish, 2022). In Alameda County, where Oakland is located, an individual was considered extremely low-income if they made less than $2725 a month ($32,700 a year) in 2024 (Kirkeby, 2024). Our study findings are most relevant to other high-cost areas that experienced large rental increases.

Policy Recommendations

Policymakers interested in ending older adult homelessness should extend permanent rental subsidies and increase access to a range of affordable housing options; expand homelessness prevention efforts, including those targeted to older adults who have recently regained housing without the support of permanent subsidies; and provide services, including case management and flexible funding, to speed homelessness exits. In addition to funding and support from HUD, other funding sources, notably Medicaid, could play a role in funding these services. To improve the ability to track the results of policy changes, policymakers should improve their ability to track outcomes using administrative data, including improving the collection of HMIS data and merging it with other data systems, along with strong privacy protections.

Not having a long-term subsidy increased the risk of returning to homelessness. Recognizing that older adults are unlikely to be able to increase their earning potential through employment, policymakers should invest in increasing permanent rental subsidies for this population. There is a need for an expansion of affordable housing options that meet the needs of older adults, including expanded affordable senior housing, shared housing, and PSH with appropriate services and supports.

The high rate of returns to homelessness among those exiting homelessness to living with friends or family, living in rental housing with a subsidy, or living in institutional settings presents an opportunity for secondary prevention. The key to homelessness prevention programs’ success is targeting those at the highest risk of homelessness. Considering their high risk of returning to homelessness, older adults who have self-resolved homelessness without a subsidy present an opportunity for homelessness prevention efforts. Effective interventions include flexible funding, case management, legal services, and housing navigation. In California, housing sustainment services through the Medicaid 1115 waiver can pay for some of these interventions.

Speeding homelessness exits among those with few barriers to exit could decrease lengths of homelessness and associated suffering. Most older adults who experienced homelessness and regained housing “self-resolved” their homelessness, moving into market-rate housing or with friends or family without the use of subsidies. We found that having a case manager was associated with regaining housing. Housing navigation, housing deposits, and problem-solving to help older adults find housing that they can afford could speed resolution. In California, the Medicaid 1115 waiver could cover many of these expenses.

The lack of longitudinal studies of homelessness outcomes highlights the need for such research. Strengthening administrative data, including HMIS data collected by Continuums of Care, could fill this role. Strengthening these data, particularly in areas with a high proportion of people experiencing unsheltered homelessness, would allow local jurisdictions to better understand effective housing solutions to homelessness, know the prevalence of reentrances into homelessness, and target interventions to prevent future episodes of homelessness. HUD suggests creating and following a high-quality data management plan, including requiring complete information about the individual and their housing outcome, requiring timely reporting of individuals’ outcomes, and requiring a consistent data structure (e.g., formatting, amount of information) (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, n.d.-b). Agencies responsible for HMIS data collection and management could urge policymakers to allow HMIS to merge other data sources, such as health sector data (e.g., Medicaid, hospital discharge data) and social service data (e.g., program enrollment) with appropriate privacy protections to improve data on housing outcomes and returns to homelessness (Colburn et al., 2025).

Conclusion

Homelessness among older adults is dynamic, with frequent exits to housing/institutions and subsequent returns to homelessness. Our findings from a novel longitudinal study of older adults who were homeless at enrollment with high follow-up over 9 years highlight the need for expansion of rental subsidies and the use of targeted secondary prevention to prevent returns to homelessness among those recently housed. Only one in three adults aged 65 and older who meet the criteria to receive Housing Choice Vouchers, the most common form of permanent rental subsidies, receive them (U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development, 2017; Center on Budget & Policy Priorities, n.d.). The supply of PSH is severely limited, with few eligible individuals receiving it (Acosta & Gartland, 2021). While case management was associated with returns to housing, many did not receive these services. Expansion of these strategies could promote homelessness exits and lessen returns to homelessness.
