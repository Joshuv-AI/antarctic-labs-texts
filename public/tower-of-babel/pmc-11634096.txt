Dear Editor,

Iatrogenic damage to the thoracic duct is a well-recognized complication of thoracic or visceral surgery, particularly esophagectomy1,2. The thoracic duct is vulnerable to iatrogenic injury during surgical resection of esophageal cancer or cervical neck dissection. Although prophylactic strategies have been developed1,2, the risk of injury is between 0.2 and 10.5%, despite surgical strategies to reduce the risk1,2. Damage to the thoracic duct can have dramatic consequences, with mortality rates approaching 30% despite treatment2. The optimal treatment strategy is controversial1. Conservative treatments are often ineffective in the case of high-volume fistulae, where early, aggressive treatment is warranted. However, re-thoracotomy and thoracoscopic procedures can be hazardous in this situation, as they pass through the surgical site of the primary operation or the site of fluid collection and inflammation3.

Posterior retroperitoneoscopic thoracic duct ligation has been proposed in order to avoid the previous operative field and to access the thoracic duct in a standardized manner3. This minimally-invasive technique takes advantage of an access approach developed for adrenal surgery. The patient is placed in a knee-elbow position (Fig. 1A), and the thoracic duct is accessed laterally from the right side. A first 1.5 cm incision is made at the tip of the 12th right rib. Blunt finger dissection is used to reach the retroperitoneal space and locate the tip of the 11th rib. Under finger guidance, a 5 mm trocar is placed at the tip of the 11th rib. An inflatable balloon blunt tip 12 mm trocar is placed into the initial incision. Capnoretroperitoneum is established and maintained with a pressure of 20–25 mmHg. The second 5 mm trocar is placed under video guidance 5 cm caudally to the lateral port. With laparoscopic forceps, Gerota’s fascia is opened, the suprarenal region is exposed and the right diaphragmatic crus is located. The para-aortic diaphragmatic muscle is split using a vessel sealing device. The thoracic duct is then visualized transdiaphragmatically between the aorta and 11th–12th vertebrae (Fig. 1B). The visualization can be simplified by the preoperative oral administration of milk cream. This fills the thoracic duct with a white-colored, fatty secretion. The thoracic duct and collateral lymphatic vessels are subsequently ligated using adsorbable clips until chylous leakage is stopped (Supplemental material, Video, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D504).

The knee-elbow position and the surgical field from endoscopic visualization. (A) Knee-elbow position and trocar placement. (B) Surgical field after opening of diaphragmatic fibers and dissection of the thoracic duct.

Existing operative approaches for thoracic duct repair/ligation include re-thoracotomy, the abdominal approach, and thoracoscopy2. Compared with the aforementioned approaches, retroperitoneoscopic thoracic duct ligation avoids the previous operative field, thereby avoiding inflammation and preventing the need for difficult dissection and localization of the thoracic duct. After esophagectomy, gastric conduit dissection and manipulation reduce the risk of damaging the esophagogastric anastomosis and the vascularization of the gastric sleeve3, while retroperitoneoscopy avoids contact with the stomach by using a posterior approach at a distance from the anastomosis and the gastric sleeve. While ventilation of both lungs is possible with this new technique, thoracotomy or thoracoscopy entails single-lung anesthesia in already compromised patients1. Retroperitoneoscopy avoids the need for cervical re-exploration following chylothorax from thyroid surgery, reducing the risk of recurrent laryngeal or vagal nerve injury. In addition, this technique enables precise identification of the thoracic duct at an anatomical site with the least inter-patient variation and enables its ligation without the need for additional measures (e.g. tissue adhesive glue). Finally, minimally-invasive access obviates the need for open surgery with its inherent morbidity and mortality risks.

The presented technique will affect clinical care. Chylothorax, with its very high morbidity and mortality, requires early, effective, and ideally minimally-invasive treatment. Compared with conventional techniques, posterior retroperitoneoscopic thoracic duct ligation offers the advantages of (i) access avoiding the initial operative field, (ii) avoiding manipulation of the gastric sleeve, and (iii) preventing the need for single-lung ventilation. In addition, easy identification of the thoracic duct may guarantee a higher success rate with this technique.

In a 2022 published systematic review of treatments for chylothorax2, the present posterior retroperitonescopic technique was not mentioned. To our knowledge, there is only one publication presenting a series of four patients treated with this technique3, with therapeutic success (resolution of chylothorax) in three cases (75.0%) and significant improvement of the chylous fistula in the last case. The three patients treated with this technique in our department had a therapeutic success rate of 100%, with no significant complications following the procedure. The posterior retroperitoneoscopic approach is mainly used for adrenalectomy and is superior to the laparoscopic technique, in terms of morbidity, according to a Cochrane database meta-analysis4. In addition, four recent meta-analyses have demonstrated the superiority of the retroperitoneoscopic technique (in comparison to the laparoscopic or open approach) in terms of operating time, blood loss, hospital stay, and postoperative pain5–7.

There are barriers to implementing this innovation more broadly. Firstly, the scarcity of studies and clinical evidence supporting this technique is the major limitation to its broad implementation. Secondly, positioning the patient in the knee-elbow position and employing posterior access required additional training due to the modified anatomical landmarks. Thirdly, dissection space in the retroperitoneal adrenal compartment is limited. This may lead to difficulty in handling the instruments and, therefore, a higher degree of complexity. Mutual interference between laparoscopic instruments may cause a ‘chopsticks’ effect, making the dissection even more difficult and prolonging the operation time.

Although retroperitoneoscopic access is used routinely for adrenal or renal surgery, there is a need to gather clinical evidence for the application of this technique in the treatment of iatrogenic chylothorax. Setting up a large-scale clinical trial may be hampered by the low incidence of this complication. The collaboration of several centers is necessary to generate adequate clinical data to support the implementation of the procedure in routine clinical practice.
