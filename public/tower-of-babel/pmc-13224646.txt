Does medical students’ perception of team-based learning have an impact on achieving planned learning outcomes?
Authors: Kauser Jabeen, Javeria Rehman, Syeda Sadia Fatima, Imran Ahmed, Kulsoom Ghias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudents’ perceptions of pedagogies can be a barrier for active learning. We explored whether students’ opinion of team-based learning (TBL) impacted knowledge acquisition.MethodologyA mixed method study was conducted during the Infectious diseases and Immunity module for undergraduate Year 2 medical students. Student evaluation after each TBL session was collected using eight parameters on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores of the end-of-session test were compared with individual readiness assurance test (iRAT) scores. Statistical analysis was done to compare and correlate knowledge scores and students’ ratings. Focused group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with students for in-depth feedback on their perceived value and effectiveness of TBL.ResultsA total of 96 students participated in the study. End-of-session test scores were significantly higher than iRAT scores (p-value < 0.0001). There was no difference in end-of session test scores (p-value < 0.664) between students who gave an overall rating of 1/2 versus 4/5. Pearson correlation showed no relationship between student rating and iRAT or end-of-session test scores. A higher proportion of students agreed that TBL enhanced critical thinking, engagement, accountability, and conceptual understanding, but only half of the students wanted TBL as a strategy in other modules. FGD with fifteen students predominantly identified that extensive pre-reading material led to learner dissatisfaction and disengagement. Though they affirmed that the quizzes and application exercises were helpful in clarification of difficult concepts.ConclusionTBLs improved students’ knowledge acquisition independent of their rating of TBL sessions. Lengthy pre-reading material, longer duration, and lack of equal participation were attributed to a lower rating. Redesigning TBL sessions according to students’ feedback may improve students’ perception of TBL in the future. This study contributes to the literature on the effectiveness and acceptability of TBL by assessing academic performance and feedback and exploring student perception.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09185-9.Keywords: TBL, Student perception, Improvement of knowledge, Pakistan

Background

Team-Based Learning (TBL) is a widely used pedagogy which is recognized to be effective in health professions education [1]. It is an innovative active learning approach that can be used in large classes with a single content expert, by dividing students into small teams [2]. This method allows students to apply their knowledge to clinical problems while fostering teamwork, engagement, and deep learning, with immediate feedback [1, 2]. TBL sessions are learner-centered and hold students accountable for their own learning [2]. TBL has been adopted by healthcare education institutions worldwide, with a recent systematic review reporting positive impacts on student and faculty experiences as well as academic performance [1, 3]. Research indicates that student engagement is positively associated with learning outcomes, most significantly in clinical practice [4]. Studies report improved student engagement, learning, and team performance with TBL sessions [1]. However, mixed reactions and negative perceptions of learners especially with initial iterations of TBL are also reported [1, 5–8]. Negative perceptions of TBL include more workload, peer assessment anxiety, unfamiliarity with the TBL process and dissatisfaction with length of reading materials [1, 5–8].

TBL at the Aga Khan University (AKU) in Karachi, Pakistan was introduced in 2018. AKU is a private medical college with a five-year undergraduate medical education program that awards an MBBS degree, with basic sciences taught in the first two years and clinical clerkships in Years 3–5. It inducts 100 students each year. The basic sciences in Year 1–2 are taught in integrated system-based modules using common clinical presentations. Problem based learning (PBL) is the predominant teaching learning modality in the first two years, although other pedagogies such as flipped classroom and Pecha Kucha are also used to facilitate learning. Over time, students’ feedback revealed that while most students agreed that TBL is an effective way to develop critical thinking and analytical skills, a significant proportion of students did not want TBL to be added to other modules. This raised concerns amongst AKU faculty about whether students’ dislike of TBL was hindering the achievement of TBL outcomes, particularly knowledge acquisition. A previous study reporting faculty perception found that faculty commonly view students’ negative perceptions and behaviors as a major barrier to active learning [9]. Although literature reveals that the learning benefits of TBL are observed irrespective of students’ affective responses [6, 10], assessment of achievement of learning outcomes remains essential, and students’ perceptions of TBL are likewise vital because they shape learning experiences. Therefore, understanding students’ perceptions is crucial for curriculum developers and faculty to tailor TBL activities effectively.

The primary aim of this study was to assess students’ academic performance in TBL with respect to their knowledge acquisition and determine if perception affected knowledge acquisition. Additionally, the study sought to gather detailed feedback from students on their perceived value and effectiveness of TBL. By assessing academic performance and feedback, and exploring student perception of TBL design and implementation, this study significantly contributes to the literature on the effectiveness and acceptability of team-based learning in medical education, particularly in the South Asian context [11]. In the South Asian and particularly Pakistani context, several factors may shape how students experience and evaluate TBL: Students are inducted at a younger age (~ 17–18 years) into an undergraduate medical education program which may impact self-regulation skills and feedback literacy. Students are primarily exam-centric with a memorization-heavy schooling that can dampen initial comfort with application-focused tasks. English-medium instruction with varied language background can raise the cognitive load of pre-class readings and readiness assurance test (RATs) and collectivist, higher power-distance norms that may foster harmony-seeking and leniency in peer rating and team talk. We considered these contextual features when interpreting students’ perceptions and the observed effects of TBL in our study.

Methods

Setting

This mixed method study was conducted at the AKU Medical College in, Karachi, Pakistan between Jan-Dec 2022. TBLs were introduced in 2018 at the institution in Year 2 of the undergraduate program in the Immunity and Infectious Diseases module. This is a six-week module in which PBL or TBL sessions are conducted twice a week. Four TBL sessions were conducted across six weeks with each TBL session having two sub-sessions. All the 100 students were divided into 10 teams with 9–10 members in each team.

TBL sessions were designed using TBL AMEE guide No.65 [2] and included all phases of a TBL session in a sequence such as orientation to the TBL process, advanced reading assignments, individual readiness assurance tests (iRAT) at the start of the session, team readiness assurance tests (tRAT) using similar items, clarifications, appeals and mini-lecture and application exercises focused on significant, same-problem, specific-choice tasks with simultaneous reporting (“4S”). As compared to traditional TBL that includes a single synchronous session, each TBL session was divided in two sub-sessions; first sub-session had iRAT and tRAT, clarifications, appeals and mini-lecture, second sub-session had administration of application exercises with discussed and peer feedback followed by end-of-session test.

The iRAT comprised of 10 single-best-answer MCQs and was completed by all students, tRAT had 10 single-best-answer MCQs, completed by 10 teams of 9–10 students. The end-of-session test also comprised of 10 single-best-answer MCQs and was completed by all students. All tests were developed by the teaching team (two content experts) using a blueprint aligned to the session’s intended learning outcomes and cognitive levels. Items were drafted to MCQ best‑practice standards, independently peer‑reviewed for content accuracy and clarity, and key‑validated prior to administration. After administration, a focused post-hoc quality check was conducted and items that had high difficulty were reviewed to verify the marked answer and to identify any items that could reasonably have two correct responses. Such items were corrected or were excluded from scoring.

This study used a whole-cohort design as all Year 2 MBBS students completing Immunity & Infectious Diseases module in year 2022 (n = 100) were included. Students for which elements of quantitative data such as iRAT, tRAT, end-of-session tests scores were missing were excluded. Given the fixed class size and census approach typical of educational evaluations, no a priori sample-size calculation was undertaken for the quantitative component. For the qualitative component (FGDs), we applied purposive sampling and invited students across four a-priori strata (high/low TBL rating × high/low end-of-session test score).

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion (quantitative): all Year 2 students completing the module, who attended all TBL sessions and completed iRAT/tRAT/end-of-session tests.

Exclusion (quantitative): students who had incomplete or missing records for any of the primary outcomes.

Inclusion (qualitative/FGD): students who completed all four TBLs, consented to participate, and met one of four pre-specified performance–perception cells: (1) TBL rating 4–5 with ≥ 80% score; (2) TBL rating 1–2 with ≥ 80% score; (3) TBL rating 4–5 with ≤ 30% score; (4) TBL rating 1–2 with 50%-60% score. Exclusion (qualitative): non-consent, inability to schedule, or incomplete participation.

Data collection

Quantitative data

All students were administered an end-of-session test after each TBL session. These tests were administered online using Microsoft Forms. These tests comprised of ten questions in a best choice format. Each test had 10 questions and students were awarded 1 mark for each correct response. Additionally, students rated their peers and faculty facilitators. Students provided an overall rating of TBL sessions on a 5-point Likert scale where 1 = poor to 5 = outstanding.

Student feedback on eight parameters of TBL was taken using on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.

Qualitative data

Students were invited, through purposive sampling, for a focused group discussion (FGD) after the completion of all four TBL sessions and the summative examination of the IID module. These students were selected across four groups: Group 1 comprised of students who rated the TBL session 4 or 5 (excellent or outstanding) on the 5-point Likert scale and scored 80% or above in the end-of-session tests; Group 2 comprised of students who rated the TBL session 1 or 2 (poor or fair) on the 5-point Likert scale and scored 80% or above in the end-of-session tests; Group 3 comprised of students who rated the TBL session 4 or 5 on the 5-point Likert scale and scored 30% or below in the end-of-session tests; Group 4 comprised of students who rated the TBL session 1 or 2 on the 5-point Likert scale and scored 30% or below in the end-of-session tests. The FGDs were conducted after getting informed consent from the students. The summative exams were conducted and grades were submitted before focus group discussions.

Focus groups were facilitated by two research team members. A semi-structured guide aligned to the study aims and session learning outcomes was drafted. Questions were addressing the following domains: (i) pre-reading material and readiness assurance tests (ii) facilitation and support by faculty during the TBL session (iii) team development and TBL process (iv) TBL as a new teaching modality and its comparison with PBL (v) effectiveness and challenges with TBL. The draft guide underwent content/face-validity review by 2–3 experts in medical education and qualitative methodology; wording was refined for clarity and neutrality. A semi-structured format with a standardized opening script, ground rules (confidentiality, “one voice at a time”), and a closing member-verification prompt (participants confirmed or corrected key points). Each session lasted 60–75 min with 6–8 participants selected via purposive sampling to capture diversity of experience. Sessions were held in-person, audio-recorded, and professionally transcribed verbatim. Field notes documented non-verbal cues and contextual factors; a simple audit trail captured procedural deviations. We monitored for thematic sufficiency and ceased data collection when no substantively new codes emerged.

Data analysis

For statistical analysis, quantitative data was entered and analyzed using Microsoft Excel and SPSS version 21 (IBM statistics). Scores of iRAT, tRAT and end-of-session test were compared using student independent t-test. Data of student’s ratings and feedback was analyzed as combined to be reported as strongly disagree/agree, neutral and strongly agree/agree. Data was presented as mean and standard deviations and/or absolute numbers and frequencies wherever applicable. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between the scores and the students’ rating. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the qualitative data, the content was analyzed for thematic analysis to create codes and themes. The transcribed data was independently studied by two researchers, identifying text and phrases to generate preliminary codes. The codes were then finalized after reaching consensus on the coding structures to ensure correct interpretation among researchers. The codes were then grouped as themes that were further refined during the writing phase.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Aga Khan University, Karachi, Pakistan (ERC number: 2019-2048-5652; extension numbers:2020-2048-14615; 2021-2048-19704; 2022-2048-23162). Although attending TBL sessions was mandatory for all students but providing feedback was voluntary. Additionally, participation in FGD was voluntary and was conducted for students who consented to participate. Participation in the study had no impact on the grading of students in the course and module faculty were unaware of the study participants. The summative exams were conducted, and grades were submitted before focus group discussions. All study data was deidentified and was saved in a password protected file and was accessible only to the study group.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

The work was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, including, but not limited to the anonymity of participants being guaranteed and the informed consent of participants being obtained.

The tRAT scores and end-of-session test scores were significantly higher than the iRAT scores (p-value < 0.0001). There was no significant difference in the iRAT and end-of session test scores between male and female students. The overall rating of the TBL sessions was 3.39. There was no difference in the iRAT (p-value 0.478) or end-of session test scores (p-value < 0.664) between students who gave an overall rating of 1 or 2 versus 4 or 5 to the sessions. There was no significant gender-based difference in the TBL rating. Pearson correlation showed no relationship between student rating and iRAT (0.05) or end-of-session test scores (-0.01).

Students’ feedback

Feedback on TBL sessions was provided by 64 students. Analysis of feedback data, as shown in Table 2; Fig. 1, revealed that a higher proportion of students agreed that TBL enhanced critical and analytical thinking, engagement and participation, accountability for learning, and in-depth conceptual understanding of basic sciences. They also agreed that the readiness assurance tests increased consolidation of concepts before application exercises, and it was easy for them to assign scores to their peers. Around 60% of the students found TBLs to be more effective for understanding basic and clinical concepts compared to PBLs. However, only half of the class wanted TBLs to be added as a teaching learning strategy in other modules.

Students’ perceptions of TBL

A total of 15 students participated in the FGDs. Thematic analysis resulted in 6 themes that are grouped into three domains as described below. The verbatim quotes are also provided with “S” followed by participant number indicating representative student for the respective theme.

Learning process in TBL

Pre-reading material and learning resources

The students believed that TBL pre-readings material should be short, concise, and relevant. The TBL sessions, having lengthy and extensive pre-reading materials, were not appreciated by the students and led to learner dissatisfaction as most students opted not to complete the pre-reading tasks and felt disengaged.

“The pre-reading material was very vague and lengthy and was based on research articles and was somehow not consolidated.” (S4)

“I read all the pre-readings for every TBL, and I really struggled with retention a lot …… so I think people who perhaps don’t like readings and like videos or other modalities for learning are inherently disadvantaged when it comes to TBL.” (S7)

Moreover, students also believed that they should be provided with the opportunity to consult different resources for pre-readings or preparatory material for TBL and felt that the pre-readings and quizzes were mismatched.

PBL versus TBL

The students preferred PBL as a learning modality as they believed the process ensures the participation of all group members in the small group, with much clearer understanding and more focused learning objectives, which they did not appreciate in TBL as a large group discussion.

“The reason I really like PBLs is because we were able to go into a lot of detail…so everyone was able to understand it and we were able to go on a pace that was suitable to the group because we were able to create the group dynamics……TBL was a good way of approaching a topic as a whole, however from the basic sciences perspective, it went too fast and a bit too accelerated.” (S1).

‘In PBL all students add their points on the topic and if anyone misses out, other group members add that point who have prepared from many different resources ……this integrated discussion does not happen in TBL…it’s only a specific question-based discussion.” (S4).

The students believed that the group members in PBL serve as saviors as different opinions and holistic discussions in PBL ensure achievement of learning objectives even if the student attends it unprepared.

Assessment process In TBL

Individual and team readiness assurance tests

Students responded that these assessments were basically the key to success, and it improved their knowledge and thinking patterns. They also agreed that the performance of students on iRATs was substantially connected to their final grades in the IID module.

However, another student (S1) mentioned that “as every student is not equally prepared for the tRAT and iRAT and during tRAT one person filled the form on behalf of all students”.

“Ideally this should not be the practice, in fact all students should be discussing and deciding on the response.” (S2)

Peer evaluation

Students found peer evaluation as the most challenging aspect of the TBL. The group unanimously quoted that “how can we assess each other? It is a difficult task to review peer honestly as all must sail the same boat”. A suggestion to have private meetings with each member of the group with the facilitator was shared by a few students. Also, one student mentioned that it also depends on the relationship between the members “if one is not happy with the group member; one may give a bad review.” (S8).

Effectiveness of TBL

Benefits

The students believed that TBL focused more on the clinical aspect of teaching whereas PBL covers the basic sciences link to clinical application. Yet, TBL shows a good way of approaching the topic and gives a holistic view of the concept. They also mentioned that TBL requires more self-study and prior knowledge. Thus, TBL provides an avenue where students can consolidate and enhance learning via the given clinical case studies and exercises in addition to clarifying misconceptions as the content expert is available and peers are there to check mistakes.

“At least for me, TBL helped me in differential diagnosis and critical thinking.” (S3)

Furthermore, some students believed TBLs helped them to exchange ideas, thoughts and opinions from others and that learning from others improved their performance.

Challenges and way forward

The most important challenge shared by the group was the lengthy reading material and the time constraints with other academic activities in the module. Secondly the number of TBLs was also considered too many in a 6-week module. The students believed that 1 or 2 TBL sessions should be offered to consolidate all the concepts; one at the midpoint and one towards the end. Maintaining group dynamics with equal and effective contribution from every team member in TBL was also considered challenging by the students. Instead of the extensive reading material, they preferred that visual learning material such as pictorials, facts mentioned, and tabular readings are a good resource that can help them retain information in an easy manner.

One of the students shared: “I think the best way would be to develop a session with combination of the elements of PBL and TBL.”

In summary, FGD predominantly reflected students’ perceptions in highlighting the barriers and resistance to acceptance of TBL despite their academic performance. It was revealed that the extensive pre-reading material led to learner dissatisfaction as most learners felt restricted to prepare from the given pre-reading material only and opted not to complete the pre-reading tasks hence felt disengaged. However, they affirmed that the quizzes and the clinical application exercises were helpful in clarification of difficult concepts.

Discussion

Our study findings show that the students achieved their learning outcome of knowledge acquisition at the end of TBL, as reflected by statistically significant difference in their tRAT and end-of-session test scores as compared to their iRAT scores. The iRAT, tRAT and end-of session test scores were comparable in both genders and did not show any significant difference. These findings are similar to many studies across various health professions education settings, documenting the effectiveness of collaborative learning in TBL for knowledge acquisition [12–15].

Although TBL is effective across settings, features of the context in which this study was conducted likely shaped the results. Students begin five-year undergraduate medical education program relatively young, and schooling pathways emphasize high-stakes, memorization-heavy assessment. These factors can temper initial comfort with application-focused tasks and amplify perceived pre-reading burden, explaining why qualitative data flagged pre-reading length and some preferred PBL’s pacing, yet quantitative data still showed learning gains. English-medium heterogeneity may also have increased the cognitive load of preparatory texts and RAT items for some students, contributing to dissatisfaction. In a collectivist, higher power-distance environment, students may avoid overt critique to preserve relationships, producing leniency and friendship bias and discomfort with peer critique; this is in concordance with FGD that reported difficulty of students in conducting peer evaluation.

Our results show positive feedback from students regarding various aspects of TBL, as indicated by their ratings for agreement on a 5-point Likert scale. Majority of the students agreed that TBL helped their critical thinking, increased their engagement, made them feel accountable for their learning and improved their in-depth analysis and understanding of basic concepts. The majority (78.1%) agreed that TBL RATs were helpful in consolidating the concepts discussed in the group before the application exercises. These findings are well supported by various studies in literature that imply these as benefits of TBL [1, 3, 16]. Despite this agreement, it was found that only 51.6% students agreed that TBLs should be included in other modules with a significant percentage (28%) showing disagreement. This indicates somewhat low acceptability or mixed reaction of students towards the implementation of TBL. The findings are similar to the studies who have also reported mixed reactions of the students towards TBL [1, 5, 17]. Moreover, the ratings of TBL did not correlate with the scores of the students in terms of their TBL test scores, and the difference in the scores between the students rating TBL higher as compared to those rating TBL lower was statistically insignificant. Fatmi et al. described mixed learner reactions towards TBL, possibly due to the increased student workload during pre-class preparation, peer assessment and the concept of accountability [5]. Although, the factors of peer assessment and accountability were rated positively in our study, these and other similar issues were highlighted on qualitative exploration of students’ perceptions. The qualitative data revealed that despite outcomes and feedback for the modality, there were certain factors for which the students did not appreciate the implementation of TBL in further modules. Our study reports that students perceived the provided pre-reading material as lengthy and extensive with misalignment with the tests, hence supporting the possibility of perceived increased workload in pre-class preparation and lack of expected level of compliance. Also, students have different learning style and preferences and may differ in their approach towards the preparation material. Literature supports these findings as associated with flipped-class approaches, such as TBL, where misestimation of the time-investment, quality and quantity of the preparatory material and increased workload have been identified as barriers towards the preparedness of students and effectiveness of TBL [18, 19]. Our findings revealed that students also perceived PBLs as comparatively more effective in terms of content, preparedness and group dynamics. The above mentioned factors can contribute to this preference as unpreparedness for the session adversely affects the teams’ learning and performance [20]. Moreover, the sense of accountability that comes with TBL’s in-class preparedness does not apply in PBL which again contributes to the decreased acceptance of TBL, as reported by the literature [5, 15, 17]. On the contrary, a study by Burgess et al. reported that this decrease sense of accountability has led to decreased satisfaction of students with PBLs thereby favoring TBL [21]. Interestingly, students also suggested a PBL-TBL hybrid approach to address the issues. Careful design, facilitation and implementation of TBL keeping in mind the above mentioned factors and concerns can help improve the effectiveness and hence acceptability of TBLs in students [2, 3].

The TBL literature typically recommends 5–7 students per team [2], yet real‑world delivery sometimes requires larger teams. In larger teams, individual contributions are less visible and decision-making takes longer, which can make effort less observable and intensify the perception that others are coasting, even when overall team performance remains adequate. Using larger teams can also inflate team test performance while obscuring individual gaps: knowledge pooling typically drives the well-known tRAT > iRAT effect, so teams may score higher even when individual preparedness is uneven, potentially overstating team learning relative to what students know on their own. Practically, coordination overhead increases with more members; reaching consensus takes longer, which can limit the number or depth of application tasks completed within a fixed session. These size-related dynamics may have impacted student engagement and accountability in TBL sessions and could be a plausible contributor to the mixed affective responses and the performance profile observed in our study and should be considered a design limitation.

This was a mixed method study which highlighted the students’ perception of TBL in detail and their associated concerns despite positive outcomes and feedback observed in quantitative measures. The utilization of mixed methods allowed for a more extensive comprehension of students’ perceptions in a variety of contexts. By merging both quantitative data and qualitative insights, mixed methods approach provided a more in-depth and nuanced perspectives on students’ experiences, attitudes, and viewpoints. The validity of the study findings was enhanced by employing triangulation of data thereby adding to the credibility and trustworthiness of the results. A comprehensive understanding of students’ perceptions of TBL was obtained by integrating the findings from the test scores, feedback and qualitative insights from focus groups. Moreover, triangulation revealed the discrepancies among the quantitative outcomes and qualitative feedback providing a deeper insight into the effectiveness and impact of TBL on students’ learning and perception. The quantitative data indicated real learning gains across the TBL cycle (end-of-session test > iRAT), while the qualitative data explain why these gains did not translate into higher satisfaction or stronger preferences for wider TBL use. Both data indicated that the TBLs supported learning. Quantitatively, end-of-session test scores exceeded iRAT scores. Qualitatively, students affirmed that quizzes and application exercises clarified difficult concepts and consolidated understanding aligning with the performance improvement observed. At the same time, they highlighted practical issues, notably extensive pre-reading, large teams (9–10) that limited individual participation and visibility of effort, and unease with peer assessment. Together, these findings support that knowledge gains occurred even when affect was mixed. Despite these gains, overall acceptability was modest (only ~ 52% wanted TBL in other modules) and ratings did not correlate with scores. FGDs explain this mismatch students described lengthy pre‑readings, limited and uneven participation in large teams (9–10 members), and discomfort with peer evaluation. On the survey, 76.6% agreed it was “easy to award scores to my peers”. In FGDs, however, students emphasized that peer evaluation was emotionally and relationally difficult (fairness concerns, reluctance to “criticize” teammates). Notably, the survey item “easy to award scores to my peers” likely indexed mechanical ease of rating, whereas FGDs surfaced the social-emotional difficulty of judging peers and managing relationships. This pattern is consistent with peer-assessment research showing that social ties and cultural norms can foster leniency and friendship bias and that anonymity and design influence students’ willingness to provide candid feedback. Accordingly, we plan to strengthen the peer-evaluation process.

These design/process issues plausibly decrease students’ satisfaction without necessarily affecting performance, helping to account for the null association between perception and achievement. The integration of qualitative and quantitative data explained our null findings for performance–satisfaction associations and identifies actionable levers (shorter, multi-modal preparatory materials; right-sizing teams to ~ 5–7; strengthening peer-assessment processes; and pacing application tasks) to reduce friction while preserving the learning benefits we observed.

However, the study has the following limitations: it was conducted for a single module in a single institution, it only included students as participants and did not investigate the faculty’s perception and experiences. Exploring faculty perception both as TBL and PBL facilitators in future studies may bring some insight into the acceptability of TBL among faculty as well as factors associated with students’ perceptions. Moreover, further study on PBL-TBL hybrid approach is recommended to incorporate the elements, from both formats to maximize students learning experiences.

Conclusion

Team based learning has been effective in improving students’ knowledge acquisition, regardless of their rating of the TBL sessions. Factors such as extensive pre-reading material, frequency, peer assessment, and lack of individual preparedness may have contributed to lower ratings while still achieving learning objectives. TBL was found to be effective in our setting, however wider student acceptability will require modifications in the TBL process according to students’ feedback.
