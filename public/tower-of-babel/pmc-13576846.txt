Professional Quality of Life Among Non-Specialist Healthcare Workers Trained in Child and Adolescent Mental Health in Kenya: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Beatrice Mkubwa, Vibian Angwenyi, Rachel Odhiambo, Charles R Newton, Marit Sijbrandij, Amina Abubakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the effects of child and adolescent mental health training on professional quality of life and to explore contextual factors influencing these outcomes among non-specialist healthcare workers in Kenya.MethodsA mixed-methods pre-post implementation evaluation with an explanatory qualitative follow-up was conducted. Quantitatively, 92 non-specialist healthcare workers were assessed pre-training and 65 at the 3-month follow-up using the Professional Quality of Life Scale-5 (ProQOL-5), measuring compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. All participants completed a 5-day training on child and adolescent mental and behavioral disorders (CAMBD). Regression analyses examined changes in ProQOL outcomes. Qualitatively, five focus group discussions (n=5) were conducted with a purposive subset of trained participants (n=60) to explore contextual influences on workforce well-being.ResultsDespite significant improvements in knowledge, no significant changes were observed across the ProQOL subscales between pre-training and follow-up (p-values 0.56–0.74). Greater knowledge gain was associated with lower secondary traumatic stress (r =- 0.29, p = 0.02). Qualitative findings revealed persistent systemic constraints, including high patient volumes, weak referral pathways, medication shortages, and a lack of career advancement, that undermine workforce well-being. Participants described moral distress arising from the inability to provide adequate CAMBD care or to translate newly acquired skills into practice, despite enhanced competence.ConclusionWhile mental health training improved knowledge, professional quality of life among non-specialist healthcare workers remained largely unchanged. Findings suggest that workforce well-being in task-sharing mental healthcare is strongly shaped by health system constraints, underscoring the need for structural interventions alongside capacity-building.Keywords: burnout, secondary traumatic stress, compassion satisfaction, task-sharing, mental health training

Introduction

Task-sharing, the systematic delegation of mental healthcare tasks from specialists to trained non-specialist healthcare workers (NSHWs), has emerged as a pragmatic strategy for low- and middle-income countries.1 This approach is valuable in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where training of NSHWs is critical to expanding coverage for mental health services, given the severe mental health specialist shortages.2,3 The World Health Organization (WHO)’s Mental Health Gap Action Programme Intervention Guide (mhGAP-IG) promotes task-sharing to integrate mental health services within primary healthcare (PHC) by training NSHWs to assess and manage priority mental, neurological, and substance use conditions, including child and adolescent mental and behavioral disorders (CAMBD).4,5

Training expands NSHWs’ capacity to expand early access to care through task-sharing, but it also introduces new professional and emotional demands.6,7 Working with children experiencing psychosocial distress is emotionally intensive, particularly when training prioritizes technical competence without parallel psychosocial support,8,9 with implications for job satisfaction, emotional exhaustion, and service quality.10,11 The assessment and management of CAMBD require interpreting complex, often atypical symptom presentations, accounting for developmental stage, and coordinating closely with caregivers, schools, and community systems.12,13 In low-resource settings, these demands are compounded by shortages of child and adolescent mental health specialists available for consultation or supervision and often fragmented referral pathways.3,12,14 This places distinct clinical and emotional burdens on NSHWs that may shape their compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. Despite these demands, supportive organizational practices, such as supervision, teamwork, and recognition, can improve motivation, empathy, and resilience.15,16

Kenya has adopted task-sharing as a national mental health strategy under the Kenya Mental Health Policy 2021–2030, with mhGAP-IG forming a core implementation framework.17–19 Yet the workforce expected to deliver on this policy operates under considerable strain: fewer than 500 specialist mental health workers serve the country nationally, and services remain concentrated at higher-level facilities with minimal PHC-level infrastructure.20,21 Motivation, retention, and burnout among Kenyan health workers have each been linked to workload and supervisory conditions,22,23 and CAMBD-specific implementation has required local adaptation to address child-specific service gaps.13 This background, a national policy commitment paired with an under-resourced delivery workforce, sets the stage for examining how NSHWs experience professional quality of life once trained.

Similar patterns are observed across helping professions more broadly, where training, supervision, and workplace conditions shape worker well-being.24–29 For example, peer refugee helpers, structured roles supported by training and supervision, influence mental health, with outcomes linked to factors such as compensation and role clarity.15 ProQOL-based studies among healthcare workers in other African settings document this dynamic, for example, nurses in Ethiopian cancer treatment centers have reported low-to-moderate compassion satisfaction alongside elevated burnout and compassion fatigue,30 a pattern echoed across other sub-Saharan African healthcare settings facing comparable workforce and resource constraints.31 Studies among other healthcare workers show that interventions targeting psychological and interpersonal skills can improve well-being and reduce burnout.25–27,29 Training is most effective when supported by reflective practice, supervision, and enabling organizational environments.24,28 The Professional Quality of Life Scale version 5 (ProQOL-5) provides a useful framework for examining well-being by capturing both the positive (compassion satisfaction) and negative (burnout, secondary traumatic stress) aspects of caregiving in a professional capacity.32 Compassion satisfaction reflects the sense of fulfillment and effectiveness derived from helping others in this professional role, while burnout and secondary traumatic stress reflect the emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and distress that can arise from the demands of this work.32 NSHWs in PHC facilities in Kenya include nurses and clinical officers delivering healthcare services. Despite the widespread use of task-sharing approaches such as the mhGAP-IG, few studies have examined the professional quality of life of among PHC-based NSHWs delivering mental healthcare, and evidence specific to CAMBD care is scarce. Existing studies, primarily conducted in humanitarian or hospital settings, suggest that lay counselors and other non-specialists may experience both satisfaction, often linked to self-efficacy and alignment with personal values, and strain, often linked to limited resources and unsupportive work environments, in these professional task-shared service roles.33,34 This highlights the need to examine professional quality of life in routine PHC settings where NSHWs increasingly deliver CAMBD care under substantial constraints. While the training strengthens competence in assessment, management, and referral of CAMBD, it may not be sufficient without a supportive work environment. In such settings, increased awareness of unmet patient needs may also lead to frustration and moral distress, showing the importance of understanding how training and system constraints interact to support task-sharing.

The present study addresses this gap using a mixed-methods design by examining (1) whether competence-focused mhGAP-IG CAMBD module training was associated with changes in compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress among PHC-based NSHWs in Kenya over three months; and (2) exploring the contextual and systemic factors that shaped these professional quality of life outcomes using qualitative methods.

Methods

Study Design

This study used a mixed-methods pre-post implementation evaluation with and explanatory qualitative follow up. The quantitative component assessed changes in ProQOL and knowledge scores among NSHWs immediately before the mhGAP-IG CAMBD training and at the 3-month follow-up. The qualitative component consisted of Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) conducted after the follow-up assessment to explore participants’ experiences of implementing post-training. Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed independently using appropriate analytical methods and subsequently integrated during interpretation to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s influence on professional quality of life.

Setting

The study was conducted in public PHC facilities in Nairobi County (urban) and Kilifi County (rural coastal) in Kenya to capture diverse health system and socioeconomic contexts. Training and baseline data collection took place between April and July 2024, with follow‑up data collected three months later between July and October 2024.

Participants and Sampling

Participants were NSHWs, mainly nurses and clinical officers, who provided outpatient services at 58 participating public PHC facilities. Facilities were selected in collaboration with county health authorities to ensure representation across diverse contexts. Eligible participants who were available to complete both the training and the three‑month follow‑up assessment were purposively identified, along with facility in-charges, and invited to participate. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment, and participation was voluntary.

Training

All consented participants completed a five-day training covering assessment, management, and referral of CAMBD, using materials adapted from the WHO’s mhGAP-IG CAMBD module.13 The training aimed to strengthen NSHWs’ capacity to identify and manage CAMBD within PHC facilities. The training was delivered by mhGAP‑trained specialists through lectures, case‑based learning, role-plays, and group discussions.

Data Collection

Quantitative data were collected using the self-administered ProQOL Version 5 questionnaire at baseline and 3 months post-training. For qualitative data, five FGDs were conducted at the three-month follow-up with a purposive subset of the trained NSHWs (n=60), with at least 9 participants per FGD. FGDs were conducted in private spaces within the PHC facilities or nearby venues and lasted approximately 60–90 minutes. FGDs were facilitated using a semi-structured guide focused on NSHWs’ post-training experiences applying skills, factors influencing care delivery, and perceived impacts, with probing questions to explore participants’ experiences in depth. All FGDs were audio-recorded, and written consent was obtained from participants.

Measurements

Sociodemographic Characteristics Questionnaire

Participants completed a questionnaire capturing a range of sociodemographic variables. These included age, gender, marital status, educational level, and occupation. Additional work‑related information was collected on the type of PHC facility where the participant was based (dispensary, health center, sub‑county hospital, or other) and whether they had previously received any mental‑health–related training.

Professional Quality of Life Scale

Professional quality of life was assessed using the 30-item ProQOL Version 5 (2009), a validated self-report measure of the positive and negative effects of caring for others. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). The ProQOL scale provides a structured means to assess the rewarding and challenging aspects of caregiving in a professional capacity through three dimensions: Compassion Satisfaction (CS), reflecting fulfilment derived from helping others; Burnout (BO), describing emotional exhaustion and reduced efficacy; and Secondary Traumatic Stress (STS), representing distress from exposure to others’ trauma.32 These dimensions together offer insight into the dual impact of professional caregiving roles, which are typical in task-shared mental healthcare interventions.33,35

Negatively worded items were reverse-scored, and subscale totals (range = 10–50) were categorized as low (≤22), moderate (23–41), or high (≥42), according to Stamm.32 Higher CS scores indicate greater professional satisfaction, whereas higher BO and STS scores indicate an increased risk of distress. Internal consistency of the ProQOL subscales was assessed at both times using Cronbach’s alpha. At pre-training, the CS subscale demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.81), STS showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.66), while BO exhibited low reliability (α = 0.44), despite applying recommended reverse coding. Post-training, reliability improved for CS (α = 0.85) and STS (α = 0.80), but BO remained unacceptably low (α = 0.32). Item-level analyses indicated that most CS and STS items correlated appropriately with their subscales, whereas several BO items showed weak or negative item-total correlations. Compared with the original ProQOL Version 5 norms (CS α = 0.88, BO α = 0.75, STS α = 0.81), CS and STS reliability in this sample were comparable after training, but the BO subscale demonstrated substantially lower internal consistency. For each ProQOL subscale, change scores were calculated by subtracting baseline scores from follow‑up scores, with positive values indicating increases and negative values indicating decreases.

Although the BO subscale showed low internal consistency, its results are reported alongside CS and STS to maintain the ProQOL scale framework and allow comparison with the qualitative findings, which also identified burnout-related themes. However, the BO results should be interpreted with caution. This limitation is acknowledged in the Results section and discussed in the Strengths and Limitations section.

Knowledge Questionnaire

Knowledge was assessed using a set of multiple‑choice questions administered immediately before the training and again at the three‑month follow‑up. Items were adapted from the WHO mhGAP‑IG CAMBD materials and reflected key content areas covered during the 5‑day training.36 Each correct response contributed one point to the total score, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Knowledge change was computed by subtracting the pre‑training score from the three‑month follow‑up score and used as a proxy for competence gain.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.1). Descriptive statistics (means, SDs, frequencies) were used for sociodemographic characteristics and ProQOL subscales. Changes in ProQOL were examined using paired comparisons. Associations between ProQOL subscales (CS, BO, STS) and knowledge change (a proxy for competence) were assessed using correlations and linear regression, adjusting for baseline ProQOL scores and demographics. Moderation analyses examined whether baseline ProQOL influenced associations between knowledge gains and ProQOL change. Paired pre-post comparisons described raw-score change, while regression models, adjusting for baseline ProQOL, tested whether knowledge gain predicted change independent of regression to the mean.

Interview audio files were transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and checked for accuracy. The qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach. Deductive coding was guided by study objectives, while inductive coding allowed identification of emerging themes related to professional quality of life. Transcripts were coded in NVivo-Lumivero©; initial coding was conducted independently by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, and B.M. coded the remaining transcripts under supervision. Quantitative and qualitative findings were later integrated during interpretation to triangulate outcomes and contextual insights.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Baseline ProQOL Scores

A total of 92 NSHWs completed the pre-training ProQOL assessment, and 65 participants (70.7%) were retained at the three-month follow-up. The sample comprised nurses (45.7%) and clinical officers (47.8%), with the majority being female (69.6%) and under 45 years of age (82.6%). Most participants (80.4%) held diplomas or below degree qualifications and had not previously received mental health training (68.5%).

Baseline ProQOL scores were generally similar across sociodemographic categories (Table 1). CS scores ranged from M = 37.2 to 43.1 across groups, with most participants reporting moderate-to-high satisfaction. BO scores ranged from M = 20.3 to 25.5, while STS scores ranged from M=18.6 to 24.5. Notably, older participants (46+ years) reported slightly elevated STS (M = 24.4, SD = 4.65), and those with postgraduate education demonstrated higher BO (M = 25.5, SD = 7.05) and lower CS (M = 37.2, SD = 7.09) than those with other education levels. However, no consistent patterns emerged across other demographic characteristics, suggesting that individual-level factors did not differentiate baseline ProQOL scores. Given the low internal consistency of the BO subscale (α = 0.44 pre-training; α = 0.32 post-training), burnout findings should be interpreted with caution.

ProQOL Changes Following Training

Among the 65 participants with complete paired data, ProQOL scores remained stable from pre- to post-training across all subscales (Table 2). Mean CS remained unchanged at 41.8 (pre: SD=4.71; post: SD=4.84). BO showed a slight decrease from M=21.4 (SD = 4.43) to M = 20.7 (SD = 4.28). STS remained essentially stable, changing from M = 22.0 (SD = 4.81) to M = 21.9 (SD = 4.90). None of these changes reached statistical significance (all p-values> 0.05), indicating that the training did not produce meaningful shifts in ProQOL scores.

Categorical distributions of ProQOL scores remained largely stable from pre- to post-training. Approximately 65% of participants remained in the moderate CS category at both time points, with no participants scoring in the low range. BO scores were predominantly moderate (52–55%), with 6–8% in the high category and approximately 37–42% in the low range. STS distributions were concentrated in the moderate range (48–51%), with 8–9% classified as high and 40–44% classified as low.

Association Between Knowledge Gains and ProQOL Changes

Changes in knowledge scores demonstrated modest associations with ProQOL outcomes. Linear regression models controlling for baseline ProQOL scores indicated that baseline ProQOL subscale scores significantly predicted post-training outcomes (CS: β = −0.43, p < 0.001; BO: β = −0.49, p < 0.001; STS: β = −0.38, p = 0.005). In contrast, knowledge improvement did not independently predict post-training ProQOL scores on any subscale after adjusting for baseline scores (CS: β = 0.29, p = 0.101; BO: β = −0.01, p = 0.927; STS: β = −0.37, p = 0.085).

Sociodemographic Predictors of ProQOL Outcomes

Regression analyses examining the role of demographic characteristics revealed that individual-level factors explained minimal variance in ProQOL scores across all three subscales and timepoints (Table 3). At baseline, female gender was associated with lower STS than male gender (p = 0.005), while older age was associated with higher STS (p = 0.034). Postgraduate or advanced educational attainment was significantly associated with elevated BO (p = 0.045) compared to diploma-level education, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size (n=4) in this category. At the post-training time point, postgraduate or advanced education was negatively associated with CS (p = 0.010) and positively associated with BO (p = 0.046). No other demographic variables were statistically significant at the follow-up.

Change-score analyses examining predictors of ProQOL shifts revealed limited demographic effects. Female gender was associated with increased BO over time (p = 0.018), while older age was associated with greater reductions in STS (p = 0.049). Age, education, and other demographic characteristics showed no significant associations with changes in CS or other subscales.

Knowledge and Demographic Predictors of ProQOL Change

Multiple regression models integrating both knowledge gains and demographic characteristics as simultaneous predictors of ProQOL change revealed that neither knowledge gains nor individual-level factors accounted for substantial variance in shifts in professional quality of life. For CS, knowledge change showed a non-significant trend toward positive association (B = 0.34, p = 0.12). BO was also not associated with knowledge change (B = −0.15, p = 0.50). For STS, a marginal trend suggested that greater knowledge gains might be associated with reduced traumatic stress (B = −0.40, p = 0.075), though this effect did not achieve conventional levels of statistical significance.

Demographic variables similarly contributed little to these models. Age, degree-level education, and postgraduate education were non-significant across all three outcomes. Female gender emerged as the only consistent demographic predictor, showing a marginally significant association with increased BO (B = 3.08, p = 0.031). These integrated models explained minimal variance in ProQOL change scores, with adjusted R2 values ranging from −0.02 to 0.10. Most of the variability in ProQOL scores remained unexplained by individual-level factors.

Moderation analyses testing whether baseline ProQOL scores influenced the relationship between knowledge gains and outcomes yielded largely null findings, with one exception: age significantly moderated the association between knowledge change and STS change (interaction β = 0.067, p = 0.024). Specifically, the protective effect of knowledge acquisition on STS reduction was attenuated among older workers, suggesting that age may diminish the emotional benefit of competence gains.

Qualitative Findings

The qualitative data provided context for the stable ProQOL scores, highlighting a disconnect between individual capacity-building and health systems realities.

Compassion Satisfaction: Competence-Driven Fulfillment, Undermined by Systemic Gaps

Across FGDs, NSHWs described how the training improved their competence and clinical confidence, enabling them to identify CAMBD more accurately, conduct structured assessments, and make informed management or referral decisions. When case management was successful, participants reported experiencing professional fulfillment. As one participant articulated:

“Even though follow-up is difficult… I’m contented, because when I see the patient stabilized, I feel satisfied” (P9, FGD_S1_Rural)

Participants described moments when successful case management provided motivation and affirmed their professional identity:

“From the training, I’ve been able to handle some cases… if not, I refer… that gives me confidence and satisfaction” (P2, FGD_S5_Urban)

However, rather than translating into improved compassion satisfaction or reduced burnout, enhanced competence heightened awareness of systemic care gaps, intensifying frustration and emotional distress. Participants described being able to apply their new skills successfully in individual cases, but consistently delivering the standard of CAMBD care they had been trained to provide was constrained by high patient volumes, understaffing, and competing service demands.

…Staffing is a challenge… you are willing to do, but because you need to clear queues… you won’t sit there and say, ‘I’ll only attend 30 patients because I want to give quality…’ It’s about who came in first. Maybe the 31st client was even more serious than those who came in first” (P7, FGD_S1_Rural)

Burnout: System-Level Failures, Not Training Inadequacy

Burnout was overwhelmingly attributed to system-level failures rather than individual deficits or training inadequacies. NSHWs described chronic understaffing that left them as sole providers across multiple service points, including outpatient departments, maternity units, and comprehensive care clinics, creating acute pressure and preventing patient-centered care. One participant explained:

As much as you want to counsel that client, you have a queue of 50 waiting for you (P9, FGD_S5_Urban)

Secondary Traumatic Stress: Emotional Burden Without Institutional Support

These conditions created significant emotional strain; several participants expressed distress about knowing what high‑quality CAMBD care required but being unable to deliver it due to structural constraints. The emotional burden was particularly pronounced when NSHWs encountered vulnerable children requiring follow-up care but lacked the means to provide continuity. As one NSHW described:

Sometimes you want to communicate or follow up…you get stuck…you feel demoralized… helpless (P6, FGD_S4_Urban)

You’ve been trained, but as you do the assessment, the other mind is asking you—what about vaccination? … staffing issues make us not provide the quality we want. (P7, FGD_S1_Rural)

In some cases, NSHWs reported using personal finances to meet professional obligations, such as purchasing medications or covering transportation for patient referrals, further illustrating the extent to which systemic neglect shifted responsibility onto individual providers.

…sometimes you have to chip in into your pocket… to follow up so you can deliver the message of mental health… (P3, FGD_S3_Urban)

Despite reporting emotional exhaustion and secondary traumatic stress, NSHWs described limited institutional support for debriefing or psychosocial support. This reflected a work environment where the NSHWs provide care to vulnerable populations without any support for their well-being. Administrative challenges, including salary delays, long probation periods, and missed promotions, further reduced morale.

These qualitative findings help explain stable ProQOL scores. Although training improved competence and confidence, the foundation for compassion satisfaction, NSHWs faced systemic barriers that limited care delivery, driving burnout and resulting emotional burden, contributing to secondary traumatic stress. Together, these conditions contributed to frustration and moral distress, reducing the impact of training on professional well-being over the three months.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined whether competence-focused mhGAP-IG CAMBD training for NSHWs in Kenyan primary care was associated with changes in professional quality of life and how contextual and health system factors shaped these outcomes. Despite improvements in competence following training, scores remained stable across the ProQOL subscales over three months. This suggests that changes in knowledge and skills do not automatically translate into changes in professional well-being, particularly in constrained health systems.

Qualitative findings clarified this disconnect. NSHWs reported increased awareness of appropriate mental health care standards post-training and were able to apply their new skills in individual encounters, describing genuine satisfaction when case management was successful. However, limited staffing, time pressure, weak supervision, and resource shortages constrained their ability to consistently deliver to consistently provide CAMBD care. This gap between what workers were trained to do and what the system allowed them to do generated moral distress. In some cases, increased competence heightened emotional strain by making service gaps more visible and personally salient.

These findings align with evidence that ProQOL among healthcare workers often remains stable over time, reflecting a balance between positive aspects of caregiving and emotional burden.34,37 Similar stability has been reported in diverse settings, including during periods of high workload, such as the COVID-19 pandemic,38 as well as in Nepal (Singh et al, 2024) and Ecuador.39 This pattern is consistent with Stamm’s conceptualization of ProQOL as a dynamic equilibrium rather than a linear outcome of training or exposure.32 Our findings extend this model by showing that competence gains alone are insufficient to shift this equilibrium in low-resource, high-constraint settings.

More broadly, our results support growing evidence from task-sharing and mhGAP implementation research that training improves competence but does not necessarily improve worker well-being when health systems remain weak. Studies from Uganda, Ethiopia, Nepal, and China similarly show that although mhGAP training strengthens skills and professional identity, it can also increase emotional burden when supervision and organizational support are inadequate.8,40–42 Across these settings, workload pressure, staffing shortages, and weak referral systems consistently emerge as key drivers of burnout and secondary traumatic stress. Our findings are consistent with evidence that structural constraints are central to NSHW well-being.6,43

Evidence from high-income contexts similarly highlights the protective role of structured supervision and reflective practice. A systematic review shows that peer support, mentorship, and regular clinical supervision can reduce compassion fatigue and improve professional engagement.44 In contrast, the absence of such systems may intensify emotional strain by leaving clinicians to manage complex cases without support. This dynamic was evident in our study, where NSHWs described how the training heightened their awareness of unmet patient needs, but had limited capacity to respond, contributing to feelings of helplessness and emotional fatigue. This suggests that competence-building interventions may inadvertently increase distress when not paired with adequate psychosocial and supervisory structures.

In SSA, these findings must be interpreted within the context of chronic health workforce shortages, high patient loads, and limited institutional support, all of which are strongly associated with burnout and emotional exhaustion.22,23,45,46 In Kenya, these pressures are compounded by administrative and systemic gaps that shift emotional and practical burdens onto frontline workers. Financial and administrative frustrations reported by NSHWs reflect evidence that fair remuneration may protect worker well-being, with humanitarian studies showing compensatory buffering effects on mental health.15,47 The absence of workplace psychosocial support normalizes unreciprocated emotional labor. Consequently, structural constraints, rather than inadequate training, shape NSHWs’ well-being, as our qualitative findings highlight. Global evidence further indicates that health workers’ well-being is directly linked to service quality, retention, and system performance.48 Without addressing these structural constraints, training interventions alone are unlikely to produce sustainable improvements in workforce well-being.

Importantly, while the WHO mhGAP framework positions task-sharing as a key strategy for expanding mental health care access in low-resource settings.49 Our findings suggest that implementation must extend beyond training delivery. Sustainable task-sharing requires investment in supervision systems, workload management, institutional recognition of mental health work, and fair administrative structures.40,41 Without these supports, competence gains may increase expectations without corresponding capacity to act, thereby sustaining or even intensifying emotional strain. This aligns with broader implementation evidence that system conditions ultimately determine whether training translates into effective and sustainable practice.14,50

Implications for Policy and Practice

Maintaining a healthy professional quality of life is central to workforce sustainability and service quality. Burnout and secondary traumatic stress are associated with poorer patient outcomes, reduced empathy, and higher turnover.11,51,52 Our study findings suggest three priority areas for intervention. First, task-sharing initiatives such as mhGAP implementation should adopt dual-focus training models that combine clinical competence with psychological support through reflective practice, peer support, and structured supervision.44 Second, organizational changes are necessary to translate competence gains into improved well-being. These should address excessive workload, ensure reliable medication supply, and reduce administrative inequities. Third, integrating professional quality of life monitoring into PHC performance frameworks could enable early detection of workforce distress and support retention, aligning with the WHO Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health.53

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study was the mixed-methods design, which combined quantitative assessment with qualitative inquiry, enabling triangulation of findings, providing an in-depth understanding of the results. Several limitations warrant consideration. During FGDs, participants’ baseline understanding of quality of life was not assessed, which may have shaped the qualitative findings. The modest sample size and three-month follow-up may have limited statistical power to detect small but potentially meaningful changes in ProQOL scores. Findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary rather than conclusive. Additionally, ProQOL scores were self-reported and may therefore be susceptible to reporting bias. As noted in the Methods and Results sections, the BO subscale demonstrated low internal consistency (α < 0.32), limiting the reliability and interpretability of burnout estimates. This challenge has also been documented in previous studies employing the ProQOL measure.54,55 Accordingly, the BO sub-scale findings should be interpreted with caution and considered alongside the qualitative data, which provide complementary insights into burnout-related experiences. Future research should prioritize cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the ProQOL scale in Kenya and other SSA settings, use longitudinal designs with larger samples, and examine the effects of combined training, supervision, and system-level interventions on provider well-being.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that competence‑focused mhGAP‑IG CAMBD training alone is insufficient to produce meaningful improvements in professional quality of life among NSHWs. While training improved competence and clinical confidence and enabled NSHWs to apply their new skills, systemic barriers constrained their application and contributed to emotional strain. Sustainable task-sharing models require simultaneous investment in workforce support and health system strengthening, including structured supervision, manageable caseloads, established referral pathways, and dedicated time for mental health consultations. Protecting the professional quality of life of NSHWs is essential not only for provider well-being but also for scaling up mental health integration within PHC.
