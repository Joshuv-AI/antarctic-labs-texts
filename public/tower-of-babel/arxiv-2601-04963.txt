Text as a Universal Interface for Transferable Personalization
Yuting Liu, Jian Guan, Jia-Nan Li, Wei Wu, Jiang-Ming Yang, Jianzhe Zhao, Guibing Guo

Abstract
We study the problem of personalization in large language models (LLMs). Prior work predominantly represents user preferences as implicit, model-specific vectors or parameters, yielding opaque “black-box” profiles that are difficult to interpret and transfer across models and tasks. In contrast, we advocate natural language as a universal, model- and task-agnostic interface for preference representation. The formulation leads to interpretable and reusable preference descriptions, while naturally supporting continual evolution as new interactions are observed. To learn such representations, we introduce a two-stage training framework that combines supervised fine-tuning on high-quality synthesized data with reinforcement learning to optimize long-term utility and cross-task transferability. Based on this framework, we develop AlignXplore+ , a universal preference reasoning model that generates textual preference summaries. Experiments on nine benchmarks show that our 8B model achieves state-of-the-art performance—outperforming substantially larger open-source models—while exhibiting strong transferability across tasks, model families, and interaction formats.

Introduction

Personalization, the tailoring of model behavior to individual user needs, is a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence (AI) applications
(
19
;
18
)
, especially as large language models (LLMs) transition from general-purpose tools into user-centric products
(
8
)
.
Central to this endeavor is the challenge of user representation learning: the process of distilling a user’s complex preferences from their behaviors into a machine-readable format
(
25
)
. For decades, the dominant paradigm has encoded user preferences into either dense vectors such as static user embeddings
(
36
;
31
)
and dynamic sequential states
(
16
)
, or user-specific parameters like LoRA
(
13
)
and adaptors
(
12
)
. These approaches have driven significant advancements in a wide range of applications, such as content recommendation
(
1
;
27
)
, targeted advertising
(
42
;
7
)
, and personalized response generation
(
24
;
50
;
49
;
4
;
29
)
.

Figure 1:
Paradigm shift from vector/parameter-based
(a)
to text-based user representations
(b)
for personalization.
(a) Traditional methods generate user-specific parameters and vectors that are tightly coupled with their training model, thus cannot be transferred.
(b) We pioneer a text-based paradigm that infers a model- and task-agnostic preference summary, which serves as a universal interface to empower any downstream model for various tasks.

Despite their success, these representations pose fundamental barriers to a unified, user-centric AI.
First, they are opaque “black boxes.”
This lack of human-readable meaning hinders interpretability and prevents users from understanding or controlling their own digital profiles
(
51
;
2
)
.

Second, and more critically, they lack the zero-shot transferability required for a unified user experience.
Here, “transferability” signifies that the user representation itself can be fully understood by any model for any task, eliminating the need for task-specific fine-tuning or adaptation layers. However, vector or parameter-based representations are tightly coupled with their original model’s architecture and objective, rendering them meaningless to a new application and creating fragmented data silos. For instance, a user’s rich movie viewing history remains unknown to their music streaming service, forcing every application to learn preferences from scratch.

In this paper, we propose using text as a universal, model- and task-agnostic interface for user representation (Figure
1
). Our approach synthesizes user history into a natural language preference summary. This textual representation is inherently interpretable, empowering users to inspect, edit, and control their digital profiles. It is also universally transferable; by decoupling the profile from model architecture, it enables seamless knowledge transfer across applications and the composition of a holistic user view. Furthermore, it is naturally suited for streaming inference, i.e., producing an updated summary by consuming the old one along with new interactions
(
23
)
. To generate such transferable summaries, we introduce a novel two-stage training framework, from which we derive a universal preference inference model,
AlignXplore+
. First, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) bootstraps the model on high-quality data from our innovative “generate-validate-merge” pipeline, ensuring the initial summaries are both comprehensive and behaviorally accurate. Second, Reinforcement Learning (RL) directly optimizes the policy for transferability using two key innovations: (1) a curriculum pruning strategy that isolates reasoning-intensive signals to foster the learning of intrinsic preference patterns; and (2) a cumulative reward that promotes evolvable summaries, making them effective foundations for streaming updates of user preferences.

Extensive experiments across nine diverse benchmarks, involving recommendation, response selection, and response generation tasks, demonstrate that our
AlignXplore+
achieves state-of-the-art performance and unprecedented transferability. Our 8B model achieves an average score of 75.10%, yielding an absolute improvement of 4.2% over GPT-OSS-20B, and consistently outperforming all baselines, including open-source models that are orders of magnitude larger, with particularly strong gains on complex reasoning tasks. Critically, we fulfill the promise of a universal interface by demonstrating systematic transferability across several key dimensions: our textual summaries effectively transfer knowledge across diverse tasks (with up to 14.00% absolute improvement), provide consistent gains across different model architectures (ranging from 0.76% to 8.70% improvements on different tasks with different downstream models), and remain robust to complex, real-world user histories, including fundamental shifts in interaction format (e.g., positive-only logs, with a 2.36% improvement over Qwen3-8B) and the presence of multiple concurrent interests. In summary, our contributions are:

I. We pioneer a new paradigm for user representation that shifts from opaque, non-transferable vectors to interpretable, universal, and composable textual preference summary.

II. We introduce a novel two-stage training framework that combines SFT with RL and develop
AlignXplore+
, a reasoning model to generate transferable preference summaries.

III. Extensive experiments on diverse personalization benchmarks demonstrate that our method not only achieves state-of-the-art performance but also exhibits remarkable zero-shot transferability across downstream models, tasks and domains

Related works

2.1
User Representation Learning

User representation learning captures user-specific context from historical behaviors to enable personalized AI systems. Traditional neural recommenders encode users as dense embeddings, initially learned by mapping user IDs to latent vectors and optimizing them via observed interactions
(
36
)
. To capture the dynamic, sequential patterns in user behavior, subsequent work moved beyond static IDs, leveraging Transformers
(
16
)
and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
(
44
;
10
)
to encode entire user histories. Further advancements incorporated multimodal item features (e.g., images) to enrich the user representations
(
45
;
26
)
. A parallel line of research bypasses explicit user embeddings, instead encoding user interactions directly into the model’s full parameters
(
17
;
20
)
or partial parameters
(
40
;
5
)
. However, both vector- and parameter-based representations are inherently opaque and model-specific. Recent methods resolve these limitations by directly prompting LLMs with users’ textual histories
(
47
;
1
;
37
)
, which presents a dual challenge: the prohibitive computational cost and latency associated with processing long user histories
(
48
)
, and the inherent difficulty for LLMs to induce user preferences from noisy interactions
(
35
;
23
)
. Our work addresses both issues by employing reinforcement learning to train a policy that incrementally transforms noisy user histories into an efficient, interpretable, and transferable textual representation.

2.2
LLMs for Personalization

The rapid progress of LLMs has inspired a growing body of work on leveraging their language understanding and generation capabilities for various personalization tasks. For recommendation, LLMs can serve as sequence encoders to generate user or item embeddings
(
53
)
, be prompted directly to produce ranked item lists
(
27
)
, or be used for user data augmentation
(
43
;
52
)
. For personalized generation tasks, LLMs can adapt style, tone, and content to match user preferences induced from the user interactions for dialogue, summarization, and creative writing
(
47
;
38
;
22
)
. Besides, LLMs can be integrated into agent frameworks equipped with explicit memory modules that store and retrieve user-specific knowledge to guide multi-step personalized interactions
(
21
)
. To align these models with individual user preferences, a variety of optimization strategies have been explored for vector- and parameter-based user representations, including fine-tuning user-specific parameters
(
14
;
34
)
and guiding LLM decoding with user-specific rewards
(
3
;
39
)
. Our work departs from these approaches and focuses on optimizing textual user representations through curriculum RL, enabling transferable personalization at inference time.

Methodology

Figure 2:
Overview of our training framework, which consists of two main stages. (a) SFT Stage: We first create high-quality training data using a “generate-validate-merge” pipeline, which synthesizes a comprehensive preference summary
P^{k}
by ensuring accurate prediction of multiple future interactions. (b) & (c) RL Stage: This stage features a curriculum pruning strategy that uses select reasoning-intensive samples, and a cumulative reward function that optimizes summaries for long-term effectiveness in streaming scenarios.

We aim to learn a policy that crafts a textual summary of user preferences from the user’s interaction history. This policy operates incrementally: synthesizing the user’s recent interactions, while also conditioning on a pre-existing preference summary
(
23
)
, which can encapsulate long-term interests or even be imported from entirely different interaction environments (e.g., from recommendation to dialogue). Our framework is thus highly efficient by obviating the need to re-process the entire interaction history at each step. To this end, we propose a novel framework combining SFT and RL, as shown in Figure
2
.

3.1
Task Formulation

To unify diverse interaction environments, we formulate user interaction as a sequence of preference pairs. Specifically, a user’s interaction history within a certain period is defined as a sequence
H^{[0,K]}=(h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{K})
. Each
h_{i}
is a triple
(p_{i},n_{i},c_{i})
where
p_{i}
and
n_{i}
represent a preference pair where the user prefers item
p_{i}
over item
n_{i}
, denoted as
p_{i}\succ n_{i}
, and
c_{i}
is the context in which this preference was expressed. This formulation intentionally accommodates various task modalities. For example, in recommendation systems,
c_{i}
is often null, and
p_{i}
could be an item the user clicked on, while
n_{i}
could be an item that was displayed but ignored, or even empty if only positive feedback is available. In personalized dialogue systems,
c_{i}
would be the user’s preceding post or the dialogue history, and
p_{i}
could be a model response that the user “liked” or continued the conversation from, while
n_{i}
is a response they “disliked.”

We aim to learn a generative policy
\pi_{\theta}
, parameterized by
\theta
, that incrementally crafts a textual summary of a user’s preferences. Faced with a continuous stream of interactions, this policy is designed to operate on any segment of the user’s history and evolve the preference summary over time. Formally, given any two time steps
k_{1}<k_{2}
, it synthesizes an updated summary
P^{k_{2}}
by conditioning on the prior summary
P^{k_{1}}
and the intervening interactions
H^{[k_{1},k_{2})}
through a reasoning process
(
32
;
6
)
:

P^{k_{2}}\sim\pi_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid P^{k_{1}},H^{[k_{1},k_{2})}\right).

(1)

While RL is powerful for optimizing our policy, it may require a strong starting point. To address this, we first perform SFT to provide the policy with a strong initialization.

3.2
Supervised Fine-tuning

A conventional approach to synthesizing SFT data might involve prompting an off-the-shelf LLM to generate a summary directly from a user’s history
(
23
)
. However, we find this unconstrained generation often produces summaries that are either too generic or fixated on a single aspect of user preference. Such summaries fail to capture the multi-faceted nature of true user preferences, severely limiting their transferability.

Our key insight is that to generate deep and comprehensive summaries, the model must be guided to explain why a user makes specific future choices. This motivates our “divide-and-conquer” synthesis pipeline, which uses future interactions as explicit grounding targets. As illustrated in Figure
2
(a), this pipeline first generates and validates multiple, fine-grained summaries anchored to specific future behaviors, before merging them into a single holistic summary (see Appendix
A
for details). This ensures the final summary is both accurate (enabling user-aligned actions) and comprehensive (capturing the full spectrum of preferences). We present the details as follows:

Stage 1: Preference Generation.

To generate a summary for a given history prefix
H^{[0,k)}
, we use subsequent interactions as relevance anchors. Specifically, we sample multiple preference pairs, which we call “target interactions”, from
H^{[k,K]}
. Each target interaction, along with the prefix
H^{[0,k)}
, forms a distinct input for an LLM to synthesize a preference summary explaining the behavior in that target. This compels the LLM to extract only the historical preferences pertinent to that specific decision, avoiding a generic and often unhelpful overview. Crucially, we treat these pairs as unlabeled targets, providing only the context and candidate items without revealing the user’s actual choice, which prevents shortcut learning.

Stage 2: Behavioral Validation.

To validate the quality of each generated preference summary, we use it to condition an LLM to perform preference prediction on its corresponding target interaction. (i.e., asking “Given this preference, which item would the user prefer?”). We discard any summary that fails to predict the user’s actual choice, thereby filtering out inaccurate or inconsistent ones.

Stage 3: Preference Merging.

After filtering, we obtain a set of target-specific preference summaries. We then prompt a powerful LLM to merge these candidates into a single, comprehensive summary. The prompt explicitly instructs the model to synthesize a non-redundant and holistic view of the user’s preferences, complete with a reasoning process. Finally, to guarantee its behavioral accuracy, this merged summary undergoes a final validation, where we verify its ability to correctly predict the majority of the original target interactions.

We can naturally adapt our synthesis pipeline for the streaming setting to capture preference evolution. First, we synthesize an initial summary from an early segment of user history. Then, for each subsequent interaction segment, we synthesize an updated summary by conditioning our pipeline on both the new interactions and the previously generated summary. This iterative process creates an SFT dataset with examples of both generating a summary from scratch and incrementally updating an existing one with new user interactions.

3.3
Reinforcement Learning

We further refine the policy
\pi_{\theta}
using an RL framework tailored to address two fundamental challenges in building a universal personalization model:
(1) Inefficient learning on heterogeneous data:
Raw interaction data presents a wide spectrum of difficulty. Naive training is inefficient, wasting resources on trivial instances while failing to learn from prohibitively hard ones. This biases the model towards simplistic solutions and harms its multi-task transferability
(
11
)
.
(2) Myopic optimization in streaming settings:
In a streaming context, a summary must be a good foundation for future updates, not just immediately accurate. A reward function focused solely on immediate utility encourages myopic, short-sighted summaries that degrade over time.

To tackle these challenges, we introduce two corresponding innovations. For the first, we employ a “curriculum-based data pruning” strategy to automatically select a calibrated set of reasoning-intensive instances. For the second, we design a “cumulative reward” function that explicitly optimizes for the long-term, streaming utility of a summary.

Curriculum-based Data Pruning.

This strategy aims to select challenging yet tractable examples across tasks that may vary dramatically in difficulty. Formally, the full interaction history of a user,
H^{[0,K]}
, can be decomposed into a set of training instances, where each instance is a pair
(H^{[0,k)},h_{k})
representing the task of predicting interaction
h_{k}
from its preceding history.
Our strategy employs a pair of models: a general-purpose “weak” model and a specialized “strong” model created by fine-tuning the weak one on a direct prediction task. These models are used to assess each potential training sample based on two key metrics:

•

Tractability Score (
S_{\text{tract}}
):
Derived from the strong model’s performance on a given sample
(H^{[0,k)},h_{k})
:

S_{\text{tract}}=p_{\text{strong}}(p_{k}\succ n_{k}|H^{[0,k)},h_{k})
.
It filters out samples that are either trivially easy or impossibly hard by enforcing the condition
\tau_{\text{low}}<S_{\text{tract}}<\tau_{\text{high}}
.

•

Learning Potential Score (
S_{\text{learn}}
):
This measures the performance lift as the difference in log-probabilities between the strong and weak models:

S_{\text{learn}}=\log\frac{p_{\text{strong}}(p_{k}\succ n_{k}|H^{[0,k)},h_{k})}{p_{\text{weak}}(p_{k}\succ n_{k}|H^{[0,k)},h_{k})}
.
We select for samples where this gap is in the top
\alpha
-percentile, as this effectively filters out simpler instances where the weak model already performs well.

Based on this pruned, high-signal dataset, we focus the learning process on instances where reasoning provides the greatest marginal utility. Appendix
B
shows more implementation details.

Rollout Generation.

To explicitly train for the streaming setting, our rollout generation is designed to mimic the core dynamic of summary evolution. For each user history, our pruning method identifies two interaction time steps
k_{1}<k_{2}
, which define the structure for our streaming rollout generation: an initial history segment
H^{[0,k_{1})}
and a subsequent segment
H^{[k_{1},k_{2})}
. The policy
\pi_{\text{old}}
then performs a two-stage hierarchical sampling process: it first generates a set of
G
initial preference summaries from the early history, and then, conditioned on one randomly selected initial summary, generates
G
updated summaries using the later history. This process is formalized as:

\displaystyle\{P_{i}^{k_{1}}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|H^{[0,k_{1})}),

(2)

\displaystyle\{P_{j}^{k_{2}}\}_{j=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid\hat{P}^{k_{1}},H^{[k_{1},k_{2})}),

(3)

where
\hat{P}^{k_{1}}
is randomly sampled from the generated summaries in the first step. This process yields a total of
2G
candidate summaries, forming a tree-like structure of potential reasoning paths.

Reward and Advantage.

Our reward function is designed to credit summaries that are not only immediately useful but also serve as a good foundation for future updates. To this end, we define an
immediate reward
,
r(P)
, which repurposes the binary filter from our SFT behavioral validation (Section
3.2
) into a continuous signal. This reward is the probability assigned by a powerful downstream model
M
to the user’s actual choice, given a summary
P
and its corresponding ground-truth interaction
h=(p,n,c)
:

r(P)=p_{M}\big(p\succ n\mid P,c\big).

(4)

Based on this, we compute a
cumulative reward
,
R(P)
, that incorporates long-horizon utility. For an updated summary
P_{j}^{k_{2}}
from the second rollout set, its cumulative reward is simply its immediate reward. For an initial summary
P_{i}^{k_{1}}
, its cumulative reward is calculated as:

R(P_{i}^{k_{1}})=r(P_{i}^{k_{1}})+\gamma\cdot\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_{j}^{k_{2}},

(5)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a discount factor that balances immediate accuracy with future utility.
Finally, we compute the advantage
A(P)
for each summary by normalizing its cumulative reward
R(P)
against the mean and standard deviation of all rewards from the same rollout set.

Policy Optimization.

Finally, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
is then updated by optimizing the following clipped surrogate objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2G}\sum_{P}\frac{1}{L_{P}}\sum_{t=1}^{L_{P}}\min(\frac{\pi_{\theta}|_{t}}{\pi_{\text{old}}|_{t}}A(P),

(6)

\displaystyle\text{clip}(\frac{\pi_{\theta}|_{t}}{\pi_{\text{old}}|_{t}},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A(P)),

where
P\in\{P_{i}^{k_{1}}\}_{i=1}^{G}\cup\{P_{j}^{k_{2}}\}_{j=1}^{G}
is a summary from the rollout set,
A(P)
is its corresponding advantage, and
L_{P}
is the length of
P
and its corresponding reasoning process, and
{\pi_{\theta}|_{t}}
and
{\pi_{\text{old}}|_{t}}
are the token-level probabilities from the current policy and the old policy for the
t
-th token, respectively.

Experiments

4.1
Experimental setup

Training Data.

For SFT, we synthesize 540K training instances based on three datasets including Amazon-Book
(
30
)
, MIND
(
46
)
and AlignX
(
22
)
, which span a wide spectrum of domains (e.g., e-commerce, news, daily life) and preference complexities.
During RL, we sampled 112K instances from the original datasets given limited computation resources.
We provide more data details in Appendix
B.2
.

Implementation Details.

We use Qwen3-8B
41
as the backbone. For SFT, we use PyTorch and the HuggingFace trl library with bfloat16 precision, Flash-attention-2 and TF32 arithmetic.
For RL training, we use the VERL library. During evaluation, the default downstream model is Qwen3-8B. More implementation details are presented in Appendix
C
.

Benchmarks and Metrics.

We evaluate our method on nine benchmarks spanning three distinct personalization tasks, evaluated by accuracy (ACC) unless stated otherwise:
(1) Recommendation (Rec.):
Predicting a user’s preferred item from a pair based on their browsing or click history. Benchmarks include MIND
46
, Amazon-Book
30
, and MovieLens-32M
(
9
)
.

(2) Response Selection (R.S.):
Selecting the user-preferred response from candidates. This is tested on datasets with varied history formats, from structured preference triplets (AlignX
22
, P-Soups
14
) to raw dialogues (PersonaMem
15
).

(3) Response Generation (R.G.):
Generating a personalized response from a conversational log, simulating an AI assistant, evaluated on HiCUPID
(
28
)
using its benchmark-specific automatic evaluation.

Baselines.

We compare our method against two categories of approaches:

(1) Direct Sequence Models w/o Preference Inference:
This category includes models that process the raw user interaction history directly to make predictions, without an explicit preference summarization step. We employ Qwen3-8B
{}_{\text{non-thinking}}
and TALLRec
(
1
)
, a strong baseline fine-tuned from Qwen3-8B on our SFT data.
(2) Preference Inference Models:
This category comprises LLMs that are prompted to first generate a textual preference summary, which is then used for the downstream task. These models span different scales, including 8B-sized models (AlignXplore
(
23
)
, Qwen3-8B
{}_{\text{thinking}}
, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B
(
6
)
), and state-of-the-art larger models (Qwen3-32B, GPT-OSS-20B
(
33
)
, DeepSeek-R1-671B). Our model and all baselines are evaluated in both the
full-history
setting (processing the entire history at once) and the
streaming
setting (processing history in sequential segments). We provide further analyses in Appendix
E.2
.

4.2
Main Results

Our main results, presented in Table
1
, lead to three key findings:
(1) Our framework outperforms similar-sized baselines in average score, and often exceeds even significantly larger models.
Even though our preference inference approach deliberately forgoes the full raw history available to direct sequence models, relying instead on a compact textual summary, it still consistently outperforms them. This demonstrates that the quality of representation matters more than the quantity of raw data, validating our framework’s ability to distill user preferences into a highly efficient and comprehensive format.

(2) Our framework’s primary advantage lies in its capacity for deep preference reasoning, especially where user intent is subtle.
This is most evident on tasks like AlignX, where preferences must be inferred from conversational context. Our model achieves substantial gains over the next-best baselines (e.g., +8.43% over AlignXplore in the full-history setting), while methods reliant on direct full-history sequence modeling struggle.
We attribute this gain to our use of more comprehensive and heterogeneous training data, together with finer-grained reward signal, which enhances both the depth and coverage of the summarized preference. This demonstrates that by explicitly generating and validating textual hypotheses about user preferences, our model captures a deeper, more accurate understanding of user intent.

(3) The explicit textual summary enables superior generalization across heterogeneous task formats, a critical challenge in personalization.
The contrast with TALLRec is particularly stark. While TALLRec is a powerful baseline for recommendation tasks, its performance collapses on the response generation task (HiCUPID), barely improving over a non-finetuned model. Our framework, however, maintains strong performance on HiCUPID, proving that a reasoned preference summary acts as a more robust and transferable representation of user interest than task-specific embeddings. This ability to bridge different tasks (e.g., from selection to generation) underscores the value of our text-based personalization approach.

4.3
Transferable ability

A key motivation for our text-based personalization approach is transferability. Unlike task-specific latent representations, an explicit preference summary should function as a “plug-and-play” representation of user preferences. This allows it to be reused across different downstream models, applied to heterogeneous tasks, and remain robust even when the format of user interaction data varies. We evaluate our framework’s performance across four critical dimensions of transferability.

Cross-domain Transfer.

A truly unified user model should capture preferences that transcend the boundaries of a single application. We therefore test a critical scenario: can a preference summary, derived from a user’s behavior in one domain (e.g., recommendation), be successfully applied to guide personalization for the same user in a completely different domain (e.g., dialogue)? This capability is vital for creating seamless, holistic user experiences across an ecosystem of services.
As shown in Table
2
, our method achieves substantially stronger cross-domain transferability than all baselines in both directions. The gains are especially pronounced when transferring rich dialogue preferences from AlignX to the MIND news recommendation task (see Appendix
D.1
for dataset construction details). This suggests that our framework effectively decouples user’s core preferences from the source domain, while the baselines lacks such a capability.
The results also reveal an inherent asymmetry. Fine-grained conversational feedback provides richer and more transferable preference signals under shared topical spaces, whereas coarse, single-faceted news-click signals are inadequate for the more nuanced preference reasoning required in dialogue prediction. Consequently, although our model can infer wider and deeper preferences, the performance improvements from MIND (which offers relatively limited information) to AlignX (which demands richer preference signals) are necessarily modest.

Cross-model Transfer.

We then evaluate if the generated summaries are useful for a variety of downstream models, not just the one used for training. Concretely, we first use the baselines and our
AlignXplore+
model to generate user preference summaries, and then feed these summaries into Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and GPT-OSS-20B to perform downstream tasks.
Table
3
shows that while stronger downstream models naturally lead to better performance, our method consistently outperforms baselines across all tested models of a similar scale. This indicates that the quality of the summaries our framework produces is intrinsic and robust, providing a stable performance lift regardless of the specific downstream “reader” model. This is a crucial practical advantage over direct sequence methods like TALLRec, whose representations cannot be easily transferred to different model architectures.

User Behavior Transfer.

We next evaluate the model’s ability to transfer from its structured training format (paired feedback) to a more realistic positive-only format. This is crucial because explicit negative feedback is often unavailable in many real-world applications (e.g., browsing logs, liked items). To simulate this, we test the model’s performance after removing all negative samples from the user histories. As seen in Table
4
, while all models experience a performance shift, our approach maintains its significant advantage over baselines, especially on response selection tasks where negative feedback is most informative. This successful transfer demonstrates that our framework’s reasoning ability is not merely dependent on explicit negative signals, making it a robust and practical solution for real-world deployment.

Multi-interest Transfer.

A central challenge for a universal user model is its ability to synthesize a coherent profile from histories containing multiple, parallel interests. This scenario is critical, as modern users’ digital footprints are fragmented across disparate services. To simulate this, we interleave a user’s history from one domain with their history in another. Specifically, for response selection, we fuse user interactions from MovieLens into AlignX histories; for response generation, we similarly merge dialogue history concerning two different topics within HiCUPID (see Appendix
D.2
for dataset construction details). As illustrated in Fig.
3
, while this fusion of interests challenges all models, our method consistently maintains a significant performance advantage. This indicates our textual summary can effectively disentangle and represent distinct interests simultaneously, rather than conflating them into a noisy average. This capability is crucial for building a truly holistic user model suitable for cross-application personalization.

(1) SFT and RL play distinct, complementary roles in achieving optimal performance.

Without this form of temporal regularization, the model becomes myopic, optimizing for the current step at the expense of long-term summary quality and reusability.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we pioneer a paradigm shift, championing text as a universal interface for user representation. Our
AlignXplore+
generates textual summaries that not only achieve state-of-the-art performance, but more critically, exhibit unprecedented transferability across diverse tasks, models, interaction formats, and complex multi-interest histories. By transforming user profiles from opaque vectors into interpretable, portable narratives, our work paves the way for a new generation of transparent, interoperable, and truly user-centric AI agents.

Limitations

While our framework demonstrates significant advantages in efficiency and effectiveness, several limitations warrant discussion to guide future research.
(1) Due to data limitations, we can only simulate transfer scenarios through synthesizing existing datasets, which may deviate from real-world deployment conditions to some extent. (2) Although we emulate a streaming setting, our method has not yet been deployed in a production environment. Therefore, its robustness under extremely long interaction histories remains unclear. (3) This work focuses on developing a relatively “universal” approach for modeling user preferences, and we therefore did not perform highly specialized or fine-grained design of the model training algorithms.

All data are used solely for scientific research, not for commercial purposes or for profiling or decision-making about individuals, and their acquisition and use comply with relevant ethical guidelines and standards of academic integrity. All existing resources used in our experiments—including datasets, pretrained models, and various APIs—are accessed and employed within the bounds of their original licenses and terms of use. Appendix A
Details for Supervised Fine-tuning Data Synthesis

This section provides the technical details for our supervised fine-tuning data synthesis strategy, as introduced in Section
3.2
.

A.1
History Segmentation and Target Selection

For each user
u
with an ordered interaction history
H=(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{K})
, we first partition it into non-overlapping, contiguous segments,
\{S_{u}^{j}\}
. For our experiments, we use three segments (
S_{u}^{1},S_{u}^{2},S_{u}^{3}
) corresponding to early, middle, and late periods of user activity. We filter out users for whom any segment contains fewer than a predefined number of interactions (e.g., 3).

The selection of “target” interactions is crucial. To identify meaningful targets, we first compute a
Tractability Score
,
S_{\text{tract}}(h_{i})
, for each interaction
h_{i}
(details in Section
3.3
). This score reflects how well a strong model can predict the user’s choice. We extract a tractable subset
\bar{S}_{u}^{j}=\{h_{i}\in S_{u}^{j}|S_{\text{tract}}(h_{i})\geq\tau_{\text{tract}}\}
, where
\tau_{\text{tract}}
is a threshold. From each
\bar{S}_{u}^{j}
, we randomly sample a small number of interactions to form the target set
T_{u}^{j}
. These targets serve as anchors for profile generation and as ground-truth labels for our self-correction step.

A.2
Pipeline Details

The process can be formalized as follows:

1.

Target-aware Generation
: For a history segment
S_{u}
and its corresponding target set
T_{u}
, we generate a profile candidate
p_{u,t}
for each target
t\in T_{u}
. If a prior profile
p_{u}^{\text{prev}}
exists (from a previous segment), it is included in the context. The generation is performed by a teacher LLM, denoted by
\mathcal{G}
:

r_{u,t},p_{u,t}=\mathcal{G}(\text{prompt}(S_{u},t,p_{u}^{\text{prev}}))

(7)

where
r_{u,t}
is the chain-of-thought reasoning and
p_{u,t}
is the resulting profile summary.

2.

Preference-level Filtering
: For each pair
(p_{u,t},t)
, we use a judgment model
\mathcal{J}
to predict the user’s choice in
t=(p_{\text{item}},n_{\text{item}},c)
. The prediction
\hat{y}_{u,t}
is compared to the ground-truth choice
y_{u,t}
.

\hat{y}_{u,t}=\mathcal{J}(\text{prompt}(p_{u,t},t))

(8)

We form a set of consistent profiles
\mathcal{P}_{u}=\{p_{u,t}|\hat{y}_{u,t}=y_{u,t},\forall t\in T_{u}\}
. If the size of
\mathcal{P}_{u}
is smaller than a predefined threshold, we discard this user and proceed to the next user from Step 1.

3.

Merging
: The consistent profiles in
\mathcal{P}_{u}
(and their corresponding reasonings
\mathcal{R}_{u}
) are merged by a separate merging function, also implemented via a teacher LLM
\mathcal{G}_{\text{merge}}
:

p_{u}^{\text{merged}}=\mathcal{G}_{\text{merge}}(\text{prompt}(\mathcal{P}_{u},\mathcal{R}_{u}))

(9)

4.

User-level Filtering
: We compute an accuracy score for the merged profile
p_{u}^{\text{merged}}
by evaluating its predictive performance on a set of validation targets
\tilde{T}_{u}\subseteq T_{u}
.

\displaystyle\text{Acc}_{u}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\tilde{T}_{u}|}\sum_{t\in\tilde{T}_{u}}\mathbb{I}(

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\text{prompt}(p_{u}^{\text{merged}},t))=y_{u,t})

We only retain the user’s data for SFT if
\text{Acc}_{u}\geq\lambda
, where
\lambda
is a quality threshold (e.g., 0.8).

This rigorous, multi-stage process ensures that the synthesized profiles are accurate, comprehensive, and behaviorally grounded, providing a high-quality dataset for SFT.

Appendix B
Details for RL Data Pruning

This section provides the technical details for our curriculum-based data pruning strategy, as introduced in Section
3.3
.

B.1
Pipeline Details

Model Setup.

Our method employs two autoregressive language models: a strong model,
M_{\text{strong}}
, and a weak model,
M_{\text{weak}}
. The weak model (
M_{\text{weak}}
) is a general-purpose language model with no task-specific training. The strong model (
M_{\text{strong}}
) is created by fine-tuning
M_{\text{weak}}
on the entire user interaction dataset
\mathcal{D}
. The fine-tuning objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth preferred item
p_{m}
in a binary choice context
(c_{m},p_{m},n_{m})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{strong}}=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{(H^{[0,m)},h_{m})\sim\mathcal{D}}

(11)

\displaystyle\left[\log p_{M_{\text{strong}}}(p_{m}\succ n_{m}|H^{[0,m)},c_{m})\right].

Score Definitions.

For each potential training point
(H^{[0,m)},h_{m})
, we define two scores:

1.

Tractability Score (
S_{\text{tract}}
):
This is the probability assigned by the strong model to the ground-truth preferred item. It measures how solvable the sample is for a knowledgeable model.

S_{\text{tract}}=p_{M_{\text{strong}}}(p_{m}\succ n_{m}|H^{[0,m)}_{u},c_{m}).

(12)

2.

Learning Potential Score (
S_{\text{learn}}
):
This is the log-ratio of the probabilities assigned by the strong and weak models. It quantifies the performance improvement gained through task-specific fine-tuning, indicating the sample’s reliance on learned reasoning.

S_{\text{learn}}=\log\frac{p_{M_{\text{strong}}}(p_{m}\succ n_{m}|H^{[0,m)}_{u},c_{m})}{p_{M_{\text{weak}}}(p_{m}\succ n_{m}|H^{[0,m)}_{u},c_{m})}.

(13)

Filtering and Selection.

We select our reasoning-intensive dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{reason}}
, by applying a three-step filtering process to all potential training points
(H^{[0,m)},h_{m})
:

1.

We first select those with a Learning Potential Score in the top
\alpha\%
percentile.

2.

From the remaining samples, we filter for samples within the “sweet spot” of tractability by applying both a lower and an upper bound on the Tractability Score:
\tau_{\text{low}}<S_{\text{tract}}<\tau_{\text{high}}
.

For each user history
H
containing two or more such filtered points, we rank these points in ascending order of their Tractability Score (i.e., from hardest to easiest). We then select the indices of the two hardest tractable points as our
k
and
K
to construct the final RL training instance
(H^{[0,k)},h_{k},H^{[k,K)},h_{K})
.

B.2
Detailed Implementation

During data pruning, we select “easy” examples from the AlignX and MIND datasets, while for the Amazon dataset we sample comparatively more challenging examples. We use Qwen3-1.7B and its fine-tuned variant as the “weak” and “strong” models to calculate scores. At filtering step, we first select the top
\alpha\%
of examples according to
S_{\text{learn}}
, then apply an additional interval-based filter on
S_{\text{tract}}
, and finally take the top (easiest) or bottom (hardest)
c\%
of the remaining examples according to
S_{\text{tract}}
. The specific sampling configurations are summarized in Table
7
. Under a fixed training budget, we proportionally sample approximately 112K instances from the original datasets according to their relative sizes. The detailed dataset statistics are reported in Table

.

Appendix D
Transfer Data Synthesis

This section explains the benchmark synthesis details for the transfer experiments, as introduced in Section
4.3
.

D.1
Cross-domain Transfer

We construct cross-domain scenario to evaluate the task transferability of the generated preferences. Specifically, we build an augmented dataset by matching user histories between MIND and AlignX using dense embeddings model. Specifically, we use the Qwen3-Embedding-8B
9
9

9

https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-8B
model, implemented via the HuggingFace
transformers
library, to encode user histories into dense vector representations.

For each user, we first convert the history into a single string using a prompt formatting. We then feed this formatted string into Qwen3-Embedding-8B and obtain one embedding vector per user. This procedure is applied independently to all users in MIND and AlignX. After encoding, we obtain one embedding for each of the
A
users in MIND and of the
B
users in AlignX. We stack the user embeddings from MIND into a matrix of size
A\times d
and those from AlignX into a matrix of size
B\times d
, where
d
is the embedding dimension. We then compute pairwise similarities between MIND and AlignX users by multiplying the two matrices, yielding an
A\times B
similarity matrix. Based on these similarity scores, we select the 1,000 most similar user pairs across the two datasets.

For each selected user pair (one from MIND and one from AlignX), we create two new testing examples by swapping their targets while keeping their own histories. From 1,000 similar user pairs, this procedure produces 2,000 new user-level instances to evaluate cross-domain transfer performance.

D.2
Multi-interest Transfer

Beyond evaluating the overall transferability of user-level preferences, we also examine the model’s generalization ability in interest-level transfer within a single user history. In realistic settings, a user may maintain multiple concurrent preferences across different platforms or domains. To approximate this setting, we construct synthetic multi-interest users by merging histories from datasets with low topical overlap while explicitly avoiding preference conflicts, and then assess whether the preference inference model can retain all parallel key preferences in the fused histories.

For the response selection task, we fuse user histories from MovieLens into AlignX. Specifically, for each user in AlignX, we associate a random counterpart user from MovieLens. For each such user pair, we randomly sample a subset of the MovieLens user’s historical interactions, where the subset size is controlled by a “noise intensity” parameter. We define “noise intensity” as the proportion of sampled interactions that are irrelevant to the target among all interactions in that user’s original history. We then insert the sampled noisy interactions into the original interaction sequence at random positions while preserving their relative order, resulting in noisy user histories for evaluation. For the response generation task, we first pair dialogues for different topics in HiCUPID dataset, and then apply the same procedure.
