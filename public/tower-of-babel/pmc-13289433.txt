Pre-treatment prediction of microsatellite instability in colon cancer: a nomogram model combining clinicopathological features and pre-treatment CT-based radiomics
Authors: Meng Wei, Congzhen Jia, Ying Zhang, Peng You, Weizhi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDetermining microsatellite instability (MSI) status in colon cancer is crucial for selecting treatment strategies in advanced stages. Thus, accurately identifying MSI status before treatment is essential.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the utility of nomogram model that integrates clinicopathological indicators and pre-treatment CT-based radiomics features for predicting DNA mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) /microsatellite instability (MSI) status in colon cancer prior to treatment.MethodsA total of 201 colon cancer patients who had undergone preoperative contrast-enhanced CT scans were categorized into the dMMR/MSI group or the proficient Mismatch Repair (pMMR)/Microsatellite Stable (MSS) group based on surgical pathology results. Multivariate logistic regression was applied to identify independent clinical predictors. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied for dimensionality reduction of radiomics features. Clinical, radiomics, and nomogram models were established through logistic regression analysis based on the risk clinicopathological predictors and radiomics features.ResultsMultivariate logistic regression identified patient age, pericentric lymph node metastasis, and CA72-4 levels as significant (P < 0.05). Four radiomic features were selected to construct the radiomics model. In the training set, the AUC values for the clinical model, Rad score, and combined model were 0.86, 0.89, and 0.94, respectively, and in the validation set, 0.81, 0.89, and 0.91, respectively. The Delong test showed the nomogram model outperformed both the clinical model and Rad score (P < 0.05). The calibration curve confirmed good consistency between predicted and actual outcomes for dMMR/MSI colon cancer using the combined model.ConclusionThe nomogram model, which combines clinicopathological features with pre-treatment CT-based radiomics features, demonstrates greater predictive accuracy for dMMR/MSI colon cancer than the standalone clinical and radiomics models.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common clinical condition with significant global burden [1–3], and it ranks among the top five most common cancers in China [4]. Currently, colorectal carcinogenesis is known to involve at least two distinct molecular pathways. The first is chromosomal instability (CIN), which involves persistent numerical or structural chromosomal abnormalities during cell division that drive tumor progression [5]. The second mechanism is deficient DNA mismatch repair (dMMR), which results in microsatellite instability (MSI) [6]. Microsatellites are short tandem repeat sequences in DNA that are prone to replication errors [7]. In normal cells, an intact mismatch repair (MMR) system promptly detects and corrects these errors to maintain microsatellite stability (MSS) [8]. However, in some tumors, replication errors escape detection, leading to insertions or deletions in MMR genes and altering the length of microsatellite sequences, resulting in MSI [9].

MSI represents a hypermutator phenotype caused by MMR system defects [10]. Inactivation of MMR genes commonly results from promoter hypermethylation of MLH1 or pathogenic mutations in genes such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2. It is important to note that germline pathogenic variants in these MMR genes are the cause of Lynch syndrome, which is the most common hereditary colorectal cancer syndrome. Therefore, the identification of MSI in a colorectal tumor, especially in patients with early-onset disease or a significant family history, should raise suspicion for Lynch syndrome and warrants further genetic evaluation [11]. According to the Bethesda guidelines, high-level microsatellite instability (MSI-H) is defined as instability at two or more loci, low-level microsatellite instability (MSI-L) as instability at one locus, and microsatellite stability (MSS) as no instability at any locus [7].

MSI status serves as a crucial predictive biomarker for prognosis and treatment guidance in colorectal cancer. First, dMMR-driven MSI tumors generate numerous immunogenic neoantigens, making them highly responsive to immunotherapy [12]. Second, compared to MSS tumors, MSI cancers generally exhibit lower invasiveness, reduced metastatic potential, and better prognosis, establishing MSI as a favorable prognostic marker [13]. Finally, early MSI testing is critical for therapeutic decision-making and for identifying individuals at risk for hereditary cancer syndromes like Lynch syndrome [14]. Per NCCN guidelines, MSI colorectal cancer patients derive little benefit from conventional 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy [15], but due to increased expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, they show enhanced sensitivity to PD-1 inhibitor therapy [16].

Accurate determination of MSI status before treatment is essential for personalized therapy planning. Currently, common clinical methods include immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MMR protein expression and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based analysis of microsatellite loci in surgical specimens. However, IHC results can be influenced by tissue fixation conditions, leading to false negatives, while PCR testing is both expensive and invasive. Thus, there is a clear clinical need for a cost-effective, efficient, and non-invasive method to predict MSI status in colorectal cancer patients, which would also facilitate broader screening for Lynch syndrome [17, 18].

Radiomics is a high-throughput approach that facilitates quantitative assessment of medical imaging by extracting and analyzing large-scale data, overcoming the limitations of qualitative and subjective evaluations by radiologists [19]. Notably, significant differences exist between rectal cancer (especially lower rectal cancer) and colon cancer in terms of carcinogenic mechanisms, clinical behavior, management strategies, imaging characteristics, and prognosis [20]. Therefore, focusing specifically on colon cancer is justified. The aim of this study is to develop a pre-treatment model for predicting dMMR/MSI status in colon cancer by leveraging clinicopathological data and radiomic features, thereby providing a novel, non-invasive approach to guide personalized disease management.

Patients and methods

Patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with pathologically confirmed colon cancer from May 2021 to May 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent enhanced CT scans within two weeks prior to surgery; (2) no neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) or other targeted treatments before surgery; (3) clearly defined postoperative pathological dMMR/MSI or pMMR/MSS status; (4) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) unclear lesion display on enhanced CT images or inability to meet study requirements due to motion artifacts; (2) a history of other malignant tumors; (3) a time interval of more than two weeks between CT examination and surgery; (4) unclear postoperative dMMR/MSI or pMMR/MSS status or incomplete clinical data. A total of 201 patients were included, comprising 118 males and 83 females, aged 51 to 94 years, with a mean age of 63.18 ± 1.80 years. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the first affiliated hospital of Jinzhou medical university .In this study, stratified random sampling was employed to divide the patients into training and validation sets at a ratio of 7:3, using MSI status as the stratification variable to ensure a balanced distribution of MSI between the two groups. (Fig. 1 illustrates the patient recruitment process and the overall research workflow).

Data and image collection

Before the examination, patients followed a low-residue diet and fasted for over 8 h. Informed consent was obtained 15 min prior to the procedure, and patients received breathing training to reduce motion artifacts. Patients were positioned supine with their arms crossed above their forehead. A Philips Brilliance 256-slice iCT scanner (Ultra-Fast CT) was utilized for imaging. The scanning range covered the area from the diaphragm to the pubic symphysis. Scanning parameters were set to 120 kVp tube voltage and 120 mAs tube current (adaptive technology), with a slice thickness and interval of 5 mm and a matrix size of 512 × 512.Iodixanol, with an iodine concentration of 280 ~ 350 mg/ml, was administered as the contrast agent for enhanced imaging. The contrast agent was dosed at 1.0 ml per kilogram of body weight. Following abdominal and pelvic plain CT scans, 15 ml of saline was injected prior to administering the contrast agent. Enhanced imaging began at 25 ~ 30 s (arterial phase), 60 ~ 80 s (portal venous phase), and 120 ~ 150 s (delayed phase) post-injection of the contrast agent. Flush the tubing with 30 ml of normal saline after completing the contrast-enhanced scan.

CT image segmentation and radiomics feature extraction and selection

Using 3D Slicer software (https://www.slicer.org/, version 5.7.0) to semi-automatically outline the tumor. Two radiologists (Dr. W.M. and Dr. Y.P.), each with three years of clinical diagnostic experience, manually outlined the ROI along the lesion’s edge on several key slices. Both radiologists were blinded to the patients’ MSI status as well as to all clinical and pathological information at the time of ROI delineation. To minimize the influence of perilesional confounding factors, the anatomical boundaries of the ROI were strictly defined as follows: the inner boundary was closely aligned with the lesion margin to exclude intraluminal contaminants (e.g., feces, intraluminal gas, and fluids); the outer boundary was maintained at a distance of 1–2 mm from the lesion edge to avoid incorporating surrounding adipose tissue, vascular structures, and serosal layers. Additionally, the ROI was verified and adjusted in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes to ensure three-dimensional anatomical accuracy. Next, the auto-fusion function of 3D Slicer was applied to generate the volume of interest (VOI). (Fig. 2 illustrates the contouring process for sigmoid colon cancer). The contoured tumor slices were saved in NRRD format. Before extracting features, the image resolution was adjusted so that each voxel (3D pixel) had equal dimensions of 1 unit in each direction (x, y, z). The open-source Python package PyRadiomics version 2.0.1 (https://github.com/AIM-Harvard/pyradiomics) was used to extract radiomics features. This toolkit was employed to extract first-order features, morphological features, and texture features from medical images for further analysis. The ROIs from all image slices were automatically merged to generate the 3D volume of interest (VOI) for the entire tumor. Before feature extraction, voxel size resampling and grayscale intensity normalization were performed on the images. All radiomic features were standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 before being entered into the LASSO regression model to ensure scale independence and avoid coefficient bias. To reduce the effects of variations in image quality, voxel size resampling was first performed to achieve a uniform spatial resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using B-spline interpolation. Grayscale intensity normalization was then applied to standardize voxel values across all patients by transforming the intensity values to a range of µ ± 3σ, where µ represents the mean intensity and σ the standard deviation of the tumor ROI.

To assess inter-observer agreement, a randomly selected subset of 50 cases (approximately 20% of the total cohort) was used to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The consistency of regions of interest (ROIs) delineated by the two radiologists was quantitatively evaluated using the ICC. Radiomics features with high consistency (ICC > 0.8) were retained for further analysis. The ICC values for all radiomic features exceeded 0.85, with mean ICC values of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95) for first-order features and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.93) for texture features, indicating excellent inter-observer agreement. Immunohistochemical criteria

In this study, the status of MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) was evaluated using immunohistochemical staining. Based on the immunohistochemical staining results of MMR proteins in postoperative tissue samples, patients(n=201) were classified into either the MSI group(n༝55) or the MSS group(n༝146). For the four MMR proteins, negative staining of one or more proteins was defined as microsatellite instability (MSI), while positive staining of all four proteins was defined as microsatellite stability (MSS) [21]. The pathological result interpretation was performed by the physician with over ten years of experience in pathological diagnosis.

Statistical methods and signature construction

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.5.6) and SPSS (version 23.0). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and non-normally distributed variables are reported as median with interquartile range (P25 to P75). Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test (χ²). For radiomic feature selection, the Maximum Relevance and Minimum Redundancy (mRMR) algorithm was implemented in R using the mRMRe package (version 2.1.0) to identify features highly associated with the outcome while minimizing redundancy among predictors. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied via the glmnet package (version 4.1.3) in R to further reduce dimensionality and select features with non-zero coefficients. The resulting radiomic signature, designated as the radscore, was computed as a linear combination of the selected features weighted by their respective coefficients according to the following formula: radscore = β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + … + βₙXₙ where βₙ represents the regression coefficient and Xₙ denotes the standardized value of each radiomic feature.

Clinical model construction

The clinical independent predictors were identified by logistic univariate and multivariate regression analyses, and a clinical model construction was created using the independent predictors.

Radiomics model construction

Optimal radiomic features were determined through dimension reduction by mRMR and LASSO. The retained optimal radiomic features whose P-values were < 0.05 to develop radiomics model construction.

Nomogram(clinical-radiomics) model construction

Clinical predictors with statistical significance in univariate regression analysis and radiomic features were included in logistic multivariate regression analysis and variables with P < 0.05 were retained to build a nomogram model.

Evaluation of the three models

The predictive performance of the clinical model, radiomics model and nomogram model was evaluated using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). The Delong test was used to compare the AUC of the models. Calibration was assessed using calibration curve. Evaluate the clinical utility of the three models using the Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) curve. Finally, draw the nomogram of the optimal model in the training set.

Model development and comparison of predictive performance

The clinical model was constructed based on age and mesenteric lymph node metastasis and CA72-4. The selected radiomics features were used to construct the radscore, and a joint nomogram model was developed by integrating clinical indicators and radiomics signature to predict dMMR/MSI colon cancer, followed by internal validation. Among these, the nomogram model demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.87–0.95) in the training group and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85–0.97) in the validation group (A detailed comparison of the performance evaluation metrics of the three models is shown in Table 4). The ROC curves of the three models, showing that the diagnostic performance of the nomogram model in both the training (Fig. 7) and validation groups (Fig. 8) is superior to that of the standalone clinical model and radiomics model. The Delong test showed that the predictive performance of the clinical model and the radiomics model was significantly different from that of the nomogram model in the training group (Z = 2.213,2.026, P < 0.05). Similarly, in the validation group, the predictive performance of the clinical model and the radiomics model was significantly different from that of the joint model (Z = 2.319,1.628, P < 0.05). Calibration curves for both the training (Fig. 9) and validation groups (Fig. 10) were plotted, indicating that the predicted probabilities from the nomogram model were in good agreement with the actual probabilities. A nomogram was constructed based on the combination of clinical independent risk features and radiomics signature (Fig. 11). In the training group, the DCA curve shows that the nomogram model has practical value for predicting MSI colon cancer within the threshold range of 9% to 91% (Fig. 12). In the validation group, the DCA curve shows that the nomogram model has practical value for predicting MSI colon cancer within the threshold range of 16% to 80%(Fig. 13) The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between the predicted and observed outcomes of the nomogram model in both the training groups (Fig. 14) and validation groups (Fig. 15).

Construction and application of a nomogram for predicting

This joint nomogram model integrated clinically independent risk factors (including age, mesenteric lymph node metastasis, and CA72-4 level) with a radiomics signature-derived radscore, and was internally validated. The construction process of the nomogram was as follows: variables significantly associated with MSI in the regression model were incorporated, and their regression coefficients were proportionally converted into points, with the variable having the highest coefficient assigned 100 points. The total points were calculated as the sum of points from all variables and then mapped to the probability of MSI status through a linear transformation of the logit function. The nomogram was generated using the rms package (version 6.7) in R, and internal validation was performed via bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions.

The nomogram was developed to provide a user-friendly tool for individualized prediction of MSI status. To estimate the probability for a specific patient: 1. Identify the value for each variable (e.g., age, CA72-4 level, radscore) on its corresponding axis; 2. Draw a line vertically to the ‘Points’ axis to assign points for each variable; 3. Sum all points to obtain the total score; 4. Locate the total score on the ‘Total Points’ axis and draw a line down to the ‘Probability’ axis to read the predicted probability of MSI.

Potential overfitting assessment of the nomogram model

Although the model demonstrated high discriminative ability, we further assessed potential overfitting through the following approaches: The difference between training and validation AUC was limited (0.03), suggesting generalizable performance; Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 repetitions) was performed, yielding a narrow confidence interval for the validation AUC (0.85–0.97); Regularization via LASSO inherently reduces overfitting by penalizing complex models.

Discussion

Clinical model evaluation of microsatellite instability status in colorectal cancer

This study identified age, peritumoral lymph node metastasis, and CA72-4 levels as independent risk factors for dMMR/MSI colon cancer through multivariate logistic regression analysis of clinical pathological data. Patients with microsatellite instability (MSI) colon cancer have lower lymph node metastasis rates compared to those with microsatellite stable (MSS) colon cancer. This phenomenon may be attributed to the unique immune microenvironment of MSI tumor cells. Genes linked to interferon responses (ISG), MHC class I/II molecules, and immune cell chemokines are more highly expressed in MSI colon cancer than in MSS colon cancer [22]. This robust immune response restricts tumor cell dissemination and metastasis, thereby reducing the likelihood of lymph node involvement. Furthermore, MSI colon cancer frequently exhibit high levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [23, 24], which help prevent cancer cell spread to adjacent tissues and lymph nodes. Chen et al. [25] reported that lymph node metastasis acts as a protective factor in dMMR colorectal cancer. Similarly, research by Merok et al. [26] confirmed that dMMR colorectal cancer has a lower tendency for lymph node and distant metastasis compared to pMMR colorectal cancer. Those above research results are consistent with the conclusions of this study. The study also revealed a higher incidence of dMMR/MSI colon cancer in younger patients, likely due to the presence of hereditary colon cancers, such as Lynch syndrome, within this age group. Patients with Lynch syndrome, characterized by mismatch repair (MMR) system defects, typically exhibit high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) [27]. Chang et al. [28] found a significantly higher incidence of MSI-H in patients under 50, further supporting the idea that increasing age is a protective factor for MSI colorectal cancer, consistent with the findings of this study. Additionally, the study analyzed three tumor markers: CEA, CA19-9, and CA72-4. The results revealed a statistically significant difference in CA72-4 levels between dMMR/MSI and pMMR/MSS groups, with higher CA72-4 levels often observed in MSI colorectal cancer patients. This difference might be linked to the invasiveness and potential for distant metastasis of colorectal tumors. Currently, no definitive studies link serum CA72-4 levels with MSI status in colon cancer, underscoring the need for further research with larger sample sizes.

Radiomics model evaluation of microsatellite instability status in colorectal cancer

Radiomics, a data-mining method, extracts numerous quantitative features from segmented medical images, enabling the characterization of microscopic individual differences [29]. Previous radiomics studies have focused on predicting MSI status in colorectal cancer patients. Cao et al. [30] reported that radiomics models utilizing delayed-phase CT images achieved the highest accuracy in predicting MSI in colorectal cancer, likely due to the retention of contrast agents in stromal and vascular gaps. Ma et al. [31] analyzed CT images of 788 colorectal cancer patients, delineating tumors and adjacent regions (extending 5 mm beyond the tumor boundary). These findings highlight the potential of radiomics in assessing microsatellite instability in colorectal cancer patients. In this study, tumor volumes were segmented during the venous phase, and radiomic features were subsequently extracted. The radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.94) in the training group and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.76–0.94) in the validation group, demonstrating consistent performance. While several studies have explored multi-phase CT (including arterial, venous, and delayed phases) for predicting microsatellite instability in colorectal cancer [32], our study specifically utilized the venous phase based on several methodological and clinical considerations. Physiologically, the venous phase provides optimal tumor-to-background contrast and reflects peak parenchymal enhancement, which enhances the reproducibility of texture feature extraction and more accurately captures intertumoral heterogeneity associated with MSI status. Furthermore, preliminary feature stability analysis in our cohort indicated higher intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs > 0.85) for venous-phase features compared to other phases, supporting their robustness. From a clinical translation perspective, a single-phase model reduces radiation exposure, simplifies image acquisition protocols, and minimizes potential registration artifacts, improving its practicality for routine application. Although multi-phase approaches may offer complementary information, our venous phase-based model demonstrated competitive performance (AUC: 0.91–0.94), suggesting that it captures robust discriminative signals. Future multi-center studies may further validate its generalizability and explore whether incorporating arterial or delayed phases provides incremental value. Colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability possess a distinct tumor microenvironment, characterized by robust immune infiltration, reduced fibrosis, and increased mucin production. These unique biological properties are reflected in specific radiomic texture features, which can serve as independent predictors for MSI status. Our analysis identified two key texture features: Large Dependence Emphasis (LDE) and Large Area Low Gray Level Emphasis (LALGLE). LDE quantifies the spatial dependency of similar gray-level pixels over large areas. A higher LDE value, indicating a coarser or more homogeneous texture, aligns with the uniformly distributed immune infiltrates (e.g., CD8 + T cells) and the less fibrotic, more structured stroma typical of MSI tumors resulting from mismatch repair deficiency [33–35]. Conversely, LALGLE measures the prevalence of large, contiguous areas with low attenuation. Elevated LALGLE in MSI cancers may correspond to pathological features such as mucinous pools or immune-mediated necrosis, which present as larger hypodense regions on CT imaging [34, 36]. Furthermore, differences in tumor perfusion between MSI and microsatellite stable (MSS) cancers may also contribute to these distinct texture patterns [37]. Additionally, significant differences in shape features were observed, potentially attributable to the expansive growth pattern commonly seen in MSI-high tumors, contrasting with the infiltrative growth pattern of their MSS counterparts.

Clinical-radiomics combined model for evaluating microsatellite instability status in colorectal cancer

This study evaluated clinical imaging indicators and radiomic features in dMMR/MSI and pMMR/MSS colon cancer groups to construct models for predicting MSI status: a clinicopathological model, a Rad score model, and a combined model. The results showed that the combined model had the best diagnostic performance, with AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.87–0.95) in the training group and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85–0.97) in the validation group. This performance surpassed that of both the clinicopathological and radiomics-only models. While the high AUC values indicate strong predictive performance, we acknowledge that these results could raise concerns regarding overfitting. However, several factors mitigate this risk: the use of regularization methods (LASSO), feature selection stability analysis, and the consistency between training and validation performance. Nevertheless, external validation in multi-center cohorts is warranted to further confirm generalizability. Previous studies have established combined models to predict the MSI status of colorectal cancer by integrating radiomics features and clinical features. FAN et al. [38] analyzed portal venous-phase CT images from 119 stage II colorectal cancer patients and concluded that integrating clinical factors with radiomic features significantly improved predictive performance compared to clinical models alone. Bian et al. [22] constructed a clinical predictive model incorporating clinical CT features and radscore by including radiomic features extracted from arterial phase, venous phase, and delayed phase images. The results showed that the nomogram model based on the combination of clinical-CT features and three-phase combination radiomics features provides the optimal prediction of MSI status in colon cancer patients. The authors noted that rectal cancer, especially lower rectal cancer, differs significantly from colon cancer in clinical and imaging characteristics as well as prognosis. As a result, rectal cancer is typically evaluated using MRI [39]. Enhanced CT is recommended by guidelines as the first-line imaging examination method for the assessment of colon cancer patients’ conditions [40], which explains why this study focused solely on colon cancer cases. To enhance clinical utility, this study constructed a clinical-radiomics nomogram by combining radiomic features with clinicopathological data. This practical scoring system enables physicians to predict MSI status in colon cancer patients prior to immunohistochemistry, aiding in the development of more tailored and effective treatment plans [41].

Limitations of the study

This retrospective study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces inherent risks of retrospective and selection biases during patient enrollment. Second, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively limited sample size. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our findings. Third, although two radiologists performed manual segmentation of the images and consistency was assessed with high ICC values, inter-observer variations in delineating regions of interest (ROIs) remained unavoidable. Such discrepancies could particularly affect features derived from textures and wavelets in regions with poorly defined tumor boundaries or complex anatomical surroundings. To minimize these inconsistencies, multiplanar verification and consensus reviews were conducted for ambiguous areas. Nonetheless, in subsequent research, we aim to incorporate automated segmentation software to decrease reliance on manual outlining and improve reproducibility. Finally, to ensure a balanced distribution of MSI status between the training and validation sets, stratified random sampling was applied using MSI as a stratification variable, dividing the cohort in a 7:3 ratio. Moving forward, we will explore automatic segmentation techniques to further reduce manual segmentation-related errors.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a clinical pathological indicator-radiomics nomogram model, combining clinical and pathological indicators of colorectal cancer patients, to predict the microsatellite instability (MSI) status of colon cancer patients before treatment. The research results show that the nomogram, which integrates three clinical pathological indicators—age, lymph node metastasis status, and serum CA72-4 levels with four optimal radiomics features, can be routinely used as a quantitative approach to predict microsatellite instability (MSI) status in colorectal cancer before treatment. This nomogram assists clinicians in designing personalized treatment plans for patients as early as possible, even before immunohistochemistry results are available, and in assessing patient prognosis. However, this study did not involve multi-center research, which will be an attempt in further investigations.
