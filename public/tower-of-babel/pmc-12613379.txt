Comparative study of corneal endothelial morphology and central corneal thickness in diabetic and non-diabetic patients at tertiary care centre
Authors: Surbhi Sharma, Veshal Vinod Madan, Susmita Banerjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus is a chronic condition that affects various organs, including the eyes. Corneal endothelial dysfunction, a key factor in ocular health, can be influenced by long-term diabetes. Early detection of corneal changes, such as central corneal thickness (CCT), endothelial cell density (CD), coefficient of variation (CV), and hexagonality (HEX), may provide insights into diabetic ocular complications. This study evaluates the differences in corneal endothelial parameters between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.AimTo compare the corneal endothelial parameters, including CCT, CD, CV, and HEX, in diabetic patients and non-diabetic controls, and assess the correlation between these parameters and the duration of diabetes.MethodologyThis cross-sectional comparative study was conducted at a tertiary eye care hospital with 100 eyes from 50 diabetic patients and 100 eyes from 50 age-matched non-diabetic controls. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation, including intraocular pressure measurement, refraction, best corrected visual acuity, and slit-lamp biomicroscopy. Corneal endothelial imaging was performed using non-contact specular microscopy to assess CCT, CD, CV, and HEX. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsIn our study, diabetic patients had significantly higher central corneal thickness (CCT) in both eyes (OD: 593.9 ± 60.2 μm; OS: 610.5 ± 60.7 μm) compared to non-diabetics (OD: 550.9 ± 66.1 μm; OS: 593.9 ± 60.2 μm). Hexagonality (HEX) was lower in diabetics (OD: 43.2 ± 12.5%; OS: 42.3 ± 15.8%) vs. non-diabetics (OD: 52.1 ± 10.5%; OS: 41.7 ± 11.9%). HbA1C was significantly higher in diabetics (7.31 ± 1.13%) than non-diabetics (5.48 ± 0.67%; p = 0.012). Cell density (CD) and coefficient of variation (CV) showed no significant differences.ConclusionDiabetic patients exhibit significant changes in corneal endothelial parameters, which may indicate early endothelial dysfunction. Regular monitoring of these parameters could provide valuable insights into the ocular health of diabetic individuals.Keywords: Diabetes, Corneal endothelium, Central corneal thickness, Endothelial cell density

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has developed as one of the most important worldwide health challenges of the 21st century. With its progressively increasing incidence, it poses a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Among the different types of diabetes, Type II diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is now recognized as a major public health concern, particularly in developing countries where lifestyle transitions and urbanization are accelerating its spread [1, 2]. According to current estimates, over 285 million people globally are affected by diabetes, and this number is projected to rise in the coming decades, particularly in low and middle-income nations [3].

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic condition characterized by raised fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels. This hyperglycaemic state results from deficient insulin secretion, resistance to insulin action, or a combination of both. Persistent hyperglycaemia in diabetes is known to cause microvascular and macrovascular complications, leading to long-term damage and functional impairment of multiple organs and tissues [4]. Among the various systems affected, the visual system is significantly vulnerable, and diabetes has been shown to affect nearly all structures of the eye, including the retina, lens, and importantly, the cornea.

The cornea plays a vital role in sustaining ocular transparency and refractive function. The corneal endothelium, in particular, is a monolayer of hexagonal cells that lines the posterior surface of the cornea, derived from the neural crest during embryologic development [5]. These endothelial cells are non-regenerative in humans; therefore, any loss or morphological alteration in these cells can lead to corneal edema and visual deterioration. At birth, the endothelial cell density (ECD) is approximately 3000 cells/mm², which gradually declines with age at an estimated rate of 0.6% per year. However, in patients with diabetes mellitus, this rate of decline is believed to be accelerated due to chronic metabolic stress and microvascular damage [5, 6].

Several corneal abnormalities have been observed in diabetic individuals, including recurrent corneal erosions, punctate epithelial keratopathy, persistent epithelial defects, impaired corneal sensitivity, corneal ulcers, delayed epithelial wound healing, and superficial keratitis [7]. Among these, corneal endothelial dysfunction is of particular interest because it can compromise the pump and barrier functions of the endothelium, affecting corneal hydration and transparency. A growing body of evidence has shown that diabetes mellitus induces significant changes in the morphology and function of the corneal endothelium [8].

These changes include a reduction in endothelial cell density, decreased percentage of hexagonal cells (a marker of pleomorphism), polymegathism (increased variation in cell size, measured by the coefficient of variation of cell area), and increased central corneal thickness (CCT) [9]. The increased CCT in diabetics may reflect subclinical corneal edema due to endothelial dysfunction. Such changes have clinical significance, particularly in the preoperative assessment for intraocular surgeries like cataract extraction, as compromised corneal endothelium may increase the risk of postoperative corneal decompensation [10].

Despite numerous studies from different parts of the world, the relationship between diabetes and corneal endothelial morphology and thickness remains an area of active investigation, with variations observed based on the duration of diabetes, glycemic control, and presence of diabetic complications like retinopathy.

Aim & objective

To compare the corneal endothelial morphology and central corneal thickness (CCT) in diabetic and non-diabetic patients using specular microscopy and assess their correlation with glycemic control with respect to gender. (HbA1c levels).

Material & method

The present cross-sectional comparative study was conducted at a tertiary care centre over a period extending from June 2024 to March 2025. This study involved 100 participants, with 50 patients in each group. Group A consisted of 50 known diabetic patients (100 eyes), while Group B included 50 non-diabetic individuals (100 eyes), making a total of 200 eyes examined. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee under approval number DPU/Ph.D./Med/223b/2024.

Participants were recruited after obtaining informed written consent. Baseline demographic data including age, gender, duration of diabetes, coexisting systemic illnesses, and current medical treatments were documented for all subjects. The inclusion criteria were adults aged between 40 and 60 years, with Group A participants diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [11]. Additionally, diabetic patients included in the study were required to have a minimum disease duration of five years. Only individuals with clear corneas—free from scarring or opacities—were considered eligible.

Participants were excluded from the study if they had a history of ocular trauma, prior ocular surgeries, known corneal dystrophies, or were contact lens users within the past six months. Pregnant or lactating women were also excluded to avoid hormonal variations that might affect corneal physiology and confound the study results.

All subjects underwent a thorough ophthalmological evaluation, which included the measurement of intraocular pressure (IOP) using a non-contact tonometer, refraction with an autorefractometer, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessment using the Snellen chart. The anterior segment of the eye was examined using slit-lamp biomicroscope (Haag-Streit), allowing for detailed visualization of the corneal surface and anterior chamber.

Corneal endothelial imaging was performed using a non-contact specular microscope, which captured images in automatic mode, focusing on the central corneal zone. From the images obtained, key endothelial parameters were analysed, including endothelial cell density (ECD), central corneal thickness (CCT), coefficient of variation (CV), and hexagonality (HEX). Each eye was scanned three times, and the median value from the most representative image was selected for data analysis to ensure accuracy and consistency. Both the right eye (OD) and left eye (OS) of each participant were independently evaluated.

A dilated fundus examination was performed on all participants using tropicamide 0.8% and phenylephrine 5% eye drops to allow for posterior segment evaluation. To maintain consistency and reduce inter-observer variability, a single experienced examiner conducted all fundus evaluations.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were systematically entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS software version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables such as age, duration of diabetes, HbA1C, central corneal thickness (CCT), endothelial cell density (ECD), coefficient of variation (CV), and hexagonality (HEX) were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables like gender were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-square test (χ² test), independent sample t-test (for normally distributed data) was used to assess associations between categorical variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 200 eyes from 100 patients were included in this study. Fifty patients in a diabetes study group and 50 patients in nondiabetic control group.

In the non-diabetic group, although females also showed slightly higher mean CCT than males in both OD (554.2 ± 65.7 μm vs. 545.3 ± 66.4 μm) and OS (549.8 ± 65.2 μm vs. 552.1 ± 67.0 μm), the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.23 for OD and p = 0.71 for OS) (Table 2).

Regarding cell density (CD), diabetic females had marginally higher values than males (OD: 2634 ± 330 vs. 2560 ± 310; OS: 2675 ± 343 vs. 2603 ± 340), but the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.119 for OD and p = 0.16 for OS). A similar non-significant pattern was observed in the non-diabetic group, with females showing slightly higher CD in both eyes compared to males (Table 2).

The CV % there were no significant gender differences in either group. In diabetics, males had a CV of 42.3 ± 12.1 in OD and 35.6 ± 9.1 in OS, while females had 40.5 ± 11.9 in OD and 33.4 ± 8.3 in OS (p > 0.05). In the non-diabetic group, the CV values between males and females were also comparable and statistically non-significant (Table 2).

The hexagonality (HEX), diabetic females had slightly higher HEX percentages than males (OD: 43.1 ± 12.5 vs. 41.2 ± 13.0; OS: 43.5 ± 15.1 vs. 41.0 ± 14.5), but these differences did not reach statistical significance. Similar findings were noted in the non-diabetic group, where HEX values between males and females were closely matched with no significant variation (Table 2).

In terms of central corneal thickness (CCT), diabetic patients exhibited significantly higher values compared to non-diabetics. The mean CCT in OD was 593.9 ± 60.2 μm for diabetics versus 550.98 ± 66.06 μm for non-diabetics. Similarly, in OS, diabetics had a CCT of 610.53 ± 60.73 μm compared to 593.9 ± 60.2 μm in non-diabetics. This suggests that diabetes is associated with increased corneal thickness.

For endothelial cell density (CD), diabetics showed lower values compared to non-diabetics. In OD, the mean CD was 2598.02 ± 324.93 cells/mm² in diabetics, while it was 2708.88 ± 240.52 cells/mm² in non-diabetics. Similarly, in OS, diabetics had a CD of 2636.51 ± 341.46 cells/mm² versus 2652 ± 260 cells/mm² in non-diabetics, indicating a reduction in endothelial cell count among diabetics.

Regarding the coefficient of variation (CV), which reflects the variability in cell size (polymegathism), diabetics showed higher CV values than non-diabetics. In OD, diabetics had a CV of 41.36 ± 12.45 compared to 34.26 ± 20.65 in non-diabetics. For OS, the CV was 34.30 ± 23.90% in diabetics versus 29 ± 6.87 in non-diabetics, indicating greater endothelial stress and cell size variation in the diabetic group.

The hexagonality (HEX), representing the percentage of hexagonal cells and indicating cellular uniformity, was lower in diabetics. In OD, HEX was 43.24 ± 12.45 in diabetics and 52.10 ± 10.50 in non-diabetics. Similarly, in OS, diabetics had a HEX of 42.32 ± 15.76, slightly higher than 41.70 ± 11.90 in non-diabetics for OS, but overall lower in OD. This suggests that diabetes may contribute to loss of normal endothelial cell shape (pleomorphism) (Table 3).

Discussion

Corneal endothelial cell morphology not only represents the quantitative parameters such as endothelial cell density, but also provides qualitative insights into functional status by assessing variation in cell size (polymegathism) and cell shape (pleomorphism). These morphological changes, along with central corneal thickness, serve as important indicators for evaluating the impact of diabetes on corneal health. The present study aimed to compare the corneal endothelial morphology and central corneal thickness (CCT) between diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

In the present study, the mean age of participants in the diabetic group was 56.41 ± 3.86 years, which was slightly higher than that in the non-diabetic group (54.58 ± 5.45 years). The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.084), indicating that the two groups were well age matched.

Our findings are in line with those reported by Gotekar et al., where the mean age of diabetics (Group A) was 58.56 ± 6.79 years and that of non-diabetics (Group B) was 60.58 ± 8.61 years. Similarly, both groups in their study were comparable in terms of age, strengthening the validity of their comparisons [12]. On the other hand, the study by Storr-Paulsen et al. included an older population, with mean ages of 72.1 ± 11.0 years for diabetics and 75.6 ± 8.9 years for non-diabetics [13]. The older age group in their study is likely to demonstrate age-related endothelial changes in addition to diabetes-related effects, which may limit direct comparability with our findings.

In the gender distribution, our study showed a slightly higher proportion of females in the non-diabetic group (70%) compared to the diabetic group (52%). Gotekar et al. reported a nearly equal male-to-female ratio in both groups (1.27:1 in Group A and 1.28:1 in Group B) [12]. Kumari et al. observed a different pattern, with a marked male predominance among diabetics (2.33:1) and a female predominance among non-diabetics (0.28:1) [14].

Thus, while our study and that of Gotekar et al. reflect broadly comparable age distribution between groups, there are differences in gender distribution across studies [12]. These variations highlight the influence of regional and demographic factors on study populations. Importantly, the relatively balanced age and gender distribution in our study enhances the reliability of comparisons between diabetic and non-diabetic patients with respect to corneal endothelial morphology and central corneal thickness.

In the present study, the mean duration of diabetes among patients in the diabetic group was 9.15 ± 8.15 years, indicating that the cohort largely consisted of individuals with a long-standing disease.

The mean HbA1c in the diabetic group was 7.31 ± 1.13%, which was significantly higher than that in the non-diabetic group (5.48 ± 0.67%, p = 0.012, Independent t-test). This difference is expected, as HbA1c reflects long-term glycemic control, and values above 6.5% are diagnostic of diabetes. The elevated HbA1c in our diabetic cohort also suggests that many participants had suboptimal glycemic control. Poor glycemic control, together with longer duration of diabetes, can accelerate endothelial cell loss, increase polymegathism and pleomorphism, and influence central corneal thickness.

In the present study, the mean central corneal thickness (CCT) was significantly higher in diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic controls. Specifically, the mean CCT in diabetics was 593.9 ± 60.2 μm (OD) and 610.53 ± 60.73 μm (OS) while in non-diabetics it was 550.98/± 66.06 μm (OD) and 593.9 ± 60.2 μm (OS) (p < 0.001). This finding indicates that diabetes is associated with increased corneal thickness, which is consistent with the hypothesis that chronic hyperglycemia leads to endothelial dysfunction, altered hydration control, and stromal swelling.

When analyzed by gender, CCT values were significantly higher in female diabetics compared to male diabetics in both eyes (OD: 605.2 ± 58.1 μm vs. 580.5 ± 60.2 μm, p = 0.041; OS: 627.6 ± 59.3 μm vs. 590.7 ± 62.1 μm, p = 0.027). However, in the non-diabetic group, no significant gender differences were observed. The diabetes not only affects the functional integrity of the endothelium but also manifests as structural corneal changes.

Our results are in agreement with those reported by Gotekar et al., Storr-Paulsen et al., Kumari et al., Abdulghani et al., who also found that diabetic patients had significantly thicker corneas compared to non-diabetic controls [12–15]. These consistent findings across different populations support the evidence that diabetes alters corneal endothelial pump function, leading to increased corneal hydration and thickness., Choo et al. reported no significant difference in mean CCT between diabetics and controls. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in study design, sample size, duration of diabetes, glycemic control, or ethnic variations in corneal thickness [3].

In the present study, analysis of corneal endothelial parameters between males and females in both groups revealed that there were no statistically significant gender differences in endothelial cell density (ECD), coefficient of variation (CV), or hexagonality (HEX). However, central corneal thickness (CCT) was significantly higher in female diabetics compared to male diabetics in both eyes (OD: p = 0.041, OS: p = 0.027), whereas no such gender difference was observed in the non-diabetic group. This suggests that diabetes-related corneal thickening may be more pronounced in females.

With respect to endothelial cell density (ECD), our study showed lower mean values in diabetics compared to non-diabetics across both eyes, though the gender-based differences were not significant. These findings are in line with the study by Gotekar et al., who reported significantly lower mean ECD in diabetics (2449.48 ± 210.52 cells/mm²) than in non-diabetics (2581.56 ± 233.03 cells/mm²) [12]. Similarly, Sudhir et al. also found reduced ECD in diabetics (2550 ± 326) compared to non-diabetics (2634 ± 256; p = 0.001) [16]. Comparable results were demonstrated by Taşlı et al. and Pont et al., further supporting the observation that chronic hyperglycemia contributes to endothelial cell loss [4, 17].

Regarding coefficient of variation (CV), our study revealed higher mean CV values in diabetics compared to non-diabetics, indicating increased polymegathism, although the differences did not reach statistical significance in our sample. This trend is consistent with the findings of Gotekar et al., who reported significantly higher CV in diabetics (38.32 ± 2.43%) than in non-diabetics (35.48 ± 2.55%), and with Choo et al., who also observed significantly elevated CV values in diabetics [3, 12]. This suggests that diabetes is associated with increased variability in cell size, reflecting endothelial stress and compensatory remodelling.

In terms of hexagonality (HEX), our study demonstrated lower values in OD than OS in diabetics compared to non-diabetics, although the gender-based differences within each group were not significant. This observation is consistent with the results of Gotekar et al., who reported reduced hexagonality in diabetics (51.24 ± 4.83%) versus non-diabetics (54.12 ± 5.28%) [12]. The findings are also supported by the meta-analysis by Zhang K et al., which concluded that diabetes mellitus is associated with a reduction in both ECD and hexagonality, along with an increase in CV [8].

Taken together, our results align with the majority of published literature, which consistently reports that diabetic patients exhibit lower ECD, higher CV, and reduced hexagonality compared to non-diabetic controls. These changes reflect progressive endothelial cell loss, polymegathism, and pleomorphism, all of which compromise endothelial function and contribute to increased corneal thickness observed in diabetic patients.

The present study compared corneal endothelial parameters between diabetic (DM) and non-diabetic (non-DM) groups. The mean duration of diabetes was 9.15 ± 8.15 years. The development of many of the diabetic complications is related to the duration of the disease and the degree of metabolic dysregulation [14].

In both eyes (OD and OS), central corneal thickness (CCT) was higher in diabetics (593.9 μm OD, 610.53 μm OS) than in non-diabetics (550.98 μm OD, 593.9 μm OS). The endothelial cell density (CD) was lower in diabetics (2598–2636 cells/mm²) compared to non-diabetics (2708–2652 cells/mm²). Diabetics also showed higher coefficient of variation (CV) (greater polymegathism) and lower hexagonality (HEX) (greater pleomorphism), indicating endothelial morphological changes.

This observation is consistent with the findings of Storr-Paulsen et al., Abdulghani et al., and Kumari et al., who also reported increased corneal thickness in diabetics. The higher CCT in diabetics is attributed to endothelial dysfunction and reduced pump efficiency, leading to increased stromal hydration [13–15].

Sudhir et al. and Gao F. et al. have reported that in diabetes, the Krebs cycle activity slows down, leading to reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Since ATP is essential for maintaining the endothelial pump function, this reduction compromises endothelial cell activity, resulting in morphological alterations and increased corneal permeability [16, 18]. These metabolic changes explain the findings in our study, where diabetic patients demonstrated increased corneal thickness, reduced endothelial cell density, greater polymegathism, and decreased hexagonality compared to non-diabetic individuals.

Limitations of the study

Firstly, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings to the broader diabetic population. A larger cohort across different demographics could provide a more comprehensive understanding of corneal endothelial alterations associated with diabetes. Secondly, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care centre, which may introduce selection bias and limit the external validity of the results. Participants from different geographic regions or healthcare settings may exhibit variations in clinical characteristics that were not captured in this analysis. Lastly, the cross-sectional design of the study allows only for observation of associations at a specific point in time and does not permit conclusions regarding causality. Therefore, while changes in corneal endothelial parameters were noted in relation to diabetes, it remains unclear whether these are direct effects of disease duration or influenced by other confounding factors. Longitudinal studies are recommended to better establish temporal and causal relationships.

Conclusion

The present study provides valuable insights into the impact of diabetes on corneal endothelial morphology and thickness. The findings demonstrate significant changes in corneal parameters such as CCT, CD, CV, and HEX in diabetics compared to non-diabetics. These changes reflect endothelial dysfunction and corneal thinning, which are consistent with findings in existing literature. The results highlight the importance of monitoring corneal parameters in diabetic patients, particularly those with long-standing or poorly controlled diabetes. Further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of these changes and to investigate potential interventions to prevent or mitigate corneal endothelial damage in diabetic patients.
