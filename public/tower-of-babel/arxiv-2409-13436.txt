Barnabás Szabó

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

In a short but very elegant paper
[
11
]
, Rudnick and Soundararajan developed a method to give lower bounds for moments of families of
L
-functions, which are believed to be optimal up to constants. In particular, they show that if
k
is a natural number and
q
is a prime, then

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}|L(1/2,\chi)|^{2k}\gg_{k}(\log q)^{k^{2}},

where
\chi
runs through the set of non-trivial Dirichlet characters mod
q
, and
L(s,\chi)
is the Dirichlet
L
-function associated to
\chi
. The method of proof can be described as follows. For each character
\chi
mod
q
, one finds a suitable proxy object
A(\chi)\geq 0
, and applies Hölder’s inequality in the form (assuming
k\geq 2
)

\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}|L(1/2,\chi)|^{2k}\Big)^{1/k}\cdot\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}A(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\Big)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}|L(1/2,\chi)|^{2}A(\chi).

If we choose
A(\chi)
, so that we are able to calculate the second and the third sum in this expression, then we certainly get a lower bound on the
2k
-th moment of
L(1/2,\chi)
, however our choice of
A(\chi)
should be such that the lower bound we get is close to optimal. The case of equality in Hölder’s inequality tells us that we should choose
A(\chi)
to be such that it behaves like
|L(1/2,\chi)|^{2(k-1)}
. Rudnick and Soundararajan choose
A(\chi)=\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{1/2}}\big|^{2(k-1)}
with
x=q^{\frac{1}{2k}}
, which is reasonable since
L(1/2,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{1/2}}
.

We use the proof method described above to give a lower bound on sufficiently high moments of unweighted character sums. Our theorem reads as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a real number,
q
a large prime. If
1\leq x\leq q^{1/2}
, then

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\gg_{k}x^{k}(\log x)^{(k-1)^{2}}.

If
q^{1/2}\leq x\leq q/2
, then

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\gg_{k}x^{k}\Big(\log\frac{q}{x}\Big)^{(k-1)^{2}}.

Theorem
1
has been proven in the special case when
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x=q^{\alpha}
for a fixed
0<\alpha<1
by Munsch and Shparlinski (see Theorem 1.6. in
[
10
]
), so our main contribution is that we are able to handle non-integer moments in a wider range.
Note that the bounds in Theorem
1
have a slightly different shape according to whether
x\leq q^{1/2}
or
x\geq q^{1/2}
, which comes from the fact that the quantities
\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)
and
\sum_{n\leq q/x}\chi(n)
are closely related by a reciprocity relation. When
q/2\leq x\leq q
, a similar type of lower bound follows easily from Theorem
1
, since in this range the orthogonality relation and periodicity imply
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|=\big|\sum_{x<n<q}\chi(n)\big|=\big|\sum_{n<q-x}\chi(n)\big|
, where
0\leq q-x\leq q/2
so Theorem
1
is applicable (we did not include this range in the statement for simplicity).

Theta functions are closely related to character sums. For a Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
q
define
\kappa=\kappa(\chi)=(1-\chi(-1))/2
, i.e.
\kappa=1
if
\chi
is odd and
\kappa=0
if
\chi
is even. The
\theta
function corresponding to
\chi
is defined as

\theta(x,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)n^{\kappa}e^{-\pi n^{2}x/q}.

Modifying the proof of Theorem
1
, we will show the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X_{q}^{+}
be the set of even characters mod
q
, let
k\geq 2
be a real number. We have

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}|\theta(1,\chi)|^{2k}\gg_{k}q^{k/2}(\log q)^{(k-1)^{2}}.

Let
X_{q}^{-}
denote the set of odd characters mod
q
, let
k\geq 2
be a real number. We have

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{-}}|\theta(1,\chi)|^{2k}\gg_{k}q^{3k/2}(\log q)^{(k-1)^{2}}.

Notice that for an even
\chi
,
\theta(1,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)e^{-\pi n^{2}/q}
behaves similarly to
\sum_{n\leq q^{1/2}}\chi(n)
, since the weight
e^{-\pi n^{2}/q}
starts decaying roughly at
n\approx q^{1/2}
, therefore it is not surprising that the lower bound has the same shape as Theorem
1
with
x=q^{1/2}
(for odd
\chi
the theta function behaves like
\sum_{n\leq q^{1/2}}n\chi(n)
so we have an extra
q^{k}
term in the lower bound). In fact the proof of Theorem
2
is essentially the same as that of Theorem
1
, except for one part of the argument where we need to alter the technicalities of the proof due the fact that the weights
e^{-\pi n^{2}/q}
are not multiplicative. We will explain these in the last section. Again, in
[
10
]
Munsch and Shparlinski proved Theorem
2
when
k
is an integer.

In
[
12
]
, the author showed the corresponding upper bounds for the
2k
-th moment when
k>2
assuming the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis (for any positive integer
q
). Note that showing sharp unconditional upper bounds for these moments when
k
is large seems hopeless with our current knowledge, since only one ‘badly behaving’ character could mess up the bound. For example, when
x=q^{1/5}
and
k=10
, say, the Burgess bound, which is currently the best upper bound for single character sums, does not rule out the possibility that there is some
\chi
mod
q
for which
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|=\lfloor x\rfloor
, so the contribution from this would be
\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\big|\sum_{n\leq q^{1/5}}\chi(n)\big|^{20}\gg q^{3}
, which is much bigger than the expected
q^{2+o(1)}
bound for the moment. This phenomenon appears in other moment inequalities too. For example, we expect the
2k
-th moment of the
\zeta
function to be

\int_{T}^{2T}|\zeta(1/2+it)|^{2k}\asymp_{k}T(\log T)^{k^{2}},

when
T
is large.
The lower bound part of this is known unconditionally for all real
k\geq 0
(see
[
7
]
), whereas the upper bound is only known when
k\leq 4
unconditionally (see
[
6
]
) and for all
k\geq 0
under the Riemann Hypothesis (see
[
4
]
). Also note that the upper bound for arbitrarily large
k
would imply the Lindelöf hypothesis namely that for any
\epsilon>0
and
T\leq t\leq 2T
one has
\zeta(t)\ll T^{\epsilon}
.

Let us go back to discussing the behaviour of

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\Big|^{2k}.

As described above, when
k\geq 2
we understand the order of magnitude of this quantity, even if the upper bounds are conditional. We expect the moment to have the same behaviour when
1\leq k\leq 2
(this will be explained later after introducing random multiplicative functions), however our methods break down for
1<k<2
. It would certainly be interesting to prove some results (even conditionally on GRH) in this direction. When
0\leq k\leq 1
the moment is now known to behave differently. In
[
5
]
Harper shows that if
q
is a prime and
0\leq k\leq 1
, then

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\Big|^{2k}\ll\bigg(\frac{x}{1+(1-k)\sqrt{\log\log\min(x,q/x)}}\bigg)^{k},

and he conjectures that this is indeed the right order of magnitude in this range.

Our proof techniques are heavily influenced by previous work of Harper (
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
), particularly the paper
[
3
]
in which Harper determines the order of magnitude of high real moments (
k\geq 1
) of the Steinhaus random multiplicative function.

We now define the Steinhaus random multiplicative function, which is a random function
f(n)
defined on the integers, and in many aspects is a good model for character sums. Let
(f(p))_{p\text{ prime }}
be a sequence of independent Steinhaus random variables (i.e. variables which are uniformly distributed on the complex unit circle
\{z:|z|=1\}
), and we make
f(n)
completely multiplicative, that is if
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{l}^{a_{l}}
, where the
p_{i}
are distinct primes, then
f(n)=f(p_{1})^{a_{1}}\cdots f(p_{l})^{a_{l}}
. One may expect that
f(n)
simulates the behaviour of
\chi(n)
for an ‘average
\chi
’, since both are completely multiplicative, take values on the unit circle and the values of
\chi
on primes do not influence each other too much, at least when we take primes up to
q
. In fact, we have

\mathbb{E}f(n)\overline{f(m)}=\mathbf{1}(n=m),

which is similar to the orthogonality relation for characters

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }q}\chi(n)\bar{\chi}(m)=\mathbf{1}\big(n\equiv m\text{ (mod }q)\big),

In fact, if
1\leq n,m<q
, we have
\mathbf{1}\big(n\equiv m\text{ (mod }q)\big)=\mathbf{1}(n=m)
, so these two orthogonality relations have the same form. This will allow us to replace a polynomial expression in the
\chi
with the corresponding polynomial expression for
f
as long as the length of the polynomial is less than
q
(see the beginning of Section 4). Generally, handling expressions involving the random multiplicative function is easier, since
f(p)
are independent, whereas the
\chi(p)
are not. Moreover random quantities allow us to directly use tools from probability theory.

Note that biggest discrepancy between these two objects is that a Dirichlet character is periodic modulo its conductor, on the other hand a random multiplicative function is almost surely non-periodic. However, when we consider initial sums
\{\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n):\chi\mod q\}
, where
x\leq q^{1/2}
is small enough, these sums can be modeled well by
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
. In particular, Harper
[
3
]
showed that for any real
k\geq 1
and
x\geq 2

\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\Big|^{2k}\asymp_{k}x^{k}(\log x)^{(k-1)^{2}},

which is the same shape as the first part of our bound in Theorem
1
. Harper’s moment bound therefore also suggests that Theorem
1
might hold for any real
k\geq 1
. Unfortunately our proof crucially uses
k\geq 2
.

Let us give a quick overview as to how the rest of the paper is organised. In Section 2 we state some results from the literature. In Section 3, assuming
x\leq q^{1/2}
, we define our proxy object
R(\chi)
, which is supposed to resemble the behaviour of
|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)|^{2(k-1)}
. Note that the main idea in this paper is really the choice of
R(\chi)
, which will be much more complicated than the proxy object
A(\chi)
defined on the first page, and the idea comes from a thorough understanding of the connection between Dirichlet characters and the Steinhaus random multiplicative function. In Section 3 we will also explain the motivation for the choice of
R(\chi)
. After applying Hölder’s inequality, one needs a lower bound on the quantity
\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|^{2}R(\chi)
and an upper bound on
\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}
. These bounds will be proven in Section 4 and 5 respectively. In Section 6 we deal with the case
q^{1/2}\leq x\leq q/2
, in Section 7 we indicate the changes one has to make in the argument to prove Theorem
2
.

Finally let us mention the paper
[
1
]
, in which the authors give a lower bound for low moments (
0\leq 2k\leq 4/3
) of character sums which is sharp up to a small power of
\log q
.

2.
Background results

Lemma 1
.

Let
f(n)
be the Steinhaus random multiplicative function.
Let
\alpha,\beta,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\geq 0
and
t_{1},t_{2}
be real numbers and
100(1+\max(\alpha^{2},\beta^{2}))\leq z<y
. Then

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\prod_{z\leq p\leq y}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+\sigma_{1}+it_{1}}}\Big|^{-2\alpha}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+\sigma_{2}+it_{2}}}\Big|^{-2\beta}\\
=&\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{p^{1+2\sigma_{1}}}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{p^{1+2\sigma_{2}}}+\frac{2\alpha\beta\cos((t_{2}-t_{1})\log p)}{p^{1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}}}+O\big(\frac{\max(\alpha,\alpha^{3},\beta,\beta^{3})}{z^{1/2}}\big)\bigg).\end{split}

Proof.

This follows from Euler product result 1 in
[
3
]
, with two small differences. One is that Harper assumes
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
in the statement, however the same calculation with trivial modifications yields us the lemma. Secondly, Harper assumes
t_{1}=0
, however
f(p)
and
f(p)p^{-it_{1}}
have the same distribution, so if we substitute
\tilde{f}(p)=f(p)p^{-it_{1}}
for all primes
p
and then make
\tilde{f}
totally multiplicative, then
\tilde{f}
is also a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, so we may apply the result to
\tilde{f}
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of complex numbers and
F(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}
be the corresponding Dirichlet series. If
\sigma_{c}
denotes its abscissa of convergence, then for any
\sigma>\max(0,\sigma_{c})
, we have

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\big|\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\big|^{2}}{x^{1+2\sigma}}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{|F(\sigma+it)|^{2}}{|\sigma+it|^{2}}dt.

Proof.

This is Harmonic Analysis Result 1 in
[
3
]
, which refers to Theorem 5.4 of
[
8
]

∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
y\geq 2
. Then

\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\cos(t\log p)}{p}\leq\begin{cases}\log\log y+O(1)&\text{if }0\leq|t|\leq 1/\log y,\\
\log(1/t)+O(1)&\text{if }1/\log y\leq|t|\leq 10,\\
\log\log|t|+O(1)&\text{if }10\leq|t|.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

This is Lemma 2.9. in
[
9
]
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
a_{n}
and
c_{n}
be two complex sequences. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite set of primes and define
\tilde{d}(n)=\sum_{d|n}\mathbf{1}(p|d\implies p\in\mathcal{P})
. For any integer
j\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}c_{n}f(n)\Big|^{2}\Big|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{a_{p}f(p)}{p^{1/2}}+\frac{a_{p^{2}}f(p^{2})}{p}\Big|^{2j}\ll\Big(\sum_{n\leq x}\tilde{d}(n)|c_{n}|^{2}\Big)\cdot(j!)\cdot\Big(2\cdot\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|a_{p}|^{2}}{p}+\frac{6|a_{p^{2}}|^{2}}{p^{2}}\Big)^{j}

Proof.

This is essentially Lemma 1 in
[
5
]
, however there is a slight difference we need to point out. There the lemma is stated using characters, i.e. the author upper bounds

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}c_{n}\chi(n)\Big|^{2}\Big|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{a_{p}\chi(p)}{p^{1/2}}+\frac{a_{p^{2}}\chi(p^{2})}{p}\Big|^{2j}

under the condition
x(\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p)^{2j}<q
. This condition ensures that the Dirichlet polynomial
\sum_{n\leq x}c_{n}\chi(n)\cdot\big(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{a_{p}\chi(p)}{p^{1/2}}+\frac{a_{p^{2}}\chi(p^{2})}{p}\big)^{j}
has length less than
q
so when applying the orthogonality relation, only the diagonal terms survive. In our case we have this by default, since
\mathbb{E}f(n)\overline{f(m)}=\mathbf{1}(n=m)
, so the same argument can be run.
∎

3.
Choosing the proxy object

Let
x\leq q^{1/2}
.
In this section we define our proxy object
R(\chi)
for each
\chi
mod
q
, then apply Hölder’s inequality and state the two bounds we need to show in order to prove Theorem 1 for
x\leq q^{1/2}
. The proof of these bounds will be given in the next two sections.

Firstly, let us give a heuristic explanation as to how we choose
R(\chi)
. As discussed in the introduction, we want Hölder’s inequality to be close to optimal, so
R(\chi)
should behave like
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|^{2(k-1)}
, however we cannot simply choose
R(\chi)=\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|^{2(k-1)}
as we would not be able to bound the arising quantities (unless
x
is very small in terms of
q
). To proceed, we model
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\big|^{2(k-1)}
by
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\big|^{2(k-1)}
, since we expect
f
to behave like an ‘average’ character
\chi
.

In fact, the main motivation comes from the work of Harper
[
3
]
on high moments of random multiplicative functions. Roughly speaking, he shows that (see equation (1.2) in
[
3
]
)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\big|^{2(k-1)}&\approx\frac{x^{k-1}}{(\log x)^{k-1}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\prod_{p\leq x}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\Big|^{-2}dt\Big)^{k-1}\\
&\approx\frac{x^{k-1}}{(\log x)^{k-1}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\log x}\sum_{|l|\leq\log x/2}\prod_{p\leq x}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log x}}\Big|^{-2}\Big)^{k-1},\end{split}

where in the second line we discretized the integral noting that the Euler product does not change by much if we shift the imaginary part by
\frac{1}{\log x}
. As
k-1\geq 1
(i.e. we are in the high moments case), Harper observes (and later proves) that the main contribution to the expectation comes from a small (bounded) number of
l
-s for which
\prod_{p\leq x}\big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log x}}\big|^{-2}
is large. Thus, roughly speaking we can write

\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{|l|\leq\log x/2}\prod_{p\leq x}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log x}}\Big|^{-2}\Big)^{k-1}\approx\sum_{|l|\leq\log x/2}\mathbb{E}\prod_{p\leq x}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log x}}\Big|^{-2(k-1)}.

In order to be able to handle the arising quantities, we will not take our Euler product up to
x
, but rather
y
, where
y
is a small but fixed power of
x
, so our proxy object
R(\chi)
will be something like

\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\prod_{p\leq y}\Big|1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}\Big|^{-2(k-1)}.

Unfortunately, working directly with this Euler product with our current knowledge is technically impossible (unless
y
is very small compared to
q
), so we approximate it by a suitable Dirichlet polynomial in a way which has now become standard in bounding moments of
L
-functions.
By taking the first two terms in the Taylor expansion of
\log\big(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\big)
and noting that the higher order terms contribute a factor of
O_{k}(1)
, we get

\prod_{p\leq y}\bigg|1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigg|^{-2(k-1)}\asymp_{k}\exp\Big(2(k-1)\Re\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2it}}\Big).

In order for us to use the orthogonality relation for Dirichlet characters, we need to replace this exponential by a suitable short Dirichlet polynomial, whose length will be a small but fixed power of
q
. To do this we use the method developed by Harper in
[
4
]
. Here the author partitions the interval
[1,y]
as
1=y_{0}<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{M}=y
for suitable parameters
y_{m}
, then for each interval
[y_{m-1},y_{m}]
one uses a truncated Taylor expansion

\exp\Big(2(k-1)\Re\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2it}}\Big)\approx\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\Big(\Re\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2it}}\Big)^{j}\bigg)^{2},

where the
J_{m}
are suitable truncation parameters.

Let us now give the proper definition of
R(\chi)
.
We assume
x\leq q^{1/2}
. Let
y=x^{\frac{1}{C_{0}}}
, where
C_{0}
is a sufficiently large absolute constant chosen at the end of the argument, which will depend on
k
.

We define the subdivision
1=y_{0}<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{M}=y
of the interval
[1,y]
recursively by taking
y_{M}=y
, for any
2\leq m\leq M
we have
y_{m-1}:=y_{m}^{1/20}
and choose
M
so that
y_{1}
lies in
\big[y^{\frac{1}{20(\log\log y)^{2}}},y^{\frac{1}{(\log\log y)^{2}}}\big]
.

We next define the truncation parameters
J_{m}
for
1\leq m\leq M
. Let
J_{1}=(\log\log y)^{3/2}
,
J_{M}=\frac{C_{0}}{10^{5}k}
, and if
2\leq m\leq M-1
, we define
J_{m}=J_{M}+M-m
. Before we go on it is important to note the inequality

(1)

\prod_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}^{10^{4}kJ_{m}}<x,

which is an easy consequence of our definition. Our preliminary assumption on
C_{0}
is that it is large enough that we have
J_{M}\geq\exp(10^{4}k^{2})
.

For any
\chi
mod
q
,
1\leq m\leq M
and
|l|\leq(\log y)/2
integers we denote the Dirichlet polynomial

D_{m,l}(\chi):=\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}.

We define the truncated exponential as

R_{m,l}(\chi):=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l}(\chi)\big)^{j}\bigg)^{2},

and let our proxy object be

R(\chi):=\sum_{|l|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l}(\chi).

The definition of
R(\chi)
written out in full is

R(\chi)=\sum_{|l|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\bigg(\Re\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}\bigg)^{j}\bigg)^{2}.

Now that we have defined our proxy objects we apply Hölder’s inequality with exponents
k
and
\frac{k}{k-1}
to get

(2)

\bigg(\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\bigg)^{1/k}\bigg(\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\bigg)^{(k-1)/k}\geq\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi).

As for the lower bound part we will show the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a bounded threshold (in terms of
k
), such that if
C_{0}
exceeds that threshold we have

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi)\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}.

For the upper bound we prove the following.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists a bounded threshold (in terms of
k
), such that if
C_{0}
exceeds that threshold we have

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\ll_{k}(\log y)^{k^{2}+1}

Thus, for some bounded
C_{0}
, these two propositions together with (
2
) imply

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\text{ mod }q\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{(k-1)^{2}},

which proves the first part of Theorem
1
, since
\frac{\log x}{\log y}=C_{0}\ll 1
.

Before moving on, we define the corresponding quantities for the random multiplicative function
f(n)
. We take

D_{m,l}(f)=\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}},

and define
R_{m,l}(f)
and
R(f)
the same way we defined
R_{m,l}(\chi)
and
R(\chi)
.

4.
The lower bound

This section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3.1
. We first go through the main steps of the argument.
As discussed in the introduction, one wants to work with random quantities instead of characters whenever possible.

Hence our first step is to show that Proposition
3.1
follows from

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}R(f)\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1},

in other words we replace the averaging over characters by taking the expectation of the corresponding quantity involving the random multiplicative function. This step is relatively straightforward and it really depends on the fact that
R(\chi)
is a sum of Dirichlet polynomials whose lengths are (much) shorter than
q
.

Next, recall that

D_{m,l}(f)=\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}},

and note that

R(f)=\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l}(f)\big)^{j}\bigg)^{2}

is a sum of truncated exponentials. Therefore we will replace
R(f)
by the actual sum of exponentials

E(f):=\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\Re\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}\bigg),

and show that

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}E(f)\gg x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}.

This is Proposition
4.1
, which is stated and proved later in this section.
Finally we need to check that in the process of replacing
R(f)
by
E(f)
the resulting error is small enough. This is probably the most involved part of the argument and will be proved in Proposition
4.2
. We note that even though the proof involves some lengthy calculations (mostly because the quantities we handle have ‘long’ definitions), the argument is of elementary nature and we do not use any heavy machinery.

Now let us start the argument. As promised, the first step is to replace

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}_{q}^{*}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi)

with the corresponding quantity involving the Steinhaus random multiplicative function. In order to do this we need to to add
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi_{0}(n)\big|^{2}R(\chi_{0})
to the expression so we can use orthogonality. We use the crude bound

D_{m,l}(\chi_{0})=\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{\chi_{0}(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{\chi_{0}(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}\leq y_{m},

moreover by definition for all
1\leq m\leq M

y_{m}
is larger then
100J_{m}
, say, therefore

R_{m,l}(\chi_{0})=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l}(\chi_{0})\big)^{j}\bigg)^{2}\leq(ky_{m})^{2J_{m}}.

Hence by (
1
), we have the bound

\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi_{0}(n)\Big|^{2}R(\chi_{0})\ll x^{2}(\log y)\prod_{m=1}^{M}(ky_{m})^{2J_{m}}<x^{2+1/10},

so the contribution from the trivial character is negligible as
\frac{x^{2+1/10}}{\phi(q)}\leq x^{1/5}
, say (we assumed
x\leq q^{1/2}
). Therefore it is enough to show that

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }q}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi)\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}.

For any
\chi
mod
q
, by expanding the brackets in the definition of
R(\chi)
we may write

(3)

\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi)=\sum_{|l|\leq(\log y)/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}\leq N\\
m_{1},m_{2}\leq x\end{subarray}}a_{n_{1},n_{2}}\frac{\chi(m_{1}n_{1})\bar{\chi}(m_{2}n_{2})}{n_{1}^{1/2+il/\log y}n_{2}^{1/2-il/\log y}},

for some complex coefficients
a_{n_{1},n_{2}}
, and most importantly

xN\leq x\prod_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}^{4J_{m}}\leq x^{3/2}<q.

So when we average (
3
) over
\chi
mod
q
the only non-zero contribution comes from when
m_{1}n_{1}=m_{2}n_{2}
(diagonal terms), thus the orthogonality relation gives us

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}R(\chi)=\mathbb{E}\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}\leq N\\
m_{1},m_{2}\leq x\end{subarray}}a_{n_{1},n_{2}}\frac{f(m_{1}n_{1})\bar{f}(m_{2}n_{2})}{n_{1}^{1/2+il/\log y}n_{2}^{1/2-il/\log y}}=\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}R(f),

so it is enough to show

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}R(f)\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}.

In order to prove this, we need the following two propositions.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have

\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\Re\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}\bigg)\geq e^{O(k^{2}\log\log k)}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}

Proposition 4.2
.

Let us denote the ‘error’

\text{Err}_{m,l}(f):=\exp\Big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\Big)-R_{m,l}(f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\geq 0\\
\max(j_{1},j_{2})>J_{m}\end{subarray}}\frac{(k-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!}(\Re D_{m,l}(f))^{j_{1}+j_{2}}.

For any
1\leq m\leq M
, we have

\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\Big(2(k-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m^{\prime}=1\\
m^{\prime}\neq m\end{subarray}}^{M}\Re D_{m^{\prime},l}(f)\Big)|\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)|\leq e^{O(k^{4})}e^{-J_{m}}\frac{\log x}{\log y}x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}

Remark
.

The exact shape of the error terms
e^{O(k^{2}\log\log)}
and
e^{O(k^{4})}
are actually unimportant, in both propositions any bounded function in terms of
k
would suffice. Since it follows easily from the calculation we decided to keep track of the dependence on
k
(the error terms are not optimal).

The important thing is that the lower bound in Proposition 4.1. is bigger than the upper bound in Proposition 4.2. by a large factor when
m=M
. Recalling that
\frac{\log x}{\log y}=C_{0}
and
J_{M}=\frac{C_{0}}{10^{5}k}
, these bounds differ by a factor of
e^{O(k^{4})}e^{-C_{0}/(10^{5}k)}C_{0}
, which can be made small for sufficiently large
C_{0}
.

Proof that Proposition
4.1
and
4.2
imply Proposition 3.1.

By definition, we have the following chain of equalities.

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}R(f)\\
=&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l}(f)\\
=&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\bigg(\exp\Big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\Big)-\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)\bigg)\\
=&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Re D_{m,l}(f)\bigg)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\bigg[1-\frac{\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)}{\exp\big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\big)}\bigg]\\
\end{split}

Let
x_{m}=-\frac{\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)}{\exp\big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\big)}
. By the definition of
\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)
and noting that
R_{m,l}(f)\geq 0
, we have
x_{m}\geq-1
.
We now use a Bernoulli type inequality in the form that if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{M}\geq-1
are real numbers, then

(4)

(1+x_{1})\cdots(1+x_{M})\geq 1-|x_{1}|-\ldots-|x_{M}|.

This can easily be proven by induction on
M
. For
M=1
, (
4
) is trivial. Assume that
(1+x_{1})\cdots(1+x_{M-1})\geq 1-|x_{1}|-\ldots-|x_{M-1}|
. If
1-|x_{1}|-\cdots-|x_{M-1}|<0
, then (
4
) holds as LHS is non-negative but RHS is negative. Otherwise, using
1+x_{M}\geq 1-|x_{M}|
and the induction hypothesis we obtain

(1+x_{1})\cdots(1+x_{M})\geq(1-|x_{1}|-\cdots-|x_{M-1}|)(1+x_{M})\geq(1-|x_{1}|-\cdots-|x_{M-1}|)(1-|x_{M}|)\geq 1-|x_{1}|-\ldots-|x_{M}|.

We thus deduce

\begin{split}&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Re D_{m,l}(f)\bigg)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\bigg[1-\frac{\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)}{\exp\big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\big)}\bigg]\\
\geq&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Re D_{m,l}(f)\bigg)\bigg[1-\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|\text{Err}_{m,l}(f)|}{\exp\big(2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l}(f)\big)}\bigg].\end{split}

Notice that this expression can be lower bounded using Proposition
4.1
and
4.2
.
Recall that
\frac{\log x}{\log y}=C_{0}
and
J_{M}=\frac{C_{0}}{10^{5}k}
, where
C_{0}
is a large absolute constant which we will choose in the next step.
We thus have

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}R(f)\geq x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}\big(e^{-O(k^{2}\log\log k)}-e^{O(k^{4})-C_{0}/(10^{5}k)}C_{0}\big),

where we used that
\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{-J_{m}}\leq 2e^{-J_{M}}
(and the factor of
2
can be absorbed by the error term).
Now we can choose
C_{0}
to be sufficiently large in terms of
k
, so that the quantity inside the bracket remains positive, thus Proposition
3.1
follows.
∎

4.1.
Proof of Proposition
4.1

Note that

\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+2il/\log y}}=-\log\Big(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}\Big)+O\big(p^{-3/2}\big).

Since
\sum_{p}p^{-3/2}\ll 1
, we have

(5)

\begin{split}&\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\Re\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+2il/\log y}}+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{2p^{1+il/\log y}}\bigg)\\
=&e^{O(k)}\sum_{|l|\leq\log y/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\prod_{p\leq y}\bigg|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il/\log y}}\bigg|^{-2(k-1)}.\\
\end{split}

The Euler product part of the expression behaves nicely, since all the
f(p)
-s are independent of each other by definition. Thus the difficulty is to see how it correlates with the term
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\big|^{2}
.
We use a conditioning argument to proceed. Let
\mathbb{E}^{(y)}
denote the conditional expectation with respect to
(f(p))_{p\leq y}
, i.e. we condition for primes up to
y
. Note that the Euler product is determined by
(f(p))_{p\leq y}
, so it is considered as a fixed quantity when conditioning. By the tower rule we have
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}^{(y)}
, so we need to give a lower bound on
\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\big|^{2}
. Our next lemma states that this can be lower bounded by roughly speaking the difference of two Euler products.
For simplicity, let us denote

F_{y}(s)=\prod_{p\leq y}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\Big|^{-1}.

Lemma 5
.

Assume that
y<x^{1/10}
is large. Then, for any
\beta>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\gg\frac{x}{\log y}\bigg[\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|F_{y}(1/2+\beta+it)|^{2}dt-x^{-\beta/4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|F_{y}(1/2+\beta/2+it)|^{2}\frac{dt}{1/4+t^{2}}\bigg],

where the implied constant is absolute.

Remark
.

We will choose
\beta=\frac{C}{\log y}
, where
C
is a sufficiently large constant. With this choice the integrals are roughly the same size, however
x^{-\beta/4}\leq e^{-C/4}
, which becomes small, thus the first term will dominate which gives us a suitable lower bound.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

The argument follows the proof of Proposition 3 in
[
3
]
. If
n
is a positive integer, let
P^{-}(n)
and
P^{+}(n)
be its smallest and largest prime factor respectively.
Any positive integer
n
can be written uniquely as a product
n=a(n)b(n)
where
P^{+}(a(n))\leq y
and
P^{-}(b(n))>y
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}=\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}f(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/n\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}.

Letting
c_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/n\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)
, we see that
c_{n}
is determined by
(f(p))_{p\leq y}
, so it may be considered a fixed quantity when taking
\mathbb{E}^{(y)}
. Moreover, if
n_{1},n_{2}
are such that
P^{-}(n_{1}n_{2})>y
, the orthogonality relation gives
\mathbb{E}^{(y)}f(n_{1})\overline{f(n_{2})}=\mathbf{1}(n_{1}=n_{2})
. We thus obtain

\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}f(n)c_{n}\bigg|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|c_{n}|^{2}.

Since we only we want a lower bound, we may restrict our range of summation to
x^{3/4}/2<n\leq x
, say, so we get

(6)

\mathbb{E}^{(y)}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigg|^{2}\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{3/4}/2<n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/n\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\geq\sum_{r\leq x^{1/4}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq r\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{x}{r+1}<n\leq\frac{x}{r}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}1,

where the second inequality is obtained via the substitution
r=\big\lfloor\frac{x}{n}\rfloor
.

The inner sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{x}{r+1}<n\leq\frac{x}{r}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}1

can be handled by well-known sieve bounds because
y<x^{1/10}
and the length of the interval
[\frac{x}{r+1},\frac{x}{r}]
is
\frac{x}{r}-\frac{x}{r+1}\gg\frac{x}{r^{2}}\gg x^{1/2}
when
r\leq x^{1/4}
. To be precise, we apply (the lower bound part of) Theorem 12.5 of
[
2
]
(i.e. the Rosser-Iwaniec sieve) for the sequence
\mathcal{A}=\{n:\frac{x}{r+1}<n\leq\frac{x}{r}\}
with
z:=y
and
X=\frac{x}{r}-\frac{x}{r+1}
. The important thing here is that
y<x^{1/10}
and
r\leq x^{1/4}
gives us
s:=\frac{\log X}{\log y}>3
, so
f(s)\gg 1
, where
f
is now the function appearing in the statement of the theorem defined by delayed differential equations (From now on we switch back to
f
being the random multiplicative function). Also
V(z)=\prod_{p\leq y}\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)\gg\frac{1}{\log y}
, thus

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{x}{r+1}<n\leq\frac{x}{r}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}1\gg\frac{x}{r^{2}\log y}.

Hence (
6

\gg\frac{x}{\log y}\sum_{r\leq x^{1/4}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq r\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\gg\frac{x}{\log y}\int_{1}^{x^{1/4}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}.

The integral is in a shape where Lemma
2
is applicable. However our integral only goes up to

and not
\infty

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{x^{1/4}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\
P^{+}(m)<y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}\geq&\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\
P^{+}(m)<y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\frac{dt}{t^{2+2\beta}}-\int_{x^{1/4}}^{\infty}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\
P^{+}(m)<y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\frac{dt}{t^{2+2\beta}}\\
\geq&\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\
P^{+}(m)<y\end{subarray}}f(m)\bigg|^{2}\frac{dt}{t^{2+2\beta}}-x^{-\beta/4}\int_{x^{1/4}}^{\infty}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq t\\

which proves the lemma (note the restriction

was only needed so that we could drop the denominator in the first integral as it was less than 1).

To prove (

=&\log\zeta(1+2\beta)+O(1)\\

=&\log\log y-\log C+O(1).\\
\end{split}

Similarly, using

\begin{split}\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\cos\big((t-l/\log y)\log p\big)}{p^{1+\beta}}&\geq\Re\log\zeta\big(1+\beta+(t-l/\log y)i\big)+O(1)\\

&=\log\log y-\frac{1}{2}\log\big(C^{2}+(t\log y-l)^{2}\big)+O(1)\\
&=\log\log y-\log C+O(1).\end{split}

Therefore, if

, we get

\mathbb{E}|F_{y}(1/2+\beta+it)|^{2}|F_{y}(1/2+il/\log y)|^{2(k-1)}\geq e^{O(k^{2}\log\log k)}(\log y)^{k^{2}}C^{1-2k},

which proves (

We now handle the second integral and show

For notational simplicity, we assume that

Lemma 1 gives us

\mathbb{E}|F_{y}(1/2+\beta/2+it)|^{2}|F_{y}(1/2)|^{2(k-1)}\leq(\log y)^{(k-1)^{2}+1}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\cos(t\log p)}{p^{1+\beta/2}}+O(k^{2}\log\log k)\bigg).

By Lemma

we have

\begin{split}&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(2(k-1)\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\cos(t\log p)}{p^{1+\beta/2}}\bigg)\frac{dt}{t^{2}+1/4}\\
\leq&e^{O(k)}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1/\log y}(\log y)^{2(k-1)}dt+\int_{1/\log y}^{10}t^{-2(k-1)}dt+\int_{10}^{\infty}\frac{(\log t)^{2(k-1)}}{t^{2}}dt\bigg]\leq e^{O(k)}\big((\log y)^{2k-3}+1\big).\end{split}

Since

Finally, (

8

\begin{split}&\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mathbb{E}|F_{y}(1/2+\beta+it)|^{2}|F_{y}(1/2+il/\log y)|^{2(k-1)}dt-\\

Now we choose

5.
Upper bound

This section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3.2
, in fact we will prove the slightly stronger bound

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\ll_{k}(\log y)^{k^{2}+1}.

Recall that
R(\chi)
is the sum of (short) Dirichlet polynomials and we raise it to the non-integer
\frac{k}{k-1}
-th power, so we cannot immediately replace this by the corresponding random multiplicative function quantity. However, we are able to replace the fractional power by a genuine Dirichlet polynomial whose size is (up to a small multiplicative error) larger, and then use the random multiplicative function correspondence.

Before we do that, we first apply the inequality

(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{M})^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\leq x_{1}^{\frac{1}{k-1}}+\cdots+x_{M}^{\frac{1}{k-1}},\;\;\;\text{ where }k\geq 2\text{ and }x_{1},\ldots,x_{M}\geq 0.

Note that it is crucial that
k\geq 2
, so
\frac{1}{k-1}\leq 1
. Note that
\frac{k}{k-1}=1+\frac{1}{k-1}
, so this inequality implies

(11)

\begin{split}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}=\bigg(\sum_{|l|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l}(\chi)\bigg)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\leq&\bigg(\sum_{|l_{1}|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{|l_{2}|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\\
\leq&\sum_{|l_{1}|,|l_{2}|\leq(\log y)/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}.\\
\end{split}

Note that this step of the argument is inspired by the proof of Key Proposition 1 in
[
3
]
.
From now on (until the very end), we fix the values of
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
and aim to arrive at the bound

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\ll\frac{(\log y)^{k^{2}}}{|l_{1}-l_{2}|^{2(k-1)}+1}.

Summing over
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, noting that
k-1\geq 1
, we immediately get the proposition. Note that we get a stronger upper bound as
|l_{1}-l_{2}|
gets larger, and this is the part of the argument where it becomes important that
R(\chi)
is the sum of a Dirichlet polynomial at the shifts
\{1/2+il/\log y:|l|\leq\log y/2\}
and not just at one point.

We now want to replace
R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
by a Dirichlet polynomial that has roughly the same size and also short enough that we can switch to the corresponding random multiplicative quantities. It turns out that the shape of this polynomial depends on the size of
\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
, therefore we partition the set of characters into subclasses according to these sizes.

For any
1\leq m\leq M
, we partition
[0,\infty)
into intervals
(I^{(m)}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, where
I^{(m)}_{0}=[0,\frac{J_{m}}{100k}]
, and for any
n\geq 1
we define the dyadic interval
I^{(m)}_{n}=\frac{J_{m}}{100k}\cdot[2^{n-1},2^{n}]
. We let

\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})=\{\chi\in\mathcal{X}_{q}:\forall 1\leq m\leq M\,\,|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)|\in I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}\}.

Since the ‘expected value’ of
|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)|
is much less than
\frac{J_{m}}{100k}
, most characters lie in
\mathcal{X}(0,\ldots,0)
and we expect to get the main contribution to our sum from here.

Let us now fix non-negative integers
n_{1},\ldots n_{M}
and consider
\chi\in\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})
. For simplicity let us denote
W_{m}=\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}
.
Our quantity that ‘replaces’
R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
is defined as follows. Let
a_{m}=2\lceil 200kJ_{m}\rceil
.

U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)=\begin{cases}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{1}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\big)^{j}\Big)^{2}&\text{ if }n_{m}=0,\\
e^{4W_{m}}|D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)W_{m}^{-1}|^{a_{m}}&\text{ if }J_{m}/100k\leq W_{m}\leq 100kJ_{m},\\
\Big(2\frac{(k-1)^{J_{m}}}{J_{m}!}(2W_{m})^{J_{m}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}|D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)W_{m}^{-1}|^{a_{m}}&\text{ if }100kJ_{m}\leq W_{m}.\\
\end{cases}

Remark
.

Note that
U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
is non-negative. When
n_{m}=0
, i.e.
|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)|
is its ‘typical’ size, both
R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
and
U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
are truncated versions of the same exponential, and since we take enough terms in the truncation they are close.

When
n_{m}\geq 1
,
|D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)W_{m}^{-1}|^{a_{m}}
is a penalising term inserted to detect the fact that
n_{m}\geq 1
is rare. It turns out that
W_{m}^{-a_{m}}
will outweigh all the other terms in our calculation so an appropriate saving will be obtained.

In the next lemma we show that
U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
is larger than
R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
up to a small multiplicative error.

Lemma 7
.

For any
\chi
mod
q
and
1\leq m\leq M
, we have

R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\leq\big(1+O(e^{-J_{m}})\big)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi).

Proof.

First, assume that
n_{m}=0
, i,e,
|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)|\leq\frac{J_{m}}{100k}
. We then have

R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}(\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi))^{j}\Big)^{2}=e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)}\big(1+O(e^{-J_{m}})\big).

On the other hand

U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{1}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\big)^{j}\Big)^{2}=e^{2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)}\big(1+O(e^{-J_{m}})\big),

from which the lemma follows.

Let us now handle the case
n_{m}\geq 1
. By definition
W_{m}\leq|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)|\leq 2W_{m}
, so

R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\leq\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}(2W_{m})^{j}\Big)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}=e^{4W_{m}},

and also
|\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)W_{m}^{-1}|\geq 1
. So if
\frac{J_{m}}{100k}\leq W_{m}\leq 100kJ_{m}
we are done.

When
W_{m}\geq 100kJ_{m}
, we start with the trivial bound

R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\leq\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}(2W_{m})^{j}\Big)^{2},

moreover each term in the sum is at least twice as big as the previous one, hence

\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}(2W_{m})^{j}\leq 2\frac{(k-1)^{J_{m}}}{J_{m}!}(2W_{m})^{J_{m}},

from which the lemma follows.
∎

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix non-negative integers
n_{1},\ldots n_{M}
. We then have

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\ll\frac{(\log y)^{k^{2}}}{|l_{1}-l_{2}|^{2(k-1)}+1}\prod_{m=1}^{M}(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}.

Proof that Proposition
5.1
implies Proposition
3.2
.

Firstly, note that
\prod_{m=1}^{M}(1+O(e^{-J_{m}}))\ll 1
, so by Lemma
7
we have

(12)

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\ll\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi).

We also have

\begin{split}\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}\geq 0}\prod_{m=1}^{M}(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}=&\prod_{m=1}^{M}\Big(\sum_{n_{m}=0}^{\infty}(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}\Big)\\
\leq&\prod_{m=1}^{M}\Big(1+2\cdot\big(\frac{100k}{J_{m}}\big)^{2}\Big)\\
\leq&\exp\Big(10^{5}k^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}J_{m}^{-2}\Big)\\
\ll&1.\end{split}

These two inequalities together with Proposition
5.1
imply

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\ll\frac{(\log y)^{k^{2}}}{|l_{1}-l_{2}|^{2(k-1)}+1},

so by (
11
) we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\leq&\sum_{|l_{1}|,|l_{2}|\leq\log y/2}\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\text{mod}\,q}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)R_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\\
\ll&\sum_{|l_{1}|,|l_{2}|\leq\log y/2}\frac{(\log y)^{k^{2}}}{|l_{1}-l_{2}|^{2(k-1)}+1}\\
\ll&(\log y)^{k^{2}+1},\\
\end{split}

so Proposition
3.2
follows.
∎

To show Proposition
5.1
we first state and prove two lemmas. Note that we are switching to the random multiplicative function for now. We define the polynomials
R_{m,l}(f)
and
U_{m,l}(f)
the same way, except that
\chi
is replaced by
f
.

Lemma 8
.

For
U_{m,l_{2}}
in the case where
n_{m}=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big|e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)+2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f)}-R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\big|\leq e^{-J_{m}}.

Remark
.

This is the same proof method as in the previous section where we replace a truncated exponential by the actual exponential at the cost of a small error.

Proof.

By definition
R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}(\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f))^{j}\Big)^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{1}{j!}(\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f))^{j}\Big)^{2}
. Using the Taylor series of the exponential we may bring
e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)+2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f)}
into similar form, except that the summation goes to infinity in both brackets. By taking absolute values we thus obtain

(13)

\big|e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)+2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f)}-R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\big|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\\
\max(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})>J_{m}\end{subarray}}\frac{(k-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!j_{3}!j_{4}!}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{j_{1}+j_{2}}|D_{m,l_{2}}(f)|^{j_{3}+j_{4}}.

We apply Cauchy-Schwarz and Lemma
4
to get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{j_{1}+j_{2}}|D_{m,l_{2}}(f)|^{j_{3}+j_{4}}\leq&\big(\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{2(j_{1}+j_{2})}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{2(j_{3}+j_{4})}\big)^{1/2}\\
\ll&[(j_{1}+j_{2})!(j_{3}+j_{4})!]^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{2}{p}+\frac{3}{p^{2}}\Big)^{(j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4})/2}.\end{split}

For simplicity, let us denote
A_{m}=(k-1)^{2}\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\big(\frac{2}{p}+\frac{3}{p^{2}}\big)
. We will see that
A_{m}
is a fairly small quantity.

Taking expectations in (
13
), noting that
k-1\geq 1
and
n!\leq n^{n}
, we arrive at the upper bound

(14)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\\
\max(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})>J_{m}\end{subarray}}\frac{A_{m}^{(j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4})/2}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!j_{3}!j_{4}!}(j_{1}+j_{2})^{(j_{1}+j_{2})/2}(j_{3}+j_{4})^{(j_{3}+j_{4})/2}.

This is symmetric in the
j_{i}
-s so we may assume that
\max(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})=j_{1}
at the cost of a factor of 4. This assumption implies
j_{1}>J_{m}
,
j_{1}+j_{2}\leq 2j_{1}
and
j_{3}+j_{4}\leq 2j_{1}
, so (
14
) can be upper bounded by

4\sum_{j_{1}>J_{m}}\frac{A_{m}^{j_{1}/2}}{j_{1}!}(2j_{1})^{j_{1}/2}\sum_{j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2j_{1}A_{m})^{(j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4})/2}}{j_{2}!j_{3}!j_{4}!}.

The inner sum is really the Taylor expansion of the exponential, so we arrive at

4\sum_{j_{1}>J_{m}}\frac{(2j_{1}A_{m})^{j_{1}/2}}{j_{1}!}e^{3\cdot(2j_{1}A_{m})^{1/2}}

We claim that
1000A_{m}\leq J_{m}
(with room to spare). Indeed, if
m=1
, then
A_{1}\leq 4(k-1)^{2}\log\log y
, say, and
J_{1}=(\log\log y)^{3/2}
. If
m\geq 2
, then (recall
y_{m-1}^{20}=y_{m}
)
A_{m}\leq 20(k-1)^{2}
, so
1000A_{m}\leq 20000k^{2}\leq J_{M}\leq J_{m}
. Therefore
3\cdot(2j_{1}A_{m})^{1/2}\leq j_{1}
, so (
14
) is at most

4\sum_{j_{1}>J_{m}}\Big(\frac{A_{m}^{1/2}(2j_{1})^{1/2}e^{2}}{j_{1}}\Big)^{j_{1}}

Here the numerator
A_{m}^{1/2}(2j_{1})^{1/2}e^{2}\leq\frac{j_{1}}{3}
, which gives the bound

4\sum_{j_{1}>J_{m}}3^{-j_{1}}\leq e^{-J_{m}}.

∎

Lemma 9
.

For
U_{m,l_{2}}
in the case where
n_{m}\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\leq(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}.

Remark
.

Here the bound could be made much stronger, however this is enough for us (recall how
5.1
implies
3.2
above). Moreover, in the proof we are allowed to apply crude bounds as well. This comes down to the fact that
n_{m}\geq 1
corresponds to rare characters, whose contribution is negligible.

Proof.

In this proof, for simplicity let us denote

P_{m}=\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{2}{p}+\frac{3}{p^{2}}\leq\begin{cases}10\text{ if }m\geq 2,\\
3\log\log y\text{ if }m=1.\\
\end{cases}

By Cauchy Schwarz, we have

\big(\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\big)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)^{2}\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}.

We first bound
\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)^{2}
. When
m\geq 2
this expectation turns out to be less than a constant depending on
k
. When
m=1
it will be at most a fixed
k
-dependent power of
\log y
. Taking absolute values we get

\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{J_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}\big(\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)\big)^{j}\bigg)^{4}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k-1)^{j}}{j!}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{j}\bigg)^{4}\\
=\mathbb{E}e^{4(k-1)|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|}.

Note that for any real
x
, we have
e^{x}\ll\cosh(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2j}}{(2j)!}
. We apply this with
x=4(k-1)|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|
together with Lemma
4
to obtain

(15)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}e^{4(k-1)|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|}\ll\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(4k)^{2j}}{(2j)!}\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{1}}(f)|^{2j}\ll&\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(4k)^{2j}}{(2j)!}j!\Big(\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{2}{p}+\frac{3}{p^{2}}\Big)^{j}\\
\leq&\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(4k)^{2j}}{j!}P_{m}^{j}\\
=&\exp\big(4k^{2}P_{m}\big).\\
\end{split}

For the second line we used the inequality
(j!)^{2}\leq(2j)!
.

We next bound
\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}
, which will give us the saving for Lemma
9
. For simplicity, let us denote
W=\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}
(note that
m
and
n_{m}
are fixed in this lemma).

U_{m,l_{2}}(f)
has a slightly different shape according to whether
J_{m}/100k\leq W\leq 100kJ_{m}
or
W\geq 100kJ_{m}
. We first handle
J_{m}/100k\leq W\leq 100kJ_{m}
. Here by definition and Lemma
4
we have

\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}\leq e^{8W}W^{-2a_{m}}\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{2}}(f)|^{2a_{m}}\ll e^{8W}W^{-2a_{m}}a_{m}!P_{m}^{a_{m}}\leq\Big(\frac{e^{2}a_{m}P_{m}}{W^{2}}\Big)^{a_{m}}

For the last inequality we used
a_{m}!\leq a_{m}^{a_{m}}
and that
8W\leq 800kJ_{m}\leq 2a_{m}
. We claim
\frac{e^{2}a_{m}P_{m}}{W^{2}}\leq e^{-1}
. Recall that
W\geq J_{m}/100k
, whereas
a_{m}\leq 500kJ_{m}
, say. If
m\geq 2
, then we have
P_{m}\leq 10
, so

W^{2}a_{m}^{-1}P_{m}^{-1}e^{-3}\geq\frac{J_{m}}{10^{10}k^{2}}\geq 1.

If
m=1
, then we proceed similarly and use
a_{1}\leq 500kJ_{1}
,
W\geq\frac{J_{1}}{100k}
,
P_{1}\leq 3\log\log y
,
J_{1}=(\log\log y)^{3/2}
, hence

\frac{e^{2}a_{m}P_{m}}{W^{2}}\leq 10^{10}k^{3}(\log\log y)^{-1/2},

say, which is arbitrarily small if
y
is large enough. Therefore in the case when
J_{m}/100k\leq W\leq 100kJ_{m}
, we have shown that
\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}\ll e^{-a_{m}}\leq e^{-W}
, so to prove the lemma we need to show
e^{4k^{2}P_{m}}e^{-W}\leq(W+1)^{-4}
, which holds with a lot of room to spare. If
m\geq 2
, we have
P_{m}\leq 10
and
W\geq\frac{J_{m}}{100k}\geq e^{1000k^{2}}
. When
m=1
we have
P_{1}\leq 3\log\log y
and
W\geq\frac{(\log\log y)^{3/2}}{100k}
.

Now consider the case of
W\geq 100kJ_{m}
. Using
J_{m}!\geq\big(\frac{J_{m}}{e}\big)^{J_{m}}
and
\frac{2J_{m}}{k-1}\leq 200kJ_{m}\leq a_{m}/2
, we have

\Big(2\frac{(k-1)^{J_{m}}}{J_{m}!}(2W_{m})^{J_{m}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\leq\Big(\frac{10kW}{J_{m}}\Big)^{\frac{2J_{m}}{k-1}}\leq\Big(\frac{10kW}{J_{m}}\Big)^{a_{m}/2}.

By Lemma
4
we obtain

\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{10kW}{J_{m}}\Big)^{a_{m}}W^{-2a_{m}}\mathbb{E}|D_{m,l_{2}}(f)|^{2a_{m}}\ll\Big(\frac{10kP_{m}a_{m}}{WJ_{m}}\Big)^{a_{m}}

If
m\geq 2
, we have
10kP_{m}a_{m}\leq 10^{5}k^{2}J_{m}
, so

\Big(\frac{10kP_{m}a_{m}}{WJ_{m}}\Big)^{a_{m}}\leq\Big(\frac{10^{5}k^{2}}{W}\Big)^{a_{m}}\leq W^{-a_{m}/2}.

If
m=1
, then note that
P_{1}\leq 3\log\log y
and
W\geq J_{1}=(\log\log y)^{3/2}
, so

\Big(\frac{10kP_{m}a_{m}}{WJ_{m}}\Big)^{a_{m}}\leq\Big(\frac{10^{5}k^{2}\log\log y}{W}\Big)^{a_{m}}\leq W^{-a_{m}/4},

when
y
is large enough.
Therefore, when
100kJ_{m}\leq W
we have shown that
\mathbb{E}U_{m,l_{2}}(f)^{2}\ll W^{-100kJ_{m}}
, so we need to show that
e^{40k^{2}P_{m}}W^{-100kJ_{m}}\leq(W+1)^{-4}
, which again holds with a lot of room to spare.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

By (
1
),
\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
is a Dirichlet polynomial of length at most

\prod_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}^{(4+4)J_{m}+2a_{m}}<q,

therefore by the orthogonality relation and since
R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)
is a non-negative quantity,

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{M})}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)\leq\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}_{q}}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(\chi)U_{m,l_{2}}(\chi)=\mathbb{E}\prod_{m=1}^{M}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f).

Note that the random quantities
\big(R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\big)_{1\leq m\leq M}
are independent of each other, so we can bring the expectation inside. By Lemma
1
we also have

(16)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)+2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f)}\\
=&e^{O\big(k\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\big)}\mathbb{E}\prod_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il_{1}/\log y}}\Big|^{-2(k-1)}\cdot\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+il_{2}/\log y}}\Big|^{-2}\\
=&\exp\Big(\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{p}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{2(k-1)\cos\big(\frac{l_{1}-l_{2}}{\log y}\log p\big)}{p}+O\big(\frac{k^{3}}{y_{m-1}^{1/2}}+k\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\big)\Big)\\
\end{split}

Hence, this expectation is at least
e^{-O(k^{3})}
, so by Lemma
8
, if
n_{m}=0
, then

(17)

\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\leq\exp\big(e^{O(k^{3})-J_{m}}\big)\mathbb{E}e^{2(k-1)\Re D_{m,l_{1}}(f)+2\Re D_{m,l_{2}}(f)}.

Now if we sum these error terms for
1\leq m\leq M
, we get the bound

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Big(e^{O(k^{3})-J_{m}}+\frac{k^{3}}{y_{m-1}^{1/2}}+k\sum_{y_{m-1}<p\leq y_{m}}\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Big)\ll e^{O(k^{3})}.

When
n_{m}>0
, we use Lemma
9
, that is
\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\leq(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}
. Note that when
n_{m}=0
, we can multiply our bound by
1=(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}
, as it does not change the value.
Therefore (
16
), (
17
) and Lemma
9
give

\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}R_{m,l_{1}}(f)U_{m,l_{2}}(f)\leq\exp\Big(\sum_{p\leq y_{M}}\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{p}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{2(k-1)\cos\big(\frac{l_{1}-l_{2}}{\log y}\log p\big)}{p}+O\big(k^{3}\big)\Big)\prod_{m=1}^{M}(\inf I^{(m)}_{n_{m}}+1)^{-2}.

We know apply Lemma
3
, to get

\sum_{p\leq y_{M}}\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{p}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{2(k-1)\cos\big(\frac{l_{1}-l_{2}}{\log y}\log p\big)}{p}\ll\frac{(\log y)^{k^{2}}}{|l_{1}-l_{2}|^{2(k-1)}+1},

from which the proposition follows.
∎

6.
The large
x
case

In this section we explain how to modify our argument to the case when
q^{1/2}\leq x\leq q/2
. Firstly, we show that we can assume that
x\leq\frac{q}{C}
, where
C
is a large constant chosen later. By Hölder’s inequality we have

\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\geq\bigg(\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}\bigg)^{k}.

Note that we divide by
\phi(q)-1=q-2
because this is the number of non-trivial characters. By the orthogonality relation, we have

\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2}=\lfloor x\rfloor-\frac{\lfloor x\rfloor^{2}}{q-1},

which is at least
\frac{x}{3}
, say, so we get

\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\geq x^{k}3^{-k},

which is the desired bound when
x\geq q/C
.

When
q^{1/2}\leq x\leq q/C
, we choose our proxy object
R(\chi)
the same way as if we had
x\leq q^{1/2}
, but the parameter
y
is chosen as
y=\big(\frac{q}{x}\big)^{1/C_{0}}
(and the subdivison
1=y_{0}<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{M}=y
is chosen accordingly), with the same large constant
C_{0}
. In particular the proxy objects
R(\chi)
corresponding to
x
and
\frac{q}{x}
are the same.
Now it is enough to show that with this choice of
y
and
R(\chi)
, Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
hold, since then (
2
) (Hölder’s inequality) implies

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{X}_{q}^{*}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\bigg|^{2k}\gg_{k}x(\log y)^{(k-1)^{2}},

which is sufficient for us as
\log y\asymp\log\frac{q}{x}
. Proposition
3.2
clearly holds, since the argument does not involve
x
whatsoever, however the same is not true for Proposition
3.1
. At the start of the argument, we added the contribution from the trivial character
\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi_{0}(n)\Big|^{2}R(\chi_{0})
so we could use orthogonality and switch to the random multiplicative function. There we used that
x\leq q^{1/2}
. However, now we have

\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi_{0}(n)\Big|^{2}R(\chi_{0})\ll x^{2}(\log y)\prod_{m=1}^{M}(10ky_{m})^{2J_{m}}\ll x^{2}\Big(\frac{q}{x}\Big)^{1/10},

so the contribution is

\ll\frac{x^{2}}{\phi(q)}\Big(\frac{q}{x}\Big)^{1/10}\leq 2x\Big(\frac{x}{q}\Big)^{1-1/10}\leq 2xC^{-9/10}.

We choose
C
so large in terms of
k
that this contribution becomes negligible.

The second thing we need to be careful of when reproving Proposition
3.2
is that in Lemma
5
we assumed
y<x^{1/10}
, but this holds now ‘even more’, since
y^{10}\leq\frac{q}{x}\leq x
.

7.
Proof of Theorem
2

We show how to modify the proof of Theorem
1
to obtain Theorem
2
.

Firstly, we consider even characters. Let
x=q^{1/2}(\log q)^{2}
,
y=x^{1/C_{0}}
, where
C_{0}
is a sufficiently large constant. Our proof method is essentially the same. We define our proxy object
R(\chi)
corresponding to
y
as in Section 3, and apply Hölder’s inequality

\bigg(\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}|\theta(1,\chi)|^{2k}\bigg)^{1/k}\bigg(\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\bigg)^{(k-1)/k}\geq\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}|\theta(1,\chi)|^{2}R(\chi).

By Section 5, we clearly have the same upper bound

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}R(\chi)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\ll(\log y)^{k^{2}+1},

on the other hand, getting the lower bound for

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}|\theta(1,\chi)|^{2}R(\chi)

is trickier. For any character
\chi
, we have the crude bound

\Big|\sum_{n\geq x}\chi(n)e^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{q}}\Big|\leq qe^{-\frac{x^{2}}{q}}\leq e^{-(\log q)^{3}},

so it suffices to show

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in X_{q}^{+}\backslash\{\chi_{0}\}}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)e^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{q}}\Big|^{2}R(\chi)\gg q^{1/2}(\log y)^{k^{2}-1}.

As we did at the start of Section 4, we add
\chi_{0}
into the summation as its contribution is negligible compared to the lower bound we want to show. Next we use the orthogonality relation for even characters (here we use that
q
is prime), namely

\frac{2}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\in\chi_{q}^{+}}\chi(n)\bar{\chi}(m)=\mathbf{1}(n\equiv\pm m\,\text{mod }q),

and note that the Dirichlet polynomial
\big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)e^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{q}}\big|^{2}R(\chi)
has length at most
x^{1+1/10}<q
(to be precise, here by length we mean the largest
n
such that a term
\chi(n)
or
\bar{\chi}(n)
occur), so only the diagonal terms survive and we can switch to proving

\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)e^{-\frac{-\pi n^{2}}{q}}\Big|^{2}R(f)\gg x(\log y)^{k^{2}-1},

for which we would like bounds corresponding to Proposition
4.1
and
4.2
. The proof of Proposition
4.2
is done in the very same manner as in the unweighted case, the only slight difference is that during the proof of Lemma
6
, the quantity (
10
) is a bit different with a weight
e^{-\frac{\pi l^{2}}{q}}
introduced in the inner sum, but since this weight as at most
1
, this does not change rest of the argument.

On the other hand, the proof of Proposition
4.1
with weights
e^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{q}}
is not as straightforward, because these weights are not multiplicative. After using the same conditioning argument (condition with respect to primes up to
y
), we need to get a lower bound on

\mathbb{E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/n\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}e^{-\pi\frac{(mn)^{2}}{q}}f(m)\Big|^{2}\prod_{p\leq y}\Big|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it_{0}}}\Big|^{-2(k-1)},

where
t_{0}=\frac{il}{\log y}
for some
|l|\leq\log y/2
. The actual value of
t_{0}
is unimportant as the argument goes the same for all values.
Firstly, note that we may extend the summation over
m
to infinity by the fast decay of the exponential weight, in particular we have

(18)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/n\\
P^{+}(n)\leq y\end{subarray}}e^{-\pi\frac{(mn)^{2}}{q}}f(m)\Big|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\frac{(mn)^{2}}{q}}f(m)\Big|^{2}+O\big(e^{-(\log q)^{2}}\big).

Similarly to the unweighted case, our next step is to replace the sum over
n
by an integral (in the unweighted case we actually used a substitution
r=\lfloor x/n\rfloor
and then switched to an integral). Let us denote

g(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}f(m).

We show that
g(t)
changes relatively slowly for sufficiently large
t
.

Lemma 10
.

For any
t>0
and
0<\alpha<t
, we have

|g(t+\alpha)-g(t)|\ll\frac{\alpha q^{1/2}}{t^{2}}

.

Proof.

Using the approximation
e^{u}=1+O(u)
when
u\leq 0
, we obtain

\Big|e^{-\pi\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}-e^{-\pi\frac{m^{2}(t+\alpha)^{2}}{q}}\Big|=e^{-\pi\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}\Big|1-e^{-\pi\frac{m^{2}(2\alpha t+\alpha^{2})}{q}}\Big|\ll\frac{m^{2}\alpha t}{q}\cdot e^{-\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}

Therefore, by the triangle inequality, we obtain

|g(t+\alpha)-g(t)|\leq\frac{\alpha t}{q}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}e^{-\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}.

We have

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}e^{-\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\frac{kq^{1/2}}{t}<m\leq\frac{(k+1)q^{1/2}}{t}}m^{2}e^{-\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}\ll\frac{q^{3/2}}{t^{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k+1)^{2}e^{-k^{2}}\ll\frac{q^{3/2}}{t^{3}},

which proves the lemma.
∎

Fix
t
in the range
x^{3/4}\leq t\leq x
. Let
\delta=\frac{1}{100}
. Note that
|g(t)|\ll\frac{q^{1/2}}{t}
, so if
t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}
, then
|g(n)|^{2}-|g(t)|^{2}\ll\frac{q^{1/2}}{t}|g(n)-g(t)|\ll\frac{qx^{\delta}}{t^{3}}
by the previous lemma. Therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(n)|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(t)|^{2}+O\Big(\frac{x^{2\delta}q}{t^{3}}\Big).

Recall that
x=q^{1/2}(\log q)^{2}
, so the error term is
O(x^{-1/10})
, say. A similar argument yields

|g(t)|^{2}=x^{-\delta}\int_{t}^{t+x^{\delta}}|g(u)|^{2}du+O\big(x^{-1/10}\big).

The same sieve argument as in the proof of Lemma
5
implies (assume
y<x^{1/1000}
, say) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}1\gg\frac{x^{\delta}}{\log y},

so our last three equations put together imply

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(n)|^{2}\gg\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{t}^{t+x^{\delta}}|g(u)|^{2}du+O\big(x^{-1/10}\big).

If we sum this over the appropriate
t
-s we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{3/4}\leq n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(n)|^{2}\gg\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{x^{3/4}}^{x}|g(u)|^{2}du+O\big(x^{1-1/10}\big)=\frac{q^{1/2}}{\log y}\int_{(\log q)^{-2}}^{q^{1/8}(\log q)^{-3/2}}\frac{|g(q^{1/2}/v)|^{2}}{v^{2}}dv+O\big(x^{9/10}\big),

where we applied the substitution
v=\frac{q^{1/2}}{u}
(recall that
x=q^{1/2}(\log q)^{2}
). For simplicity, let us denote
h(v)=g(q^{1/2}/v)
, so

h(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\frac{m^{2}}{v^{2}}}f(m).

As in the proof of Lemma
5
, we apply Rankin’s trick, however we cannot apply Lemma
2
, because our sum is weighted, so we make use of (the first part of) Theorem 5.4 in
[
8
]
, which is a Parseval-type identity and a generalisation of Lemma
2
. Thus, we let
K(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(v)v^{-s-1}dv
, so for any constant
\beta>0
we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{q^{1/2}}{\log y}\int_{(\log q)^{-2}}^{q^{1/8}(\log q)^{-3/2}}\frac{|h(v)|^{2}}{v^{2}}dv\gg&\frac{q^{1/2}}{\log y}\bigg[\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|K(1/2+\beta+it)|^{2}dt-q^{-\beta/9}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|K(1/2+\beta/2+it)|^{2}\frac{dt}{1/4+t^{2}}\bigg]+\\
&+O\Big(\frac{q^{1/2}}{\log y}\int_{0}^{(\log q)^{-2}}\frac{|h(v)|^{2}}{v^{2}}dv\Big).\end{split}

Here the error term appears because to apply the Parseval-type identity we need to integrate from 0. Luckily, the error term is negligible, since in the range
0<v<(\log q)^{-2}
we have
|h(v)|\ll e^{-1/v^{2}}
. As for
K(s)
, some calculation yields

K(s)=\frac{\Gamma(s/2)}{2\pi^{s/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{f(m)}{m^{s}}=\frac{\Gamma(s/2)}{2\pi^{s/2}}\prod_{p\leq y}\Big(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\Big)^{-1},

so the argument can be finished the same way as in Section 4 (in fact, the presence of the
\Gamma
factor makes the technicalities easier).

For odd characters, the modification is almost the same. Here (
18
) is modified to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}n^{2}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}me^{-\pi\frac{(mn)^{2}}{q}}f(m)\Big|^{2},

so we now let

g(t)=t\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}me^{-\pi\frac{(mt)^{2}}{q}}f(m).

Here, by a similar argument
|g(t)|\ll t\cdot\big(\frac{q^{1/2}}{t}\big)^{2}=\frac{q}{t}
and if
x^{3/4}\leq t\leq n
then
|g(t+\alpha)-g(t)|\ll\alpha\cdot\frac{q}{t^{2}}

so

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leq n\leq t+x^{\delta}\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(n)|^{2}\gg\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{t}^{t+x^{\delta}}|g(u)|^{2}du+O\big(\frac{x^{2\delta}q^{2}}{t^{3}}\big).

This implies

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{3/4}\leq n\leq x\\
P^{-}(n)>y\end{subarray}}|g(n)|^{2}\gg\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{x^{3/4}}^{x}|g(t)|^{2}dt+O(q^{3/2-1/100}).

Here it is important that the error term is less than
q^{3/2}
. Letting
h(v)=g(q^{1/2}/v)
, we get

\frac{1}{\log y}\int_{x^{3/4}}^{x}|g(t)|^{2}dt=\frac{q^{1/2}}{\log y}\int_{(\log q)^{-2}}^{q^{1/8}(\log q)^{-3/2}}\frac{|h(v)|^{2}}{v^{2}}dv=\frac{q^{3/2}}{\log y}\int_{(\log q)^{-2}}^{q^{1/8}(\log q)^{-3/2}}\frac{1}{v^{4}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\frac{m^{2}}{v^{2}}}mf(m)\Big|^{2}dv.

Here we can apply Parseval and Rankin’s trick the same way.
