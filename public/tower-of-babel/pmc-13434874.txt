Professional value of the advanced palliative and hospice social work certification: Findings from a national survey of palliative social workers
Authors: Arden O’Donnell, Victoria Leff, Chad Reilly, Myra Glajchen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Advanced Palliative Hospice Social Work Certification (APHSW-C) is the first exam-based credential to define and objectively verify standardized competencies for palliative and hospice social workers in the United States. As the certification reaches its first five-year milestone, little is known about how certificants perceive its professional value, impact on professional identity, and recognition by healthcare institutions.ObjectivesTo examine how those holding the APHSW-C perceive the certification’s impact on their professional identity, interprofessional recognition, institutional support, and career development.DesignA cross-sectional, mixed-methods survey was distributed electronically to all APHSW-C holders (N=844) via the Hospice and Palliative Credentialing Center and supplemented by a single listserv posting.MethodsThe survey instrument included closed-ended items assessing motivations, employer recognition, perceived benefits, exam perceptions, and renewal intentions; open-ended items captured narrative reflections on certification impact. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively; qualitative responses were thematically coded by three reviewers and integrated with quantitative findings during interpretation.ResultsA total of 178 certificants responded (21% response rate). Most pursued certification to validate their advanced expertise (94%) and support the professionalization of palliative social work (76%). Respondents reported stronger professional identity and greater interdisciplinary credibility. Employer recognition was variable; fewer than one-third received reimbursement for exam fees, fewer than 20% reported salary increases, and 27% reported no formal recognition. Despite this, two-thirds (67%) planned to renew, citing professional identity and commitment to the field, while those uncertain cited limited institutional benefit, financial burden, or changing career circumstances. Qualitative themes reinforced that certificants view the APHSW-C as both a symbol of advanced practice and a mechanism for advocacy and interprofessional parity, though a persistent gap between professional value and organizational reward was noted.ConclusionThe APHSW-C is perceived by certificants as a meaningful validation of specialty expertise that strengthens professional identity, credibility, and interprofessional standing. However, limited and inconsistent institutional recognition remains a significant barrier, particularly regarding financial support, salary incentives, and career advancement. Realizing the credential’s full potential requires aligned action from healthcare organizations, professional associations, educators, and policymakers to formally recognize, reward, and integrate the APHSW-C into workforce structures, reimbursement pathways, and career-ladder frameworks.

Introduction

Palliative Social Workers (PSWs) bring important psychosocial expertise to interdisciplinary palliative care, including advanced skills in mental health assessment, nuanced understanding of social determinants of health, and culturally attuned communication.1–4 Yet despite growing recognition of their contributions, the field has lacked rigorous evidence on whether specialty certification — now available through the Advanced Palliative and Hospice Social Work Certification (APHSW-C) — is achieving its intended aims to elevate, standardize and formally recognize the specialty expertise of social workers in hospice and palliative care. This study offers the first empirical examination of the APHSW-C’s real-world impact as perceived by certified practitioners.

The need for a validated credential in palliative social work emerged alongside the broader professionalization of the field. When palliative care officially emerged as a field in the early 2000s, other professions in the United States, such as medicine and nursing, introduced certifications to define specialty practice and establish education and practice standards.5,6 Social work followed in 2005, with a comprehensive set of competencies defining the knowledge, skills, and values central to hospice and palliative social work practice. 1 However, these competencies were not accompanied by a standardized method for verifying advanced practice or distinguishing specialty-level expertise across settings.

In 2008, the National Association of Social Workers (NASW), with support from the National Hospice and Palliative Care Organization (NHPCO), launched the Advanced Certified Hospice and Palliative Social Worker (ACHP-SW) credential. 7 This portfolio-based certification relies on practice experience, references, self-attestation, and continuing education rather than a standardized, psychometrically validated examination. However, uptake was limited; a 2012 national survey of 1,169 hospice and palliative care social workers found that while over 70% were aware of the NASW certificate, fewer than 10% had obtained it. 8 The low uptake has been linked to the subjective limitations of portfolio-based certification, cost and continuing education requirements. 8

These findings motivated a new initiative to develop an exam-based certification. (Timeline in Supplemental Materials). Research across health professions suggests that exam-based certification confers distinct advantages over portfolio-based models. 9 Validating specialty-level palliative social work expertise is not only a matter of professional recognition but also a mechanism for strengthening consistent, equitable psychosocial support for seriously ill patients and their families. Studies in palliative nursing link certification to higher levels of self-confidence, enhanced professional credibility, and increased recognition from employers and peers.9–11 Similarly, there is a link between confidence and competence for social workers in healthcare, especially within hierarchical interprofessional teams. 10 Literature further shows a positive impact on job satisfaction and retention, suggesting that examination-based certification may function as both a visible marker of quality care and a strategy for workforce retention.11,12 Given the unique contribution of health social workers to advocacy and continuity of care for underserved and marginalized groups, retention of the social work workforce is essential to maintain quality of care for seriously ill patients. Workforce stability is therefore also an equity issue, particularly for marginalized patients and families who depend on social workers for advocacy, resource connection and continuity across fragmented systems of care.

In 2016, a group of palliative social work leaders, led by Dr. Barbara Head and supported by the Social Work Hospice & Palliative Network (SWHPN) and the Gordon & Betty Moore Foundation, conducted the first national job analysis survey of hospice and palliative social work practice. 13 This analysis identified the tasks, knowledge, and skills required for advanced competency in palliative social work and assessed interest in an exam-driven certification program; 85% of respondents expressed interest.13,14 In 2017, a nonprofit organization (dba, Advanced Palliative & Hospice Social Work Certification Organization) partnered with Psychological Services Inc (PSI), to develop and administer a psychometrically rigorous examination. 15 Eligibility criteria reflectes advanced social work practice: a CSWE-accredited social work degree, state licensure (where required), attestation to follow the NASW Code of Ethics, and at least two years (4,000 hours) of post-graduate practice in serious illness care. 16 The three-hour, 175 item, exam assesses competence across four domains: Assessment and Reevaluation, Planning and Intervention, Grief and Bereavement, and Professionalism. 16 The cost of the exam ranges from $295 (SWHPN membership) to $435 (non-members). Renewal applications cost $250- $360 every four years.

The first in-person examination was offered in January 2019 with positive uptake by the profession. In 2022, the Hospice and Palliative Credentialing Center (HPCC), a national leader in certifying hospice and palliative care professionals, assumed administrative responsibility, ensuring ongoing psychometric rigor, impartial oversight, and continual program evaluation.5,16 Like other nationally accredited certification programs, the APHSW-C undergoes ongoing, rigorous item analysis, periodic recalibration of scoring standards, and a new job analysis every six years. 17 The overall passage rate for the exam is approximately 70%, which is well aligned with expected national standards. 16

As the APHSW-C exam completes its first full five-year cycle, the field is now positioned to examine its impact on professional recognition, workforce development, and interdisciplinary credibility. While the credential was designed to strengthen the field’s identity, ensure competency, and improve patient care, secondary aims included increased recognition by employers, pay equity, and perceived value within institutions. This study examines how PSWs perceive the personal and professional impact of the APHSW-C. Specifically, how has the credential influenced professional recognition, institutional support, and the perceived legitimacy of palliative and hospice social work as a specialized practice area?

Methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional, mixed-methods survey design to examine the perceived impact of the APHSW-C on certificants’ practice, professional recognition, and career advancement. A mixed-methods approach was used because the study sought to quantify patterns in perceived certification impact while also capturing certificants’ narrative explanations of how the APHSW-C shaped professional identity, recognition, and career experiences. Sample and recruitment

The target population included social workers who had obtained the APHSW-C as of the time of survey distribution (N=844). The Hospice and Palliative Credentialing Center (HPCC) distributed electronic survey invitations by direct email to eligible certificants in three consecutive weekly emails beginning August 22, 2025. This distribution yielded N=149 responses. To broaden reach, a single request with a unique survey link was shared once through the Social Work Hospice and Palliative Care Network Listserv, which includes more than 900 members, many of whom hold the APHSW-C.

Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Respondents created a unique study ID using birth month, birth date, and the last two digits of their ZIP code, and reported their year of certification. These responses were used to support de-identified tracking and provide a self-report check on credential status. For respondents recruited through the Listserv (N=29), APHSW-C status was not independently re-verified beyond self-attestation. Respondents who did not hold the APHSW-C were excluded.

Instrument

The survey instrument, included in the Supplemental Materials, was developed by adapting selected topics from prior hospice and palliative social work workforce and job-analysis studies to maintain continuity with earlier field-level research. Additional certification-specific items were informed by the specialty certification literature and the aims of the present study. Draft items were reviewed by the research team and HPCC collaborators and piloted with a small group of APHSW-C holders (N = 3) for relevance, clarity, and face validity before distribution.

The final instrument included closed-ended and open-ended items. Closed-ended items assessed motivations for pursuing certification, employer recognition, perceived benefits of certification, perceptions of whether the examination reflected advanced practice knowledge, renewal intentions, and whether respondents would recommend the credential to others. Open-ended items invited respondents to describe factors influencing renewal, the credential’s impact on the field, reasons for recommending or not recommending certification, and suggestions for improvement. As the survey instrument was designed primarily to capture descriptive perceptions, it was not independently validated.

Data collection

Data were collected through Qualtrics. The survey was open for six weeks, from August 22 through September 28, 2025. Recruitment included three HPCC email invitations and one listserv posting. Participants provided informed consent before beginning the survey. No personally identifying information was collected. Data were screened for duplicate IDs and IP addresses, and none were identified. A total of 178 certificants completed the survey, representing approximately 21% of the HPCC-identified eligible certificant group.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for continuous variables, as appropriate. Because many survey items allowed multiple selections, percentages for those items were calculated using the number of respondents to that item as the denominator. Qualitative open-text responses were first summarized across major survey domains, including certification impact, employer recognition, renewal intentions, and perceived professional value. Three authors independently reviewed the open-text responses to familiarize themselves with the data and generate initial codes. The team then met to compare codes, develop a preliminary codebook, refine code definitions, and organize codes into broader themes. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Qualitative themes were then used to contextualize, explain, and elaborate these quantitative patterns, with particular attention to areas of convergence and tension between perceived professional value and institutional recognition. No qualitative analysis software was used. Mixed-methods integration occurred during interpretation.

Ethical considerations

The study (Protocol #7972) was reviewed, approved, and deemed exempt from review by the Boston University Institutional Review Board on June 16, 2025, due to its minimal-risk design. Participants provided informed consent before starting the survey.

Results

A total of 178 respondents completed the survey (Table 1). Most were female (90%) and held a master’s in social work (MSW) (90%). Nearly half worked in hospital-based programs (47%), followed by hospice (37%) and integrated palliative or health system roles (17%). Most identified as clinical social workers (68%), with the remainder in supervisory, administrative, or educational roles. Motivations for pursuing APHSW-C (Figure 1)

The majority of respondents reported pursuing the APHSW-C to strengthen their professional credibility and validate their specialized skills (94%). As one respondent noted, “I saw it as an opportunity for professional advancement.” A large majority (76%) cited a collective commitment to the field’s professionalization, with another explaining, “I find value in holding a certification that elevates our experience as professionals.” Over three-quarters (76%) endorsed contributing to the professionalization of palliative social work, 68% selected affirming their commitment to palliative care values and principles, and almost half (49%) cited the importance of aligning their practice with national standards. One respondent explained, “I took the exam because I believe palliative social work should have the same evidence-based expectations as our clinical colleagues,” underscoring a desire for parity and shared standards across disciplines. Respondents further noted that the credential supported career development, with 47% indicating that it enhanced opportunities for leadership and demonstrated ongoing commitment to career growth. As one social worker summarized, “This certification is about advancing the field—it’s about saying, ‘Our work matters.’”

Interprofessional recognition from colleagues

Professional recognition emerged as a key outcome of earning the certification. Most respondents (70%) stated that the APHSW-C provided formal acknowledgment of advanced competence, and two-thirds (67%) felt it demonstrated their commitment to patient and family care. Increased visibility and respect within the interdisciplinary team were important motivators, with 36% reporting greater respect from colleagues and enhanced team visibility. Respondents highlighted that the credential “legitimized my expertise in ways that my master’s degree alone didn’t,” and that “Having the APHSW-C makes a difference. It signals that social work is an equal part of the clinical conversation, not an afterthought.”

Many emphasized that the certification helped articulate palliative and hospice social work as a clinical specialty, drawing parallels to exam-based credentials in other disciplines:“It names and defines our skillsets, similar to our physician colleagues in palliative care. In this way, I think a certification like APHSW-C helps us advocate and claim space in palliative care as highly skilled partners in the work.”

“It names and defines our skillsets, similar to our physician colleagues in palliative care. In this way, I think a certification like APHSW-C helps us advocate and claim space in palliative care as highly skilled partners in the work.”

Notably, about one in seven (16%) indicated that the credential had not produced tangible benefits in their current position: “Outside of our department, few people even know what the credential means. I often have to explain it.”

Employer recognition (Figure 2)

Employer recognition for APHSW-C was common, but inconsistent, across settings. Over half (58%) reported the credential was incorporated into their job title, email signature, or business card. Forty-six percent felt it was recognized as evidence of clinical expertise, and 42% reported that it was formally acknowledged as a professional achievement. Most recognition was symbolic, such as inclusion in internal communications and acknowledgment at staff meetings. However, one in four (27%) indicated no formal recognition, reflecting uneven institutional support across settings. Several respondents voiced frustration that institutional recognition did not match the credential’s professional promise:“In my institution, while management will recognize the certification, this does not translate to an increase in pay and/or professional mobility. It is a bit disheartening that this certification has not had the impact I thought it would have.”

“In my institution, while management will recognize the certification, this does not translate to an increase in pay and/or professional mobility. It is a bit disheartening that this certification has not had the impact I thought it would have.”

Importantly, only one-third (32%) reported reimbursement of exam or renewal fees, less than 20% received a pay increase and 11% received a one-time bonus, prompting one participant to reflect, “It has strengthened the field, but has not translated to pay equity or advancement.”

Impact on the field

Open-ended reflections revealed a strong consensus that the APHSW-C has led to a significant impact on the profession. Respondents described the credential as both a ‘symbol of advanced practice’ and a ‘mechanism for advocacy.’ Many participants emphasized that certification provides “a framework for the expectations of the role” and “a way to differentiate palliative care social work from unit-based practice.” Several drew parallels to exam-based certifications in medicine, nursing, and chaplaincy, noting that it “puts us on equal footing with our physician and nursing colleagues.” Others highlighted its importance for workforce retention, describing it as “the first step toward a true career ladder for palliative social workers.”

Even among those with limited organizational rewards, many emphasized the credentials’ broader significance. As one respondent explained,“The APHSW-C certification elevated professional standards by defining a clear set of core competencies for advanced palliative and hospice social workers. I believe it enhances the credibility and visibility of social workers within medical and palliative care teams. While not always recognized, it is something we as professionals can speak to and empower others to do the same.”

“The APHSW-C certification elevated professional standards by defining a clear set of core competencies for advanced palliative and hospice social workers. I believe it enhances the credibility and visibility of social workers within medical and palliative care teams. While not always recognized, it is something we as professionals can speak to and empower others to do the same.”

Perceptions of the exam and renewal intentions (Figure 3)

Overall perceptions of the certification exam were positive. Nearly nine in ten respondents (89 %) agreed that the APHSW-C accurately reflects the knowledge base required for advanced hospice and palliative social work practice. Many described the exam as “challenging but fair,” requiring applied knowledge rather than rote memorization. The questions reflected what we actually do: communication, ethics, and complex family work.” A smaller group offered constructive feedback, suggesting that future versions could expand content areas, “It focused more on hospice than palliative care,” or better reflect practice diversity, “Pediatrics and cultural aspects need more attention.”

Commitment to the credential was evident in renewal intentions: two-thirds (67 %) planned to renew. Renewal decisions were driven by continued dedication to the field, not financial gain: “I will renew because it represents my professional identity.” Open-ended responses for those uncertain about renewal fell into 3 categories: limited institutional benefit, financial burden, and changing professional circumstances. Many noted seeing “no benefit or real recognition,” making the effort to maintain it not worth the time or money, especially when linked to a lack of reimbursement from their employer. The remainder cited moving out of the field or approaching retirement as reducing their motivation for holding the credential.

Discussion

Findings from this national survey provide initial insights into how the APHSW-C has shaped professional identity and practice in palliative social work. Participants perceived the APHSW-C as both a meaningful validation of expertise and a collective investment in the future of the field. This aligns with broader certification research, which shows that specialty credentials enhance credibility and affirm professionals’ commitment to their field of practice.10,18 Several participants noted that the credential “puts us on equal footing with our physician and nursing colleagues,” reflecting longstanding goals of parity within interprofessional teams.

Beyond individual recognition, respondents viewed the APHSW-C as an important mechanism for workforce development by creating a clearer career pathway and demonstrating the value of advanced palliative social work within interdisciplinary teams. These findings echo broader calls to strengthen social work leadership in palliative care and expand opportunities for professional growth, program development, education, quality improvement, and research. Despite broad enthusiasm for the credential, limited organizational recognition remains a barrier to realizing its full value. Fewer than one-third of respondents received financial support or reimbursement for exam fees, and even fewer received salary incentives. These findings mirror trends in nursing, where institutional support often lags behind clinician commitment to specialty certification. 18

The uneven employer recognition reported by respondents may reflect more than a workforce implementation gap; it may also represent a structural inequity within healthcare organizations, where social work expertise in psychosocial, cultural, and systems-level care is less formally rewarded than the specialty credentials of other disciplines. This lack of recognition has implications not only for social workers’ career advancement, but also for the sustainability of expert psychosocial care for seriously ill patients and families. If advanced social work expertise continues to be undervalued, structural inequities may persist, limiting access to mental health and social services for already disadvantaged and vulnerable populations.

Overall, the development and implementation of the APHSW-C represents a pivotal milestone in the professionalization of palliative social work in the US. Participants viewed certification as enhancing professional identity, credibility, and perceived legitimacy, while also providing a shared framework for advanced practice. However, the APHSW-C can realize its full potential only when institutional structures, salary scales, reimbursement mechanisms, and educational pathways align with the expertise the credential recognizes. Such reflections echo the profession’s call to strengthen social work leadership in palliative care and expand opportunities for professional growth, program development, and education. 19

These findings also identify important implications for employers, educators, professional organizations, and policymakers. Healthcare organizations should formally recognize the APHSW-C as evidence of specialty expertise by incorporating certification into hiring preferences, promotion and career-ladder structures, salary advancement, reimbursement of examination and recertification costs, and protected time for continuing education. Professional organizations, including the American Academy of Hospice and Palliative Medicine (AAHPM), the Center to Advance Palliative Care (CAPC), NASW, SWHPN, and other national palliative care stakeholders, can further advance the credential by educating employers, expanding scholarship opportunities, promoting reimbursement for certification expenses, and integrating certification-aligned competencies into postgraduate training and continuing education. At the policy level, advocacy should extend to CMS and other regulatory bodies to establish mechanisms—such as taxonomy codes, staffing standards, or reimbursement pathways—that formally recognize specialty-trained palliative social workers as essential members of interdisciplinary serious illness care teams. Strengthening the connections among certification, education, employer recognition, and reimbursement will support the long-term sustainability of the APHSW-C and the continued professionalization of hospice and palliative social work.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. The self-selected sample and 21% response rate may limit generalizability and overrepresent certificants who are especially invested in the APHSW-C; optional open-text responses may also overrepresent participants with strong views. Although qualitative responses helped contextualize quantitative findings, the cross-sectional, descriptive mixed-methods design cannot establish causal relationships between certification and professional, institutional, or patient-level outcomes. No a priori power analysis was conducted because the study relied on responses of the APHSW-C certificant population; findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. In addition, APHSW-C status for 29 respondents recruited through the listserv could not be independently verified beyond data checking and self-attestation. Subgroup analyses by role or setting were not feasible because comparable administrative data were unavailable and some subgroup sizes were too small to support meaningful comparisons. Future research should use standardized role and setting data, include employer, interprofessional, and patient/family perspectives, and examine longitudinal economic, clinical, retention, advancement, and workforce diversity outcomes among certified and non-certified palliative social workers.

Conclusion

This national survey suggests that the APHSW-C has become an important marker of advanced specialty practice that strengthens professional identity, credibility, and workforce development in hospice and palliative social work. However, certification alone cannot achieve these goals. Its full value depends on organizational recognition through hiring, promotion, reimbursement, protected professional development time, and policy mechanisms that acknowledge specialty expertise. Aligning certification with educational pathways, employer support, and workforce policy will strengthen the sustainability of the credential while helping ensure that patients and families have access to highly qualified palliative social workers capable of meeting the complex psychosocial needs of serious illness care.
