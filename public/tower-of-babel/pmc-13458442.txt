Soil health and economic evaluations of direct seeded rice through metabolomic and biochemical fingerprinting
Authors: Prabhjit Kaur, Jasvir Singh Gill, Mukesh Chander, Adarsh Pal Vig, Satwinderjeet Kaur, Rajbir Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study was conducted for three consecutive cropping seasons from 2020–2022 in Amritsar of Punjab with the objective to analyze the soil health and economic impact of direct seeded rice (DSR) technology over puddled transplanted rice (PTR). It was recorded high number of average earthworm count in the soil sample of DSR fields (Mean value 124.6) as compared to PTR fields (Mean value 68). The soil health was also improved through DSR technique as various physical parameters i.e. porosity, bulk density & water holding capacity observed better in DSR fields over PTR technique. The soil samples of DSR fields also showed high amount of NPK (N: 219, P: 816, K, 101.4) and mineral content (Mg: 1298, Ca: 1336, Zn: 4.85, Mn: 87.5, Cu: 8.51 & Fe; 9.92) as compared to PTR fields. It was also observed that soil sample of DSR field showed improved carbon composition (HAs, FPA, GRSPs, WSOC, HWSOC, EE) & soil enzymatic activity (DHA, ALK, ACP & PMN) as compared to PTR soil sample. The various reproductive, photosynthetic, and yield parameters of rice crops was observed improved in DSR over the PTR. The HPLC–MS based fingerprinting showed up-regulation of amino acid and phenolics in rice grains grown through DSR technique over the PTR. The cost of field preparation and tillage was reduced by 62.15% in DSR over PTR. The DSR technology resulted into yield improvement by 3.44—18.21% as compared to transplanted rice. Introduction

Global agricultural production systems are increasingly threatened by weather vagaries, the consequences of climate change, depletion of natural resources, and the relentless pressure of a growing population demanding food and diverse bioproducts such as feed, fibre, fuel, and fodder. According to projections, by the year 2050 the global population may reach 9.5 billion, necessitating at least a 1% annual increase in grain production to meet food security requirements.1

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the second most consumed staple food after wheat and it ranks second in global production after maize in the world. The rice cultivation under the Puddled Transplanted Rice (PTR) technique has led to rice being labeled as a “water guzzler” crop and indicate that producing one kilogram of rice requires around 3500 lts. of water, making it one of the most resource-intensive cereals.2 India, as one of the world’s leading rice producers, reflects this challenge acutely. In particular, the state of Punjab, despite occupying only 2.5% of the nation’s geographical area, contributes nearly 20% of rice and 53% of wheat to the national central pool through the predominant rice–wheat cropping system. Punjab’s agricultural success has been underpinned by strong technological innovations, assured procurement at minimum support prices (MSP), and a well-established production and marketing infrastructure. However, this growth has come at a severe cost to groundwater resources.

Current estimates suggest that intensive groundwater extraction in Punjab has reached with 79% of the administrative blocks classified as overexploited.3 During 1966–2017, the proportion of the area irrigated by tube wells increased from 24.5% to 71.5%, largely due to non-functional & inefficient canal irrigation networks, free electricity supply to agri sector, policy-driven promotion of water-intensive crops, and private investment in groundwater extraction.4,5 The overdependence on tube wells has accelerated groundwater depletion, leading to ecological imbalance, increased energy requirements for pumping, higher input costs for farmers, and a widening of socioeconomic disparities.6,7.

Through PTR, puddling is performed to create a soft, saturated seedbed that promotes seedling establishment while minimizing weed infestation by inducing anaerobic conditions for aerobic weeds. However, puddling alters soil’s physical properties by breaking capillary pores, reducing aggregate stability, dispersing fine clay particles, lowering soil strength, and diminishing soil permeability. These edaphic changes results in long-term soil structure degradation, reduced percolation, and impaired root growth of succeeding crops such as wheat, ultimately affecting system productivity. Thus, reliance on puddling and transplanting warrants reconsideration.

In the prevailing scenario of escalating water scarcity, declining labor availability, and environmental concerns, diversification of rice production technologies has become imperative. Reliance on conventional Puddled Transplanted Rice (PTR) has often been criticized for its unsustainable water footprint and associated ecological consequences. Therefore, a paradigm shift toward aerobic rice cultivation, widely recognized as Direct-Seeded Rice (DSR), represents a viable and eco-efficient alternative. The Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), Ludhiana, has developed a comprehensive package of agronomic technologies tailored for DSR cultivation under the agro-ecological conditions of Punjab and adjoining states in northwestern India.

In contrast DSR, involves direct sowing of rice seed into unpuddled, well-prepared, tilled fields, using tractor-mounted or drawn precision seed-cum-fertilizer drills equipped with dual-frame members. These machines generally contain a fertilizer box with fluted roller metering systems and a seed box with inclined plate metering mechanisms to ensure uniform seed placement. The equipment utilizes inverted T-shaped tines to open narrow slits, facilitating proper seed and fertilizer deposition in rows while conserving soil moisture. Agronomically, DSR offers multiple advantages like water savings, labor reduction, enhanced resource-use efficiency, climate change mitigation, and groundwater recharge.8,9.

Considering these comparative benefits, the present investigation was designed to evaluate the adoption of DSR technology at a commercial scale under realistic farmer-field conditions in Amritsar district of Punjab during 2020–2022. This integrated approach provides not only insights into yield and resource-use efficiency but also into nutritional and phytochemical dimensions, bridging agronomy with food quality and human health. The primary objectives were twofold:

To assess the agronomic, ecological, and economic performance of DSR relative to traditional PTR under local conditions.

To assess the agronomic, ecological, and economic performance of DSR relative to traditional PTR under local conditions.

To undertake a comparative nutritional profiling and HPLC–MS-based phenolic metabolomics of rice grains produced under PTR and DSR systems, thereby exploring potential qualitative differences in bioactive metabolites linked to cultivation methods.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The various chemicals and reagents of analytical grade used in the present study were procured from Ranbaxy Laboratories Limited, CDH India and Sigma-Aldrich USA.

Experimental site

The field experiment was conducted over three consecutive kharif seasons (2020, 2021, and 2022) at Village Dashmesh Nagar (31.6313°N, 75.0743°E), near Jandiala Guru, District Amritsar, Punjab, India. The topography of the site was flat and homogeneous with minimizing spatial variability in water flow and nutrient distribution. The soil type of the site was classified as sandy loam, characterized by moderate water-holding capacity, good drainage, and neutral to slightly alkaline pH, making it suitable for cereal–legume–cereal crop rotations, which is widely practiced in the region. The sowing of rice was carried out consistently during the second fortnight of June (onset of monsoon rainfall) and harvesting was completed by the last week of September in all three years, ensuring uniformity in the growth period and environmental exposure. To reduce year-to-year variability, all inputs and cultivation practices, including land preparation, sowing methods, irrigation scheduling, nutrient management, and plant protection measures, were kept consistent throughout 3 years of the study. The dosages and chronology of field operations were executed according to the standard recommendations of the Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), Ludhiana with minor modifications.

The experiment was laid out under a rice–pea–wheat rotation system, which is widely practiced in the region to ensure cropping intensity, soil fertility restoration, and income diversification. The crop sequence followed during the 3 years of the study included:

Rice (Oryza sativa L., cv. Basmati Pusa-1509): a short-duration, high-yielding, aromatic basmati variety, known for its export potential and widespread adoption in Punjab.

Rice (Oryza sativa L., cv. Basmati Pusa-1509): a short-duration, high-yielding, aromatic basmati variety, known for its export potential and widespread adoption in Punjab.

Green Pea (Pisum sativum L., cv. Azad-3): chosen as a rabi legume due to its nitrogen-fixing ability, which improves soil fertility and provides dietary protein.

Green Pea (Pisum sativum L., cv. Azad-3): chosen as a rabi legume due to its nitrogen-fixing ability, which improves soil fertility and provides dietary protein.

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L., cv. HD-3086): a high-yielding variety resistant to rusts and lodging, representing the dominant winter cereal in the rice–wheat cropping system.

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L., cv. HD-3086): a high-yielding variety resistant to rusts and lodging, representing the dominant winter cereal in the rice–wheat cropping system.

All three crops were grown in quick succession without leaving fallow periods, thereby simulating a high-intensity triple-cropping system. Fertilizer application was executed based on recommended doses for each crop: N-P-K levels of 120:60:40 kg ha⁻1 for rice, 20:60:40 kg ha⁻1 for pea, and 120:60:40 kg ha⁻1 for wheat, supplemented with farmyard manure at the beginning of the cycle. Irrigation scheduling was maintained to meet crop-specific critical stages, and integrated weed, pest, and disease management practices were uniformly applied across the study years.

Meteorological parameters

The various meteorological parameters like, rainfall, average temperature, relative humidity, and sunshine hours were recorded daily from the nearest Agro-Meteorological Observatory at Amritsar to account for inter-annual weather variability and its impact on crop performance. The experimental design was framed to ensure that management factors remained constant across study years, thereby allowing evaluation of rice establishment methods (PTR & DSR) as the principal experimental variable. Rice cultivation through DSR method

Land preparation

In the month of April, after the wheat crop was harvested with a combine harvester, the crop residues were subsequently managed using a straw reaper, an implement designed to cut and collect loose straw and standing stubbles. The straw reaper chopped and converted the residues into finely shredded, dry animal fodder referred to as “Turi” (locally) and “wheat bhusa” (scientifically). Following residue management, the experimental plots were subjected to three sequential passes of a disc harrow to incorporate crop residues and break soil clods. The fields were then left fallow for approximately 20 days to facilitate soil solarization, allowing exposure of the soil surface to solar radiation, which contributes to the reduction of weed viability and soil-borne pathogens. Subsequently, the fields were prepared for sowing by Laser Land Leveler, a precision land-shaping technology that improves field uniformity, enhances irrigation efficiency, minimizes water losses due to runoff and ponding, and ensures more uniform crop establishment.

Rice sowing

In the last week of May, rice sowing was done using the Dry Direct-Seeded Rice (DSR) technique with tractor-operated seed drill equipped with an inclined plate metering mechanism, ensuring uniform seed placement and depth. The certified seed of the aromatic basmati variety ‘PUSA-1509’ was purchased from Amritsar Grain Market and used at @ 20 kg ha⁻1. Prior to sowing, seeds were treated with Bavistin (carbendazim @ 2.5 g kg⁻1) to protect against seed and soil borne pathogens. The treated seeds were soaked in water for 2 h to initiate early physiological activity and then air-dried for 8 h to restore safe handling moisture content. To mitigate risks of thermal stress under high ambient summer temperatures, sowing was deliberately performed during the late evening hours.

The experiment was conducted using six plots (1–6), each measuring 1,400 m2 (0.35 acre). For comparative assessment of establishment methods, odd numbered plots (1, 3, & 5) were sown under direct-seeded rice (DSR) system, whereas even numbered plots (2, 4, & 6) were established using the conventional puddled transplanted rice (PTR) method. To prevent lateral seepage of irrigation water between treatments, each plot was demarcated and separated by 1.5 m in width.

In DSR plots, flood irrigation was applied immediately after sowing to facilitate seed imbibition and germination. On third day after the first irrigation, a tank-mix pre-emergence herbicide application was performed using a power sprayer at a spray volume of 500 L ha⁻1. The mixture consisted of Pendimethalin 30% EC @ 2.5 lts ha⁻1 (a pre-emergence herbicide effective against annual grasses and some broadleaf weeds), combined with Pyrazosulfuron-ethyl 10% WP @ 200 g ha⁻1 (a sulfonylurea herbicide targeting sedges and certain broadleaf weeds). This integrated herbicide application was aimed at ensuring early-season weed suppression, a critical requirement for successful DSR establishment.

Rice cultivation through PTR method

Nursery establishment

Adjacent to the main experimental field, a nursery area of 76 m2 was simultaneously prepared following the same field preparation methodology adopted for the DSR plots. To raise seedlings of the same rice variety (‘PUSA-1509’) and of comparable age for Puddled Transplanted Rice (PTR) plots. To ensure adequate nutrient availability during seedling establishment, 2 kg of diammonium phosphate (DAP) per 25.33 m2 was applied as a basal dose, providing both nitrogen and phosphorus for vigorous seedling growth. For nursery sowing, 9 kg of seed from the same certified lot was used. Prior to sowing, the seeds were subjected to seed treatment with Bavistin (carbendazim @ 2.5 g kg⁻1) solution for 12 h. to prevent seed-borne fungal infections. During this process, floating seeds were discarded to ensure only healthy & viable seeds were used. The soaked seeds were then spread as 2-inch thick uniform layer on moist gunny bags and covered with additional wet gunny bags under shaded conditions for 30 h to promote sprouting. The gunny bags were sprinkled with water every 3–4 h to maintain high humidity, facilitating uniform germination. Once sprouted, the seeds were broadcast in standing water in the prepared nursery plots.

Weed management in the nursery was achieved in two stages:

On the day of sowing, Pyrazosulfuron-ethyl 10% WP @ 200 g ha⁻1 was mixed with sand and broadcast uniformly over the nursery bed to suppress early-season weed emergence.

On the day of sowing, Pyrazosulfuron-ethyl 10% WP @ 200 g ha⁻1 was mixed with sand and broadcast uniformly over the nursery bed to suppress early-season weed emergence.

Later, when weed pressure developed, Bispyribac-sodium @ 250 ml ha⁻1 (Nominee Gold) was applied as a post-emergence spray. Prior to herbicide application, standing water was drained from the nursery to ensure maximum herbicide efficacy, and irrigation was resumed 24 h after spraying, followed by flood irrigation to maintain optimum soil moisture.

Later, when weed pressure developed, Bispyribac-sodium @ 250 ml ha⁻1 (Nominee Gold) was applied as a post-emergence spray. Prior to herbicide application, standing water was drained from the nursery to ensure maximum herbicide efficacy, and irrigation was resumed 24 h after spraying, followed by flood irrigation to maintain optimum soil moisture.

The nursery was irrigated on alternate-day basis, with daily irrigation provided during periods of high ambient temperature to avoid moisture stress. After 23 days, healthy and vigorous seedlings were transplanted into the designated PTR plots for comparison with DSR plots.

Land preparation and seedling transplantation

PTR plots were prepared 25 days after DSR sowing following the standard puddling procedure. The field preparation involved ploughing and planking in the same manner as other plots, followed by flooding with approximately 180 mm of irrigation water prior and during puddling. Puddling was performed by churning the upper 10–15 cm soil layer in standing water, a process that disrupted soil aggregates, dispersed fine particles, and facilitated soil particle resettlement. This sequential deposition resulted in the formation of a compact, impervious surface layer that effectively minimized downward water percolation. Once the suspended particles had settled, the standing water becomes clear, signifying completion of the puddling process. In traditional practice, farmers further reinforced the field’s capacity to retain water by plastering field bunds with clay slurry, thereby minimizing lateral seepage losses. Following puddling, manual transplanting of 23 day old seedlings (raised nursery) was undertaken. On the following day, a pre-emergent herbicide, Butachlor 50% EC @ 2.5 lts. ha⁻1, was applied to suppress initial flushes of grassy weeds.

To ensure proper seedling establishment, PTR plots were maintained under 4–5 cm of standing water during the first 20 DAT (Days After Transplanting). This constant flooding approach minimized soil cracking, reduced transplanting shock, and created anoxic conditions that effectively suppressed germination & growth of aerobic weed species. After 4—6 DAT, transplanted seedlings exhibited recovery from shock and initiation of active tillering. After the establishment phase, irrigation scheduling was adjusted. The PTR plots were irrigated every third day from the 21st day of transplanting till the grain filling stage.

Herbicide applications

The herbicide were applied uniformly across all replicates within each treatment and were maintained consistently over the three experimental years (2020–2022). Specifically, Pendimethalin was applied as a pre-emergence herbicide at the recommended dose within 1–2 DAS (Days After Sowing). Pyrazosulfuron and Bispyribac were applied as post-emergence herbicides at their recommended growth stages, following standard agronomic practices. The application rates, timing, and method of application were identical for all replicates within a given treatment and remained unchanged across the three study years to ensure experimental consistency.

Irrigation, fertilization and biotic stress management

In DSR, the second irrigation was applied 7 days after pre-emergence herbicide spray, ensuring uniform crop emergence. Subsequent irrigations were scheduled at 5–7 day intervals, depending on weather conditions. Urea (300 kg ha⁻1) served as the sole nitrogen source and was applied in three equal splits (40 kg each) at 31, 43, 55 DAS.

In PTR plots, 300 kg urea ha⁻1 was applied in three equal splits (40 kg each) at 8, 20, and 32 DAT. To address zinc deficiency, ZnSO₄·H₂O (25 kg ha⁻1) was broadcast 2 doses in DSR and PTR. Additionally, both techniques received two foliar sprays of 1% ferrous sulphate at 15 and 25 DAS & DAT for DSR and PTR respectively.

For pest and disease management, 20% Chlorpyriphos @ 2 lts. ha⁻1 was applied at the maximum tillering stage against stem borer and leaf folder. At the flowering stage, a fungicidal mixture of Azoxystrobin 18.2% + Difenoconazole 11.4% was sprayed. Chlorantraniliprole 8.8% was applied at both booting and grain filling stages to control stem borers and leaf folders. One spray of Pymetrozine was given at the milking stage against rice plant hoppers. Spray volumes were standardized: 500 L ha⁻1 for fungicides and 375 L ha⁻1 for insecticides, applied using a power sprayer.

Crop harvesting & marketing

Rice crop was harvested at physiological maturity, winnowed, and grain yield recorded. Yields were adjusted to 17% grain moisture following the IRRI (Los Baños, Philippines) formula. Random grain samples were collected for various analyses, and the produce was sold at the local grain market (Dana Mandi, Jandiala Guru, Amritsar) under APMC regulations. Harvesting, yield, and selling price data were recorded consistently over all three years of the study i.e. 2020–2022.

Analysis of soil parameters

Earthworm collection & identification

To assess earthworm populations, soil samples were randomly collected from all DSR and PTR plots during May (pre-sowing) and October (post-harvest) in each of the three consecutive study years. Earthworms were extracted by in situ hand-sorting, then euthanized in 70% ethanol and preserved in 4% formalin solution for further examination. Earthworm abundance was quantified using a soil auger, expressed as individuals per cubic foot (ft3), while biomass was measured in the laboratory and expressed as g ft⁻3. Species identification was carried out based on external and internal morphological characteristics, following standard earthworm identification keys.10 Additionally, soil parameters such as pH, color, and texture were recorded for all samples both before sowing and after harvest to evaluate changes in soil properties under the both rice establishment systems.

Analysis of soil quality parameters

In the month of October, after rice harvest the random soil samples were collected at depth of 0–15 cm from both DSR and PTR plots using a bucket auger. For each plot, soil samples were taken randomly from three different spots of each cultivation technique.

For the chemical analysis, soil samples were shade-dried and sieved through a 2-mm mesh. For microbiological and enzymatic assays, fresh samples were stored at 0–4 °C for upto one week to preserve microbial activity. The soil biological parameters viz. Dehydrogenase Activity (DHA), Phosphatase Activity (Alkaline Phosphatase (ALK) & Acid Phosphatase (ACP) and Potentially Mineralizable Nitrogen (PMN) were analyzed. In addition different soil physical properties like, Percentage of Porosity, Capillary Porosity, Non-Capillary Porosity, Water-Holding Capacity and Saturated Hydraulic Conductivity (cm h⁻1) & Bulk Density (g cm⁻3) were also determined. These analyses were conducted to compare the biological activity and physical quality of soils under DSR and PTR cultivation systems.

Analysis of soil macronutrients

After the rice crop harvesting in the month of October (2020–2022), the random soil samples from DSR and PTR fields were collected and the basic physical parameters like color, texture, appearance, temperature, moisture, electrical conductivity (EC), and pH were recorded. For pH & EC measurements, 100 g of soil was mixed with 100 ml distilled water, stirred for 10 min, and allowed to stand. The suspension was kept at room temperature for 24 h before recording pH, and for 1 h before recording EC with pH meter (Model Li-120, Elico) & EC meter (Model 1056).

Soil organic matter (OM), organic carbon (%), & nitrogen (%) were estimated using the Walkley–Black method11 with slight modifications. Briefly, 1 g of dried soil was digested with 10 ml K₂Cr₂O₇ (1 N) and 20 ml concentrated H₂SO₄, swirled gently, and allowed to stand for 30 min. The mixture was then diluted with 200 ml distilled water, followed by 10 ml orthophosphoric acid and 1 ml phenolphthalein indicator. The resulting deep violet solution was titrated against 0.5 N ferrous ammonium sulfate until the color shifted from violet to blue and finally bright green. A blank titration (without soil) was also performed. Calculations were made as: Organic carbon (%) = (X–Y) × 0.003 × 100(X–Y) × 0.003 × 100(X–Y) × 0.003 × 100 / (2 × W) = Z × 1.3 (Where X = blank reading, Y = sample reading, W = soil weight); Organic matter (%) = %C × 1.72; Nitrogen (%) = O.M × 0.05.

Available phosphorus (%) was determined using the modified Olsen method.12 1 g air-dried soil sample was shaken with 20 ml NaHCO₃ (0.5 M) for 30 min, and phosphate concentration was quantified colorimetrically after developing a blue color with molybdate–ascorbic acid reagent, using a flame photometer.

Potassium (%) was analyzed by the ammonium acetate method.13 1 g of soil sample was extracted with 20 ml N-neutral ammonium acetate, filtered through Whatman No. 1 paper, and the filtrate was read on a flame photometer (calibrated with ammonium acetate solution: 77.80 g in 1000 ml distilled water). Total potassium (%) was calculated as: %K = (0.1 × flame value) / (10 × sample weight). In addition, Sulphur concentration in the soil was measured using atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS).

Measurement of mineral content

The mineral content of soil samples from DSR and PTR fields including Ca, Mg, Zn, K, Mn, Cu, and Fe was determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS, Perkin Elmer Analyst, USA). 0.5 g of soil sample was ashed and digested in 2 ml concentrated nitric acid, filtered through ashless Whatman No. 42 filter paper, and the volume was made up to 10 ml with deionized water.

Humic and free phenolic acids

Humic acids (HAs) were extracted from 30 g of lyophilized soil sample using the modified Swift method.14 Free phenolic acids were determined following Krygier.15 10 g of lyophilized soil sample was extracted thrice with 80% methanol, and the solvent was evaporated under vacuum. The residue was dissolved in methanol and reacted with 0.5 mL Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, 1 mL 20% Na₂CO₃, 0.2 mL soil sample, and 8.3 mL water. After incubation (60 min, dark, room temperature), samples were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and absorbance was measured at 725 nm. Results were expressed as µg sinapic acid equivalents (SAE) per g of lyophilized soil sample, using a sinapic acid calibration curve.

Glomalin related soil proteins

Total glomalin-related soil proteins (T-GRSPs) and easily extractable GRSPs (EE-GRSPs) were extracted from lyophilized soil samples using 50 mM citrate buffer (pH 8.0) and 20 mM citrate buffer (pH 7.0), respectively.16 10 g soil sample was autoclaved at 121 °C for 60 min (T-GRSPs) or 30 min (EE-GRSPs), with repeated extractions until the organic fraction was removed. Extracts were pooled, adjusted to equal volume, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the GRSP content in supernatants was quantified by the Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as standard17.

Water-soluble organic carbon

The amounts of water-soluble organic carbon (WSOC) and hot-water-soluble organic carbon (HWSOC) were determined in soil samples using the Haynes and Francis method with slight modifications.18.

Analysis of rice crop parameters

Analysis of physiochemical constituents of rice crop

At the heading stage, fresh leaves of rice plants (DSR and PTR) were analyzed for their chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids following Fadeels19 with contents (mg g⁻1 FW) calculated using the modified Wettstein formula20. Total carbohydrates in fresh leaves were estimated as per Williams.21 Amino acids were extracted from oven-dried leaves with 80% ethanol and quantified following the method of Christias.22.

Growth, yield attributes and yield

In September, at maturity stage, 10 healthy plants were randomly collected from each DSR and PTR plot to record plant height (cm) and panicle density (m⁻2). Yield attributes measured included panicle length, spikelets panicle⁻1, filled grains panicle⁻1, filled grain percentage, panicle grain weight, and 1000-grain weight. Grain yield was recorded at 17% moisture, while straw yield was determined by subtracting grain weight from bundle weight (bundles obtained by beating against a drum before threshing). The harvest index was calculated accordingly. To avoid border effects, the outer rows were harvested separately and excluded from yield calculations.

Analysis of rice grains of PTR & DSR

Plant extraction and post extraction techniques

Rice grains from DSR and PTR experimental fields were washed thoroughly with tap water (thrice) to remove dust and impurities, shade-dried, and ground into fine powder. 500 gms of powdered material was macerated in 500 ml lab grade methanol for 24 h with intermittent shaking at room temperature. The supernatant was collected, concentrated using a rotary vacuum evaporator, and the dried methanolic extracts were stored in zip-lock bags at − 20 °C for further analysis.

Determination of phenolic, flavonoid content and phytochemical screening

The Total Phenolic Content (TPC) and Total Flavonoid Content (TFC) of methanolic rice grain extracts from DSR and PTR were estimated as gallic acid equivalents (GAE) and rutin equivalents (RE), respectively.23,24 Phytochemical screening for alkaloids, reducing sugars, quinones, saponins, coumarins, triterpenoids, resins, flavonoids, and tannins was conducted following standard protocols.25–27.

Amino acid analysis

Amino acids and phenolic compounds in methanolic rice grain extracts (DSR and PTR) were analyzed using a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system. Gradient elution began with 100% solvent A (40 mmol/L Na₂HPO₄, pH 7.8 adjusted with 10 mol/L NaOH) for 1.9 min, then shifted to 57% solvent B (45% acetonitrile, 45% methanol, 10% water) over the next 18.1 min. The gradient reached 100% B at 18.6 min, maintained until 22.3 min, then returned to 100% A by 23.2 min and held until 26 min. The column was maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 2 mL/min.

The method sensitivity was evaluated by determining the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) for individual amino acid standards based on signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. Calibration curves for all amino acids showed good linearity (R2 ≥ 0.99) within the working range. Importantly, the LOQ values obtained for low-abundance amino acids (tyrosine, valine and methionine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, leucine) were sufficiently low to allow reliable quantification of trace concentrations in the samples. All reported amino acid concentrations were above their respective LOQ values, confirming the accuracy and analytical validity of the measurements.

HPLC–MS based metabolomics studies

The methanolic rice grain extracts were filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane and analyzed using a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system equipped with a Luna-C18 reversed-phase column (5.0 µm, 4.6 × 150 mm). Separation was achieved with a gradient elution of Eluent A (150 mM CH₃COONa·3H₂O, 0.05% v/v TEA, 6% v/v acetonitrile, pH 6.4) and Eluent B (acetonitrile:water, 6:4 v/v) at a flow rate of 1 ml/min during a 35-min run. The gradient program was: 0 min (100% A), 5.5 min (80% A:20% B), 15 min (54% A:46% B), 15.5–22.5 min (100% B), and 23–35 min (100% A). Each run was followed by a 5-min washing cycle and 2-min equilibration. The phytochemicals were detected in both ESI positive and negative ionization modes, and their relative abundance was estimated from peak area proportions.

Data processing and multivariate analysis

Spectra obtained from the HPLC system were calibrated and aligned using Shimadzu LC Solution (ver. 121 SPI) with internal lock mass peak picking. Metabolite annotations were generated by matching m/z features against online available directories database within a 5 ppm mass tolerance, and further validated by comparison with a fragmentation library of standards. Ion peak areas were normalized to median values across datasets to minimize offsets and ensure equal weighting of metabolites in multivariate analyses. Statistical analyses, including PCA, correlation patterns, one-way ANOVA, and heatmap visualization, were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0. PCA plots illustrated distribution patterns across datasets, while heatmaps were constructed using Random Forest and Tukey’s HSD-based correlations to highlight the top dysregulated metabolites. Results were expressed as mean ± SE of triplicates.

Economic analysis

The total variable cost (TVC) of agricultural inputs, including seeds, fertilizers, irrigation, power, labor, and machinery, was calculated based on actual market prices. Three key economic indicators were assessed: total income (INR ha⁻1), net return (INR ha⁻1), and return on investment (ROI). Net return was estimated as the difference between total income and TVC, while ROI was calculated as the ratio of total income to total cost. All economic values are expressed in Indian Rupees (INR) with equivalent values in United States Dollars (USD) in parentheses, calculated using an exchange rate of 1 USD = ₹83.

Statistical analysis

All biochemical, soil health, and economic analyses were performed using plot-level data, and statistical analyses were conducted on replication means (n = 3 per treatment). The treatment comparisons were therefore based on three independent replications for DSR and three for PTR. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA to evaluate the effects of cultivation technique (DSR vs PTR), year of study (2020–2022) and their interaction. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s (HSD) test was applied for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Earthworm count

The earthworms count revealed that DSR has edge over the PTR technology. In month of April, before the sowing of rice crop, per cubic feet soil sample of DSR fields showed significantly higher number of earthworms i.e. 109, 126 & 139 (mean value 124.6) as compared to soil sample PTR fields i.e. 65, 71, 68 (mean value 68) for the year 2020, 21, 22 respectively. After crop get harvested, it was notice that again the soil samples DSR fields showed significantly higher number of earthworm count (84, 95, 102) as compared to PTR soil samples (53, 55, 52) in 2020, 2021 & 2022 respectively. Yield of rice crop

The weed biomass reduction under herbicide treatments shows a consistent trend across all study years, with comparable levels of suppression observed in 2020, 2021, and 2022, indicating stable and uniform treatment efficacy over time. The results of the present study confirmed the effect of increased growth and photosynthetic pigments on the yield of DSR and PTR technologies. The direct seeded crop matured in 111, 104 & 101 days while the same crop variety of PTR technique matured in 130, 122 & 118 days in kharif season of 2020, 2021 & 2022 respectively. This might be due to absence of transplanting shock in DSR crop that delayed PTR crop for 18 days to mature. This early maturation trait of DSR technology makes DSR crop fit well in multiple cropping systems, by vacating field earlier. This is much beneficial for the farmer to fetch good selling price of rice in the grain market and offers ample window period for rice residue management and timely sowing of next Zaid crop like pea, potato, cauliflower, French bean, etc. When these high value crops are sown timely then they also fetches early market premium. This early maturity character by virtue of DSR technology and short duration of variety helps in dousing the attack of later generation of insect-pest (Table 6). The average panicle weight (g) & weight of 1000 grains (g) were also higher for the crops raised by DSR technique i.e. 2.85 & 22.46 for the kharif season 2020, 2021 & 2022 respectively, than the crop of PTR technique i.e. 2.50 & 2 0.56 for the kharif season 2020, 2021 & 2022 respectively. This lead to higher grain yield (59.12 q/ha) and percent harvest index (50.33) for the DSR technique than the PTR crop i.e. grain yield, 53.54 q/ha and percent harvest index, 48.66 for the kharif season 2020, 2021 & 2022 respectively. The yield advantage ranged from 3.44—18.21 percent in DSR over PTR during all three cropping seasons. It is important to note that nitrogen application was standardized across all treatments; therefore, the observed variations in yield traits are attributable to the cultivation methods rather than differences in nitrogen availability. Thus, the yield differences primarily reflect the effect of cultivation method and not nitrogen availability. (Table 6).

The greater accumulation of amino acids and phenolic compounds under DSR is driven by its aerobic soil environment relative to PTR. Enhanced oxygen availability in DSR improves root respiration and stimulates microbial-mediated nitrogen mineralization, thereby increasing nitrogen uptake and its assimilation into amino acids. Elevated nitrogen availability supports the synthesis of essential and stress-related amino acids. Concurrently, aerobic conditions can impose mild oxidative cues that activate the Phenylpropanoid pathway, promoting phenolic biosynthesis. Upregulation of Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) enhances the accumulation of phenolic compounds involved in antioxidant defense. In contrast, anaerobic conditions in PTR constrain oxidative metabolism and suppress these pathways. Improved soil structure and balanced redox status under DSR further enhance root function and metabolic efficiency, collectively explaining the elevated amino acid and phenolic content.

HPLC–MS based phenolic metabolomics

The phenolic compounds, especially flavonoids are known as the most important due to their strong antioxidant & antimicrobial potential and are thought to have positive effects on human health. Studies on flavonoid derivatives have shown a wide range of antibacterial, anti-allergic, antiviral, anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities. Therefore, the presence of flavonoids in medicinal plants supports their folkloric use against various disease and infections.

The HPLC–MS analysis detected highest numbers of molecular features i.e. 2416 in DSR grain extract as compared to PTR extract i.e. 2271 in both + ve & -ve ionization modes (Fig. 6). Farm input costs and profitability analysis

Data presented in Table 11 clearly revealed the economic edge of DSR over PTR technique. It was observed that farm expenditures (farm preparation, seed, labour, fertilizers, pesticides, harvesting etc.) are lesser in case of DSR than the PTR technique while the gross & net returns are higher in case of DSR than the PTR technique. The results also showed the 28.8%, 13.9% and 31.6% of net income (INR) through DSR technique than the PTR technique of PB-1509 rice variety of 2020, 2021 & 2022 kharif season respectively with 5–15 days early maturity in 2020–2022. Typically, DSR is established earlier than PTR without growth delays from transplant injury; which hastens physiological maturity and reduces vulnerability to late-season drought.29,30 The previous reports also showed that using DSR into non-puddled soils found 35–57% water savings and the DSR on raised beds decreased water use by 12–60%, and increased yield by 10%, compared with PTR.31.

The economic analysis (Table 11) demonstrates clear profitability gains under DSR, which substantially strengthens its policy relevance, particularly for water-scarce regions. Higher net returns and improved benefit–cost ratios under DSR indicate that the system is not only agronomically viable but also economically attractive for farmers facing rising input costs and limited water availability. Reduced irrigation requirements directly translate into lower production costs and improved resource-use efficiency, aligning with policy priorities on water conservation and climate-resilient agriculture. These findings support the promotion of DSR through targeted policy interventions—such as incentives for water-saving technologies, capacity-building programs, and region-specific extension services—to accelerate its adoption in drought-prone and water-limited agro-ecosystems.

Conclusion

The results of the present study are very much confirmatory with the previous reports and revealed that DSR has several advantages over PTR in addition to higher economic return with ease in execution, less labour intensive and more water productive. The DSR crop matured 15–18 days earlier making crop fit well in multiple cropping systems. The previous studies showed that yield in DSR is often lower than PTR principally owing to poor crop stand and high weed infestation. Moreover, cost for weed control is usually higher than PTR.28 High weed infestation is a major constraint for broader adoption of DSR. On contrary, the results of present study shows higher yield in case of DSR as compared to PTR in all three years of study period which was attributed to increased panicle number, higher 1000 kernel weight, lower sterility percentage and other reproductive and vegetative parameters. This was also get possible due to good crop management & weed practices. Previous reports also showed more yield in DSR as compared to PTR techniques.5,31.

In present study it was observed that the relatively aerobic soil environment under Direct-Seeded Rice (DSR) cultivation enhances root respiration, nutrient mineralization, and oxidative metabolism compared to the predominantly anaerobic conditions in Puddled Transplanted Rice (PTR). The aerobic soil regime may stimulate nitrogen assimilation pathways, leading to increased amino acid biosynthesis. Additionally, moderate oxidative stress and improved oxygen availability under DSR conditions may activate the phenylpropanoid pathway, thereby promoting the accumulation of phenolic compounds as part of plant defense and adaptive metabolic responses. In contrast, prolonged anaerobic conditions in PTR systems can alter redox potential, reduce nutrient availability, and suppress certain secondary metabolic pathways. Furthermore, soil moisture dynamics under cover cropping and irrigation can strongly influence nutrient cycling and crop performance.32.

In the present study, it was noticed that, weeds are the major constraint towards the success of DSR and estimated losses from weeds in rice are 10% and much higher of total grain production.33 During present study, pre-emergence herbicides i.e. Pendimethalin, Pyrazosulfuron ethyl 20 g /ha, Almix were applied within 2 DAS and post emergent herbicides thiobencarb, oxadiazon, nitrofen were also applied, alone or supplemented with hand weeding, in all the three years of the study i.e. 2020–2022. At the end of the study, it was concluded that on the face of global water scarcity and escalating labour rates, DSR offers an attractive alternative. Despite controversies, if properly managed, higher yield may be obtained from DSR compared with PTR with early maturity of the crop, good return and less methane emission.

The nitrogen fertilizer was applied in split doses (three equal splits of 40 kg each to DSR & PTR plots) and this staggered application likely improved nitrogen use efficiency and reduced the availability of excess mineral nitrogen at any single time point, thereby potentially mitigating peak nitrification denitrification events that contribute to N₂O emissions. It was also emphasized that optimized nutrient management, including split nitrogen applications synchronized with crop demand, may help minimize emission risks in DSR systems alongside future genetic and agronomic interventions. The present study was conducted within a single agro-climatic zone; therefore, the results may vary across regions with differing climatic conditions, soil properties, and management practices, and should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to other environments. Additionally, we recommend that future studies include multi-location and multi-season trials to validate the consistency and wider applicability of the results.
