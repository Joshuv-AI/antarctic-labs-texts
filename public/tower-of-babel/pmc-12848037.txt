Exploring the links between emotional intelligence resilience support and mental health in college students
Authors: Tingting Wang, Ping Zhu, Yanping Fu, Soo-rang Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines how emotional intelligence (EI) relates to mental health among college students and whether psychological resilience and social support mediate this association. Using purposive and snowball sampling, we collected 706 valid online questionnaires (October–November 2023). Data were analyzed with structural equation modeling (SEM) in AMOS 26.0. EI showed significant positive associations with psychological resilience, social support, and mental health; resilience and social support were each positively associated with mental health. Mediation tests (bootstrap, 5,000 resamples) indicated that resilience and social support partially mediated the EI–mental health relationship. Introduction

Mental health issues among college students have increasingly become an important global concern. The American College Health Association (ACHA) reported that approximately 77% of students experience some degree of psychological stress, 35% of surveyed students were diagnosed with anxiety disorders, and 27% of college students were reported to have depression. In 2023, the Institute of Psychology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences found that about 21.48% of college students may be at risk for depression, and 45.28% may be at risk for anxiety. Particularly in the post-pandemic era, college students are facing unprecedented mental health challenges, including increased academic pressure, a tough job market, and changes in social relationships, among other multiple stressors1. This significant increase not only affects students’ academic performance and quality of life but also presents a huge challenge to the mental health services system in higher education institutions. Existing research on college students’ mental health has mainly focused on two perspectives: risk factor identification and protective mechanism studies. From the perspective of risk factors, scholars have primarily focused on academic pressure, employment anxiety, interpersonal relationships, and other aspects2. For example, a large-scale longitudinal study showed that improved emotional intelligence and mindfulness were associated with reduced academic stress and enhanced motivation3. Furthermore, excessive use of social media and internet addiction have also been confirmed as significant risk factors affecting college students’ mental health4. From the perspective of protective mechanisms, researchers have explored various positive factors that may enhance mental health, such as social support, coping strategies, and psychological resilience5. However, although existing studies have extensively explored the factors influencing college students’ mental health, the role of emotional intelligence—this core psychological trait—in mental health protection remains under-researched. Emotional intelligence, defined as an individual’s ability to recognize, understand, and regulate emotions, may play a key role in maintaining mental health6. Recent meta-analytic studies have found that high emotional intelligence is significantly correlated with lower depression risk and better psychological adaptation (r = 0.52, p < 0.01)7.

So, emotional intelligence (EI) might play a big role in mental health by several ways. First of all, prior research suggests that people with high EI seem to be really good at figuring out their own feelings. This means they can notice when something’s off with their mental health and start dealing with it pretty quickly8. But also, folks with higher EI usually get al.ong better with others, which makes it easier for them to ask friends for help when stressed out. This support is like having a safety net during tough times.

Plus, prior research suggests that there’s this link between emotional intelligence and being able to bounce back from problems—kinda like a psychological rebound effect9. So basically, EI could boost mental health by making people tougher mentally.

This study I came across aims to figure out how EI impacts college students’ mental health using the positive psychology theory framework. It proposes that EI improves mental health through increasing resilience and getting social support. Positive psychology focuses on strengths and resources that protect our mental wellbeing10.

The goal here involves three things: checking how directly EI affects student mental health, seeing if resilience and social support are the bridges connecting EI to better mental health, and giving universities ideas for useful mental health programs. In the end, this research should give more clarity on what drives student’s mental well-being while guiding practical university interventions. Although there might be some flaws in understanding these connections fully yet, I’m planning to explore this topic further!

Literature review

Emotional intelligence theory

Emotional intelligence theory was first proposed by Salovey and Mayer, who defined emotional intelligence as “the ability to monitor one’s own and others’ emotions, to discriminate between different emotions, and to use this information to guide thinking and behavior.” Goleman further expanded this concept, suggesting that emotional intelligence includes four dimensions: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management. Numerous studies have shown that individuals with high emotional intelligence are better at regulating negative emotions and exhibit fewer symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress11. At the same time, emotional intelligence promotes the experience of positive emotions, increases individual well-being, and enhances psychological resilience. Additionally, some studies have found that emotional intelligence can significantly predict students’ academic performance, job satisfaction, and stress management ability12 In other words, students with higher emotional intelligence are better able to manage stress and perform better academically. The expression and impact of emotional intelligence may vary across different cultural and social contexts. In collectivist cultures, emotional intelligence tends to promote group harmony, while in individualist cultures, it emphasizes support for individual goals13.

Within the context of college students, improving emotional intelligence can effectively alleviate psychological health issues such as depression and anxiety. This study aims to fill the gap in existing research by exploring the relationships among emotional intelligence, psychological resilience, social support, and mental health, in order to better understand the mechanisms behind emotional intelligence development.

EI to social support

Studies show that individuals with higher emotional intelligence tend to have richer social support networks. This is primarily because they are able to accurately recognize others’ emotional states and exhibit better emotional regulation skills in social interactions. Individuals with higher emotional intelligence also perceive more external support and are better able to learn prosocial behaviors20. This helps them better cope with confusion brought by external factors and better adjust their psychological states21. This positive association can be verified from multiple dimensions. First, at the interpersonal level, individuals with higher emotional intelligence display greater empathy and emotional regulation abilities. They not only accurately interpret nonverbal cues but also flexibly use emotional strategies in social contexts. This ability allows them to establish and maintain good relationships, thus gaining more social support resources22. Second, from the perspective of cognitive processing, individuals with higher emotional intelligence tend to have superior emotional cognition abilities and emotion-regulation strategies. These individuals can better understand complex social situations and respond appropriately to emotional cues in stressful contexts. This ability makes it easier for them to gain recognition and support from others, forming a stable social support network23. From a practical application perspective, intervention studies have also confirmed that improving emotional intelligence can effectively promote individuals in gaining more social support24. Designed emotional intelligence training programs have shown that participants’ self-efficacy increased after improving their emotional recognition and management abilities. This further supports the close relationship between the two.

Resilience to social support

Psychological resilience is a stable trait that reflects an individual’s ability to effectively cope with adversity or trauma27. Research has found that individuals with higher psychological resilience are more likely to receive social support28. This may be because individuals with higher resilience are better at establishing and maintaining social relationships. Studies show that individuals with higher psychological resilience tend to exhibit better interpersonal skills and are more adept at seeking and utilizing social support resources. This ability enables them to more effectively mobilize their social support network in stressful situations. Furthermore, students with higher psychological resilience report more social support resources after their first year in university, are more likely to form deeper friendships with classmates, and are better at maintaining positive interactions with family members. Additionally, there is a significant positive predictive relationship between psychological resilience and social support29. This finding has been validated across different cultural backgrounds and age groups. In other words, psychological resilience not only helps individuals acquire social support, but the support they receive also further enhances their psychological resilience, creating a virtuous cycle.

Resilience to mental health

The relationship between psychological resilience and mental health has been widely verified in psychological research. A large number of studies show that psychological resilience not only significantly predicts mental health but also promotes the maintenance and improvement of mental health through various mechanisms30,31. The level of psychological resilience is a significant predictor of college students’ mental health. This positive correlation suggests that individuals with higher psychological resilience tend to have better mental health. Psychological resilience plays a significant role in enhancing well-being. Higher psychological resilience is associated with stronger functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the amygdala, a neural mechanism that enhances emotional regulation abilities, thus promoting the maintenance of mental health. Moreover, psychological resilience enables individuals to cope more effectively with stressful situations and adopt positive emotional regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal and problem-solving. It also helps individuals access and utilize social support resources more easily, thereby alleviating stress and improving mental health32. In summary, a large body of research supports that enhancing psychological resilience can significantly improve individuals’ mental health.

Social support to mental health

Social support refers to the emotional and material support that an individual receives from their social relationships, including emotional support, informational support, and practical assistance from family, friends, and classmates33. A large number of studies have shown that social support has an important impact on mental health. Social support influences mental health through life satisfaction and psychological resilience. When individuals receive social support, they experience a buffering effect that reduces the negative impact of stress on mental health, directly promoting positive emotional experiences and thereby reducing the risk of psychological problems. The positive correlation between students’ perceived social support and mental health has been validated across different cultural backgrounds. This relationship is particularly prominent in the college student population. In conclusion, social support is significantly positively correlated with college students’ mental health. Increasing students’ social support levels may help improve their mental health and reduce the occurrence of psychological problems.

Validity analysis

Convergent validity refers to the degree of correlation between the measurement results of items that measure the same construct. When the standardized factor loadings, Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and Composite Reliability (CR) for each measurement item on the corresponding construct are above 0.5, the construct is considered to have good convergent validity. In this study, we used AMOS software to calculate the standardized factor loadings for each measurement item. From the standardized factor loadings, we then computed the corresponding latent variables’ Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) to test for convergent validity. Discussion

Theoretical contributions

This study suggests that emotional intelligence (EI) is positively associated with students’ mental health both directly and indirectly via psychological resilience and social support. By positioning resilience and social support as potential resource-based mechanisms that may help explain the EI–mental health association, our findings extend prior correlational evidence in college populations. From an applied perspective, EI-oriented training and campus-based resilience and peer/mentor-support initiatives may be promising components of mental health promotion programs in higher-education settings, although causal effects require further verification in longitudinal or intervention studies.

It can also enhance university students’ psychological resilience and social support, thus influencing their mental health. Previous studies have focused more on the direct impact of emotional intelligence on individual characteristics, such as depression and anxiety34. This study provides empirical evidence that emotional intelligence not only improves university students’ depression and anxiety but also enhances other dimensions of mental health, contributing to the literature and expanding the application of emotional intelligence theory in the Chinese cultural context.

Thirdly, this study highlights the mediating role of psychological resilience and social support in the relationship between emotional intelligence and mental health, deepening the understanding of factors influencing university students’ mental health.

Practical implications

The findings of this study have important practical implications for mental health education in universities. Firstly, the results emphasize the key role of emotional intelligence in university students’ mental health, suggesting that universities should incorporate emotional intelligence development into their mental health education systems. Specifically, universities could offer courses on emotional management, conduct emotional awareness training, and design contextualized practical activities to help students improve their ability to recognize and regulate emotions.

In terms of cultivating psychological resilience, this study confirms its significant mediating role, providing a new entry point for university mental health education. Universities can offer workshops on psychological resilience training, design stress management courses, and provide platforms for peer support to help students improve their ability to cope with setbacks and stress. At the same time, establishing a peer support system is essential for offering practical opportunities and experience-sharing platforms for students. The construction of social support networks should also not be overlooked. The study found that social support plays an important mediating role in the relationship between emotional intelligence and mental health, which suggests that universities need to focus on building a multi-level campus social support system. This includes strengthening teacher-student interactions, promoting peer support, optimizing accessibility to counseling services, and establishing a home-school support mechanism. By building a multidimensional social support network, universities can provide robust support for the development of students’ mental health.

Limitations

Although this study has achieved some research outcomes, there are still several limitations that need to be addressed and improved upon in future research. The primary limitation concerns the representativeness of the sample. The participants in this study were mainly university students from a single province, and this geographic limitation may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should expand the sample scope to include students from different regions and universities of various types to enhance the external validity of the results.

Secondly, the limitations in the research design should not be ignored. This study used a cross-sectional design, which, although able to reveal the correlations between variables, cannot confirm the causal relationships between them. Future research could adopt a longitudinal tracking design or experimental research design to more accurately reveal the causal relationships and developmental changes between emotional intelligence, psychological resilience, social support, and mental health through long-term tracking or experimental interventions.

Furthermore, in terms of research methods, this study mainly relied on self-report scales for data collection. This single measurement method may introduce common method bias. Future studies could consider employing a variety of data collection methods, combining self-reports with peer evaluations, or introducing objective measurement indicators and behavioral observation data. By cross-validating with multiple measurement techniques, the reliability of the data and the robustness of the research conclusions can be enhanced.

Conclusion

In sum, emotional intelligence is positively associated with college students’ mental health, with psychological resilience and social support serving as partial mediators in our proposed model. The model demonstrated good fit to the data. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating EI development with resilience-building and multi-level support systems on campus. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional and self-report nature of this study, future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs and incorporate multi-informant or behavioral measures to strengthen causal inference, improve generalizability, and reduce common-method bias.
