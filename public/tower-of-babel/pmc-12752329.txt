Background

Malaria is one of the most important tropical diseases, with Plasmodium falciparum infection carrying the highest risk of severe disease and death. Cerebral malaria (CM) is the most serious neurological complication, occurring in approximately 2% of cases, and is associated with a mortality rate of up to 50%, even with intensive care [1, 2].

The pathogenesis of CM is multifactorial and involves the sequestration of parasitized erythrocytes within the cerebral microvasculature, inflammatory cytokine release, and impaired microcirculatory perfusion, culminating in neuronal injury and cerebral oedema [3].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a valuable tool for characterizing cerebral involvement. In paediatric CM, MRI has demonstrated that increased brain volume is strongly associated with mortality, whereas survivors often show reversible oedema. Typical MRI changes include FLAIR nonspecific hyperintensities located in the bilateral periventricular white matter (PVWM), corpus callosum, occipital subcortical, basal ganglia, and bilateral thalamic regions. Susceptibility-Weighted Imaging (SWI) shows regions of hypointensity representing either petechial haemorrhages or regions of haemorrhage in infarcted tissue [4]. However, in adults, imaging findings are less frequently reported, and computed tomography (CT) scans may underestimate the degree of cerebral involvement, appearing normal despite significant pathology.

We report here an adult patient with fulminant cerebral malaria, whose MRI findings illustrated cytotoxic oedema, highlighting the diagnostic role of advanced neuroimaging in atypical or severe cases where CT may be unrevealing.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman with a known case of hypertension and hypothyroidism presented to the emergency department with a history of fever, chills, and dizziness for 7 days, multiple episodes of generalized tonic–clonic seizures over the preceding 3 days, and loss of consciousness for 2 days. Her medication history included antihypertensive (cilnidipine 10 mg) and thyroxine 50 mcg.

At presentation to the emergency department (ED), her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was E3V2M5 without any focal neurological deficit. She had tachycardia with a heart rate of 110/min and hypotension with a blood pressure of 88/54 mmHg. Her room-air oxygen saturation was 86%. Arterial blood gas analysis (ABG) revealed severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.129, HCO3⁻ 9.7 mmol/L) with high serum lactate (9.8 mmol/L) and blood glucose (46 mg/dL) levels. In view of severe metabolic acidosis with hypotension, she was intubated, and fluid resuscitation and vasopressors were initiated. Empirical antibiotics and anti-epileptics were started, and the patient was shifted to the intensive care unit(ICU).

Laboratory investigations showed increased creatinine (3.97 mg/dL), hyperkalaemia (serum potassium 7.0 mmol/L), and total bilirubin (13.0 mg/dL) levels. A rapid diagnostic immunochromatographic test for malaria was positive for P. falciparum, which was later confirmed with a peripheral smear report. Intravenous artesunate was administered, and the patient underwent haemodialysis. A non-contrast CT of the head was performed for altered sensorium but revealed no abnormalities. Lumbar puncture revealed cerebrospinal fluid with no pleocytosis; glucose and protein levels were within normal limits. Gradually, her neurological status deteriorated to a GCS of E1V1M1, and an MRI brain and electroencephalography (EEG) were performed. EEG was normal (Fig. 1), but MRI of the brain showed subtle altered signal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the bilateral basal ganglia, thalami, and bilateral centrum semiovale (Figs. 2 and 3) and diffusion-weighted imaging showed left peritrigonal periventricular white matter showing patchy areas of restricted diffusion (Fig. 4). Focal altered signal subcortical T2/FLAIR hyperintensity with restricted diffusion was noted in the right occipital lobe (Fig. 5). SWI demonstrated no microhaemorrhages in the cortex, deep gray nuclei, or corpus callosum (Fig. 6). Despite the early initiation of antimalarial therapy, critical care support, and organ support measures, her condition continued to worsen. She developed refractory multiorgan failure and succumbed to her illness on the twelfth day of ICU admission. Discussion

Cerebral malaria is a severe manifestation of P. falciparum infection, characterized by impaired consciousness in the presence of parasitaemia and the absence of alternative causes of encephalopathy. Despite being a clinical diagnosis, imaging can provide vital insights into disease severity, complications, and prognosis, especially in atypical or fulminant presentations.

In our case, the patient presented with seizures, coma, and multiorgan dysfunction, and MRI revealed bilateral T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the thalami, basal ganglia, centrum semiovale, and periventricular white matter, with patchy restricted diffusion and right occipital white matter diffusion restriction, findings compatible with cytotoxic oedema. These features are consistent with earlier reports of CM-associated imaging abnormalities involving the deep gray matter, particularly the basal ganglia and thalami, which are considered hallmark findings in both paediatric and adult patients with CM [5]. The spectrum of neuroimaging changes in CM ranges from normal findings to diffuse brain swelling, haemorrhagic infarcts, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)-like patterns [6, 7]. In a prospective MRI study of 152 children with CM, Potchen et al. reported basal ganglia abnormalities in over 80% and significant brain swelling in nearly 50%, with some progressing to herniation and death [4]. Similarly, Mohanty et al. identified vasogenic oedema with PRES-like imaging changes in adults and children, emphasizing the reversible nature of these lesions with appropriate therapy [8]. Unlike most adult reports describing mixed vasogenic and cytotoxic injury with frequent haemorrhagic transformation, our patient’s early MRI demonstrated isolated cytotoxic oedema without SWI-evident microhaemorrhages.

The cytotoxic oedema seen in our case was due to impaired microcirculation secondary to the sequestration of parasitized erythrocytes, a known pathogenic mechanism in CM. Microvascular obstruction, endothelial activation, and inflammatory cytokines contribute to hypoperfusion, metabolic derangement, and ultimately, ischaemic or haemorrhagic injury [4, 9].
