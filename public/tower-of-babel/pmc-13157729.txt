Endogenous neoantigen-specific antibodies mediate antitumor responses and determine vaccine efficacy
Authors: Sirajbir S Sodhi, John S Wang, David T Severson, Joey V Ragusa, Nicole Moon, Timothy Trotter, Li-Chung Tsao, Herbert Kim Lyerly, Zachary Conrad Hartman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe contribution of B cells to antitumor immunity remains controversial, with studies reporting varied effects across cancer types. Even less is known about the role of the endogenous humoral response, including when tumor-elicited antibodies are protective, when they are deleterious, and how they might be modulated to influence antitumor immunity. Critically, it remains unclear whether specific antigenic features govern the efficacy of humoral immune responses against cancer. We aim to define the conditions under which endogenous antibodies mediate antitumor immunity and to leverage these principles to improve neoantigen-directed immunotherapies, including cancer vaccines.MethodsWe performed a pan-cancer analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) to assess associations between intratumoral IgG and clinical outcomes. Using syngeneic mouse tumor models expressing membrane or cytoplasmic neoantigens, we examined how antigen localization influences endogenous antibody responses. We evaluated strategies to enhance humoral immunity via cytokine and chemokine modulation and assessed antibody responses in the context of cancer vaccination.ResultsOur pan-cancer TCGA analysis revealed marked heterogeneity in the prognostic impact of intratumoral IgG. In vivo, expression of membrane-localized, but not cytoplasmic, neoantigens drove potent class-switched IgG responses, activated myeloid cells, and restricted tumor growth independently of CD8 T cells. Notably, membrane-restricted antigen expression was sufficient to convert tumor types such as colorectal cancer, where IgG correlates with poor prognosis, into settings in which antibodies mediate tumor suppression. These effects required CD4 T-cell help, antigen-specific IgG, and Fc receptor engagement. Enhancing B-cell recruitment (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13) or B-cell help (interleukin (IL)-21) further amplified antibody-mediated tumor control, with co-expression providing the strongest benefit. Finally, we establish that antigen localization dictates vaccine efficacy: the point-mutated trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (Trop2) T256R, which exhibits impaired membrane localization and reduced antibody binding relative to wild-type Trop2, failed to elicit comparable vaccine-induced tumor control.ConclusionsThese findings identify antigen subcellular localization as a key regulator of endogenous antibody-mediated antitumor immunity and cancer vaccine efficacy, providing a mechanistic framework for leveraging humoral immunity in immunotherapeutic strategies. CXCL13 and IL-21 emerge as candidate approaches to selectively enhance antibody-mediated tumor control in settings where membrane-bound antigens are present.Keywords: Antibody, B cell, Adaptive, Humoral, Vaccine

B cells have been implicated in both tumor control and immune suppression, but their precise role in antitumor immunity remains unclear. The contribution of endogenous, tumor-elicited antibodies has not been systematically examined, including which antigenic features govern their effectiveness. This gap is particularly critical given that neoantigen therapies have historically focused on enhancing T-cell responses yet have encountered variable clinical efficacy.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We show that endogenous, tumor-elicited antibodies can independently mediate antitumor immunity when neoantigens are membrane-bound, even in tumor models typically considered unresponsive to antibodies. We identify antigen subcellular localization as a primary determinant of functional humoral immunity and demonstrate that single point mutations can selectively alter antibody recognition without affecting T-cell recognition, thereby significantly influencing vaccine efficacy. In addition, we show that B cell-supportive cues such as interleukin (IL)-21 and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13) selectively enhance tumor control only in the presence of membrane-bound antigens.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings may reshape how neoantigens are stratified for immunotherapy development, particularly in the context of cancer vaccines. Our results highlight the need to incorporate structural and subcellular localization analyses to more accurately predict neoantigen immunogenicity and therapeutic relevance, challenging neoantigen selection pipelines that prioritize T cell-centric features alone. In this context, targeting B cell-supportive pathways such as CXCL13 and IL-21 may represent rational adjunct strategies in tumors expressing membrane-bound neoantigens, expanding current frameworks for immunotherapy stratification.

Background

Advances in tumor sequencing and immunopeptidomics have revealed a vast repertoire of candidate neoepitopes that could serve as potential targets for cancer immunotherapy through vaccines and other neoantigen-directed modalities; however, the clinical benefit of such therapies remains constrained by variable efficacy and resistance.1,3 That discrepancy suggests that current frameworks for target selection, which focus primarily on major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-binding predictions and T cell-mediated recognition, capture only a fraction of the biology underlying therapeutic response.4 5

This assumption is further reflected in studies of immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI), where responsiveness is linked to neoantigen-driven cytotoxic T-cell activity.6 However, this perspective overlooks a critical component of adaptive immunity, particularly the humoral response. In many disease settings, antibodies are a major determinant of therapeutic efficacy, as demonstrated across infectious and autoimmune contexts, and increasingly in cancer, where antibodies to tumor neoantigens can be detected in patients receiving ICI.7 8

Although growing evidence underscores the importance of B cells in cancer biology, the role of the antibody response remains poorly defined. Across tumor types, antibody responses display heterogeneous effects, with some mediating tumor control,9,14 while others promote tolerance.15,17 Thus, a framework is needed to predict how antibodies influence immune-mediated tumor control, and defining this biological context is crucial for incorporating humoral immunity into therapeutic design.

The variability of antibody responses across cancers may, in part, reflect differences in the physical localization of target antigens, which determines whether antibodies can effectively recognize and engage tumor cells. Unlike T cells, which recognize intracellular peptides presented on MHC molecules, IgG antibodies generally require direct access to epitopes displayed on the cell surface to activate effector functions, such as antibody-dependent cytotoxicity (ADCC) and phagocytosis (ADCP). As a result, IgG antibody responses against tumor-expressed antigens and neoantigens can mediate tumor killing only when the target is accessible at the membrane. Yet, most neoantigen selection approaches do not consider subcellular localization, even though it plays a major role in determining whether antibodies can be effective.4 5 Moreover, the spatial organization of the cancer proteome is poorly characterized, and oncogenic mutations can disrupt protein trafficking and structure, complicating the reliable prediction of neoantigen subcellular localization.18 19 Consequently, the localization of many tumor neoantigens, as well as their availability for antibody-mediated mechanisms, remains unclear.

Nevertheless, HER2 therapies provide a clear example of how antibodies function when a target is consistently present at the cell surface. In previous studies, we have defined how trastuzumab and pertuzumab activate innate immune effector cells,20 21 as well as how antibody-drug conjugates with added cytotoxic activity can also elicit adaptive responses.22 But these systems differ sharply from most tumor neoantigens, whose localization is far less predictable, and they motivate our efforts to understand how antibodies function in these more variable settings.

To address these challenges, we first assessed the impact of intratumoral IgG expression across all cancer types to define the prevalence and heterogeneity of the humoral immune response. Guided by these patterns, we selected tumor contexts exhibiting distinct associations with IgG and engineered corresponding tumor models to express model neoantigens (ovalbumin (OVA) and enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP)), both of which are known to elicit robust adaptive immune responses in immune-competent settings,23 24 to interrogate adaptive immune responses. We hypothesized that altering the cellular localization of these model neoantigens would clarify the contributions of B cells and antibody-mediated immunity. Finally, we extended this approach to a neoantigen form of the tumor-associated antigen trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (Trop2). The mutant Trop2 contains a single cancer-specific point mutation, which we used to examine how changes in antibody affinity and protein localization translate into differences in antitumor efficacy. Collectively, our investigation provides a framework for leveraging humoral immunity in cancer to inform the design of immunotherapeutic strategies that elicit antibody-driven tumor control.

Methods

Cell lines

CT26 and B16F10 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection via the Duke Cell Culture Facility, and E0771 cells were purchased from CH3 Biosystems. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. CT26 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin, and 1% streptomycin. E0771 and B16F10 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin, and 1% streptomycin.

Cell lines were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding membrane-bound or cytoplasmic OVA. These plasmids were generated from cloning OVAs from pCI-neo-cOVA/mOVA (from Maria Castro, Addgene plasmids #25097/25099) into pENTR1A vectors and recombining these with pLX301 to generate lentiviral puromycin selection vectors. Lentivirus was generated from these plasmids (along with an empty-vector control) and used to infect cells at an multiplicity of infection <1. Puromycin selection concentrations were 10 µg/mL for CT26, 3 µg/mL for E0771, and 2 µg/mL for B16F10. OVA expression was confirmed by western blot.

CT26 cells were also engineered to express murine interleukin (IL)-21, murine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13, eGFP, or pHR-eGFP (Addgene plasmid #79129). Expression of IL-21 and CXCL13 was verified by ELISA (IL-21: R&D Systems DY594; CXCL13: BioLegend 441907), and eGFP expression was confirmed by flow cytometry.

The T256R-mutant TROP2 construct was generated using the QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent) with the following primer: 5′-CTGCAGGTGGAGCGCAGGCTCATCTATTACCTG-3′. Successful expression of the mutant protein was confirmed by flow cytometry and western blot.

All cloning procedures were performed using the Gateway cloning system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cloning details are available on request.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors were generated in 293T cells using second-generation packaging plasmids. Viral supernatants were collected 24 hours after transfection and concentrated overnight at 4°C by adding PEG 8000 and NaCl. The precipitated virus was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and added to culture medium containing 1 µg/mL polybrene. Target cells were incubated for 24 hours before subculturing, and transduced populations were selected either by puromycin resistance or by sorting for eGFP-positive cells.

Animal procedures

All animal experiments were conducted at Duke University. BALB/c (BALB/cAnNTac), SCID-beige (C.B-Igh-1b/GbmsTac-Prkdcscid-Lystbg N7; model CBSCBG), JH BALB/c (C.Cg-Igh-Jtm1Dhu), and Fcεr1γ⁻/⁻ (C.129P2(B6)-Fcer1gtm1Rav N12) mice were obtained from Taconic Biosciences, and JB6 albino (B6(Cg)-Tyrc-2J/J) and IgHEL (C57BL/6-Tg(IghelMD4)4Ccg/J) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. IgHEL mice were crossed onto a BALB/c background for one generation prior to use. Fcεr1γ⁻/⁻ mice were backcrossed for at least 12 generations. Male and female mice (6–14 weeks) were used for experiments.

For tumor studies, cells were suspended in sterile PBS with 50% Matrigel/PBS and implanted subcutaneously into the flank or orthotopically into the inguinal mammary fat pad (E0771). Tumors were measured by calipers at the indicated time points and tumor volume was calculated as (length×width²)/2. Mice were sacrificed at tumor burden 2,000 mm3.

Adenoviral production and vaccination

Adenoviral vectors were generated using Gateway cloning to recombine ENTRY plasmid clones into pAd-CMV5 vectors (Invitrogen) using LR clonase. Vectors were linearized using PacI and adenoviral stock amplified using previously described techniques.25 Animals were vaccinated with 1×10¹⁰ viral particles of adenoviral vectors via footpad injection in 25 µL of A195 buffer diluted with PBS, under isoflurane anesthesia.26

Depletion studies

Depleting antibodies were from Bio X Cell: anti-CD8α (53–6.7, BE0004-1), anti-CD4 (GK1.5, BE0003-1), and anti-CSF1R (AFS98, BE0213). Anti-Asialo GM1 (Fujifilm Wako, 986–10001) was used for natural killer (NK) cell depletion. Mice received 500 µg 1 week before tumor implantation, 200 µg 2 days before, and 200 µg weekly thereafter. Anti-CSF1R was also given at 300 µg two times per week. Anti-Asialo GM1 was administered at 50 µL initially, followed by 20 µL two times per week.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein lysates were resolved by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were probed with anti-ovalbumin (ab181688) and anti-Trop2 (EPR20043), then IRDye secondaries (LI-COR 926–68072; 926–32211). Blots were imaged on a LI-COR CXL system.

ELISpot

As described previously,22 splenocytes (5×10⁵ per well) were plated on interferon (IFN)-γ-coated plates and stimulated with SIINFEKL or Trop2 T256R peptide pools, the latter consisting of overlapping 15-mer peptides spanning the mutation junction. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin served as positive controls. Plates were developed using anti-IFN-γ antibodies and were quantified using a Cytation 7 imager.

ELISA

Serum was collected at sacrifice. Plates were coated with ovalbumin (Sigma-Aldrich) or recombinant Aequorea victoria GFP (Abcam, ab84191) and incubated with serially diluted serum samples. Bound antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibodies against total IgG (Cell Signaling Technology) or IgG subclasses (SouthernBiotech: IgG1 1070–05, IgG2a 1081–05, IgG2b 1091–05, IgG2c 1078–05), developed with 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), and read at 450 nm. Comparative ELISAs used samples collected on the same day.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using BLOXALL (Vector Laboratories). Sections were stained with antibodies against CD4 (Cell Signaling Technology, clone D7D2Z) and CD19 (Cell Signaling Technology, clone D4V4B), followed by ImmPRESS polymer detection and DAB development (Vector Laboratories). Slides were counterstained and imaged by light microscopy. CD4 T cells and CD19 B cells were quantified from five randomly selected fields per tumor.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from tumors and tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLN) as previously described.22 For all experiments, approximately 5–10×10⁶ cells were used for staining. Fc receptor blockade (BioLegend 101319) was applied prior to antibody staining. Flow cytometry was performed on a Cytek Northern Lights Aurora spectral cytometer, and live cells were identified using ViaDye Red or Live Dead Aqua viability dye. Endpoints and population quantification strategies are described in the corresponding figure legend. Gating strategies are shown in online supplemental figures 10–12.

Trop2: Trop2 expression was assessed using an antibody-based staining approach, as depicted in Figure 6. Single-cell suspensions were prepared, and cells were incubated with sacituzumab (MedChemExpress) and AF488 anti-human IgG1 H+L secondary (Invitrogen) or PE-conjugated anti-Trop2 (clone NY18, BioLegend) to detect surface and intracellular Trop2. All staining was performed after Fc receptor blocking and samples were acquired on the Cytek Northern Lights Aurora.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing count data and clinical information for The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cancer types were processed to generate transcripts per million values for immunoglobulin genes IGHG1-4, IGHM, IGHA1, IGHA2, IGHD, IGHE, as well as IL-21 and CXCL13.27 Total antibody abundance was defined as the sum of these nine immunoglobulin TPMs, and two proportional metrics were derived: total IgG proportion (sum of IGHG1-4 divided by total antibody+0.01) and IgG1 proportion (IGHG1 divided by total antibody+0.01). Immune cell infiltration was estimated using CIBERSORT (LM22), after converting raw counts to TPM, removing genes with mean TPM ≤1, and retaining samples with CIBERSORT deconvolution p≤0.05.28 A composite B-cell score was calculated as the sum of naïve B cells, memory B cells, and plasma cells; CD8 T-cell proportions were used for IL-21 and CXCL13 analyses.

Cancer-specific survival analyses were restricted to cancer types with sufficient sample size and events to ensure model stability: ≥40 patients and ≥10 events for continuous and median-split analyses, ≥30 patients and ≥8 events for tertile-split analyses (middle tertile excluded), and ≥50 patients and ≥15 events for IL-21/CXCL13 models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism V.9 (GraphPad Software) or R V.4.4.2. All numerical data are presented as mean±SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney test. One-way or two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to assess differences among multiple groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p<0.05.

Results

Prognostic impact of antibody composition is heterogeneous across cancer types

A pan-cancer analysis of B-cell infiltration in TCGA revealed significant heterogeneity in its association with survival outcomes (online supplemental figure 1A). To better define the mechanisms linking B-cell infiltration to survival, we evaluated whether intratumoral IGHG abundance, measured relative to total antibody reads, carries prognostic value, given the role of IgG antibodies as the primary mediators of antibody-dependent effector function.29 We also examined the prognostic role of IGHG1, as IgG1 was the predominant IgG present in tumors (online supplemental figure 1B) and is well characterized as the principal effector class of IgG. This pan-cancer analysis of antibody composition revealed substantial heterogeneity in how these IgG signatures relate to survival (figure 1A,B) and was robust across thresholds (online supplemental figure 1C,D).

Across cancer types, the association between IGHG1 and survival varied widely, and three distinct phenotypes emerged. As examples, melanoma showed a significant survival benefit with high IGHG1/total antibody ratios (figure 1C), breast cancer showed no detectable prognostic effect (figure 1D), and colorectal adenocarcinoma showed significantly worse outcomes with higher IGHG1/total antibody ratios (figure 1E). These phenotypes likely reflect tumor-specific differences rather than isotype-specific effects, given that the relative expression ratios of IgG subclasses were broadly similar across cancer types (online supplemental figure 1B).

Taken together, these pan-cancer analyses indicate that neither B-cell infiltration nor intratumoral IgG production alone is sufficient to explain the heterogeneous associations between humoral immunity and survival across tumor types.30 31 Instead, the prognostic meaning of local IgG appears strongly context dependent, consistent with recent pan-cancer studies suggesting that B-cell epitope features may influence immunotherapy outcomes.32

Although bulk intratumoral immunoglobulin transcripts do not resolve antigen specificity and therefore represent an imperfect surrogate for tumor antigen targeting, emerging clinical data support the biological relevance of IgG enrichment as a marker of tumor-reactive humoral immunity. In studies of programmed cell death protein 1 blockade, clinical responders exhibit tumor-enriched, clonally expanded IgG1+ plasma cells and circulating IgG1 antibodies reactive to intracellular, soluble, and membrane-bound tumor-associated antigens.8 Similarly, in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, somatically hypermutated intratumoral plasma cells produce tumor-reactive antibodies against the surface protein MMP14, and IgG-tumor coating was associated with overall survival in patients.33 Moreover, in patients treated with trastuzumab, augmentation of endogenous HER2-specific IgG responses has been associated with clinical benefit.34

These observations raise a central question: What tumor-intrinsic features determine whether productive antibody responses translate into effective tumor control? Guided by our prior findings with anti-HER2 antibody therapies, we initially decided to investigate an overlooked yet fundamental necessity for robust antibody function across cancers: antigen availability on the cell surface.

Membrane-bound model neoantigens enhance tumor control independent of CD8 T cells

We hypothesized that the prognostic heterogeneity of antibodies across cancer types (figure 1) may reflect differences in the availability of cell surface neoantigens or tumor-associated antigens. This interpretation is consistent with the well-documented variation in mutational burden and resulting neoantigen load across cancers,35 as well as the corresponding differences in membrane-bound neoantigens observed in melanoma, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer.36 Melanoma generally exhibits a high neoantigen load and high levels of membrane-bound neoantigens,36 37 whereas breast cancers show more moderate and heterogeneous levels of both total and membrane-associated neoantigens.36 37 TCGA colorectal cancer cohorts are dominated by microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, a subtype characterized by limited mutational burden, leading to comparatively lower neoantigen levels.37 38 To test whether overexpression of tumor antigens at the cell surface could shift tumors toward an antibody-responsive state, we engineered membrane-bound OVA (mOVA) and cytoplasmic OVA (cOVA) constructs and introduced them into several tumor models.

We first examined this question in the MSS colorectal carcinoma line CT26, a tumor type in which antibody signatures showed an adverse association with survival in our pan-cancer analysis (figure 1). Consistent with our hypothesis that surface neoantigen availability may enhance antibody-mediated control, CT26-mOVA tumors grew significantly more slowly than CT26-cOVA or empty-vector controls in wild-type (WT) mice (figure 2A, online supplemental figure 2E).

To assess whether that effect depended on CD8 T cells, we then depleted CD8 cells before implantation. As expected, CD8 depletion accelerated tumor growth across all groups (online supplemental figure 2A–D). However, the increase was noticeably smaller for mOVA tumors than for cOVA or empty-vector controls, suggesting that membrane-bound antigens benefit from additional CD8-independent mechanisms of control. Consistent with this interpretation, CT26-mOVA tumors remained significantly smaller than CT26-cOVA tumors even after CD8 depletion (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 2F). Notably, these phenotypes were not unique to OVA, as we observed a similar pattern with membrane versus cytoplasmic eGFP in both WT and CD8-depleted mice (figure 2C,D, online supplemental figure 2G,H).

We next asked whether the antitumor advantage conferred by membrane-localized model neoantigens extended to tumor types that either show a positive prognostic association or lack any association with antibody signatures (figure 1). We used B16F10 melanoma and E0771, a murine breast cancer line, to model prognostic and non-prognostic tumor types, respectively. In both models, mOVA tumors exhibited superior control: they were rejected more frequently than cOVA tumors in E0771 (figure 2E) and grew significantly more slowly than cOVA tumors in B16F10 (figure 2G, online supplemental figure 2I). Notably, we found that this difference remained after CD8 depletion (figure 2F,H, online supplemental figure 2J,K), supported by equivalent systemic T-cell priming in cOVA and mOVA mice (online supplemental figure 2L).39

To rule out the contribution of differing antigen levels, we performed western blot analysis on OVA-expressing cells and flow cytometry on eGFP-expressing cells, which confirmed that cytoplasmic constructs expressed antigen at levels comparable to or greater than their membrane-bound counterparts (online supplemental figure 3A–I). The consistently elevated antitumor responses of membrane-localized antigens across CT26, B16F10, and E0771 led us to hypothesize that enforcing membrane expression of an immunologically recognized neoantigen may be sufficient to shift even antibody-insensitive or agnostic tumors toward an antibody-responsive state.

Membrane-bound antigens drive robust antibody responses and downstream myeloid activation

We reasoned that the improved tumor control seen with membrane-localized antigens (figure 2) may reflect more effective B-cell engagement, as both B-cell receptors (BCRs) and effector IgG antibodies can typically access only surface-exposed antigens and not intracellular proteins. To test this hypothesis, we measured antigen-specific serum antibody titers. OVA-specific ELISA assays revealed CT26-mOVA tumors elicited markedly higher levels of OVA-specific antibodies compared with cOVA or empty-vector controls, with significantly elevated production of total IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, and IgG2b antibodies (figure 3A–D, online supplemental figure 4A). A similar membrane-skewed antibody response was also observed in B16F10 melanoma, where mOVA tumors generated higher titers of IgG than cOVA tumors (online supplemental figure 4B–E). Comparable antibody levels were seen in GFP-expressing tumors, despite higher antigen expression in the cytoplasmic GFP line (online supplemental figure 4F). Because only membrane-localized GFP can bind circulating IgG on intact cells, antibody-mediated effector functions are restricted to mGFP tumors, consistent with CD8-independent control of these tumors (figure 2C,D). By the same logic, growth differences between parental and cytoplasmic antigen-expressing tumors likely reflect T cell and innate immune, rather than antibody, mediated effects.

A critical mechanism whereby elevated IgG titers may influence antitumor immunity is by modulating the activity of antigen-presenting cells through Fc receptor engagement. To evaluate this possibility, we assessed myeloid activation in mOVA tumors compared with cOVA or empty-vector tumors by flow cytometry. CD11c+ dendritic cells in mOVA tumors expressed higher levels of CD86 (figure 3E–G), and tumor-associated macrophages showed both increased CD86 expression (figure 3H) and an elevated frequency within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (figure 3I). In contrast, within the lymphoid compartment, CD8 T-cell frequency increased in both cOVA and mOVA tumors relative to controls (figure 3J,K) but was not different between these OVA-expressing groups. CD4 T-cell frequencies were similar across all conditions as well (figure 3L). These data suggest that the superior control of mOVA tumors compared with cOVA tumors is not mediated by shifts in T-cell infiltration. Likewise, NK and NKT cell frequencies did not vary across groups (online supplemental figure 4G,H).

Membrane-dependent tumor control requires CD4 T cells, antibodies, and Fc receptor signaling

Given that the effect of membrane-bound antigens on tumor growth persisted after CD8 T-cell depletion, we examined the role of CD4 T cells in our models, as they are known to be important for adaptive B-cell function. Indeed, depleting both CD4 and CD8 T cells prior to implantation eliminated the growth difference between mOVA and cOVA tumors (figure 4A). Furthermore, CD4 depletion alone rescued the growth penalty of membrane-bound antigen (figure 4B, online supplemental figure 5A,B). Notably, loss of the mOVA-cOVA differential growth coincided with a complete loss of OVA-specific IgG after CD4 depletion (figure 4C), whereas CD8 depletion had no effect on antibody levels (online supplemental figure 5C). Moreover, to verify that the mOVA-cOVA growth disparity was not tumor-intrinsic, we implanted the tumors into SCID mice and observed no growth differences (online supplemental figure 5D).

To directly confirm that antigen-specific antibodies were necessary for the differential growth observed between mOVA and cOVA tumors, we evaluated growth in antibody-skewed F1 IgHEL+mice, generated by crossing IgHEL transgenic mice onto the BALB/c background. These mice lack OVA-specific antibodies due to expression of HEL-specific transgenic BCRs. As expected, cOVA and mOVA tumors grew equivalently in these animals (figure 4D, online supplemental figure 5E). Splenic immune composition was comparable to WT controls (online supplemental figure 5F), demonstrating that the lack of phenotype reflected loss of OVA-specific antibody (online supplemental figure 5G,H) rather than global immune defects. Additional experiments using BALB/c JH⁻/⁻ mice, which lack mature IgG-expressing B cells, also demonstrated no difference between cOVA and mOVA growth, consistent with the effect observed from IgHEL mice (figure 4E, online supplemental figure 5I). Together, these data suggest that membrane-bound tumor antigens actively drive specific B-cell responses, and that disrupting this process—either upstream via CD4 T-cell depletion or directly by blocking antibody production—abrogates tumor control.

We next asked whether downstream Fc receptor signaling was required for effective antibody-mediated antitumor immunity against membrane-localized antigens. In Fcεr1γ-KO mice, which lack the common γ-chain necessary for activating Fc receptors, cOVA and mOVA tumors grew similarly (figure 4F, online supplemental figure 5J), indicating that Fc-dependent effector pathways are essential for the selective control of membrane-bound antigen. Finally, we examined the contribution of innate effector populations downstream of Fc engagement. Depleting either NK cells or macrophages, principal mediators of Fc-mediated ADCC and ADCP, prior to implantation increased the growth of CT26-mOVA tumors (online supplemental figure 5K). Flow cytometry confirmed effective depletion (online supplemental figure 5L,M), supporting the conclusion that both populations function as antitumor effectors against neoantigens in this model.

IL-21 and CXCL13 enhance antibody-dependent control of membrane-localized antigens

IL-21, which is often produced by CD4 cells,40 is required for class-switched antibody production and affinity maturation.41 Given the importance of upstream CD4 T-cell help to target membrane-bound antigens, we surmised that supplemental IL-21 could directly enhance antibody-mediated control of susceptible mOVA tumors. As expected, stable expression of IL-21 in CT26 cells significantly improved control of CT26-mOVA tumors, whereas CT26-cOVA and empty-vector tumors were not significantly affected by IL-21 expression (figure 5B–D). This phenotype was also reflected by the IL-21-mediated induction of higher systemic OVA-specific total IgG and class-switched isotypes in mOVA tumor-bearing mice (figure 5E; online supplemental figure 6A–D). Although IL-21 increased antibody titers in cOVA tumor-bearing mice (online supplemental figure 6E), this did not translate into improved tumor control.

We next asked whether increasing B-cell recruitment could similarly enhance antibody-mediated control. Because closer proximity between B cells, CD4 T cells, and antigen would be expected to increase the likelihood of productive BCR engagement and downstream effector activity, we sought to improve the recruitment of B cells through the expression of CXCL13, a well-characterized B-cell chemoattractant.42 As before, we observed that expression of CXCL13 in CT26 tumors selectively improved control of mOVA tumors, but not of cOVA and empty-vector tumors (figure 5G–I). Additionally, we found that serum from mOVA-CXCL13 mice contained significantly higher OVA-specific IgG levels (figure 5J; online supplemental figure 6F–I), consistent with increased B-cell availability enhancing the humoral response.

Given that CXCL13 and IL-21 support different aspects of the B-cell response, we next assessed if their combined expression could further enhance control of membrane antigen-expressing tumors. In mOVA cancers, we found that CXCL13 and IL-21 co-expression produced greater control of mOVA tumors than either cytokine alone (figure 5K, online supplemental figure 6J). Flow cytometry showed that only CXCL13-expressing tumors, with or without IL-21, contained elevated frequencies of CD19+ B cells and activated B cells (CD19+CD80+MHC-IIhigh) (figure 5L,M).43 We observed that these B cells localized to the tumor interior in CXCL13-expressing tumors (online supplemental figure 7A), where increased antigen contact likely drives their activated phenotypes and enhances antibody efficacy; no differences in infiltration were detected at the tumor border (online supplemental figure 7B). In contrast, T-cell and NK cell frequencies remained similar across groups (online supplemental figure 7C–E), indicating that the improved control likely reflected increased B-cell presence and activation rather than changes in other immune populations. To determine if these alterations extended beyond the TME, we quantified B-cell populations in the TDLN. We observed a trend toward fewer CD19+ B cells in CXCL13-expressing groups (online supplemental figure 7F), suggesting that CXCL13 functions primarily to recruit B cells into the tumor. However, the remaining nodal B cells exhibited elevated MHC-II and CD80 expression (online supplemental figure 7G,H), consistent with a systemic role for CXCL13 that potentially enhances the likelihood of antigen encounter. Finally, we examined whether Fc receptor signaling was required for the enhanced control of mOVA tumors driven by IL-21 or CXCL13. In Fcεr1γ-deficient mice, the advantage seen with either cytokine was lost, and all groups showed comparable tumor growth (online supplemental figure 7II). Collectively, these results demonstrate that strengthening B-cell help or increasing B-cell recruitment selectively improves tumor control only when membrane neoantigens are present.

To assess the clinical relevance of these genes, we examined the prognostic value of IL-21 and CXCL13 as a combined signature across cancers. Across the TCGA, tumors with high expression of both cytokines showed significantly improved pan-cancer outcomes compared with co-low tumors (online supplemental figure 8A). Moreover, combined IL-21/CXCL13 effects correlated with IgG-associated effects (online supplemental figure 8B,C). To better assess this pattern, we examined bladder cancer, where IgG predicts a favorable prognosis. In bladder cancer, IL-21/CXCL13 associated with improved survival; co-high tumors had the best outcomes without significant differences in clinical stage (online supplemental figure 8D,E). Based on these findings, we speculate that IL-21 and CXCL13 co-expression may help identify patients more likely to benefit from B cell-directed immunotherapies.

Antigen localization and antigen-antibody binding affinity determine vaccine response efficacy

Having established that membrane-bound antigens are important for generating effective endogenous antibody responses (figures25), we next investigated whether this principle extends to cancer vaccines, which often focus on encoding short neoepitopes to target cancer. Because vaccine efficacy depends on appropriate neoantigen selection and builds on the body’s natural humoral response, we reasoned that targeting membrane-associated neoantigens would be particularly important. To test this idea, we focused on the Trop2 T256R mutation, a cancer-associated point mutation recently described to reduce surface expression of Trop2 and contribute to clinical resistance to the antibody-drug conjugate sacituzumab govitecan.44 Using lentiviral vectors encoding either WT Trop2 or Trop2-T256R, we generated CT26 clones with equivalent total protein levels, confirmed by western blot analysis using an antibody that recognizes the intracellular portion of Trop2 (figure 6A; online supplemental figure 9A,B). However, despite comparable overall expression, T256R cells showed markedly reduced binding by different Trop2-specific monoclonal antibodies (figure 6B; online supplemental figure 9C), which recognize the Trop2 extracellular domain (ECD)—where the T256R mutation resides. Our result was consistent with prior reports that this point mutation disrupts efficient trafficking to the cell surface.44 More unexpectedly, we also observed diminished intracellular binding of these antibodies to Trop2 T256R (figure 6C,D; online supplemental figure 9D), indicating that the mutation likely affects antibody recognition independent of localization. In addition, the T256R variant displayed a shifted molecular weight by western blot analysis, suggesting altered post-translational processing that could further impact antibody binding. These findings hint that the T256R mutation introduces structural or biochemical changes within the ECD that influence antibody interactions beyond its known effects on surface localization, although further structural studies will be needed to define these changes.

To better evaluate how these mutations affect antigen-specific antitumor responses, we implanted mice with WT Trop2 and T256R tumors. Because human Trop2 is xenogeneic in this murine system, both WT Trop2 and T256R constructs function as neoantigens in the host. However, our intent was not to model de novo neoepitope generation or recapitulate self-antigen tolerance, but rather to isolate how alterations in antigen trafficking shape humoral immune engagement. With this framework in mind, the tumors exhibited no significant differences in growth, consistent with partial retention of Trop2 surface expression in T256R tumors and the high conservation of Trop2 across species (online supplemental figure 9E).45 However, implantation alone produced a trend toward differential antibody responses (online supplemental figure 9F).

To further investigate these differences, we vaccinated mice with adenoviral vectors encoding either Trop2 WT or Trop2 T256R (figure 6E). Serum from WT-vaccinated animals bound strongly to cells expressing Trop2 WT, whereas antibodies from T256R-vaccinated mice showed only weak binding to either Trop2 WT or T256R cells (figure 6F). We attribute this differential antibody response to two factors: first, the reduced surface expression of T256R limits BCR engagement during vaccine priming, resulting in weaker antibody responses; second, antibodies generated against T256R likely recognize different epitopes than those that bind the WT variant, as demonstrated by the antibody binding assays (figure 6B–D). In tumor challenge experiments, WT Trop2 tumors were most effectively controlled in mice vaccinated with WT Trop2, whereas the T256R vaccine provided only partial protection (figure 6G, online supplemental figure 9G). This pattern is consistent with the T256R vaccine generating an equivalent T-cell response but a poor antibody response, in comparison to the WT vaccine. When tumors expressed the T256R variant, both vaccines produced similarly modest control (figure 6H, online supplemental figure 9H), likely due to poor antibody recognition resulting from reduced surface expression of the mutant protein. Critically, Enzyme-Linked Immunospot (ELISpot) assays using peptides spanning the T256R region showed no clonal T-cell responses specific to the mutation, indicating that the T256R substitution does not generate preferential T-cell recognition (online supplemental figure 9I), but instead alters Trop2-specific antibody responses.

Together, these findings suggest that mutations which elicit changes in antibody cellular recognition may be a critical element of cancer immunotherapy approaches and that optimal vaccine efficacy likely depends on coordinated cellular and humoral immunity. These results indicate the importance of selecting neoantigens in the design of cancer vaccines with appropriate structure, trafficking, and immunogenicity to support robust activation of both arms of the adaptive immune response.

Discussion

Although T cell-mediated immunity has long been a central focus of cancer immunology, growing evidence shows that antibodies influence tumor progression and therapeutic response.9,13 Yet the conditions under which humoral immunity exerts protective, ineffective, or even detrimental effects remain poorly defined.46 In our models, antibodies served as CD8 T cell-independent effectors of tumor control. However, their effectiveness depended on whether target antigens were accessible to antibody binding in the TME. Furthermore, overexpression of membrane-bound antigens was sufficient to convert tumors such as colorectal cancer, where antibody responses typically predict poor survival, into targets of protective humoral immunity. We found that antibody-driven tumor control was significantly enhanced by the expression of IL-21 in the TME, which promotes B-cell activation and class switching, as well as CXCL13, which functions to recruit B cells. Finally, we establish that humoral immunity is a critical determinant of vaccine efficacy, as demonstrated by the markedly reduced antitumor effect of a vaccine targeting Trop2 T256R. This single-amino acid substitution impairs membrane localization and alters antibody binding relative to wild-type Trop2, thereby diminishing vaccine-induced antigen-specific tumor control and highlighting an underappreciated dimension of cancer immunoediting.

Our data address a long-standing paradox in cancer immunology: across studies, B-cell infiltration has been associated with both improved and worsened outcomes, with no consistent pattern across tumor types. In melanoma, breast, and sarcoma, B cells and tertiary lymphoid structures correlate with enhanced survival and responsiveness to immunotherapy,47,51 whereas in subsets of renal, ovarian, and gastric cancers, they have been linked to resistance or poor prognosis.52,54 However, no study has systematically examined intratumoral IgG expression across cancers to define how humoral immunity relates to patient outcomes; prior analyses rely on surrogate B-cell markers (eg, CD20, CD138). By quantifying IgG expression across tumor types, our study reveals striking variability in the relationship between antibody responses and survival, underscoring tumor-intrinsic factors as likely drivers of these context-specific effects.

Accordingly, recent efforts have focused on defining the antigens recognized by tumor-infiltrating B cells. To date, approximately 70 distinct antigen specificities have been described across breast, lung, and ovarian cancers, encompassing nuclear, cytoplasmic, transmembrane, and secreted proteins.55 Many identified targets correspond to non-mutated self-antigens, including p53 and NY-ESO-1, whereas others include tumor-associated surface proteins such as MMP14.33 In virally driven malignancies such as human papillomavirus-positive head and neck cancer and Merkel cell carcinoma, humoral responses develop against intracellular viral antigens expressed by tumor cells.56 We believe that our study may help guide the selection and prioritization of antibody targets as sequencing and proteomic efforts continue to define the antigenic landscape of human cancers. That said, it will be important to consider how B-cell tolerance influences antigen selection, as some neoantigens will share greater similarity with endogenous proteins than others.55 In our study, the model antigens used were either fully foreign (OVA, GFP)23 24 or shared substantial homology with endogenous murine proteins (Trop2),45 leaving open how these principles operate under physiological tolerance constraints. As neoantigens with defined surface localization are increasingly identified, they will provide opportunities to address this question.

Our findings also help reconcile prior work examining B-cell–T-cell collaboration in tumor immunity. In engineered tumor models expressing defined B-cell antigens, coordinated interactions between B cells and T follicular helper cells enhanced antitumor responses, often through augmentation of CD8 T-cell effector function.9 In other systems, differentiation of antigen-specific B cells into plasma cells yielded tumor control, but this required substantial enhancement of B-cell activation and the antitumor effects were not clearly independent of T cell-mediated killing.57 Yet, many of these studies relied on soluble antigens, which differ fundamentally in accessibility and immune engagement compared with membrane-bound targets. A prior study employing a tumor-line expressing a membrane form of hen egg lysozyme reported reduced lung metastases; however, these experiments were conducted in immunodeficient mice, limiting evaluation of antibody function within a fully intact inflammatory microenvironment.58 Together, our findings indicate that antigen localization may explain prior inconsistencies in the literature on B cell-mediated tumor immunity, by delineating the contexts in which humoral responses meaningfully contribute to tumor control.

Indeed, our determination that the therapeutic role of endogenous antibodies relies heavily on Fc-mediated mechanisms, including ADCC and ADCP, supports efforts to engineer antibodies with enhanced Fc effector functions.59 60 However, antibody function extends beyond canonical Fc pathways. Endogenous anti-HER2 antibodies can block receptor activity,61 while certain IgA antibodies can antagonize RAS signaling.62 Thus, understanding how endogenous antibody responses modulate signaling, and how these effects contrast by antibody subclass, represents an area for future investigation.

While antibody effector mechanisms vary, it is clear that increasing both the potency and abundance of IgG antibodies specific for membrane-bound antigens can enhance their therapeutic impact. IL-21 and CXCL13 represent two promising avenues for doing so. IL-21, already in clinical trials,63 plays an increasingly appreciated role in shaping antitumor B-cell responses, including in breast cancer models where IL-21-producing T cells enhance B-cell function.43 Similarly, CXCL13 supports humoral immunity, correlating with tertiary lymphoid structures.64 Yet, the combined ability of IL-21 and CXCL13 to potentiate humoral immunity, as well as their dependence on neoantigen localization, remains unexplored clinically. Advances in sequencing could identify which tumors harbor membrane-bound neoantigens, guiding the selection of patients for such interventions.

Our results also provide a mechanistic basis for clinical observations linking antigen localization to therapeutic response. Patients with higher proportions of predicted membrane-bound neoantigens have shown improved responses to ICI, a pattern attributed to enhanced cytotoxic T-cell activity.36 Our data suggest another explanation: when neoantigens are displayed on the cell surface, antibodies can mediate tumor control. Moreover, oncogenic mutations frequently disrupt normal protein trafficking, particularly within the secretory pathway, which can redirect cytoplasmic proteins to the membrane and create new antibody-accessible targets.18 These findings suggest that membrane-bound neoantigens may be far more common than previously appreciated, expanding the potential role of humoral immunity in cancer immunotherapy. Our studies examining the impact of the Trop2 T256R variant44 illustrate how cancer-specific mutations can simultaneously alter protein localization, structure, and targetability by IgG antibodies, affecting immunotherapy efficacy.

In contrast to T-cell epitopes, antibody recognition depends on protein conformation and surface accessibility.65 Consequently, even subtle amino acid substitutions may create large alterations in antibody epitope recognition without generating alterations against T-cell antigens,65 as in Trop2 T256R. This distinction expands the repertoire of targetable tumor antigens beyond those accessible to T cells and underscores the value of incorporating structural modeling and localization prediction into antigen selection, especially in the context of vaccination. Notably, analyses of human neoantigen vaccine trials show that enrichment for predicted B-cell epitopes is associated with increased IFN-γ-positive responses.32 Moreover, recent advances in prediction of linear B-cell neoepitopes offer a starting point for antigen nomination, but their utility will hinge on whether the corresponding proteins are properly folded and displayed on the membrane.32

In conclusion, our findings reveal that the effectiveness of humoral immunity in cancer is fundamentally dictated by the accessibility and structure of neoantigens, establishing membrane localization as the central factor governing antibody-mediated tumor control. By combining pan-cancer analyses with mechanistic studies across tumor models, we show that membrane-bound neoantigens uniquely elicit potent, CD4-dependent, Fc receptor-driven IgG responses, which are influenced by the cytokine environment of the tumor and function independently of CD8 T cells. These observations suggest that directing immune responses toward membrane-bound targets may be essential for effective antitumor immunity, as some neoepitopes are inherently more amenable to B-cell recognition than to T-cell recognition. As such, our study establishes a conceptual framework for integrating humoral immunity into the rational design of immunotherapies and cancer vaccines.
