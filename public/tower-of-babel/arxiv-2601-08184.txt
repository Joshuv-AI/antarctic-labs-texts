Wasserstein - p Central Limit Theorem Rates: From Local Dependence to Markov Chains
Yixuan Zhang, Qiaomin Xie

Abstract
. Finite-time central limit theorem (CLT) rates play a central role in modern machine learning. In this paper, we study CLT rates for multivariate dependent data in Wasserstein- p p ( 𝒲 p \mathcal{W}_{p} ) distance, for general p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 . We focus on two fundamental dependence structures that commonly arise in machine learning: locally dependent sequences and geometrically ergodic Markov chains. In both settings, we establish the first optimal 𝒪 ( n − 1 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}) rate in 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} , as well as the first 𝒲 p \mathcal{W}_{p} ( p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 ) CLT rates under mild moment assumptions, substantially improving the best previously known bounds in these dependent-data regimes. As an application of our optimal 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} rate for locally dependent sequences, we further obtain the first optimal 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} -CLT rate for multivariate U U -statistics. On the technical side, we derive a tractable auxiliary bound for 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} Gaussian approximation errors that is well suited for studying dependent data. For Markov chains, we further prove that the regeneration time of the split chain associated with a geometrically ergodic chain has a geometric tail without assuming strong aperiodicity or other restrictive conditions. These tools may be of independent interests and enable our optimal 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} rates and underpin our 𝒲 p \mathcal{W}_{p} ( p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 ) results.



1.
Introduction

Finite-time Central Limit Theorem (CLT) rates are important in modern machine learning, as learning and decision-making are often based on limited data and therefore require quantitative uncertainty guarantees. In particular, CLT rates are essential for obtaining finite-time
error bounds and for enabling statistically valid inference on parameters of
interest in stochastic algorithms and dynamical systems

(
hastings1970monte
;
chen2018gaussian
;
srikant2024rates
;
samsonov2024gaussian
;
wu2025uncertainty
;
chernozhukov2013gaussian
)
.

Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in quantifying the CLT rates through the use of transportation metrics
(
rio2009upper
;
gallouet2018regularity
;
raivc2018multivariate
;
bonis2020stein
;
srikant2024rates
)
. In particular, the Wasserstein-
p
(
\mathcal{W}_{p}
) distance
(
villani2008optimal
)
between two probability measures
\nu,\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

(1)

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\nu,\mu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu,\mu)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\gamma}\bigl[\|X-Y\|^{p}\bigr]\right)^{1/p},\quad\forall p\geq 1,

where
\Gamma(\nu,\mu)
denotes the set of couplings of
(\nu,\mu)
. Given a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued observations
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
, define the partial sum

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}X_{i}
and the normalized covariance
\Sigma_{n}:=n^{-1}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n})
. Throughout this section, we assume for convenience that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
for all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
and that
\Sigma_{n}=I_{d}
.
CLT rates bound the Gaussian approximation error in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
:

(2)

\mathcal{W}_{p}\big(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\big),\quad\text{where }\mathcal{L}(\cdot)\text{ denotes the law of a random variable.}

When
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
are independent, a large literature establishes the
CLT rates (
2
). In the univariate case (
d=1
), the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 1
)
under the moment condition
\sup_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{2+p}<\infty
has been established
(
petrov2012sums
;
rio2009upper
;
bobkov2013entropic
)
.
In the multivariate case, obtaining the optimal rate is more delicate. In particular,
the regularity-based approach
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
typically leads to an
additional logarithmic factor, yielding suboptimal rates of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
.
Moreover, for
p>1
, the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
generally lacks a tractable
dual representation in dimensions
d\geq 2

(
bobkov2024fourier
)
, which limits the direct use of
Lipschitz-test-function arguments that are available in the univariate setting.

Recently, for
\mathcal{W}_{1}
, a rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\delta/2})
for
\delta\in(0,1)
was established under the moment condition

\sup_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{2+\delta}<\infty

(
gallouet2018regularity
)
, while the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate for
\delta=1

was obtained via a new Gaussian approximation framework
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
.
For
\mathcal{W}_{p}
with
p\geq 2
, the rate

\mathcal{O}\!\left(n^{-(p+q-2)/(2p)}\right)
under

\sup_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{p+q}<\infty
for some
q\in(0,2]
was proved
(
bonis2020stein
)
.
In particular, taking
q=2

yields the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate. These results for independent data are summarized in the blue-shaded region of Table
1
.

However, in many modern machine learning applications, the data is not necessarily independent.
Estimators commonly take the form of partial sums along algorithmic trajectories
or correlated data streams, as in stochastic gradient methods

(
dieuleveut2020bridging
;
yu2021analysis
;
zhang2024prelimit
;
zhang2025piecewise
)

and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) inference
(
hastings1970monte
)
. While the theory for independent data has been well-established, quantitative CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
under dependence remain significantly less developed. In this paper, we focus on two fundamental dependence structures that commonly arise in machine learning: (1) locally dependent data and (2) Markov chains. We review the related literature for these two settings separately below.

Locally dependent data

Locally dependent data form an important subclass of dependent sequences, in which each
observation depends only on a bounded neighborhood in an underlying dependency graph

(
rinott1994normal
)
; see Section
2
for a precise definition.
Such dependence arises naturally in graph-structured learning through network interactions,
for example in node-level prediction with graph neural networks

(
kipf2016semi
;
hamilton2017inductive
)
. For locally dependent data in the univariate setting, the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

rate in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
for all
p\geq 1
was established
(
liu2023wasserstein
)
.
In the multivariate setting, under an almost-sure boundedness assumption, a rate of

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
for
p\geq 1
was obtained
(
fang2023p
)
.

To the best of our knowledge, however, CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
for multivariate locally dependent data under mild moment assumptions remain largely unavailable for
p\geq 1
.

Markov chains

Markov chains provide a unifying framework for modeling and analyzing stochastic
dynamics in modern machine learning. They underpin sequential decision-making models such as
Markov decision processes and reinforcement learning

(
puterman2014markov
;
sutton1998reinforcement
;
zhang2024constant
)
, and they
also drive generative modeling pipelines through Markovian diffusion dynamics

(
ho2020denoising
)
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
be a Markov chain on a general state space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})

with unique stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
\{h_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
be measurable functions

h_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h_{i}]=0
for all
i\geq 0
.
Then the sequence
\{h_{i}(x_{i})\}_{i\geq 0}
serves as the dependent analogue of the observations

\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
considered above.
Recent work studied geometrically ergodic Markov chains satisfying a drift condition with Lyapunov function
V

(
srikant2024rates
)
.
In the homogeneous case (
h_{i}\equiv h
), they obtained
a rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\delta/2})
under the domination condition
\|h\|^{2+\delta}\leq V
for
\delta\in(0,1)
,
and a rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
under
\|h\|^{3}\leq V
. Time-inhomogeneous
\{h_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
are considered in follow up work
(
wu2025uncertainty
)
, but only a
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
rate is obtained under
stronger assumptions: the transition kernel has a positive spectral gap, and the functions

h_{i}
are uniformly bounded.
Both results are restricted to
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and are suboptimal due to the additional
logarithmic factor; they are summarized in the yellow-shaded region of
Table
1
.

In
(
srikant2024rates
)
, the authors highlight several open problems for geometric ergodic Markov chain CLT rates,
including: (i) whether the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

\mathcal{W}_{1}
rate can
be achieved (under mild conditions), and (ii) what
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates are attainable when
p>1
.

In this work, we establish CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 1
)—optimal in some regimes—for
multivariate locally dependent sequences and geometric ergodic Markov chains under mild moment
conditions. Before stating our contributions, we highlight the main challenges in obtaining
such results.

1.1.
Challenges for
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT Rates with Multivariate Dependent Data

We emphasize that extending
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates (
p\geq 1
) from the univariate setting (
d=1
)
to the multivariate setting (
d\geq 2
) is highly nontrivial, even for independent data.
Two main challenges arise in the multivariate case.

The first challenge arises when one attempts to leverage regularity bounds for
solutions to Stein’s equation in order to obtain the
optimal

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
multivariate CLT rate in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. Regularity estimates for
Stein solutions are a standard ingredient in Stein’s method and have long been
used to derive classical
univariate
CLT rates, for instance in the

\mathcal{W}_{1}
and Kolmogorov distances
(
chen2010normal
;
nourdin2012normal
)
.
However, a key obstacle emerges when
d\geq 2
. To illustrate this issue, we
recall a regularity lemma from
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
; the
notation used below is defined in Section
1.4
.

Lemma 0 (Proposition 2.2 in
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
).

When
d\geq 2
, for any
h\in C^{0,1}
let
f_{h}
denote the solution to the multivariate Stein equation
\Delta f_{h}(x)-x^{\top}\nabla f_{h}(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. Then
f_{h}\in C^{2,\delta}
and
[\nabla^{2}f_{h}]_{\operatorname{Lip},\delta}\lesssim\frac{1}{1-\delta}
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Unlike the univariate case, where an analogous regularity result holds even for

\delta=1
(see, e.g.,
(
barbour1986asymptotic
, Lemma 6)
), in the multivariate setting
the exponent
\delta
must be taken strictly less than one; moreover, it has been shown that

f_{h}
need not belong to
C^{2,1}

(
raivc2004multivariate
)
.
For illustration, suppose the data are i.i.d. and satisfy a uniform

(2+\alpha)
-moment condition for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
As shown in the proof of

(
gallouet2018regularity
, Theorem 1.1)
, choosing any
\delta\in(0,1)
with

\delta\leq\alpha
leads to an error term of the form
n\cdot(n^{-1/2})^{2+\delta}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\delta}\in\mathcal{O}(\frac{n^{-\delta/2}}{1-\delta})
. If
\alpha<1
, choosing
\delta=\alpha
yields the rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha/2})
.
This matches the standard and optimal dependence on
n
under a finite

(2+\alpha)
-moment in the univariate case
(
ibragimov1966accuracy
)
, demonstrating that Lemma
1
is sufficient in this regime.
However, when
\alpha=1
(finite third moment),
Lemma
1
forces
\delta<1
, and the optimal choice is

\delta=1-2/\log n
, which yields the suboptimal

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
rate rather than
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. In summary, the first challenge can be stated as follows.

Challenge 1.

Relying on Lemma
1
alone precludes achieving the optimal

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate.

The second challenge concerns multivariate CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
for
p>1
.
When
p=1
, the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\zeta_{1}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{f\in C^{0,1}}|\int f\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\mathrm{d}\nu|
provides a powerful route to bounding
\mathcal{W}_{1}
rates via test functions.
In the univariate setting, an analogous comparison is available for
p>1
, namely
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\lesssim\zeta_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{f\in C^{p-1,1}}|\int f\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\mathrm{d}\nu|
, see
(
liu2023wasserstein
)
. In contrast, when
d\geq 2
the situation is more subtle.

Challenge 2.

Unlike the univariate setting, in high dimensions the Zolotarev distance
\zeta_{p}(\mu,\nu)

might not be well defined for
p>1
, and the precise relationship between
\mathcal{W}_{p}
and
\zeta_{p}

is not fully understood; see
(
bobkov2024fourier
)
. Consequently, techniques that control

\zeta_{1}
do not directly yield upper bounds on
\mathcal{W}_{p}
when
p>1
and
d\geq 2
.

A natural workaround is to consider the

sliced
or
max–sliced
Wasserstein distances
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{p}
built from one–dimensional
projections, and then invoke univariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
–CLTs (e.g.
(
liu2023wasserstein
)
). However, for
p\geq 2

there is no general reverse inequality that controls
\mathcal{W}_{p}
by a function of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{p}
.
Even for
p=1
, the recent quantified Cramér–Wold inequality
(
bobkov2024quantified
)
controls
\mathcal{W}_{1}
by a
power
of the max-sliced
\mathcal{W}_{1}
with an exponent degrading as
1/d
, leading at best to rates of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/(2d)})
. Such bounds are
too weak in high dimensions.

Resolving Challenges
1
and
2
in the Independent Case

Recent work
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
proposed a new framework for Gaussian approximation
(rigorously reviewed in Section
3.1
) and showed how, in the independent setting,
it yields the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. For
\mathcal{W}_{p}
with

(

Persistence of Challenges Under Dependence

We emphasize that Challenges
1
and
2
persist in
the dependent setting. Specifically, Challenge
1
remains a bottleneck in obtaining the optimal

\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rates for Markov chains: existing techniques
(
srikant2024rates

)

(

)
and
(

(
raivc2018multivariate
)
and the exchangeable-pair construction in
(
bonis2020stein
)
crucially rely on independence, making them difficult to apply to dependent data.

1.3.
Related Work

Central Limit Theorem Rates and Probability Metrics

A classical line of work studies Gaussian approximation in
integral probability metrics

(
muller1997integral
)
. In the univariate
case, the Kolmogorov distance admits the celebrated Berry–Esseen bound of order

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
under a finite third-moment assumption
(
petrov2012sums
)
. In higher dimensions, a common analogue is the

convex-set distance

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbb{P}(S_{n}/\sqrt{n}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(Z\in A)|
, where
\mathcal{C}

denotes the class of convex Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Sharp dimension dependence in Berry–Esseen-type bounds
for convex sets was established by Bentkus
(
bentkus2003dependence
)
. Another prominent
metric in modern high-dimensional statistics is the
hyperrectangle
distance

(
chen2018gaussian
;
chen2019randomized
)
, defined as the supremum
over axis-aligned rectangles.

Recently, transport distances have attracted significant attention in CLT theory

(
rio2009upper
;
gallouet2018regularity
;
raivc2018multivariate
;
bonis2020stein
;
srikant2024rates
)
.
These metrics endow probability laws with the geometry of the underlying space by
interpreting distributional discrepancy as the minimal cost of transporting mass

(
villani2008optimal
)
. This geometric perspective, together with stability
properties
(
kuhn2019wasserstein
)
and powerful dual
representations (e.g., against Lipschitz test functions for
\mathcal{W}_{1}
), makes
transport-based CLT rates both robust and practically meaningful, with broad
applications in modern machine learning theory
(
panaretos2019statistical
;
huo2024collusion
)
.

Nummelin’s Splitting

Nummelin’s splitting construction
(
nummelin1978splitting
)
is a foundational
tool for introducing
regeneration
into general state-space Markov chains.
Splitting and regeneration are now standard in the stability and ergodic theory
of Markov chains
(
Meyn12_book
)
, and they also underpin modern MCMC analysis
(
mykland1995regeneration
;
jones2001honest
)
.

Beyond asymptotic ergodic results, splitting-based regenerative decompositions
have been widely used to derive nonasymptotic concentration inequalities for
additive functionals of Markov chains

(
adamczak2008tail
;
adamczak2015exponential
;
bertail2018new
;
lemanczyk2021general
)
:
the works
(
adamczak2008tail
;
adamczak2015exponential
;
bertail2018new
)
focus on
the strongly aperiodic setting, while
(
lemanczyk2021general
)
treats general

m
-step minorization. Regeneration methods have also been used to obtain
Berry–Esseen-type bounds for Markov chains

(
bolthausen1980berry
;
bolthausen1982berry
;
douc2008bounds
)
; however, these
results are largely restricted to the

case, the univariate setting, and
metrics such as the Kolmogorov distance. Our work extends the use of split

1.4.
Notation

We write
\nabla
and
\Delta
for the gradient and Laplacian operators on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the Hölder

consists of functions

that are

For
L>0
, let
\operatorname{Lip}_{L}
denote the class of real-valued
L
-Lipschitz functions on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{Lip}_{L}:=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\ |h(x)-h(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|\ \ \forall x,y\}.

By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{h\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}}\Bigl|\int h\mathrm{d}\mu-\int h\mathrm{d}\nu\Bigr|.

For symmetric matrices
A,B
, we write

A\succeq B
(resp.
A\succ B
) if
A-B
is positive semidefinite (resp. positive definite), and
\lambda_{\min}(A)
for the smallest eigenvalue of
A
.

For
p\geq 1
,
\|Z\|_{L^{p}}:=(\mathbb{E}\|Z\|^{p})^{1/p}
denotes the
L^{p}
norm of a random
vector
Z
, and
L^{q}(\mathbb{P})
denotes the usual space of
q
-integrable random
variables under
\mathbb{P}
. Unless stated otherwise, we use the standard order notations

\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
and
\Omega(\cdot)
to hide absolute constants that do not
depend on the sample size
n
. We also write
a\lesssim b
(resp.
a\gtrsim b
)
to mean that
a\in\mathcal{O}(b)
(resp.
a\in\Omega(b)
).

Given a Markov kernel
P
and a measurable function
V:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

PV(x):=\int V(y)P(x,\mathrm{d}y)
. For a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, we use

\nu(V):=\int V\mathrm{d}\nu
. Given two measurable functions

, we write

to

, and

.

2.
Central Limit Theorems for Local Dependent Data

In this section, we focus on locally dependent data
(
rinott1994normal
)
. Let
I=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, and let

\Gamma=(I,E)
be a graph with vertex set
I
and edge set
E
. We say that

\Gamma
is a dependence graph for the collection
\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
if, for
any disjoint subsets
K,L\subseteq I
such that there is no edge in
\Gamma

between
K
and
L
, the subcollections
\{X_{k}:k\in K\}
and
\{X_{\ell}:\ell\in L\}

are independent. Write
\deg(i;\Gamma)
for the degree of vertex
i
in
\Gamma
, and define
D:=1+\max_{i\in I}\operatorname{deg}(i;\Gamma)
, noting that
D
may depend on
n
. For
i\in I
, let
\mathcal{N}[i]
denote the
closed neighborhood of
i
in
\Gamma
, namely

\mathcal{N}[i]:=\{i\}\cup\{j\in I:\ (i,j)\in E\}.

A common subclass of locally dependent data is
M
-dependence (where
M

may depend on
n
); see, e.g.,
(
diananda1955central
;
romano2000more
)
. We say
that
\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
is
M
-dependent if the subcollections

\{X_{i}:i\in K\}
and
\{X_{j}:j\in L\}
are independent whenever

\min\{|i-j|:\ i\in K,\ j\in L\}>M.

Equivalently, the dependence graph
\Gamma
has an edge between distinct
vertices
i\neq j
if and only if
0<|i-j|\leq M
. In particular, the maximum
degree satisfies
D\leq 1+2M
. The case
M=0
reduces to the independent
setting (no edges, hence
D=1
).

We consider a collection
\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vectors. The
matrix- or tensor-valued cases can be handled verbatim by vectorizing each

X_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we assume the sequence is centered:

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
for all
i\in I
. Define

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}X_{i},\qquad\Sigma_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n}).

In this section, we focus on the nondegenerate case

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})\in\Omega(1)
.

2.1.
CLT Rates in Wasserstein-1 Distance

We now state a theorem that quantifies the gap between

\mathcal{L}\big(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)
and the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})
in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

Theorem 1 (Wasserstein–1 CLT rates for local-dependent data).

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be locally dependent as defined in Section
2
. Assume that
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X_{i}\|^{2+\delta}\right]\in\mathcal{O}(1)
for some
\delta\in(0,1]
. Then

\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\big)\in\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n})^{1/2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})^{\delta/2}}\cdot D\cdot n^{-\delta/2}\Big)&\text{ if }\delta\in(0,1),\\
\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n})^{1/2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})^{3/2}}\cdot D^{2}\cdot n^{-1/2}\Big)&\text{ if }\delta=1.\end{cases}

Here,
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
hides constants that are independent of
D
and
n
.

To our knowledge, Theorem
1
establishes the first
optimal

W_{1}
CLT rates for
multivariate locally-dependent data under a finite
(2+\delta)
-th moment assumption with
\delta\in(0,1]
. The dependence on
n
matches the optimal rates known in the i.i.d. setting for
\delta\in(0,1)

(
gallouet2018regularity
)
and the independent case for
\delta=1

(
raivc2018multivariate
)
.
Futhermore, Theorem
1
extends the univariate results of

(
liu2023wasserstein
)
to the multivariate setting and provides a comprehensive treatment of the full range
\delta\in(0,1]
, whereas

(
liu2023wasserstein
)
focuses only on the case
\delta=1
.

Raič
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
developed an abstract Gaussian approximation framework that achieved the optimal multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rate for
\delta=1
in the independent
setting. However, this success relies on constructing auxiliary objects specifically tailored to independence, which does not extend directly to dependent data. In this work, we generalize the framework of
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
by introducing novel objects that capture dependent structures, resulting a tractable bound (
4
) on the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT error for general dependent data (Proposition
2
). Leveraging this bound (
4
), we
establish the optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
in Theorem
1
for

\delta=1
. For
\delta\in(0,1)
, we adapt the Stein-equation regularity theory

(
gallouet2018regularity
)
from the i.i.d. setting to locally dependent sequences.
An overview of Raič’s framework, our construction of new objects, and a proof sketch are provided in
Section
3
, with full details deferred to Appendix
A
.

2.2.
CLT Rates in Wasserstein-p (
p\geq 2
) Distance

As discussed in Section
1.1
, extending multivariate CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{p}

from the case
p=1
to
p\geq 2
is
not
a routine generalization; in particular,
techniques that control
\mathcal{W}_{1}
do not directly yield bounds for
\mathcal{W}_{p}
when
p\geq 2
.
Bonis
(
bonis2020stein
, Theorems 4 and 9)
developed an exchangeable-pairs framework for
Gaussian approximation that attains optimal multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
rates for independent
data with
p\geq 2
. Building on this approach, recent work
(
fang2023p
)
derived
\mathcal{W}_{p}
bounds of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)
for
p\geq 2
under
bounded
locally dependent data.
Nevertheless, multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) CLT rates for locally dependent sequences
under mild moment assumptions remain poorly understood.

To narrow this gap, we establish the following theorem, which provides multivariate

\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) CLT rates for
M
-dependent data under mild moment conditions.

Theorem 2 (Wasserstein–p CLT rates for
M
-dependent data).

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be
M
-dependent as in Section
2
. Assume that
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X_{i}\|^{p+q}\right]\in\mathcal{O}(1)
for some
p\geq 2
and
q\in(0,2]
. Then

\mathcal{W}_{p}\big(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\big)\in\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n})^{1/2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})^{1/2}}\cdot(2M+1)^{1+\frac{2-q}{2(2p+q-2)}}\cdot n^{-\frac{p+q-2}{2(2p+q-2)}}\Big).

Here,
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
hides constants that are independent of
M
and
n
.

We emphasize that, to our knowledge, Theorem
2
provides the
first
multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates (
p\geq 2
) for
M-
dependent data under finite-moment assumptions. We also acknowledge that the bound may not be optimal: when
q=2
our best dependence on
n
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/4})
, whereas the common conjectured optimal rate is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
; closing this gap in full generality remains open. Nevertheless, Theorem
2
significantly narrows the gap between the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
theory and the
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) regime in high dimensions. Furthermore, it enables the characterization of Markov chain CLT rates under
\mathcal{W}_{p}
for
p\geq 2
as we will discuss in Section
5.3
, where existing results for bounded random variables
(
fang2023p
)
do not apply. Obtaining optimal
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates for general locally dependent sequences under
finite-moment assumptions remains an important direction for future research. One
promising avenue is to extend the exchangeable-pair framework of

(
bonis2020stein
)
, which is currently tailored to independent data, to
settings with dependence.

We establish Theorem
2
by combining the multivariate CLT rates for
independent data
(
bonis2020stein
)
with a standard big–small block decomposition
(
Meyn12_book
, Equation 17.36)
. The argument reduces the sum of

M
-dependent vectors to a sum of independent
big
blocks, while deriving a
precise upper bound for the contribution of the
small
blocks (of length
M
),
together with an optimal choice of the big-block length. An outline of the proof is
given in Section
3.4
, and full details appear in
Appendix
B
.

2.3.
Applications to U-statistics

In this section, we apply our multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rates for locally dependent data
(Theorem
1
) to derive
W_{1}
CLT rates for
U
-statistics. Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be i.i.d. random vectors taking values in a measurable space

(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z}))
. Fix an integer
r\geq 1
, and let

h:\mathcal{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a symmetric measurable kernel. Denote the target

\theta:=\mathbb{E}[h(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{r-1})]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

A natural estimator for
\theta
is the U-statistic of order
r

(
hoeffding1992class
)
:

(3)

U_{n}:=\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n-1}h\left(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{r}}\right).

We emphasize that
U
-statistics have numerous practical applications. Notable examples
include covariance matrix estimation

(
dempster1972covariance
;
bickel2008covariance
;
bickel2008regularized
)

and subbagging/ensemble methods such as random forests

(
breiman1996bagging
;
breiman2001random
;
mentch2016quantifying
)
.
Below we present two concrete illustrations.

Example 0 (Sample covariance).

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be i.i.d. with mean
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]
and covariance

\Sigma:=\operatorname{Var}(Z_{0})
. Define
\bar{Z}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Z_{i}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(Z_{i}-\bar{Z}_{n})(Z_{i}-\bar{Z}_{n})^{\top}.
Then
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}]=\Sigma
. Moreover,
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}
is a matrix-valued (
d=p\times p
)
U
-statistic of order

2
with symmetric kernel

h(z,z^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}(z-z^{\prime})(z-z^{\prime})^{\top}
for
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, namely,

\hat{\Sigma}_{n}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n-1}h(Z_{i},Z_{j}).

Example 0 (Subbaging).

Consider supervised learning with features
X\in\mathcal{X}
and response
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Suppose we observe an independent training sample
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}=\{(X_{0},Y_{0}),\ldots,(X_{n-1},Y_{n-1})\}
.
Fix
r\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and a symmetric base learner
T
that, given a subsample

\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, produces a prediction at a target point

x^{*}
, denoted
T_{x^{*}}\big(Z_{i_{1}},\dots,Z_{i_{r}}\big).
The subbagged predictor
(
breiman2001random
;
mentch2016quantifying
)
averages these
predictions over all
\binom{n}{r}
subsamples:

b_{n}(x^{*}):=\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n-1}T_{x^{*}}\big(Z_{i_{1}},\dots,Z_{i_{r}}\big).

Averaging over subsamples typically reduces variance (and hence improves predictive accuracy)
for base learners
(
breiman1996bagging
)
.

Without loss of generality, assume
\theta=0
. We focus on the nondegenerate regime in which
\lambda_{\min}\big(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[h(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{r-1})\mid Z_{0}])\big)\in\Omega(1)
, a standard condition in the CLT literature for
U
-statistics

(
callaert1978berry
;
gotze1987approximations
;
chen2018gaussian
)
. Under this assumption,
the following result characterizes
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rates for
U
-statistics of order
r
.

Corollary 0.

Given i.i.d.
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
as in Section
2.3
such that
\mathbb{E}[\|h(Z_{0},\cdots,Z_{r-1})\|^{3}]<\infty
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\Big(\mathcal{L}\big(\tfrac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{r}\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[h(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{r-1})\mid Z_{0}]\big)\big)\Big)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}).

Corollary
5
provides the first
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rate for multivariate

U
-statistics of order
r
. In contrast, much of the existing literature
focuses on Gaussian approximation in other stronger metrics, such as the
Kolmogorov distance or the hyperrectangle distance, including the univariate
result
(
callaert1978berry
)
and high-dimensional extensions
(
gotze1987approximations
;
chen2018gaussian
)
.
Related work on locally dependent data
established univariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates and discussed applications to
U
-statistics

(
liu2023wasserstein
;
fang2019wasserstein
)
, but these approaches do not extend
to the multivariate setting, as explained in Section
1.1
. The proof of Corollary
5
follows from Theorem
1
and is
deferred to Appendix
C
.

3.
Technical Overview of CLT Rates under Local and

M
-Dependence

This section is organized as follows. Section
3.1
reviews the Gaussian
approximation framework of
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
, which yields optimal multivariate

\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rates in the independent setting. Section
3.2
extends this framework by deriving a tractable auxiliary bound on the

\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT error for general dependent data.
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
then outline the proofs of
Theorems
1
and
2
, respectively. Throughout, we
restrict to the normalization
\Sigma_{n}:=n^{-1}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n})=I_{d}
; the general nondegenerate
case
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})=\Omega(1)
follows by a standard linear-algebra reduction,
as detailed in Appendix
A
.

3.1.
Raič’s Framework for Gaussian Approximation

The work by Raič
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
presents a general multivariate Stein framework for Gaussian approximation. Specifically, let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}\|W\|^{2}<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}W=0
, and
\operatorname{Var}(W)=I_{d}
, and let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. For test functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
in the Hölder class
\mathcal{C}^{0,1}
, the framework provides explicit bounds on
|\mathbb{E}f(W)-\mathbb{E}f(Z)|
. The argument is summarized in three steps with suitable constructions of auxiliary objects:

Step 1: Generalized size-biased transformation.

Construct
a measurable index space
\Xi
, a family of random vectors

\{V_{\xi}:\xi\in\Xi\}
, and a vector measure
\mu
on
\Xi
such that

\mathbb{E}[f(W)W]=\int_{\Xi}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f(V_{\xi})]\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi).

The above identity generalizes classical size-biased representations

(
baldi1989normal
)
and their multivariate extensions

(
goldstein1996multivariate
)
, which are standard tools for obtaining
quantitative CLT rates
(
chen2010normal
)
.

Step 2: Interpolation.

For each
\xi\in\Xi
,
interpolate explicitly
along the straight line from
V_{\xi}
to
W
:

W_{s}^{(\xi)}:=(1-s)V_{\xi}+sW,\qquad s\in[0,1],\qquad Z_{\xi}:=W-V_{\xi}.

Construct
a measurable selector

\psi:\Xi\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Xi
and define a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
–valued vector measure

\nu_{\xi}(B):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}\{\psi(\xi,s,z)\in B\}z\mathcal{L}(Z_{\xi})(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\forall B\in\mathcal{B}(\Xi).

The selector
\psi
is chosen such that the conditional laws match:

\mathcal{L}\big(W_{s}^{(\xi)}\big|Z_{\xi}=z\big)=\mathcal{L}\big(V_{\psi(\xi,s,z)}\big),\quad\forall s\in[0,1]\text{ and }\mathcal{L}(Z_{\xi})\text{-a.e.\ }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Then, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}f(W)-\mathbb{E}_{\xi}f(V_{\xi})=\int_{\Xi}\big\langle\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[\nabla f(V_{\eta})],\nu_{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\big\rangle.

Consequently, the discrepancy between the laws of
W
and
V_{\xi}
is captured by the
vector measure
\nu_{\xi}
.

Step 3: Quantitative bounds.

By introducing some
\beta
-functionals,
(
raivc2018multivariate
, Theorem 2.9)

provides quantitative bounds comparing
W
with the standard Gaussian

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
:

Lemma 0 (Theorem 2.9 of
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
).

Consider the objects
\mathcal{S}=(\Xi,\{V_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi},\mu,\psi,\{\nu_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi})
constructed in
Steps 1–2.
Define the quantities

\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{(\xi)}:=|\nu_{\xi}|(\Xi),\qquad\beta_{2}:=\int_{\Xi}\beta_{1}^{(\xi)}|\mu|(\mathrm{d}\xi),\qquad\beta_{2}^{(\xi)}:=\int_{\Xi}\beta_{1}^{(\eta)}|\nu_{\xi}|(\mathrm{d}\eta),

\displaystyle\beta_{123}^{(\xi)}(a,b,c):=\int_{\Xi}\min\left\{a,b\beta_{1}^{(\eta)}+c\sqrt{\beta_{2}^{(\eta)}}\right\}|\nu_{\xi}|(\mathrm{d}\eta),\quad\beta_{234}(a,b,c):=\int_{\Xi}\beta_{123}^{(\xi)}(a,b,c)|\mu|(\mathrm{d}\xi).

If
\beta_{2}<\infty
, then for any
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[f(W)]-\mathbb{E}[f(Z)]|\leq\beta_{234}(1.8,3.58+0.55\log d,3.5)[f]_{\operatorname{Lip},1}.

Through Steps 1 and 2, this framework overcomes Challenge
1
by
introducing a Gaussian smoothing of
f
. This smoothing bypasses the need for a bounded third derivative; instead, the
analysis relies on second- and fourth-order derivative bounds for the smoothed
test function. This mechanism is made explicit in the proof of
Lemma
1
; we refer further details to
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
. This framework is instrumental in achieving the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

CLT rate for
independent
data under a finite third-moment assumption

(
raivc2018multivariate
)
. By contrast, approaches based on regularity bounds
for Stein’s equation (e.g.,
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
) typically yield only
the suboptimal
\mathcal{O}\bigl(n^{-1/2}\log n\bigr)
rate; see
Section
1.1
.

3.2.
Constructing
\mathcal{S}
for Dependent Data and A Tractable Auxiliary Bound

The
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rates for multivariate
independent
data were established by Raič
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
via an explicit construction of the objects

\mathcal{S}=(\Xi,\{V_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi},\mu,\psi,\{\nu_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi})
.
However, this specific construction is restricted to the independent setting and
does not extend to dependent data. In this work, we generalize Raič’s approach
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
by introducing a novel construction of

\mathcal{S}
that faithfully captures the underlying dependence structure. Importantly,
our choice of
\mathcal{S}
yields an auxiliary bound on the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rate for general
dependent data, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 0.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued sequence such that
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{i}\|^{2}\big]<\infty.
Define the normalized variables
U_{i}:=X_{i}/\sqrt{n}
for
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and
W:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{i}.
Assume that
\mathbb{E}[W]=0
and
\operatorname{Var}(W)=I_{d}
. Then,

(4)

\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\bigr)\;\lesssim\;\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{i}\|\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\bigr)\right].

The inequality (
4
) bounds the CLT rates in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
by aggregating, over indices
i
, a weighted measure of dependence quantifying how much the law of
W
changes when conditioning on a single increment
U_{i}
. Therefore, to bound the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT error, it suffices to control the right-hand side of
(
4
), which is typically more tractable since it directly reflects the
underlying dependence structure. Below we outline the proof of
Proposition
2
by describing our construction of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof outline of Proposition
2

Construct the index space

\Xi:=I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad I=\{0,\dots,n-1\}.

For each
\xi=(i,x,t,y)\in\Xi
, define
V_{\xi}
as follows. First, construct
\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}=(\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{0},\dots,\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{n-1})
by

\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{i}:=x,\qquad\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{i}\overset{d}{=}(U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{n-1})\mid U_{i}=x.

Set
\widetilde{V}_{i,x}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{j}
and
Y_{i,x}:=W-\widetilde{V}_{i,x}
. Put

V_{(i,x,0,y)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{V}_{i,x}\quad\text{(independent of $y$)},\qquad V_{(i,x,t,y)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(1-t)\widetilde{V}_{i,x}+tW\ |\ Y_{i,x}=y\quad(t\in(0,1]).

Define the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measure
\mu
on
\Xi
by

\mu\big(\{i\}\times B\times\{0\}\times\{\bm{0}\}\big):=\mathbb{E}\big[U_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{U_{i}\in B\}\big],\quad\forall B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\mu(\text{outside }\{t=0,y=\bm{0}\})=0.

For all bounded measurable
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we then have

\mathbb{E}\big[f(W)W\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[f(W)U_{i}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[f(W)\mid U_{i}]U_{i}\big]=\int_{\Xi}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[f(V_{\xi})\big]\ \mu(\mathrm{d}\xi).

The above construction completes Step 1 in Section
3.1
. Next, we consider the following mapping

\psi:\ \Xi\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \longrightarrow\ \Xi,\qquad\psi\big((i,x,t,y),s,z\big):=\big(i,\ x,\ t^{\prime}:=t+s(1-t),\ y^{\prime}:=\tfrac{z}{1-t}\mathds{1}_{\{t<1\}}\big),

and its associated
\{\nu_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\Xi}
. A direct calculation shows

\mathcal{L}\big((1-s)V_{\xi}+sW\mid W-V_{\xi}=z\big){=}\ \mathcal{L}\big((1-t^{\prime})\widetilde{V}_{i,x}+t^{\prime}W\ |\ Y_{i,x}=y^{\prime}\big),\quad\forall s\in[0,1]\text{ and }\mathcal{L}(Z_{\xi})\text{-a.e.\ }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The above holds because
W-V_{\xi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(1-t)Y_{i,x}
, conditioning on
W-V_{\xi}=z
is equivalent to conditioning on
Y_{i,x}=z/(1-t)
. Then, by the very definition of the family
\{V_{\eta}\}
,

\mathcal{L}\big((1-s)V_{\xi}+sW\ |\ W-V_{\xi}=z\big)=\mathcal{L}\big(V_{\psi(\xi,s,z)}\big).

Therefore, we complete Step 2 in Section
3.1
. With our new construction of
\mathcal{S}
and Lemma
1
, a careful control of
the
\beta
-functionals yields Proposition
2
; the detailed proof is deferred to
Appendix
A.1
.

3.3.
Proof Outline of Theorem
1

Case 1:
\delta=1

When
\delta=1
, we leverage Proposition
2
to derive the
W_{1}
CLT rate. Specifically, in order to control each term
\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{i}\|\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\bigr)\right]
on the right-hand side of (
4
), we construct an explicit
coupling between
W
and
W\mid U_{i}
. In particular, for each
i\in I
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the following
block-resampling
coupling:

\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{i}:=x,\qquad\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{\mathcal{N}[i]\setminus\{i\}}\sim\mathcal{L}\big(U_{\mathcal{N}[i]\setminus\{i\}}\mid U_{i}=x,\ U_{\mathcal{N}[i]^{c}}\big),\qquad\widetilde{U}^{(i,x)}_{j}:=U_{j}\ (j\notin\mathcal{N}[i]).

The above coupling fixes the
i
th coordinate at
x
, keeps all coordinates outside its neighborhood unchanged, and only resamples its neighborhood
\mathcal{N}[i]\setminus\{i\}
conditional on

(U_{i}=x,U_{\mathcal{N}[i]^{c}})
.
Crucially, this mechanism
localizes the effect of conditioning to the block
\mathcal{N}[i]
. Under the uniform third-moment bound

\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{3}=\mathcal{O}(1)
(i.e.,
\delta=1
), one can show
that each term
\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{i}\|\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\bigr)\right]
is of order
\mathcal{O}(D^{2}n^{-3/2})
. Summing over
i
then yields the rate

\mathcal{O}(D^{2}n^{-1/2})
in Theorem
1
. The detailed
proof is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Case 2:
\delta\in(0,1)

The extension of Raič’s framework to the regime
\delta\in(0,1)
is not immediate.
Under the condition
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{2+\delta}\in\mathcal{O}(1)
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, we instead invoke the classical regularity theory for the Stein equation
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
. Specifically, for any
h\in C^{0,1},
let
f_{h}
denote the solution to the multivariate Stein equation

\Delta f(x)-x\cdot\nabla f(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}h(Z),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By setting
W=W_{i}+U_{i}
, and employing a second–order Taylor expansion, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[h(W)-h(Z)\big]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta f_{h}(W)-nU_{i}^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{h}\left(W_{i}+\theta U_{i}\right)U_{i}\right],

where
\theta
is uniformly
distributed in
[0,1]
and independent of everything else. While
f_{h}\in C^{2,\delta}
by
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
, the locally dependent structure implies that
W_{i}
is not independent of
U_{i}
. Consequently, the argument for independent data
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
does not apply directly. To circumvent this, for each
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, we define
W_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{N}[i]}U_{j}
and decompose the above equation into three terms

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(W)-h(Z)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Delta f_{h}(W_{i}^{\prime}+U_{i})-nU_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{h}\big(W_{i}^{\prime}+\theta U_{i}\big)U_{i}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Delta f_{h}(W)-\Delta f_{h}(W_{i}^{\prime}+U_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[U_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{h}\big(W_{i}^{\prime}+\theta U_{i}\big)U_{i}-U_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{h}\big(W_{i}+\theta U_{i}\big)U_{i}\Big].

We then bound the above three terms individually. The first term is analyzed following the approach in
(
gallouet2018regularity
)
, as
W_{i}^{\prime}
is independent of
U_{i}
by construction. The remaining two terms are controlled by leveraging the
C^{2,\delta}
regularity of
f_{h}
. The detailed proof is deferred to
Appendix
A.3
.

3.4.
Proof Outline of Theorem
2

We establish the
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates via a standard
big–small block
decomposition

(
Meyn12_book
)
, which approximates the sum of
M
-dependent data by a sum of independent
big blocks, while tightly controlling the contribution of the small blocks (of length
M
) and
optimizing the big-block length.
Fix an integer
\ell\geq M
(to be chosen later) and set

k:=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\ell+M}\right\rfloor.

Define consecutive
big blocks
of length
\ell

B_{j}:=\{(j-1)(\ell+M),\dots,(j-1)(\ell+M)+\ell-1\},\qquad j=1,\dots,k,

and
small blocks
of length
M

G_{j}:=\{(j-1)(\ell+M)+\ell,\dots,j(\ell+M)-1\},\qquad j=1,\dots,k,

together with the remainder

R:=\{k(\ell+M),\dots,n-1\},\qquad|R|\leq\ell+M.

Let

U_{j}:=\sum_{i\in B_{j}}X_{i},\quad V_{j}:=\sum_{i\in G_{j}}X_{i},\quad A:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{j},\quad B:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}V_{j},\quad\Delta:=B+\sum_{i\in R}X_{i}.

Then
S_{n}=A+\Delta
holds exactly. Moreover, since the gap between successive big
blocks satisfies
\min B_{j}-\max B_{j-1}=M+1>M
, the sequence
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
is
independent; similarly,
\{V_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
is independent. We next invoke the following
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates for independent
data to control
\mathcal{L}(A)
.

Lemma 0 (Theorem 6 of
(
bonis2020stein
)
).

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be an independent sequence of of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vectors with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
and
\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n})=I_{d}.
If
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}[\|X_{i}\|^{p+q}]\in\mathcal{O}(1)
for some
p\geq 2
and
q\in(0,2]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}))\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{p+q-2}{2p}}).

Finally, applying the triangle inequality, together with bounds on
\mathbb{E}\|\Delta\|^{p}
and on the
discrepancy between
\operatorname{Var}(A)/k
and
\Sigma_{n}
, and optimizing the big-block length
\ell
,
yields Theorem
2
.

Remark:
The big–small block technique attains independence by discarding correlations; however, applying the triangle
inequality requires a tradeoff between: (i) the reduced effective sample size (as larger

\ell
results in fewer independent big blocks) and (ii) the error induced by the discarded remainder. Balancing these two
errors typically leads to a rate that is suboptimal compared with the best available rate in the
independent case (Lemma
3
). Nevertheless, as noted following Theorem
2
, our results significantly narrow the existing gap between
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT theories. Achieving the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate remains an important direction for future research.

4.
Markov chain central limit theorem

We consider a Markov chain
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
with transition kernel
P
on a general state
space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, and assume it satisfies the following geometric ergodicity
condition.

Assumption 1 (Geometric Ergodicity).

The Markov kernel
P
is
\psi
-irreducible and aperiodic. There exist a petite set
C
,
constants
\lambda\in[0,1)
and
L<\infty
, and a function
V:\mathcal{X}\to[1,\infty)
such
that

\displaystyle PV\leq\lambda V+L\mathds{1}_{C}.

Moreover, the initial distribution
x_{0}\sim\mu
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[V(x_{0})]<\infty
.

Assumption
1
is standard in the derivation of CLTs for Markov
chains; see, e.g.,
(
Meyn12_book
;
douc2008bounds
;
srikant2024rates
)
. In this section, we study additive functionals.
Let
h_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h_{i}]=0
and
\|h_{i}\|^{2}\leq V
for all

i\geq 0
. For
n\geq 1
, define

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_{i}(x_{i}),\qquad\Sigma_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n}).

We consider the nondegenerate regime where the normalized covariance matrices are uniformly well-conditioned, i.e.,
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})\geq\Omega(1)
. We have the following lemma; its proof is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Lemma 0.

Under Assumption
1
, the matrix
\Sigma_{n}
is well defined for
every
n\geq 1
, and
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n})=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Moreover, in the homogeneous
case where
h_{t}\equiv h
for all
t\geq 0
, the limit
\Sigma_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}(S_{n})
exists, and
\|\Sigma_{n}-\Sigma_{\infty}\|=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

It therefore suffices to control the distance to
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})
. Indeed,
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\infty}))\lesssim\|\Sigma_{n}-\Sigma_{\infty}\|^{1/2}\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
for any

p\geq 1
, by Lemma
2
. Since
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
is already the optimal order
for Gaussian approximation, this covariance-mismatch term is of the same (or smaller) order
as the intrinsic CLT error. In particular, by the triangle inequality, we have

\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\infty})\bigr)\leq\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\bigr)+\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\infty})\bigr)\lesssim\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\bigr).

We establish two theorems characterizing
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates
for Markov chains with
p=1
and
p\geq 2
.

4.1.
CLT Rates in Wasserstein-1 Distance

We now state a theorem that quantifies the gap between

\mathcal{L}\big(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)
and the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})
in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

Theorem 2.

Under Assumption
1
, if
\|h_{i}\|^{2+\delta}\leq V
for all
i\geq 0
and some
\delta>1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\bigr)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}).

We emphasize that the best rate available in the literature is

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n)

(
srikant2024rates
;
wu2025uncertainty
)
. The
logarithmic factor appears because existing analysis relies on Lemma
1
; see the discussion in Section
1.1
.
In contrast, Theorem
2
establishes the first
optimal

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

\mathcal{W}_{1}
–CLT rate for geometrically ergodic Markov chains,
thereby resolving an open problem highlighted in the recent work by Srikant
(
srikant2024rates
)
.

We remark that our assumptions on the Markov chain are strictly weaker than those in

(
wu2025uncertainty
)
, which require a positive spectral gap and uniform boundedness of the
test functions
h_{i}
. Furthermore, unlike
(
srikant2024rates
)
, which is is restricted to the homogeneous
case
h_{i}\equiv h
, Theorem
2
allows for time-varying test
functions
\{h_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
. To achieve the optimal
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
, Theorem
2
imposes a slightly stronger
moment condition on the chain: we assume a
V
-dominated
(2+\delta)
-moment for some

\delta>1
, while
(
srikant2024rates
)
works with
\delta=1
.
Nevertheless, the approach of
(
srikant2024rates
)
does not yield the

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate—even under stronger moment assumptions—due to
its reliance on Lemma
1
.
It remains unclear whether a
V
-dominated third moment alone suffices to
achieve an
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

\mathcal{W}_{1}
–CLT rate. We leave this question for
future research.

Our proof attains the optimal rate by leveraging Proposition
2
, which reduces the
analysis to controlling
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\bigr)
.
We accomplish this by first
showing that the time-reversal kernel
P^{*}
also satisfies
Assumption
1
, and then applying a combination of
forward and backward maximal couplings to localize the effect of conditioning
on
U_{i}
.

As an intermediate step, we prove that the regeneration time of the split chain

(
nummelin1978splitting
)
associated with a geometrically ergodic Markov chain
has a geometric tail, without assuming strong aperiodicity
(
roberts1999bounds
)
or other restrictive
conditions
(
rosenthal1995minorization
)
. The details are presented in Section
5.1
and may be
of independent interest.
Finally, the
proof of Theorem
2
is outlined in Section
5.2
,
with full details deferred to Appendix
E
.

4.2.
CLT Rates in Wasserstein-p (
p\geq 2
) Distance

The
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) CLT rates for Markov chains
remain poorly understood. To narrow this gap, we establish the following
theorem, which provides multivariate
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) CLT rates for
geometrically ergodic Markov chains under mild moment conditions.

Theorem 3.

Under Assumption
1
, if
\|h_{i}\|^{p+q}\leq V
for all
i\geq 0
and some
p\geq 2
and
q\in(0,2]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\bigr)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{p+q-2}{2(2p+q-2)}}).

Theorem
3
establishes the first
\mathcal{W}_{p}
–CLT convergence rates for Markov chain under
mild moment conditions. These rates match the best bounds currently available
for
m
-dependent data (Theorem
2
) and resolve another open
problem highlighted in the conclusion of
(
srikant2024rates
)
. As discussed
following Theorem
2
, these rates may not be optimal; however,
they substantially narrow the gap between the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
theory and the
\mathcal{W}_{p}

(

From a technical perspective, roughly speaking, we proceed as follows. Using the
split-chain construction in Section

, we first apply a
martingale decomposition to the partial sum, and then perform a regeneration

1
-dependent sequence (a special case of

(

) CLT rates for

-dependent sequences (Theorem

).
The proof is outlined in Section
5.3
and detailed in
Appendix
F
.

In terms of applications, establishing convergence rates for the Markov chain
CLT is crucial for assessing asymptotic efficiency, deriving finite-sample error
bounds, and enabling statistical inference for parameters of interest in
stochastic algorithms and dynamical systems

(
srikant2024rates
;
samsonov2024gaussian
;
wu2025uncertainty
)
. Since prior work
on Markov chain CLT rates is largely restricted to
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and often yields suboptimal
bounds, our Theorems
2
and

make it possible to
derive sharper guarantees under
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and to develop results under more general
\mathcal{W}_{p}

metrics for

Technical Overview of CLT Rates for Markov Chains

This section is organized as follows. Section

(

;

)
and

5.1.
Nummelin’s Splitting

Under Assumption
1
, Theorem 15.0.1 of
(
Meyn12_book
)
and Theorem 15.1.5 of
(
douc2018markov
)
indicate that there exists an accessible small set
\bar{C}
and
\kappa>1
such that

(6)

M(a):=\sup_{x\in\bar{C}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[a^{\sigma_{\bar{C}}}\big]<\infty\quad\forall a\in(1,\kappa].

where
\sigma_{\bar{C}}:=\inf\{n\geq 1:x_{n}\in\bar{C}\}
. Then, by Proposition 5.2.4 of
(
Meyn12_book
)
, there exist
m>0
and a measure
\nu(\cdot)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
yields the minorization condition

(7)

P^{m}(x,\cdot)\geq\beta\mathds{1}_{\bar{C}}(x)\nu(\cdot),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

with
\nu(\bar{C})=1
,
\nu(\bar{C}^{c})=0
. Under the minorization condition (
7
), we employ Nummelin’s splitting

(
nummelin1978splitting
)
to augment the chain and obtain the associated split chain.
Specifically, we construct a process

(8)

\Phi=\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}\qquad\text{on}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}:=\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\},

where
x_{n}
is the
state
and
y_{n}\in\{0,1\}
is the
level
. The first coordinate

\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
has exactly the same law as the original Markov chain. We defer the details
of the construction of
\Phi
to
(
Meyn12_book
;
lemanczyk2021general
)
. Two key properties of (
8
) are: (i) At each block boundary
km
, the level is determined from the current state only. Furthermore,
if
x_{km}\in\bar{C}
, draw
y_{km}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\beta)
; (ii) If
y_{km}=1
, we regenerate
(x_{(k+1)m},y_{(k+1)m})
by

\mathbb{P}\left(x_{(k+1)m}\in\mathrm{d}x\middle|x_{km}\in\bar{C},y_{km}=1\right)=\nu(\mathrm{d}x),

so
(\bar{C},1)
is an atom for the
m
-skeleton
\{(x_{km},y_{km})\}_{k\geq 0}
. Define the regeneration times
0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
of this skeleton, where
\tau_{i}=\min\{k\geq\tau_{i-1}:(x_{km},y_{km})\in(\bar{C},1)\}+1.

Let the cycle lengths be
L_{i}:=\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}
for
i\geq 1
.
The sequence
\{L_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
satisfies the following lemma; its proof is deferred to

.

Lemma 0.

The cycle lengths
\{L_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
are independent,
\{L_{i}\}_{i\geq 2}
are i.i.d. and there exist

and
\rho\in(0,1)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{i}>\ell)\leq b\rho^{\ell},\quad\forall i\geq 1,\ell\geq 0.

Lemma
1
plays a key role in proving that the time-reversed chain
P^{\ast}

is geometrically ergodic in Section
5.2
, and in controlling the moments
arising from the regeneration decomposition in Section
5.3
. Results akin to Lemma

were obtained by
(
roberts1999bounds
)

in the strongly aperiodic case

.
(

)
considers general
m
, but under a stronger structural
assumption: a sublevel set
\{V\leq R\}
satisfies (
6
). This property does not follow
directly from the standard split–chain construction. A technical issue raised in
(
rosenthal1995minorization
)
was later clarified in

(
roberts1999bounds
)
. Our Lemma

recovers the
m=1
bounds of

(
roberts1999bounds
)
and extends them to general

5.2.
p=1
: Raič’s Framework + Forward and Backward Maximal Coupling

To streamline the analysis, we first reduce to the stationary initialization

as shown in Appendix

, this initialization mismatch contributes at most

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
to the final CLT rate.

Inspired by Proposition
2
, we
consider the normalization

U_{i}:=\frac{h_{i}(x_{i})}{\sqrt{n}},\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}\quad\text{and}\quad W:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{i}.

Our goal is to control

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{i}\|\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\bigr)\right]
.
Since the mapping
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu)
is convex in
\mu

(
matthes2009family
)
, the arguments before
(

by a deterministic index

where the increments

are

A natural alternative to deterministic approximation of

However, this approach is hindered by the fact that the summands themselves depend on

, it may be

directly, rather than reducing to a fixed-length

;

closely related to this framework. Existing CLT-rate results for compound renewal processes are largely confined to
the univariate setting and focus on metrics such as the Kolmogorov distance
(
cox1962renewal
;
cox2017theory
;
malinovskii2021level
)
.
Developing

transportation metrics such as

—is an interesting direction for future
work.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we establish
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 1
) CLT convergence rates—optimal in
some regimes—for multivariate locally dependent sequences and geometric ergodic Markov chains
under mild moment condition.
We also
discuss an application of our optimal
\mathcal{W}_{1}
rates to multivariate

U
-statistics. These results may be broadly useful for providing quantitative
uncertainty guarantees across a wide range of problems in machine learning.

From a technical perspective, we extend Raič’s framework
(
raivc2018multivariate
)
and obtain a tractable bound for the

\mathcal{W}_{1}
Gaussian approximation error under dependence. We then leverage this bound to establish optimal
\mathcal{W}_{1}
rates for both locally dependent data
and geometric ergodic Markov chains. We expect this bound to be useful more broadly for
deriving rates under other dependent structures. In addition, we show that the
regeneration time of the split chain associated with a geometrically ergodic
Markov chain has a geometric tail, without assuming strong aperiodicity or other
restrictive conditions commonly imposed in the literature; this result may be of
independent interest.

There are several natural directions for future work:

•

For locally dependent data, an open question is
whether one can achieve the CLT rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{p+q-2}{2p}})
for
\mathcal{W}_{p}
with
p\geq 2
, matching the best available rate for independent data

(
bonis2020stein
)
. The analysis in
(
bonis2020stein
)
relies on an
exchangeable-pair construction that is highly tailored to independence. A
promising direction is to generalize the exchangeable-pair technique to capture
dependence structures.

•

In Theorem
2
, we require a
V
-dominated
(2+\delta)
-moment
condition with
\delta>1
in order to obtain the optimal

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

\mathcal{W}_{1}
CLT rate for Markov chains.
Determining whether a
V
-dominated third
moment alone would suffice to achieve this optimal rate remains an important challenge.

•

As discussed in Section
5.3
, one potential avenue for improving
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(
p\geq 2
) CLT rates for Markov chain is to study
\mathcal{W}_{p}
CLT rates for
multivariate compound renewal processes. This would likely require extending existing renewal
theory—which are largely confined to the univariate setting and to
metrics such as the Kolmogorov distance—to multivariate transportation metrics
(e.g.,
(
cox1962renewal
;
cox2017theory
;
malinovskii2021level
)
).

•

This work focuses on capturing the dependence on the sample size
n
.
Establishing sharp dependence on the dimension
d
is another interesting direction worth pursuing, particularly for high-dimensional statistical applications.

For the case
\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{2+\delta}<\infty

For the first term, since

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Delta f_{h}(W_{i}^{\prime}+U_{i})-nU_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}f_{h}\big(W_{i}^{\prime}+\theta U_{i}\big)U_{i}\Big]\right|

V^{1/2}

) and telescoping,

(23)

S_{n}=g_{0}(x_{0})-Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})+\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}m_{t}=:R_{n}+M_{n}.

Step 4: Uniform
O(1)
control of the boundary term
R_{n}
.

By (
20
) and (
18
),

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|R_{n}\|^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|g_{0}(x_{0})\|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})\|^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mu}V(x_{0})+\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}V(x_{t})<\infty.

In particular,

(24)

\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(R_{n})\|<\infty\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(R_{n})\|<\infty.

Step 5:
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n}),\lambda_{\max}(\bar{\Sigma}_{n})\in\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Since
\{m_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}
, we have

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}m_{t}^{\top}].

Define the conditional covariance matrix

H_{t}(x):=\mathbb{E}\Big[(g_{t}(Y)-Pg_{t}(x))(g_{t}(Y)-Pg_{t}(x))^{\top}\Big|Y\sim P(x,\cdot)\Big],

so
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}m_{t}^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[H_{t}(x_{t-1})]
and
H_{t}(x)\succeq 0
.
Then, we have

\|H_{t}(x)\|\leq\mathbb{E}\|g_{t}(Y)-Pg_{t}(x)\|^{2}\leq 2P(\|g_{t}\|^{2})(x)+2\|Pg_{t}(x)\|^{2}\leq 4P(\|g_{t}\|^{2})(x).

By (
20
) and
PV\leq\lambda V+L\mathds{1}_{C}\leq(\lambda+L)V
(since
V\geq 1
),

(25)

\|H_{t}(x)\|\lesssim PV(x)\lesssim V(x)\qquad\forall t,x.

Taking expectations and using
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}V(x_{t})<\infty
gives

\sup_{t\geq 1}\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}m_{t}^{\top}]\|<\infty
, hence

(26)

\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})\|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}m_{t}^{\top}]\|\lesssim n.

Now consider
\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n}+R_{n})
.
First,
\operatorname{Cov}_{\mu}(M_{n},g_{0}(x_{0}))=0
because
g_{0}(x_{0})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}[m_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
.
It remains to bound
\operatorname{Cov}_{\mu}(M_{n},Pg_{n}(x_{n-1}))
.
For
t\leq n-1
, the Markov property yields

\mathbb{E}[Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=P^{n-1-t}(Pg_{n})(x_{t})=P^{n-t}g_{n}(x_{t}).

Hence, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})^{\top}]\|=\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}(P^{n-t}g_{n}(x_{t}))^{\top}]\|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|m_{t}\|^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|P^{n-t}g_{n}(x_{t})\|^{2}}.

The first factor is uniformly bounded by (
25
)–(
18
).
For the second factor, note that
\pi(g_{n})=0
(since
\pi(h_{n+k})=0
for all
k
) and

\|g_{n}\|_{V^{1/2}}\leq c_{0}/(1-\rho_{0})
by (
20
), so applying (
19
) to
g_{n}
gives

\|P^{n-t}g_{n}(x)\|=\|P^{n-t}g_{n}(x)-\pi(g_{n})\|\leq c_{0}\rho_{0}^{n-t}\|g_{n}\|_{V^{1/2}}V^{1/2}(x)\lesssim\rho_{0}^{n-t}V^{1/2}(x).

Therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|P^{n-t}g_{n}(x_{t})\|^{2}\lesssim\rho_{0}^{2(n-t)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}V(x_{t})\lesssim\rho_{0}^{2(n-t)}
,
and so
\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})^{\top}]\|\lesssim\rho_{0}^{n-t}
.
Summing over
t
yields

(27)

\Big\|\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m_{t}Pg_{n}(x_{n-1})^{\top}]\Big\|=\mathcal{O}(1).

Combining (
24
), (
26
), and (
27
) in (
23
) gives

\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})\|\lesssim n,

hence
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{n})=\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})\|/n=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The same argument under stationarity shows
\lambda_{\max}(\bar{\Sigma}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Step 6:
\|\Sigma_{n}-\bar{\Sigma}_{n}\|=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

From the preceding bounds and the decomposition (
23
), we have

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[H_{t}(x_{t-1})]+\mathcal{O}(1),\qquad\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(S_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\pi(H_{t})+\mathcal{O}(1),

Moreover, (
25
) implies
\sup_{t\geq 1}\|H_{t}\|_{V}<\infty
, where

\|F\|_{V}:=\sup_{x}\|F(x)\|/V(x)
.

By
V
-geometric ergodicity (again from
[
5
]
), there exist
c>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
for any (matrix-valued)
F
with
\|F\|_{V}<\infty
,

\|\mu P^{k}F-\pi(F)\|\leq c\rho^{k}\mu(V)\|F\|_{V}.

Applying this with
F=H_{t}
and
k=t-1
gives

\|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[H_{t}(x_{t-1})]-\pi(H_{t})\|=\|\mu P^{t-1}H_{t}-\pi(H_{t})\|\lesssim\rho^{t-1}.

Summing over
t
shows
\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})-\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(S_{n})\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
, hence

\|\Sigma_{n}-\bar{\Sigma}_{n}\|=\frac{1}{n}\|\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})-\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(S_{n})\|=\mathcal{O}(1/n).

Step 7: Lower bound on
\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma}_{n})
.

Under the standing nondegenerate regime assumption
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})\gtrsim 1
,
Weyl’s inequality yields

\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma}_{n})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{n})-\|\Sigma_{n}-\bar{\Sigma}_{n}\|\gtrsim 1-1/n\gtrsim 1.

Step 8: Homogeneous case and existence of
\Sigma_{\infty}
.

Assume now
h_{t}\equiv h
. Then
g_{t}\equiv g
and
H_{t}\equiv H
are time-independent, and

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[H(x_{t-1})].

Define

\Sigma_{\infty}:=\pi(H)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[m_{1}m_{1}^{\top}]\succeq 0.

As above,
\|\mu P^{t-1}H-\pi(H)\|\lesssim\rho^{t-1}
, so

\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})-\Sigma_{\infty}\Big\|=\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\big(\mu P^{t-1}H-\pi(H)\big)\Big\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\|\mu P^{t-1}H-\pi(H)\|=\mathcal{O}(1/n).

Finally, (
23
)–(
27
) imply
\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})+\mathcal{O}(1)
, hence

\Big\|\Sigma_{n}-\Sigma_{\infty}\Big\|=\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})-\Sigma_{\infty}\Big\|\leq\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(M_{n})-\Sigma_{\infty}\Big\|+\mathcal{O}(1/n)=\mathcal{O}(1/n).

In particular,
\Sigma_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})
exists. This completes the proof of Lemma
1
, and hence also of
Lemma
1
.

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
2

In what follows, we prove Theorem
2
by filling in the details of the proof outline
presented in Section
5.2
.

E.1.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect several useful lemmas that will be used in the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 0.

Under Assumption
1
, we have
\pi(V)=\int V\mathrm{d}\pi\leq\frac{L}{1-\lambda}<\infty.

Proof.

Since
\pi
is invariant,

\pi(V)=\int V\mathrm{d}\pi=\int PVd\pi\leq\lambda\int Vd\pi+L\int\mathds{1}_{C}d\pi\leq\lambda\pi(V)+L.

Therefore,

\pi(V)\leq\frac{L}{1-\lambda}<\infty.

∎

Lemma 0.

Let
A,B
be symmetric positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices.
Then for any
p\geq 1
,

W_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(0,A),\mathcal{N}(0,B)\bigr)\lesssim\|A-B\|^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and consider the coupling
X=A^{1/2}Z
and
Y=B^{1/2}Z
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathcal{L}(Y)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\|X-Y\|^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl\|(A^{1/2}-B^{1/2})Z\bigr\|^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}\|\lesssim\|A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\overset{\text{(i)}}{\leq}\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{1/2}\lesssim\|A-B\|^{1/2}.

where (i) holds by the Powers–Størmer inequality
[
59
]
.
∎

E.2.
Reduction to Stationary Initialization

In this section, we show that, without loss of generality, we may assume the chain is initialized from the stationary distribution
\pi
: the effect of a nonstationary initialization contributes only a higher-order term to the final CLT rate.

Let
x_{0}\sim\mu
and
y_{0}\sim\pi
be independent. Conditional on
(x_{0},y_{0})
,
apply Lemma
3
to obtain a coupling

(X_{t}^{x},X_{t}^{y})_{t\geq 0}
of two copies of the
P
-chain with

X_{0}^{x}=x_{0}
,
X_{0}^{y}=y_{0}
, and meeting time

T^{+}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ X_{t}^{x}=X_{t}^{y}\},

satisfying

(28)

\mathbb{P}(T^{+}>k|x_{0},y_{0})\ \lesssim\ (V(x_{0})+V(y_{0}))\rho^{k},\qquad k\geq 0.

Define the coupled partial sums

S_{n}^{x}:=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}h(X_{t}^{x}),\qquad S_{n}^{y}:=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}h(X_{t}^{y}),\qquad\bar{S}_{n}^{x}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}S_{n}^{x},\ \bar{S}_{n}^{y}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}S_{n}^{y}.

By construction,
X_{t}^{x}=X_{t}^{y}
for all
t\geq T^{+}
, hence

S_{n}^{x}-S_{n}^{y}=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\bigl(h(X_{t}^{x})-h(X_{t}^{y})\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\{T^{+}>t\}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bar{S}_{n}^{x}-\bar{S}_{n}^{y}\|\ \big|\ x_{0},y_{0}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|h(X_{t}^{x})\|+\|h(X_{t}^{y})\|\mathbf{1}_{\{T^{+}>t\}}\ \Big|\ x_{0},y_{0}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[\|h(X_{t}^{x})\|^{2}\mid x_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[\|h(X_{t}^{y})\|^{2}\mid y_{0}]\Bigr)^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(T^{+}>t\mid x_{0},y_{0})^{1/2}.

By Assumption
1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|h(X_{t}^{x})\|^{2}\mid x_{0}]\leq\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t})\mid x_{0}]\leq\lambda^{t}V(x_{0})+\frac{L}{1-\lambda}.

and

\mathbb{E}[\|h(X_{t}^{y})\|^{2}\mid y_{0}]\leq\lambda^{t}V(y_{0})+\frac{L}{1-\lambda}
. Then, by inequality (
28
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bar{S}_{n}^{x}-\bar{S}_{n}^{y}\|\ \big|\ x_{0},y_{0}\bigr]\

\displaystyle\lesssim\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(\lambda^{t}V(y_{0})+\lambda^{t}V(y_{0})+1)^{1/2}\sqrt{V(x_{0})+V(y_{0})}\rho^{t/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(V(x_{0})+V(y_{0}))\rho^{t/2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(V(x_{0})+V(y_{0})).

Then,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bar{S}_{n}^{x}-\bar{S}_{n}^{y}\|\ \big|\ x_{0},y_{0}\bigr]\ \lesssim\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigl(V(x_{0})+V(y_{0})+1\bigr).

Consequently,

(29)

W_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\bigr)\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bar{S}_{n}^{x}-\bar{S}_{n}^{y}\|\bigr]\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[V(x_{0})]+\pi(V)+1\Bigr)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

where the last inequality uses Lemma
1
(in particular,
\pi(V)<\infty
)
together with the standing assumption
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[V(x_{0})]<\infty
.

E.3.
Main Proof

Following the discussion in Section
E.2
, we first work under
stationary initialization and normalization: assume
x_{0}\sim\pi
and
\bar{\Sigma}_{n}=I_{d}
. Moreover, Lemma
2
shows that the backward process admits a
time-reversal kernel
P^{\ast}
which is
\psi
-irreducible, aperiodic, and
geometrically ergodic.

We utilize the Raic’s framework and the objects
\mathcal{S}
we constructed in Section
3.2
. As discussed in
(
4
), to derive a Wasserstein–
1
CLT rate for the normalized sum

W:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{i},

it suffices to control the quantity

(30)

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|U_{i}\|W_{2}^{2}\big(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i})\big)\Big].

In what follows, we set
U_{i}=\frac{h_{i}(x_{i})}{\sqrt{n}}
for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. Let
\nu:=\mathcal{L}(W)
,

\nu_{x}:=\mathcal{L}(W\mid x_{i}=x)
and

\nu_{u}^{U}:=\mathcal{L}(W\mid U_{i}=u)
.
By disintegration,

\nu_{u}^{U}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nu_{x}(\cdot)\kappa_{u}(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\kappa_{u}
is a regular conditional law of
x_{i}
given
U_{i}=u
. The map
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\mu)
is convex in
\mu
.
Hence

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu_{u}^{U})\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu_{x})\kappa_{u}(\mathrm{d}x).

Then, the quantity (
30
) can be further bounded by

(31)

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|U_{i}\|\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\big(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{L}(W\mid x_{i})\big)\Big].

In the sequel, we work with a bi-infinite stationary version

(x_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
of the chain, so that
x_{0}\sim\pi
. Fix

i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
. By Lemmas
3

and
2
, we construct, on the same probability space, a
process
(y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that:

•

y_{i}=x
almost surely;

•

(y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
has the same law as
(x_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
conditional on

x_{i}=x
;

•

there exist nonnegative, integer-valued random variables
T^{+}
and
T^{-}

such that

x_{t}=y_{t}\quad\text{whenever}\quad t\geq i+T^{+}\ \text{or}\ t\leq i-T^{-}.

By Lemma
2
, the time-reversed kernel
P^{\ast}
admits a
Lyapunov function
V^{\prime}
and constants
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and
L^{\prime}<\infty
such
that

P^{\ast}V^{\prime}\leq\lambda^{\prime}V^{\prime}+L^{\prime}.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
(to be specified later). Then
V^{\alpha}
and

(V^{\prime})^{\alpha}
are valid Lyapunov functions for
P
and
P^{\ast}
, respectively.
Indeed, since
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
, Jensen’s
inequality yields

PV^{\alpha}\leq(PV)^{\alpha}\leq(\lambda V+L)^{\alpha}\leq\lambda^{\alpha}V^{\alpha}+L^{\alpha},

and similarly,

P^{\ast}(V^{\prime})^{\alpha}\leq(P^{\ast}V^{\prime})^{\alpha}\leq(\lambda^{\prime}V^{\prime}+L^{\prime})^{\alpha}\leq(\lambda^{\prime})^{\alpha}(V^{\prime})^{\alpha}+(L^{\prime})^{\alpha}.

Moreover, because
V,V^{\prime}\geq 1
and Lemma
1
gives

\pi(V)<\infty
and
\pi(V^{\prime})<\infty
, we also have

\pi(V^{\alpha})\leq\pi(V)<\infty,\qquad\pi\bigl((V^{\prime})^{\alpha}\bigr)\leq\pi(V^{\prime})<\infty.

Consequently, applying Lemma
3
to
P
and to
P^{\ast}
,
there exist constants
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that, for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(T^{+}>k)\lesssim\bigl(\mathbb{E}[V(x_{i})^{\alpha}]+V(x)^{\alpha}\bigr)\rho^{k}\lesssim V(x)^{\alpha}\rho^{k},\qquad\mathbb{P}(T^{-}>k)\lesssim(V^{\prime}(x))^{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime})^{k}.

Define

J:=\{t\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}:|t-i|\leq T^{+}\vee T^{-}\},\qquad D:=\sum_{t\in J}\Delta_{t},

where we define
\Delta_{t}:=h_{t}(x_{t})-h_{t}(y_{t}).
By construction,
\Delta_{t}=0
whenever
t\notin J
, and moreover

|J|\leq 2T+1,\qquad T:=T^{+}\vee T^{-}.

Therefore, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|D\|^{2}\middle|y_{i}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sum_{t\in J}\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|J|\sum_{t\in J}\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[(2T+1)\sum_{t\in J}\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x\big].

Using
\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\leq 2\big(\|h_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}+\|h_{t}(y_{t})\|^{2}\big)
and the fact that

\{t\in J\}\subseteq\{T>|t-i|-1\}
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|D\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[(2T+1)\big(\|h_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}+\|h_{t}(y_{t})\|^{2}\big)\mathds{1}\{t\in J\}\mid y_{i}=x\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[(2T+1)\big(\|h_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}+\|h_{t}(y_{t})\|^{2}\big)\mathds{1}\{T>|t-i|-1\}\mid y_{i}=x\right].

Applying Hölder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|D\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[(2T+1)^{\frac{2(2+\delta)}{\delta}}\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{\delta}{2(2+\delta)}}\mathbb{E}[(\|h_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2+\delta}+\|h_{t}(y_{t})\|^{2+\delta})\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{P}(T>|t-i|-1)^{\frac{\delta}{2(2+\delta)}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[(2T+1)^{\frac{2(2+\delta)}{\delta}}\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{\delta}{2(2+\delta)}}\mathbb{E}[(1+\|h(y_{t})\|^{2+\delta})\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{P}(T>|t-i|-1)^{\frac{\delta}{2(2+\delta)}}.

Choose
\alpha=\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}\in(0,1)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(2T+1)^{\frac{2(2+\delta)}{\delta}}\mid y_{i}=x]\lesssim V(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}+V^{\prime}(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T>|t-i|-1)\lesssim(V(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}+V^{\prime}(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}})\tilde{\rho}^{|t-i|},

where
\tilde{\rho}^{|t-i|}:=\max\{\rho,\rho^{\prime}\}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|D\|^{2}\mid y_{i}=x]\lesssim\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(V(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{2+\delta}}+V^{\prime}(x)^{\frac{\delta-1}{2+\delta}})\mathbb{E}[(1+\|h(y_{t})\|^{2+\delta})\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}(\max\{\rho,\rho^{\prime}\})^{|t-i|}.

Finally, using
U_{i}=h_{i}(x_{i})/\sqrt{n}
and
y_{i}\sim\pi
,
we bound the target quantity as

(
31
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}\sim\pi}\left[\|h_{i}(y_{i})\|\mathbb{E}\left[\|D\|^{2}\middle|y_{i}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}\sim\pi}\left[\|h_{i}(y_{i})\|\big(V(y_{i})^{\frac{\delta-1}{2+\delta}}+(V^{\prime}(y_{i}))^{\frac{\delta-1}{2+\delta}}\big)\mathbb{E}[(1+\|h(y_{t})\|^{2+\delta})\mid y_{i}=x]^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\right]\tilde{\rho}^{|t-i|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}\sim\pi}[\|h_{i}(y_{i})\|^{2+\delta}]^{\frac{1}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}\sim\pi}[V(y_{i})+V^{\prime}(y_{i})]^{\frac{\delta-1}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}\sim\pi}[(1+\|h(y_{t})\|^{2+\delta})]^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\tilde{\rho}^{|t-i|}

Under
\|h(\cdot)\|^{2+\delta}\leq V(\cdot)
and
\pi(V)+\pi(V^{\prime})<\infty

(Lemma
1
), the expectation is uniformly finite, and hence

\eqref{eq:W1-goal-true}\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\tilde{\rho}^{|t-i|}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}),

Then, by (
4
), in the stationary setting
x_{0}\sim\pi
with

\bar{\Sigma}_{n}=I_{d}
, we obtain

\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\Sigma}_{n})\big)=\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{x_{0}\sim\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\big)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}).

Next, for a general
\bar{\Sigma}_{n}
, we apply
the linear-algebraic reduction in Appendix
A
together with
Lemma
1
to obtain

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\Sigma}_{n})\big)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}).

Then, combining the triangle inequality with Lemmas
1

and
2
, and using (
29
)
and (
32
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\big)+\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tfrac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}),\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\Sigma}_{n})\big)+\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\Sigma}_{n}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}).

In the homogeneous case (
h_{t}\equiv h
), the triangle inequality together with
Lemma
1
yields

E.5.
Proof of Lemma
3

Recall that for two probability measure
\mu,\nu
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{\operatorname{TV}}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\|f\|\leq 1}|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]|.

Also define the weighted
V
-norm (for
V\geq 1
):

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{V}:=\sup_{\|f\|\leq V}|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]|.

Since
V\geq 1
,
\|\mu-\nu\|_{\operatorname{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{V}.
Under Assumption
1
, by
[
51
, Theorem 15.0.1]
, there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-\mu\|_{V}\lesssim V(x)\rho^{k}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-P^{k}(y,\cdot)\|_{\operatorname{TV}}\lesssim(V(x)+V(y))\rho^{k},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

Recall from
[
35
]
that for any Markov kernel
P
and any

x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, one can construct a maximal coupling of the
entire trajectories
\{(X_{t}^{x},X_{t}^{y})\}_{t\geq 0}
with
X_{0}^{x}=x
and
X_{0}^{y}=y
,
such that once the two chains meet they evolve together thereafter, and for every

k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{k}^{x}\neq X_{k}^{y})=\bigl\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-P^{k}(y,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\operatorname{TV}}.

Given such a coupling, the meeting time
T^{+}(x,y):=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}^{x}=X_{t}^{y}\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{+}(x,y)>k)=\mathbb{P}(X_{k}^{x}\neq X_{k}^{y})=\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-P^{k}(y,\cdot)\|_{\operatorname{TV}}\lesssim(V(x)+V(y))\rho^{k},\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Therefore, for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(T^{+}(x,y))^{p}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}((k+1)^{p}-k^{p})\mathbb{P}(T^{+}(x,y)>k)

\displaystyle\leq p\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(T^{+}(x,y)>k)

\displaystyle\lesssim(V(x)+V(y))\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{p-1}\rho^{k}\lesssim(V(x)+V(y)).

This completes the proof of Lemma
3
.

, where
D:=\{V\leq R\}
.
Let

T_{D}\ :=\ \inf\{t\geq 0:x_{tm}\in D\}

be the entrance time of the skeleton into
D
. The process

\rho^{-(t\wedge T_{D})}V(x_{(t\wedge T_{D})m})
is a nonnegative supermartingale, hence

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\rho^{-(t\wedge T_{D})}V(x_{(t\wedge T_{D})m})\big]\ \leq\ V(x),\qquad t\geq 0.

Since
V\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle V(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{\rho}^{-t}\{T_{D}>t\}]=\rho^{-t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(T_{D}>t).

Then, for
1<s\leq\rho^{-1}
,

(65)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[s^{T_{D}}]=s\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}s^{t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(T_{D}>t)\leq s\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(s\rho)^{t}V(x)=\frac{s}{1-s\rho}V(x).

Step 2: From
D
to
\bar{C}
.

Under the geometric drift with a petite set and aperiodicity, every sublevel set

D=\{V\leq R\}
is small for some power of the chain
(see, e.g.,
[
51
, Lem. 15.2.8]
).
Hence, there exist
n_{D}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon_{D}>0
and a probability measure

\nu_{D}
such that

P^{n_{D}}(x,\cdot)\ \geq\ \epsilon_{D}\nu_{D}(\cdot),\qquad x\in D.

Pad to a multiple of
m
: let
q:=\lceil n_{D}/m\rceil
,
r_{D}:=mq-n_{D}\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
,
and define
\mu:=\nu_{D}P^{r_{D}}.
Then,

(66)

P^{mq}(x,\cdot)\ \geq\ \epsilon_{D}\mu(\cdot),\qquad x\in D.

Since
P
is
\psi
–irreducible and aperiodic and
\bar{C}
is accessible, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}P^{m\ell}(x,\bar{C})>0

[
29
, Theorem 9.3.11]
.
Let
E_{\ell}:=\{x:P^{m\ell}(x,\bar{C})>0\}
. Because
\bigcup_{\ell\geq 1}E_{\ell}=\mathcal{X}
,
we have
\mu(E_{\ell_{*}})>0
for some
\ell_{*}
. By
[
32
, Proposition 2.16]
,

(67)

\mu P^{m\ell_{*}}(\bar{C})=\int P^{m\ell_{*}}(x,\bar{C})\mu(dx)>0.

Set

r:=q+\ell_{*},\qquad p:=\epsilon_{D}\mu PK^{m\ell_{*}}(\bar{C})\in(0,1].

Combining (
66
), (
67
), and the Markov property,
for every
x\in D
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(x_{rm}\in\bar{C}\big)=\int P^{m\ell_{*}}(z,\bar{C})P^{mq}(x,dz)\geq\epsilon_{D}\mu PK^{m\ell_{*}}(\bar{C})=p.

Therefore

(68)

\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(x_{rm}\in\bar{C}\big)\geq\ p,\quad\forall x\in D.

Let
G(s):=\sup_{x\in D}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}\big].
From (
68
), for every
x\in D
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq s^{r}\Big\{p+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathbb{E}_{x_{rm}}[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}]\big]\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq s^{r}\Big\{p+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathbb{E}_{x_{rm}}[s^{T_{D}}]\big]G(s)\Big\},

Using (
65
) and
V\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathbb{E}_{x_{rm}}[s^{T_{D}}]\big]\ \leq\ \frac{s}{1-s\rho}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[V(x_{rm})\big]<c<\infty,

because
PV\leq\lambda V+b
and

V\leq R
on
D
. Hence, for all
x\in D
,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}\big]\ \leq\ s^{r}\Big\{p+(1-p)cG(s)\Big\},

and taking suprema gives

G(s)\ \leq\ s^{r}\Big\{p+(1-p)cG(s)\Big\}.

Choose
s>1
so close to
1
that
s<1/\rho
and
s^{r}(1-p)c<1
; then

(69)

G(s)\ \leq\ \frac{s^{r}p}{1-s^{r}(1-p)c}\ <\ \infty.

Finally,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{D}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{T_{D}m}}[s^{T_{\bar{C}}}]\big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[s^{T_{D}}\big]G(s)\leq\frac{s}{1-s\rho}G(s)V(x),

Integrating over the initial law of
x_{0}
finishes the proof of Lemma
7
.

F.4.
Proof of Lemma
4

1
-dependence follows from the strong Markov property at the atom
\bm{\alpha}
. Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the natural filtration of the split chain

\Phi=\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}
, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma\{(x_{k},y_{k}):0\leq k\leq n\}
. Fix
i\geq 1
and define the within-cycle block boundary times

B_{i,j}:=T_{i-1}+jm,\qquad j\geq 0,

so that the
i
th cycle consists of the blocks
j=0,1,\dots,L_{i}-1
and

\{t:\ T_{i-1}\leq t\leq T_{i}-1\}=\{B_{i,j}+s:\ 0\leq j\leq L_{i}-1,\ 0\leq s\leq m-1\}.

Set

Z_{i,j}:=x_{B_{i,j}},\qquad a_{i,j}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V(Z_{i,j})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\bigr],\qquad j\geq 0.

First note that
a_{i,0}=\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{i,0})]
since
L_{i}\geq 1
.
For
i=1
, we have
a_{1,0}=\mathbb{E}[V(x_{0})]<\infty
by Assumption
1
.
For
i\geq 2
, the split-chain construction in Section
5.1
yields

Z_{i,0}\sim\nu
, and hence

a_{i,0}=\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{i,0})]=\nu(V).

We next show that
\nu(V)<\infty
.
By the minorization (
7
), for every
x\in\bar{C}
,

P^{m}(x,\cdot)\ \geq\ \beta\nu(\cdot).

Integrating
V
against both sides gives

P^{m}V(x)=\int V(x^{\prime})P^{m}(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\ \geq\ \beta\int V(x^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=\beta\nu(V),

and therefore

(70)

\nu(V)\ \leq\ \frac{P^{m}V(x)}{\beta}\leq\frac{\lambda^{m}V(x)+\frac{L}{1-\lambda}}{\beta},\qquad\forall x\in\bar{C}.

It remains to justify that
\bar{C}
contains at least one point at which
V
is finite.
Suppose for contradiction that
V(x)=\infty
for all
x\in\bar{C}
.
Since
\pi(V)<\infty
by Lemma
1
, this would force
\pi(\bar{C})=0
.
Now use accessibility of
\bar{C}
: for each
n\geq 1
define

A_{n}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ P^{n}(x,\bar{C})>0\}.

Accessibility implies
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}A_{n}=\mathcal{X}
.
On the other hand, by invariance of
\pi
we have, for every
n\geq 1
,

\pi(\bar{C})=\pi P^{n}(\bar{C})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}P^{n}(x,\bar{C})\pi(\mathrm{d}x).

If
\pi(\bar{C})=0
, then the nonnegativity of
P^{n}(x,\bar{C})
implies

P^{n}(x,\bar{C})=0
for
\pi
-a.e.
x
, i.e.
\pi(A_{n})=0
for all
n\geq 1
.
Consequently,

1=\pi(\mathcal{X})=\pi\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}A_{n}\Big)\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\pi(A_{n})=0,

a contradiction. Therefore, there exists
x^{*}\in\bar{C}
such that
V(x^{*})<\infty
.
Plugging
x=x^{*}
into (
70
) yields

\nu(V)\ \leq\ \frac{\lambda^{m}V(x^{*})+\frac{L}{1-\lambda}}{\beta}\ <\ \infty.

In particular, we have the uniform bound

(71)

\sup_{i\geq 1}a_{i,0}<\infty.

Next, since
\{L_{i}>j+1\}\subseteq\{L_{i}>j\}
, we have

a_{i,j+1}=\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{i,j+1})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j+1\}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{i,j+1})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}].

Because
\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{B_{i,j}}
-measurable,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{i,j+1})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\mathbb{E}[V(x_{B_{i,j}+m})\mid\mathcal{F}_{B_{i,j}}]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}(\lambda^{m}V(Z_{i,j})+\frac{L}{1-\lambda})]\leq\lambda^{m}a_{i,j}+\frac{LP(L_{i}>j)}{1-\lambda}

By Lemma
1
,
\mathbb{P}(L_{i}>j)\leq b\rho^{j}
for all
i\geq 1
and
j\geq 0
.
Hence

(72)

a_{i,j+1}\leq\lambda^{m}a_{i,j}+\frac{Lb}{1-\lambda}\rho^{j},\qquad j\geq 0.

Let
\alpha:=\max\{\lambda^{m},\rho\}\in(0,1)
. Iterating (
72
) gives

(73)

a_{i,j}\leq(\lambda^{m})^{j}a_{i,0}+\frac{Lb}{1-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}(\lambda^{m})^{j-1-k}\rho^{k}\leq a_{i,0}\alpha^{j}+\frac{Lb}{1-\lambda}\cdot j\alpha^{j-1}\lesssim(\sqrt{\alpha})^{j},

where the last inequality follows from (
71
) together with the fact that
\lim_{j\to\infty}j(\sqrt{\alpha})^{j-2}=0.
Next, we extend (
73
) to within-block times.
Fix
s\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
and define

a_{i,j}^{(s)}:=\mathbb{E}\big[V(x_{B_{i,j}+s})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\big].

Similarly, we have

a_{i,j}^{(s)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\mathbb{E}\big[V(x_{B_{i,j}+s})\mid\mathcal{F}_{B_{i,j}}\big]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\big(\lambda^{s}V(Z_{i,j})+\frac{L}{1-\lambda}\big)\right]=\lambda^{s}a_{i,j}+\frac{Lb}{1-\lambda}\rho^{j}.

Combining with (
73
) yields,

(74)

a_{i,j}^{(s)}\lesssim\alpha^{j/2},\qquad\forall i\geq 1,j\geq 0,s\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}.

Write the cycle sum as block series:

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=T_{i-1}}^{T_{i}-1}g_{t+1}(x_{t+1})-Pg_{t+1}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}(g_{B_{i,j}+s+1}(x_{B_{i,j}+s+1})-Pg_{B_{i,j}+s+1}(x_{B_{i,j}+s}))\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}.

Then,

\|\widetilde{M}_{i}\|\lesssim\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}(V(x_{B_{i,j}+s})^{1/r}+V(x_{B_{i,j}+s+1})^{1/r})\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}.

If
r\geq 1
, Minkowski’s inequality applied to the nonnegative summands gives

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{M}_{i}\|^{r}\big)^{1/r}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(V(x_{B_{i,j}+s})+V(x_{B_{i,j}+s+1}))\mathds{1}_{\{L_{i}>j\}}\right]\right)^{1/r}.

applying (
74
) yields

\big(\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{M}_{i}\|^{r}\big)^{1/r}\lesssim\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\alpha^{j/2r}<\infty,

and the bound is uniform in
i
.
This proves
\sup_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{M}_{i}\|^{r}<\infty
. For the remainder, recall
R_{n}=\sum_{t=n}^{T_{K_{n}}-1}\xi_{t+1}
and note that, by definition of
K_{n}
,

T_{K_{n}-2}\leq n<T_{K_{n-1}}.

Hence
R_{n}
contains one complete
K_{n}
-th cycle cum and a
truncated
version of the
K_{n-1}
-th cycle sum. Therefore, repeating the same Minkowski argument as above
and using the uniform bound (
74
), we obtain

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|R_{n}\|^{r}<\infty.

Finally, applying Lemma
5
with
p=2
to the martingale
\{M_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

stopped at
t\wedge T_{i}
, and using the geometric tail of the cycle lengths from
Lemma
1
, we obtain the uniform
L^{2}
bound

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\big[\|M_{t\wedge T_{i}}\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}(x_{k})-Pg_{k}(x_{k-1})\|^{2}\mathds{1}\{T_{i}\geq k\}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k+1}(x_{k+1})-Pg_{k+1}(x_{k})\|^{r}]^{\frac{2}{r}}\mathbb{P}(T_{i}\geq k)^{\frac{r-2}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(T_{i}\geq k)^{\frac{r-2}{2}}<\infty.

Therefore the uniform integrability condition (
59

Lemma
3
holds, and we conclude that

(75)

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i-1}]=0\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}_{i}]=0,\quad\forall i\geq 1.

This completes the proof of Lemma

.
