Macrolide-resistant Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease: A narrative review
Authors: Yoonis Cumar, Lina Davies Forsman, Thomas Schön, Christian Wejse, Victor Naestholt Dahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) is on the rise across the world, where only about two-thirds are cured by current treatment strategies. Treatment of MAC-PD includes macrolides as the key drug in a three-drug regimen, making the emergence of macrolide resistance particularly concerning, as treatment success is reduced to about 21%. Macrolide-resistant (MR) MAC is typically caused by a mutation in the 23S rRNA gene and usually develops after inappropriate treatment, such as macrolide monotherapy or omission of ethambutol from the regimen. However, treatment options for MR-MAC-PD are highly limited. The combination of lung resection surgery with or without amikacin, along with continued ethambutol use, has been associated with improved outcomes relative to regimens without these. Although limited, clinical observational data suggest that isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides should not be used for MR-MAC-PD. Repurposing and development of novel drugs may hold promise for improving the treatment of MAC disease in the future. For instance, clofazimine and minocycline have both been proposed as a replacement for rifampicin. Bedaquiline has also been suggested as rescue therapy. This narrative clinical review describes the prevalence of MR-MAC-PD, resistance mechanisms, clinical management strategies, and provides a brief overview of the drug pipeline for MAC-PD, aiming to inform clinical practice and guide evidence-based management.

Introduction

The global incidence and prevalence of pulmonary disease caused by nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are steadily increasing [1], [2], [3]. NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is predominantly caused by Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), including M. avium, M. intracellulare, M. chimaera, among others [4], [5]. MAC pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) is typically observed in patients with underlying structural lung disease, such as cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [6], [7], [8], [9], whereas MAC infections in patients with immunosuppression are mainly extrapulmonary and disseminated disease [10]. MAC-PD is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality [11]. Moreover, the long treatment duration, lasting at least one year, is challenged by frequent adverse drug effects with an overall incidence of 57% in one meta-analysis [12]. Clinical guidelines for treating MAC-PD were updated in 2020 by the American Thoracic Society (ATS), the European Respiratory Society (ERS), the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [13]. A three-drug regimen, including a macrolide as the key drug, ethambutol, and a rifamycin, is recommended for patients with macrolide-susceptible strains [13], [14]. Azithromycin is preferred over clarithromycin due to a lower pill burden, better tolerance, fewer drug-drug interactions, and equal efficacy in terms of culture conversion [15], [16], [17]. However, clarithromycin is an adequate alternative if azithromycin is unavailable or not tolerated [13]. Treatment is recommended for at least 12 months after culture conversion [13]. For patients with advanced/severe bronchiectatic, cavitary, or macrolide-resistant MAC-PD (MR-MAC-PD), the guidelines recommend including intravenous amikacin or streptomycin in the initial regimen despite poor tolerability and impracticality [13]. MAC-PD is associated with poor outcomes, with macrolide-susceptible MAC-PD having a pooled five-year mortality of 25% and a treatment success rate between 61.4% and 65.7% in a systematic review of 17 data sets from 9035 patients [11], [18], [19]. In contrast, the five-year mortality associated with MR-MAC-PD ranges from 29% to 47%, with a treatment success rate of only 21% [20], [21], [22]. Still, recommendations on treating MR-MAC-PD are essentially nonexistent in current guidelines.

This narrative review describes the prevalence of MR-MAC-PD, resistance mechanisms, clinical management strategies, and provides a brief overview of the drug pipeline for MAC-PD, aiming to inform clinical practice.

Prevalence of MAC and MR-MAC-PD

Understanding the epidemiology of MAC-PD is challenging because reporting of positive cultures and disease is neither mandatory nor nationwide in most countries. Moreover, it may be difficult to separate environmental contamination and asymptomatic colonization or infection from symptomatic disease [10]. Recent genotyping studies have also demonstrated that reinfection is common and that patients may harbor different MAC subspecies, complicating clinical understanding and management [4], [23], [24]. Molecular epidemiological studies have shown that up to 74% of recurrent cases may be attributed to reinfection [25]. Although such data are scarce for MR MAC, genomic typing can help distinguish relapse from reinfection, thereby improving the interpretation of treatment outcomes. Reinfection after successful treatment likely reflects repeated exposure of susceptible individuals to environmental MAC reservoirs, particularly household water and soil [26]. Measures to reduce exposure, such as limiting aerosolized water exposure, reducing water stagnation, and avoiding inhalation of soil or potting-soil dust, have been proposed [26]; however, most have not been rigorously evaluated, and there is currently no evidence that these interventions reduce the risk of MAC-PD reinfection.

Available population-based and laboratory surveillance studies from North America, Europe, and Asia consistently indicate an increasing burden of MAC-PD [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. A U.S. study (2005–2006) of all Oregon residents found a 2-year prevalence of 8.6 NTM-PD cases per 100,000 persons [27], with MAC as the etiology in 87.5% of cases. A laboratory surveillance study from Ontario, Canada, reported annual MAC-PD isolation prevalence of 5.6 per 100,000 population in 1997 and 8.4 per 100,000 population in 2003 [28], indicating a concerning increase in MAC-PD prevalence. A recent study from Denmark, spanning three decades, observed that MAC accounted for 62% of all incident NTM isolates, with the incidence of definite and possible MAC-PD increasing by an average of 4.6% annually [29]. A similar increase in the incidence of MAC-PD has been observed in East Asia [30], [31]. A study from Seoul, South Korea reported that the annual incidence increased from 1.2 NTM-PD cases per 100,000 in 2006 to 4.8 NTM-PD cases per 100,000 in 2016. MAC was the most frequently isolated NTM species in the study (63.6%) [30]. A laboratory surveillance study from Japan reported the annual incidence increased from 15.8 NTM-PD cases per 100,000 in 2013 to 19.2 NTM-PD cases per 100,000 in 2017. MAC was identified as the etiological agent in 93% of NTM-PD cases [31]. However, to our knowledge, no nationwide population-based studies have specifically explored the annual prevalence or incidence of MR-MAC-PD.

Macrolide resistance mechanisms

Macrolide resistance in MAC is commonly caused by genomic mutations in the 23S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene (also known as the rrl gene), which encodes the macrolide ribosomal binding site [32], [33]. The first step in the development of MR-MAC is believed to be the induction of efflux pumps, which leads to high levels of resistance [34]. A study demonstrated a 56-fold increase in efflux pump gene expression in M. avium after only three days of exposure to subtherapeutic levels of azithromycin [34]. The genes involved in this mechanism are MAV_3306 and MAV_1406, which encode an ATP-binding cassette transporter and a major facilitator superfamily efflux pump, respectively [34]. A study examining the role of efflux activity in MR-MAC [32] included four MAC strains with clarithromycin and erythromycin resistance and showed that the efflux pump inhibitors verapamil, thioridazine, and chlorpromazine reduced the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) in all four isolates. However, efflux pump inhibitors did not restore macrolide susceptibility [32]. These findings suggest that efflux pump activity may contribute to reduced macrolide susceptibility in MAC, but may alone be insufficient to result in a resistant phenotype [32]. Moreover, the precise genetic correlates of MR in MAC are yet to be determined.

The best-characterized mechanism of acquired (mutational) resistance is a point mutation at positions 2058 or 2059 of the 23S rRNA gene, which alters the macrolide-binding site, preventing the antibiotic from blocking the polypeptide exit tunnel of the 50S ribosomal subunit [35]. A nucleotide substitution from adenine to guanine is the most common point mutation, followed by adenine to cytosine and adenine to thymine [21]. Using 23S rRNA sequencing, such mutations at positions 2058 or 2059 have been identified in 92–100% of MR-MAC isolates detected by phenotypic AST [21], [35], [36]. In the studies, 0–8% of MR-MAC isolates lacked a point mutation at positions 2058 or 2059; however, the mechanism underlying this was not explored [21], [35], [36]. These mutations are also targeted in routine clinical practice by the widely used line probe assay GenoType NTM-DR VER 1.0 (HAIN Lifescience) [37]. Several studies have reported successful application of this assay for detecting point mutations at positions 2058/2059 [38], [39]. In another study of 12 MR-MAC isolates, 66.7% harbored a point mutation at one of these positions [40]. Point mutations from adenine to guanine (n = 5 isolates) or adenine to cytosine (n = 2) were associated with a higher level of clarithromycin resistance (MICs of 100–250 mg/L), compared with adenine-to-thymine mutations (n = 2) or no detectable mutations in rrl (n = 6), which were associated with clarithromycin resistance at a lower level (MICs of 25–99 mg/L) [41]. In contrast, a study involving 28 patients found no significant difference in mutation type according to treatment outcome [21].

In addition to mechanisms associated with increased MICs in vitro, biofilm formation may contribute to macrolide tolerance. M. avium growing in biofilms has demonstrated reduced susceptibility to macrolides compared with planktonic bacteria, while bacteria recovered from biofilms may retain conventional macrolide susceptibility, suggesting that this represents a reversible tolerance rather than stable acquired resistance [42], [43]. Persister-like variants may also contribute to macrolide tolerance. Clarithromycin has been shown to induce a viable but nonculturable state in M. avium, potentially allowing bacterial survival during antimicrobial exposure and thereby contributing to persistence, treatment failure, or recurrence without necessarily representing stable antimicrobial resistance [44].

MAC isolation and macrolide susceptibility testing

Macrolides are the most important component of the MAC treatment regimen [13], [45]. MAC isolation by culture is required before phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) can be performed [13]. Following decontamination, respiratory samples are cultured on both liquid and solid media to optimize MAC recovery, after which species identification can be performed using molecular or mass spectrometry-based methods [13]. A meta-analysis of 9 studies reported isolation sensitivities of 80% with BACTEC MGIT 960 and 57% with Löwenstein-Jensen media, increasing to 86% combined [46]. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) has suggested clarithromycin breakpoints for MAC: susceptible (MIC ≤8 mg/L) and resistant (MIC ≥32 mg/L) by broth microdilution (BMD) in Müller-Hinton media enriched with OADC [47]. For macrolide AST, clarithromycin is used as a representative of the drug class; however, the CLSI breakpoints lack supporting data, including wild-type MIC distributions, PK/PD, and clinical outcome data [48]. No breakpoints exist for azithromycin, and thus it is a poor representative of macrolide AST. The epidemiological cut-off value for M. avium is 16 mg/L (range 0.06–16 mg/L) and for M. intracellulare it is 8 mg/L (range 0.06–8 mg/L) by BMD [48]. If an initial test indicates intermediate susceptibility to clarithromycin by the CLSI definition, repeat testing is recommended to confirm the result, and the patient should be closely monitored for the potential emergence of macrolide resistance [45], [49]. This also illustrates that poorly standardized AST methods and the absence of clearly defined clinical breakpoints complicate the definition of macrolide resistance in MAC, as well as the choice of an appropriate gold standard against which new genotypic resistance mutations should be validated. A similar challenge is well recognized in M. tuberculosis [50]. Genotypic resistance testing using molecular methods such as line probe assays, targeted sequencing, or whole-genome sequencing may provide complementary information by detecting established macrolide-associated rrl mutations at positions 2058/2059 and amikacin-associated rrs mutations at position 1408, as described above.

For patients receiving the standard MAC-PD three-drug therapy regimen, macrolide resistance has been reported in 9.2% (n = 22/238) [51] to 12% (n = 12/100) [52] of cases. Both were retrospective studies: one included 238 MAC-PD patients without prior targeted antibiotic therapy who received the standard three-drug regimen for at least 6 months [51], while the other included 100 patients without prior treatment for >1 year [52]. Risk factors for MR-MAC

The emergence of MR-MAC-PD is strongly associated with prior MAC-PD treatment (Table 2), particularly when treatment was based on macrolide monotherapy or regimens without ethambutol [20], [35]. A study found that 76% (n = 39/51) of MR-MAC-PD patients had a history of MAC-PD treatment with macrolide monotherapy or a macrolide combined with a fluoroquinolone only [35]. In comparison, only 18% (n = 9/51) of MR-MAC-PD cases were treated with the standard three-drug treatment before the emergence of resistance [35]. Another study (n = 53/88) reported a similarly high proportion (60.2%) of patients with MR-MAC-PD who had a history of receiving macrolide monotherapy, macrolide plus a fluoroquinolone, or regimens without ethambutol [20]. Likewise, few (13%, n = 12/90) MR-MAC-PD cases had received previous treatment with the standard three-drug regimen. A comparable study from the Netherlands observed that a high proportion (50%, n = 8/16) of MR-MAC-PD patients had been treated with macrolide monotherapy [62]. Two other studies reported a smaller proportion of cases that did not follow the standard three-drug treatment before resistance was detected (35%, n = 12/34 Moon et al. [21]; 30%, n = 10/33 Kadota et al. [63]). Moon et al., however, did not include patients who were treated with macrolide monotherapy before detection of MR-MAC-PD, potentially contributing to their findings [21]. Regimens without ethambutol appear to be a major reason for the development of MR-MAC isolates, as demonstrated by the consistently higher rates of MR-MAC-PD among individuals treated with macrolide monotherapy, macrolide plus fluoroquinolone, and regimens without ethambutol [20], [21], [35], [62], [63]. Use of these inadequate regimens resulted in macrolide-resistant cases ranging from 30% to 76%, whereas only 13–18% of resistant cases were treated with the standard three-drug regimen [20], [21], [35], [62], [63]. Among the included studies that used the standard three-drug treatment before the development of MR-MAC, one explicitly reported using both daily and three-times-weekly regimens [35], while the other studies did not clarify treatment frequency [20], [21], [63]. Two studies found no significant increase in MR-MAC when nodular /bronchiectatic disease was treated with the standard three-drug regimen administered three times weekly compared to daily administration [64], [65]. Long-term macrolide monotherapy is often used in patients with bronchiectasis or cystic fibrosis for its anti-inflammatory effects. This practice warrants pre-treatment NTM screening and raises concerns about the development of macrolide resistance [62]. Interestingly, a study of 91 patients with bronchiectasis receiving long-term macrolide therapy found a low incidence of NTM culture positivity (3.3%) compared with 319 patients not receiving macrolides (13.8%), suggesting a potential protective role for prophylactic therapy against NTM in patients at high risk of NTM-PD [66]. Still, clinicians should consider the risk of acquiring resistance by selection with macrolide monotherapy as prophylactic treatment among patients who develop subsequent MAC-PD [20], [35], [62].

The role of rifampicin in reducing the risk of macrolide resistance in MAC-PD is unclear. Two clinical studies did not demonstrate an increased risk of macrolide resistance with a macrolide-ethambutol regimen compared with a macrolide-ethambutol-rifampicin regimen and suggested that the two-drug regimen can be as effective as the standard three-drug regimen [14], [67]. A randomized trial of rifampicin/ethambutol/azithromycin versus ethambutol/azithromycin in the treatment of MAC-PD is ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03672630), which may help clarify if rifampicin contributes to treatment efficacy and risk reduction of emerging macrolide resistance.

Guideline recommendations on MR-MAC-PD management and expert considerations

A multidrug regimen is recommended for the treatment of MR-MAC-PD [1], [13]; however, the current guidance remains ambiguous and rather vague, highlighting the need for further research and expert guidance. The 2017 guidelines from the British Thoracic Society (BTS) provide recommendations for the treatment of MR-MAC-PD [1]. This guideline recommends treating MR-MAC-PD using a combination of ethambutol, rifampicin, isoniazid, or fluoroquinolones, and considering an injectable aminoglycoside (amikacin or streptomycin) [1]. Yet, both isoniazid and fluoroquinolones exhibit little to no activity against MAC [68], and the efficacy of rifampicin is controversial [69]. For MR-MAC-PD, the ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA guidelines from 2020 recommend incorporating intravenous amikacin or streptomycin into the initial treatment regimen and consultation with experts [13]. Both the BTS and the ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA recommend that selected MR-MAC-PD patients may undergo lung resection if the benefits of the surgery outweigh the risk [1], [13]. To our knowledge, no studies have addressed the management of MR-MAC-PD patients who are refractory to guideline-based regimens [70]. A recent narrative review by experts suggests formulating and guiding treatment by AST and expert consultation [71], [72]. Specifically, the authors recommend: 1) stopping the macrolide, 2) continuing ethambutol, 3) changing rifampicin to rifabutin (based on a theoretically slightly higher in vitro activity), 4) add intravenous amikacin, transition to amikacin liposome inhalation suspension (ALIS) if intravenous amikacin is not tolerable 5) add another drug(s), 6) avoid the addition of isoniazid or a fluoroquinolone (more risk than benefit and worse outcomes), 7) consider surgery, and 8) consult an expert. Still, some of these recommendations are based on conflicting evidence, such as stopping macrolides [63], [73] and using rifamycins [14], [63].

Optimal management of MR-MAC-PD requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that extends beyond antimicrobial therapy [74]. Treatment should address, but not be limited to, underlying conditions, optimization of nutritional status, and, in patients with chronic lung disease, implementation of individualized interventions, including airway clearance strategies. Treatment and outcomes of MR-MAC-PD

Despite advances in treatment, the management of MAC-PD remains suboptimal, particularly for MR-MAC-PD, which is characterized by limited treatment options and poor clinical outcomes (Table 3). A systematic review of treatment outcomes for patients with MR-MAC-PD, including 9 studies, found that reported sputum culture conversion (SCC) rates ranged from 11% to 38%, with an overall estimate of 21% [22]. The treatment modalities used in the studies were macrolides, rifamycins, ethambutol, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, ALIS, and surgery [22]. Four of the studies reported one-year mortality rates, varying from 0% to 25%, with a pooled estimate of 10% [22]. Five-year all-cause mortality rates of MR-MAC-PD have been reported by Moon et al. [21] and Morimoto et al. [20] as 47% and 29%, respectively. For comparison, a pooled estimate of the 5-year all-cause mortality rate of MAC-PD patients with macrolide-susceptible isolates was reported as 27% [11]. Evidence regarding continuation of macrolides in MR-MAC-PD is conflicting [63], [73]. Kadota et al. compared 26 patients treated for MR-MAC-PD with a macrolide-based regimen to seven patients treated with a non-macrolide regimen [63]. The patients in both groups received different combination regimens, which included rifamycins, ethambutol, fluoroquinolones, isoniazid, or kanamycin. The study reported that 39% of patients (n = 10) on a macrolide-based regimen (including 2–4 drugs) achieved SCC after a year of treatment, compared to 29% (n = 2) on a non-macrolide regimen. Fukushima et al. [73], not included in the review [22], also compared outcomes for MR-MAC-PD patients treated with a macrolide-based regimen (n = 43) versus a non-macrolide-based regimen (n = 25). Macrolide-susceptible MAC strains re-emerged in one patient (2%) in the macrolide-based regimen group, compared with 13 patients (52%) in the non-macrolide-based regimen group [73]. These findings appear to be conflicting, as they measured different endpoints, hindering comparability [63], [73]. Kadota et al. measured SCC after a year, whereas Fukushima et al. measured reemergence of macrolide-susceptible MAC strains, which does not necessarily correspond to clinical improvement [63], [73]. Neither study controlled for disease phenotype, such as nodular/bronchiectatic vs. cavitary, which is expected to substantially affect bacterial burden and drug penetration [63], [73], [76]. The study did not stratify results by MAC subspecies, which could potentially also influence treatment response [24], [63], [73]. Lastly, background regimen compositions varied considerably within and between the two studies, limiting attribution of outcomes to macrolide continuation alone [63], [73]. These differences highlight the need for prospective studies with comparable and clinically meaningful outcome definitions.

The high prevalence of distinct genotypes in MAC-PD, potentially reflecting reinfection rather than relapse, should be considered when determining and evaluating the treatment regimen [77]. Fukushima et al. found that 7 of 60 (23.3%) isolates from 30 MR-MAC-PD patients exhibited re-emergence of macrolide susceptibility, all of whom had received a non-macrolide-based treatment regimen [73]. Among the seven isolates, two had variable number tandem repeat changing genotypes, suggesting reinfection with a macrolide-susceptible strain. Based on the methodology and sample size used by Fukushima et al., a definitive conclusion could not be drawn [73].

Ethambutol, which is part of the recommended three-drug treatment of MAC-PD, may play a role in MR-MAC-PD treatment, with one study reporting an association between ethambutol use and SCC [13]. The study included 37 patients with MR-MAC-PD, of whom 23 patients received a treatment regimen that included ethambutol. SCC was achieved in 11 patients, 10 of whom (91%) received ethambutol. The overall conversion rate was 29.7% [78], consistent with previous studies [22]. While these findings suggest a potential benefit of ethambutol, further investigation is needed to clarify whether it contributes to treatment efficacy beyond its apparent protective role against macrolide resistance [35].

Intravenous amikacin has also been associated with improved treatment outcomes in MR-MAC-PD when combined with lung resection [20], [35]. Still, the use of amikacin is complicated by a considerable risk of adverse events (ototoxicity and/or nephrotoxicity) and requires intravenous access, making it unsuitable for long-term use [79].

Evidence for ALIS in MR-MAC-PD is limited. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated a higher SCC in MR-MAC-PD patients who received the standard three-drug regimen plus ALIS, compared with those receiving the standard three-drug regimen alone [80]. The study included 73 MR-MAC-PD patients: 51 received ALIS in addition to a standard three-drug regimen, and 22 received a standard three-drug regimen alone. SCC was achieved in 7 of 51 (14%) MR-MAC-PD patients who received ALIS in addition to standard treatment, compared with 1 of 22 (5%) MR-MAC-PD patients who received standard treatment alone. Adverse events were rare and generally comparable between the ALIS plus standard therapy and standard therapy groups, except for tinnitus [80]. In contrast, a case series reported limited effectiveness of ALIS-containing regimens in the treatment of MR-MAC-PD [81]. The study included three MR-MAC-PD patients, who received ALIS and a combination of azithromycin, ethambutol, rifabutin, moxifloxacin, clofazimine, and prothionamide. None of the patients achieved SCC, and two developed amikacin resistance [81]. Although these findings appear inconsistent with the randomized controlled trial, substantial differences in study design, sample size, and background treatment limit direct comparison and attribution of outcomes to ALIS [80], [81]. Thus, further investigation is warranted to fully define ALIS's place in MR-MAC-PD therapy. Off-label inhalation of amikacin intravenous suspension may be an alternative, although no evidence is available to support this.

Four smaller observational studies reported on surgery for MR-MAC-PD as a treatment modality. Griffith et al. divided 51 patients into four groups with different treatment regimens, with the highest culture conversion rate observed in those receiving a combination of surgery, prolonged aminoglycoside therapy, and ethambutol and rifabutin [35]. Similar results were found in a study conducted in Canada, although based on a very small sample size, which compared MR-MAC-PD treatment with surgery (n = 4) and without surgery (n = 4) [82]. They observed a higher culture conversion rate in the surgery group compared with the non-surgery group [82]. Moon et al. [21] and Morimoto et al. [20] found that surgery combined with intravenous aminoglycosides was the only treatment approach associated with significantly favorable outcomes in MR-MAC-PD patients. Collectively, the data indicate that surgical resection, particularly when combined with intravenous aminoglycoside therapy, serves as an effective treatment strategy for MR-MAC-PD in carefully selected patients. However, larger prospective studies are necessary to validate its efficacy and safety.

The role of oxazolidinones in the treatment of MAC-PD remains uncertain, and linezolid appears to have limited utility because its relatively high MICs against MAC may necessitate high doses, increasing the risk of adverse effects and treatment discontinuation [83], [84]. Tedizolid has lower MICs and potentially more favorable pharmacokinetic and tolerability properties, although clinical evidence supporting its use in MAC-PD remains limited [85].

In addition to novel drugs, some established drugs are being repurposed for MAC-PD, potentially broadening available treatment options [71]. In a mouse model, a combination regimen of clarithromycin, clofazimine, and bedaquiline has also shown a 98% reduction in bacterial load after just one week of treatment (n = 5) [92]. Notably, all three drugs can cause prolonged QT interval [93], [94], [95]. Bedaquiline alone has demonstrated potent in vitro activity against MAC isolates (MIC90 = 0.015 mg/L), although a hollow-fiber study found no additional benefit and no prevention of resistance when combined with azithromycin, ethambutol, and clofazimine [96], [97]. In a small observational study, patients with MR-MAC-PD (n = 4) were treated with bedaquiline and a combination of ethambutol, rifabutin, and aminoglycosides for at least 24 weeks. No patients attained sustained culture negativity [98]. Omadacycline, a tetracycline derivative, has shown greater efficacy than current first-line drugs, such as ethambutol and rifabutin, in hollow-fiber model studies [99]. Mavintramycin A, a compound derived from Streptomyces sp., has demonstrated bactericidal activity against MAC, including MR-MAC and multidrug-resistant isolates, with additional in vivo activity observed in both mice and silkworm models [100]. Epetraborole, an orally available boron-containing antibiotic targeting leucyl-tRNA synthetase, has shown efficacy equal to or greater than that of the standard three-drug therapy in a chronic mouse lung infection model [101]. Another novel agent is SPR720, a prodrug converted in vivo to SPR719, which targets DNA gyrase B. In murine models infected with clarithromycin-resistant MAC, SPR720 demonstrated activity with a MIC90 of 2 mg/L and was generally well tolerated in humans at doses up to 1000 mg/day [102], [103]. Preclinical studies have yielded encouraging results for novel compounds and repurposed drugs [92], [99], [100], but only a few have progressed to clinical trials, with mixed outcomes [91], [98].

Conclusion

MR-MAC-PD is a significant clinical challenge due to very limited treatment options, a poor prognosis, and a lack of clear guidelines. The development of MR-MAC is largely due to deviations from guideline-based treatment, including macrolide monotherapy, macrolide-fluoroquinolone combination therapy, and omission of ethambutol, highlighting the importance of adherence to expert and guideline recommendations. Despite the existence of clinical guidelines, a comprehensive recommendation for MR-MAC-PD treatment is not available; we have therefore summarized the limited knowledge. Lung resection surgery, with or without amikacin, ALIS, and continued ethambutol, has been associated with improved outcomes. Moreover, evidence suggests that abstaining from using isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides may be beneficial. Clofazimine, minocycline, and bedaquiline may be included in a treatment regimen, although more evidence is still needed, especially for bedaquiline, while rifampicin use is highly controversial. Improving understanding of resistance mechanisms, developing new therapeutic strategies, and enhancing treatment protocols are needed to optimize patient outcomes.
