Effects of additional weight-bearing on the in vivo kinematics of the human ankle joint complex during walking
Authors: Shengli Wang, Zhihui Qian, Xiangyu Liu, Guangsheng Song, Zhende Jiang, Kunyang Wang, Jianan Wu, Jing Liu, Lei Ren, Luquan Ren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies focusing on the kinematics of the ankle joint complex (AJC) have long been a key area of interest for biomechanists and orthopedic surgeons. However, it is not clear how additional weight-bearing walking affects the motion of the AJC compared to walking with a normal body weight (BW) or what adjustments the AJC would instinctively make to accommodate the additional load. To address this gap in knowledge, advanced dynamic biplane radiography combined with a model-based 2D-3D tracking technique was employed to elucidate the inherent kinematics of the AJC during the stance phase while walking with and without additional weight-bearing. It was found that walking with additional 50% body weight (BW + 50%) resulted in a greater dispersion of instantaneous axes of rotation in the talocrural and subtalar joints during the stance phase of gait. The talocrural joint is more plantarflexed and anteriorly translated during the early and late stance phases than during the midstance phases, which suggests that additional weight-bearing affects the stability of the AJC. Moreover, walking with BW + 50% showed that the center of rotation of the talocrural joint was positioned more superiorly and posteriorly during the foot flat to heel-off phase. Introduction

The biomechanics of human locomotion have been the subject of extensive research due to their fundamental importance in understanding human movement and their implications for various fields, such as sports science, rehabilitation, and orthopedics. Walking, as one of the most common forms of human locomotion, involves intricate coordination of multiple joints and muscles. In particular, walking with additional weight-bearing, including activities such as carrying loads for labor and training for athletes and soldiers, poses more challenges for the human musculoskeletal system. As the largest weight-bearing part of the human body during bipedal locomotion1–4, the ankle joint complex (AJC) which comprises the talocrural joint and the subtalar joint, plays a crucial role in providing stability and propulsion during the stance phase of walking1,5,6. In bipedal locomotion, the AJC serves as the first part of the body to contact the ground. It directly absorbs the initial impact force at heel strike and is responsible for subsequently transferring the load to the leg. This role is particularly prominent during weight-bearing activities, when the AJC should provide both stability and flexibility to accommodate varying loading conditions. Increased loads during activities such as weight-bearing walking exacerbate the demands placed on the AJC, making it a key research point for understanding biomechanical responses. The talocrural and subtalar joints specialize in subtle adjustments to ensure smooth transitions between gait phases, especially during the stance phase when stability and propulsion are critical. Given the complexity and variability of these adjustments under different loading conditions, studying the spatial movements of the AJC will provide unique insights. Additionally, due to the role of the AJC as a primary weight-bearing joint, it is more susceptible to injury and degenerative disease. Therefore, understanding its kinematic characteristics under additional loading is not only critical for optimizing athletic performance, but also important for developing injury prevention and rehabilitation strategies.

Most of the existing studies investigating the in vivo kinematics of AJC have used optical motion capture and multisegmental foot models7–10. However, these in vivo kinematic studies typically involve activities that uses the body’s own weight as the load. To our knowledge, there are few studies on the effects of additional weight-bearing on the motion of in vivo AJC. A typical study utilizing optical motion capture and a multisegment foot model examined the kinematic changes in foot segments under additional load, revealing distinct features of increased foot energy11. Furthermore, currently, the accuracy of this method used to study AJC motion is limited due to the effect of soft tissue artifacts and the lack of external bony landmarks in the talus12,13. Invasive bone pins are used as a method with credible accuracy for studying the in vivo kinematics of AJC14–16, but due to their more invasive nature, they are difficult to use, especially under conditions of additional weight-bearing. Dynamic biplane radiography technology has emerged as a powerful tool for assessing in vivo joint kinematics with high accuracy and precision, and the model-based 2D-3D registration technique analyzes joint kinematics, with errors considered at the submillimeter and subdegree levels17,18. Compared to traditional optical motion capture and invasive bone pin methods, it offers the advantages of high precision, in vivo measurement, and noninvasiveness. Some studies have used this method to investigate the in vivo 3D motion of the AJC18–21; however, the precise in vivo 3D motion of the AJC and the regulation of the ankle musculoskeletal system during additional weight-bearing walking remain unclear.

In this study, we aimed to make a detailed kinematic comparison of the AJC during walking with a normal body weight (BW) and walking with an additional 50% body weight (BW + 50%) using biplane radiography. Based on our preliminary experiments and previous literature, low weight-bearing conditions have little effect on the kinematics of the AJC, as seen in the studies by Papachatzis et al. and Kern et al., which reached 30% additional weight-bearing11,22. According to the feedback from most subjects, BW + 50% approaches the upper limit of tolerance for most individuals. Therefore, the relatively high level of BW + 50% was determined in this study. The spatial distribution of the joint rotation axes and centers of rotation, as well as the continuous motion curves of the AJC in six degrees of freedom (DOFs) during the stance phase, were analyzed. Combining the structural aspects of the joint with its kinematic characteristics, we assessed the impact of additional weight-bearing on the stability of the AJC. Furthermore, we examined the coupling relationships between joint rotations or translations across the six DOFs and the flexion angle (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion) of the talocrural joint. Additionally, the plantarflexors are crucial for generating the force required to push off the ground during the later stages of the stance phase. When the body is subjected to increased loads during walking, the demand on the plantarflexors increases, which may lead to adjustments in their activation patterns and force generation. This, in turn, could affect the overall stability and function of the AJC. Therefore, the impact of additional weight-bearing on the activation and force adjustment behaviors of the plantarflexors was also speculated.

Methods

Participants and ethics

Ten healthy adult males (aged 27.5 ± 2.2 years; height 174.8 ± 5.1 cm; weight 76.1 ± 6.1 kg), all without foot- or ankle-related disorders and with normal foot arches (neither flatfoot nor high-arched), volunteered to participate in this study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jilin University (No. 2020085), and all the subjects provided informed consent before the experiment. We confirmed that all experiments and methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Acquisition of Biplanar radiograph sequences

Prior to the acquisition of the dynamic biplanar radiograph sequences of the foot, static radiographs of porous meshes of known dimensions and Lego cubes (with built-in metal markers) were first acquired for undistorted radiograph sequence and 3D spatial calibration, respectively. The participants walked on a 5.6-meter-long walkway at their self-selected natural pace. Each participant needed to complete two walking tests, namely, walking with BW and walking with BW + 50%. The laboratory is equipped with a high-speed camera (FDR-AX700, Sony, Japan) to capture gait, and the calculated walking speeds fell within a small range (1.2 m/s to 1.6 m/s). The walking with BW + 50% was conducted with subjects wearing weighted vests (Decathlon, France) featuring symmetrical distributions both anterior-posteriorly and medial-laterally to prevent the asymmetric distributions of weight from affecting gait. The weight of the vest was adjusted using small steel plates (0.1 kg each) to ensure that the weight is balanced both front-to-back and side-to-side. Biplane radiographic sequences of the right foot from heel strike to toe-off were acquired by a dynamic biplane radiography system (Imaging Systems & Service, Inc., USA). The operating conditions of the X-ray tubes were set to 58 kV and 80 mA. The acquisition frequency of the system was 100 Hz, and the images were 1024 × 1024 pixels. A force plate (BMS400600, AMTI, USA) on the footpath simultaneously captured ground reaction forces (1000 Hz) and was used to determine heel strike and toe-off events using a vertical reaction force threshold of 30 N. Each subject completed 8 replicate trials. The process of image acquisition and data processing is illustrated in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Video S1. To measure muscle activation in the gastrocnemius muscle and soleus muscle during walking, surface electromyography (EMG) signals were recorded at a frequency of 2000 Hz using a wireless biological signal acquisition system (DELSYS Inc., USA). The electrodes for the medial and lateral gastrocnemius muscles were placed on the muscle bellies on the posteromedial and posterolateral aspects of the lower leg, approximately one-third of the way down from the popliteal fossa. The electrode for the soleus muscle was placed on the medial aspect of the lower leg, about two-thirds of the way down toward the connection point of the soleus and the calcaneus.

Flowchart of image acquisition and data processing. The whole process mainly includes biplanar radiograph sequence acquisition, CT image acquisition, 3D modeling, model-based 2D-3D tracking and kinematic computation.

CT scans and the creation of a local coordinate system (LCS) for bones

The subjects were in a supine position, and images of the right foot from the middle leg to the sole of the foot were obtained using a computed tomography (CT) scanner (Brilliance iCT 256, Philips, Netherlands). The main CT scanning parameters were as follows: thickness, 0.8 mm; image matrix, 512 × 512 pixels; and average resolution, 0.5625 mm × 0.5625 mm × 0.4 mm. The medical image processing software OsiriX (Pixmeo, Geneva, Switzerland) was used to perform segmentation operations on the CT images and to create polygonal mesh models of the tibia, talus, and calcaneus as well as 3D volumes of the regions of interest23–25. The LCS was established based on a polygonal mesh model of each bone using a guideline combining skeletal geometry and anatomical landmarks according to a previously published method (Supplementary Fig. S1)2,26–30. The origin of the LCS of the tibia was located at the center of the distal tibial plateau and is determined by the intersection of the centerline of the cylinder fitted to the tibial body and the plane fitted to the distal tibial plateau. The origin of the LCS of the talus is located at the center of the talus body-fitting sphere. The origin of the LCS of the calcaneus is located at the midpoint of the line connecting the most lateral point of the posterior articular surface with the most medial point of the middle articular surface. For each bone LCS, the medial-lateral axis was defined as the X-axis, the posterior-anterior axis was defined as the Y-axis, and the inferior-superior axis was defined as the Z-axis.

Image processing

The image sequence of the foot was undistorted using XMALab (XMALab 1.5.1, Brown University, USA) software, and 3D spatial calibration was conducted using static radiographs of porous meshes and Lego cubes30–32. The position and pose of each bone at each frame were determined by a model-based tracking method. Model-based tracking of the target joints was performed using Autoscoper software (Autoscoper V2, Brown University, USA)2,33–36. Digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) were generated based on the 3D volumes segmented from the CT images, the initial frame registration was performed manually, the DRRs were aligned with two different orientations of the X-ray images, and then automatic tracking was performed for the entire radiograph sequence. Model-based 2D-3D semiautomatic registration was completed for each bone, and finally, the spatial pose matrix of each frame corresponding to each bone was output. The overall root-mean square error of the model-based tracking method averaged 0.59 ± 0.10 mm and 0.71 ± 0.12° for dynamic trials36–38. The dynamic process of model-based tracking is shown in supplementary Video S1.

Data analysis

The talocrural joint axis and the subtalar joint axis were represented by the geometric surface fitting method (Fig. 2a and d)20,39–43. The talocrural joint axis passes through the fitted spherical centers of the medial and lateral trochlear facets of the talus, and the subtalar joint axis passes through the fitted spherical centers of the medial and posterior facets of the calcaneus (Supplementary Fig. S2). The instantaneous axes of rotation and their discrete distribution are represented in the proximal LCS, i.e., the axis of the talocrural joint is represented in the LCS of the tibia, and the axis of the subtalar joint is represented in the LCS of the talus. The angle between the axis and the three anatomical planes (sagittal, frontal, and transverse) reflects the orientation of the axis. In addition, the concept of the gear ratio was utilized to speculate on the effect of additional weight on the muscle force of the ankle plantarflexors. The ratio of the moment arm of the ground reaction force R to the moment arm of the ankle plantarflexors r was used to define the gear ratio44,45. The gearing or lever-like function of the distal structures of the foot enhances the force generation of the plantarflexors, and increasing the gear ratio will increase the muscle force output of the plantarflexors while reducing the shortening speed of the fascicle44,45. In this study, the center of rotation was approximately replaced by the intersection of the axis and the sagittal plane. To analyze the position of the center of rotation of the talocrural joint, the stance phase was divided into four periods based on previously published methods46,47 (Supplementary Fig. S3). To analyze the 6-DOF continuous motion of the AJC, joint angles were calculated by the transformation matrix and converted to Euler angles (y-x-z sequence) for statistical analysis according to previously published methods2,48,49. Joint translations were defined as the movement of the origin of the distal skeletal LCS in the proximal skeletal LCS. For each trial in each subject, the heel strike to toe-off phase was normalized to 100%. In the early phase (before the maximum plantarflexion point) and late phase (after the maximum dorsiflexion point) of gait, correlation analysis was conducted between continuous rotational and translational motions of each joint and the ankle flexion angle. The peak value of the EMG signal was used to represent the signal amplitude.

Statistical analysis

The intersection points of the instantaneous axis of rotation of the talocrural joint with the sagittal plane and the intersection points of the instantaneous axis of rotation of the subtalar joint with the transverse plane are circled with 95% confidence ellipses to depict the distribution of reliable data within the plane. The area of the confidence ellipse represents the degree of dispersion of the joint axis. The differences in the ellipse areas between the BW and BW + 50% groups were identified using paired sample t-tests. Continuous kinematic waveforms were analyzed using one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping (SPM) to identify differences, while paired sample t-tests were used to assess differences in the range of motion (ROM). All significant differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. In the coupling analysis section, the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) was used to express the degree of linear correlation.

Results

Position and orientation of the instantaneous rotation axis

The instantaneous axis of the talocrural joint was an inclined axis along the medial-lateral side, and the angles to sagittal plane were 81.8 ± 3.2° and 81.4 ± 3.1° for walking with BW and BW + 50%, respectively (Fig. 2b and supplementary Table S1). Compared with walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% corresponded to a greater area of distribution of the intersection of the axis of rotation of the talocrural joint with the sagittal plane (YZ plane) and significantly differed (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). The instantaneous axis of the subtalar joint was an inclined axis from the lateral-inferior to the medial-superior, and the angles to the transverse plane were 42.0 ± 2.4° and 41.8 ± 1.9° for walking with BW and BW + 50%, respectively (Fig. 2e and supplementary Table S1). Compared with walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% corresponded to a greater area of distribution of the intersection of the instantaneous axis of rotation of the subtalar joint with the transverse plane (XY plane) and significantly differed (P = 0.02) (Fig. 2f). Therefore, compared to walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% results in a more dispersed spatial distribution for both the instantaneous rotation axes of the talocrural joint and the subtalar joint. Supplementary Video S2 shows a 3D visualization of the instantaneous axis of rotation of the talocrural and subtalar joints.

The angles of the talocrural and subtalar axes to the three anatomical planes (sagittal, transverse, and coronal planes) vary to some extent with the percentage of gait (Fig. 3). This small variability is somewhat more pronounced at the beginning and end stages of the stance phase, indicating that both the talocrural joint and the subtalar joint may not rotate about a fixed axis. However, there was no significant difference in these angles when walking with BW + 50% was compared to walking with BW. As shown in supplementary Table S1, the mean and deviation of the angles between the two joint axes and the three anatomical planes were quantified, providing a clearer and more accurate description of the position and variability of the joint axis.

Center of rotation of the talocrural joint and muscle activation

The in-plane distribution of the intersection of the instantaneous axis of rotation of the talocrural joint with the sagittal plane varies markedly at different stages of the stance phase. The intersections at the second stage (foot flat to mid stance) and the third stage (mid stance to heel off) were more superior and posterior during walking with BW + 50% than during walking with BW (Fig. 4). Compared to walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% significantly increased the magnitude of EMG signals in the soleus, medial gastrocnemius, and lateral gastrocnemius (Fig. 5a). The peak EMG signals were significantly different (P < 0.001).

Continuous 3D motion waveforms

Walking with BW and BW + 50% had consistent kinematic waveforms during the stance phase of gait in six-DOF motion (Figs. 6 and 7). With BW + 50% walking, the talocrural joints were more plantarflexed at 6–15% and 85–100% of the stance phase compared with walking with BW (P < 0.01). During the striking phase, the subtalar joint exhibited more eversion (P < 0.02). The tibiocalcaneal joint was more plantarflexed in 5–18% and 85–100% of the stance phase (P < 0.001) and more adducted at the end of gait (85–100%) (P < 0.02). The movements of the tibiocalcaneal joint are a synthesis of the talocrural and subtalar joints but are not purely superimposed. Dorsiflexion/plantarflexion of the tibiocalcaneal joint follows mainly the talocrural joint (Fig. 6a and c), inversion/eversion follows mainly the subtalar joint (Fig. 6b and c), and adduction/abduction is dominated by the talocrural joint at the beginning of the stance phase and by the subtalar joint at the end of the stance phase.

Compared to walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% showed that during the initial and final stages of the stance phase of gait, the position of the talus was more anterior relative to the tibia (P < 0.001) and more medially from 55 to 72% of the phase (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7). For the tibiocalcaneal joint, the position of the calcaneus was relatively further posterior to the tibia at 8–17% of the stance phase and more proximal during the early contact period (0–13%) (P < 0.03). From the perspective of motion range, the translation of the tibiocalcaneal joint is more pronounced than that of the subtalar and talocrural joints.

Importantly, Fig. 7d shows in detail the comparative data for dorsiflexion/plantarflexion and anterior/posterior translation of the talocrural joint in walking with BW and BW + 50% at the beginning phase (0–25%) and at the end phase (80–100%). The mean curves indicate that additional weight-bearing leads to increased plantarflexion and anterior shifting (talus relative to the tibia) in both phases.

Kinematic coupling

The talocrural joint, subtalar joint, and tibiocalcaneal joint exhibited strong correlations with the talocrural flexion (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion) angle during the early phase (before maximum plantarflexion) and late phase (after maximum dorsiflexion) of gait in multiple DOFs, with an absolute value of R exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. S4). However, correlations are weaker during the midstance phase. Specifically, during the midstance phase, there was a strong correlation between subtalar joint dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, tibiocalcaneal joint dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, and tibiocalcaneal joint adduction/abduction and talocrural flexion (the absolute value of R exceeded 0.8). Additionally, for rotational movements, differential analysis of correlation coefficients for all subjects revealed no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) between walking with BW and walking with BW + 50%, except for adduction/abduction of the talocrural joint in the midstance phase.

The coupling between the translation of the talocrural, subtalar, and tibiocalcaneal joints and talocrural flexion also exhibited a similar relationship (Supplementary Fig. S4). Translation of the talocrural, subtalar, and tibiocalcaneal joints is strongly correlated with talocrural flexion during the early and late phases of gait in multiple DOFs. Specifically, for walking with BW + 50%, during the early phase of gait, the correlation between the medial/lateral translation of the talocrural and tibiocalcaneal joints and talocrural flexion was weak (absolute value of R < 0.5), while during this phase, under both BW and BW + 50% conditions, there was a statistically significant difference in correlation coefficients (P < 0.05).

ROM values in 6-DOF

Supplementary Table S2 shows the ROM in 6-DOF for the three joints, including walking with BW and BW + 50%. From the mean values of ROM, walking with BW + 50% had larger ROM values corresponding to multiple DOFs. Specifically, the inversion/eversion ROM, which is the primary motion of the subtalar joint, was smaller in the BW + 50% condition. Compared to walking with BW, walking with BW + 50% significantly increased the ROM in dorsiflexion/plantarflexion and anterior-posterior translation at the talocrural joint, as well as dorsiflexion/plantarflexion at the tibiocalcaneal joint (P < 0.05). The maximum values of the three directions of rotation are shown in supplementary Table S3. The maximum plantarflexion and dorsiflexion of the talocrural joint as well as the maximum plantarflexion of the tibiocalcaneal joint were significantly different between the two weight-bearing walking conditions (P < 0.05). Supplementary Table S4 shows the maximum values of translation in the three directions, the maximum anterior and posterior translation of the talocrural joints, and the maximum posterior translation of the tibiocalcaneal joints, which significantly differed between the two weight-bearing walking conditions (P < 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, a dynamic biplane radiography system and a model-based tracking technique were used to quantify the spatial position of the joint axes of the AJC, as well as the distribution pattern of the center of rotation of the talocrural joint at different stages of gait. In addition, the continuous motion in 6-DOF and the ROM of the AJC and the coupling relationship of continuous motion in each DOF to the flexion angle of the talocrural joint were analyzed. All the data included walking with BW and walking with BW + 50%. This study provided a detailed quantification of the effects of additional weight-bearing on the motion of the AJC, which may reflect to some extent the self-regulatory mechanism of the AJC in response to additional loads.

For the position and orientation of the talocrural and subtalar axes, the statistical results in this study are within the range of previous reports16,39,40,42,43. The intersections of the instantaneous joint axis with the anatomical plane are more dispersed (larger confidence ellipse) with additional weight-bearing walking. According to previous reports in the literature, the instantaneous axis of rotation reflects, to some extent, the motion stability of the joint50–52. In the present study, the talocrural and subtalar joint axes were more spatially dispersed during walking with BW + 50%. This to some extent reflects the fact that additional weight-bearing walking reduced the stability of the AJC.

During the phase from foot flat to heel off, walking with BW + 50% causes the intersection point of the talocrural joint axis and the sagittal plane to be superior and posterior, which is a new finding with functional significance. The intersections are superior as a result of the talus shifting upward relative to the tibia under greater impact force. The intersection of the talocrural joint axis with the sagittal plane was more posterior during walking with BW + 50% than during walking with BW, which may have altered the ankle-foot gear ratio (Figs. 4 and 5). In 1994, Carrier et al.. proposed that the human foot improves locomotor performance by varying the gear ratio (i.e., velocity ratio) of the ankle-foot44. The center of pressure gradually moves forward, and therefore, the ground reaction force moment arm R increases, causing an increase in the ankle-foot gear ratio (R/r). Due to the force-velocity relationship, an increased gear ratio promotes force production in the plantarflexors and decreases the shortening velocity of the muscle bundles. In this study, the center of rotation of the ankle (talocrural) joint was no longer a fixed point relative to the proximal bone, and its position was adjusted with the change in the percentage of gait. A more posterior position of the center of rotation causes the moment arm of the ground reaction force R to increase and the moment arm of the ankle plantarflexor force r to decrease (Fig. 5b); therefore, the gear ratio becomes larger, which is the meaningful finding of this study. Carrier et al.. proposed the formula R × Fr = r × Fm, where Fr is the ground reaction force and Fm is the plantarflexors force44. In conjunction with this study, weight-bearing causes R to increase and r to decrease, and as the right foot is flattened and the heel is gradually lifted, the left foot enters a swing phase, so the load is gradually loaded onto the right foot, and Fr increases, thus inevitably leading to an increase in Fm. During the stance phase of walking with BW + 50%, the EMG signals of the soleus, medial gastrocnemius, and lateral gastrocnemius were significantly greater, suggesting that weight-bearing walking resulted in greater muscle activation of the ankle plantarflexors (Fig. 5a). Unfortunately, the EMG was not synchronized with X-ray image acquisition, so we could only assess the EMG during the entire stance phase and could not pinpoint the EMG corresponding to the second stage (foot flat to mid stance) or third stage (mid stance to heel off) of the stance phase. To some extent, it was confirmed that additional weight-bearing walking increased the muscle force of the ankle plantarflexors. That is, additional weight-bearing leads to increased muscle force of the plantarflexors and a decrease in the rate of shortening of the muscle bundles44 (human walking speed is also reduced compared to walking with BW, as an average of 15 more frames of images were acquired during the stance phase), which is similar to a low gear in a car, where speed is reduced and traction is increased. This may be a self-regulatory mechanism of the ankle joint to cope with the additional load. We speculate that this modulation mechanism enables the foot to handle external loads more effectively, enhancing locomotor efficiency and lowering the risk of injury. This self-regulatory mechanism has led to new insights into the movement of the AJC.

The continuous kinematics of the AJC in 6-DOF in this study are similar to the results of previous studies13,18,53. Although additional weight-bearing during specific gait phases resulted in some kinematic differences, these differences corresponded to transient phases, and the differences were reflected in limited movement amplitude. This may be an instinctive constraint regulation of the human body or foot, even if there is an interference of external loads, but there will be muscle and ligament control and neural and central regulation so that it is able to complete normal physiological motor functions, and within a certain range of additional loads, it will be able to complete normal walking11,22. Another novel finding is that walking with BW + 50% causes the talocrural joint to be more plantarflexed and anteriorly shifted (talus relative to the tibia), which is critical for ankle stability. Based on the mortise structure of the ankle joint and the anteriorly wide and posteriorly narrow shape of the talus cross-section, this would result in the release of tension within the mortise and tenon of the ankle joint, which becomes relatively lax, with reduced joint stability. This is consistent with the fact that additional weight-bearing leads to a greater spread out of the joint axis and reduced joint stability. Therefore, additional weight-bearing walking is more prone to ankle injuries, and out of human instincts, self-regulation reduces walking speed to maintain stability against this instability or susceptibility to injury. This may be an instinctive self-regulatory mechanism for ankle stabilization in the human body54,55.

The ROM of the three joints showed that additional weight-bearing induced a large average ROM for multiple DOFs, indicating that additional weight-bearing amplifies the kinematic characteristics to some extent. In fact, a larger ROM is needed to satisfy the demand for motion under larger loads. However, this amplitude of movement is very limited, which is consistent with the control of the muscles and ligaments and the regulation of the nerves and centers, as mentioned above. In particular, the ROM of inversion/eversion, which is the primary motion of the subtalar joint, was smaller in both walking conditions with and without additional weight bearing. This indicates that, as the joint with the hindfoot portion closest to the ground and the first to be subjected to an impact force, the subtalar joint displayed greater stability and reduced flexibility under greater loads.

Dorsiflexion/plantarflexion of the talocrural joint is the most pronounced form of movement in the AJC and has received the most attention from researchers. However, we found that the movements of the talocrural, subtalar, and tibiocalcaneal joints in six DOFs were significantly coupled to the dorsiflexion/plantarflexion angles of the talocrural joints. In particular, a strong linear correlation was shown at the beginning and end of the gait. The linear correlation was weaker during the midstance phase of gait. This phenomenon may be related to the need for greater functionality of the foot-ankle complex during the early strike and late push-off periods. With the flexion of the ankle (the joint with the largest ROM in the foot), the initial period of the stance phase requires a flexible and smooth landing to absorb the impact; the push-off period requires the foot structure to be as strong as a lever and the AJC to transmit the force; and the midstance period is mainly about maintaining stability and bearing the load. This may provide valuable guidance for the design of ankle joints in exoskeletons or bipedal robots, as many exoskeletons or bipedal robots adopt only one DOF (flexion in the sagittal plane), although some have two DOFs56,57. Considering the multi-DOF coupling of the human ankle joint, this may be important for the future development of robots or rehabilitation aids with load-bearing functionality or multi-DOF ankle joint.

There are some limitations to this study, such as the fact that the subjects were all male and aged 20 to 30 years. Therefore, the effects of sex differences and different age groups on the kinematics of the AJC were not considered. Future studies should focus on this topic. In addition, due to the limited imaging area of the biplane radiography system, the tibia is beyond the field of view during the late push-off phase; therefore, strictly speaking, the 100% phase of the stance phase is insufficient. This limitation is also common to similar studies and should be acknowledged when interpreting the results18,33,34.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study illustrated that the talocrural joint and the subtalar joint that comprise the AJC do not rotate on a fixed axis; rather, the axis of rotation is slightly adjusted with gait. Additional weight-bearing resulted in greater plantarflexing and anteriorly shifting of the ankle and greater spread out of the joint axis, suggesting that additional weight-bearing reduces the stability of the AJC and that subconsciously, out of human instinct, walking speeds are lowered to resist instability or susceptibility to injury. The center of rotation of the talocrural joint is variably adjusted with the percentage of gait, and walking with BW + 50% results in a larger gear ratio, which has the benefit of increasing plantarflexor force and decreasing the fascicle shortening velocity. These results demonstrated that there may be a self-regulatory mechanism for the AJC during additional weight-bearing walking, which is efficient for bipedal walking in humans.

Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the biomechanical adaptations of the human AJC to increased loading during walking, which may have implications for optimizing athletic training protocols and informing clinical interventions for individuals with gait-related disorders. In addition, from an engineering perspective, this instinctive movement or regulation of the human AJC is instructive for the development of bipedal robots or rehabilitation aids with weight-bearing capabilities or flexible movement properties.
