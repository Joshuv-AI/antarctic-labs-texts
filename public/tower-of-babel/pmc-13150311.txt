An antiviral jacalin‐like lectin gene contributes to nonhost resistance and host determination of potexviruses among Brassicaceae
Authors: Takumi Suzuki, Kazuya Ishikawa, Akira Yusa, Nozomu Iwabuchi, Kan Kimura, Tetsuya Yoshida, Mari Ota, Masato Suzuki, Masanobu Nishikawa, Kensaku Maejima, Shigetou Namba, Yasuyuki Yamaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Plant resistance influences the host range of pathogens; however, the mechanism driving this influence remains poorly understood. Brassicaceae species are not natural hosts of potexviruses, for reasons that have not yet been determined.This study investigated the molecular basis of nonhost resistance (NHR) to potexviruses in the Brassicaceae, focusing on the non‐nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) resistance gene JACALIN‐TYPE LECTIN REQUIRED FOR POTEXVIRUS RESISTANCE1 (JAX1).We found that JAX1 is a Brassicaceae‐specific conserved gene that has been lost or pseudogenized in some species, and duplicated in others. Most JAX1 orthologs from diverse Brassicaceae species effectively inhibit plantago asiatica mosaic virus (PlAMV) accumulation, indicating that antiviral activity is conserved among the Brassicaceae. However, we demonstrated that some Brassicaceae plants in which JAX1 expression was not detected allow local or systemic viral infection. Quadruple knockout of JAX1 genes via gene editing in Sisymbrium irio, a nonhost of PlAMV, led to loss of resistance and potexvirus susceptibility in this species.These results clearly indicate that JAX1 represents one layer of multi‐layer NHR against potexviruses in the Brassicaceae, shedding light on the pivotal role of non‐NLR antiviral resistance in shaping the host range of these plant viruses. Keywords: Brassicaceae, host‐range determinant, jacalin‐like lectin, plant virus, potexvirus

Introduction

All organisms are subject to infection by pathogens. However, each pathogen can infect only a specific subset of organisms, which is referred to as a host range or host specificity. For example, fungal pathogens, such as Botrytis cinerea and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, or viral pathogens, such as cucumber mosaic virus, can infect hundreds of plant species (Boland & Hall, 1994; Hull, 2013; Singh et al., 2024), while rust, powdery mildew, and many other widespread plant virus diseases have highly limited host ranges (Shukla et al., 1994; Helfer, 2014; Kusch et al., 2024).

Host‐range restrictions are presumed to be determined by complex interactions between pathogens and plants (Morris & Moury, 2019). One factor influencing host range is acquired plant resistance. The resistance shown by a single plant species to a specific pathogen is termed nonhost resistance (NHR; Heath, 2000). NHR is broadly categorized into several layers of plant immunity: physical defense barriers, chemical defenses through secondary metabolites such as phytoanticipins and phytoalexins, pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) induced by the recognition of pathogen‐ or damage‐associated molecular patterns by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), effector‐triggered immunity (ETI) induced by the recognition of pathogen effectors by nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptors (NLRs), and other undefined factors (Uma et al., 2011; Piasecka et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017). These layers of plant immunity play different roles in NHR; physical defenses, chemical defenses, and PTI mediated by PRRs underlie the NHR of nonhost plants that are phylogenetically distant to the host; and ETI mediated by NLRs plays a more significant role in NHR in more closely related nonhosts (Schulze‐Lefert & Panstruga, 2011). Genetic approaches using mutant screening in Arabidopsis thaliana have identified several new factors, such as PEN1–4 (Collins et al., 2003; Lipka et al., 2005; Stein et al., 2006; Hématy et al., 2020), NHO1 (Kang et al., 2003), and PSS (Wang et al., 2018; Kambakam et al., 2021), which play important roles in NHR to fungal and bacterial pathogens. However, whether the host range of pathogens is determined solely by these layers of plant immunity and immunity‐related genes remains largely unknown (Panstruga & Moscou, 2020).

The genus Potexvirus currently comprises 52 identified virus species, each with a relatively narrow host range. Nevertheless, potexviruses have been reported in a wide variety of monocot and dicot plants, spanning > 41 plant families, including 15 of the 20 plant families most frequently reported to be hosts of plant viruses in the Virus–Host Database (Mihara et al., 2016; Supporting Information Table S1). The family Brassicaceae is a notable exception; despite its global cultivation and the inclusion of the model plant A. thaliana, none of its members act as a potexvirus host, which suggests that Brassicaceae plants may have evolved NHR that restricts potexvirus infection. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying NHR among the Brassicaceae remain unclear.

Virus resistance is acquired through dominant or recessive inheritance; most of the identified dominant virus resistance genes encode NLR proteins, although some non‐NLR resistance genes have also been identified (de Ronde et al., 2014). One such non‐NLR resistance gene is the jacalin‐type antiviral resistance gene, which encodes a jacalin‐like lectin (JAL). To date, two jacalin‐type antiviral resistance genes have been identified from A. thaliana. One is Restricted TEV Movement 1 (RTM1), which inhibits long‐distance movement of tobacco etch virus, plum pox virus, and lettuce mosaic virus, members of the genus Potyvirus (Mahajan et al., 1998; Chisholm et al., 2000; Decroocq et al., 2006). RTM1‐mediated resistance has been suggested to require at least four additional genes (RTM2–RTM5), with RTM2 encoding a small heat shock protein‐like protein with a transmembrane domain and RTM3 encoding a MATH domain‐containing protein, while the others remain uncharacterized (Whitham et al., 1999, 2000; Cosson et al., 2010, 2012). Although the N‐terminal region of the coat protein (CP) has been suggested to be involved in this resistance, the detailed molecular mechanism remains unclear (Decroocq et al., 2006, 2009). The other is JACALIN‐TYPE LECTIN REQUIRED FOR POTEXVIRUS RESISTANCE1 (JAX1), a potexvirus resistance gene that targets the viral replication complex precursor, thereby inhibiting virus replication (Yamaji et al., 2012; Yoshida et al., 2019). While JAX1 has exhibited resistance to all tested potexviruses and functions as a resistance gene across plant families (Yamaji et al., 2012; Okano et al., 2020), it remains unknown whether JAX1 is conserved in plants other than A. thaliana.

This study investigated the role of the non‐NLR antiviral gene JAX1 in the determination of potexvirus host ranges among Brassicaceae plants. Our results reveal that JAX1 is a Brassicaceae‐specific gene that is functionally conserved across the family Brassicaceae. Notably, the resistance of Brassicaceae plants to plantago asiatica mosaic virus (Potexvirus marmorplantagonis, PlAMV) was found to be correlated with JAX1 expression, suggesting that JAX1 is a determinant of PlAMV resistance. Furthermore, SiJAX1 knockout in the potexvirus‐resistant plant Sisymbrium irio resulted in potexvirus susceptibility. These findings indicate that JAX1 contributes to NHR against potexvirus in Brassicaceae plants.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials

Seeds of the Brassicaceae plants used in this study (Aethionema arabicum (L.) Andrz. ex DC, Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., Arabis alpina L., Brassica rapa subsp. pekinensis (Lour.) Kitam., Boechera stricta (Graham) Al‐Shehbaz, Cardamine hirsuta L., Sisymbrium irio L.) are listed in Table S2. All the Brassicaceae plants were grown in a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark condition growth chamber at 22°C. Nicotiana benthamiana Domin was grown under natural light conditions at 25°C and maintained in a 16 h : 8 h, 25°C : 20°C, light : dark condition growth chamber during the experiments for antiviral activity of JAX1 orthologs.

In silico identification of JAX1 orthologs

Genome information and protein annotation data for various angiosperm plants were collected from online genome databases as listed in Table S3. For the identification of JAX1 orthologs, bidirectional best‐hit search (BBH) was performed as follows: the amino acid sequence of JAX1 from A. thaliana ‘Bay‐0’ (NCBI accession no.: BAL48823.1) was searched by Blastp in Blast v.2.10.0+ (Altschul et al., 1990; Camacho et al., 2009) against the protein annotation files of each plant species, with the parameter e‐value < 1e‐30. The top hit sequences were subjected to a reverse Blastp search against the protein annotation data of A. thaliana ‘Columbia‐0 (Col‐0)’. Of these, only the best hits with JAX1 were considered as JAX1 orthologs. For tBlastn search against Brassicaceae plant genomes, the amino acid sequence of JAX1 was searched by tBlastn in Blast 2.10.0+ against the genomic sequence of each Brassicaceae species, with the parameter e‐value < 1e‐20. For each hit, regions showing 65% > identity were extracted. To search for protein sequences containing JAL domain in each plant, hmmscan was performed in Hmmer v.3.3.2 (Eddy, 2011; http://hmmer.org) against protein annotation data using the hidden Markov model of the JAL domain (Pfam accession no.: PF01419) as a query with the parameter e‐value < 1. Obtained sequences were filtered with amino acid (aa) sequence lengths longer than 70 aa (about half of the longest domain) using seqkit v.2.8.2 with the following command (seq ‐m 70; Shen et al., 2016, 2024). Filtered domains were aligned by hmmalign program in Hmmer v.3.3.2 to the JAL domain (PF01419), with the ‐‐trim parameter to cut unaligned residues. The output alignment was trimmed using trimAl v.1.4.rev15 (Capella‐Gutiérrez et al., 2009) with the ‐gappyout parameter to remove columns containing gaps in many sequences with automated selection. The maximum‐likelihood (ML) tree of the plant JAL domain was computed using IQ‐Tree v.2.2.0.3 (Minh et al., 2020). Branch support was assessed by ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot; Hoang et al., 2018) and Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test (SH‐aLRT; Anisimova et al., 2011), with 1000 replicates. The amino acid substitution model was determined using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017), and JTT + R9 model was chosen. The midpoint‐rooted dendrogram was visualized using the R package ggtree v.3.8.2 (Yu et al., 2017), and subsequently adjusted for layout and appearance using Adobe Illustrator 2024. To reconstruct the ML tree of Clade II, all the JAL domains forming Clade II were concatenated with a Clade III sequence for the root (AT2G39330.1, from 323 to 454 aa, Clade III). The ML tree was reconstructed as described above, using the Q.plant+I + I + R5 model for amino acid substitution. The dendrogram was visualized as described above, rooted with the root sequence. The identities of JAX1 orthologs were calculated by Sequence Identity And Similarity (SIAS) web tool (http://imed.med.ucm.es/Tools/sias.html).

Vector construction

Full‐length JAX1 ortholog fragments were amplified from the cDNA or genomic DNA of each plant using KOD plus NEO (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) with primers designed based on genome information (Table S4). The amplified fragments were digested with BamHI‐HF (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) and XhoI (NEB) and cloned into the pENTA (Himeno et al., 2010) using Ligation‐Convenience Kit (Nippon Gene, Toyama, Japan). pENTA‐GUS and pENTA‐JAX1 constructions were described previously (Okano et al., 2014, 2020). For transient expression in N. benthamiana, each JAX1 ortholog cloned into the pENTA was subcloned into the pFKBH202 vector, and for the transformation of A. thaliana, each JAX1 ortholog was subcloned into the pFAST‐G02 (Shimada et al., 2010), using Gateway LR Clonase II enzyme mix (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The pFKBH202 vector was constructed as follows: For expression of N‐terminal FLAG‐tagged fusion proteins, sgRNA‐Cas9 cassette in pKI1.1R, a gift from Tetsuya Higashiyama (Addgene #85808; http://n2t.net/addgene:85808; RRID:Addgene_85808; Tsutsui & Higashiyama, 2017), was replaced by 35S promoter‐FLAG tag‐attR1‐CmR‐ccdB‐attR2 cassette. The 35S promoter‐FLAG tag‐attR1‐CmR‐ccdB‐attR2 cassette was amplified from pEarleyGate202 (Earley et al., 2006) using primers pki‐pEG‐35S‐F and pEG‐att_backR, and heat shock protein 18.2 terminator (HSP ter) and OLE1 promoter fragment (OLE1 pro) were amplified from pKI1.1R using primer sets pKI‐HSP‐F/R and pKI‐pOLE‐F/R, respectively. Those three fragments were assembled with KpnI‐HF and StuI (NEB) digested pKI1.1R vector using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB), yielding in pFK202 vector, where the insert gene is expressed with an N‐terminal FLAG tag under the control of the 35S promoter and HSP terminator. To add Bialaphos resistance gene (BlpR) for plant selection marker with the Hygromycin resistance gene (HygR) in pFK202, NOS pro‐HygR‐NOS ter cassette in pFK202 was replaced with NOS pro‐BlpR‐P2A‐HygR‐NOS ter cassette. The fragment of NOS pro‐HygR was amplified from pKI1.1R with pFKBH‐inF/R and cloned into pCR‐Blunt II‐TOPO vector (Invitrogen), yielding TOPO‐HygR vector. Then, BlpR gene was amplified from pFAST‐G02 vector using primers BlpR_F/R and 2A peptide of porcine teschovirus (P2A) with GSG linker sequence was amplified from synthesized sequence (GGATCTGGAG CTATGACAGT TATGGCTTTT CAAGGACCTG GTGCTACTAA CTTTTCTTTG CTTAAGCAAG CTGGTGATGT TGAAGAGAAT CCTGGACCA) using primer sets blpr‐P2A_F/hygr_P2A_R. The P2A fragment was fused to the 3′ end of BlpR by recombinant PCR using primer pair BlpR_F/hygr_P2A_R. The fused fragment was assembled with the linearized TOPO‐HygR vector, obtained via inverse PCR using primer pair HygR‐1F/blpr_FKBH_invF, using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix. This process yields the TOPO vector inserted with NOS pro‐BlpR‐P2A‐HygR, in which BlpR‐P2A fragment directly upstream of the ATG start codon of HygR. Finally, NOS pro‐BlpR‐P2A‐HygR fragment was amplified with pFKBH‐inF/R, and the amplified fragments were assembled with pFK202 digested with KpnI‐HF and AseI (NEB) using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix.

For the CRISPR/Cas9‐based genome editing in S. irio, pKI1.1R vector was used following the protocol described previously (Tsutsui & Higashiyama, 2017), with some modifications. Briefly, pKI1.1R was linearized by AarI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and dephosphorylated using bacterial alkaline phosphatase (from E. coli C75; Wako, Osaka, Japan). Guide RNA targeting sequence identical to the four copies of SiJAX1 was designed using the CRISPR MultiTargeter (Prykhozhij et al., 2015). The two oligonucleotides harboring the gRNA target (SiJAX1‐tarF/SiJAX1‐tarR) were phosphorylated using T4 PNK (NEB) and hybridized. The digested plasmid and the hybridized oligonucleotides were ligated using Ligation‐Convenience Kit, yielding pKI1.1R‐SiJAX1tar vector.

For AltMV–sGFP construction, pCAM‐AltMV (Yusa et al., 2019) was linearized between 36 and 37 nt of ORF encoding CP by inverse PCR using KOD Plus NEO with sgfp‐AltMV‐invF/p2a‐cpopt‐AltMV‐invR primers. Fragments of sGFP and P2A were amplified by sGFP‐F/p2a‐sGFP‐R and P2A‐F/P2A‐R using KOD Plus NEO. Then, the two fragments were assembled by recombinant PCR using sGFP‐F/P2A‐R, yielding sGFP‐P2A. Finally, linearized pCAM‐AltMV and sGFP‐P2A were ligated using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix, yielding pCAM‐AltMV‐sGFP.

Antiviral activity assay on N. benthamiana leaf

For the expression on N. benthamiana, GUS or JAX1 orthologs subcloned into pFKBH202, pBin61‐P19, and pCAM‐PlAMV–sGFP vector (Yamaji et al., 2012) were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 (Hood et al., 1993). The pBin61‐P19 plasmid was kindly provided by Dr. David Charles Baulcombe (University of Cambridge). The Agrobacterium was grown overnight at 28°C in 2xYT broth (16 g l−1 tryptone, 10 g l−1 yeast extract, 5 g l−1 NaCl) containing 100 mg l−1 Spectinomycin or 50 mg l−1 Kanamycin and collected, then resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, and 150 μM acetosyringone, pH 5.6). The Agrobacterium suspension was mixed to a final optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of pFKBH202 = 0.5, P19 = 0.05 and PlAMV–sGFP = 0.05, and co‐infiltrated into 5–8‐wk‐old N. benthamiana leaves. The inoculated leaves were collected at 4 d postinoculation (dpi) and then photographed under blue light to observe sGFP fluorescence. The red channel of the photographs was converted to magenta using ImageJ. Accumulation of PlAMV‐CP and each JAX1 ortholog was analyzed by western blotting. Briefly, c. φ8mm of infiltrated leaf area was collected and grounded in liquid nitrogen. Total protein was extracted with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer (62.5 mM Tris–HCl [pH 6.8], 10%[v/v] glycerol, 5%[v/v] 3‐mercapto‐1,2‐propanediol, 2.3%[w/v] SDS, 50 ppm bromophenol blue), and after centrifugation of the lysate at 21 500 g for 5 min, the supernatant was treated at 95°C for 5 min. The proteins were separated on 12.5% polyacrylamide gel using NuPAGE MOPS SDS Running buffer (Invitrogen), blotted onto a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Immobilon‐P; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and detected using an anti‐FLAG antibody (F1804; Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) and anti‐PlAMV‐CP antibody (Ozeki et al., 2006). All experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

Arabidopsis thaliana transformation and genome editing of SiJAX1 in S. irio

Arabidopsis thaliana ‘Col‐0’ plants were transformed with Agrobacterium EHA105 strain carrying JAX1 orthologs subcloned into pFAST‐G02 by floral dip method (Clough & Bent, 1998). Transformed plants were selected by green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence in seed coats. To generate frameshift mutations of four copies of SiJAX1 by CRISPR/Cas9 system, S. irio plants were transformed with Agrobacterium EHA105 strain carrying pKI1.1R‐SiJAX1tar by floral dip method as A. thaliana. T1 transformants were selected by tag‐RFP fluorescence in seed coats under green light. T‐DNA insertion was confirmed using PCR. Leaf DNA of T1 plants was extracted according to the CTAB method using GENE PREP STAR PI‐80X (Kurabo, Osaka, Japan); then, partial T‐DNA fragment was amplified using KOD One (TOYOBO) with primers attB1F and SiJAX1‐tarR under the following conditions: 40 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 5 s, and 68°C for 1 s. The 18S rDNA was amplified as control with 25e/1769 primers (Nickrent & Starr, 1994), under 35 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 5 s, and 68°C for 10 s. To confirm Cas9 expression, total protein was extracted from leaves of T2 seedlings showing RFP fluorescence and subjected to western blotting. To confirm the frameshift mutations of SiJAX1, leaf DNA of T2 (Line 1‐4, 5‐3) and T3 plants (Line3‐3) was extracted as described above, and two copies of SiJAX1a‐SiJAX1b gene cassette (SiJAX1a1‐SiJAX1b1 and SiJAX1a2‐SiJAX1b2) were amplified using KOD Plus Neo (TOYOBO), yielding 3 and 5 kb DNA fragments, respectively. The amplified fragments were cloned into pCR‐Blunt II‐TOPO vector according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cloned vectors were transformed with E. coli (DH5α high Champion™; Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan), and extracted from the single colonies using GENE PREP STAR PI‐80X. At least eight clones obtained from each amplified fragment were sequenced by M13F/R or SiJAX1a_tar_gen_F/SiJAX1b_tar_gen_F and confirmed each clone had the same sequence.

Virus inoculation assay

For mechanical inoculation, N. benthamiana leaves infected with PlAMV‐sGFP, AltMV (Iwabuchi et al., 2016), or AltMV‐sGFP were ground in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.01 M dithiothreitol (DTT), and the sap mixed with carborundum (600 mesh; Nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was used as inoculum. For mock inoculum, 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.01 M DTT with carborundum was used. For inoculation of A. thaliana lines transformed with JAX1 orthologs, 10 μl of inoculum (0.1 g of leaves in 500 μl of buffer) was inoculated to two leaves of 3‐wk‐old plants using fingers fitted with disposable latex finger cots. Upper leaves were collected at 21 dpi. For inoculation assay in Brassicaceae plants, 5 μl of inoculum (0.1 g of leaves in 1 ml of buffer) was inoculated to two leaves of 5‐wk‐old plants to assess local infection, and to four leaves of 4‐wk‐old plants to assess systemic infection. The inoculated leaves were collected at 7 dpi, and upper leaves were collected at 21 dpi. sGFP fluorescence was observed and photographed under blue light, with the red channel of the photographs converted to magenta using ImageJ.

RNA extraction and RT‐PCR

Samples inoculated with PlAMV were collected as described above. Organ‐specific samples for assessing the expression of JAX1 orthologs were collected from noninoculated plants and divided into two or three parts: leaf, stem (only for nonrosette plants; Aal and Aar), and root. Total RNA was extracted using Favorprep Plant Total RNA kit (Favorgen, Pingtung, Taiwan) and treated with DNase I (Nippon Gene). One hundred nanograms of the total RNA was then reverse transcribed using SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) with random hexamers.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) was performed using Takara Taq polymerase (Takara, Shiga, Japan) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, annealing at the temperature indicated in Table S5 for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min. A final elongation step was conducted at 72°C for 7 min. The same PCR conditions were applied for the amplification of PlAMV or AltMV fragments from the cDNA using PlAMV‐detF/R primers or AltMV_3622F/AltMV_4064R primers except for annealing temperature at 63°C or 49°C with a final primer concentration of 0.3 μM each. For PlAMV detection in inoculated leaves, the number of cycles was changed to 30 cycles. JAX1 orthologs were amplified under the same PCR conditions except for the use of primers specific for each JAX1 and annealing temperatures indicated in Table S5, with a final primer concentration of 0.3 μM. For RT‐PCR control, the TIP41‐like family gene (AT4G34270.1; Czechowski et al., 2005) was selected as a target. Orthologs of AT4G34270.1 were identified by searching BBH as described above (Table S6), and a primer set was designed in the conserved region of the orthologs. Identified Brassicaceae TIP41‐like gene orthologs were amplified using TIP41 uniF5/R5 primers with a final primer concentration of 1.2 μM each and an annealing temperature of 49°C. The PCR amplification was confirmed by electrophoresis of PCR products on 2% (w/v) agarose gels in TAE buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM acetic acid, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid).

Results

JAX1 is originated in a Brassicaceae common ancestor and conserved within the Brassicaceae

To explore the conservation of JAX1 orthologs among angiosperms, we first identified the bidirectional best hit (BBH) of JAX1 from annotated protein information from 18 angiosperm species (Table S3) using the full‐length JAX1 gene as a query. BBHs were found in nine species classified into four lineages of the Brassicaceae (two from Lineage I, five from Lineage II, one from Lineage IV, and one from basal genus Aethionema), excluding Capsella rubella (Table S7), and were not found in other plant families. To search for putative duplicated JAX1 orthologs and JAX1 orthologs overlooked in the current annotated protein data, we performed a tBlastn search against the genome sequences (Fig. S1; Table S8). In B. stricta, C. hirsuta, Thlaspi arvense, Schrenkiella parvula, Eutrema salsugineum, A. alpina, and A. arabicum, a single JAX1 ortholog identical to the BBH hit was found. In addition to BBH hits, other hits (BraA06000864 and SI_scaffold791_35) were found in B. rapa and S. irio, suggesting duplicated JAX1 orthologs in these species. Notably, E. salsugineum has a premature stop codon in the first exon, suggesting that the JAX1 ortholog of E. salsugineum is a pseudogene. These results suggest that JAX1 is a Brassicaceae‐specific gene (Fig. 1) exhibiting some diversification.

JAX1 encodes a 157‐amino acid (aa) protein with a single JAL domain that belongs to the JAL family, in which some genes encode tandemly repeated JAL domains (Esch & Schaffrath, 2017). To reveal the evolutionary relationships of JAX1 orthologs with other JAL family proteins, we performed domain‐based phylogenetic analysis. Before this analysis, we tested whether the antiviral activity of JAX1 is determined by the JAL domain alone (Fig. 2a). FLAG‐tag‐fused JAX1 or the JAL domain (aa 24–152) was co‐expressed with GFP‐expressing PlAMV (PlAMV–sGFP) and the silencing suppressor P19 in N. benthamiana leaves. At 4 dpi, GFP fluorescence and PlAMV CP accumulation were significantly reduced compared with the control when PlAMV–sGFP was co‐expressed with either the JAL domain alone or full‐length JAX1, indicating that the JAL domain of JAX1 alone is sufficient to inhibit PlAMV accumulation (Figs 2b, S2). Therefore, we performed a comprehensive search for JAL domain sequences among annotated protein sequences. HMMER analysis using a profile hidden Markov model of the JAL domain (PF01419, E < 1) identified 1542 JAL domains (aa 9–153) from 19 plant species (Tables S9, S10). More JAL domains were found in Brassicaceae plants and Tarenaya hassleriana (family Cleomaceae) than in other plants, except for Oryza sativa (Table S9). A phylogenetic tree based on the amino acid sequences of the 1323 JAL domains of about half the length of the longest JAL domain (≥ 70 aa) was composed of three clades (I–III; Fig. 2c). The clade divisions were generally consistent with previous classifications of A. thaliana JAL domains (Fig. S3; Nagano et al., 2008; Eggermont et al., 2017). Clade I was considered an ancestral clade, comprising 271 JAL domains from all angiosperm species analyzed in this study. Clades II and III comprised 280 and 772 JAL domains, respectively, obtained exclusively from 10 Brassicaceae species and the Cleomaceae species Tarenaya hassleriana (Fig. 2d,e). A reconstructed phylogenetic tree for Clade II (Fig. 2f) showed that JAX1 formed a monophyletic clade with its orthologs from Brassicaceae plants, except for SI_scaffold791_35 from S. irio, which was excluded from the HMMER searches. Notably, SI_scaffold791_35 also formed a monophyletic clade with JAX1 orthologs in a phylogenetic tree of the JAL domain of Clade II (Fig. S4), confirming that SI_scaffold791_35 is also a JAX1 ortholog. However, the sister clades contained other JAL domains in A. thaliana that were paralogous to JAX1 (Fig. 2f). These results indicate that JAX1 orthologs originated from a common ancestor of the Brassicaceae and have been conserved in Brassicaceae plants, while some orthologs have been lost or pseudogenized during Brassicaceae evolution, as observed in species, such as C. rubella and E. salsugineum (Fig. 1).

Conservation of JAX1 orthologs in terms of domain structure and sequence identity

To verify the function of JAX1 orthologs, full‐length cDNAs were cloned and the sequences were compared with the corresponding reference genomic sequences of five Brassicaceae plants representing different lineages: B. stricta (Bs) and C. hirsuta (Ch) from Lineage I, B. rapa (Bra) and S. irio (Si) from Lineage II, A. alpina (Aal) from Lineage IV, and A. arabicum (Aar; Fig. 3a). The results revealed that cDNAs of JAX1 orthologs of Bra (BraJAX1a and BraJAX1b), Aal (AalJAX1), and Aar (AarJAX1) were identical to those of the reference genome. However, several differences were observed for Bs, Ch, and Si. For Bs and Ch, BsJAX1 and ChJAX1 lacked the N‐terminal end compared with the annotated protein (Fig. S5a,b). For Si, four copies of JAX1 orthologs with a single JAL domain were cloned (SiJAX1a1, SiJAX1a2, SiJAX1b1, and SiJAX1b2). Two copies (SiJAX1a1/a2) were similar to SI_scaffold791_34, and two other copies (SiJAX1b1/b2) were similar to SI_scaffold791_35 (Fig. S5c–e). These 10 JAX1 orthologs cloned from six Brassicaceae plants shared 70.7–85.4% identity and 75.3–88.5% similarity with A. thaliana JAX1 at the amino acid level (Tables S11, S12). Phylogenetic analysis of cloned JAX1 orthologs implied that JAX1 duplication had occurred in part of Lineage II, as observed in Bra and Si (Fig. 3b,c).

Antiviral activity against PlAMV is conserved among JAX1 orthologs

Antiviral activity against PlAMV–sGFP among the 10 cloned JAX1 orthologs (Fig. 3a) was tested using a transient agroinfiltration patch assay in N. benthamiana leaves (Yamaji et al., 2012). GFP fluorescence and PlAMV CP accumulation were significantly abolished when co‐expressed with any of the seven JAX1 orthologs: AarJAX1, BsJAX1, ChJAX1, SiJAX1a1, a2, b2, or BraJAX1b (Fig. 4a,b). By contrast, slight GFP fluorescence and CP accumulation were observed when co‐expressed with AalJAX1 (Figs 4a,b, S6), and high viral accumulation similar to that of β‐glucuronidase (GUS, a negative control) was observed when co‐expressed with either BraJAX1a or SiJAX1b1 (Fig. 4a,b). To assess the resistance of the JAX1 ortholog against systemic infection by PlAMV, A. thaliana ‘Col‐0’ wild‐type (WT) plants, in which JAX1 is a pseudogene due to the introduction of a premature stop codon, enabling infection by PlAMV (Yamaji et al., 2012), were transformed with several JAX1 orthologs under the 35S promoter, including BraJAX1a, which showed a similar accumulation level to that of GUS in a N. benthamiana patch assay. When PlAMV–sGFP was inoculated on these transgenic lines, GFP fluorescence was detected in WT, AalJAX1, and BraJAX1a transgenic A. thaliana, suggesting that these orthologs don't inhibit systemic infection of PlAMV. By contrast, GFP fluorescence was not detected in any transformants with the other five JAX1 orthologs (Fig. S7). While this function may have been lost in some orthologs, these results suggest that the antiviral activity of JAX1 orthologs against PlAMV is generally conserved within the Brassicaceae.

PlAMV resistance and JAX1 ortholog expression analysis in Brassicaceae plants

Because most of the tested Brassicaceae species had at least one functional JAX1, we hypothesized that they would be resistant to PlAMV. To test this hypothesis, PlAMV–sGFP was mechanically inoculated onto five Brassicaceae plants (B. stricta, C. hirsuta, S. irio, A. alpina, and A. arabicum) harboring functional JAX1 orthologs. Arabidopsis thaliana WTs ‘Col‐0’ and ‘Bay‐0’ were used as positive and negative controls for viral infection. Viral infection was analyzed on inoculated leaves and upper leaves at 7 and 21 dpi, respectively, through GFP fluorescence observation and RT‐PCR. Consistent with our hypothesis, most of the tested species were resistant to PlAMV, to varying degrees (Figs 5, S8). In Si and Aal, PlAMV–sGFP was not detected in either inoculated or upper leaves, similar to the resistance observed in 35S‐driven JAX1 ortholog‐transformed A. thaliana (Fig. S7). In Bs and Aar, PlAMV–sGFP was detected in inoculated leaves but not in upper leaves, indicating that the infection was confined to local leaves, as in A. thaliana ‘Bay‐0’. However, Ch was fully susceptible to PlAMV, with viral accumulation observed in both inoculated and upper leaves, as in ‘Col‐0’. These results indicate that most Brassicaceae species harboring functional JAX1 orthologs are resistant to PlAMV, with the exception of Ch.

To investigate the cause of these differences in PlAMV resistance, we assessed the organ‐specific expression patterns of JAX1 orthologs in different organs (leaves, roots, and stems; stems were assessed only for nonrosette plants) using RT‐PCR analysis (Fig. 6). In Ch, which was fully susceptible to PlAMV, ChJAX1 expression was not detected in both leaves and roots (Fig. 6c). In Bs and Aar plants, in which PlAMV infection is limited to leaves, BsJAX1 and AarJAX1 were detected in the roots and stems, but not clearly in the inoculated leaves, similar to the pattern of JAX1 in ‘Bay‐0’ plants (Figs 6a,b,f, S8). In Bs plants, BsJAX1 was also detected in upper, noninoculated leaves (Fig. S9). In Si, which exhibited local and systemic resistance to PlAMV, while SiJAX1a tended to be detected in roots, SiJAX1b was detected in both leaves and roots (Fig. 6d). These results suggest that JAX1 ortholog expression was correlated with PlAMV resistance. Notably, in Aal, which also showed local and systemic resistance to PlAMV, AalJAX1 was not detected in leaves, roots, or stems (Fig. 6e). These findings indicate that JAX1 expression patterns are correlated with PlAMV resistance in most cases. Collectively, our results support the hypothesis that JAX1 is a key regulator of potexvirus host determination across a broad range of Brassicaceae plants.

JAX1 orthologs exhibit NHR against potexvirus in S. irio

To elucidate whether JAX1 regulates potexvirus host determination among the Brassicaceae, we generated knockout mutants of the JAX1 orthologs of Si, which is a nonhost of PlAMV and did not infect even in inoculated leaves (Figs 5, S8). We designed guide RNAs targeting a conserved sequence shared by the four copies of SiJAX1 within the JAL domain (346–365 nt for SiJAX1a1/a2 and 349–368 nt for SiJAX1b1/b2; Fig. S10). Si plants were transformed via the floral dip method and successful transformants were selected according to seed‐specific RFP fluorescence, T‐DNA detection, and Cas9 protein detection (Fig. S11). Genotyping of the SiJAX1 sequences identified three independent T1 self‐reproductive lines (L1‐4, L3‐3, and L5‐3) carrying homozygous frameshift mutations in all four SiJAX1 copies (Fig. 7a). These quadruple mutants (sijax1a1/a2/b1/b2) exhibited no significant differences in growth compared with WT Si (Figs 7a, S12), suggesting that JAX1 orthologs have minimal impact on plant growth. To evaluate PlAMV resistance in these mutants, they were mechanically inoculated with PlAMV–sGFP. At 21 dpi, WT Si was resistant to PlAMV, whereas all three quadruple mutant lines were susceptible, with PlAMV–sGFP detected in both inoculated and upper leaves (Fig. 7b,c). To test whether JAX1 knockout leads to susceptibility to another potexvirus, we inoculated alternanthera mosaic virus (AltMV) onto WT and mutant Si plants. The WT plants were resistant, but quadruple mutant lines were also susceptible to AltMV, similar to the PlAMV results (Fig. 7d,e). Therefore, SiJAX1 genes are the key factors restricting potexvirus infection in Si. These results provide direct evidence that JAX1 contributes to potexvirus NHR in Brassicaceae plants.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the genetic and functional conservation of the non‐NLR viral resistance gene JAX1 in plants. We found that JAX1 originated in a Brassicaceae common ancestor and was widely conserved within the family Brassicaceae (Figs 1, 2, 3), and that these orthologs generally retained antiviral activity against potexvirus (Fig. 4). Correlation between the expression patterns of functional JAX1 orthologs and PlAMV resistance implied that JAX1 is a major factor determining NHR to PlAMV among the Brassicaceae (Figs 5, 6). To verify this hypothesis experimentally, we generated potexvirus‐resistant S. irio mutants lacking all JAX1 orthologs and found that these mutants became susceptible to potexvirus infection (Fig. 7). Collectively, these data demonstrate that JAX1 contributes to potexvirus NHR and is a key determinant of potexvirus host ranges within the Brassicaceae.

Previous studies on NHR against plant pathogenic fungi have revealed that NHR consists of multi‐layered resistance responses (Uma et al., 2011; Piasecka et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017). As a major determinant of potexvirus NHR in Brassicaceae plants, JAX1‐mediated resistance represents one layer within these multi‐layered responses. Considering that JAX1 virus‐resistant activity is widely conserved among Brassicaceae plants, JAX1‐mediated resistance may be a universal layer of NHR in the Brassicaceae. Importantly, we found that although JAX1 orthologs were not detected in Aal, it was still completely resistant to PlAMV (Figs 5, 6), indicating the existence of a resistance mechanism other than JAX1‐mediated resistance. Additionally, RNA silencing has been reported to contribute to resistance against potato virus X (PVX) in A. thaliana (Jaubert et al., 2011; Brosseau et al., 2020). Together, these results suggest that there are multiple layers of resistance to potexviruses among Brassicaceae plants.

The components comprising NHR are thought to differ according to interactions between plants and pathogens (Uma et al., 2011; Piasecka et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017). Among these, ETI plays an important role in NHR in nonhost plants that are relatively closely related to a host, while PTI contributes to NHR in distantly related nonhosts (Schulze‐Lefert & Panstruga, 2011). This hypothesis may be applied to plant virus NHR, because NLR resistance genes conferring ETI are responsible for cultivar resistance. Tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV) induces host resistance responses in several nonhost Nicotiana species, which are close to its host plants, tomatoes and peppers. As host resistance is induced by proteins encoded by TBSV, it is thought to be an ETI‐like response (Angel & Schoelz, 2013). Although the concept remains controversial, RNA silencing has been proposed as a candidate for PTI in antiviral immunity (Nakahara & Masuta, 2014) and appears to be a determinant of NHR to PVX in A. thaliana (Jaubert et al., 2011). In this study, we showed that the non‐NLR gene JAX1 represents a layer of potexvirus NHR in Brassicaceae plants. Because the Brassicaceae are not used as natural hosts by potexviruses, RNA silencing and JAX1‐mediated resistance can play a role in NHR in relatively distantly related nonhosts. Recessive resistance can also be involved in plant virus NHR. Nicotiana benthamiana is a nonhost of melon necrotic spot virus due to incompatibility between the cap‐independent translational enhancer and the eIF4E of N. benthamiana (Nieto et al., 2011). Thus, various plant virus resistance, sometimes acting alone and sometimes in combination, constitute plant virus NHR.

Although we revealed that JAX1 orthologs possessing antiviral activity are usually conserved in Brassicaceae plants, their existence in plant genomes and expression levels differ according to plant species. We observed that some Brassicaceae species were susceptible in inoculated leaves, but failed to infect systemically (Figs 5, S8, S9). In these plants, JAX1 expression was clearly detected in roots and stems, whereas it was barely detected in leaves (Figs 6, S8, S9). This detection pattern resembles that of JAX1 in A. thaliana ‘Bay‐0’ (Figs 6, S8, S9) and suggests that, in leaves, JAX1 transcripts may be absent or present at levels insufficient to confer resistance to PlAMV. Also, when the susceptible A. thaliana ecotype ‘Col‐0’ is complemented with JAX1 driven by its native promoter, PlAMV accumulation in inoculated leaves is the same level as those of the WT; however, PlAMV did not infect the upper, uninoculated leaves (Yamaji et al., 2012). It is therefore possible that Brassicaceae species exhibit the same phenomenon and that the high expression of JAX1 in roots interferes with the systemic spread of the potexvirus. In Bs plants, BsJAX1 expression in upper noninoculated leaves might also confer resistance against PlAMV infection in those leaves (Fig. S9). Furthermore, in Ch, ChJAX1 is not detected in either leaves or roots, and they are fully susceptible to PlAMV (Figs 5, 6). These findings are important because both the existence of a resistance gene and its expression and activity are crucial for the induction of resistance. These results highlight the importance of examining genomic sequences, expression levels, and activity, which cannot be obtained from plant genome sequences alone and may make it difficult to identify resistance genes through analyses, such as single‐nucleotide polymorphism‐based genome‐wide association studies.

This study revealed that JAX1 is widely conserved in Brassicaceae plants. However, in certain Brassicaceae species, JAX1 is either absent, pseudogenized, or has lost its expression (Figs 1, 3, 6, 8). Thus, the resistance mechanism against potexviruses may have been important for Brassicaceae plants and inherited through the course of evolution, then eventually become unnecessary and consequently lost. Nonetheless, because JAX1 orthologs are expressed in Brassicaceae plants under noninoculated conditions (Fig. 6), and their expression in inoculated leaves was unchanged upon virus infection (Figs S8, S9), it is possible that JAX1 has another function beyond virus resistance in plants. Several Brassicaceae plants and cultivars lacking the JAX1 gene or its expression have shown normal developmental phenotypes, such that JAX1 may not be essential for normal plant development and metabolism. Rather, it may be associated with stress responses, because some JAL family members confer resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses in monocot and dicot species (Ma et al., 2010; Xiang et al., 2011; Weidenbach et al., 2016; He et al., 2017; Das et al., 2023). In A. thaliana, several JAL genes belonging to Clades II and III (e.g. JAL22, JAL23, JAL31, and JAL32) are involved in the formation of endoplasmic reticulum‐derived bodies, organelles characteristic of the Brassicaceae, Cleomaceae, and other closely related families within order Brassicales (Nagano et al., 2008; Nakano et al., 2014; Fig. S3). Given that some JAL genes have Brassicaceae‐specific functions, JAX1 may also be associated with both antiviral resistance and stress responses unique to the Brassicaceae. Such additional functions may impose fitness costs under certain environmental conditions, which could have contributed to the loss of JAX1 in some Brassicaceae species. Elucidating these additional functions may provide important insights for future applications and utilization of JAX1.

Ancestral Brassicaceae are suggested to be nonhosts by possessing JAX1, but some plants became hosts again through the pseudogenization or downregulation of JAX1 orthologs during diversification. In certain plants, such as Arabis alpina, resistance is observed despite the absence of JAX1‐mediated resistance, implying the existence of an alternative resistance mechanism, the timing of which remains unclear.

JAX1 has practical potential for crop improvement, as transgenic expression confers resistance to pepino mosaic virus in tomato (Okano et al., 2020). However, resistance‐breaking isolates have been experimentally generated in potato virus X (Sugawara et al., 2013), indicating that JAX1‐mediated resistance alone may not always be evolutionarily durable. In this study, we identified multiple JAX1 orthologs in the Brassicaceae that confer resistance to PlAMV (Fig. 4) and exhibit sequence diversity (Fig. 3; Table S11), suggesting that individual orthologs may differ in their effectiveness against diverse potexvirus isolates. Functional comparison and pyramiding of distinct orthologs may provide a strategy to enhance resistance durability and expand antiviral spectra in crops. These findings suggest that the natural repertoire of JAX1 orthologs represents a valuable genetic resource for the rational design of durable virus resistance.

Non‐NLR resistance genes, including JAX1, have been identified from resistant cultivars or ecotypes (Chisholm et al., 2000; Whitham et al., 2000; Ishibashi et al., 2007, 2019; Cosson et al., 2010; Yamaji et al., 2012; Verlaan et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2017). However, their conservation, particularly functional conservation, remains largely unknown. We discovered that JAX1 is conserved in the family Brassicaceae both genetically (Fig. 2) and functionally (Figs 4, S7). Our results suggest that non‐NLR resistance could be functionally conserved across a wide range of plant species and may play a key role in determining pathogen host range. These findings suggest that the currently known repertoire of non‐NLR resistance genes represents only a small fraction of their potential, and that plants may possess unidentified resistance genes that have been overlooked due to high conservation among plant species. These resistance genes could become valuable genetic resources for developing highly durable resistance cultivars.
