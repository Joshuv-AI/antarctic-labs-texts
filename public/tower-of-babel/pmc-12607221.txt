Infection control competency in surgical settings among clinical medical students in Sudan: a cross sectional study
Authors: Maria Badraldin Ali, Ahmad Mohammad Al Zamel, Ola Hatim Elniema, Hala Fathi EmamElkhir Omer, Tawasul Abdelhalim Ibrahim Bilal, Tanzeel Abdullateef Modawi, Qabbas Hani Eltahir, Aiat Osama Elmabrouk Hassan, Amjed Abdel Al, Sami Fatehi Abdalla, Ali Awadallah Saeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) pose a major challenge to healthcare systems worldwide, with a disproportionately higher impact in low- and middle-income countries. Medical clinical students are actively involved in patient care and thus play a vital role in implementing infection prevention protocols. However, deficiencies in their knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding infection control may undermine patient safety and surgical outcomes. This study aimed to assess the KAP of clinical medical students in Sudan concerning infection control measures in surgical settings and to explore perceived barriers and potential strategies for improvement.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 420 clinical medical students from 13 Sudanese universities between November 2024 and February 2025. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire covering demographic data, KAP components and contextual barriers. Scores were categorized into poor, moderate or good levels. Independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to assess the differences in KAP scores across demographic variables.ResultsMost students demonstrated moderate knowledge (73.8%) and practices (50.2%) regarding infection control while 43.8% exhibited a neutral attitude. Notably, Only 28.8% of participants had received formal infection control training. Significant knowledge gaps were observed in hand hygiene duration, personal protective equipment (PPE) sequencing and sterile field management. The most reported barriers were limited supplies (70%) and insufficient training (69.3%). Interestingly, students who had not completed a surgical rotation had significantly higher attitude scores toward infection control compared to those who had (p = 0.008).ConclusionSudanese medical students demonstrate moderate awareness and practice regarding infection control in surgical settings, with notable gaps in training and resource availability. Targeted educational interventions, enhanced supervision, and improved access to infection control supplies are critical to strengthening adherence and promoting safer clinical environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08132-4.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a major global public health concern, contributing significantly to patient morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where infection control infrastructure is often inadequate [1–5]. In Africa, the incidence of SSIs is disproportionately high, with recent systematic reviews confirming a persistent burden across surgical disciplines, including orthopedics and general surgery [6, 7]. The rate of surgical site infections (SSIs) in sub-Saharan Africa is among the highest globally, with reported rates ranging from 2.5% to 30.9% [8]. In Tanzania, SSI prevalence has been documented between 10% and 26%, with a recent 2023 study reporting 26% at Buganda Medical Centre [6]. Across Africa, prevalence varies by region, including 10.2% in West Africa, 12.1% in East and Central Africa, and 7.2% in North Africa for SSIs following cesarean Sect. [7]. Country-specific studies also reflect significant variability, such as 4.2% in Ethiopia and 39% in orthopedic surgeries in Togo. The pooled prevalence of post-cesarean SSIs across African countries is estimated at 11%, with notably higher rates in Tanzania (34.1%) and Uganda (15%) compared to lower rates in Tunisia (5%) and Egypt (5.3%) [6].

Effective infection prevention and control (IPC) practices are essential for reducing healthcare-associated infections and protecting both patients and healthcare workers. Key IPC measures include proper hand hygiene, appropriate use of personal protective equipment (PPE), sterile technique adherence, and safe handling of contaminated instruments [9, 10]. However, compliance with these measures remains inconsistent, especially in surgical environments where time constraints, hierarchical barriers, and limited resources can hinder adherence [11, 12]. In Sudan, healthcare institutions face persistent challenges in implementing effective infection prevention and control (IPC) protocols. Hospitals frequently operate under conditions of resource scarcity with inconsistent access to personal protective equipment (PPE), inadequate sterilization infrastructure and limited institutional enforcement of IPC policies [13, 14]. These issues are compounded in teaching hospitals where high patient loads and constrained supervision create environments where medical trainees may adopt unsafe practices by imitation or necessity. Clinical-year medical students particularly during surgical rotations are at a critical stage of professional development. Their early clinical experiences strongly influence long-term attitudes and behaviors toward patient safety and infection control [15, 16]. Studies have shown that students’ IPC competence is shaped not only by formal instruction but also by exposure to institutional norms and the behavior of senior staff [17]. In LMICs like Sudan, this effect may be amplified by structural limitations and cultural hierarchies, which discourage students from challenging poor practices or requesting support [18, 19]. Despite their close involvement in patient care, medical students in Sudan often receive minimal formal training in IPC, and there is little published data on their preparedness for infection control in surgical settings. Medical students, particularly those in clinical and surgical rotations, are frequently exposed to infection-prone environments and are expected to follow IPC protocols. Yet, multiple studies have shown that their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) are often insufficient, influenced by gaps in training, inadequate supervision, and poor institutional support [20, 21]. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has further underscored the critical importance of strong IPC foundations in surgical education and practice [22, 23]. Despite global efforts to improve IPC compliance, there remains a lack of data from many LMICs, including Sudan. To address this gap, the present study investigates the knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding infection control among Sudanese medical students undergoing surgical training. By identifying key barriers and evaluating the effectiveness of current educational and clinical exposures, this study aims to inform future improvements in surgical IPC training and policy development.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of clinical medical students regarding infection control in surgical settings. This design was selected to capture a birds’ eye view of students’ KAP at a specific point in time which will enable the identification of prevailing strengths and deficiencies. Data collection was conducted between November, 2024, and January, 2025.

Study setting and population

The study was conducted among clinical medical students from 13 Sudanese universities including both public and private institutions. The target population comprised students in their 4th, 5th, and 6th years as they had already been exposed to clinical training and surgical rotations. Students in pre-clinical (1st, 2nd and 3rd) years and those who declined to participate were excluded. The selection of universities was guided by geographical distribution, institutional diversity and the presence of active clinical training programs to ensure a representative sample of medical students across Sudan.

Sampling

The required sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula for an unknown population proportion [24] assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 50% estimated prevalence of adequate knowledge: n = \frac{Z^2 p(1-p)}{e^2}, n = \frac{(1.96)^2 \times 0.5 \times (1–0.5)}{(0.05)^2} = 384.16. To account for potential non-response a larger number of clinical-year medical students were invited to participate in the study. The final sample size exceeded the minimum requirement (420 participants). A stratified random sampling was used to ensure equal representation of students across different universities and academic years. However, the final distribution of responses did not fully reflect equal representation due to variation in response rates across strata.

Data collection and scoring system

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire distributed via Google Forms using academic email lists, student WhatsApp groups and class representatives across the selected universities. The questionnaire (Supplementary 1) was developed based on internationally recognized infection control and healthcare quality guidelines. The content and structure of each item (Supplementary 2) were informed by evidence-based guidelines and validated tools issued by reputable health authorities including the World Health Organization (WHO), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and other global infection prevention and control (IPC) frameworks. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 20 medical students from two different universities in Sudan to identify and address any issues related to clarity, wording or content validity. The pre-test data were not included in the final analysis. The questionnaire consisted of four sections: demographic information, knowledge assessment, attitude assessment, and practice assessment. The demographic section included variables such as age, gender, academic year, university type (public or private), prior clinical rotation in a surgical department, practical experience in surgical settings, and previous training in infection control. The knowledge assessment comprised multiple-choice and true/false questions covering standard infection control protocols, surgical site infection (SSI) prevention, hand hygiene, and personal protective equipment (PPE). Attitudes were measured using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), evaluating students’ perceptions of infection control importance, personal responsibility, and confidence in their training, except for question 5, which was reverse scored (e.g., a rating of 5 was converted to 1) so that higher scores uniformly indicate a more positive attitude toward infection control. The practice assessment focused on self-reported adherence to infection control measures, handling of sterile techniques, and responses to real-life scenarios such as needlestick injuries and contaminated instrument handling.

Scoring was standardized across all KAP components. Knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 19, with one point assigned for each correct response and zero for incorrect responses. Participants who scored 80% or higher were classified as having good knowledge, those scoring between 50% and 79% had moderate knowledge, and those scoring below 50% had poor knowledge. Attitude scores ranged from 9 to 45, with higher scores reflecting more positive attitudes. Participants were categorized as having a positive attitude if they scored 80% or higher, a neutral attitude if they scored between 50% and 79%, and a negative attitude if they scored below 50%. Practice scores, which ranged from 6 to 30, Participants with scores of 80% or higher were classified as having good practice, those scoring between 50% and 79% demonstrated moderate practice, and those below 50% exhibited poor practice. Two additional questions assessed specific infection control behaviors: [1] responses to a needlestick injury and [2] actions taken after accidental contact with a non-sterile surface. These questions were based on established guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), World Health Organization (WHO), and Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN) guidelines on occupational exposure and aseptic technique [1–3]. Since these items were not Likert-scale based, they were analyzed separately as supplementary findings rather than being included in the practice score.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v.22 [25]. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means and standard deviations were used to summarize demographic data and KAP scores. Independent Samples t-test was used to compare mean KAP scores across binary sociodemographic variables with Welch’s correction applied when variances were unequal. One-way ANOVA was employed to compare KAP scores across variables with more than two categories such as academic year. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 420 students completed the questionnaire, exceeding the initially calculated sample size of 384. There were no non-responses or substitutions made to achieve the sample size, as the final number of respondents exceeded the calculated minimum. 50% were from Public Universities and 50% from Private Universities. Of them, 82.4% were in the age group 21–25, and 67.4% were female. Additionally, 51.4% were in their 5th year of study and the rest were in the 4th and 6th years. 44% had completed a clinical rotation in the surgical department, and 41.9% had practical experience in surgical settings. However, only 28.8% had received formal training in infection control [Table 1].

Participant’s knowledge of infection control

The majority of participants (92.6%) recognized the use of sterile gloves, face masks, and hand hygiene as standard precautions in infection control. However, there were clear knowledge gaps in some areas. Just 24.3% of respondents recognized that hand washing should last at least two minutes, and only 33.8% knew that surgical masks primarily protect surgeons from patient infections. 41.4% of respondents knew that high-touch surfaces in the operating room must be cleaned continuously. Only 33.6% and 36.9%, respectively, knew the proper order for donning and taking off personal safety equipment (PPE). Alarmingly, 14.8% of those surveyed claimed that hand washing with soap and water was only required in cases of visible contamination.

In terms of the sterile field, 78.6% of respondents correctly recognized that it includes the floor surrounding the operating bed, the patient’s body, and the instrument table. Of those surveyed, 72.4% recognized that contaminated gloves should be replaced, but only 51.4% knew that infected surgical equipment must be sterilized immediately. Regarding commonly used antiseptic solutions, 73.6% of respondents answered correctly, with 52.6% identifying alcohol-based rub and 21% identifying chlorhexidine as commonly used antiseptics. However, less than half (37.4%) of respondents acknowledged MRSA as the pathogen most commonly associated with surgical site infections. Additionally, 43.6% and 28.1% of respondents accurately pointed out that “after aseptic procedures” and “After septic procedures”, respectively are not included in the “five moments for hand hygiene.” [Table 2]. Overall, the mean knowledge score (SD) was 11.2 (2.4) with a range of 3–19. 73.8% of participants demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge, 22.1% had a poor level, and only 4% exhibited a good level of knowledge [Fig. 1].

Attitudes toward infection control in surgical settings

Half of the participants strongly agreed that strict adherence to infection control protocols is crucial for ensuring patient safety and preventing complications. 44.8% strongly agreed that following infection control guidelines improves patient trust in healthcare services, and only 50.5% strongly agreed that following these guidelines is a personal responsibility. However, only 12.9% strongly agreed that infection control training is sufficiently covered in their curriculum, and less than a fifth (14.8%) strongly felt confident in their comprehension of proper protocols for maintaining a sterile field. Similarly, just 13.1% believed their training had prepared them well to adhere to infection-control protocols. A minority (7.9%) strongly viewed infection control measures in surgical settings as occasionally excessive or impractical. Furthermore, only 22.4% reported feeling comfortable addressing breaches in infection control practices by peers or superiors [Table 3]. Overall, the attitude score ranged from 13 to 45, mean SD of 31.0 (8.4) (Table 3). The majority of participants (43.8%) demonstrated a neutral attitude, while 36.7% exhibited a positive attitude and 19.5% displayed a negative attitude. [Fig. 2].

Practice and compliance

The findings revealed that 39.8%, 40.7%, and 42.6% of clinical-year medical students consistently practiced proper hand hygiene, wore appropriate PPE, and adhered to correct procedures for handling contaminated instruments, respectively. Around one-third expressed high likelihood of following PPE guidelines despite limited resources (32.4%) and reporting breaches in sterile technique (31.7%). However, only 25.5% expressed high confidence in maintaining infection control in busy surgical settings [Table 4]. Overall, the mean Practice score (SD) was 23.0 (4.3). Only 3.6% showed negative practice, 46.2% showed good practice, and the majority 50.2% showed moderate practice [Fig. 3]. Notably, 34.5% had experienced needle-stick injuries, yet only 20.2% followed correct post-injury protocols [Fig. 4]. When faced with accidental contact with non-sterile surfaces, 83.3% chose the appropriate response (changing gloves) while 16.7% selected incorrect actions[Fig. 5].

Barriers to infection control and methods for improving adherence

The most frequently reported barrier is the lack of supplies reported by 70% of the participants followed by insufficient training (69.3%) and lack of supervision (55.5%) [Fig. 6]. Regular infection control training sessions, easy access to PPE and other infection control supplies, more supervision or guidance from faculty and access to online infection control modules were reported by 86.2%, 81.9%, 79% and 67.1% of the participants, respectively as methods that can improve adherence to infection control practices among Sudanese medical students [Fig. 7].

Differences in KAP scores according to sociodemographic characteristics

Among all sociodemographic characteristics and KAP scores. Only the attitude score showed a significant difference based on the completion of a clinical rotation in the surgical department. The Independent Student t-test indicated that students who had not completed the rotation had significantly higher mean attitude scores (31.94) compared to those who had completed it (29.80) (T = 2.620, P = 0.009) [Table 5 ].

Discussions

The current study assessed the general knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of Sudanese clinical medical medical students regarding infection control in surgical settings. Overall, the majority demonstrated moderate knowledge (73.8%) and practice (50.2%) levels, while 43.8% had a neutral attitude. Key deficiencies were noted in hand hygiene, PPE sequencing, sterile field management and post-exposure responses. Notably, only 28.8% of participants had received formal infection control training. The findings reveal a concerning trend of moderate knowledge, mixed attitudes, and suboptimal practices, with only a small proportion of participants achieving high scores across all domains in the questions. These outcomes echo existing literature and highlight persistent gaps in understanding infection control and compliance among healthcare trainees in both low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and globally. This aligns with findings from similar contexts. Chauhan (2017) reported that Indian medical students showed poor awareness of standard precautions, hindering their capacity to apply infection control measures effectively [13]. Similarly, Alshammari et al. (2024) identified moderate knowledge levels among Saudi medical students, attributing this to insufficient emphasis on infection control within medical curricula [21]. These gaps are particularly alarming given the high prevalence of surgical site infections (SSIs) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The World Health Organization (WHO) states that proper hand hygiene could prevent up to 50% of healthcare-associated infections, yet it remains underutilized in resource-constrained settings [15, 16]. Moreover, Allegranzi et al. (2011) emphasized that the incidence of such infections is significantly higher in LMICs compared to high-income countries, highlighting the urgent need for strengthened infection control education [17].

Although the majority of students acknowledged the importance of infection control, their responses revealed attitudinal ambivalence. Fewer than half expressed confidence in their training or felt adequately prepared to address breaches in infection control, and only around 20% reported feeling comfortable confronting peers or superiors about noncompliance. Similar reluctance has been noted in other cultural settings. For example, Singh et al. (2021), in a qualitative study conducted in India and South Africa, reported that hierarchical team dynamics discouraged junior staff from speaking up, thereby weakening the enforcement of infection prevention protocols [18].

Interestingly, our analysis revealed that students who had not completed surgical rotations had significantly higher attitude scores than those who had, suggesting that direct clinical exposure might contribute to disillusionment or perceived impracticality of infection control protocols. This aligns with Troughton et al. (2019), who observed that lack of shared team ownership and strong hierarchies reduced motivation for infection control compliance in surgical teams [19].

The practice domain results were mixed, with most students falling into moderate or good categories, but few achieving consistently high scores. Nonchalancy, over one-third had experienced a needle stick injury, yet only 20.2% followed proper post-exposure protocols. This mirrors trends seen in multiple studies and raises serious concerns about occupational safety awareness [14, 21, 26]. The CDC underscores the importance of immediate and appropriate action following such incidents to mitigate the risk of blood borne pathogen transmission [27, 28].

Additionally, participants cited limited access to PPE and insufficient supervision as top barriers to compliance. WHO and CDC guidelines stress the need for reliable infrastructure, consistent supply chains, and institutional accountability as prerequisites for effective infection prevention [15, 16, 27]. Encouragingly, students identified structured infection control training, better access to supplies, and increased supervision as key strategies for improvement. Pelzer et al. (2021) showed that implementing practical, structured learning experiences significantly improved healthcare students’ infection control behavior [29]. Similarly, Righi et al. (2024) emphasized the value of evidence-based guidelines and targeted prophylaxis for high-risk cases in clinical settings [30].

Global and national infection control guidelines, such as those from the WHO and the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), advocate for multifaceted approaches including preoperative decolonization, continuous education, and institutional leadership in infection control programs [16, 30]. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic emphasized the urgency of robust infection prevention systems in surgical settings. Zhang et al. (2021) and Moletta et al. (2020) highlighted adaptive protocols and international collaboration as critical for maintaining safe surgical practices during health crises [31, 32].

Notably, students who had completed a surgical rotation demonstrated significantly lower attitude scores (p = 0.009) which suggest the current clinical training environments may not adequately reinforce positive attitudes toward infection prevention. In addition, the near significant difference in attitudes by university type (p = 0.077) highlights potential institutional disparities that may influence IPC learning. Given these findings, Sudanese medical education institutions should prioritize comprehensive infection control training both in theoretical and practical formats. The integration of case-based learning, supervised clinical simulations and e-learning modules aligned with WHO and CDC standards could bridge current knowledge and practice gaps. Ensuring access to PPE and fostering a culture of shared responsibility, transparency and peer accountability will be crucial. The implementation of context-specific guidelines, such as those proposed by Badia et al. (2020) [33] for general surgery or Bali (2021) for operating room protocols, could further strengthen infection control culture among surgical trainees [34]. Institutions should also address psychological and hierarchical barriers that prevent students from reporting breaches and promote interprofessional collaboration in clinical environments.

Study implications

The study underscores the urgent need to enhance infection control education within Sudanese medical schools. It highlights significant gaps in medical students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding infection control, particularly in surgical settings. The findings suggest that improving curricula with structured, simulation-based training, providing better supervision during surgical rotations, and ensuring adequate resources such as PPE are essential to enhancing students’ preparedness. Furthermore, fostering a culture of open communication and confidence in addressing infection control breaches will be critical in improving adherence to safety protocols. These improvements not only have implications for patient safety but also for strengthening the broader healthcare system’s capacity to respond to both routine surgical risks and future public health challenges.

Limitations

This study relied on self-reported data which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Its cross-sectional design limits assessment of long-term knowledge or behavioral changes. Additionally, while students from various universities were included, findings may not be fully generalizable to all settings or practicing clinicians. Furthermore, the inability to calculate a response rate may also affect the generalizability. Online nature of data collection may have introduced a selection bias favoring students with reliable internet access.

Conclusion

The current study underscores significant gaps in the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of Sudanese medical students regarding infection control in surgical settings. While the majority exhibited moderate awareness and adherence to infection control practices very few achieved high standards across all aspects. The main challenges identified were insufficient formal training, limited practical exposure, and attitudinal barriers such as low confidence in addressing breaches in sterile technique. These findings highlight the pressing need to strengthen infection control education within Sudanese medical schools’ curricula. Integrating structured, simulation-based training, increasing supervision during surgical rotations and ensuring access to infection control resources could substantially improve students’ preparedness and compliance. Enhancing infection control training for medical students is essential not only for safeguarding patient safety but also for equipping the future healthcare workforce to tackle both routine surgical risks and potential public health emergencies. Future research should investigate why attitudes decline after surgical exposure and whether surgical training environments provide sufficient reinforcement of IPC principles.
