Lepidic pattern as a favorable prognostic factor in pathologically high-risk stage I invasive lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zhaokang Huang, Jie Cao, Nan Chen, Zhiyu Peng, Zetao Liu, Xizhou Liao, Xiaorong Zong, Peidi Li, Chenglin Guo, Lunxu Liu, Jiandong Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic value of lepidic pattern in small stage I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) with pathological high-risk features remains unclear. This study evaluated whether lepidic pattern was associated with improved survival outcomes in this pathologically high-risk population.MethodsConsecutive patients with surgically resected stage I invasive LUAD measuring ≤3 cm at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2018 and April 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients with at least one pathological high-risk feature were included and stratified according to the presence or absence of lepidic pattern. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were compared before and after propensity score matching. Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 1,233 patients were included, including 412 without lepidic pattern and 821 with lepidic pattern. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 315 matched pairs were generated. The median follow-up duration was 44 months. In the overall cohort, patients with lepidic pattern had better RFS than those without lepidic pattern, with 3-year RFS rates of 96.8% and 87.0%, respectively, and estimated 5-year RFS rates of 94.0% and 81.9%, respectively (HR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.70; P<0.001). This association persisted after matching, with 3-year RFS rates of 96.3% and 86.6%, respectively, and estimated 5-year RFS rates of 92.2% and 81.2%, respectively (HR, 0.37; 95% CI: 0.20–0.69; P=0.001). In multivariable analysis, lepidic pattern remained independently associated with improved RFS (adjusted HR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.70; P=0.001). OS was not significantly associated with lepidic pattern after matching or multivariable adjustment.ConclusionsLepidic pattern was independently associated with improved RFS, but not OS, in patients with pathologically high-risk small stage I LUAD. Lepidic pattern may serve as a complementary histologic marker for postoperative recurrence risk stratification.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lepidic pattern, pathological high-risk features, recurrence-free survival (RFS), prognosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• Lepidic pattern was independently associated with improved recurrence-free survival in patients with pathologically high-risk small stage I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), but was not independently associated with overall survival.

What is known and what is new?

• Histologic architecture and pathological high-risk features are important for postoperative risk stratification in early-stage LUAD. Previous studies have suggested that lepidic growth is associated with favorable outcomes in broader early-stage populations.

• This study shows that lepidic pattern retains favorable recurrence-related prognostic value even among patients already harboring pathological high-risk features.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Lepidic pattern may serve as a complementary histologic marker for refining postoperative recurrence risk stratification in pathologically high-risk small stage I LUAD. It should be interpreted alongside established adverse pathological factors rather than used as a standalone determinant of postoperative management.

Introduction

Early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is primarily managed with complete surgical resection and is generally associated with favorable postoperative outcomes. However, recurrence still occurs in a clinically relevant subset of patients, even among those with small stage I tumors, particularly when adverse pathological features are present (1). Pathological high-risk features, including lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, poor differentiation, and high-grade histologic patterns, have been associated with increased recurrence risk and may help identify patients who require more individualized postoperative risk assessment (1-6).

LUAD is characterized by substantial histologic heterogeneity, and architectural patterns provide prognostic information beyond tumor size and stage (7). The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system incorporates histologic patterns into prognostic stratification, with lepidic-predominant tumors generally classified as low grade and tumors containing substantial solid or micropapillary components classified as higher grade (8-11). Previous studies have suggested that lepidic growth is associated with favorable outcomes in early-stage LUAD (12-15). However, most available evidence has been derived from unselected early-stage cohorts, rather than from patients enriched for pathological high-risk features.

Whether lepidic pattern retains favorable prognostic value in patients who already harbor pathological high-risk features remains unclear (12,16). This question is clinically relevant because pathologically high-risk patients are not biologically homogeneous, and recurrence risk may vary even among patients with a similar burden of adverse features. In this context, focusing on patients with pathological high-risk features allowed us to assess whether lepidic pattern provides additional prognostic information beyond conventional adverse pathological factors, rather than simply reflecting the favorable prognosis of low-risk tumors.

In this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the prognostic significance of lepidic pattern in patients with completely resected stage I invasive LUAD measuring ≤3 cm and at least one pathological high-risk feature. We compared recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) between patients with and without lepidic pattern before and after propensity score matching, and further assessed whether lepidic pattern remained independently associated with survival outcomes after adjustment for clinicopathological variables and the number of pathological high-risk features. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0770/rc).

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2026 Review [1374]). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All data were anonymized before analysis.

Study population

Consecutive patients with surgically resected stage I invasive LUAD measuring ≤3 cm at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2018 and April 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. Pathological staging was determined according to the eighth edition of the TNM classification. The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Flow diagram of patient selection and propensity score matching. LVI, lymphovascular invasion; PSM, propensity score matching; STAS, spread through air spaces; VPI, visceral pleural invasion.

Patients were eligible if they had pathologically confirmed invasive LUAD, pathological stage I disease, a maximum tumor diameter of ≤3 cm, complete surgical resection with curative intent, available information on histologic pattern and pathological high-risk features, at least one pathological high-risk feature, and available follow-up data. Patients were excluded if pathological high-risk features could not be reliably assessed, if they had received neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, had invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma, or had unavailable follow-up data. Patients with missing or indeterminate pathological high-risk feature status were excluded because the primary analysis required reliable ascertainment of all predefined high-risk features; incomplete or inconsistent documentation, particularly for features that were not uniformly reported in earlier pathology reports, could have led to exposure misclassification. Patients who received neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy were excluded to minimize treatment-related confounding of recurrence and survival outcomes, because treatment decisions and treatment effects may both be associated with pathological risk profile and prognosis. Patients without any pathological high-risk feature were excluded from the analytic cohort.

Demographic and clinicopathological data were extracted from the electronic medical record system and final pathology reports. Variables included age, sex, smoking history, tumor diameter, surgical procedure, lepidic pattern, and the number of pathological high-risk features.

Definition of lepidic pattern and pathological high-risk features

Lepidic pattern was determined based on the final pathology report and analyzed as a binary variable. Patients were classified as having lepidic pattern if any lepidic growth pattern was identified in the tumor, regardless of whether it represented the predominant histologic pattern. Patients without any reported lepidic growth pattern were classified as having no lepidic pattern. Accordingly, this study evaluated the presence or absence of lepidic pattern rather than lepidic-predominant adenocarcinoma. Quantitative percentages of lepidic growth were not consistently available in routine pathology reports during the study period; therefore, a reliable analysis according to lepidic proportion could not be performed. To minimize misclassification from inconsistently reported semiquantitative estimates, lepidic pattern was predefined and analyzed as a presence-or-absence variable (16,17).

Pathological high-risk features were defined as the presence of at least one of the following: lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, poor differentiation, and/or high-grade histologic pattern (1,5,8,9). Lymphovascular invasion was defined as the presence of tumor cells within lymphatic or vascular spaces. Visceral pleural invasion was defined as tumor extension beyond the elastic layer of the visceral pleura. Spread through air spaces was defined as tumor cells spreading within air spaces beyond the edge of the main tumor (5). High-grade histologic pattern was defined as the presence of high-grade architectural components, including solid and/or micropapillary patterns, according to the pathology report and contemporary International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC)/World Health Organization (WHO) histologic grading principles (8-11). Poor differentiation was recorded according to the original pathological diagnosis. In routine pathology reports, poor differentiation reflected the overall differentiation status assigned by the reporting pathologist, whereas high-grade histologic pattern referred specifically to the presence of high-grade architectural components. Although these two variables may be related, they were recorded as distinct pathological descriptors in the source pathology reports and were therefore retained as separate high-risk features.

For subsequent analyses, pathological high-risk burden was quantified as the number of high-risk features present in each patient and categorized as one, two, or three or more high-risk features.

Follow-up and outcomes

Follow-up information was obtained from routine postoperative outpatient visits and supplemented by telephone follow-up when necessary. In our institutional practice, patients were generally followed every 3 to 6 months during the first 2 years after surgery and annually thereafter. Follow-up evaluations included chest computed tomography, with additional examinations performed when recurrence or metastasis was suspected.

The primary outcome was RFS, defined as the interval from surgery to the first documented recurrence or metastasis. Patients without documented recurrence or metastasis were censored at the date of last follow-up. The secondary outcome was OS, defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up were censored at the date of last contact. Recurrence or metastasis was determined based on radiological, pathological, or clinical evidence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.2, with the survival, survminer, MatchIt, and tableone packages. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Propensity score matching was performed to reduce baseline imbalance between patients with and without lepidic pattern. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included age, sex, smoking history, tumor diameter, surgical procedure, and the number of pathological high-risk features. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement. Covariate balance after matching was assessed by comparing baseline characteristics, and survival analyses were repeated in the propensity score-matched cohort.

Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed in the overall cohort to evaluate whether lepidic pattern was independently associated with RFS and OS. The primary models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, tumor diameter, surgical procedure, and the number of pathological high-risk features. In addition to the primary models using the number of pathological high-risk features to represent high-risk burden, additional multivariable Cox models including individual pathological high-risk features were performed to evaluate whether the association between lepidic pattern and survival outcomes remained consistent after accounting for specific high-risk features. These additional models included lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, high-grade histologic pattern, and poor differentiation as separate covariates. Subgroup analyses were performed according to pathological high-risk burden, dichotomized as a single high-risk feature versus multiple high-risk features (≥2), and interaction was assessed by including an interaction term between lepidic pattern and high-risk burden in the Cox model. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection

Between January 2018 and April 2025, 6,449 patients with stage I invasive LUAD measuring ≤3 cm who underwent complete surgical resection were screened. After application of the predefined exclusion criteria, 4,024 patients remained eligible for further assessment. Among them, 2,791 patients without pathological high-risk features were excluded, leaving 1,233 patients in the final analytic cohort. Of these, 412 were classified as having no lepidic pattern and 821 as having lepidic pattern. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 315 matched pairs were generated. The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics before and after propensity score matching are summarized in Table 1. In the overall cohort, the median age was 61.0 years (IQR, 53.0–68.0 years), and 677 patients (54.9%) were female. The median tumor diameter was 1.70 cm (IQR, 1.30–2.20 cm). Lobectomy was performed in 826 patients (67.0%), whereas 407 patients (33.0%) underwent sublobar resection. Regarding pathological high-risk burden, 685 patients (55.6%) had one high-risk feature, 296 (24.0%) had two, and 252 (20.4%) had three or more.

Before matching, several baseline characteristics differed between patients with and without lepidic pattern. Patients with lepidic pattern were more frequently female (58.7% vs. 47.3%; P<0.001), more commonly never-smokers (75.8% vs. 66.7%; P=0.001), and more likely to undergo sublobar resection (38.5% vs. 22.1%; P<0.001). They also had a lower pathological high-risk burden, with three or more high-risk features observed in 9.4% of patients with lepidic pattern and 42.5% of those without lepidic pattern (P<0.001). Age and tumor diameter were comparable between groups.

After 1:1 propensity score matching, 315 matched pairs were generated. Baseline imbalance was substantially reduced, and no significant differences remained between groups in age, sex, smoking history, tumor diameter, surgical procedure, or number of pathological high-risk features.

Follow-up and survival events

The median follow-up duration was 44 months (IQR, 19–59 months). During follow-up, 85 patients (6.9%) developed recurrence or metastasis, including 56 of 412 patients without lepidic pattern and 29 of 821 patients with lepidic pattern. A total of 48 deaths (3.9%) were observed, including 28 in patients without lepidic pattern and 20 in patients with lepidic pattern. Given this follow-up duration, both 3-year survival rates and estimated 5-year survival rates were reported to provide a more complete interpretation of survival outcomes.

RFS before and after propensity score matching

In the overall cohort before propensity score matching, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly better RFS in patients with lepidic pattern than in those without lepidic pattern (P<0.001; Figure 2A). The 3-year RFS rate was 96.8% (95% CI: 95.0–97.9%) in patients with lepidic pattern and 87.0% (95% CI: 82.8–90.3%) in those without lepidic pattern, and the estimated 5-year RFS rates were 94.0% and 81.9%, respectively. Cox analysis showed that lepidic pattern was associated with a lower risk of recurrence or metastasis (HR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.70).

Tick marks indicate censored observations; numbers at risk are shown below each panel. CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; PSM, propensity score matching; RFS, recurrence-free survival.

After propensity score matching, the RFS benefit associated with lepidic pattern remained evident (P=0.001; Figure 2B). The 3-year RFS rate was 96.3% (95% CI: 92.9–98.1%) in patients with lepidic pattern and 86.6% (95% CI: 81.6–90.3%) in those without lepidic pattern, and the estimated 5-year RFS rates were 92.2% and 81.2%, respectively. A similar association was observed in Cox analysis (HR, 0.37; 95% CI: 0.20–0.69).

OS before and after propensity score matching

In the overall cohort before propensity score matching, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed more favorable OS in patients with lepidic pattern than in those without lepidic pattern (log-rank P=0.01; Figure 3A). The 3-year OS rate was 98.4% (95% CI: 97.1–99.2%) in patients with lepidic pattern and 95.7% (95% CI: 92.8–97.4%) in those without lepidic pattern, and the estimated 5-year OS rates were 96.8% and 90.8%, respectively. The corresponding Cox estimate was HR, 0.64 (95% CI: 0.33–1.26).

Tick marks indicate censored observations; numbers at risk are shown below each panel. CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival; PSM, propensity score matching.

After propensity score matching, the OS difference was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (log-rank P=0.15; Figure 3B). The 3-year OS rate was 98.9% (95% CI: 96.6–99.6%) in patients with lepidic pattern and 96.3% (95% CI: 93.0–98.1%) in those without lepidic pattern, and the estimated 5-year OS rates were 96.3% and 90.8%, respectively, with a corresponding HR of 0.53 (95% CI: 0.22–1.25).

Multivariable Cox regression analysis

In multivariable Cox regression analysis, lepidic pattern remained independently associated with improved RFS after adjustment for age, sex, smoking history, tumor diameter, surgical procedure, and the number of pathological high-risk features (adjusted HR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.70; P=0.001). In contrast, lepidic pattern was not significantly associated with OS after adjustment for the same covariates (adjusted HR, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.33–1.25; P=0.19).

In an additional multivariable Cox model including individual pathological high-risk features rather than the number of high-risk features, lepidic pattern remained independently associated with improved RFS (adjusted HR, 0.43; 95% CI: 0.25–0.73; P=0.002), but was not significantly associated with OS (adjusted HR, 0.70; 95% CI: 0.34–1.41; P=0.32; Table S1). Among individual pathological high-risk features, lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, and poor differentiation were independently associated with RFS, whereas lymphovascular invasion and poor differentiation were independently associated with OS.

In subgroup analyses stratified by pathological high-risk burden, lepidic pattern remained associated with improved RFS both in patients with a single high-risk feature (adjusted HR, 0.24; 95% CI: 0.11–0.51; P<0.001) and in those with multiple high-risk features (adjusted HR, 0.47; 95% CI: 0.26–0.86; P=0.02), with no statistically significant interaction by high-risk burden (P for interaction =0.12; Table S2). For OS, lepidic pattern was not significantly associated with survival in either subgroup.

Discussion

This study evaluated the prognostic significance of lepidic pattern in patients with completely resected small stage I invasive LUAD harboring pathological high-risk features. Lepidic pattern was consistently associated with improved RFS in the overall cohort and after propensity score matching, and this association remained significant after adjustment for major clinicopathological variables and the number of pathological high-risk features. In contrast, lepidic pattern was not independently associated with OS. These findings suggest that lepidic pattern may identify a favorable recurrence-related prognostic subgroup within a population traditionally considered at increased pathological risk.

Our findings are consistent with the established view that histologic architecture provides clinically relevant prognostic information in LUAD (7-11). The IASLC grading system and subsequent validation studies have shown that lepidic-predominant tumors generally exhibit less aggressive behavior, whereas solid and micropapillary patterns are associated with higher-grade disease and poorer outcomes (8-11). In this context, the favorable association between lepidic pattern and RFS observed in our cohort is consistent with the biological and prognostic gradient reflected by histologic architecture. Importantly, our study extends this concept to a more specific population: patients with small stage I invasive LUAD who already harbor pathological high-risk features.

Previous studies have generally supported the favorable prognostic implication of lepidic growth in early-stage LUAD (12-14). A higher proportion or presence of lepidic growth has been associated with improved outcomes, whereas a low proportion or absence of lepidic growth has been linked to less favorable prognosis in selected stage I populations. However, the prognostic impact of lepidic growth may vary according to lepidic proportion, invasive tumor size, and histologic context (14,15). Most previous studies included broader early-stage cohorts in which patients without adverse pathological features were also represented. The present study extends these observations by showing that lepidic pattern remains prognostically meaningful even after restricting the analysis to patients with pathological high-risk features. The relatively strong association between lepidic pattern and improved RFS may reflect the coexistence of favorable and adverse architectural signals within the same tumor. Even in tumors harboring pathological high-risk features, the presence of lepidic growth may indicate a residual non-destructive growth pattern and a less invasive histologic background. However, because lepidic pattern was analyzed as a binary variable, this finding should be interpreted as an association with a composite histologic phenotype rather than as evidence that minimal lepidic growth itself has a causal protective effect.

Pathological high-risk features are commonly used to identify early-stage patients at increased risk of recurrence (1-6). Features such as lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, poor differentiation, and high-grade histologic patterns have been associated with aggressive tumor behavior (1,5,8,9). However, these features do not define a biologically uniform group. Patients with a similar number of adverse pathological features may still differ in tumor architecture and invasive potential. In the present study, lepidic pattern remained associated with improved RFS after matching for the number of high-risk features and after additional adjustment for individual high-risk features, including lymphovascular invasion, visceral pleural invasion, spread through air spaces, high-grade histologic pattern, and poor differentiation. These findings suggest that lepidic pattern provides prognostic information beyond the overall burden and specific type of conventional high-risk features.

The subgroup analysis also showed a consistent RFS association in patients with a single high-risk feature and in those with multiple high-risk features, with no statistically significant interaction by high-risk burden. Therefore, the recurrence-related prognostic value of lepidic pattern was not limited to patients with only one adverse pathological feature. Nevertheless, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because of limited event numbers.

Clinically, these results suggest that lepidic pattern may help refine postoperative recurrence risk stratification among patients who already meet pathological high-risk criteria. The presence of lepidic pattern may indicate a relatively favorable recurrence-risk profile, whereas its absence may identify patients at higher recurrence risk despite similar tumor size and high-risk burden. Therefore, lepidic pattern should be interpreted as a complementary histologic feature rather than as a replacement for established pathological risk factors.

The OS findings should be interpreted with caution. Although patients with lepidic pattern appeared to have more favorable OS in the overall cohort, this association was no longer significant after propensity score matching or multivariable adjustment. This finding is not unexpected in small stage I invasive LUAD, in which long-term survival is generally favorable and death events are relatively infrequent. In addition, OS may be influenced by competing risks, comorbidities, subsequent therapies, and non-cancer-related mortality. RFS may therefore be a more sensitive endpoint for evaluating the prognostic impact of histologic pattern in this setting. The estimated 5-year RFS and OS rates should also be interpreted in the context of the median follow-up duration of 44 months. Although Kaplan-Meier analysis allows estimation of survival probabilities beyond the median follow-up time, the number of patients remaining at risk decreases over time. Therefore, we additionally reported 3-year RFS and OS rates, which are more directly supported by the available follow-up duration.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Patients with missing or indeterminate pathological high-risk feature status were excluded to ensure reliable exposure definition, and patients who received neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy were excluded to reduce treatment-related confounding. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting surgically treated pathologically high-risk stage I LUAD patients who did not receive perioperative systemic therapy. Second, pathological assessment was based on routine clinical reports rather than centralized pathology review, which may have introduced interobserver variability (18). Third, lepidic pattern was analyzed as a binary variable because quantitative percentages of lepidic growth were not consistently available; therefore, the prognostic impact of lepidic proportion and a potential dose-response relationship could not be evaluated. Fourth, high-risk burden was quantified by the number of high-risk features, which is clinically practical but does not fully account for the different prognostic weights of individual features. Although we performed an additional multivariable Cox analysis including individual high-risk features, more refined risk models are needed. Finally, molecular alterations, including EGFR and KRAS status, were not available. The high proportion of never-smokers is consistent with surgically treated East Asian LUAD cohorts, but may limit generalizability to populations with different smoking profiles and molecular landscapes. Future multicenter studies with longer follow-up, standardized quantitative histologic assessment, and integrated molecular profiling are warranted.

Overall, this study suggests that lepidic pattern retains prognostic relevance in a pathologically high-risk subset of small stage I invasive LUAD. These findings support a more nuanced approach to postoperative recurrence risk stratification, in which favorable histologic features are considered alongside adverse pathological factors.

Conclusions

In patients with completely resected small stage I invasive LUAD harboring pathological high-risk features, lepidic pattern was independently associated with improved RFS, but not OS. These findings suggest that lepidic pattern may serve as a complementary histologic marker for refining postoperative recurrence risk stratification in this pathologically high-risk population. Further multicenter studies with longer follow-up and quantitative pathological assessment are warranted to validate these findings.
