Introduction

Toric codes are the natural extensions of Reed-Solomon codes. They were originally introduced by Hansen for two-dimensional spaces, (see
[
2
]
and
[

]
), and subsequently studied by various authors including Joyner
[

, Little and Schenck
[

[

[

[

\mathbb{F}_{q}

be an integral convex polytope, which is contained in the
m

[0,q-2]^{m}

To determine if a given code is “good" from a coding theoretic perspective, one considers parameters associated to the code: the block length, the dimension, and the minimum distance (see Section
2

N=(q-1)^{m}
, and the dimension of the code is given by the number of lattice points
k=|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}|

7

[
2
]
,
[
3
]
), Little and Schenck determined upper and lower bounds for the minimum distance of a toric surface code by examining Minkowski sum decompositions
[
5
]
, and Soprunov and Soprunova improved these bounds for surface codes by examining the Minkowski length
[
8
]
. In the higher dimensional case, Little and Schwarz used Vandermonde matrices to compute minimum distances of simplices and rectangular boxes
[
6
]
, and Soprunov and Soprunova extended these results in
[
9

In this paper, we will focus on integral convex polytopes
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the toric codes they define. We begin in Section
3

by investigating the Minkowski length of integral convex polytopes in the plane, and how polytope operations, such as dilation and Minkowski sum, effect the Minkowski length. In particular, we compute the Minkowski length of certain classes of polytopes. We further investigate which polytopes are of period

Building on the results of Soprunov and Soprunova

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Linear Codes

We begin with some basics from coding theory.
Fix a prime power
q
, a
linear code

C
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes the finite field with
q
elements.
Any
{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C
is called a
codeword
of
C
and
n
is called the
block length
of
C
.
Since
C
is a vector space, we define the
dimension
of the code
C
to simply be the dimension of
C
as a vector space:
k=\dim C
.
Intuitively, the dimension of a linear code represents the amount of information that a codeword contains.

Given two code words
a,b\in C
the
Hamming distance
between
a
and
b
is the number of non-zero entries in
a-b
.
The
Hamming weight
of
{a}\in C
is simply the number of non-zero entries in
{a}
.
The
minimum distance
of
C
, denoted
d(C)
, is the minimum Hamming distance between pairs of distinct codewords.
Since
C
is linear, this is equivalent to the minimum Hamming weight of any non-zero codeword. Intuitively, the minimum distance of the code represents the number of errors the code can correct.

2.2.
Newton Polytopes and Minkowski Sum

Let
f\in\Bbbk[t_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{m}^{\pm 1}],
where
\Bbbk
is a field.
Write

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}c_{\alpha}t^{\alpha}

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
and
t^{\alpha}=t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots t_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
.
The
Newton polytope
of
f
, denoted
P_{f}
, is defined as

P_{f}=\operatorname{conv}\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}:c_{\alpha}\neq 0\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Note that there are only finitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with
c_{\alpha}\neq 0
.
Therefore,
P_{f}
is an integral convex polytope.

Recall the
Minkowski sum
of two polytopes
P,Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is defined as

P+Q=\left\{p+q:p\in P,q\in Q\right\}.

Being able to write a polytope,
P
, as the Minkowski sum of “simpler” polytopes allows for factorization of any Laurent polynomial whose Newton polytope is
P
. In the case of Newton polytopes, note that
P_{fg}=P_{f}+P_{g}.

By a
primitive line segment
we mean a line segment whose only lattice points are the endpoints. If a polytope is a Minkowski sum of primitive lattice segments, we’ll call it a
zonotope
.

2.3.
Toric Codes

A
toric code
is defined as follows: fix an integral convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
\mathcal{L}_{P}
denote the set of all
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{m}^{\pm 1}]
such that
P_{f}\subseteq P
or, equivalently,

\mathcal{L}_{P}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left\{t^{\alpha}:\alpha\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}\right\}.

Define the evaluation map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\operatorname{ev}:\mathcal{L}_{P}&\to&\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(q-1)^{m}}\\
f&\mapsto&\left(f(\gamma):\gamma\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{m}\right)\end{array}.

The
toric code associated to
P
, denoted by
C_{P}
, is defined to be the image of
\operatorname{ev}
.
It is clear that the block length of
C_{P}
is
n=(q-1)^{m}
, and by
[
7
]
, the dimension of
C_{P}
is
\#P
where
\#P:=|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}|
is the number of lattice points of
P
.
Computing the minimum distance for
C_{P}
is a harder problem. Note, by definition

d(C_{P})=(q-1)^{m}-\max_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{L}_{P}}|Z(f)|

where
Z(f)
denotes the set of all
\gamma\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{m}
such that
f(\gamma)=0
.

2.4.
Minkowski Length

Associated to an integral convex polytope
P
, we have a geometric invariant, the
Minkowski length
of
P
, which was first introduced by Soprunov and Soprunova
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an integral convex lattice polytope.
The
Minkowski length
of
P
, denoted
L(P)
, is the maximum number of non-trivial (i.e. at least one dimensional) polytopes whose Minkowski sum is contained in
P
.
Equivalently,
L(P)
is the maximum number of primitive line segments whose Minkowski sum is contained in
P
.
We say a polytope contained in
P
is a
maximal decomposition
in
P
if it can be written as the Minkowski sum of
L(P)
non-trivial polytopes.

Newton polytopes allow us to interpret
L(P)
in
\mathcal{L}_{P}
, namely,
L(P)
is the maximum number of irreducible polynomials in a factorization of any polynomial in
\mathcal{L}_{P}
.
To see this, take
f\in\mathcal{L}_{P}
then write
f=f_{1}\cdots f_{r}
where each
f_{i}
is a nonconstant irreducible polynomial, then
P_{f}=P_{f_{1}}+\cdots+P_{f_{r}}\subseteq P
so
r\leq L(P)
.
Conversely, suppose that
Q_{1}+\cdots+Q_{L(P)}=Q\subseteq P
is a maximal decomposition then
f=f_{1}\cdots f_{L(P)}\in\mathcal{L}_{P}
where
f_{i}=\sum_{\alpha\in Q_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}}t^{\alpha}
since
P_{f}=Q
.

The Minkowski length is a geometric invariant in the sense that for any affine transformation
T
of the form
T({x})=A{x}+\lambda
where
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
A\in\operatorname{GL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
, we have
L(P)=L(T(P))
.
We denote the set of all such affine transformations by
\operatorname{AGL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
.
To see this, first take a maximal decomposition in
P
, say
Q=Q_{1}+\cdots+Q_{L(P)}
, then
T(Q)=\mathbf{\lambda}+AQ_{1}+\cdots+AQ_{L(P)}\subseteq T(P)
so
L(P)\leq L(T(P))
.
Note that
T^{-1}({x})=A^{-1}{x}-A^{-1}{\lambda}
is contained in
\operatorname{AGL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
.
Furthermore,
(T\circ T^{-1})({x})={x}=(T^{-1}\circ T)({x})
so, by the above argument,
L(T(P))\leq L(T^{-1}(T(P)))=L(P)
.
Therefore, we have that
L(P)=L(T(P))
.
This motivates a notation of equivalence between lattice polytopes, first introduced by Little and Schwarz,
[
6
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
P,Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be integral convex lattice polytopes.
We say that
P
and
Q
are
lattice equivalent
, and write
P\approx Q
, if there exists a
T\in\operatorname{AGL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
so that
T(P)=Q
.

This is a useful notation of equivalence when discussing toric codes due to a result of Little and Schwarz
[
6
, Theorem 4]
which states that lattice equivalent polytopes produce monomially equivalent codes.
If
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both codes with block length
n
and dimension
k
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
G_{1}
is a generator matrix for
C_{1}
, then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are said to be
monomially equivalent
if there exists a
n\times n
invertible diagonal matrix
D
and a
n\times n
permutation matrix
\Pi
so that

G_{2}=G_{1}D\Pi

is a generator matrix for
C_{2}
.
Note that monomially equivalent codes have the same block length, dimension, and minimum distance. In particular, if
P\approx Q
, then
d(C_{P})=d(C_{Q}).

3.2.
Periodicity

In the preceding three corollaries, all the polytopes satisfy the property that
L(tP)=tL(P)
for all

.
However, this is not true for all polytopes — for example the polytope in Proposition
3.5
when

and
2\nmid r
, does not satisfy this condition.
For another example, consider the exceptional triangle
T_{0}=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,2),(2,1)\}
.
We have that
L(T_{0})=1
and

(1)

L(tT_{0})=t+\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor

by
[
10
, Example 4.1]
.
In general, we can only say that

L(tP)\geq tL(P).

Definition 3.7
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a lattice polytope.
We say that
P
has
period 1
if
L(tP)=tL(P)
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
We say that
P
has
period strictly greater than 1
if
P
is not a period 1 polytope.

We note that this definition is equivalent to that given
[
10
, Definition 2.17]
.
If
P
has period 1 (in the sense of
[
10
, Definition 2.17]
) then

\frac{L(1\cdot P)}{1}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\frac{L(tP)}{t}\iff tL(P)\geq L(tP)

for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
So we have
tL(P)=L(tP)
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and the two definitions are equivalent.

As we have seen, line segments are period
1
polytopes and simplices are period
1
polytopes which leaves the exceptional triangle as the only polytope which can appear in a maximal decomposition that has period greater than 1.
As such, we begin by investigating the periodicity of polytopes which contain the exceptional triangle as a summand in some maximal decomposition.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an integral convex polytope which has a maximal decomposition
Q\subseteq P
containing the exceptional triangle as a summand, then
P
has period strictly greater than 1.

Proof.

We may write

Q\approx T_{0}+m[0,{e}_{1}]+n[0,{e}_{2}]+\ell[0,{e}_{1}+{e}_{2}]

by
[
8
, Theorem 1.6]
.
Using Equation (
1
), we have

L(tP)=L(tQ)\geq L(tT_{0})+L(t(m[0,{e}_{1}]+n[0,{e}_{2}]+\ell[0,{e}_{1}+{e}_{2}]))=t(m+n+\ell+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor>tL(Q)

when

.
∎

While simple, this result will be useful when computing the minimum distance of period
1
polytopes in the next section.

Let

\displaystyle 4\ell^{2}+4\ell+1-\left(\frac{1}{2}\ell^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ell\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}\ell^{2}+\frac{7}{2}\ell+1

Thus,

.

Lemma 5.7
.

Every subset

with

\ell
staircase configuration.

Proof.

In
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
, there are exactly
q-1
choices for
x
. If we assume every
x
-coordinate belongs to
2\ell
points, we can have at most
2\ell(q-1)
points. Thus, by the pigeonhole principle, in order to show that there exists one line
x_{0}\subset T

2\ell+1
lattice points, we need to show that
|T|>2\ell(q-1)
.

The expression
|T|=3\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell+1>2\ell(q-1)

q>\frac{2\ell^{2}+\ell-1}{\ell}
, however recall that our polytope
P\subseteq[0,q-2]^{2}
, thus
q
is already bounded by
q>2\ell+1
. Since
2\ell+1>\frac{2\ell^{2}+\ell-1}{\ell}
we know that
|T|>2\ell(q-1)
for all
q
. Thus, by the pigeonhole principle,
T
must contain a line,
x=a_{1}
, with at least
2\ell+1
lattice points. To show that
T
contains a second line,
x=a_{2}
with at least
2\ell+1
points on it, we notice that there can be at most
q-1
lattice points on
x=a_{1}
. Thus,
T
has at least
|T|-(q-1)
lattice points left after considering the first group of
2\ell+1
points on
x=a_{1}
. When this is true, the line
x=a_{1}
cannot contain more points, so there are
q-2
possible choices for
x
remaining. Thus, we need to show that
|T|-(q-1)>2\ell(q-2)
. We first consider the case when
\ell=1
. Then
|T|-(q-1)=3q-2-3+1-q+1=2q-3
, while
2\ell(q-2)=2q-4
, so
|T|-(q-1)>2(q-2)
. If
\ell>1
, then to show the desired inequality, we note it is equivalent to show that
q>\frac{2\ell^{2}-\ell-2}{\ell-1}
, since:

\displaystyle 3\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell+1-(q-1)

\displaystyle>2\ell(q-2)

\displaystyle\ell q-q

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-\ell-2

\displaystyle q(\ell-1)

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-\ell-2

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle>\frac{2\ell^{2}-\ell-2}{\ell-1}

Because
q>2\ell+1
it suffices to show that
2\ell+1>\frac{2\ell^{2}-\ell-2}{\ell-1}
, which in turn is equivalent to showing that
4\ell>-1
, since:

\displaystyle 2\ell+1

\displaystyle>\frac{2\ell^{2}-\ell-2}{\ell-1}

\displaystyle(\ell-1)(2\ell+1)

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-\ell-2

\displaystyle 2\ell^{2}+3\ell-1

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-\ell-2

\displaystyle 4\ell

\displaystyle>-1

which is always true, because
\ell>1
.

In general, to show that
T
contains
\ell+1
lines,
x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell}
with at least
2\ell+1
lattice points on each line, we must show that:

(3\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell+1)-i(q-1)>2\ell(q-1-i),\text{with }i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell

When
i=\ell
, we must verify:

(3iq-2i^{2}-3i+1)-i(q-1)>2i(q-1-i)

However, note the left-hand side is
2iq-2i^{2}-2i+1
, while the right-hand side is
2iq-2i^{2}-2i
, so the desired inequality is true.

When
i\neq\ell
, it suffices to show that
q>\frac{2\ell^{2}-2\ell i+\ell-i-1}{\ell-i}
, since:

\displaystyle(3\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell+1)-i(q-1)

\displaystyle>2\ell(q-1-i)

\displaystyle\ell q-iq+i-2\ell^{2}+1

\displaystyle>\ell-2\ell i

\displaystyle\ell q-iq

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-2\ell i+\ell-i-1

\displaystyle q(\ell-i)

\displaystyle>2\ell^{2}-2\ell i+\ell-i-1

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle>\frac{2\ell^{2}-2\ell i+\ell-i-1}{\ell-i}

When
i\neq\ell
, when viewed as a function of
i
, the expression
\frac{2\ell^{2}-2\ell i+\ell-i-1}{\ell-i}
is decreasing on the interval
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
so the function achieves its maximum
i=0
. Thus,
q
need only satisfy
q>\frac{2\ell^{2}+\ell-1}{\ell}
. Again, since
q
is already bounded below by
q>2\ell+1
, it suffices to show that
2\ell+1>\frac{2\ell^{2}+\ell-1}{\ell}
. This is equivalent to
2\ell^{2}+\ell>2\ell^{2}+\ell-1
, which is always true.

Thus, we know that for all
q>2\ell+1
, each subset
T\subset(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
must contain
\ell+1
lines

each containing at least
2\ell+1
lattice points.

Each line
x=a_{1},\ldots,x=a_{\ell+1}
has at most
q-1
lattice points,

is at least

after considering each line. Furthermore, in the case where there are
q-1
lattice points on each line

, we are left with exactly
(q-1-(\ell+1))
choices for
x
in our finite field. Thus, to show that there exists one line
x=b_{1}
containing

lattice points, we need to show that
|T|-(\ell+1)(q-1)>(2\ell-1)(q-1-(\ell+1))
. However, note,

\displaystyle(2\ell-1)(q-1-(\ell+1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell-q+2

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2\ell q-2\ell^{2}-2\ell-q+2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\ell q-2\ell^{2}-3\ell+1-(\ell+1)(q-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|T|-(\ell+1)(q-1).

By the pigeonhole principle, we know that there must exist a line
x=b_{1}
with at least
2\ell
lattice points on it. Thus, in order to show that for each integer

there exists a line

such that each line contains

\displaystyle|T|-(\ell+1+j)(q-1)

\displaystyle>(2\ell-1-j)(q-2-\ell-j)

\displaystyle(-2\ell^{2}-q+2\ell q-2\ell+2)-j(q-1)

\displaystyle>(2\ell-1-j)(q-2-\ell-j)

Multiplying out the left and right hand side, we see this is

Now, the expression

, so

, which implies

Thus

for

.
On the other hand, if

, we have

∎

Proposition 5.8

.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let

Then the toric code
