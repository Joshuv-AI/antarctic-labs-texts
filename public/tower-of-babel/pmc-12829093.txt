Targeting OxLDL-mediated CD36 + CAF reprogramming potentiates PD-1 immunotherapy in osteosarcoma
Authors: Anyu Zeng, Hongmin Chen, Tianqi Luo, Weiqing Chen, Yihui Song, Yanyang Xu, Zhihao Chen, Qinglian Tang, Xiaojun Zhu, Chuangzhong Deng, Huaiyuan Xu, Anqi Wang, Hao Wu, Guohui Song, Jinchang Lu, Jin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma demonstrates limited responsiveness to PD-1 blockade, largely due to its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). The specific mechanisms by which cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) contribute to immunosuppression in osteosarcoma are not fully understood.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on osteosarcoma tissues from patients treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy and anti-PD-1 therapy to investigate the tumor microenvironment. Cellular composition, gene expression programs, and signaling pathways were analyzed. Functional assays, pull-down and PLA-flow binding validation, and in vivo mouse models were used to dissect the mechanisms by which CAF-derived factors influence CD8⁺ T cell function and contribute to immunotherapy response.ResultsWe identified a subpopulation of CD36⁺ CAFs, characterized by adaptive uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OxLDL) and activation of the PPARG–FABP4 axis. This metabolic program promoted ANGPTL4 secretion, which bound integrin on CD8⁺ T cells and activated the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, leading to T cell exhaustion and impaired effector function. In vivo, administration of VitE effectively scavenged OxLDL, reprogrammed the TME, enhanced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and synergized with PD-1 blockade to improve tumor control.ConclusionsCD36⁺ CAFs drive immunosuppressive metabolic reprogramming via the OxLDL–PPARG–ANGPTL4 axis, promoting CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and resistance to immunotherapy in osteosarcoma. Targeting this pathway with VitE alleviated CAF-mediated immune suppression and enhanced PD-1 blockade responses in preclinical models, providing a rationale for metabolism-based combinatorial strategies in osteosarcoma.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02516-2.

Background

Osteosarcoma (OSA), the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, is an aggressive malignancy with a high propensity for metastasis and a poor prognosis [1]. While advancements in surgery and chemotherapy have improved survival rates for localized disease, the prognosis for metastatic or recurrent osteosarcoma remains dismal, with a five-year survival rate below 30% [2]. Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), has revolutionized cancer treatment by harnessing cytotoxic CD8 + T cells to restore anti-tumor immunity [3]. However, the efficacy of ICB in osteosarcoma is limited by the complex and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), along with the tumor’s inherent heterogeneity [4, 5]. Therefore, targeting the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in osteosarcoma to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy is a promising strategy.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are essential components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [6]. Heterogeneity and plasticity are key characteristics of CAFs. Heterogeneity indicates that different CAF subtypes may exhibit distinct biological behaviors, which in turn determine their diverse functions. Plasticity indicates that CAFs can be influenced by other components in the tumor microenvironment (TME), leading to changes in their biological behavior [7, 8]. Compelling evidence suggests that CAFs regulate cancer progression in multiple ways, including promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis, stimulating angiogenesis, and inducing chemotherapy resistance [6, 9]. Additionally, CAFs can reshape the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), leading to tumor immune evasion [10, 11]. However, studies on CAFs in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of osteosarcoma remain limited. More evidence and research are needed to elucidate the relationship between CAF and immune suppression, especially in osteosarcoma (OSA).

Lipid metabolism reprogramming is a hallmark of tumor progression. This process not only provides energy for tumor cell survival and function maintenance but also contributes to reshaping the immune microenvironment [12, 13]. For example, due to elevated lipid oxidation levels in tumors, CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) increase the uptake of oxidized lipids, leading to lipid peroxidation. Inhibiting lipid peroxidation enhances the effector function of CD8 + TILs and strengthens the anti-tumor response [14]. Similarly, lipid metabolism reprogramming in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) plays a crucial role in reshaping the immune microenvironment [15]. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), CAFs secrete macrophage migration inhibitory factors through lipid metabolism pathways, contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment [16]. However, research on the mechanisms of lipid metabolism reprogramming in reshaping the immune tumor microenvironment in osteosarcoma remains limited. Increasing research suggests that CAF heterogeneity and plasticity can be influenced by lipid metabolism reprogramming, driving changes that promote tumor growth and immune suppression [15, 17]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of lipid metabolism reprogramming in CAFs in osteosarcoma is crucial for understanding the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Here, we performed single-cell sequencing on eight osteosarcoma specimens and identified a subpopulation of CD36⁺ CAFs. This subpopulation was found to maintain its phenotype by adaptively uptaking OxLDL, which activates the PPARG signaling pathway. In turn, these CAFs secrete ANGPTL4, which binds to integrin α5β1 on CD8⁺ T cells and activates the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, leading to CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and functional impairment. Furthermore, the levels of OxLDL in the TME and serum can be used to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ICB. Using the antioxidant vitamin E to eliminate OxLDL significantly enhanced the efficacy of ICB therapy in a murine subcutaneous osteosarcoma model. In summary, our findings reveal a mechanism by which CD36⁺ CAFs in the osteosarcoma TME drive immune evasion through OxLDL uptake and ANGPTL4–integrin α5β1 signaling, thereby linking lipid metabolism reprogramming to CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction. These results provide new insights into rational combinational ICB therapies for osteosarcoma.

Results

CD36 + CAFs accumulate in the Non-Responder group and predict Immuno-chemotherapy resistance

To investigate the cellular heterogeneity and immune landscape of osteosarcoma (OSA) patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on resected osteoblastic osteosarcoma specimens from eight patients (Fig. 1a and Table S1). Among these, five patients were classified as responders (tumor necrosis rate [TNR] > 50%), and three as non-responders (TNR < 50%) based on postoperative pathological evaluation. A total of 37,673 cells were annotated into six major cell types using canonical markers (Figs. 1b-c and S1a-b): Osteoblastic osteosarcoma cells (ALPL, SP7, PTH1R), Fibroblasts (TAGLN, COL1A1, DUN, LUM), Endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF, CDH5), T & NK cells (CD3D, CD3E, GZMA), Myeloid cells (CD14, MS4A7, VSIG4), Osteoclasts (MMP9, ACP5, CTSK) [18]. UMAP clustering analysis revealed distinct cellular distributions between responders and non-responders (Fig. 1b). CAFs were significantly enriched in the non-responder group, while T & NK cells infiltration were significantly lower in this group (Figs. 1d and S1c), suggesting a potential role of CAFs in immunotherapy resistance. To further explore the role of CAFs, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for α-SMA, a CAF marker, on 52 clinical cases from our institutional cohort. α-SMA H-scores were significantly higher in the non-responder group compared to the responder group (Fig. 1e), and negatively correlated with TNR (Fig. 1f), further supporting the involvement of CAFs in therapy resistance. Considering that this phenomenon might be chemotherapy-induced, we next analyzed paired biopsy specimens and post-treatment surgical samples following neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone from our center. IHC analysis of paired biopsy and post-treatment specimens revealed that α-SMA scores increased after chemotherapy, with no significant difference between chemotherapy alone and chemotherapy plus ICB (Figs. S1d-e). Given that different CAF subtypes may exert distinct functions in tumor progression and immune evasion [19], we first examined the expression patterns of canonical CAF markers (TAGLN, COL1A1, DCN, LUM, COL3A1, FN1, THY1, and ACTA2), which validated the accuracy of our CAF annotation (Fig. S1f). Subsequent dimensional reduction and clustering analysis identified four CAF subpopulations (Figs. 1 g-h). CD36 + CAFs, characterized by the expression of CD36, FABP4, APOE, STEAP4, APOLD1, were potentially involved in lipid metabolism. MYH11 + CAFs, enriched for MYH11, RGS5, MCAM, MYH9, TGFBI, were associated with angiogenesis and vascular development. C7 + CAFs, expressing C7, PTGDS, TNXB, FGL2, IGF2, CXCL12, were linked to inflammatory processes. FNDC1 + CAFs, defined by FNDC1, COMP, MMP2, COL11A1, COL10A1, P4HA3, were involved in ECM synthesis and metabolism (Figs. 1i-k and S1g). Comparative analysis between responders and non-responders revealed that CD36 + CAFs were significantly enriched in the non-responder group (Figs. 1l-m and S1h). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that CD36 + α-SMA + CAFs were more abundant in the non-responder group, consistent with the single-cell data (Fig. 1n). Our findings reveal that CD36 + CAFs are significantly enriched in the non-responder group and may contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in osteosarcoma.

CD36 + CAFs promote tumor progression by interacting with CD8 + T cells

In our previous results, we observed that T & NK cell infiltration was significantly lower in the non-responder group (Figs. 1d and S1c). To further explore this population, we classified these immune cells into CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, NK cells, and other subsets (Figs. S2a-b). We found that all four T/NK subsets were significantly more abundant in responders than in non-responders. This was further validated by CD8 IHC staining (Figs. S2c-d), supporting their contribution to treatment efficacy. Using CellChat analysis, we then examined CAF–immune interactions and observed that CD36⁺ CAFs exhibited the strongest and most frequent interactions with CD8⁺ T cells, suggesting that CD36⁺ CAFs may directly modulate CD8⁺ T cell function (Figs. S2e-f). To functionally validate these findings, we established a K7M2 subcutaneous osteosarcoma model in BALB/c mice. CAFs were isolated and cultured in vitro, and CD36 was knocked down using CRISPR-Cas9 (sgCD36). We then sorted and expanded CD36 + CAFs and co-injected them with K7M2 cells at a 1:3 ratio to establish subcutaneous tumors (Figs. 2a and S2g-i). Compared to the control groups, CD36WT CAFs and CD36 + CAFs significantly promoted tumor growth, with CD36 + CAFs exhibiting the stronger tumor-promoting effects than CD36KD CAFs (Figs. 2b-d). Next, we analyzed tumor tissues using Ki67 and CD8 immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that CD36WT CAFs and CD36 + CAFs significantly enhanced tumor cell proliferation, with CD36 + CAFs exhibiting the stronger effects than CD36KD CAFs. In contrast, CD36WT CAFs and CD36 + CAFs reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration, with CD36 + CAFs exerting the stronger suppressive effect than CD36KD CAFs (Figs. 2e-f). Multiparameter flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these findings, demonstrating a reduction in CD8 + T cell infiltration in tumors with CD36 + CAF enrichment (Fig. 2g). Given that CD8 + T cells are the primary effector immune cells responsible for tumor cell killing, previous studies have reported that CAF interactions with CD8 + T cells may lead to immune suppression and contribute to immunotherapy resistance [20]. To further dissect the CD8⁺ T cell compartment, we performed subclustering analysis and identified three subtypes (Figs. 2h-i): TCF7⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (TCF7, IL7R; naïve/memory-like), GZMB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (GZMB, GNLY, IFNG; effector cytotoxic), and PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (PDCD1, HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT, EOMES, TOX; terminally exhausted) (Figs. 2j-k and S3a-b). Comparison between responders and non-responders revealed lower proportions of GZMB⁺ and PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells but higher proportions of TCF7⁺ CD8⁺ T cells in the non-responder group (Figs. S3c-d), possibly reflecting effector T cell exhaustion and apoptosis, thereby contributing to poor therapeutic outcomes. Given the limited statistical power of single-cell data, we further validated PDCD1 expression by IHC, which was reduced in non-responders (Fig. S3e), consistent with our scRNA-seq findings. To further investigate CD36 + CAF-mediated immune suppression, we performed CellChat analysis on the interactions between CD36 + CAFs and the three CD8 + T cell subtypes. The results showed that CD36 + CAFs exhibited the highest number and strongest interactions with PDCD1 + CD8 + T cells (Figs. 2l and S3f), suggesting that CD36 + CAFs may contribute to CD8 + T cell exhaustion and ultimately promote tumor progression.

CD36⁺ CAFs adaptively uptake OxLDL in a CD36-Dependent manner

To further explore the biological characteristics and functional mechanisms of CD36 + CAFs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the four CAF subpopulations. The results indicated that CD36 + CAFs were significantly enriched in pathways related to long-chain fatty acid transport and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle binding, suggesting a strong association with lipid metabolism (Figs. 3a and S4a). Similarly, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that CD36 + CAFs were enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway and cholesterol metabolism, further supporting their role in lipid metabolism (Figs. 3b and S4b). Moreover, GSEA based on HALLMARK gene sets showed that CD36⁺ CAFs were enriched in HYPOXIA, REACTIVE_OXYGEN_SPECIES_PATHWAY, OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION, and ADIPOGENESIS, linking CD36⁺ CAFs to metabolic reprogramming and stress adaptation (Fig. S4c). CD36, known as a scavenger receptor, is a key molecule in the PPAR signaling pathway and mediates OxLDL uptake, thereby facilitating immunosuppressive effects [20, 21]. Previous studies have reported that OxLDL is enriched in the tumor microenvironment and is closely associated with oxidative stress, where it further contributes to immune suppression [13, 15, 22]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that CD36 + CAFs may mediate immune suppression by taking up OxLDL in the tumor microenvironment. To validate this hypothesis, we firstly performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to assess OxLDL levels in clinical samples. The results showed that OxLDL was significantly enriched in the non-responder group, and OxLDL H-scores were negatively correlated with tumor necrosis rate (TNR) (Figs. 3c-d). These findings suggest that higher OxLDL levels may be associated with poor therapeutic response. Next, we isolated normal fibroblasts (NAFs) and CAFs for in vitro OxLDL and fatty acid (FA) uptake assays. The results demonstrated that CAFs exhibited significantly higher OxLDL and FA uptake capacity compared to NAFs (Figs. 3e and S4d). To determine whether this phenomenon was CD36-dependent, we used flow cytometry to sort CD36 + CAFs from the CAF population. We then knocked down CD36 expression in CD36 + CAFs using siRNA (Figs. S4f-g) and treated the cells with a CD36 small-molecule inhibitor (SSO) before repeating the OxLDL and FA uptake experiments. The results showed that CD36 + CAFs exhibited significantly higher OxLDL and FA uptake compared to CD36 − CAFs, while CD36 knockdown (siRNA) and SSO treatment reduced OxLDL uptake in CD36 + CAFs (Figs. 3f and S4e). OxLDL reprograms CD36 + CAFs by activating the PPAR signaling pathway

To investigate the molecular changes occurring in CD36 + CAFs after OxLDL uptake, we stimulated CAFs with OxLDL and performed RNA-seq analysis. Interestingly, KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment of the PPAR signaling pathway and cholesterol metabolism, consistent with findings from the single-cell analysis of CD36 + CAFs (Fig. S5a). To further explore key transcription factors (TFs) involved in this process, we conducted TF activity analysis using the Dorothea package in single-cell CAF data. The results indicated that PPARG exhibited the highest transcriptional activity in CD36 + CAFs (Fig. 4a). Moreover, PPARG expression was predominantly observed in CD36 + CAFs (Figs. 4b). As a key regulator of the PPAR pathway, PPARG has been implicated in tumor progression and immune suppression [21, 22]. Next, to determine downstream targets of PPARG, we performed co-expression analysis and identified the top 10 genes correlated with PPARG, including FABP4 and CD36(Figs. 4c-d), which are involved in long-chain fatty acid transport [23]. Additionally, analysis of the TCGA sarcoma database confirmed a positive correlation between PPARG, FABP4, and CD36 (Fig. S5b). Since FABP4 was also enriched in CD36 + CAFs (Fig. 1j), we further explored its role. We first performed qPCR analysis to examine mRNA levels of PPARG, CD36, and FABP4 in CD36 + CAFs and CD36 − CAFs. The results showed that all three genes were significantly upregulated in CD36 + CAFs (Fig. 4e). Moreover, OxLDL stimulation increased PPARG, CD36, and FABP4 mRNA levels (Fig. 4f), and Western blot (WB) analysis confirmed that protein levels were also upregulated. Conversely, siRNA-mediated knockdown of CD36 and treatment with the CD36 inhibitor SSO reduced PPARG, CD36, and FABP4 protein expression, indicating that OxLDL-induced upregulation of PPARG, CD36, and FABP4 was CD36-dependent (Fig. 4g). PPARG has been reported to act as a promoter of CD36 and FABP4 transcription [24]. To validate this, we knocked down PPARG in CAFs, which led to a decrease in CD36 and FABP4 expression (Figs. 4h-i). Motif analysis using the JASPAR database revealed that PPARG-binding motifs were present in the promoter regions of CD36 and FABP4 (Fig. S5c). Since the mechanism by which OxLDL activates PPARG in CD36 + CAFs remains unclear, we investigated potential metabolic intermediates. In previous studies, it has been shown that OxLDL uptake via CD36 leads to the hydrolysis of OxLDL into 13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (13-HODE) [25, 26], a potential ligand for PPARG activation. To test this hypothesis, we performed an ELISA assay and found that OxLDL stimulation significantly increased 13-HODE levels in CAFs (Fig. 4k). Furthermore, 13-HODE treatment increased the expression of CD36 and FABP4 (Figs. 4l -m). FABP4 is commonly known as a transport protein that binds to long-chain fatty acids and other hydrophobic ligands [27]. Currently, there is no direct research explicitly proving the binding between 13-HODE and FABP4. To confirm the role of FABP4 in 13-HODE transport, we knocked down FABP4 in CAFs (Fig. S5d) and found that FABP4 knockdown impaired 13-HODE-mediated activation of CD36 and FABP4 expression (Figs. 4n-o), demonstrating that FABP4 is required for 13-HODE transport and PPARG activation. In silico docking analysis using SwissDock predicted a strong binding affinity between 13-HODE and FABP4, supporting the role of FABP4 in 13-HODE transport. Similarly, molecular docking suggested that 13-HODE binds to the ligand-binding domain of PPARG, indicating that 13-HODE can directly activate PPARG as a ligand (Fig. 4p). These results suggest that FABP4 may transport 13-HODE into the nucleus and facilitate its delivery to PPARG. To further validate this mechanism, using AF protein interaction prediction, we identified a strong binding affinity between FABP4 and PPARG (Fig. 4q). Then we performed a co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay, confirming that FABP4 physically interacts with PPARG, supporting its role in delivering 13-HODE as a PPARG ligand (Fig. 4r). Together, Our findings indicate that OxLDL reprograms CD36 + CAFs by activating the PPAR signaling pathway in a CD36-dependent manner. Mechanistically, OxLDL hydrolysis generates 13-HODE, which is transported into the nucleus by FABP4 to activate PPARG transcriptional activity.

ANGPTL4 derived from CD36 + CAFs promotes CD8 + T cell exhaustion and dysfunction

Our previous results indicated that CD36 + CAFs contribute to tumor progression and immune suppression by interacting with CD8 + T cells. To further investigate the specific mechanisms of this interaction, we explored potential modes of communication between CD36⁺ CAFs and CD8⁺ T cells. According to previous studies, various interaction mechanisms exist within the tumor microenvironment, including the secretion of cytokines and proteins [28, 29]. By analyzing the intersection of differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq data after OxLDL stimulation and a secreted protein gene set derived from CD36⁺ CAF marker genes, we identified ANGPTL4 as a secreted protein of interest. (Fig. 5a). Single-cell UMAP analysis confirmed that ANGPTL4 was predominantly expressed in CD36 + CAFs (Fig. 5b). To validate these findings, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and found that ANGPTL4 expression was significantly elevated in the non-responder group (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, qPCR and Western blot (WB) analysis demonstrated that ANGPTL4 expression was upregulated following OxLDL and 13-HODE stimulation (Figs. 4g and m and 5d, and S6a). Interestingly, ANGPTL4 was identified as a downstream target of PPARG. PPARG knockdown via siRNA led to a significant decrease in ANGPTL4 expression, as confirmed by qPCR and WB analysis (Figs. 4i and 5e). ChIP-qPCR experiments further confirmed that PPARG directly binds to the ANGPTL4 promoter, suggesting that PPARG transcriptionally regulates ANGPTL4 (Fig. 5f). As a member of the ANGPTL protein family, ANGPTL4 has been reported to promote angiogenesis, tumor progression, and CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion [30, 32]. To examine its pro-angiogenic role in our dataset, we calculated angiogenesis scores across CAF subsets and found that CD36⁺ CAFs and MYH11⁺ CAFs exhibited higher scores (Figs. S6c-d), and ANGPTL4⁺ CAFs had higher scores than ANGPTL4⁻ CAFs (Fig. S6e), suggesting that CD36⁺ CAFs may additionally facilitate resistance through angiogenesis. We next hypothesized that ANGPTL4 acts as a mediator of CD36⁺ CAF–CD8⁺ T cell interactions. Analysis of TIMER2 immune infiltration data showed that ANGPTL4 expression was positively correlated with CAF infiltration but negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in sarcomas (Fig. 5g). Consistently, CIBERSORT analysis of TARGET osteosarcoma data revealed that higher ANGPTL4 expression was associated with reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 5h). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining further confirmed elevated ANGPTL4 expression and reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in non-responders (Fig. 5i). Moreover, TISIDB analysis demonstrated that ANGPTL4 expression was positively correlated with immune checkpoint molecules linked to T cell exhaustion, including PDCD1, HAVCR2, TIGIT, and CTLA4 (Figs. 5j and S6b), which were predominantly expressed in PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (Figs. 2k and S3b). Taken together, these findings suggest that ANGPTL4 contributes to immune resistance by driving CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and limiting their infiltration, with an additional role in promoting angiogenesis.

To validate whether CD36⁺ CAFs influence CD8⁺ T cells through ANGPTL4 secretion, we applied CellChat analysis to examine ANGPTL signaling pathway interactions. The results showed that PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells were the only CD8⁺ T cell subset interacting with CD36⁺ CAFs via this pathway, with CD36⁺ CAFs serving as the primary signal senders and PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells as the receivers (Figs. S6f-g). These findings suggest that CD36⁺ CAFs may secrete ANGPTL4 to specifically act on PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells. We next isolated peripheral blood CD8⁺ T cells and performed in vitro coculture with CAF-conditioned medium (CAF-CM). Flow cytometry revealed increased PD1 expression and reduced proportions of GZMB⁺, TNFα⁺, and IFNγ⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (Figs. 5k and S7a). Notably, CAF-CM derived from OxLDL-stimulated CAFs exerted a more pronounced effect compared with control CAF-CM. We further measured ANGPTL4 levels in CAF-conditioned medium by ELISA and found that OxLDL-treated CAFs secreted higher levels of ANGPTL4 compared with controls (Fig. 5l). Together, these findings suggest that elevated ANGPTL4 may serve as a central mediator linking lipid metabolism in CAFs to CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction. We next stimulated CD8⁺ T cells in vitro with recombinant ANGPTL4. Flow cytometry revealed that ANGPTL4 treatment increased PD1 expression while reducing the proportions of GZMB⁺, TNFα⁺, and IFNγ⁺ CD8⁺ T cells (Figs. 5m and S7b). In addition, we assessed the effect of ANGPTL4 on cytokine secretion by CD8⁺ T cells using ELISA. ANGPTL4 treatment led to decreased secretion of GZMB, TNFα, and IFNγ (Fig. 5n), indicating that ANGPTL4 impairs the cytokine-producing capacity of CD8⁺ T cells. We further examined the effects of ANGPTL4 on CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and apoptosis. Cell-trace assays showed that ANGPTL4 did not affect CD8⁺ T cell proliferation (Fig. S7c), whereas Annexin V staining revealed that ANGPTL4 promoted early apoptosis of CD8⁺ T cells (Figs. 5o and S7d).

We next investigated the mechanisms by which ANGPTL4 acts on CD8⁺ T cells. Ligand–receptor interaction analysis between CD36⁺ CAFs and CD8⁺ T cells in our single-cell dataset suggested that integrins (ITGA5-ITGB1) may serve as potential downstream receptors for ANGPTL4 (Figs. S8a-b). To functionally validate this interaction, we treated CD8⁺ T cells with recombinant ANGPTL4 in the presence or absence of the α5β1-blocking antibody Volociximab. Volociximab attenuated the ANGPTL4-induced increase in PD1 expression and the reduction of GZMB⁺, TNFα⁺, and IFNγ⁺ CD8⁺ T cell subsets (Figs. 5m and S7b). Consistently, ELISA confirmed that Volociximab alleviated the suppression of GZMB, TNFα, and IFNγ secretion caused by ANGPTL4 (Fig. 5n). Annexin V staining further demonstrated that Volociximab rescued CD8⁺ T cells from ANGPTL4-induced early apoptosis (Figs. 5o and S7d), while cell-trace assays showed no effect on proliferation (Fig. S7c). Building on these findings, we next sought to directly demonstrate the physical interaction between ANGPTL4 and integrin α5β1 on CD8⁺ T cells. Pull-down assays using His-tagged ANGPTL4 revealed that ITGA5 and ITGB1 were co-precipitated from CD8⁺ T cell membrane protein extracts (Fig. 5p). To further validate the dynamic binding of ANGPTL4 to CD8⁺ T cell surface integrins, we incubated CD8⁺ T cells with recombinant ANGPTL4 and performed PLA-flow assays, which confirmed the interaction between ANGPTL4 and integrin α5β1 at the cellular level (Figs. 5q-r and S8c-d), together establishing integrin α5β1 as a direct receptor for ANGPTL4 on CD8⁺ T cells. We next investigated the downstream signaling activated by ANGPTL4 binding to integrin α5β1. Previous studies have reported that ANGPTL4–integrin interaction can activate FAK/SRC phosphorylation, which in turn triggers the JAK2–STAT3 pathway [33, 34]. Notably, JAK2–STAT3 activation has been shown to upregulate PD1 and other exhaustion markers, thereby promoting CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction [35]. Consistent with this, our Western blot analysis demonstrated that ANGPTL4 stimulation activated FAK/SRC and subsequently the JAK2–STAT3 signaling cascade in CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 5s). These findings suggest that CD36⁺ CAF-derived ANGPTL4 induces CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and functional impairment via the JAK2–STAT3 pathway (Fig. 5t), together establishing a mechanistic axis of CD36⁺ CAF–ANGPTL4–integrin α5β1–FAK/SRC–JAK2–STAT3 driving immune resistance.

VitE enhances the efficacy of Anti-PD-1 therapy

Our previous data demonstrated that OxLDL reprograms CD36⁺ CAFs, leading to CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion. Since antioxidants can neutralize OxLDL [36, 37], we first compared several commonly available antioxidants in vitro and found that they attenuated OxLDL-induced ANGPTL4 expression in CD36⁺ CAFs, with vitamin E showing the strongest effect among the three tested compounds (Figs. S9a-b). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that vitamin E could enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo. To test this, we established a K7M2 subcutaneous tumor model in BALB/c mice. The results showed that only the combination therapy significantly reduced tumor volume and weight compared to the control and anti-PD-1 monotherapy groups, indicating that VitE enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy (Figs. 6a–c). Next, we analyzed tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) and serum OxLDL levels using ELISA assays. The results showed that VitE treatment reduced both TIF and serum OxLDL levels, and a positive correlation was observed between serum OxLDL levels and TIF OxLDL concentrations (Figs. S9c–e). These findings suggest that serum OxLDL levels may serve as a potential biomarker for assessing OxLDL levels in the tumor microenvironment (TME). To further examine the effect of VitE on the TME, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that VitE treatment reduced the proportion of CD36 + α-SMA + CAFs and increased CD8 + T cell infiltration (Fig. 6d and S10a). Multiparameter flow cytometry analysis confirmed that CD8 + T cell infiltration was highest in the combination therapy group (Fig. 6e and S10b). Next, we assessed CD8 + T cell exhaustion markers (PD1) and cytotoxic markers (GZMB, IFNγ, TNFα). The results showed that compared to anti-PD-1 monotherapy, combination therapy significantly reduced the proportion of PD1 + CD8 + T cells and increased the proportions of GZMB+, IFNγ+, and TNFα + CD8 + T cells (Fig. 6f and S10c).

Given the essential role of the bone microenvironment in osteosarcoma biology, we next employed a tibial paratibial orthotopic model using luciferase-labeled K7M2 cells. Consistent with the subcutaneous model, combination therapy significantly reduced tumor bioluminescence intensity compared to control and anti-PD-1 monotherapy groups (Figs. 6g-h and S10d-e). Discussion

Osteosarcoma (OSA) poses a significant challenge to immunotherapy due to its low immunogenicity and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [38]. Leveraging single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), we investigated the TME of OSA patients treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy and Camrelizumab (anti-PD-1), identifying CD36 + cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) as key mediators of immunotherapy resistance. Our findings link lipid metabolism dysregulation, T cell exhaustion, and poor anti-PD-1 responses, providing novel mechanistic insights and therapeutic avenues for OSA.

Lipid metabolism reprogramming in CAFs plays a crucial role in reshaping TME [39]. Previous studies have reported that, to adapt to the TME, CAFs undergo metabolic alterations, including increased lipid uptake, synthesis, and storage [16, 17, 40]. In our study, we identified that CD36 + CAFs exhibit enhanced OxLDL uptake in osteosarcoma, likely as an adaptive response to the OxLDL-enriched TME. Upon OxLDL uptake, CD36 + CAFs activate lipid metabolism-related pathways, particularly the PPAR signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of CD36 and FABP4, thereby forming a positive feedback loop. This further supports the notion that CD36 + CAFs exhibit adaptive metabolic behavior in response to their microenvironmental conditions. Extensive research has demonstrated that CAFs interact with immune cells and other immune components to regulate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), ultimately suppressing anti-tumor immunity [19]. Our findings provide strong additional evidence that CD36 + CAFs secrete ANGPTL4, which induces CD8 + T cell exhaustion and dysfunction, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive TIME.

An immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) is a major contributor to the limited efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies. Targeting the TME with combination strategies is therefore considered an essential and effective approach to enhancing ICB responses [41]. Vitamin E (VitE), a well-known antioxidant, has also been recognized for its immune-boosting properties [42]. Recent studies have reported that VitE enhances the anti-tumor efficacy of ICB therapy by acting on dendritic cells (DCs) [43]. In our study, we demonstrated that VitE effectively scavenges OxLDL in the TME, thereby reshaping the immune microenvironment and significantly enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in murine osteosarcoma models. These findings provide new insights into potential strategies for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy in osteosarcoma.

This study has several limitations. We mainly focused on CAFs and their immunosuppressive mechanisms, without providing a comprehensive profiling of other TME components such as MDSCs and macrophages, which are also critical in shaping immune suppression [2, 44]. Moreover, the relatively small single-cell cohort (n = 8; 3 responders vs. 5 non-responders) may limit statistical power, largely due to difficulties in sample acquisition and tissue dissociation. Nonetheless, we complemented our findings with immunohistochemistry in an expanded clinical cohort, which helps strengthen the reliability of our conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified CD36⁺ CAFs as a critical immunosuppressive population in osteosarcoma. These CAFs adaptively uptake OxLDL, activating the PPARG–FABP4 axis and upregulating ANGPTL4, which binds integrin α5β1 on CD8⁺ T cells to activate the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, thereby driving CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and dysfunction. Targeting this lipid-driven CAF–T cell crosstalk with the antioxidant vitamin E effectively scavenges OxLDL, remodels the tumor microenvironment, and synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo. Together, these findings suggest that lipid metabolism–immune interactions may contribute to immunotherapy resistance in osteosarcoma and indicate that targeting CAF-mediated pathways could represent a potential strategy to improve therapeutic responses.

Methods

Human OSA samples

For scRNA-seq, 8 surgical samples from OSA patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with anti-PD1 immunotherapy were obtained from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) and confirmed histologically (Supplementary Table S1). Clinical response to treatment was assessed based on postoperative pathological tumor necrosis rate(Supplementary Table S3). A tumor necrosis rate > 0.5 was considered the response group, while the remaining samples were classified as the non-response group. All samples were obtained from patients with informed consent. The procedures followed in this study adhere to the ethical standards confirmed by the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the SYSUCC Ethics Committee.

Tissue processing for scRNA-seq

Osteosarcoma specimens designated for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) were processed using an enzymatic digestion protocol to obtain a single-cell suspension. Briefly, the samples were washed with PBS, minced into small fragments, and digested in a solution containing 2 mg/ml Collagenase II, 2 mg/ml Collagenase IV, 1 mg/ml DNase I, and 100 mg/ml CaCl₂ (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) at 37 °C under constant agitation for 30 min. The digestion was extended for an additional 40 min, with thorough resuspension every 10 min. The digested mixture was then filtered through a 70 μm MACS SmartStrainer (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and centrifuged at 400 ×g for 4 min at 4 °C. To remove red blood cells, the pellet was resuspended in RBC lysis buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) and incubated on ice for 7 min, followed by centrifugation at 400 ×g for 4 min at 4 °C. The final cell pellet was resuspended in DPBS (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and diluted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml for scRNA-seq.

Bioinformatics analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed using R package “Seurat” for clustering and differential expression. The 3D UMAP plots were generated using R package “scatterplot3d”. Cell communication networks were assessed using R package “CellChat”. GO and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted using R package “clusterProfiler”. Transcription factor activity analysis was performed using R package “Dorothea”. Sarcoma immune infiltration analysis was performed using the TIMER 2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/)and TISIDB database (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/). Target-OS project was accessed through cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Immune infiltration analysis of Target-OS project was analyzed using R package “CIBERSORT”. Protein structure prediction and molecular docking analysis were performed using AlphaFold (https://alphafoldserver.com/), Swissdock (https://www.swissdock.ch/) and ChimeraX (1.8 version), respectively.

Primary cell isolation

Human primary fibroblasts were isolated using magnetic cell separation (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Briefly, freshly collected osteosarcoma primary tumor (OSA pTU) or matched periosteum tissue was transferred into Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Gibco, USA, #14025134). The tissue was minced into small fragments and digested in Collagenase I (200 U/mL, Corning, USA, #354249), Hyaluronidase (200 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), and DNase I (150 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, #H3506) at 37 °C for 30 min. The mixture was then mechanically dissociated using a GentleMACS™ Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany, #130-093−235), followed by filtration through a 70 μm cell strainer. Red blood cells were selectively lysed using RBC lysis buffer, and the remaining cells were washed several times with PEB buffer (PBS containing 2 mM EDTA and 0.5% BSA) via centrifugation at 300 ×g. For human fibroblast isolation, the Human Anti-Fibroblast MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-050−601) were incubated with the single-cell suspension in PEB buffer at 4 °C for 15 min. Positive selection was performed using two sequential LS/MS separation columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). For mouse fibroblast isolation, the Tumor-Associated Fibroblast Isolation Kit, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec, #130–116474−1) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of peripheral blood CD8 + T cells

Peripheral blood (5 mL) was collected from human donors, and CD8 + T cells were isolated following the manufacturer’s instructions for the human CD8 + T cell isolation kit(RWD, China, K1203-10). Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained using density gradient centrifugation. A 100 µL cell suspension (~ 1 × 10⁷ cells) was incubated with 10 µL of magnetic antibody labeling reagent. The labeled suspension was then passed through a magnetic separation column, where unlabeled cells were depleted, yielding purified CD8 + T cells for further analysis.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/cJGpt (BALB/c, RRID: IMSR_GPT: N000020) mice, aged 4–6 weeks, were purchased from Gempharmatech-GD (Guangdong, China). Mice were housed under standard conditions in the animal facility of SYSUCC. All murine experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at SYSUCC. Further details of the animal experiments are provided below. For the co-injection experiment, 1 × 10⁶ K7M2 cells were mixed with pretreated CAFs at a 3:1 ratio and subcutaneously injected into mice to establish a subcutaneous tumor model. For the immunotherapy experiments, 1 × 10⁶ K7M2 cells were injected subcutaneously into mice to establish subcutaneous tumor models, or inoculated adjacent to the tibia to establish orthotopic paratibial models. When tumors reached approximately 50 mm³, mice with comparable tumor burdens were randomly assigned to different treatment groups. Mice were treated with α-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, five doses), vitamin E (50 mg/kg, daily, oral gavage), or a combination of both until the experimental endpoint, at which time they were euthanized. For the subcutaneous tumor model, xenografted tumor volumes were monitored and measured in three dimensions using Vernier calipers, and calculated according to the formula: volume (mm³) = 1/2 × length (mm) × (width (mm))². For the orthotopic paratibial model, tumor progression was assessed by in vivo bioluminescence imaging to quantify fluorescence intensity.

Uptake of fatty acids or Lipoproteins, lipid peroxidation assay

For measuring uptake of fatty acids, cells were incubated in PBS containing 5ug/mL C1BODIPY 500/510 C12 (Beyotime, C2055) for 20 min at 37 °C. For measuring OxLDL uptake, cells were incubated in PBS containing 10 μg/mL DiI-labeled human high oxidized low density lipoprotein (yiyuan, #YB-0010) for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed with MACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS) for surface staining. For measuring lipid peroxidation, cells were incubated in PBS containing 2 mM BODIPY 581/591 C11 reagent (Cayman, 27086) for 30 min at 37 °C before live dead and surface staining.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin-embedded slides were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated with gradient ethanol and then subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval. Hydrogen peroxide (0.3%) in methanol was used to inhibit endogenous peroxidase, and goat serum (Thermo, USA, # 16210064) with 0.3% Triton-X100 (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany, #T8787) in PBS was applied for nonspecific antigen blocking. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing and incubating with an appropriate biotin-conjugated secondary antibody HRP donkey anti-rabbit or mouse antibody (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA, # 715-035−150 or #711-035−152) for 45 min, sections were stained with a TSA biotin detection kit (Perkin-Elmer, USA, # NEL700001KT) for signal amplification and DAB (Dako, Denmark, # K3468) for detection. Images of six pictures of randomly chosen visual fields from each section at 20X and 40X magnification were obtained with a NIKON Eclipse Ni-U Microscope (NIKON, Japan). Images were analyzed with NIH ImageJ 1.51. The final immunohistochemistry scoring was evaluated by two independent pathologists without knowing the patients’ relevant information. H scores were calculated as the multiplication of the density and intensity scores. The averages of the scores above were calculated for statistical analysis.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence of OSA sections

Multiplex immunofluorescence of paraffin-embedded OSA specimens was performed using the PANO TSA Opal multiplex kit (Panovue, China) on an automated staining platform. BOND Polymer Refine Detection was employed for polymeric HRP-linker secondary antibody conjugates, amplified by tyramide signal amplification (TSA) with fluorescent Opal dye. Tissue specimens were stained with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and TSA-Alexa Fluor for signal amplification. Antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase quenching were performed before restaining with different primary-secondary HRP complexes and corresponding TSA-Alexa Fluor dyes. Nuclei were counterstained with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo, USA, # P36962). Whole-slide digital images were acquired using the PANNORAMIC SCAN II System (3DHISTECH) at 20x magnification, and analysis was conducted with HALO image analysis software (Leica Labs, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Subcutaneous tumors from mice in each experimental group were collected and cut into small pieces in a culture dish. The samples were treated with 1 mg/ml Collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, #C5138) and 20 units/ml DNase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, #D5205), then placed into gentleMACS™ C tubes for tissue dissociation using the gentleMACS™ dissociator (Miltenyi Biotech, #130-093−235). The resulting single-cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm filter and red blood cells were removed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min using 1x BD Pharm Lyse™ (BD Biosciences, #555899). The cells were then resuspended in PBS for flow cytometry analysis. In brief, the single-cell suspension was stained with KO525 viability dye 510 (BD Biosciences, #564406) at 4 °C for 15 min and washed twice by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min with staining buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA). Cells were then blocked with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibody (BD Biosciences, #553143) in 50 µl Brilliant Staining Buffer (BD Biosciences, Cat#566349) at 4 °C for 5 min. After blocking, cells were incubated with a specific antibody cocktail targeting surface markers of interest for 30 min at 4 °C. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed in Fix/Perm buffer (BD Biosciences, #554714) for 15 min to permeabilize the membrane, followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min and two washes with permeabilization buffer. The cells were then stained with primary antibodies against intracellular proteins for 30 min at 4 °C in permeabilization buffer. Flow data were acquired using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo 10 software.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Cells were lysed using a lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and the cell debris was removed by centrifugation. The supernatant was incubated with protein A/G beads for 1 h to eliminate nonspecific binders. Antibodies against OxLDL and PPARG, along with IgG as a negative control antibody, were added to the supernatant and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G agarose beads were then added, and incubation continued for 1–2 h. The beads were washed multiple times with PBS buffer to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. The target protein was eluted using SDS sample buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting to detect the target protein and its interacting partners.

Co-Culture experiment

After isolation, CD8⁺ T cells were counted using trypan blue exclusion and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml. Cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10 mM HEPES, and 100 U/ml IL-2. For activation, CD3/CD28 beads (Proteintech, #KMS310-10) were added at 10 µl/ml, and cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For the co-culture experiment, CAF-conditioned medium was filtered using a 0.22 μm filter and mixed with CD8⁺ T cell complete culture medium at a 1:1 ratio before being used for CD8⁺ T cell culture. Additionally, ANGPTL4 recombinant protein was added at a final concentration of 10 µg/ml.

Pull-down assay

Membrane proteins were extracted from activated human CD8⁺ T cells using a membrane protein extraction kit (Beyotime, P0033) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0012). Recombinant His-tagged human ANGPTL4 protein was incubated with 100 µg of membrane proteins at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rotation. Subsequently, His-tag protein magnetic beads (Beyotime, P2226) were added and incubated for an additional 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with wash buffer containing 20 mM imidazole to reduce non-specific binding. Bound proteins were eluted with 250 mM imidazole in elution buffer, boiled in SDS sample buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by Western blot using antibodies against specific membrane proteins. Pull-down assays without ANGPTL4 were used as negative controls.

Flow Cytometry-Based proximity ligation assay (FlowPLA)

Activated and expanded human CD8⁺ T cells were collected and incubated with recombinant human ANGPTL4 protein on ice for 90 min. Following incubation, cells were immediately processed for proximity ligation assay. FlowPLA was performed using the Duolink® flowPLA Mouse/Rabbit Starter Kit – Green (Sigma-Aldrich, DUO94011) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and blocked using the provided blocking buffer for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies raised in mouse and rabbit targeting the proteins of interest. After washing, PLA probes (anti-mouse PLUS and anti-rabbit MINUS) were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Ligation and rolling circle amplification were performed sequentially at 37 °C for 30 min and 100 min, respectively. After amplification, cells were washed, resuspended in flow buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry using the FITC channel. Negative controls (without primary antibodies) and single-antibody controls were included to validate signal specificity.

RNAi-mediated Silencing

For siRNA transfection, CAFs were plated into 6-well plates at 30–50% confluency after 18 h. Cells were then transfected with 200 nM control siRNA or siRNA for human CD36, FABP4 and ANGPTL4 using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo, USA, Cat# L3000015) at 37 °C for 48 h following the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfected cells were harvested for analysis. The siRNA target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

CRISPR-Cas9 gene knockout

For CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, K7M2 CAF cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 50–70% confluency. Lentivirus was added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10, along with polybrene (8 µg/mL), and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. After transduction, the viral supernatant was removed and replaced with 2 mL of RPMI 1640/DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 h. Cells were then continuously selected in a conditioned medium containing 5.0 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days to obtain stable knockout cell lines. The sgRNA target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed on tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) and serum. Subcutaneous tumors and serum were harvested from K7M2 tumor-bearing mice after treatment. Tumors were carefully excised, weighed, and processed to extract TIF. Briefly, tumors were minced into small fragments and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 3000 g, 4℃, 10 min and filtered through a 70 μm membrane. Blood samples were collected from cardiac puncture separated by centrifugation at 3000 g, 4℃, 10 min. OxLDL (Elabscience, E-EL-M0066), 13-HODE (MEIMIAN, MM-60095H2) and ANGPTL4 (Proteintech, KE00186) levels were measured using ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolated and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit (AG, China, AG21024) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was performed using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix for qPCR Kit (AG, China, AG11706) to synthesize cDNA following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit Ⅲ (AG, China, AG11739), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. GAPDH was used as the endogenous control. The relative expression of RNA was calculated using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences are provided in the Supplementary Table S4.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was conducted using a SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (CST, USA, #9003) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cross-linked at room temperature with 1% formaldehyde to covalently link proteins to chromatin. Chromatin was then sheared by nuclease digestion and sonication to break the nuclear membrane, resulting in Chromatin lysates. Immunoprecipitation was performed using an antibody against PPARG (CST, USA, #2443S) and a control IgG (CST, USA, #2729). The cross-linked DNA-protein complexes were dissociated and the DNA fragments were purified for qPCR analysis. Primer sequences for each promoter region are provided in the Supplementary Table S4.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed with PBS three times, and protein was extracted from the cells using RIPA cell lysis buffer (Thermo, USA, # 89900) supplemented with 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, China, # B14001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, China, # B15001). Total protein concentration was determined and calculated using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo, USA, # 23225), separated on SDS–PAGE gels in 1x running buffer and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Germany, # ISEQ00010). The specific primary antibodies for immunoblotting against GAPDH (1:2000, CST Cat# 5174), phosphor-JAK2 (1:1000, Abcam #ab32101-40), phosphor-JAK1 (1:1000, Abcam #ab138005-40), JAK2 (1:1000, Abcam #ab108596), JAK1 (1:1000, Abcam #ab133666), phosphor-STAT3 (1:1000, Abcam #ab267373), STAT3 (1:1000, Abcam #ab68153), CD36 (1:1000, Abcam # ab252922), phosphor-p38 (1:1000, Abcam # ab195049), p38 (1:1000, Abcam # ab170099), PPARG (1:1000, CST #2443S), β-Tubulin (1:2000, CST Cat# 2128), ANGPTL4 (1:1000, Proteintech #18374-1-AP), FABP4 (1:1000, Proteintech # 12802-1-AP) and OxLDL(1:1000, Cloud-Clone Corp #PAA527Hu08) were used for incubation overnight at 4 °C. Following washes, western blots were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG secondary antibody (CWBIO, China, # CW0102/CW0103) for 2 h at room temperature. Clarity Luminol-based Western ECL Substrates for HRP-catalyzed detection (Bio-Rad, USA, #1705061) were incubated with blots, and luminescence was exposed and captured on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA, #12003154).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). The two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to determine statistical significance between two groups. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was performed to assess significant differences between specific groups. In cases where the data did not meet the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, non-parametric tests, such as the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, were applied. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (****p < 0.0001, ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05).
