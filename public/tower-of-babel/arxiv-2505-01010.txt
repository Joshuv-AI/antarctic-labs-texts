Quasi-local Frustration-Free Free Fermions
Shunsuke Sengoku, Hoi Chun Po, Haruki Watanabe

Abstract
Recent studies have revealed that frustration-free models, expressed as sums of finite-range interactions or hoppings, exhibit several properties markedly different from those of frustrated models. In this work, we demonstrate that, by relaxing the finite-range condition to allow for exponentially decaying hoppings, one can build gapped frustration-free systems that realize Chern insulators as well as quasi-degenerate ground states with finite-size splittings. Moreover, by permitting power-law decaying hoppings, we also construct a gapless band metal whose finite-size gap scales inversely with the system size L L . These findings serve as an important step toward clarifying the general properties of frustration-free systems and those represented by tensor network states.



I
Introduction

Frustration-free models have played a pivotal role in theoretical studies of quantum many-body systems, owing to the relative ease with which their ground-state properties can be analyzed. Such models have deepened our understanding of diverse phenomena, including symmetry-broken phases
Majumdar and Ghosh 1969
;
Majumdar 1970
;
Mielke 1993
;
Mielke 1999
;
Tasaki 1992
, symmetry-protected topological phases
Affleck et al. 1988
;
Tasaki 2020
;
Katsura et al. 2015
;
Wouters et al. 2018
;
Jevtic and Barnett 2017
, and topologically ordered phases
Kitaev 2003
;
Levin and Wen 2005
. A system is said to be frustration-free if its Hamiltonian can be expressed as a sum

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i},

(1)

such that the ground state of the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
simultaneously minimizes each individual term
\hat{H}_{i}

Tasaki 2020
.

Recent investigations have uncovered several distinctive properties that arise when each
\hat{H}_{i}
involves only finite-range interactions and hoppings. For instance, in gapped systems, ground-state degeneracies often remain exact even at finite system sizes
Masaoka et al. 2024
. In gapless systems, the finite-size energy gap typically scales at least as quickly as
1/L^{2}
, where
L
denotes the linear system size
Changlani et al. 2018
;
Palle and Benton 2021
;
Jones et al. 2021
;
Ogunnaike et al. 2023
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Watanabe et al. 2024
;
Masaoka et al. 2024
. This statement has been rigorously proven under open boundary conditions
Gosset and Mozgunov 2016
;
Anshu 2020
;
Lemm and Xiang 2022
;
Lemm and Lucia 2024
. However, it remains unclear whether these properties persist when the local terms
\hat{H}_{i}
are allowed to include longer-range tails, such as exponentially or algebraically decaying interactions.

Another important feature of frustration-free systems concerns their topological properties.
It is known that local commuting projector Hamiltonians acting on finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces cannot host chiral topological phases, such as those with thermal Hall conductance or (fractional) quantized Hall conductance
Kitaev 2003
;
Kapustin and Fidkowski 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
. Although free-fermion chiral phases can be realized by Gaussian fermionic projected entangled-pair states (PEPS),
these PEPS exhibit power-law correlations, and their parent Hamiltonians are necessarily either gapless or nonlocal with power-law hopping tails
Wahl et al. 2013
;
Wahl et al. 2014
,
1
1

1

For the example in Ref.
Wahl et al. 2014
, the power-law exponent is
3
.

reflecting the fact that Gaussian fermionic tensor network states with local, gapped parent Hamiltonians cannot realize chiral phases
Dubail and Read 2015
. This is closely related to the absence of compactly supported Wannier functions in Chern insulators
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Read 2017
.

These observations have led to the following conjecture:
no gapped frustration-free Hamiltonian can realize a chiral phase
.
Evidence for this has been found in free-fermion frustration-free systems with strictly short-range interactions
Ono et al. 2025
;
Masaoka et al. 2025
, and further supported by the incompatibility between chiral edge modes with linear dispersion (implying a finite-size gap of order
L^{-1}
) and the general
O(L^{-2})
scaling of the finite-size gap in gapless frustration-free systems
Lemm and Mozgunov 2019
.
However, it remains open
whether a gapped frustration-free Hamiltonian with exponentially decaying hoppings and/or many-body interactions under periodic boundary conditions could host a chiral phase.

In this work, we address how the relaxation of the assumption of strictly finite-range terms in the frustration-free decomposition affects the scaling property of the excitation gap and the representability of gapped chiral phases.
We show that allowing each local term
\hat{H}_{i}
to have exponentially decaying tails enables the realization of gapped, frustration-free free-fermion models exhibiting nontrivial band topology. These include models with nonzero Chern numbers and edge states, leading to quasi-degenerate ground states with finite-size splittings. A similar construction was demonstrated recently in Ref.
Jones et al. 2021
for the transverse-field Ising model, showing finite-size splitting in a sequence of exponentially decaying frustration-free Hamiltonians. Moreover, extending the decay profile to algebraic (power-law) forms allows us to construct gapless models whose finite-size gaps scale as
1/L
. Crucially, while the individual terms
\hat{H}_{i}
may exhibit exponential or power-law decay, the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
itself remains strictly local, consisting solely of finite-range hoppings.

These findings underscore the sharp distinction between frustration-free decompositions with strictly finite-range terms and those permitting longer-range tails. Our results thereby sharpen the aforementioned conjecture into

no gapped frustration-free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
can realize a chiral phase if each local term
\hat{H}_{i}
in the frustration-free decomposition is finite-ranged
. As such, we provide a framework for investigating more general classes of frustration-free systems—including those described by tensor network states such as PEPS—and highlight the role of extended hopping tails in circumventing previously assumed constraints.

II
Formulation

II.1
Tight-binding model

In this section, we consider a general tight-binding model of the following form:

\displaystyle\hat{H}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}h_{i,j}\hat{c}_{j}=\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}h\hat{\bm{c}},

(2)

where
h
is the matrix whose elements are the hopping amplitudes
h_{i,j}
. This matrix can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix
U
as

\displaystyle h_{i,j}=\sum_{n}U_{i,n}\epsilon_{n}U_{j,n}^{*}.

(3)

Using the projector

\displaystyle(P_{n})_{i,j}\coloneqq U_{i,n}U_{j,n}^{*}

(4)

onto the eigenspace corresponding to
\epsilon_{n}
, we can decompose
h
into its positive-energy and negative-energy components:

\displaystyle h=\sum_{n}\epsilon_{n}P_{n}=h^{(+)}+h^{(-)},

(5)

\displaystyle h^{(\pm)}\coloneqq\sum_{n|\pm\epsilon_{n}>0}\epsilon_{n}P_{n},

(6)

so that the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
2
) can also be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}h^{(+)}\hat{\bm{c}}+\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}h^{(-)}\hat{\bm{c}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}h^{(+)}\hat{\bm{c}}+\hat{\bm{c}}^{T}(-h^{(-)}{}^{T})\bigl(\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}\bigr)^{T}+E_{0},

(7)

where
E_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}[h^{(-)}]=\sum_{\epsilon_{n}<0}\epsilon_{n}
is the ground-state energy.

To write down the ground state
|\Phi\rangle
, we introduce the creation operators for eigenmodes

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}U_{i,n}.

(8)

The ground state is the fully occupied state of the negative-energy levels:

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle\coloneqq\prod_{\epsilon_{n}<0}\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{\dagger}|0\rangle.

(9)

Here and hereafter, we assume that
h
has no eigenvalue exactly equal to zero.
2
2

2

If such an eigenvalue exists, the degeneracy of the ground state must be taken into account.

The projector in Eq. (
4
) is related to the ground-state two-point functions:

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}U_{j,n}^{*}\langle\Phi|\hat{\gamma}_{n}\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle U_{i,n}=\sum_{\epsilon_{n}>0}(P_{n})_{i,j},

(10)

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}U_{j,n}^{*}\langle\Phi|\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\Phi\rangle U_{i,n}=\sum_{\epsilon_{n}<0}(P_{n})_{i,j}.

(11)

II.2
Frustration-free decomposition

Next, we convert this Hamiltonian to a frustration-free form. In Appendix D.1.2 of Ref.
Kitaev 2006
, the case of quadratic Hamiltonians of Majorana fermions is discussed. Here, we translate that argument into tight-biding models of complex fermions.

To this end, note that
+h^{(+)}
and
-h^{(-)}
in Eq. (
6
) are both positive-definite. Hence, their square-root

\displaystyle\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\coloneqq\sum_{n|\pm\epsilon_{n}>0}\sqrt{|\epsilon_{n}|}P_{n}

(12)

is well-defined. We can rewrite the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
7
) as

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}\sqrt{h^{(+)}}\sqrt{h^{(+)}}\hat{\bm{c}}

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{\bm{c}}^{T}\sqrt{-h^{(-)}{}^{T}}\sqrt{-h^{(-)}{}^{T}}(\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger})^{T}+E_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}+E_{0},

(13)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(+)})^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(+)}+\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(-)}(\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(-)})^{\dagger},

(14)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(\pm)}\coloneqq\sum_{j}\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{i,j}\hat{c}_{j}=\sum_{n|\pm\epsilon_{n}>0}\sqrt{|\epsilon_{n}|}U_{i,n}\hat{\gamma}_{n}.

(15)

Clearly,
\hat{H}_{i}
is a sum of positive-semidefinite terms. Moreover, both
\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(+)}
and
\big(\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(-)}\big)^{\dagger}
annihilate the ground state
|\Phi\rangle
because
\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(+)}
is a sum of
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
of positive energy states and
\big(\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(-)}\big)^{\dagger}
is a sum of
\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{\dagger}
of negative energy states. Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}|\Phi\rangle=0

(16)

for all
i
simultaneously, implying that the Hamiltonian with the decomposition in Eq. (
13
) is frustration-free.

The square-root of the matrices in Eq. (
12
) can also be represented by the ground-state correlation functions as

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{+h^{(+)}}\Big)_{i,j}=\langle\Phi|\hat{c}_{i}\sqrt{\hat{H}-E_{0}}\,\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle,

(17)

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{-h^{(-)}}\Big)_{i,j}=\langle\Phi|\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{H}-E_{0}}\,\hat{c}_{i}|\Phi\rangle.

(18)

II.3
Exponential localization

In general,
\hat{H}_{i}
constructed above is not necessarily local. However, if
\hat{H}
is local and its spectrum is gapped, each
\hat{H}_{i}
becomes quasi-local with exponentially decaying tail.

To state this result more precisely, suppose that
\hat{H}
is local in the sense that

\displaystyle|h_{i,j}|<C_{0}e^{-A_{0}d(i-j)}

(19)

for positive constants
C_{0}
and
A_{0}
, where
d(i,j)
denotes the distance between the indices
i
and
j
.
In one-dimensional models with open boundary conditions, one may simply set
d(i,j)=|i-j|
, but we are interested in more general settings.
Furthermore, assume there is a gap

\displaystyle\Delta\coloneqq\min_{n|\epsilon_{n}>0}\{\epsilon_{n}\}-\max_{n|\epsilon_{n}<0}\{\epsilon_{n}\}>0

(20)

between the positive and negative eigenvalues, and that this gap remains nonzero in the limit of large system size. Then one can show

\displaystyle\big|\big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\big)_{i,j}\big|<Ce^{-Ad(i-j)}

(21)

for some constants
C
and
A
, implying that each
\hat{\psi}_{i}^{(\pm)}
is exponentially localized.
As we discuss below in more details for the example of a Chern insulator, when the system is translation-invariant the asserted exponential decay in Eq. (
21
) follows from the analyticity of its momentum-space counterpart when it is extended to complex momenta.
We will see this exponential decay in more detail for band insulators.

III.1
One-band model

We consider the simplest one-dimensional tight-binding model:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-t\sum_{i=1}^{L}\big(\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}+\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i+1}\big)\quad(t>0),

(22)

which can be solved by the Fourier transformation
\hat{c}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{-ikj}\hat{c}_{j}
.
The band dispersion
\epsilon_{k}=-2t\cos k
is negative for
|k|<\frac{\pi}{2}
and positive for
\frac{\pi}{2}<|k|<\pi
as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). To avoid a zero-energy eigenstate, we choose
L

not
to be an integer multiple of four.

The ground state is the fully occupied state of negative energy states and the vacuum state of positive energy states:

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle=\prod_{-\frac{\pi}{2}<k<\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}|0\rangle.

(23)

The ground state energy is
E_{0}=\sum_{-\frac{\pi}{2}<k<\frac{\pi}{2}}\epsilon_{k}
.

In this model, excitations are gapless, and the finite-size gap scales as
O(L^{-1})
. For example, when the system size is
L=4m+2
, the smallest excitation energy occurs at at
k_{*}\coloneqq\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi}{L}
[see Fig.
1
(a,b)]

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k_{*}}=2t\sin\Big(\frac{\pi}{L}\Big)=O(L^{-1}).

(24)

III.2
Frustration-free decomposition

The Hamiltonian (
22
) can be written in a frustration-free form:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}+E_{0}=\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}\,\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k},

(25)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j}\coloneqq(\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(+)})^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(+)}+\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(-)}(\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(-)})^{\dagger},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(\pm)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j^{\prime}}\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{j,j^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{j^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k|\pm\epsilon_{k}>0}\sqrt{|\epsilon_{k}|}\,e^{ikj}\hat{c}_{k}.

(27)

Because
\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(+)}
and
(\hat{\psi}_{j}^{(-)})^{\dagger}
annihilate the ground state
|\Phi\rangle
, it follows that
\hat{H}_{j}|\Phi\rangle=0
for all
j
and the Hamiltonian is frustration-free under this decomposition.

In this model, excitations are not gapped and

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{i,j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k|\pm\epsilon_{k}>0}\sqrt{|\epsilon_{k}|}\,e^{ik(i-j)}

(28)

do not decay exponentially in
|i-j|
. Instead, we find a power-law decay of order
|i-j|^{-3/2}
:

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{h^{(+)}}\Big)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sqrt{-h^{(-)}}\Big)_{i,j}\,e^{i\pi(i-j)},

(29)

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{-h^{(-)}}\Big)_{i,j}

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\sqrt{2t\cos k}\,e^{ik(i-j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{t\pi}}{4}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{5}{4}-\frac{i-j}{2})\Gamma(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{i-j}{2})}

\displaystyle\simeq\sqrt{\frac{t}{4\pi}}\frac{(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{|i-j|-1}{2}\rfloor}}{|i-j|^{3/2}}.

(30)

Here,
\Gamma(z)
is the gamma function and
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer not exceeding
x
. In deriving the final expression, we replaced the sum over
k
by an integral, which becomes exact in the large-
L
limit. We then used the reflection formula
\Gamma(z)\Gamma\bigl(1-z\bigr)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)
and Stirling’s approximation
\Gamma(z)\simeq\sqrt{\tfrac{2\pi}{z}}\bigl(\tfrac{z}{e}\bigr)^{z}
. A numerical check of this power-law decay is shown in Fig.
1
(c).

IV.2
Frustration-free decomposition

Let us decompose the Hamiltonian into a frustration-free form.
To this end, we note that the projector onto the eigenvalue
\pm\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
of
h_{\bm{k}}
can be written as

\displaystyle P_{\bm{k},\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}\pm\frac{h_{\bm{k}}}{2\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}.

(35)

Using this expression, we define

\displaystyle(h^{(\pm)})_{\bm{R}\sigma,\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\bm{k}}(\pm\epsilon_{\bm{k}})(P_{\bm{k},\pm})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime})}.

(36)

Then the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
31
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}+E_{0},

(37)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma=1}^{2}\Big(\big(\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(+)}\big)^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(+)}+\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(-)}\big(\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(-)}\big)^{\dagger}\Big),

(38)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(\pm)}\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{\bm{R}\sigma,\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}.

(39)

Clearly,
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is the sum of positive-semidefinite terms. Also,
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle=0
since
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(+)}|\Phi\rangle=\big(\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(-)}\big)^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle=0
. Therefore, the Hamiltonian with this decomposition is frustration-free.

Now we show that
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
has an exponentially decaying tail.
Since
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}>0
for any
\bm{k}
, the quantity
\sqrt{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}P_{\bm{k},\pm}
is an analytic function of
\bm{k}
.
Moreover,
we can extend
\bm{k}
to complex values, and
\sqrt{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}P_{\bm{k},\pm}
remains analytic in
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
within the domain
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}[\mathrm{Im}(k_{\alpha})]^{2}<A^{2}
. Here
A>0
defined by

\displaystyle A\coloneqq\begin{cases}\log(|m|+1)&(\sqrt{2}>|m|>0)\\
|\log(|m|-1)|&(|m|>\sqrt{2})\end{cases}.

(40)

and Fig.
2
(b) shows that
A
depends on
m
in a way similar to the excitation gap in Fig.
2
(a).
It follows that the matrix elements exhibit exponential decay of the form

\displaystyle\Big|\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{\bm{R}\sigma,\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\Big|<Ce^{-A|\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}|}

(41)

for a constant
C>0

3
3

3

Mathematically this result is known as the Paley–Wiener theorem. See Sec 4 and Sec 7.1 of Ref.
Katznelson 2004
for details.

. This can be seen by
approximating the sum in Eq. (
36
) by an integral

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{\pm h^{(\pm)}}\Big)_{\bm{R}\sigma,\bm{R}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\simeq\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sqrt{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}(P_{\bm{k},\pm})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime})}

(42)

and then shifting
\bm{k}
to
\bm{k}+iA\frac{\bm{R}}{|\bm{R}|}
using Cauchy’s integral theorem.
This establishes the exponential locality of
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\sigma}^{(\pm)}
.
We numerically verify these results in Fig.

the ground-state correlations and hoppings of our Hamiltonian decay exponentially rather than following a power law.

V
Finite-size splitting of quasi-degenerate ground states

Finally, we consider a gapped frustration-free Hamiltonian that exhibits degenerate ground states with finite-size splitting.

V.1
Su–Schrieffer–Heeger model

We consider the Su–Schrieffer–Heeger model
Su et al. 1979
under open boundary conditions:

Let us now discuss the relation of our results to the more general arguments of Ref.

VII
Conclusion

Appendix D of Kitaev’s celebrated work
Kitaev 2006
contains two profound observations concerning frustration-free systems.
Applying the general arguments on free Majorana fermions to tight-binding models of complex fermions, we have constructed three explicit frustration-free, free-fermion models of the form
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
, each displaying behaviors previously deemed unachievable in this class. These include a system with a nonzero Chern number, another with quasi-degenerate ground states whose finite-size splitting persists, and a third with gapless excitations that exhibit finite-size gaps scaling as
1/L
.

In all three cases, the individual terms
\hat{H}_{i}
possess long tails—exponential in the first two and algebraic in the third. Crucially, however, this modification affects only the decomposition of the Hamiltonian: the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
itself remains composed entirely of finite-range interactions and hoppings.

We also revisited the second, more general argument in the same appendix
Kitaev 2006
. There, we pointed out that the modified local terms

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{i}
need not be positive-semidefinite.
We note that, in the limit of vanishing filtering gap
\Delta\rightarrow 0
, positive-semidefiniteness of the transformed local Hamiltonian terms may be restored. However, as the finiteness of the gap
\Delta
controls the spatial decay of the transformed interactions, this observation does not establish the existence of a frustration-free decomposition with exponentially decaying interactions.
Consequently, this argument does not rigorously establish frustration-freeness in the broader setting considered there.

Together, these findings offer new insights into both the potential and limitations of frustration-free systems, helping to refine our understanding of their general structure and physical realizability. As a natural direction for future research, it is important to rigorously establish or disprove the refined conjecture concerning the incompatibility between chiral phases and gapped frustration-free Hamiltonians composed of strictly finite-ranged local terms. Heuristically, if such a model under periodic boundary conditions were to exist, one could remove terms across the boundary to reinterpret the system as an open-boundary model while preserving frustration-freeness. This would conflict with the gap scaling constraints established under open boundary conditions, such as those in Ref.
Lemm and Mozgunov 2019
. While this heuristic supports the conjecture, a fully rigorous justification remains an important open problem.

\displaystyle\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\text{sign}(\sin k)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{B-\cos k}{\epsilon_{k}}\right)},

(100)

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}\coloneqq\sqrt{1+B^{2}-2B\cos k}.

(101)

Since
\theta_{-k}=-\theta_{k}
and
\epsilon_{-k}=\epsilon_{k}
, the definition
\hat{\gamma}_{k}=\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\hat{c}_{k}-i\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\hat{c}_{-k}^{\dagger}
is applicable when
k
is replaced by
-k
.
We have

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{k}=\frac{\sin k}{\epsilon_{k}},\quad\cos\theta_{k}=\frac{B-\cos k}{\epsilon_{k}}.

(102)

As we assume
B>1
,
\epsilon_{0}=B-1,\theta_{0}=0
and
\epsilon_{\pi}=B+1,\theta_{\pi}=0
.
After all, one can combine all terms and

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K_{P}}2\epsilon_{k}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k}-\sum_{k\in K_{P}}\epsilon_{k}-Le_{0}.

(103)

The ground state in the positive parity sector is

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0}\rangle\coloneqq N_{0}\prod_{k\in K_{P=+1}}\hat{\gamma}_{k}|0\rangle,

(104)

where
N_{0}>0
is the normalization factor.
The ground state energy is

\displaystyle E_{0}=-Le_{0}-\sum_{k\in K_{P=+1}}\epsilon_{k}=-Le_{0}-\sum_{m=1-L/2}^{L/2}\epsilon_{k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(m-\frac{1}{2})}

(105)

so that we identify

\displaystyle e_{0}=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=1-L/2}^{L/2}\epsilon_{k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(m-\frac{1}{2})}

(106)

assuming that the ground state in the other sector has a higher energy, which is indeed the case in this model.

On the other hand, the ground state in the odd parity sector (which contains both
k=0,\pi
) is

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1}\rangle\coloneqq\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}|\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\rangle,

(107)

where

\displaystyle|\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\rangle\coloneqq\tilde{N}_{0}\prod_{k\in K_{P=-1},k\neq 0,\pi}\hat{\gamma}_{k}|0\rangle

(108)

and
\tilde{N}_{0}>0
is the normalization factor. The ground state energy is

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=-Le_{0}-\sum_{k\in K_{P=-1}}\epsilon_{k}+2\epsilon_{0}=2(B-1)+\delta,

(109)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big(\sum_{m=1-L/2}^{L/2}\epsilon_{k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(m-\frac{1}{2})}\Big)-\Big(\sum_{m=1-L/2}^{L/2}\epsilon_{k=\frac{2\pi}{L}m}\Big).

(110)

Note that the quantity
\delta
is exponentially small with the system size.

Single-particle excited states, labeled by
k\in K_{P=-1}
including
k=0,\pi
, and their energy eigenvalues can be written as

\displaystyle|k\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}|\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\rangle,

(111)

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq E_{1}+2(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{0})=2\epsilon_{k}+\delta.

(112)

In particular, we identify the gap

\displaystyle E_{1}-E_{0}>\Delta\coloneqq 2(B-1).

(113)

B.2
Local Hamiltonians

Now we expand the local Hamiltonian using quasi-particle operators.

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{q^{\prime}\in K_{P}}e^{iq^{\prime}j}\Big(\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{q^{\prime}}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{q^{\prime}}+i\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{q^{\prime}}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{-{q^{\prime}}}^{\dagger}\Big),

(114)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{q\in K_{P}}e^{-iqj}\Big(\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{q}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{q}^{\dagger}-i\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{q}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{-q}\Big).

(115)

We keep only terms that keeps the number of quasi-particles (i.e., terms of the form
\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}
):

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in K_{P}}\frac{1}{L}e^{-ikj+ik^{\prime}l}\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in K_{P}}\frac{1}{L}e^{-ikl+ik^{\prime}j}\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}{2})\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quad+\text{($\hat{\gamma}_{-k}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}$ and $\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{-k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}$ terms)}.

(116)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{l}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in K_{P}}\frac{1}{L}e^{-ikj+ik^{\prime}l}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}i\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in K_{P}}\frac{1}{L}e^{-ikl+ik^{\prime}j}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}i\cos(\tfrac{\theta_{k}}{2})\sin(\tfrac{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\text{($\hat{\gamma}_{-k}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}$ and $\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{-k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}$ terms)}.

(117)

B.3
The spectrally filtered local operator

Since contributions from terms of the form
\hat{\gamma}_{-k}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{-k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}
are annihilated by the spectral filter, we arrive at the exact expression (
l=1,2,3
):

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{H}}_{j}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in K_{P}}e^{i(k^{\prime}-k)j}\tilde{h}_{k,k^{\prime}}^{(l)}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}+\frac{\delta}{L}\delta_{P,-1},

(127)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{k,k^{\prime}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tilde{w}(2(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k^{\prime}}))h_{k,k^{\prime}}^{(l)}.

(128)

We obtain the exact spectrum of
\hat{\tilde{H}}_{j}^{(l)}
by diagonalizing
H_{k,k^{\prime}}^{(l)}
.
