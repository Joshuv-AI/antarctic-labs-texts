Characterizing model structures on finite posets
Kristen Mazur, Angélica M. Osorno, Constanze Roitzheim, Rekha Santhanam, Danika Van Niel, and Valentina Zapata Castro

Abstract
. Transfer systems on finite posets have recently been gaining traction as a key ingredient in equivariant homotopy theory. Additionally, they also naturally occur in the data of a model structure. We give a complete characterization of all model category structures on a finite lattice, using transfer systems as our main tool, resulting in new connections between abstract homotopy theory and equivariant methods.



1.
Introduction

Model category structures are a strong foundation for homotopy theory, providing a practical and workable platform to discuss homotopy-related phenomena in different categories. Model structures usually arise in the context of spaces or spectra with extra structure, or elaborate categories arising from homological algebra, which is dependent on background knowledge of that category.
Studying model structures on the much simpler category of a finite lattice strips away the specialized technical layer and allows us to focus solely on the model category techniques.

The original definition of a model structure on a category is given via the three classes of morphisms: weak equivalences, fibrations, and cofibrations satisfying a set of strict but naturally occurring axioms. Due to the nature of the interactions between the classes of morphisms, all data of a model structure can be determined from less than the data given in the classical definition. In particular, a model structure can be completely determined from its weak equivalences and acyclic fibrations, which are the weak equivalences that are also fibrations.

When the underlying category is a finite lattice, the acyclic fibrations of a model structure form a
transfer system
, a subcategory that contains all objects and is closed under pullbacks. The name “transfer system” is a hint to their origins in equivariant homotopy theory. A transfer system defined on the subgroup lattice of an abelian group
G
encodes a wealth of information related to
G
-equivariant multiplicative structures in homotopy theory.
Therefore, considering model structures through the lens of transfer systems provides a fresh link between category theory and equivariant homotopy theory.

Consider, for example, the finite total order
[n]
, which is the subgroup lattice of the cyclic group of order
p^{n}
. The set of all transfer systems on
[n]
is well understood and can be connected to explicit notions from combinatorics
[
1
]
. Moreover, weak equivalence classes of model structures of
[n]
are given by partitions of
[n]

[
3
]
, and thus each model structure on
[n]
is defined by a partition that makes up the weak equivalence classes and a transfer system supported on the partition that makes up the acyclic fibrations. In fact, any partition
\mathsf{W}
of
[n]
paired with any transfer system contained in
\mathsf{W}
gives rise to a model structure on
[n]

[
3
]
. Therefore, we have the following bijection.

\{\text{model structures on }[n]\}\Leftrightarrow\{(\mathsf{W},T)\mid\text{$\mathsf{W}$ a partition of $[n]$ and $T\subseteq\mathsf{W}$ a transfer system}\}

We would like a generalization of this result for arbitrary finite lattices.
The appropriate generalization of a partition to any finite lattice is a
wide decomposable subcategory
,
i.e.
, a subcategory that contains all objects and that satisfies the property that if
g\circ f
is in the subcategory, then so are both
g
and
f
. Thus, on
[n]
every wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
with transfer system
T\subseteq\mathsf{W}
gives rise to a model structure. For a general lattice, Droz and Zakharevich prove in
[
6
]
that the weak equivalences of a model structure always form a wide decomposable category, but the result observed for
[n]
does not extend to other lattices. For instance, on the lattice
[1]\times[1]=\text{Sub}(C_{pq})
, every wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
occurs as the weak equivalence class of at least one model structure, but there are transfer systems
T\subseteq\mathsf{W}
where
T
does not occur as the acyclic fibrations to a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
, see
example

4.1
for more details. Even worse, on the lattice
[2]\times[1]
there are wide decomposable subcategories that do not occur as the weak equivalence class of a model structure at all, as we show in
example

4.2
.

Therefore, in order to fully understand model category structures on finite lattices, we have to answer the following questions.

•

Which wide decomposable subcategories of a finite lattice occur as the weak equivalence class of a model structure?

•

Suppose we know that a wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
is the weak equivalence class of at least one model structure. Which transfer systems
T\subseteq\mathsf{W}
occur as the acyclic fibrations of a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
?

In this paper, we provide complete and explicit answers to these questions.
The following are our main results.

Theorem

(
Theorem

5.8
)
.

Let
P
be a finite lattice and let
Q
be a wide decomposable subcategory of
P
. Then
Q
is the weak equivalence class of at least one model structure if and only if, for all morphisms
f\in Q
, there exists a factorization
f=\sigma_{n}\circ\sigma_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{1}
into indecomposable arrows such that for some
0\leq k\leq n
, both of the following hold.

•

For any
i\leq k
, all pushouts of
\sigma_{i}
are in
\mathsf{W}
.

•

For any
i>k
, all pullbacks of
\sigma_{i}
are in
\mathsf{W}
.

Theorem

(
Theorem

4.20
)
.

Let
P
be a finite lattice and
\mathsf{W}\subseteq P
a wide decomposable subcategory that is the weak equivalence class of at least one model structure. Then the set of transfer systems contained in
\mathsf{W}
that occur as the acyclic fibrations of a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
have a maximum element
\mathsf{AF}_{max}
and a minimum element
\mathsf{AF}_{min}
, such that a given transfer system
T\subseteq\mathsf{W}
occurs as the acyclic fibrations of a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
if and only if

\mathsf{AF}_{min}\subseteq T\subseteq\mathsf{AF}_{max}.

We furthermore show that
\mathsf{AF}_{max}
is the largest transfer system contained in
\mathsf{W}
, and give an explicit method for constructing
\mathsf{AF}_{min}
.

Finally, we not only give a complete characterization of model categories on finite posets, but also show that the characterizing conditions are practical, feasible, and applicable to a notable class of examples. In addition, our use of transfer systems provides a new, tangible link between the worlds of abstract homotopy theory, category theory, and equivariant topology.

A poset
(P,\leq)
defines a category with the elements of

(1)

Any diagram of composable arrows in a poset category commutes.

(2)

The join of two elements of

4.
From Transfer Systems to Model Structures

In this section, given a finite lattice

.

P
be a finite lattice and let

\mathsf{W}
and acyclic cofibrations

First, if there is a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{AF}=T

\mathsf{AC}={}^{\boxslash}T\cap\mathsf{W}

Definition 4.12

(
\mathrm{AF}(\mathsf{W})
and

\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{W}

By

The order-reversing bijection in

implies that
{}^{\boxslash}(T_{1}\wedge T_{2})
is the cotransfer system given by

(^{\boxslash}T_{1})\cup(^{\boxslash}T_{2})

Now take

A dual proof gives the second statement.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let

. There are
2^{2n+2}-2^{n+1}-2^{n}n

[n]\times[1]
.

Proof.

We first describe the form weak equivalence sets on
[n]\times[1]
must take. Since weak equivalence sets are decomposable, we can fully describe a weak equivalence set
\mathsf{W}
in terms of its short arrows.
By
Theorem

5.8
,
5.4
is satisfied, and hence the vertical arrows of
\mathsf{W}
must follow one of the following criteria:

•

none of the the vertical arrows are present,

•

all vertical arrows are present, or

•

if some are present and others are not then either:

–

the ones present are all stacked to the left,

–

the ones present are all stacked to the right, or

–

there is exactly one gap of any size in the middle.

5.4
does not impose any condition on the horizontal arrows of
\mathsf{W}
because if a horizontal arrow of
\mathsf{W}
is in the top row of

then there are no nontrivial pushouts of the arrow and if it is in the bottom row then there are no nontrivial pullbacks. Instead, requirements on the horizontal arrows stem from the layout of the vertical arrows, specifically whether or not two neighboring vertical arrows are in
\mathsf{W}

Since

\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{W}
. Thus, the two horizontal arrows between neighboring vertical arrows are either both in
\mathsf{W}
or both not in
\mathsf{W}
.

Now suppose that a vertical arrow is in
\mathsf{W}
but one of its neighboring vertical arrows is not. For example, suppose

\mathsf{W}
but

\mathsf{W}
, otherwise, composition and decomposability would imply

is in
\mathsf{W}
. However, there are no restrictions on the other horizontal arrow
(i,0)\to(i+1,0)
