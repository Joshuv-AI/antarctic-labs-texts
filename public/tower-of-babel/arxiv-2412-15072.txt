ScamChatBot: An End-to-End Analysis of Fake Account Recovery on Social Media via Chatbots
Bhupendra AcharyaCISPA, Dominik SautterSaarland UniversityMuhammad SaadX (formerly Twitter)Thorsten HolzCISPA

Abstract
Social media platforms have become the hubs for various user interactions covering a wide range of needs, including technical support and services related to brands, products, or user accounts. Unfortunately, there has been a recent surge in scammers impersonating official services and providing fake technical support to users through these platforms. In this study, we focus on scammers engaging in such fake technical support to target users who are having problems recovering their accounts. More specifically, we focus on users encountering access problems with social media profiles (e.g., on platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, Gmail, and X) and cryptocurrency wallets. The main contribution of our work is the development of an automated system that interacts with scammers via a chatbot that mimics different personas. By initiating decoy interactions (e.g., through deceptive tweets), we have enticed scammers to interact with our system so that we can analyze their modus operandi. Our results show that scammers employ many social media profiles asking users to contact them via a few communication channels. Using a large language model (LLM), our chatbot had conversations with 450 scammers and provided valuable insights into their tactics and, most importantly, their payment profiles. This automated approach highlights how scammers use a variety of strategies, including role-playing, to trick victims into disclosing personal or financial information. With this study, we lay the foundation for using automated chat-based interactions with scammers to detect and study fraudulent activities at scale in an automated way.

Introduction

In recent years, social media platforms have emerged as popular communication instruments for information exchange, opinion sharing, news gathering, and advertisement. Realizing the potential of social media becoming a one-stop shop that caters to all such needs of its users, popular brands have established their portfolios to engage with their customers and offer useful services such as technical support, dispute resolution, and discounts. As such, social media platforms have invariably paved the way for convenient interactions between businesses and customers for service requests and resolutions. A popular manifestation of this interaction is online technical support, where customers report issues about accessing their social media accounts or cryptocurrency wallets and expect a resolution service from their business counterparts. Users typically send these requests to the official profiles of the relevant business by making public posts and expecting a response from their customer service. However, there is an inherent risk in publicly posting such requests on social media, exposing users to scams
[

,

]
. Any user can impersonate an official business representative and offer fake technical support to the customer. In fact, this form of social engineering attack has significantly increased in recent years, and it is colloquially called
Technical Support Scam

This type of support scams can be launched under any pretext as long as a user is seeking online support and a scammer offering help by falsely claiming to be an expert. Across all scam typologies, the end goal is to gain the victim’s trust and lure them into making a payment or surrendering sensitive information that can be monetized. Although technical support scams have been ongoing since the early days of social media, their prevalence has significantly increased in recent years
[
5
,
7
]
especially targeting cryptocurrency users and users operating multiple social media accounts. This trend is not surprising, as cryptocurrencies have gained significant popularity due to the support of cryptocurrencies by some of the major online payment platforms, including PayPal. Moreover, with the increasing number of social media platforms, users with accounts on multiple platforms often experience account access issues due to lost passwords or account takeovers.

In this paper, we focus on these two scam typologies and deploy techniques to uncover technical support scammers.
Our proposed detection method is inspired by a recent work by Acharya et. al
[
8
]
where they conducted a deception study to bait scammers into revealing their identity. Their methodology involved requesting technical support through tweets, and upon receiving a response from the scammers, engaging with them through messages. Despite their methodology being highly effective, it had several limitations that could be improved to perform a more comprehensive study of the scam life cycle. In particular, the work
[
8
]
heavily relied on manually crafted messages during the scam engagement process, which reduced the scalability of their proposed system.

In this paper, we address the limitations and propose a more scalable and robust system that leverages more refined techniques to engage with scammers and gain deeper insights into the scam life cycle. With the rise of ML-based tools and Large Language Models (LLMs), we use the most advanced tools, ensuring efficiency and avoiding redundant efforts. More specifically, we use LLMs to craft human-style messages that are perceptually more believable and possess a higher propensity of trapping scammers. We introduce
ScamChatBot
, a composition of automated techniques designed to trap technical support scammers targeting cryptocurrency and social media users.

The second component engages scammers who offer support through communication channels (often outside of X), initiating conversations, and seeking assistance via the chat module ➍.
To mimic more believable and human-style conversations,
ScamChatBot
uses OpenAI’s ChatGPT for automated conversations with scammers.
Our goal is to uncover their methods and especially information related to the payment profiles used in scams because the payment profile is crucial to understanding and disrupting fraud.

To this end, we have developed a chatbot that can pretend to be different personas to interact with scammers.
We conducted conversations across three communication channels (Instagram, X, and email) because we found that these are the three channels commonly used by scammers.
Eventually, as the scam activity proceeds, scammers demand payments prior to offering technical support. We collect the payment methods and take appropriate actions to restrict the scam proliferation (i.e., sharing the collected information with payment providers). Novel to this work, our system exposes a worrying aspect of technical support scams. In prior works
[
8
]
, such scam campaigns were perceived to be operated by fraudsters who merely intended to pickpocket unsuspecting users. However, in our experiments, we discovered that scammers may also employ extortion techniques to threaten users with harm including personal information leakage. As a result, the impact of such scam campaigns extends far beyond financial losses, thus mandating the adoption of countermeasures and a necessity for more comprehensive works in this direction.

In the measurement period between the last week of November 2023 and the end of March 2024, we generated about 19,000 honeyposts on our honey profiles that attracted over 11,700 scammers interacting with our system asking to contact them back via X direct messages, Instagram, and email. Scammer accounts with a large number of scammer profiles have been found to be operated by a single scammer
[

. Through our analysis of scam network operation, we discovered that scammers utilize numerous social media profiles during interaction with honey profiles, and some scammers were found to employ as many as 71 social media profiles, prompting users to contact them via 1-2 communication channels. Among these scammers, we engaged in conversational dialogue with 450 scammers with our chatbot. By analyzing the timing of scammers’ responses, we observed their promptness, typically replying on all three scamming channels within two hours of the conversation initiation and normally spending approximately one hour before realizing that the victim is unlikely to comply with any requested payment method.

Through qualitative analysis of these conversations following a victim’s engagement with a scam, we identified several techniques used by scammers. These include requesting live video or phone verification, soliciting private information, urging victims to pay for fake support, and potentially resorting to extortion or emotional manipulation if payment demands are not met. In addition, we measured ML-related text generation from scammers and found that 28.4% of scammers are likely to use some form of automated text generation tools. In addition, analyzing the length of dialogue between the system and the scammer, we found that scammers on average reveal scamming payment profiles after seven interactive dialogues asking the system to pay for fake account recovery support, which highlights that an interaction is actually required to obtain relevant information. Furthermore, we collaborated with X, PayPal, and the cryptocurrency abuse database Chainabuse
[
10
]
, sharing scamming PayPal addresses and cryptocurrency wallets that scammers asked the system to pay for fake support, and we received validation of scam activities. Our work thus provides a foundational model for the industry and security community to develop similar frameworks for identifying different categories of fraud on social media platforms.

Contributions

We make the following key contributions:

•

Interactive Scam Detection.
We introduce
ScamChatBot
, an interactive, scalable, and LLM-based framework capable of unveiling scammer life cycles and techniques through interactive dialogues. The framework can be generalized to decoy scammers of various scam categories across multiple social media platforms.

•

Detecting Fraudulent Payments.
Our system collects the scamming payment profiles of fraudsters in real time, which would otherwise remain inaccessible without engaging with scammers directly. In an empirical study, we analyze technical support scammers on social media.

Ethical Consideration and Data Disclosure

In keeping with the ethical guidelines required for a deception study, we obtained IRB approval from our institution. When managing data related to scammers, we strictly adhered to the GDPR and the EU Data Management Policies
[
11
]
. We conducted a deceptive study to ensure scammers remained unaware of being observed, preserving the authenticity of their responses and behaviors. Informing them or allowing withdrawal would have compromised the research, and retroactive debriefing potentially risks alerting other scammers and retribution attacks from scammers. Our study may have impacted scammers by risking the banning of their payment-related information after it was disclosed to PayPal and the cryptocurrency abuse database. However, this disclosure potentially prevents future attacks on other victims. Our tweet module was configured to adhere to ethical standards when tweeting. In total, we generated about 19,000 tweets across six different accounts over 101 days, a small number compared to the enormous volume of tweets on X (approximately 500 million per day). We posted innocuous tweets to attract scammers targeting users seeking help to recover their accounts. We made sure not to engage with official cryptocurrency wallet support teams or social media accounts. Thus, the sole objective of our system was to interact with the scammers by faking unsuccessful payment attempts after we were asked to pay for the fake account recovery in order to collect the fraudsters’ payment profiles. Moreover, we communicated our findings to Meta, Google, X, ten cryptocurrency wallets, PayPal, and the cryptocurrency abuse database Chainabuse and received numerous positive responses. We worked closely with X, PayPal, and Chainabuse, confirming the scam validation and financial loss from these fraud profiles.

We share our code at Github repository
[
12
]
. However, for data related to scammers, we refrain from publicly sharing to avoid potential retribution attacks and plan to provide it to interested researchers or academics upon request.

System Design

In this section, we discuss the design of our framework
ScamChatBot
, which creates unique tweets to bait scammers and performs on-demand chat initiation with scammers using a chatbot.
As shown in Figure
1
, the system consists of three main elements to: (i) attract scammers via decoy interactions, (ii) an automated engagement mechanism powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) for conversations, and (iii) a tracking module that monitors and links the various interactions we have observed.
In the following, we discuss the individual components in more detail.

2.1
Attracting Scammers via Decoys

As a first step, we need to somehow get scammers to interact with us.
To do this, we create unique tweets (called
honeyposts
) that mimic a request for technical support to attract scammers. The tweets ask for assistance with logging into social media accounts or support for cryptocurrency wallets.
To generate honeyposts, we have designed tweets based on two main criteria: i) adherence to the length limits set by X posts
[
13
]
and ii) addressing users experiencing account access issues. These tweets are tailored to be posted every 15 minutes on X in English. To compose a tweet, we use logic with a three-sentence structure. The first sentence usually contains introductory phrases such as “Good morning”, “What happened”, or “I can’t believe it”. The second sentence, which is often coupled with the first, is aimed at users who are having problems with their accounts. Finally, the third sentence asks for urgent help, with or without a hashtag. These hashtags are randomly generated based on account-related keywords.

We utilized six X profiles, which we referred to as
honey profiles
, each dedicated to posting unique content for these accounts. Three profiles focused on providing social account recovery support, while the remaining three focused on support for wallet-related recovery as follows:

•

Social Media Account Honeyposts
: For social media tweets, we selected five popular services including Gmail, Instagram, Youtube, X, and Facebook based on their popularity in Google searches
[
14
,
15
]
. We then generated tweets in which the account pretends to be a user of those services experiencing login issues.

•

Cryptocurrency Honeyposts
:
For cryptocurrency-related tweets, we selected ten popular wallets reported in a prior study performed in a similar setup
[

]
. The wallets included

2.2
Automated Engagement with Chatbots

In this section, we provide an overview of the filtration techniques implemented to ensure automated chat conversations are performed exclusively with identified scammer communication channels, as well as the necessary chat configuration and account setup for engaging with scammers. Below we provide such details.

2.2.1
Engagement Pre-processing Filtration

The tweets posted by ScamChatBot prompt scammers to interact with them through
replies
and
quoted tweets
. In those interactions, scammers typically provide their contact details, such as email or social handles, asking for further interactions. As a follow-up to the scammer’s communication channel, we implemented an engagement mechanism that initiates an automated chat dialogue conversation with the scammer’s social media profile. To ensure that we do not interact with benign accounts in an automated way, we apply various filtration techniques before initiating a chat interaction, such as excluding verified X accounts, official social media platforms such as X, Google, cryptocurrency wallet services, and benign accounts. We provide further details on filtration techniques below.

•

Exclude Verified Accounts.
The engagement module removes all verified accounts that respond to the tweets. Typically, verified accounts are associated with official representatives of a platform, and they may respond in a sincere attempt to offer technical support. We therefore remove them from our study. We acknowledge that recent changes in X’s verification policy can lead to scammers acquiring fake verification for their profiles
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. However, we preferred a more conservative approach in an attempt to remove potential false positives even at the risk of ignoring true negatives.

•

Exclude official social media services.
In the current setup, the engagement module also excludes the top 20 social media services along with the top 100 wallet services to ensure that our chat module does not initiate any conversation with official support services. We collected 20 popular social media services
[
14
,
15
]
and official cryptocurrency wallet services
[
19
,
20
]
based on social media popularity search.

•

Benign Account Filtration.
For benign account filtration, we faithfully replicated the approach in
[
8
]
where the authors identified two key characteristics of scam accounts that were distinctly different from benign accounts. During the engagement process, scam accounts provide new communication channels (often outside of X) to continue the conversation and complete the scam activity. Moreover, scammers also pose as official representatives of the platform to gain the user’s trust. In contrast, there are also benign accounts that interact with users, often providing advice or expressing sorrow for the user’s despair. Those accounts do not interact with users beyond the initial interaction, thus displaying no indication of fraudulent intentions. We label such accounts as benign and exclude them from our study.

Additionally, we provide a manual qualitative analysis in Section
2.3.1
, which shows that the filtration techniques work well in practice.

2.2.2
Scammer Engagement

After the account filtration process, the engagement module creates personas using a chatbot based on an LLM such as ChatGPT to interact with the scammers on the platform channels they provided. For each communication channel, including Instagram, Gmail, or even X, we generate unique personas with the purpose of (i) understanding the scam activity, and (ii) extracting payment information provided by scammers. Therefore, the ChatGPT-configured persona mimics a human-like behavior while conversing with scammers and leverages context awareness and organic articulation approaches to gain the scammer’s confidence. In every new conversation, we ensure that there is no LLM-related text present that could be picked up by the scammer. Moreover, before our live experiments, we also conducted manual conversations with
ScamChatBot
to ensure it was fully capable of performing organic conversations and collecting the desired information, especially related to payment profiles.

Dialogue Generation and Direct Messaging

Dialogue
refers to the conversation between the scammer and our system aimed at resolving account-related issues. To facilitate dialogue communication, we selected communication channels based on fraudsters’ popularity asking our system to contact for fake support. These platforms consist of three channels: Email, X, and Instagram direct messages. In total, we create 15 distinct accounts (i.e., 5 per platform: X, Instagram, and Email). We allocate four accounts for each channel for the personas involving cryptocurrency and social media account recovery, while the remaining three are designated for language-based experimental personas. For each communication channel, we integrate the respective
APIs

[
21
,
22
,
23
]
to receive real-time updates when the scammer interacts with our system. Upon receiving messages from the scammer, we aggregate all previous interactions between the system and the scammer and generate a response based on the personas. Subsequently, we deliver the generated message to the scammer via each API as our response. In our direct messaging interactions with scammers, we avoid using the accounts designated for tweets. We separate our direct messaging accounts from our X platform tweet post accounts to effectively communicate with the scammers without suspicion.

We ended our conversations in two cases: (i) after the scammers provided multiple payment profiles (e.g., cryptocurrency addresses and PayPal email addresses) and (ii) when the scammers stopped communicating with us. In the first scenario, we concluded by stating that we would explore further alternatives (after receiving all payment profiles from the fraudsters). In the second scenario, we made at least one attempt to reconnect and asked the scammer if they would like to continue the chat in order to receive further assistance.

In Section
2.3.2
, we present the results of a manual qualitative analysis of our system’s ability to maintain context continuity and discussion on limitations in Section
7
. We empirically find that the approach is able to effectively identify payment methods from scammers.

Persona Types and Configuration

ChatGPT requires a specified persona configuration to delineate its role in direct message interactions with scammers. We establish two primary personas for ChatGPT’s configuration. The initial persona involves a user having issues with a cryptocurrency wallet, particularly tailored for newcomers to cryptocurrency or crypto investment who find themselves unable to access their wallet. The second persona simulates a user encountering difficulties accessing a social media platform such as X, Instagram, Facebook, or Gmail. In this scenario, the user communicates their inability to access their account and seeks assistance in recovering it. Furthermore, we conduct an experiment that expands upon these personas by introducing a user who lacks proficiency in English and desires to switch to other languages. In this context, the system initially prompts the scammer to determine if they are conversant in languages other than English and endeavors to communicate in the languages agreed upon during the dialogue.

Additionally, the persona includes additional rules in text generation. During the conversation, as the scammer asks questions about the account-related issues, the persona is asked to create a fake reason for having an access problem. Scammers often ask for some form of payment to resolve such an account issue via various payment methods. While doing so, scammers create a fake reason such as needing to buy software to recover the account or sending money to re-enable the account-blocked workflow. The persona set for ChatGPT also tries to ask for multiple payment methods so that the scammer can reveal as many payment methods as possible. For this, we take a deceptive approach by falsely claiming failure in the first payment method provided by the scammer. We repeat the process until the scammer runs out of options to share with us, at which point the engagement module ends the conversation. We provide additional detail on the configuration of chat persona and sample conversation between our system
ScamChatBot

2.3
Qualitative Evaluation of Dataset

In this section, we provide a manual qualitative evaluation of our dataset across several categories. To assess the effectiveness of our system conversations with scammers, we randomly selected 15 conversations each from Instagram, email, and X platform, evaluating a total of 45 interactions. Below, we provide details of our evaluation findings.

2.3.1
Legitimacy of Data Filtration and Methodology Accuracy

In this category of qualitative analysis, our focus was to identify the accuracy of our methodology. Specifically, we looked for instances where interactions occurred between our system and both malicious and legitimate accounts. Out of 45 conversations from three platforms, we found no instances where the system mistakenly engaged with legitimate users. Additionally, we observed that none of the conversations involved official support accounts.

2.3.2
Repetition in Conversation/Suspicion

In this category of qualitative analysis, our focus was on observing our system’s ability to maintain context continuity. We assessed the quality of conversations, particularly those involving repetitive exchanges or discussions where scammers attempted to determine if the system was a bot. We identified 3/45 conversations in which scammers noted repetitive apologies for not being able to send screenshots due to technical problems and perceived a lack of seriousness in taking action. In all three cases, scammers expressed suspicion, stating either that the system was likely a robot or that it was wasting time without genuine intent to resolve the issue. While the scammers became suspicious that they were interacting with a bot, they still disclosed their payment profiles. This demonstrates that our system was effective in identifying fraudulent payment profiles. Such identification can enable proactive measures to block fraudulent payments and prevent further scamming of potential victims.

2.3.3
Limitation to External Channel Communication

In this category of qualitative analysis, our system faced limitations in cases where scammers redirected communication to an external channel. Specifically, in three instances, scammers on X requested to connect via direct message on another X account. Since our system was not configured to interact through newly introduced channels during the conversation, these interactions did not proceed in-depth. We identified three accounts—two from X and one from email—that attempted this redirection. In one case, the system politely ended the conversation, while in the other two, it falsely claimed to have already contacted the referenced person and received no response. Additionally, in one email interaction, the scammer provided a template asking for cryptocurrency addresses and a passphrase. In all of these three instances, our system had a limitation in further communicating with the scammer-provided channel.

Scammer’s preference for email shows that not all scammers operate within the instant messaging model. In the following, we dive deeper into the modus operandi of scammers and provide an overview of their interactions.

Scam Lifecycle

For both account recovery contexts, scammers typically follow a consistent pattern: initially, scammers begin by creating a verification process for the victim’s account information. Once some form of verification is established, the scammer fabricates a false pretext and requests payment in various forms. Following this initial stage of verification, the scammer often instructs the potential victim to conduct an internal audit and then return with the details. Finally, the scammer suggests that after the payment is made, it may take some time for the account to be fully recovered due to essential technical actions required for recovery. Below, we provide further insights into detailed operational cycles performed by scammers.

Victim Verification Procedure

Initially, the scammer requests information such as the victim’s account information (e.g., wallet address or social media account), the timing of the issue occurrence, and any actions such as withdrawals or login actions performed lately. This initial conversation is followed by additional details to establish identities, such as location, email, name, phone number, and a video recording of the issue with the wallet or account. Oftentimes, scammers try to initiate communication via WhatsApp or Facebook to further verify the victim’s authenticity. During this verification phase, scammers frequently request screenshots of the account, and in some instances, they may also ask for secret phrases.

Advance Fee

After the victim verification process, scammers request an advance fee to initiate the recovery process. The advance fee is often requested under the pretext of purchasing software (e.g.,
Zeus Software
or
Trojan Horse
) which helps with the recovery process. In some cases, scammers also request seed phrases for wallets, which is one of the easiest methods to steal money. Seed phrases can be easily converted into private keys, which can then be used to transfer cryptocurrency assets to a new address.

Payment Method Instructions

Scammers request payment for their services through various methods. We observed different kinds of payout methods, e.g., Amazon or Apple gift cards, cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, or other online payment methods such as PayPal, Venmo, and CashApp. Alternatively, the scammer might direct the victim to an external link for payment. In the case of PayPal, scammers are found to instruct the victim to use the “send to family and friends” option. Upon consulting PayPal, we learned that payments through this option are not eligible for refund or dispute. Therefore, once the scam activity is completed, the victim cannot dispute the charge with PayPal. Finally, when the payment is completed, scammers also request a payment screenshot for confirmation.

Scammer’s Reaction to Failed Payment Attempt

As mentioned earlier, we used failed payment excuses to bait scammers into revealing all their payment methods. We noticed that scammers expressed resentment or frustration when we mentioned payment failures to them. Occasionally, they also used profane language and threatened to cause personal harm. Although we were using fake personas while interacting with scammers, it is indeed concerning since real users might have experienced such unwarranted abuse in the form of threats to them and their families. It is therefore pertinent to curtail this form of technical support scam, as it can also transition into extortion and abuse.

Investigation into Scammer Tactics

As a result, we could assess the communication channels being actively monitored by scammers, indicated by their swift response time. In the second analysis, we study the total duration of our engagement with the scammer where they persistently tried to trap us in hopes of obtaining any financial benefit. Our second analysis revealed the persistence shown by scammers in engaging with their victims.

4.2.2
Scam Network Operation

In this section, we perform a collaborative network analysis, centering on (i) the creation of scammer profiles, which includes examining names, descriptions, followers, and following; (ii) the utilization of shared communication channels by scammers such as instances of scammers sharing a common communication channel through tweet posts, urging potential victims to contact them for technical support.

Shared Profile Information

We perform an analysis of scammer profile details, including names, descriptions, followers, and following. Our investigation into scam networks revealed that 24.5% (2,877/11,764) scammers shared a common name, 33.6% (3,949/11,764) shared a common description, and 5.2% (610/11,764) scammers were found to have multiple common followers in their profiles. Among these 610 shared followers, we detected 503 instances of mutual following to more than one account. This leads us to suspect that these profiles represent a cluster of organized scamming operations aimed at executing targeted scamming campaigns.

Shared Communication Channels

Our analysis of clustering scamming profiles by communication channels indicates that 42.6% (5,012/11,769) of the profiles engaged in scamming activities were observed to use more than one communication channel. Within this subset of 5,012 scamming profiles, the highest number of communication channels employed by a single profile was 71, while the median value for other scamming profiles was 3 communication channels. Additionally, we found that 16.5% (1,939/11,769) of the scamming accounts used all three communication channels, prompting users to contact them.

5
Qualitative Study on Chat Dialogue

Next, we present a qualitative examination of both our system and the scammer’s interactions during the chat dialogue. Initially, we manually inspected 150 randomly selected chat dialogues from scammers interacting with our system. This manual assessment enabled us to create a questionnaire that effectively captures the qualitative aspects of the conversation. Subsequently, using these questionnaires, we formulated a prompt for
ChatGPT
to conduct an individual qualitative analysis of the scammer’s and our system’s context. The detailed prompt questionnaire is provided in Appendix
A.4
. We perform a manual qualitative analysis on each of these listed prompts output and present the findings below.

5.1
System Interaction with Scammer Analysis

System Persona

The system persona generation reflected a chat communication with a scammer based on the type of account recovery tweet module post interaction. An account recovery on cryptocurrency wallet and social media reflected three main persona generation: (i) cryptocurrency user who lost access and does not know what happened (152/450), (ii) naive cryptocurrency investor unable to access the wallet (48/450), and (iii) account user afraid of being a phishing victim (46/450). In both cryptocurrency wallet and social media account recovery cases, the persona generation reflected the inability to access accounts and may have been a victim of phishing or scams. The persona also reflected human-like emotions with mixed feelings of distress, fear, naiveness, and the need for urgent assistance to gain access to the address account.

System Refusal Sentiments

Even though we configured our system to generate fake data or deceit in some cases, we notice the system displaying diverse refusal sentiments in its interactions with scammers. For instance: hesitancy to share personal verification details (8/450), reluctance to disclose private secrets (6/450), preference for skepticism over PayPal requests, caution regarding visiting potentially suspicious restoration links (12/450), reluctance to make security deposits (7/450), suspicion towards support that may be potential scammers due to the inability to establish official support channels (4/450), and resistance to verifying account status by sharing screenshots of the system’s account (37/450). In some cases, such as screenshot verification, our system always refused to provide reasoning due to technical issues.

Fake Payment Confirmation

For any payment-related transfer requests, our system initially asks for a PayPal account associated with scammers and later fakes that the system is unable to send the payment amount due to some technical issue and asks for alternative forms of payment. At some point during the conversation after exhausting scammers to send multiple payment methods, the system confirmed scammers sending 117/450 payments via cryptocurrency, and 16/450 via PayPal.

System Action Reasoning to Ending Conversation

In system-related ending conversation reasoning, we observe the likelihood of ending the conversation that may have been the result of System chat dialogue creation. Out of 450 conversations, 152 conversations ended asking systems as follow-up whether scammers would like to continue further after exhausting scammers. These ending conversations were rather a form of polite follow-up. For the remaining scammers, our analyses reveal various instances of ending conversations. For instance, 22/450 scammers left conversations after providing account addresses. We suspect this may have been a result of a fake account address generation, and the scammer likely detected these as fake so the victim might not be serious. Some other reasons for ending the conversation related to the refusal to provide sensitive information (11/450), the inability to provide private secrets (7/450), and the inability to verify the security deposit (7/450).

5.2
Scammer Interaction with System Analysis

Role Representation

Our analysis of how scammers portray themselves during conversations reveals a variety of roles they adopt. Out of 450 communications analyzed, 137 instances involved scammers presenting themselves as part of a support team, assuming roles like customer support, wallet support, tech support, media recovery support, or official support. Additionally, some scammers identified themselves with titles related to hacking (25/450), such as white hat hacker, legitimate hacker, recovery hacker, or private hacker. Another group of scammers (17/450) referred to themselves as experts, using titles like blockchain expert, software bypass expert, or cybersecurity expert. Interestingly, 271/450 scammers initiated conversations by portraying themselves as representatives or agents offering assistance in account recovery.

Verification of Victims

We observed scammers performing various methods for victim verification. Among the 450 scammers analyzed, 69 requested private key secrets. Additionally, 47/450 asked for screenshots containing transaction hashes, associated email, and phone numbers, investment details, location information, or evidence of VPN usage. Moreover, 19/450 scammers sought verification through phone calls, WhatsApp, or video calls, while 29/450 requested photo screenshots of passports or driver’s licenses for verification purposes.

False Issue Reasoning

Scammers were found to provide various explanations when asked about the issues with the account. These included the problem for instance i) system bug (33/450), ii) claiming that the account was blocked due to suspicion (16/450), or iii) stating that there were access issues (57/450) such as the account being stuck, blacklisted, or accessed fraudulently. In such cases, scammers typically insisted that purchasing an activation code or activation tool was necessary to regain access to the account.

Fake Service Charge

Scammers frequently provided different pricing for account recovery services. We found that out of 450 scammers, 161 required the pay as part of the account recovery before proceeding further. The median price was $150, ranging from a minimum of $20 to a maximum of $5,700. Notably, three scammers requested less than $1 as part of a pre-authorization verification process before disclosing the final service price. For funds that are asked to pay via PayPal, scammers mentioned sending payments as “Family and Friends”, whereas for cryptocurrency transactions asked to send a transaction address information followed by a screenshot of a successful payment.

Attitudes Towards Time Wasting

Upon reaching a point of exhaustion during the conversation, or not receiving payment after providing payment details, scammers expressed various sentiments indicating frustration, disbelief, or annoyance, often accompanied by profanity, threats, emojis, and question marks. Examples of such sentiments include phrases like, "You don’t seem serious. That’s what I have been waiting for; I am about to block you", and "You’re being dumb. F**k! Off!". Our analysis revealed that 207 out of 450 scammers conveyed some form of resentment before terminating the conversation.

Reasoning Behind Scammers Ending Conversations

In understanding why scammers choose to conclude or abandon conversations, we analyzed various factors influencing this decision. We observed several common reasons for ending conversations, including instances where the system was unable to provide payment proof (85/450), cases where scammers required a prompt response from the system (39/450), situations where scammers directed communication to external channels such as social media or a restoration page (22/450), instances where the system suggested seeking alternate solutions (19/450), and cases where the system was unable to provide sufficient information or verification (17/450).

To comprehend scammers’ preference for languages beyond English when communicating with victims, we devised our system’s persona using ChatGPT to prompt scammers to engage in chat dialogues in languages other than English. In this setup, we allow scammers to select the language to converse with the victim, indicating that the system’s English proficiency is limited by intentionally introducing errors or linguistic inconsistencies. We instructed ChatGPT to produce English responses with errors or inaccuracies. To ensure we do not engage with scammers through previously used channels, we created a never-used account.

We randomly selected 122 scammers from previously successful interactions with scammers and let our experiment module run for a week in mid of the first week of April 2024. We sent a message to 122 scammers requesting account recovery and 63/122 scammers responded. Regarding the preferences of languages, 45/63 scammers chose to continue the conversation in English despite acknowledging the language issue with the victim. 4/63 scammers declined to provide services in languages other than English, while 3/63 suggested using a translator service for account recovery, even though they only knew English. However, 11/63 scammers engaged in automated chats in languages other than English, expressing a preference for communication in other languages: 5/63 in Spanish, 1/63 in Dutch, 4/63 in German, and 1/63 in French. For 11 communications from 4 different languages that our system communicated with the scammer, we sought out a native speaker qualitative analysis of the text and deduced the following observations from each native speaker.

German Native Evaluation

In the evaluation of the conversation shared by German natives believed that the text generated by both the system and the scammer does not seem to be native German in all 4 contexts. The system seemed to be formal for German native conversation whereas scammers seem to use some form of translation for the conversation text. 3/4 of scammers seem to believe the System conversation is human, however, 1/4 of scammers seem to raise suspiciousness of chatting with robots, where the message from the scammer here “‘…und ich hoffe, du bist echt…“‘, where one possible translation is “‘I hope you are real“ which showed that scammers have some doubts about the system.

French Native Evaluation

The French native evaluation shared that communication from the system had a mix of formal and informal settings. Initiation from the formal conversation and directly jumped to informal in the middle of the conversation. At one point system was unable to send a screenshot of the payment confirmation via PayPal, which raised suspiciousness to the scammer, and mentioned whether the system was a robot. Moreover, a scammer was found to use some forms of the translator in English to French, with an English context word, "Aren’t you just a robot trying to pull my legs?" which does not have a literal meaning translated using the exact word to French “vous n’êtes qu’un robot essayant de me tirer les jambes”.

Dutch Native Evaluation

In the qualitative evaluation from a native Dutch speaker concerning the dialogue analysis between the system and scammers, the system seems to be more formal than regular conversations, although it switches between formal and informal pronouns regularly. In practice, it is unusual to switch formal and informal pronouns as if someone forgot the grammar context. The system also mistakenly used a German sentence at one point, however, the scammer seemed to not notice or comment on such context switches.

Spanish Native Evaluation

The native speaker text conversation evaluation on the system and scammers shared that 3/4 of scammers’ language tone rather represents a formal or more document write tone and less of native speakers. These 3/4 scammer’s text represented some form of translator usage. An example case such as “No tienes que preocuparte por nada, te ayudaré Déjame tener el enlace del sitio web“ which translates to English as “You don’t need to worry about anything. I will help you just let me have the website link.“ are considered rather a direct copy from the translator.

However, in 1/4 text conversation, the native speaker shared likely of real human side of Spanish native scammer where the use of language and tone reflected more of the general human native side of the conversation.

Scam Validation and Tracking

In this section, we present the efficacy of
ScamChatbot
by providing feedback received from industry partners. In prior works
[
8
]
the authors mainly focused on obtaining financial loss metrics from a payment platform. While their approach was largely effective, we believe it may not fully remediate the problem as scammers can easily set up new profiles on social media and pivot to the payment platform. Therefore, in order to fully populate the threat landscape and obtain validation for our results we partnered with X, PayPal, and Chainabuse, and shared our data. Specifically, we shared (i) 11,769 X profile accounts with the X platform, (ii) 743 email addresses, including 165 that requested payments via PayPal, with PayPal, and (iii) 51 cryptocurrency addresses collected during the experiment to Chainabuse. This collaboration aimed to shed light on real-world attacks facilitated by these usernames, email addresses, and cryptocurrency addresses.

6.1
Scam Validation through X

Among the total accounts that we shared with X, we received abuse confirmation for over 80% of the accounts. In other words, 80% of the accounts in our population were also caught by the safety rules in X. Moreover, using their internal linking tools, they identified and remediated over 3 million accounts linked to the dataset we shared. The accounts were predominantly involved in platform spam, impersonation, scams, and bot behavior. The feedback received from X showcases that the accounts detected by the
ScamChatbot
were indeed involved in deceptive behaviors, thus demonstrating value in our approach. Moreover, despite prior efforts in this direction, the fraud pressure is still concerning and X has applied strong measures to combat the problem. Finally, it is to be noted that these scam accounts are not merely involved in one type of abusive activity that is visible to us through
ScamChatbot
. In fact, scammers are involved in multiple fraudulent activities simultaneously which reflects on their capability to pivot and target a wider segment of social media users apart from the ones seeking technical support.

6.2
Tracking Email-based Payment Methods

We shared 743 email addresses with PayPal and requested feedback on (1) account hygiene, (2) scam confirmation, (3) geolocation distribution, and (4) account affinities. PayPal confirmed that 163 of 743 email addresses had a PayPal account with a prominent majority (35%) of US-based accounts. The remaining accounts were spread across different geolocations including Kenya (4.9%), Canada (3%), and Great Britain (3%). 41.1% of the accounts were linked to various risk and fraud typologies including technical support scams, collusion attacks, and fake identity. In terms of account affinities, 33 accounts shared similar attributes and behaviors which led to their blocking. Finally, 10 accounts were observed to be using PayPal’s cryptocurrency product to send and receive funds through cryptocurrency tokens.

Overall, PayPal’s feedback confirmed that scam accounts were indeed involved in suspicious activities which validates our approach and methodology. However, a more concerning observation in their feedback is the involvement of technical support scammers in other types of abuse and scams. It appears that technical support scams are one among many fraud types that these scam groups engage in. On one hand, this observation reflects a coordinated activity occurring at scale and mandating ecosystem-level remediation. On the other hand, the data shows that mitigating one scam operation through our proposed methodology can invariably stop other scam activities in the wild.

6.3
Tracking Cryptocurrency Addresses

Out of 51 cryptocurrency addresses requested by scammers for payment, 3 were found to be invalid. Below, we present an analysis of 48/51 valid cryptocurrency addresses.
Out of these 48 valid addresses, 14 had no transaction history. It is common for owners of crypto addresses to create new ones to maintain anonymity, especially for first-time senders. However, 37/48 valid addresses had transaction histories available. Among these, we examined the incoming volume in USD at the time of writing and found the highest received amount to be $22,786,052, the median at $1826.5, the lowest at $4, with the total sum of incoming transactions from these 37 addresses amounting to $23,650,278. Regarding identified fraud categories, 4 out of the 37 accounts were reported as recovery fraud, with a total incoming amount of $56,126 and a median value of $6,720 for these incoming amounts. Upon analyzing the transaction history, we noted that 2/37 accounts primarily transacted with a Nigerian exchange, with a total fund of $21,510. Additionally, 5/37 addresses were found to receive funds from crypto ATMs (virtual currency kiosks), totaling $159,035, with a median value of $6,065. The rise in scammers in Crypto ATMs is notable, as it enables them to employ social engineering techniques. Victims without cryptocurrency addresses are directed by scammers to deposit cash into these kiosks, purchase virtual currency, and unwittingly provide address information to the scammers.

Disclaimer.
Note that this evaluation is based on the observed transaction histories and reported fraud categories. However, we do not have conclusive proof that all transactions related to these addresses are linked to scams.

7
Discussion

Our proposed system,
ScamChatBot
, has three main limitations: (i) detecting official account recovery support entities, (ii) engaging with scammers promptly, and (iii) missing conversations from other popular platforms (see also Appendix
2.3.3
).
The first limitation, detecting official account recovery support entities, arises from the necessity of pre-existing knowledge about global and regional popular platforms to incorporate official account recovery procedures.
The second limitation, timely engagement with scammers, is contingent upon the API limits imposed by the integrated platforms within a given timeframe. Some scammers may remain active only briefly before becoming inactive.
The third limitation involves missing communications on other platforms such as Telegram, phone calls, forms, and other channels likely used by scammers that may not be included in an automated system.
We argue that these limitations can be addressed as follows:

•

The range of official account-related platforms can be expanded.

•

Prompt interactions with scammers can be improved by subscribing to higher bandwidth API limits.

•

Conversational platforms can be tailored based on the scammers’ preferred communication channels.

Expanding beyond these limitations will require additional resources and compute allocation.

9
Conclusion

In this study, we conducted automated interactions with scammers using chatbots that adopted different personas and specifically targeted individuals experiencing account recovery challenges. By posting decoy tweets on X, we attracted 11,769 scammers who responded by urging users to contact them through private direct messages and emails. Our system then contacted 450 of these scammers using automated chatbot interactions and uncovered the various tactics they use to trick individuals into sharing private keys or sensitive information. Through collaboration with industry partners such as PayPal and the Chainabuse Cryptocurrency Abuse Database, we were able to validate financial losses and analyze real-world attacks by these scammers, uncovering details that would not be apparent from a pure analysis of social media profiles. We believe that our interactive framework lays the foundation for future research and highlights the importance of actually engaging with scammers to uncover their methods of operation.
