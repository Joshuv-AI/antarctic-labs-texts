Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) Risk Stratification in Otolaryngology: Socioeconomic and Subspecialty Insights
Authors: Corinne A Pittman, Hudson Liu, Lovedeep Singh, Serenity G Budd, Sabrina Heman‐Ackah, Selena E Briggs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is a leading cause of preventable perioperative mortality in otolaryngology, highlighting the urgent need for validated risk assessments such as the Caprini score to guide prophylaxis and enhance patient safety. The objective of this study was to determine VTE outcomes following otolaryngology surgeries, stratified by Caprini scores, otolaryngological subspecialties, and socioeconomic conditions represented as Area Deprivation Index (ADI).MethodsThis study was conducted via a multi‐year retrospective review of 9036 otolaryngology procedures performed at multiple tertiary care hospitals within a large health system from June 2016 through December 2022. Logistic regression and multivariable models were used to investigate the relationship between VTE and Caprini score, subspecialty, and ADI.ResultsA total of 9036 otolaryngology surgeries were analyzed. Of those, 174 (1.9%) patients experienced a VTE and 33 (0.4%) of those patients experienced a VTE within 2 weeks after otolaryngological surgery (VTE Subset). A Caprini score of ≥ 6 resulted in a VTE (VTE: OR Unadjusted = 1.49, OR Adjusted = 1.47, p < 0.001; VTE Subset: OR Unadjusted = 1.43, OR Adjusted = 1.43, p < 0.001). In those who experienced a VTE, a Caprini score of ≥ 6 yielded a sensitivity of 0.672, specificity of 0.809, positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.065, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.992. In patients who experienced a VTE following otolaryngology surgery, a Caprini score of ≥ 6 showed a sensitivity of 0.727, specificity of 0.802, PPV of 0.013, and NPV of 0.999.ConclusionCaprini guidelines can function as a robust tool for ruling out VTE risk in otolaryngology surgical patients regardless of subspecialty and ADI.Level of EvidenceIII.Keywords: Area Deprivation Index (ADI), Caprini, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), otolaryngology, pulmonary embolism (PE), venous thromboembolism (VTE)

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a leading cause of preventable perioperative morbidity and mortality, yet its risk profile in otolaryngology has historically received less attention than in higher‐volume surgical specialties such as orthopedics or general surgery [1, 2]. In the United States, approximately 900,000 new cases of VTE are estimated to occur each year, contributing to as many as 100,000 VTE‐related deaths annually [3]. Approximately one‐third of these deaths occur after a surgical procedure [4, 5]. Among patients who develop symptomatic pulmonary embolism (PE) or deep vein thrombosis (DVT), approximately 50% occur in the first week postoperatively with approximately 75% of cases presenting within the first 2 weeks post‐operatively [6]. Up to 25% of patients expire within the first hour of onset [7]. In the postsurgical setting, an estimated additional 5%–10% of patients who experience acute VTE may expire from the complication [8], and patients who survive will have an increased risk of postthrombotic syndrome characterized by cardiac dysfunction in the form of right heart strain, pulmonary hypertension, and recurrent thrombosis [9, 10]. Even with appropriate intervention, the overall mortality rate from treated DVT or PE remains around 6%–8%, reflecting the persistent threat posed by thrombotic events [8, 11, 12].

The Caprini risk assessment model has been validated in general, orthopedic, and plastic surgery populations as a tool to stratify VTE risk based on comorbidities, procedural factors, and patient demographics [13, 14]. However, its role in otolaryngology is unclear. The question of the Caprini score's utility in otolaryngology is particularly salient given that many otolaryngology procedures are performed on relatively healthy patients, while certain subspecialties, such as head and neck oncology, traditionally include a patient population with a greater number of medical comorbidities [15, 16]. Subspecialties like rhinology and laryngology may involve shorter outpatient interventions with smaller surgical fields [17, 18]. Conversely, with head and neck oncology, major oncologic resections and reconstructive surgeries requiring free tissue transfer have each shown increased VTE rates, presumably due to longer operative duration, significant fluid shifts, and complex surgical fields [17, 18]. Yet these represent only a portion of otolaryngology procedures, leaving uncertainty regarding how best to tailor prophylaxis across a wide range of operations, from outpatient interventions to complex neck dissections or microvascular reconstructions. Such heterogeneity highlights the value of a systematic tool, like the Caprini score, that can stratify risk for individual patients by incorporating comorbidities, procedural details, and demographic factors to guide prophylactic strategies. Additionally, as a key performance indicator (KPI) with potential economic implications as well, it is important to accurately assess risks and prescribe prophylaxis accordingly [19, 20].

Research into the applicability of the Caprini model in otolaryngology is expanding, but existing studies are limited in scope. Most focus on subspecialties or single institution cohorts, limiting generalizability [19]. Risk factors such as elevated body mass index (BMI) and advanced age appear relevant in extensive head and neck reconstructive surgeries but may be less significant in brief outpatient procedures [18, 20]. Although early data support the use of the Caprini score in otolaryngology [21], the literature has few large, multi‐year analyses that span numerous subspecialties.

An additional variable which may impact VTE risk and outcomes is variation in socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic variables, assessed through metrics like ADI, have been recognized for their potential influence on surgical outcomes, given that they can affect preoperative health status, adherence to postoperative instructions, and access to timely follow‐up [22]. Their inclusion is particularly relevant in otolaryngology, where patients come from a broad range of geographic locations and socioeconomic backgrounds, potentially introducing additional barriers or stressors that could shape postoperative outcomes. If ADI modifies the association between the Caprini score and VTE risk, a truly comprehensive risk model may need to incorporate these sociodemographic factors to avoid underestimating or overestimating thrombotic risk in certain communities. However, if ADI does not impact this relationship, then the Caprini score may be applied widely to different patient populations with little adjustment needed for socioeconomic status.

The present study aims to evaluate the predictive validity of the Caprini risk assessment model within the inpatient otolaryngology surgical population, while considering subspecialty and the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) as potential confounding variables.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective chart review was conducted of 9036 operative otolaryngology patients at a large regional healthcare system between June 2016 and December 2022. Data extracted included VTE status by ICD codes, variables used to calculate Caprini score by ICD codes, and data used to calculate ADI (home address, zip code, and census tract code). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. VTE (defined as PE or DVT) per ICD code was noted in 174 patients at any time post‐operatively. Within the literature, it is reported that approximately 75% of VTE occur within the first 2 weeks post‐operatively. Within this study, data were extracted regarding patients noted to have VTE within the first 2 weeks post‐operatively labeled as “VTE subset.” Thirty‐three patients met criteria for VTE occurring within 2 weeks after otolaryngology surgery (VTE subset).

Caprini score parameters were identified and recorded using ICD codes and were verified using retrospective chart review. The total Caprini score was calculated using Microsoft Excel. One point was added for each of the following: congestive heart failure, sepsis, pneumonia, current swollen legs, varicose veins, history of inflammatory bowel disease, BMI over 25, acute myocardial infarction, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Two points were added for current central venous access or malignancy. Three points were added for each of the following: history of PE/DVT, family history of thrombosis, positive factor V Leiden, positive prothrombin 20210A, elevated serum homocysteine, positive lupus anticoagulant, elevated cardiolipin antibody, heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia, and congenital or acquired thrombophilia. Five points were added for acute spinal cord injury, multiple trauma, stroke, or hip, pelvis or leg fracture. The age score was assigned as 0 for patients aged 40 or younger, 1 for those aged 41 to 60, 2 for those aged 61 to 74, and 3 for those aged 75 or older. Surgery and mobility scores were estimated for each procedure code.

Outcomes were measured for both VTE and VTE Subset. The independent variable was Caprini score, a discrete variable. The covariates were otolaryngology subspecialty and ADI, a validated measure of neighborhood socioeconomic status that increases with higher levels of neighborhood deprivation. Subspecialty was a categorical variable, whereas ADI was a continuous variable. ADI was centered and scaled to the national average of ADI, meaning zero represented the national average of ADI in 2019 with a standard deviation of one.

The relationship between Caprini score and VTE (or VTE Subset) was assessed using logistic regression. Unadjusted odds ratios (OR) and p values were reported from the simple logistic regression models. Adjusted ORs and p values were reported from the multivariable models which included subspecialty and ADI as possible confounders of the effect of Caprini score on VTE (or VTE Subset). Predictive value of VTE was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Precision Recall (PR) curves. An optimal cut point was chosen that maximized the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Using the optimal cut point, accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were reported. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 (2024‐06‐14) using a significance level of 0.05.

Table 2 shows the unadjusted and adjusted OR for Caprini score, subspecialty, and ADI with VTE. Without adjusting for possible confounders, Caprini score was significantly positively associated with VTE (OR = 1.49; p < 0.001). After adjusting for subspecialty and ADI, the OR for Caprini score only changed by 1.3% (OR = 1.47; p < 0.001). The magnitude of confounding was much less than 10%, so we can conclude that subspecialty and ADI are not confounders for the effect of Caprini score on VTE. Figure 2 shows the ROC and PR curves for the unadjusted model of Caprini score and VTE. The AUC was 0.812 (95% CI [0.780,0.843]), which shows good discrimination between patients with and without a VTE. The optimal cut point (i.e., the cut point that maximized the sum of sensitivity and specificity) was a Caprini score of 6. This meant that patients with a Caprini score ≥ 6 were classified as having a VTE, and patients with a Caprini score < 6 were classified as not having a VTE. Using this cut point, the overall accuracy was 0.806, meaning approximately 80.6% of patients (both VTE and non‐VTE) were correctly classified. Balanced accuracy, which averages sensitivity and specificity to account for any class imbalance, was 0.741, indicating a fair performance in detecting both true cases of VTE and true non‐VTE cases. Using this framework, the sensitivity was 0.672, specificity was 0.809. The PPV was 0.065, reflecting that only about 6.5% of all patients labeled high risk truly ended up with a VTE–likely because the overall prevalence of VTE in this population was low. Conversely, the NPV was 0.992 indicating that almost all patients identified as low risk truly did not develop a VTE. Clinically, these findings demonstrate that although a Caprini score of ≥ 6 effectively identifies the majority of VTE events, it also yields a considerable proportion of false positives, underscoring the significance of a negative Caprini score below 6 accurately ruling out VTE in this patient population.

Discussion

VTE remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality across surgical specialties, including otolaryngology, warranting careful assessment of patient risk and selective implementation of preventive measures. The present study sought to evaluate the predictive validity of the Caprini risk assessment model within the inpatient otolaryngology surgical population, while considering subspecialty and the ADI as potential confounding variables.

A primary outcome of this study was the statistically robust and clinically relevant association between higher Caprini scores and elevated odds of developing VTE. The unadjusted OR for VTE per one‐point increase in Caprini score was 1.49 (p < 0.001), and this only marginally decreased to 1.47 (p < 0.001) after adjusting for ADI and subspecialty—an approximate 1.3% change in the OR, which is well below the 10% threshold typically used to define a meaningful level of confounding [23]. A similar pattern emerged when focusing on the subset of VTE cases that occurred after otolaryngology surgery in the hospital system. The unadjusted OR for Caprini score was 1.43 (p < 0.001), and after adjusting for potential confounders it decreased only marginally to 1.41 (p < 0.001), signifying the Caprini score remained a robust predictor of postoperative VTE within this population. These findings suggest that the risk captured by the Caprini score is stable across otolaryngology subspecialty and socioeconomic backgrounds, an important consideration for widespread applicability.

Moreover, the predictive performance of the Caprini score was evaluated using both ROC and precision‐recall (PR) curves. The Caprini score demonstrated significant discriminative capability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.812 in the overall VTE model and 0.808 in the VTE Subset model. These AUC values exceed the conventional 0.8 threshold for what is typically considered “good” discrimination [24], highlighting the clinical utility of the Caprini risk assessment in identifying at‐risk patients. Additionally, an optimal Caprini cutoff of 6 was found to maximize sensitivity and specificity for both VTE outcomes, aligning with prior studies in other surgical populations that also propose similar threshold ranges [13, 25].

At this cutoff, the overall accuracy of the Caprini score was 80.6% (balanced accuracy = 74.1%) for predicting VTE and 80.1% (balanced accuracy = 76.4%) for predicting the VTE Subset. Sensitivity and specificity for the overall VTE outcome were 67.2% and 80.9%, respectively, whereas in the VTE Subset sample, they were 72.7% and 80.2%. The NPVs were especially high—99.2% in the overall sample and 99.9% for the VTE Subset—indicating that among patients classified as “low risk” (Caprini < 6), nearly all did not develop a VTE. This result is particularly relevant clinically, as it supports the use of the Caprini score as a robust tool for stratifying VTE risk in otolaryngology patients.

This analysis explored whether the type of subspecialty (e.g., head and neck) or ADI significantly confounded the relationship between Caprini score and VTE risk. The data revealed minimal evidence of confounding, with OR changes of only 1.3% and 1.8% when adjusting for these variables in the overall VTE sample and VTE Subset, respectively. While neighborhood socioeconomic status, as encapsulated by ADI, can influence access to care, postoperative compliance, and baseline health status, it did not substantially alter the predictive capability of the Caprini model [22]. Similarly, whether a patient underwent surgery by a head and neck subspecialist versus another otolaryngology subspecialist did not significantly modify the Caprini score's predictive effect. These observations suggest that the risk captured by the Caprini score is stable across different practice settings and socioeconomic backgrounds, an important consideration for widespread applicability.

In addition to socioeconomic factors, prior research has highlighted how specific otolaryngology subspecialties, for instance, complex head and neck oncologic surgeries, may exhibit higher baseline VTE rates [17, 20, 26, 27, 28]. The present study, however, demonstrated that the Caprini score reliably predicted VTE risk even after taking subspecialty into account, suggesting that it can be confidently applied across a broad range of otolaryngologic procedures. The high AUC values (> 0.80) illustrate that the Caprini model has “good” to “excellent” discrimination, further validating its use in this specialty. This finding builds upon existing literature by offering a more comprehensive perspective, given that many earlier studies concentrated on narrowly defined patient cohorts or single subspecialty populations. With a large sample of 9036 otolaryngology surgeries encompassing different otolaryngology practices, the current results bridge a gap in the literature, thereby establishing a foundation for further prospective research and refined prophylaxis protocols in this field.

Study Limitations

Given the retrospective design, there is a possibility of misclassification bias or incomplete data entry. While the Caprini score is widely recognized, it includes many items that may vary in accuracy of documentation within the health records, such as length of surgery, length of hospital stay, presence of genetic thrombophilias, or precise BMI metrics. Additionally, the Caprini score is typically calculated in a prospective manner. A limitation of this study is the reliance on available retrospective data to calculate the Caprini score. This lends to the possibility of omission of relevant data due to miscoding, lack of coding, incomplete documentation, VTE events occurring at outside facilities without follow‐up encounters within the facility, or patients lost to follow‐up. The incidence of VTE in the dataset remains relatively low, which reflects real‐world otolaryngology practice but also affects the PPV and might limit the detection of significant interactions or subgroup differences. Finally, our analysis centered primarily on VTE events captured during inpatient hospitalization or within the same health system; patients who seek care for VTE symptoms elsewhere or present after discharge to an outside facility could be missed. Within the analysis, there is interaction between Caprini score and subspecialty as well as Caprini score and ADI. No significant interactions were observed. However, effect modification was not assessed in the study.

Future Directions

Several avenues emerge for further research. Prospective validation of these findings, including standardized data collection of Caprini scores and patient follow‐up for VTE outcomes beyond the index hospitalization, could strengthen confidence in the observed associations. Additionally, individualized prophylaxis protocols based on Caprini risk strata in otolaryngology might reveal whether more or less aggressive prophylaxis strategies could optimize patient outcomes while minimizing complications from anticoagulation or mechanical prophylaxis. Detailed cost–benefit analyses would also be pertinent, given the growing emphasis on value‐based care models.

Another intriguing direction would be to examine interactions of the Caprini score with other factors not captured in this study, such as specific operative details (e.g., type and duration of anesthesia, intraoperative fluid management, use of thromboxane) or patient‐level biomarkers (e.g., D‐dimer, genetic thrombophilias) that might further refine risk stratification and serve as a confounder in this study. Finally, since ADI did not appear to confound the Caprini–VTE relationship substantially, future research might investigate whether unique social determinants of health, like health literacy or access to transportation, play a role in determining patient adherence to postoperative prophylaxis and follow‐up—thereby providing a more nuanced picture of patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study offers further insights into VTE risk stratification for otolaryngology patients. There is a high NPV of developing VTE among patients with a Caprini score of less than 6, Even after adjusting for subspecialty and neighborhood deprivation, the Caprini score threshold of 6 showed significant discrimination (NPVs of 99.2% and 99.9% in the overall and VTE subset), specificity, and NPV across over 9000 procedures. This may be useful in highlighting the minimal risk of VTE within this patient population and identifying patients who are less likely to develop a VTE postoperatively. Despite the relatively low PPV, these findings support the use of Caprini score to identify low risk patients that receive minimal benefit from prophylaxis. Future prospective studies will be essential to refining risk assessment and prophylaxis protocols in otolaryngology.
