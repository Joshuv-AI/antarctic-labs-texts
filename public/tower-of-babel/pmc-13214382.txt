Assessing non-porous antimicrobial surfaces against bacteria, phages and fungi: a comparative study under varied normative conditions using glass as a benchmark for microbial persistence
Authors: Sabine Poelzl, Eva Zarschenas, Rozita Nokhbehzaeim, Daniela Dreisiebner, Stephanie Maitz, Clemens Kittinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe survival of pathogenic microorganisms on surfaces is contingent on environmental factors, including humidity and temperature. In order to analyze the survival and persistence in vitro, different protocols have already been implemented. This has resulted in a wide range of survival rates of all kinds of microorganisms on various surfaces tested, due to different procedures across laboratories.This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the antimicrobial performance of glass, as an inert reference material, and low alloyed carbon steel with different antimicrobial additives. Therefore, three complementary testing strategies that simulate both dry and moist environmental conditions were utilized: the standardized ISO 22196:2011 for humid exposure, a spray-chamber method mimicking aerosol-based contamination with dry test conditions, and a biofilm formation assay under air/liquid interface conditions. A range of microorganisms, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and bacteriophages demonstrated long-term persistence on glass, ranging from at least two days to weeks. In the liquid test, low alloyed carbon steel uncoated and coated with zinc or an antimicrobial lacquer achieved good efficacy against bacteria and bacteriophages. However, in the dry tests and in tests against fungi, only the antimicrobial lacquer was effective. Furthermore, none of the tested surfaces prevented biofilm formation by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Thus, the study demonstrated that different test scenarios yield disparate outcomes, emphasizing the importance of in vitro testing being oriented towards the intended product applications. It also demonstrated the requirement of suitable reference materials like glass to benchmark microorganism persistence under specific testing scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05046-z.Keywords: Antimicrobial surface, ISO 22196:2011, Dry test, CAMAG, Biofilm, Drip flow biofilm reactor

Introduction

The persistence of microorganisms on inanimate surfaces poses significant challenges for hygiene management, particularly in environments prone to bacterial, viral and fungal contamination, such as healthcare facilities and the food industry. Studies have demonstrated that pathogenic microorganisms can survive on surfaces such as steel and glass for extended periods, depending on environmental conditions such as humidity and temperature. As demonstrated in the study conducted by Katzenberger et al. [1], Staphylococcus aureus can survive on these two types of surfaces at least one week, while Candida has been reported to persist between a few minutes and a month on various surfaces, with extended survival rate being found on plastics or steel surfaces [2]. Viruses like Influenza A or SARS-CoV-2 can survive from a few hours to several weeks on various surfaces. On stainless steel, for instance, these viruses persisted from 24 to 72 h to at least 7 days [3–6]. The wide range in survival time could be attributed to differences in environmental conditions, the surfaces tested, strain subtypes or initial concentrations [2]. Even longer persistence times have been reported for vaccinia virus, which was able to survive for 56 days on various substrates, including glass, galvanized steel and painted cinder blocks, exhibiting only a 2 log10 reduction in viral titer [7].

Stainless steel of the 316 l grade has been shown to exhibit both favorable mechanical properties and satisfactory biocompatibility, making it a popular choice for a variety of medical applications such as orthopedic prosthesis or cardiovascular stents. However, it often does not prevent bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, owing to its absence of antimicrobial properties [8, 9]. In order to enhance the antimicrobial activity of these surfaces, different approaches have already been investigated [8], Since 1893, the ability of metal ions to kill or prevent cell propagation of bacteria has been known as the ‘‘oligodynamic effect’’ by Carl Nägeli [10]. Therefore, especially coatings containing metals such as copper, silver, zinc, titanium, and molybdenum are particularly common. Copper is often described as the metal with the greatest antimicrobial potential [11] leading to a complete inactivation of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria within a few hours under different test conditions [9, 12]. It is particularly effective as it can produce all four types of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce oxidative stress. This mainly leads to changes in cell membrane permeability, to increased gene expression of proteins involved in cell apoptosis and to an attack of proteins and, thus, their depression of activity [13]. Therefore, it is the most common metal used for antimicrobial coatings [14]. Silver, while it takes longer for an antimicrobial effect to become apparent, disrupts microbial DNA, proteins, enzymes or intracellular cofactors, demonstrating sustained antimicrobial efficacy [11, 15]. Zinc has also shown to possess antimicrobial properties, particularly when present in the form of complexes or zinc oxide nanoparticles [16]. The process has been observed to result in the generation of two types of ROS: H2O2 and ·OH [13]. In general, conjunctions between different metals to generate synergistic effects is a widespread practice [17, 18]. Titanium, despite its versatility, shows limited activity under dry conditions and has been observed to support microbial growth, such as Listeria monocytogenes, under high humidity [11]. Glass, on the other hand, is traditionally considered inert. While not inherently antimicrobial, its use as a reference in testing the efficacy of antimicrobial coatings is becoming more prominent, allowing researchers to benchmark performance under controlled condition [19, 20]. Furthermore, glass is an ideal material for simulating real-world applications in in vitro testing scenarios due to its use in a variety of critical interfaces, such as in healthcare, aircraft and even space [21, 22].

To evaluate the antimicrobial performance of coated and uncoated non-porous surfaces against bacteria, standardized testing methodologies such as ISO 22196:2011 [23] have been widely adopted. ISO 22196:2011 provides a reliable framework for quantifying the antimicrobial activity of non-porous surfaces under controlled conditions by measuring bacterial reductions over a 24-hour period. However, this standardized and reproducible method focuses primarily on artificial conditions (e.g., the application and distribution of the bacterial suspension under a polyethylene film), which may not fully replicate real-world scenarios [24–27]. Optimization of the protocol, particularly concerning the application of the microbial suspension on the test surfaces have already been carried out. For instance, the large-droplet inoculation method [28], touch transfer assays [29] or spraying techniques to mimic formation of aerosols through sneezing or coughing [9, 30, 31] have been implemented. However, tests involving droplet-based contamination must consider how the behavior of the droplets is affected by the surface itself, as well as by external environmental factors, which may impact the persistence of microbes and the outcome of test results [32]. A new standard test procedure called ISO 7581:2023 has also recently been published [33]. However, although this surface test is conducted under dry environmental conditions, which are more realistic, the implementation is partly difficult (e.g., applying and distributing 1 µl of bacterial suspension) [25]. Another issue with ISO 22196:2011 that could be discussed critically, is the restriction of the testing procedure to two bacterial testing strains: Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Although these include both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, which often or occasionally cause foodborne illnesses [34], the scope of testing can be expanded further. Some researchers have already addressed this limitation by extending the antimicrobial surface test to other relevant microorganisms. For instance, some studies have successfully included fungi such as Candida albicans [35, 36]. In addition, bacteriophages can also be used and serve as surrogates for human viruses, thereby providing a secure way to test the antiviral efficacy of surfaces [37–39].

The aim of this study is to explore the antimicrobial properties of various surfaces by employing three distinct test strategies, simulating dry and moist environments, respectively. ISO 22196:2011, the standard test procedure for liquid testing, is frequently referenced. In the dry test, a spray chamber is used to simulate aerosol formation and generate dry environmental conditions as well as cyclic contaminations. Finally, the biofilm test investigates the biofilm resistance of surfaces in an air/liquid environment. These methods provide a more comprehensive understanding of a coating’s performance under both dry and wet stress conditions. The tests were carried out under standardized conditions and with different microorganisms including bacteria, fungi and phages to facilitate a comparative analysis of survival and persistence across different surfaces. Glass was utilized as an inert overall reference material in this study, serving as basis for comparison with low alloyed carbon steel coated with various additives.

Materials and methods

Samples

Different non-porous surfaces were analysed in this study, including glass (Cloeren Technology GmbH, Wegberg, Germany) used as inert overall reference material and low alloyed carbon steel (LACS). The LACS surfaces were provided and coated with zinc (Zn) by voestalpine Stahl GmbH (Linz, Austria) using hot-dip galvanization. The LACS with hot-dip galvanization was further processed by G&S Solution GmbH (Bruchsal, Germany) through the application of a layer of clear lacquer, both in the absence (TX-7770) and presence of antimicrobial active substances (AMC-TX-7770) (Table 1). The samples had either the size of 50 × 50 mm (liquid and dry test) or 76 × 26 mm (biofilm test) and were stored at room temperature and prior to testing, the surfaces were sterilized with 70% ethanol (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Testing of antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the surfaces was evaluated through three test methods:

Liquid test

The antibacterial/antifungal assay was based on ISO 22196:2011 Measurement of antibacterial activity on plastics and other non-porous surfaces [23] with some adaptations: Three different strains were used for the testing procedure: Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) DSM 346, Escherichia coli (E. coli) DSM 1576 and Candida albicans (C. albicans) DSM 1386 (Leibniz Institute DSMZ - German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Braunschweig-Sued, Germany). The S. aureus and E. coli strains were selected according to the ISO specifications, while C. albicans was used according to “Deutsche Gesellschaft für Hygiene und Mikrobiologie e.V.” (Deutsche Gesellschaft Für Hygiene und Mikrobiologie, 2022) to include a yeast/eukaryotic test organism. Prior to testing, the bacterial strains were cultivated overnight on Columbia Blood agar plates (Becton Dickinson Austria GmbH, Vienna, Austria) at 36 °C ± 2 °C. C. albicans was cultivated for 48 h on Columbia Blood agar plates at 30 °C ± 2 °C. Cell material was inoculated in a 0.2% v/v Tryptone Soy Broth (TSB, Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, United Kingdom) diluted in distilled water. A VITEK® DensiCHEK instrument (Biomerièux, Vienna, Austria) was used to obtain a bacterial/fungal solution with 1 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml. An amount of 200 µl of bacterial/yeast suspension with an expected concentration of 2.5–10 × 105 cells/ml was pipetted on the sterile surfaces of the samples. The suspension was covered and distributed under a sterilized 40 × 40 mm polyethylene film (VWR International, Vienna, Austria). After that, the inoculated test specimens were incubated in a wet chamber at strain specific temperatures (S. aureus and E. coli at 36 °C ± 2 °C; C. albicans at 30 °C ± 2 °C) with a relative humidity (RH) of approximately 96% for 10 min (min), 30 min, 1 h (h), 3 h and 24 h. As a control, the bacterial/fungal suspension was harvested immediately after pipetting below the foil (0 h) to ensure initial concentration from each specimen. After the exposure treatment, the suspensions were rinsed off the surface by four washing steps with 10 ml of neutralizer (SCDLP medium) containing TSB (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, United Kingdom), lecithin (Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany), and Tween®80 (Amresco Inc., Solon, OH, USA) in order to rescue surviving bacteria/fungi. The samples were then shaken in the neutralization medium for 3 min at 200 rpm on a Battery Shaker KM 2 Akku (Edmund Bühler GmbH, Bodelshausen, Germany). An amount of 500 µl of appropriate dilutions in phosphate-buffered solution (PBS, Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) was plated on tryptic soy agar (TSA, VWR International Ltd., Vienna, Austria) plates in duplicates. CFUs for S. aureus and E. coli were counted after incubation for 24 h at 36 °C ± 2 °C, while C. albicans was incubated 48 h at 30 °C ± 2 °C.

The antiviral assay was also based on ISO 22196:2011, but additionally on ISO 21702:2019 Measurement of antiviral activity on plastics and other non-porous surfaces [40] and ISO 18071:2016 Fine ceramics—Determination of antiviral activity of semiconducting photocatalytic materials under indoor lighting environment—Test method using bacteriophage Q-beta [41]. For the experimental setup, the bacteriophage Phi6 (ϕ6) DSM 21,518 and its host Pseudomonas syringae (P. syringae) DSM 21482, the bacteriophage Qbeta (Qβ) DSM 13768 as well as bacteriophage MS2 (DSM 13767) and their host E. coli DSM 5210 were purchased from a collection of the Leibniz Institute DSMZ (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany).

The bacteriophage propagation was done according to the manufacturer’s specifications: The bacterial strains were cultivated overnight in lysogenic broth (LB, Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) containing CaCl2 (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) at 25 °C ± 2 °C (P. syringae) or at 36 °C ± 2 °C (E. coli) and 110 rpm. The overnight host cultures were diluted at 1:50/1:100 into 10 ml fresh LB media and grown to OD600 0.2. To determine the cell density by OD600, an UV/VIS spectrophotometer (VWR International GmbH, Vienna, Austria) was used. The cultures were infected with an approximate multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 and then incubated for 4 h at 36 °C ± 2–25 °C ± 2 °C and 50 rpm. The suspensions were stored at 4 °C overnight. On the next day, the phage lysates were centrifuged (10,000 x g, 20 min, 4 °C) and then filtered through a sterile syringe 0.2 μm filter (VWR International GmbH, Austria). The phage suspensions were stored at 4 °C and the titer were determined by plaque assay.

Experimental procedure: The viability of the bacteriophage particles on the surfaces were carried out according to ISO 21702:2019. The sterile surfaces were inoculated with 200 µl viral suspensions with an expected viral titer of 1–4 × 107 plaque forming units (PFU)/ml and were then covered by a sterilized 40 × 40 mm polyethylene film. 1 h, 3 h and 24 h were used as incubation time points and a RH of approximately 96% as used in the antibacterial/antifungal assay were employed. The incubation temperature depended on the test organism used (ϕ6 at 25 °C ± 2 °C, Qβ and MS2 at 36 °C ± 2 °C). After incubation the remaining infectious viral particles were recovered by using 10 ml SCDLP medium as neutralizer. After washing four times, dilutions in peptone saline solution (Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) were prepared. For the bacteriophage plaque assay, ISO 18071:2016 was applied. Therefore, 0.1 ml of the appropriate host and 1 ml of the viral dilution were added to 2 ml liquid Top-Agar containing peptone, saline, yeast extract, CaCl2, and agar (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany). After gently mixing, the solution was poured over LB agar (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) plates containing CaCl2. Each dilution was determined in duplicates. PFUs were counted after incubation for 24 h at 36 °C ± 2–25 °C ± 2 °C.

Furthermore, a long-term test was conducted with the same microorganisms and conditions as described above. For these test series the incubation period was extended individual for each strain until no further colonies or plaques could be detected after incubation on the overall reference material glass. Bacterial and yeast incubations were performed until the 19th or 23rd day, while bacteriophage tests were limited to one week (7 d).

Since the evaluation differs between the used ISOs (antibacterial and antiviral assay), an equal calculation of the results was chosen to make it comparable. Therefore, the calculation is based on ISO 18071:2016 [41]:

N the number of viable microorganisms recovered per test specimen [CFU]

C average plate count for the duplicate plates

V volume, in ml, of SCDLP added to the specimen (10 ml)

D dilution factor for the plates counted

The calculation of the reduction compared to 0 h results was done with following formula:

R the antimicrobial activity [%] or [log10]

U0 the average of the common logarithm of the number of viable bacteria, fungi or still infectious viral particles recovered from the test specimens immediately after inoculation (0 h)

Ut the average of the common logarithm of the number of viable bacteria, fungi or still infectious viral particles recovered from the test specimens after 1 h

The verification of the methodology was calculated according to the guideline of ISO 22196:2011 [23] through the 0 h triplicates:

Lmax 10 logarithm of the maximum number of viable microorganisms found on a specimen

Lmin 10 logarithm of the minimum number of viable microorganisms found on a specimen

Lmean 10 logarithm of the mean number of viable microorganisms found on the specimens

A value ≤ 0.2 indicated a valid test result

The applied load is defined as the actual number of bacterial/yeast cells or viral particles applied to the samples in the experiment. Time point 0 h is defined as the test point where the bacterial/yeast/viral suspension is harvested immediately after pipetting under the foil to ensure initial concentration of each sample. For each incubation time, sextuplicates (n = 6) were used either in one run (glass, LACS and LACS + Zn) or in two independent runs (TX-7770 and AMC-TX-7770) to calculate mean and standard deviation for the antimicrobial activity of the specimens. When no colonies/plaques were countable on the plates, the limit of detection was set as 10 CFU/PFU, since 10 ml of the neutralization medium was used.

Dry test

A methodology for dry testing of non-porous materials has already been developed [31] and subsequently employed and was further developed in this study. For the testing procedure the following bacterial strains from the collection of the Leibniz Institute DSMZ (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany) were used: E. coli DSM 1576, Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) DSM 1798 and S. epidermidis DSM 3269. The bacterial suspensions were prepared as outlined above for the bacterial assay (ISO 22196:2011), but the application was conducted through the spray chamber CAMAG Derivatizer Base Unit (CAMAG® Derivatizer, Muttenz, Switzerland, Fig. 1, A). In each spraying process, eight samples were simultaneously sprayed with 500 µl bacterial suspension of 2.5 × 105 – 10 × 105 CFU/ml (Fig. 1, B). After the process in the aerosol generating chamber, the samples were transferred on a sterilised plastic rack for drying (Fig. 1, C). After drying, the samples for 0 h were transferred with the test surface facing downwards into a plastic container containing 10 ml SCDLP medium and 12 –14 g glass beads (2.85–3.3 mm, Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) to rescue surviving bacteria. The whole process from the start of spraying in the chamber to complete dry surfaces takes approximately 5 to 10 min. The other samples for further incubation (1 h) were each transferred into a sterile petri dish (90 mm x 16.2 mm, Fisher Scientific, Schwerte, Germany) with the test surface facing upwards. The incubation was performed at 20 °C ± 1 °C with a RH between 30% and 65% for 1 h. After 1 h these samples were also transferred into the plastic container with neutralizer and glass beads. The samples were shaken in the recovery liquid for 3 min with 200 rpm on a Battery Shaker KM 2 Akku (Edmund Bühler GmbH, Bodelshausen, Germany). The dilution series were performed in PBS. Finally, 500 µl of appropriate dilutions were plated on TSA plates in duplicates. All plates with 30–300 CFU after incubation for 24 h at 36 °C ± 2 °C were counted.

Setup for spraying process under the laminar flow. Entire experimental setup (A). Eight samples after the spraying process (B). Drying of the samples on plastic racks (C). Illustrations taken from Poelzl et al. [31]

In addition to the normal test, a multiple test was carried out with two spray processes. AMC-TX-7770 and glass were used for this type of test. Eight samples were sprayed in the chamber as indicated above. Half of the samples were treated according to the protocol (2 × 0 h and 2 × 1 h), while the other four samples were re-sprayed with the same bacterial suspension after drying. Of these double-sprayed samples, two were transferred to the plastic containers after drying (0 h), while the other two were incubated for 1 h under standard conditions before further processing.

The same formulas as in the case of the liquid test have been used to calculate the results as well as the reduction and the verification of the method. An additional verification of the method [31] was implemented with following formula:

X initial suspension concentration (applied load) in CFU (divided by 2, since only half of the samples (= 0 h) of one run were included)

S0 sum of the common logarithm of the number of viable bacteria recovered from the test specimens immediately after inoculation (0 h)

The average of the two independent runs was calculated and a value ≤ 0.5 log10 showed a low loss of the test organism and therefore a good initial concentration. Exceptions can be assumed for surfaces with a rapid antimicrobial efficacy (= AMC-TX-7770).

The applied load is defined as the actual number of bacterial cells sprayed in the chamber and onto the eight specimens in the experiment. Time point 0 h is defined as the test point where the bacterial suspension is completely dry on the surface and harvested immediately to ensure initial concentration of each sample. For each incubation time, duplicates (n = 2) of each sample type were used in two independent runs (n = 4) to calculate mean and standard deviation for the antibacterial activity. When no colonies were countable on the plates, the limit of detection was set as 10 CFU, since 10 ml of the neutralization medium was used.

Biofilm test – air/liquid interface

The biofilm test was based on ASTM E2647-20 Standard test method for quantification of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm grown using drip flow biofilm reactor with low shear and continuous flow [42] with some adaptations. The specified strain Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) DSM 939 was purchased from the collection of the Leibniz Institute DSMZ (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany). The bacterial strain was cultivated overnight in 10 ml 3000 mg/l TSB (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, United Kingdom) at 36 °C ± 2 °C and 110 rpm. The cell density was determined by OD600 through an UV/VIS spectrophotometer (VWR International GmbH, Vienna, Austria) and also checked by serial dilution and plating on TSA agar plates (incubation at 36 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h). The drip flow biofilm reactor® DFR 110-6 (DFR, Biosurface technologies corporation, Montana, USA, Fig. 2, C) was used. For the batch phase each of the six channels already containing the sterilised test samples (All sample types were tested with a surface area of 76 × 26 mm.) were filled with 15 ml of 3000 g/l TSB and 1 ml ONC (average concentration of 1.3 × 109 CFU/ml). The incubation was done at room conditions (RT and relative indoor humidity) for 6 h, until the continuous flow phase was started. During this phase, a pump (Masterflex® L/S® Precision Variable-Speed Console Drives, VWR International GmbH, Vienna, Austria, Fig. 2, B) with a eight channel minicartridge pump head for Masterflex® (VWR International GmbH, Vienna, Austria, Fig. 2, B) was used to generate an average flow rate of 0.51 ml/min per channel. This system pumped approximately 8.56 l of 270 mg/l TSB from a 10 l bottle (VWR International GmbH, Vienna, Austria, Fig. 2, A) into the reactor and over the test surfaces and finally in a 10 l waste bottle (Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany, Fig. 2, D) within 45 h. For the biofilm extraction, 50 ml tubes (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria) containing 12–14 g glass beads (2.85–3.3 mm, Carl Roth GmbH + Co Kg, Karlsruhe, Germany) and 5 ml biofilm buffer were used. The biofilm was dissolved by shaking and the surfaces were rinsed off with biofilm puffer to remove the remaining components of the biofilm and to increase the final volume to 45 ml. An amount of 500 µl of appropriate dilutions in PBS from these biofilm-suspensions were plated on TSA plates in duplicates. CFUs were counted after incubation for 24 h at 36 °C ± 2 °C.

Data analysis

Results were expressed as described with corresponding mean ± standard deviation (SD). Depictions were generated using CorelDRAW 2019 (Corel Coperation, Ottawa, Canada) and GraphPad Prism Version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Statistical analyses have been performed through GraphPad Prism Version 10 using either nonparametric Mann-Whitney U-test (mean with 95% CI, p-value: <0.05) or parametric Welch`s test (mean with 95% CI, p-value: <0.05). For the liquid test, only reductions from the overall reference material glass to the other sample types are marked. Data for 0 h are not shown, as there would be significant differences due to different initial concentrations (= applied load). For the dry test, all significant differences between glass and the other surfaces are shown (0 h and 1 h; decrease and increase).

Results

Liquid test

The liquid test was performed according to ISO 22196:2011 and glass served as the overall reference material, as no antibacterial or antifungal effect was detectable after 24 h (Fig. 3). Both S. aureus and E. coli as well as C. albicans remained stably detectable on glass over the period tested. On the contrary, the concentration of all three microorganisms increased after 3 h at the latest compared to the initial concentration used (Table 2). Subsequently, a statistical comparison was performed on the results yielded by testing glass and the other modified surfaces to identify statistically significant reductions within one time point. After 10 min, the Mann–Whitney U test showed a significant reduction on AMC-TX-7770 for all three test organsims. A statistically relevant difference between glass and LACS after 10 min could only be calculated for C. albicans (Fig. 3, C). However, this difference was less pronounced than for AMC-TX-7770. Furthermore, C. albicans remained quite stable on all surfaces (except AMC-TX-7770) over the time points tested. Even after 24 h incubation, only a small reduction of 0.75 log10 on LACS and 0.23 log10 on LACS + Zn was detectable compared to the initial concentration (0 h) (Table 2). AMC-TX-7770 showed the best antifungal effect, achieving complete elimination of the fungus to the detection limit after 24 h. In comparison to the fungus, the bacterial strains died faster and were more susceptible to attacks from several surfaces. For S. aureus (Fig. 3, A), a complete reduction of more than 4 log10 to the detection limit of 10 CFU was achieved on LACS and LACS + Zn after only 3 h. After 24 h, the Gram-positive bacterium was no longer detectable on all surfaces with the exception of 6.8 × 105 colonies on the overall reference glass. E. coli (Fig. 3, B) was able to survive slightly longer on the surfaces. On LACS with and without Zn, a reduction of approximatly 1 log10 was observed after 3 h compared to 0 h, while TX-7770 showed no considerably reductions (Table 2). In comparison, the lacquer with antimicrobial additives (AMC-TX-7770) showed good antibacterial activity after just 1 h. After 24 h, E. coli could only be detected on glass and TX-7770.

Bacteria were incubated on glass for up to 23 d, with detectable survival observed up to 19 d (S. aureus) and 16 d (E. coli) under these experimental conditions. Dry test

These experimental procedures were conducted within an aerosol generation chamber, thereby establishing a test setup where the sprayed bacterial suspensions of three different strains can dry quickly on the surfaces. The results obtained for both S. epidermidis strains were comparable (Fig. 6, A, and B). A significant reduction to the overall reference material glass was determined for LACS + Zn and AMC-TX-7770 after 1 h. However, for LACS + Zn an average of 5 × 102 CFU were detectable after 1 h, while for AMC-TX-7770 nearly all results were below the 10 CFU detection limit. The high efficacy of AMC-TX-7770 was evident even after the drying period (0 h) with a decline in bacterial concentration of nearly 2 log10 (S. epidermidis DSM 3269) and > 0.5 log10 (S. epidermidis DSM 1798) compared to glass. Analogous outcomes were also observed for the test strain of E. coli (Fig. 6, C), whereby the reduction after 1 h was in general higher on all samples (including glass) compared to the Gram-positive bacterial strains. A significant decrease in the concentration of LACS + Zn and AMC-TX-7770 in comparison to glass after 1 h and also after 0 h was observed against E. coli DSM 1576. The calculation of the reduction using the 0 h results of each sample also showed similar results (Table 5). S. epidermidis DSM 1798 showed a 1.47 log10 (LACS + Zn) and a 2.24 log10 (AMC-TX-7770) reduction, while S. epidermidis DSM 3269 exhibited a reduction of 1.22 log10 on LACS + Zn and a reduction of 1.70 log10 on AMC-TX-7770. All other samples demonstrated minimal efficacy of less than 1 log10 reduction compared to the 0 h results. For E. coli DSM 1576, a minimum reduction of 1.19 log10 (glass) to a maximum of 2.15 log10 (AMC-TX-7770) was calculated. Moreover, as demonstrated in Table 5, it is evident that solely the AMC-TX-7770 specimen exhibited an inability to attain the predetermined threshold of loss to the applied load (≤ 0.5 log10). However, these results are attributed to the rapid antimicrobial effect of this surface modification.

Biofilm test – air/liquid interface

The biofilm experiment was performed with all five samples to ascertain the ability of the surfaces to prevent or limit the colonisation of P. aeruginosa through biofilm formation in an air/liquid interface of the DFR system. Glass as a neutral baseline material demonstrated the highest biofilm colonisation with 9.2 log10 (CFU/cm2) and was thus selected to determine the statistical significance of the differences between it and the other surfaces. A significant difference was determined for all samples using the Welch’s t-test. However, in comparison to the previous results of the liquid (Figs. 3 and 4) and dry tests (Fig. 6), it was found that LACS and LACS + Zn had the lowest biofilm formation, with 8.3 log10 (CFU/cm2) and 7.7 log10 (CFU/cm²), respectively. These results indicate that they had a better effect against the colonisation of P. aeruginosa then the other two surfaces. In this particular experiment, TX-7770 and AMC-TX-7770 exhibited a negligible discrepancy in their performance in relation to glass (Fig. 8, A). Moreover, it was also possible to observe clear visual differences between the samples (Fig. 8, B). The most successful colonisation of P. aeruginosa was observed on glass, as well as on TX-7770 and AMC-TX-7770. However, on both TX-surfaces the biofilm was optically more difficult to detect due to their white colouration and could only be recognised with good lighting. Little biofilm could be detected optically on LACS and LACS + Zn, whereby LACS displaying a notably high propensity for corrosion formation. These results therefore correspond well with the count of log10 (CFU/cm2) (Fig. 8, A).

Discussion

The survival of problematic microorganisms on various materials has been studied extensively. Research has been conducted not only on inanimate surfaces [19, 43] but also in food [44] and even in space [45]. Test results generated in laboratories are as diverse as the test materials themselves, owing to differences in testing procedures or laboratory conditions. In order to more effectively assess and compare the effectiveness of the surfaces to be tested in this study, a variety of strategies were employed: Initially, glass was tested as an inert overall reference material to establish a benchmark for the persistence time under comparable controlled laboratory conditions. Then, the various survival results on glass were utilized to compare the effectiveness of low alloyed carbon steel and its different antimicrobial additives more accurately under the same testing conditions. Secondly, three distinct test methods were used to simulate both liquid and dry test conditions. Finally, a range of microorganisms encompassing bacteria, fungi and bacteriophages, were examined.

Survival on glass

The central focus of this study was to test the persistence on glass, which was used as an inert overall reference material [20] and as a negative control to determine the general survival span of the different microorganisms. It is often impossible to compare data from the literature because different testing protocols or laboratory conditions can lead to different outcomes. For instance, a review reported that E. coli survived on glass for 1 to 14 d [19]. However, as E. coli survived much longer in our test setup (becoming undetectable after 19 days), it is important to generate a reference value using the own standardized laboratory conditions. Furthermore, an adequate reference material that does not exhibit antimicrobial properties can also be used to evaluate the suitability of individual microorganisms in a specific testing setup. Returning to E. coli, its survival rate is often limited under dry testing conditions. As demonstrated by Hirai, E. coli cannot survive on glass under dry conditions for more than 7 h [46]. This survival characteristic could lead to false positive results in some surface tests. Nevertheless, E. coli was used in the dry test in this study, because we have previously demonstrated that this particular strain used is more stable than others under dry test conditions [25, 31] and the focus was on a short incubation time of 1 h. One surprising result, however, is the long persistence of C. albicans on glass. Throughout the entire 23-day test period, this fungus remained stably detectable, with no reduction compared to the applied load. Previous studies have also demonstrated the long-lasting survival of Candida [47, 48] and this result further highlights the risk of transmission via contaminated surfaces. Another unexpected result was the difference observed between the bacteriophages Qβ and MS2. While Qβ died quite quickly after 2 d of incubation on glass, MS2 was still detectable on the last day of testing (7 d). This was not anticipated, given that both phages have a similar structure, with an icosahedral morphology, a non-enveloped capsid and a single-stranded RNA genome. However, researchers have previously documented the higher stability of MS2 compared to Qβ under different testing conditions. They explained this variance through differences in the molecular weights of the capsid protein and the A protein as well as other protein components [49]. In this study the incubation temperature on glass was selected according to the optimum temperature of the bacteriophage’s hosts. Thus, both coliphages were incubated at 37 °C. A lower temperature might increase the survival rate of Qβ; this is an approach that should be pursued in future experiments.

Three distinct test methods

The liquid test according to ISO 22196:2011 is the best-known test method for antimicrobial non-porous surfaces and provides a good first assessment of its efficacy. In this study, all surfaces tested showed the complete elimination of bacteria and bacteriophages to the detection limit after 24 h except for glass and TX-7770 (for E. coli and Phi6). This was surprising, since low-alloyed carbon steel (LACS) without an additional coating also showed antimicrobial efficacy. In the literature, stainless steel is frequently tested and modified to enhance its antimicrobial properties [8, 12]. For instance, Ojeil et al. reported a reduction of 2.2 log10 on the stainless steel control after 24 h [9], whereas another study involving influenza A virus showed the same log10 reduction only over seven days [3]. Therefore, this effect is insufficient to designate stainless steel as antimicrobial. The steel used in this study contains additional chemical elements to improve certain properties. One addition is manganese, which is already known to exhibit good antibacterial activity through ROS-mediated pathways [50, 51] and which may explain the antimicrobial efficacy of LACS. However, its efficacy is quite slow, as this surface does not achieve a sufficient reduction after 1 h of incubation. To avoid transmission of infections, an effective antimicrobial surface must eliminate microorganisms within minutes [52]. Therefore, the rapid effect of the lacquer containing antimicrobial additives (AMC-TX-7770) can be emphasized. It even managed to kill C. albicans completely after 24 h. However, these results of the liquid test must be viewed with caution, as they often do not correspond to realistic environments of the final product [24, 25, 27, 53]. Furthermore, wet exposure conditions with a high surface area to inoculum volume ratio are known to overestimate antimicrobial activity compared to dry exposure [20], as prolonged humid conditions can increase the release of biocidal substances [53]. Thus, further testing strategies were also implemented in this study.

In the dry test, using an aerosol-generating chamber to perform tests under more realistic conditions, the good antimicrobial efficacy of AMC-TX-7770 (no bacteria detectable after 1 h) was confirmed once again. LACS + Zn exhibited moderate antibacterial properties, reducing bacteria by between 1.2 log10 and 2.1 log10. Due to the time-consuming and resource-intensive nature of the testing, as well as the importance of rapid efficacy, the 24-hour incubation period was not performed within the dry test. This may have revealed superior efficacy for low alloyed carbon steel surfaces. Additionally, this type of test is not suitable for all microorganisms that can be tested in ISO 22109:2011. Firstly, some bacteria such as E. coli, die quickly under dry conditions [46] without being affected by the antimicrobial surface. Thus, it is important to include an inert reference, such as glass, for comparison purposes, as a kind of negative control [20]. Secondly, some microorganisms are too dangerous to create aerosols with, including S. aureus [31]. However, within this study an experiment was conducted to prove the comparability between the non-hazardous S. epidermidis and the pathogenic S. aureus. Experiments in the liquid tests yielded the same results (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1), so it can be assumed that S. aureus would also behave similarly to S. epidermidis under dry conditions on the surfaces. As a precautionary measure, tests against bacteriophages were not performed to avoid aerosol-formation of these organisms [54], given that some phages can survive for long periods on laboratory surfaces [55] and decontamination may not always be effective. For instance, some phages have been reported to be insensitive to high concentrations of ethanol [56]. The authors of this study did not intend to introduce general bacteriophage contamination in the laboratory that might interfere with further laboratory experiments.

The study successfully developed the biofilm model, achieving a yield of 9.2 log10 (CFU/cm2) biofilm on glass. This is consistent with the results of the collaborative study based on ASTM E2647-20, in which a mean log density of 9.3 log10 (CFU/cm2) was achieved in ten different laboratories [57]. However, growing low shear biofilms at an air/liquid interface, presented the greatest challenge for the antimicrobial surfaces. None of the surfaces tested were able to prevent the biofilm formation or even achieve sufficient reduction. This may be due to the complex structure of biofilms, in which microbial communities adhere to surfaces surrounded by a self-produced extracellular matrix [58], which protects against different environmental factors and antimicrobial agents [59]. Additionally, the efficacy of antimicrobial surfaces can be circumvented by forming a conditioning layer that covers the active surface, rendering it harmless. This can occur through dead cells or, in case of P. aeruginosa, via an active attachment process involving flagella, which leads to the suggestion that this bacterium may be the pioneer colonizer [60]. Other microorganisms were not tested in this study, since the surfaces were not efficient enough against the specified strain of the standardized protocol. In general, other microorganisms should be used e.g., S. aureus [61], since biofilm formation is strain-dependent [62] and multi-species biofilms have been shown to exhibit increased fitness and pathogenicity [60]. In the future, other biofilm set-ups could also be investigated, including shear-based testing methods involving fluid dynamics, such as those used in a CDC biofilm reactor [63]. Using a different biofilm technique could also improve the analysis. In this study, the evaluation is based solely on colony counting, as described in ASTM E2647-2. This is because conventional biofilm analysis methods caused discrepancies in the results, and some techniques are mutually exclusive and cannot be applied to the same sample. Initially techniques such as safranin staining [64], weighing and biochemical quantification [65] like protein or carbohydrate analysis were investigated, but are not mentioned in the final version of the study. The differences in the results are probably due to the inhomogeneity of the biofilms. Variability in the structure, composition and thickness of the biofilm across different samples may be due to differences in nutrient supply in each chamber of the drip flow biofilm reactor. In this case, a comparison using evaluation methods across multiple samples does not appear possible, nor meaningful, since the formation of biofilms could not be prevented by the antimicrobial components on the surfaces anyway. The only conclusion that can be drawn from this study’s results is that the surface modification that performed best in the other tests (AMC-TX-7770) failed the biofilm model, whereas LACS + Zn achieved better results. This is probably due to white rust forming during the biofilm assay, consisting mainly of zinc hydroxide, with smaller amounts of zinc oxide and zinc carbonate. Zinc oxide compounds have been proven to have an antimicrobial effect. However, their efficacy against Gram-negative bacteria including P. aeruginosa is often reduced, because the cell compounds like lipopolysaccharides can protect the bacteria from the attack [66, 67]. This may explain the lower yield of biofilm on LACS + Zn compared to glass, but a complete avoidance of biofilm formation remains elusive.

Range of microorganisms

The selection of microorganisms was extended in this study. ISO 22196:2011 only specifies the test strains E. coli and S. aureus, both are listed as foodborne pathogens [68]. C. albicans was also selected due to its complex eukaryotic cellular structure, and because it belongs to a genus that causes life-threatening outbreaks, particularly in healthcare settings (e.g., Candida auris). Furthermore, some researchers have already successfully included this fungus in the test setup of ISO 22196:2011 [35, 36, 69, 70]. In the liquid test performed on glass, this fungus proved its ability to persist on ordinary surfaces over extended periods, as also evidenced in prior studies, with survival lasting for weeks [47, 48]. The efficacy against C. albicans was also the lowest on the other surfaces tested, indicating a new severity level for evaluating antimicrobial surfaces. Finally, three different bacteriophages were included to cover a wide range of human viruses as surrogates, including influenza [71], SARS-CoV-2 [39, 72] and norovirus [73].

Although this study has already included detailed test strategies, further testing is required to confirm the durability of the surfaces, particularly of AMC-TX-7770. This could include abrasion tests or the application of impurities (e.g., albumin).

Conclusion

This study comprehensively evaluated the survival and reduction of various microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi and bacteriophages, on different surfaces using three distinct test setups. Glass was used as an inert overall reference material to benchmark microbial persistence, revealing prolonged survival rates, particularly for C. albicans. In comparison to glass, low alloyed carbon steel (LACS) and its various surface modifications exhibited a microbial reduction over the testing period. However, efficacy depended on the test method used. Notably, the lacquer containing antimicrobial additives (AMC-TX-7770) demonstrated strong performance in liquid and dry conditions, respectively. Nevertheless, none of the surfaces were effective in preventing biofilm formation. Overall, the study highlights the importance of using multiple test conditions and a diverse microorganism panel to accurately assess the real-world efficacy of antimicrobial surfaces. It also emphasizes the necessity of using an appropriate inert reference material to exclude antimicrobial efficacy through environmental test conditions, enabling a better evaluation of antimicrobial surfaces.
