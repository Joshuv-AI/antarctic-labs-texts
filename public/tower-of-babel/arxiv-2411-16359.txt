1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of all algebraic polynomials of degree at most
n

and
w
be a weight function on an interval
I
of
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
L_{q}(w)\equiv L_{q,w}(I)
be the Lebsgue space on
I
endowed with the finite norm

\|f\|_{L_{q}(w)}:=\left\{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{q}w(x){\rm d}x\right\}^{1/q},1\leq q<\infty.

For a finite interval
I
, we replace the space
L_{\infty}(w)
by
C(I)
with the uniform norm

\|f\|_{C(I)}:=\sup\limits_{x\in I}|f(x)|.

For simplicity, we denote
\|\cdot\|_{L_{q}(I)}
and
L_{q}(I)
if
w(x)=1
. Specifically,
L_{2}(w)
is a Hilbert space endowed with an inner product

(1.1)

\langle f,g\rangle_{w}:=\int_{I}f(x){g(x)}w(x){\rm d}x.

Inequalities of Bernstein-Markov type have been fundamental for
proofs of many inverse theorems in polynomial approximation
theory, cf.
[
8
,
12
,
13
,
15
]
. It provides estimates for the
norms of derivatives of algebraic and trigonometric polynomials.
The classical Markov inequality is for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

\|p^{\prime}\|_{C[-1,1]}\leq n^{2}\|p\|_{C[-1,1]}.

Here
n^{2}
is sharp which can be achieved by the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
of the first kind of degree
n
, cf.
[
14
]
.
And the Bernstein inequality for the derivative of an algebra
polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is

\|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,p^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}[-1,1]}\leq n\|p\|_{L_{q}[-1,1]},\ 1\leq q\leq\infty.

Here
n
is sharp which can be also achieved by the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
, cf.
[
5
]
for
q=\infty
and
[
1
]
for
1\leq q<\infty
.

More generally, let
{\rm D}
be a differential operator on the
interval
I
, and
w
be a weight function on
I
. Consider the
following Bernstein-Markov type inequality

(1.2)

\|{\rm D}p\|_{L_{q}(w)}\leq M\|p\|_{L_{q}(w)},\ 1\leq q<\infty,

where the least constant
M>0
such that (
1.2
) holds for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is often
called Bernstein-Markov factor of the differential operator
{\rm D}
for
L_{q}(w)
. Precisely, the Bernstein-Markov factor of
{\rm D}
for
L_{q}(w)
is defined by

M_{n}(L_{q}(w),{\rm D}):=\sup\limits_{0\neq p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\|{\rm D}p\|_{L_{q}(w)}}{\|p\|_{L_{q}(w)}},\ 1\leq q<\infty.

For such extremal problem, we are mainly interested in its exact
value and extremal polynomials. We note that the extremal
polynomials are often given by the orthogonal polynomials with
respect to the inner product (
1.1
). Some beautiful results
were obtained by many researchers, especially for
p=2
, see cf.

[
3
,
9
]
and the references therein.

•

(Hermite case) For the classical Hermite weight
w(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
on the real line
\mathbb{R}
, Schmidt in
[
16
]
discovered that

(1.3)

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(e^{-x^{2}}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\sqrt{2n},

with the extremal polynomials
cH_{n}
, where
H_{n}
is the classical Hermite polynomial. However,
the exact values for
M_{n}(L_{2}(|x|^{2\lambda}e^{-x^{2}}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})
,
\lambda\neq 0
have not been found so far. A
partial answer of this problem given by Draux and Kaliaguine in

[
6
]
is

(1.4)

\sqrt{2n-\frac{4\lambda}{1+2\lambda}}<M_{n}\left(L_{2}(|x|^{2\lambda}e^{-x^{2}}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)<\sqrt{2n},\ \lambda>0,\ n=2m+1,

and

\sqrt{2(n-1)}<M_{n}\left(L_{2}(|x|^{2\lambda}e^{-x^{2}}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)<Cn^{\frac{1-\lambda}{2}},\ -1/2<\lambda<0.

•

(Jacobi case) For the classical Jacobi weight
w(x):=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
with
\alpha,\beta>-1
on the interval
[-1,1]
, A. Guessab and G.V. Milovanovic in
[
10
]
proved

(1.5)

M_{n}\left(L_{2}((1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\sqrt{n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)},

with the extremal polynomials
cJ_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
, where
J_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
is the classical Jacobi polynomial and
c
is an arbitrary nonzero constant.

•

(Laguerre case) For the classical Laguerre weight
w(x)=e^{-x}
on the half real line
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
, Turán in
[
17
]
showed that

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(e^{-x}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\left(2\sin\frac{\pi}{4n+2}\right)^{-1},

with the extremal polynomials of the form

c\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sin\frac{j\pi}{2n+1}L_{j}(x),

where
L_{j}
is the classical Laguerre polynomial normalized by
L_{j}(1)=1
and
c
is an arbitrary nonzero constant. While, for more general case of
w(x)=x^{\kappa}e^{-x},\,\kappa>0
, A. Guessab and G.V. Milovanovic in
[
10
]
showed that

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(x^{\kappa}e^{-x}),\sqrt{x}\,\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\sqrt{n},

with the extremal polynomials
L_{n}^{\kappa}
, where
L_{n}^{\kappa}
is the generalized Laguerre polynomial.

where
W_{\lambda}(x)
and
A(x)
are given in Table

Secondly, we also explore the same problems for Dunkl operators.
Dunkl operators were originally proposed by C.F. Dunkl in 1989 as
an extension of the classical Fourier transform and has been
widely employed in diverse areas of mathematics and physics (see

[
7
]
). An important application of Dunkl operators lies in
the realm of special functions, where they generate a set of
orthogonal polynomials. Extending the concept of the sharp
Bernstein-Markov inequalities to the Dunkl operators has both
intriguing implications and crucial significance. The Dunkl
operator
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
associated with the index

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is defined by

(1.8)

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}f(x)=f^{\prime}(x)+\lambda\,\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{x}

for a function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. For simplicity, we usually denote

\sigma\left(f\right)=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{x}.

Obviously, if
\lambda=0
then
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
reduces to the usual differential operator
{\rm d}/{{\rm d}x}
.
Its square
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}
, which is also called Dunkl-Laplace operator, is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}f=f^{\prime\prime}+2\lambda\frac{f^{\prime}}{x}+\frac{\sigma\left(f\right)}{x}

for a function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular, when
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}
is restricted to the even subspace

C_{e}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\,f(x)=f(-x)\right\},

it reduces to the singular Sturm-Liouville operator

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}|_{C_{e}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}f=f^{\prime\prime}+2\lambda\frac{f^{\prime}}{x}.

The extremal problem for the Dunkl operators can be given by

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(W_{\lambda}),\sqrt{A}\,\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\right)=\sup_{0\neq p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\|\sqrt{A}\,\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p\|_{L_{2}(W_{\lambda})}}{\|p\|_{L_{2}(W_{\lambda})}},\ \lambda\geq 0,

where
W_{\lambda}(x)
and
A(x)
are given in the Table
1
.
As stated in Lemma
2
, its exact value is the supremum of all those
M>0
for which the differential equation

A(x)\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p+B(x)\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p+M^{2}p=0

has a nontrivial solution
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and the extremal polynomial can be obtained by the corresponding generalized orthogonal polynomials, where
B(x)
is given in Table
1
.

1.1.
New Results

Our first theorem gives the exact value of Bernstein-Markov factor
M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})
, which supplements the previous result (
1.4
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
\lambda>0
. Consider the generalized Hermite weight

w_{\lambda}(x):=|x|^{2\lambda}e^{-x^{2}}
on
\mathbb{R}
, we have for
all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{2n},\ &&{\rm if}\ n\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}},\ &&{\rm if}\ n\ {\rm is\ odd},\end{aligned}

where
\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}
is the large positive root of the polynomial

F_{\frac{n+1}{2}}(t):=\det\left(\left\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)d_{2i+2j}+(t-4j-2)d_{2i+2j+2}\right\}_{i,j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right),

if
n
is odd,
and
d_{2s}=\Gamma(s+\lambda+1/2)
for
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Remark 1
.

When
n
is odd, it can be seen from the proof of Theorem
1
that the polynomial
F_{\frac{n+1}{2}}
has at least one positive root.

Remark 2
.

(Extremal polynomials)

(i)

For all
n
being even, the extremal polynomials are given by
cH_{n}^{\lambda}
, where
H_{n}^{\lambda}
is the generalized Hermite polynomial with respect to
the weight
w_{\lambda}
, and
c
is an
arbitrary nonzero constant;

(ii)

for all
n
being odd, the extremal polynomials
p=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{(n-1)/2}a_{2j+1}x^{2j+1}
are odd and
can be determined by the linear equation system

\sum_{j=0}^{{(n-1)}/2}\left\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)d_{2i+2j}+(\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}-4j-2)d_{2i+2j+2}\right\}a_{2j+1}=0,\ i=0,\dots,{\frac{n-1}{2}}.

Our second theorem determines the exact value of Bernstein-Markov factor
M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})
, which generalizes (
1.5
) with
\alpha=\beta=\mu-1/2,\,\mu>-1/2
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mu>-1/2
. Consider the generalized
Gegenbauer weight

w_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=|x|^{2\lambda}(1-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
on

[-1,1]
, we have, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\max\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n}{2}}},\sqrt{n(n+2\lambda+2\mu)}\right\},\ &&{\rm if}\ n\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&\max\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}},\sqrt{(n-1)(n+2\lambda+2\mu-1)}\right\},\ &&{\rm if}\ n\ {\rm is\ odd},\end{aligned}

where
m=(n-2)/2
if
n
is even and
m=(n-1)/2
if

n
is odd,
\nu_{m+1}
is the large positive root of the
polynomial

G_{m+1}(t):=\det\left(\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)c_{2i+2j}+[t-(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda+2\mu+1)]c_{2i+2j+2}\}_{i,j=0}^{m}\right),

(set
\nu_{m+1}=0
if the polynomial

G_{m+1}
does not have any positive roots), and

c_{2s}=\frac{\Gamma(s+\lambda+1/2)\Gamma(\mu+1/2)}{\Gamma({\lambda}+\mu+s+1)},\ \ s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Remark 3
.

(Extremal polynomials)

(i)

For all
n
being even, if
M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})=\sqrt{n(n+2\lambda+2\mu)}
,
then the extremal polynomials are given by
cC_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
, where
C_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
is the generalized Gegenbauer polynomial with respect to
the weight
w_{\lambda,\mu}
, and
c
is an arbitrary nonzero
constant; if

M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})=\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n}{2}}}
, then the extremal polynomials

p=\sum_{j=0}^{(n-2)/2}a_{2j+1}x^{2j+1}
are odd and can be
determined by the linear equation system

\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)c_{2i+2j}+[\nu_{\frac{n}{2}}-(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda+2\mu+1)]c_{2i+2j+2}\right\}a_{2j+1}=0,\ i=0,\dots,{\frac{n-2}{2}}.

(ii)

For all
n
being odd, if
M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})=\sqrt{(n-1)(n+2\lambda+2\mu-1)}
, then the extremal polynomials are given by
cC_{n-1}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
; if

M_{n}(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x})=\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}}
, then the extremal polynomials

p=\sum_{j=0}^{(n-1)/2}a_{2j+1}x^{2j+1}
are odd and can be
determined by the linear equation system

\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)c_{2i+2j}+[\nu_{\frac{n+1}{2}}-(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda+2\mu+1)]c_{2i+2j+2}\right\}a_{2j+1}=0,\ i=0,\dots,{\frac{n-1}{2}}.

.

2.
Generalized weights and orthogonal polynomials

In this section we recall some properties of orthogonal
polynomials which can be found in
[
7
, Chapter 1]
. In the
follows, we always assume
I=\mathbb{R}
or
I=[-1,1]
. First we
state some observations. Obviously, the classical Hermite weight

e^{-x^{2}}
and the Gegenbauer weight

(1-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\mu>-1/2
satisfy a first-order differential equation systems of
the form

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\quad\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}(A(x)w(x))=B(x)w(x),\\
\quad w(x)=w(-x),\end{cases}

where
A(x)
and
B(x)
are given in Table
1
. Then the weight functions we considered are the form of

W_{\lambda}(x):=|x|^{2\lambda}w(x),\ \lambda\geq 0,

where
w\in CW_{e}:=\big\{(1-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},e^{-x^{2}}\big\}
.
We also note that
B(x)=B^{\prime}(0)x
, and
AW_{\lambda}
vanishes at the end points of interval
I
, i.e.,
AW_{\lambda}|_{\partial I}=0
. Here
\partial I=\{\pm\infty\}
if
I=\mathbb{R}
and
\partial I=\{\pm 1\}
if
I=[-1,1]
.

It is easy to check that for any
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

(2.2)

2\int_{I}q\frac{\sigma(p)}{x}AW_{\lambda}=\int_{I}\sigma(p)\sigma(q)AW_{\lambda},

and

(2.3)

\int_{I}q\frac{p(x)+p(-x)}{x}\,AW_{\lambda}=\int_{I}p\,\sigma(q)\,AW_{\lambda}.

2.1.
Generalized Gegenbauer polynomials

For
\alpha,\beta>-1
, the classical Jacobi polynomials
J_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\,n\in\mathbb{N}
are given by

J_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x):=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}x}\left[(1-x)^{\alpha+n}(1+x)^{\beta+n}\right],

which is an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with respect to
the classical Jacobi weight
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
,

x\in[-1,1]
. It is well known that
J_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
is the
unique polynomial solution of the differential equation

(1-x^{2})p^{\prime\prime}-[\alpha-\beta+(\alpha+\beta+2)x]p^{\prime}+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)p=0

in the meaning of a constant.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
and
\mu>-1/2
. The generalized Gegenbauer polynomial
C_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
can be given by the classical Jacobi polynomials. Precisely,

\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &C_{2m}^{(\mu,\lambda)}(x)=\frac{(\lambda+\mu)_{m}}{(\lambda+\frac{1}{2})_{m}}\,J_{m}^{(\mu-1/2,\lambda-1/2)}(2x^{2}-1),\\
&C_{2m+1}^{(\mu,\lambda)}(x)=\frac{(\lambda+\mu)_{m+1}}{(\lambda+\frac{1}{2})_{m+1}}\,xJ_{m}^{(\mu-1/2,\lambda+1/2)}(2x^{2}-1).\end{aligned}

Moreover, it follows from
[
4
]
that the coefficients
a_{k},k=0,\dots,n
of
C_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
satisfy

a_{k}=0
if
n-k
is odd, and one of the following recurrence relations:

(2.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=(k-n)(k+n+2\lambda+2\mu)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ n,k\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&(k+1)(k+2\lambda+2)a_{k+2}=(k-n)(k+n+2\lambda+2\mu)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ n,k\ {\rm is\ odd}.\end{aligned}\right.

It is well known that
C_{s}^{(\mu,\lambda)},s=0,\dots,n
form an
orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with respect to the
generalized Gegenbauer weight

w_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=|x|^{2\lambda}(1-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
on
[-1,1]
.
Meanwhile, it has been proved in
[
4
]
that the generalized
Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
is the unique polynomial
solution of differential-difference equation system

(2.5)

\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\ (1-x^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p-(2\mu+1)x\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p+\lambda_{n}^{2}p=0,\\
&\ p(-x)=(-1)^{n}p(x),\end{aligned}

with

(2.6)

\lambda_{n}^{2}=\left\{n(n+2\lambda+2\mu)+2\lambda\mu[1-(-1)^{n}]\right\}

in the meaning of a constant. In fact, the symmetric property in (
2.5
) is not necessary, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
. If the differential-difference equation

(2.7)

\mathcal{L}[\,p\,]:=(1-x^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p-(2\mu+1)x\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p+M^{2}p=0,

has a nontrivial solution
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for some
M>0
, then

p=cC_{s}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
with
M=\lambda_{s}
given by (
2.6
),
where
s=1,\dots,n
and
c
is an arbitrary nonzero constant.

Proof.

For
1\leq s\leq n
, let
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{s}a_{k}x^{k}
satisfy (
2.7
) with some
M>0
and
a_{s}\neq 0
. Set
a_{s+1}=a_{s+2}=0
.
Then the straightforward calculation gives

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\left\{k+\lambda\left[1-(-1)^{k}\right]\right\}a_{k}x^{k-1},

and

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\left\{k+1+\lambda\left[1-(-1)^{k+1}\right]\right\}\left\{k+2+\lambda\left[1-(-1)^{k}\right]\right\}a_{k+2}x^{k},

which leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\,p\,]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\{\{k+1+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k+1}]\}\{k+2+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}a_{k+2}

\displaystyle\quad+\{M^{2}-\{k+2\mu+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k-1}]\}\{k+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}\}a_{k}\}x^{k}.

This yields that for all
k=0,\dots,s
,

\{k+1+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k+1}]\}\{k+2+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}a_{k+2}=\{\{k+2\mu+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k-1}]\}\{k+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}-M^{2}\}a_{k}.

Since
a_{s}\neq 0
and
a_{s+1}=a_{s+2}=0
, we get
M=\lambda_{s}
,
and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a_{s-1}=a_{s-3}=\dots=0,\\
&(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=(k-s)(k+s+2\lambda+2\mu)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ s,k\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&(k+1)(k+2\lambda+2)a_{k+2}=(k-s)(k+s+2\lambda+2\mu)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ s,k\ {\rm is\ odd}.\end{aligned}\right.

which, by (
2.4
), means
p=cC_{s}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We also remark that for
n
being even, the generalized Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
is the unique polynomial solution of differential equation

(1-x^{2})p^{\prime\prime}-(2\lambda+2\mu+1)xp^{\prime}+\frac{2\lambda}{x}p^{\prime}+n(n+2\lambda+2\mu)p=0

in the meaning of a constant, which can be seen from the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mu>-1/2
. If
n\geq 2
and the differential equation

(2.8)

L[\,p\,]:=(1-x^{2})p^{\prime\prime}-(2\lambda+2\mu+1)xp^{\prime}+\frac{2\lambda}{x}p^{\prime}+M^{2}p=0,

has a nontrivial solution
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for some
M>0
, then

p=cC_{s}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
with
2\leq s\leq n

being even and
M^{2}=s(s+2\lambda+2\mu)
, where

c
is an arbitrary nonzero constant.

Proof.

For
2\leq s\leq n
, let
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{s}a_{k}x^{k}
satisfy (
2.8
) with some
M>0
and
a_{s}\neq 0
. Set
a_{-1}=a_{s+1}=a_{s+2}=0
. Then

p^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{s}ka_{k}x^{k-1}\ \ {\rm and}\ \ p^{\prime\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=2}^{s}k(k-1)a_{k}x^{k-2},

which leads to

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})p^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{s-2}(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2}x^{k}-\sum_{k=2}^{s}k(k-1)a_{k}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{s}\left[(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2}-k(k-1)a_{k}\right]x^{k},

and

\displaystyle\frac{2\lambda}{x}p^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=2\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{s}ka_{k}x^{k-2}=2\lambda\sum_{k=-1}^{s}(k+2)a_{k+2}x^{k}.

Thus we obtain

L[\,p\,]=\sum_{k=-1}^{s}\Big\{(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}+\left[M^{2}-k(k+2\lambda+2\mu)\right]a_{k}\Big\}x^{k}.

which yields

(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=\left[k(k+2\lambda+2\mu)-M^{2}\right]a_{k},\ k=-1,0,1,\dots,s.

Since
a_{s}\neq 0
and
a_{s+2}=0
, we get
M^{2}=s(s+2\lambda+2\mu)
. It
follows from the equality
a_{-1}=0
that
a_{1}=a_{3}=\dots=0
,
which yields that
s
is even. We obtain

(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=(k-n)(k+s+2\lambda+2\mu)a_{k},\ k=0,2,4,\dots,s-2.

According to (
2.4
) we know
p=cC_{s}^{(\mu,\lambda)}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Generalized Hermite polynomials

For
\kappa>-1
, the generalized Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{\kappa},\,n\in\mathbb{N}
are given by

L_{n}^{\kappa}(x):=\frac{1}{n!}x^{-\kappa}e^{x}\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{n}(x^{n+\kappa}e^{-x}),

which is an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with respect to
the generalized Laguerre weight
x^{\kappa}e^{-x}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
.
The generalized Hermite polynomials
H_{n}^{\lambda}
can be given by the generalized Laguerre polynomials. More precisely,

\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &H_{2m}^{\lambda}(x)=(-1)^{m}2^{2m}m!\,L_{m}^{\lambda-1/2}(x^{2}),\\
&H_{2m+1}^{\lambda}(x)=(-1)^{m}2^{2m+1}m!\,xL_{m}^{\lambda+1/2}(x^{2}).\end{aligned}

Moreover, it follows from
[
4
]
that the coefficients
a_{k},k=0,\dots,n
of
H_{n}^{\lambda}
satisfy
a_{k}=0
if
n-k
is odd, and one of the following recurrence relations:

(2.9)

\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=2(k-n)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ n,k\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&(k+1)(k+2\lambda+2)a_{k+2}=2(k-n)a_{k},&&{\rm if}\ n,k\ {\rm is\ odd}.\end{aligned}

It is well known that
H_{s}^{\lambda},s=0,\dots,n
form an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with respect to
the generalized Hermite weight
w_{\lambda}(x)=|x|^{2\lambda}e^{-x^{2}}

on
\mathbb{R}
. Meanwhile, it has been proved in
[
4
]
that
the generalized Hermite polynomial

is the unique polynomial
solution of the differential-difference equation system

(2.10)

\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\ \mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p-2x\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p+\lambda_{n}^{2}p=0,\\
&\ p(-x)=(-1)^{n}p(x),\end{aligned}

with

(2.11)

\lambda_{n}^{2}=2\{n+\lambda[1-(-1)^{n}]\}

in the meaning of a constant. In fact, the symmetric property in (
2.10
) is not necessary, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
. If the differential-difference equation

(2.12)

\mathcal{L}[\,p\,]:=\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}^{2}p-2x\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p+M^{2}p=0

has a nontrivial solution
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for some
M>0
, then

p=cH_{s}^{\lambda}
with
M=\lambda_{s}
given by (
2.11
), where

and
c
is an arbitrary nonzero constant.

Proof.

For
1\leq s\leq n
, let
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{s}a_{k}x^{k}
satisfy (
2.12
) with some
M>0
and
a_{s}\neq 0
. Set
a_{s+1}=a_{s+2}=0
.
Then following the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{s}\{\{k+1+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k+1}]\}\{k+2+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}a_{k+2}+\{M^{2}-2\{k+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}\}a_{k}\}x^{k},

which yields that for all
k=0,\dots,s

\{k+1+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k+1}]\}\{k+2+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}a_{k+2}=\{2\{k+\lambda[1-(-1)^{k}]\}-M^{2}\}a_{k}.

Since
a_{s}\neq 0
and
a_{s+1}=a_{s+2}=0
, we get
M=\lambda_{s}
,
and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a_{s-1}=a_{s-3}=\dots=0,\\
&(k+2)(k+2\lambda+1)a_{k+2}=2(k-s)a_{k},\ \ \ {\rm if}\ s,k\ {\rm is\ even},\\
&(k+1)(k+2\lambda+2)a_{k+2}=2(k-s)a_{k},\ \ \ {\rm if}\ s,k\ {\rm is\ odd},\end{aligned}\right.

which, by (
2.9
), means
p=cH_{s}^{\lambda}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We also remark that for
n

H_{n}^{\lambda}
is the unique polynomial solution of differential equation

p^{\prime\prime}-2xp^{\prime}+\frac{2\lambda}{x}p^{\prime}+2np=0

Proposition 4

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mu>-1/2

p^{*}

\|p^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}=\|p_{e}^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}+\|p_{o}^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}

and

\|p^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}=\|p_{e}^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}+\|p_{o}^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}.

If
p^{*}
is not even, then
0\neq p^{*}_{o}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
. By
(
3.7
) we have

M_{n}^{2}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)=\frac{\|p^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}{\|p^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}>M^{2}_{n-1}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)\geq\frac{\|p_{o}^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}{\|p_{o}^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}},

which gives

\frac{\|p_{e}^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}{\|p_{e}^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}>\frac{\|p^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}{\|p^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda})}^{2}}.

This contradicts the assumption that
p^{*}
is extremal. Thus

p^{*}
must be an even polynomial. Meanwhile, since
p^{*}
is
extremal and even, we get
L[\,p^{*}\,]\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}q\,L[\,p^{*}\,]\,w_{\lambda}=0,\ {\rm for\ all}\ q\in\mathcal{P}_{n},

with
M_{n}(w_{\lambda})
, which deduces that
L[\,p^{*}\,]=0
by
setting
q=L[\,p^{*}\,]
in (
3.8
). By Proposition

4
we get
M_{n}(w_{\lambda})\leq\sqrt{2n}
. Therefore,

M_{n}(w_{\lambda})=\sqrt{2n}
with the extremal polynomial

H_{n}^{\lambda}
.

Next consider the case of odd
n\geq 2
. By
\lambda>0
and (
1.4
) we have

M_{n}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)>\sqrt{2n-\frac{4\lambda}{1+2\lambda}}>\sqrt{2n-2}=M_{n-1}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda}),\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right).

Using the same method as in the case of even
n
, we
show that the extremal polynomial
p^{*}
must be odd. Hence, we can
write

p^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{2j+1}x^{2j+1}
with
m=(n-1)/2
and
a_{n}\neq 0
. We derive from (
3.8
) that

(3.9)

\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{2j+1}\langle x^{2i+1},L[\,x^{2j+1}\,]\rangle_{w_{\lambda}}=0,\ i=0,\dots,m.

By the straightforward calculation, we have, for all
j=0,\dots,m
,

L[\,x^{2j+1}\,]=(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)x^{2j-1}+(M^{2}-4j-2)x^{2j+1}.

This leads to

\langle x^{2i+1},L[\,x^{2j+1}\,]\rangle_{w_{\lambda}}=(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)d_{2i+2j}+(M^{2}-4j-2)d_{2i+2j+2},

where
d_{2s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2s}w_{\lambda}=\Gamma(s+\lambda+1/2)
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Define

F_{m+1}(t):=\det\left(\left\{(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)d_{2i+2j}+(t-4j-2)d_{2i+2j+2}\right\}_{i,j=0}^{m}\right).

Using the Crammer rule, we derive from
p^{*}\neq 0
that

F_{m+1}(M^{2})=0
. Therefore,

M_{n}(w_{\lambda,\mu})=\sqrt{\nu_{m+1}}
with the extremal
polynomials determined by (
3.9
) with
M^{2}=\nu_{m+1}
, where

\nu_{m+1}
is the large positive root of the polynomial

F_{m+1}
. It also holds for
n=1
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is completed.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

(i) For the case of
n=1
, we observe that the extremal polynomial
must be attained by
p^{*}(x)=c(x+a_{0})\ (c\neq 0)
. Then the
straightforward calculation gives

\|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,p^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}^{2}=c^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{\lambda,\mu+1}=c^{2}B\left(\lambda+\frac{1}{2},\mu+\frac{3}{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle\|p^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}^{2}

\displaystyle=c^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{\lambda+1,\mu}+c^{2}a_{0}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{\lambda,\mu}=B\left(\lambda+\frac{1}{2},\mu+\frac{3}{2}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{2\lambda+1}{2\mu+1}+c^{2}\right),

where

B({\alpha},\beta)=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+\beta)}

for
{\alpha},\beta>0
. This leads that

\displaystyle M_{1}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,p^{*}{{}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}}{\|p^{*}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu+1}{2\lambda+1}},

with the extremal polynomial
p^{*}(x)=cx,\ c\neq 0
.

(ii) For the case of
n\geq 2
, let
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
be an
extremal polynomial of

M_{n}\equiv M_{n}\left(L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu}),(1-x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right):=\sup_{0\neq p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,p^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}}{\|p\|_{L_{2}(w_{\lambda,\mu})}}.

By Lemma
1
we have

(3.10)

\int_{-1}^{1}q\,L_{n}[\,p^{*}\,]\,w_{\lambda,\mu}=0,\ {\rm for\ all}\ q\in\mathcal{P}_{n},

where

L_{n}[\,p\,]:=(1-x^{2})p^{\prime\prime}-(2\lambda+2\mu+1)xp^{\prime}+\frac{2\lambda}{x}p^{\prime}+M_{n}^{2}p.

We write
p^{*}=p_{e}^{*}+p_{o}^{*}
, where
p_{e}^{*}
and
p_{o}^{*}
are the even
and odd parts of
p^{*}
, respectively. Then we have

L_{n}[\,p^{*}\,]=L_{n}[\,p_{e}^{*}\,]+L_{n}[\,p_{o}^{*}\,],

where
L_{n}[\,p_{e}^{*}\,]\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is even and
L_{n}[\,p_{o}^{*}\,]
is odd. Taking
q=L_{n}[\,p_{e}^{*}\,]
leads to

\int_{-1}^{1}|L_{n}[\,p_{e}^{*}\,]|^{2}\,w_{\lambda,\mu}=0,

which gives
L_{n}[\,p_{e}^{*}\,]=0
. Thus we have

(3.11)

\int_{-1}^{1}q\,L_{n}[\,p_{o}^{*}\,]\,w_{\lambda,\mu}=0,\ {\rm for\ all}\ q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}.

If

p_{e}^{*}\neq 0
. Applying Proposition

2

where

),

. Then

\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{2j+1}\langle x^{2i+1},L_{n}[\,x^{2j+1}\,]\rangle_{w_{\lambda,\mu}}=0,\ i=0,\dots,m.

Meanwhile, by the straightforward calculation, we have, for all
j=0,\dots,m
,

L_{n}[\,x^{2j+1}\,]=(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)x^{2j-1}+[M_{n}^{2}-(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda+2\mu+1)]x^{2j+1}.

This leads to

\langle x^{2i+1},L_{n}[\,x^{2j+1}\,]\rangle_{w_{\lambda,\mu}}=(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda)c_{2i+2j}+[M_{n}^{2}-(2j+1)(2j+2\lambda+2\mu+1)]c_{2i+2j+2},

where

M_{n}(w_{\lambda,\mu})=\Bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\max\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{\frac{n}{2}}},\sqrt{n(n+2\lambda+2\mu)}\right\},\ &&{\rm if}\ n\ {\rm is\ even},\\

Clearly, it also holds for

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 2

Proof.

First we prove that

where
\mathcal{M}_{n}(w_{\lambda,\mu})
denotes by the supremum

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}p\,\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}q\,w_{\lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=
