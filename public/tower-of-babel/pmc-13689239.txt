Accurate prediction of CRISPR editing outcomes in somatic cell lines and zygotes with few-shot learning with inDecay
Authors: Weizhong Zheng, Lu Yu, Chao Wu, Shaoxian Cao, Mengying Zhao, Guoliang Wang, Kam Ho So, Jun Song, Chen Cheng, Joshua W. K. Ho, Xueqin Liu, Meng Wu, Zhonghua Liu, Huili Wang, Pentao Liu, Guocheng Lan, Yuanhua Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prediction of CRISPR/Cas outcomes remains unsatisfactory in embryos due to distinct DNA repair preferences and the lack of large-scale embryo editing profiles. We introduce inDecay, a flexible system for predicting the proportion of CRISPR-induced indels from the target sequence. Owing to its parameter-efficient and cell-type-aware design, inDecay performs well in both in-sample training and out-of-sample fine-tuning. Starting with cross-cell-line predictions, we observed that inDecay maintains high accuracy in mouse zygote editing and in goat, cattle and porcine embryos. inDecay may accelerate mouse model generation, livestock embryonic editing, and gene therapy applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04211-x.

Background

The CRISPR/Cas system enables precise and efficient genome editing [1–3]. The precise control of potential editing outcomes is often required in a wide range of derived applications, including generating model animals, treating genetic diseases by correcting pathogenic genetic variations, and engineering therapeutic cells [4–6]. To this end, it is helpful to have an unbiased and accurate prediction of the repair outcomes during guide RNA (gRNA) design to avoid undesired editing outcomes.

CRISPR-induced genome editing primarily occurs through the repair of a double-stranded break (DSB) at the site targeted by the guide RNA [7, 8], producing a diverse array of repair products. For template-free CRISPR editing, the main pathways involved are classical Non-Homologous End Joining (cNHEJ), which produces small insertions and deletions, and Microhomology-Mediated End Joining (MMEJ), which generally produces larger deletions with the existence of short homologous sequences [9, 10]. The choice of the repair pathway and the randomness within each repair process give rise to a complex repair profile. The repair profile is regulated at two levels. Globally, the expression of DNA repair genes shapes the overall preference over different repair pathways [11, 12]. Locally, the sequence context flanking the cleavage site determines the probability of each potential insertion or deletion (indel) based on the preference of indels by different repair pathways.

Recently, highly scalable self-targeting editing techniques have emerged, making it possible to comprehensively profile CRISPR-induced DNA repair events for a target sequence [13–15]. The generated data offer unique opportunities to decipher the impact of local sequence context on repair patterns and were used to develop computational methods for predicting CRISPR editing outcomes, with prominent examples including inDelphi [13], ForeCasT [14], and Lindel [15]. However, existing methods have primarily been trained on datasets measured in a certain cell line (or pooling multiple lines) and learned the mapping from sequence to repair profile, ignoring the variability of the global cellular environment across cell types or conditions [16, 17]. Consequently, their predictions may be inaccurate for cell types that exhibit distinct preferences for repair pathways [13, 14].

One particular example is predicting gene editing in zygotes or early embryos. Embryonic cells exhibit distinct DNA repair activity compared with somatic cells, particularly during early developmental stages [18]. Studies have suggested that embryonic cells may favor the NHEJ repair pathway at certain stages, possibly due to the absence of specific proteins and shifts in cell cycle phase distribution [18–20]. As a result, predicting CRISPR editing outcomes in embryonic cells poses greater challenges for current methods. For example, although inDelphi has developed variants tailored to several cell types [13], its predictions tend to over-represent microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) repair outcomes. Furthermore, these methods have shown poor performance when applied to germline editing [21, 22].

In this study, we present inDecay, a multi-stage deep learning method for predicting the CRISPR-induced editing outcome given the target sequence. Our method particularly considers the variability of the DNA repair system across different cell types or conditions by explicitly modeling the ratios of different repair pathways (insertion, MH deletion, and NH deletion). Moreover, we designed an informative encoding scheme for each deletion event based on its deletion length, MH track, and deletion start site. The encoded deletion features enable efficient estimation of the probabilities of deletion events and allow for post hoc customization of the model using user-adjustable hyperparameters. Our results demonstrate the superiority of inDecay over existing methods on multiple cell lines. We particularly highlight the use of few-shot learning, which effectively facilitates generalization of our model to new cell lines. Moreover, we demonstrated that this transfer-learning capability plays a critical role in accurate prediction in mouse and livestock embryos in our newly generated in vivo datasets. Therefore, with its flexibility and accuracy, inDecay holds great potential for broader application in CRISPR-based genome editing studies.

Highly informative event-based features enable a flexible and lightweight model for repair outcome prediction

The DNA repair outcomes of the CRISPR-induced double-strand break depend on both the sequence context of the target site and the cellular environment as defined by the relative activities of different repair pathways [11, 12]. We designed a novel repair-outcome prediction method, inDecay, that considers the impact of both components. Our method, inDecay, first captures the sequence context by detecting all possible microhomologous (MH) sequences (referred to as MH-track later) within the target sequences. This enables us to quickly identify the MH deletion events and craft features for each event. Next, the preference for different repair outcomes was captured by a multi-stage learning system in a cell type-specific manner, which used the manually crafted features to predict the probability of each event (indel).

The strength of MMEJ events is largely decided by the length and the starting position of the MH-tracks. A long MH-track that is close to the cut site can sometimes result in a single MH deletion event dominating the entire repair profile [23]. Characterizing MH tracks and annotating MH deletions from hundreds of possible deletion events is thus important. To quickly identify all the MH-tracks in the target sequence, we converted the problem of searching all possible homologous sub-sequences into a diagonal-line detection problem. This was achieved by matching the left and right cleaved fragments into a two-dimensional matrix similar to the sequence substitution matrix (Fig. 1A), which was traditionally used to score aligning nucleotides. Then, MH-tracks can be efficiently identified by applying convolution filters to the matrix (see Method), avoiding iterative string matching by previous methods [14, 15]. The identified MH-tracks were then used to annotate the corresponding MH-deletions in terms of their deletion start site, maximum MH length, and deletion length.

We observed a consistent frequency decay of deletion events with respect to the deletion length as well as the start site (proximity of starting position to the cut site) across different cell types (see Fig. 1A). However, depending on the sequence context, this pattern can be disrupted by larger deletions induced by long MH-tracks (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). We therefore designed features in a first-principles manner to represent this statistical frequency decay using the deletion start site, maximum MH length, and deletion length extracted by the method mentioned above. The deletion length and start site were transformed by a decay function, and the MH length was regulated by a power function (Fig. 1A and Methods), with each term having its own hyper-parameter.

In the second step, more features were generated based on the category, as well as the deleted or inserted nucleotides of each indel (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the inDecay frequency module transforms the finalized feature array of an event into its probability using a neural network. These event-specific features were constructed differently for the three classes, including microhomology (MH) deletions, non-MH deletions (NH), and insertions. NH deletion features were similar to MH deletion features, with several features set to zero (see Table S4). Insertion features mainly captured the indel size and the complement of inserted nucleotides to the flanking sequences. Additionally, we added four types of features shared among all events to inform the overall properties of the target sequence, including the insert-del proportion from the first stage, the overall MH-strength derived from the filtered substitution matrix, the GC-ratio and the one-hot encoded 9-bp sequences upstream to the PAM site. The shared features reduced the inductive bias of the neural network by learning hidden variables that can complement the hand-crafted features. Taken together, we curated a set of 61 information-dense features as the input.

In the next stage, we utilized the annotated events and first-principles-derived features to predict the full spectrum of repair outcomes along with additional sequence features. The prediction was done via two steps. The first step involved the ratio module of inDecay to predict the overall ratio between deletion and insertion from the 9-bp nucleotides before the Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM) site. This ratio was highly predictable using a linear model. In fact, different studies have reported a consistent nucleotide usage when it differentiates deletion-prone and insertion-prone sequences [13–15].

In summary, we proposed an event-based multi-stage learning method inDecay to predict the CRISPR editing outcome. inDecay accurately predicts the repair profiles in five cell lines

To evaluate the performance of inDecay for different cellular environments, we applied it to a multi-cell line dataset generated by Allen & Crepaldi et al. [14], which profiled the repair outcomes of over 35,000 guide RNAs for all 5 cell lines (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Among them, we selected 1,133 highly covered guide RNAs containing over 1,000 total counts across cell lines as the test set and kept the remaining samples that had over 500 counts to train our inDecay model. The repair profiles of the targeted sequences have manifested a strong variability among cell types in several aspects (see Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Baseline models, Lindel and FORECasT, provided only a single model without cell type specification. To ensure a fair comparison, we retrained Lindel and FORECasT for each cell line, using the same training and test split. The inDelphi authors trained different versions on an independent dataset for 4 different cell lines. Among them, mESC and K562 overlapped with our training data set. Due to the difficulties of reproducing inDelphi’s pipeline, we compared inDelphi only on mESC and K562 data. We then benchmarked inDecay with the baselines on the held-out test set. To thoroughly assess the predicted outcome distribution of each method, a variety of evaluation metrics were applied (Fig. 2A), including those previously utilized and some newly introduced ones (see Methods). FORECasT’s datasets involved two experimental replicates for each cell line; therefore, repair profiles from the two replicates were used to determine the upper bound of each evaluation metric.

We began by evaluating the yield of the most frequent events through the recall of the top-5 predicted indel sizes and events. CRISPR editing typically produces a group of long-tail distributed indels. Although each target sequence can generate over 300 unique indels, the top 5 accounted for 65.1% of the total (averaged over 59,337 samples from 5 cell lines, Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Adding five more indels increased this cumulative frequency to only 77.2%, indicating that the first five indels were the dominant events. Here, inDecay showed the highest top-5 recall in terms of indel sizes and specific events (3.6 for inDecay in Fig. 2B, 3.3 in 2C). inDecay outperformed Lindel by a large margin and was better than FORECasT and inDelphi (p=0.031, n = 5p=0.031,n=5, one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Consistently, inDecay also achieved the highest top-10 recall (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). These results together suggest that inDecay can robustly reveal the most dominant and subdominant indels despite using much fewer features than previous methods.

Next, we looked into the prediction of indel length by grouping events according to the editing length. In CRISPR/Cas gene editing experiments, the length of the editing outcome is essential for evaluating editing efficiency, specificity, and functional phenotype. By collapsing repair events of the same length, we transformed highly sparse event probabilities into a dense indel-length distribution consisting of 41 elements. The transformation allows for accurate measurement of the reconstructed distribution as it avoids the bias caused by the null entries of the rare indels (as opposed to the Mean Squared Error used in [15]). We also included Kendall’s ττ coefficient, which considers the ordering within 41 indel lengths (1–2 bp insertion, insertion>3 bp, 1–37 bp deletions and deletions >= 38 bp). inDecay could predict the most concordant ranking of indel length, with a mean Kendall’s ττ of 0.73 across cell lines (Fig. 2D). When evaluating the overall similarity of indel length, inDecay presented a KL divergence around 10% and 32% lower than inDelphi and FORECasT (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, while we found FORECasT achieved comparable top-5 recall, its Kendall’s ττ (among 41 indel lengths) was substantially lower than that of inDecay (on average 0.08 lower across cell lines), indicating its suboptimal performance in predicting the ranking of top events (similarly in the KL divergence metric). These results together demonstrate inDecay’s ability to accurately recapitulate the indel length distribution at different scopes.

We subsequently inspected how accurately our model can predict the likelihood of introducing a frameshift mutation, which is important for designing effective gene knockouts. We started by examining the R-square value between observed and predicted frameshift ratio (i.e., the proportion of indels causing frameshift and consequent loss of function) with a metric of frameshift ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 (FSR2). Across all cell lines, inDecay achieved the highest frameshift ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2. Specifically, for the mESC cell line, inDecay reached an FSR2 of 0.859 with a large improvement over existing baselines (0.622 for Lindel, 0.77 for inDelphi, and 0.804 for FORECasT), thus improving our ability to estimate the efficiency of introducing loss-of-function indels (Fig. 2F and Additional file 1: Fig. S5). This improvement is attributable to inDecay’s accurate prediction at finer reading-frame resolution, which is to distinguish in-frame, +1-bp, and +2-bp frameshift outcomes (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). Additionally, we investigated the predicted ratio of deletion events to insertion events (Fig. 2G), where inDecay consistently outperformed all other methods in five cell lines, partly owing to its multi-stage design (Fig. 2G). While Lindel and FORECasT performed reasonably well, inDelphi unexpectedly displayed disproportionately low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2, attributed to a general overestimation of the deletion events (Additional file 1: Fig. S5B).

In summary, our method, inDecay, accurately predicts the repair profiles for different cell lines. By combining the advantages of an event-based model and a multi-stage design, inDecay produces predictions that closely resemble the observed event probabilities for the most frequent events, the distribution of indel length, and the proportion of different indel types.

Model interpretation analysis confirms that inDecay’s features capture biologically meaningful repair determinants

To identify which components of inDecay’s feature design underlie its cross-cell-line performance, we combined two complementary interpretation approaches: feature ablation, which quantifies importance at the global level, and SHAP analysis, which provides local attributions at the level of individual events (see Methods). The feature set of inDecay is intentionally saturated, with several features describing different aspects of the same underlying repair mechanism (see Table S4). We performed ablation at the group level by jointly masking related features. Quantifying each group’s effect on prediction performance revealed that features related to microhomology, indel size, and insertion–nucleotide complementarity were the most influential groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A-D). SHAP analysis provides complementary event-level attributions, allowing us to test whether each feature behaves as its biological motivation predicts. For deletion events, deletion length and microhomology length ranked as the top two features by mean |SHAP| value, confirming their dominant role (Additional file 1: Fig. S7E). Start-site position ranked third, consistent with the known positional bias of Cas9-induced deletions relative to the cut site [13, 14]. The signs of the attributions matched our design assumptions: event probability decayed with increasing deletion length and start-site distance, while long MH tracks counteracted this decay and promoted longer deletions. These top-ranked features also overlapped with those highlighted by the ablation analysis, indicating consistency between global and local interpretation. For insertion events, SHAP attributions revealed how inDecay hierarchically discriminates event categories (Additional file 1: Fig. S7F). The model first uses a microhomology length of zero to flag non-MMEJ events, then a deletion size of zero to confirm an insertion. Among insertion-specific features, left nucleotide match, shift, and insertion length emerged as the top drivers — consistent with the templated insertion mechanism, in which the nucleotide identity at the cut-site overhang determines the dominant +1 insertion.

Transfer learning enables effective cross-cell-line and cross-editor prediction with inDecay

In practice, CRISPR/Cas genome editing is carried out across various cell types and organisms, many of which are not encompassed within the dataset for training inDecay or other existing models. Transfer learning represents a way to effectively overcome the cell type variability for models pre-trained on another cell line (Fig. 3A). To demonstrate this, we selected two cell pairs, CHO-mESC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {KLD}_{IDL}=1.2$$\end{document}KLDIDL=1.2) and CHO-iPSC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {KLD}_{IDL}=1.5$$\end{document}KLDIDL=1.5, see Additional file 1: Fig. S3), that have two of the largest KL divergences in indel length distribution. To assess the minimum number of samples required to overcome cell line variability, we designed a series of transfer learning experiments to evaluate the effect of fine-tuning data size. The source model, pre-trained on CHO cells, was fine-tuned using incrementally larger subsets of the query cell line’s data, specifically 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 80, 100, 300, 500, and 1000 guide RNAs. These fine-tuning samples were randomly drawn 10 times from the guides in the query cell line, and the resulting models were evaluated on 1,133 test samples of the query cell line to calculate the average performance.

When applied to unseen cell lines without fine-tuning, inDecay’s performance dropped substantially compared to models trained exclusively on those cell lines, e.g., the major event recall (ββ > 0.15) fell to 0.05 in mESC and 0.04 in iPSC, approaching zero in both cases. However, the gap between cell lines can be quickly closed: for example, in mESC (Fig. 3B), fine-tuning with as few as 30 samples brought major event recall to over 0.70 and the frameshift ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 to over 0.74 from 0.53. Other metrics, including absolute error of frameshift, Kendall’s ττ of indel length, top-5 indel length accuracy, and KL divergence, also showed the largest improvements within this range. Similar patterns were also observed for the most distantly related cell line pair (CHO to iPSC), where 30 fine-tuning samples gave substantial improvement, and after 30–50 samples the marginal improvements (performance gain per extra sample) became lower (Fig. 3C). Importantly, the five cell lines shared the same set of gRNA-target sequence pairs; that is, the fine-tuning dataset does not introduce new sequences. This means that the performance gain during the transfer learning process is not due to additional data but rather to adaptation to the repair preference of the target cell line.

To further test whether inDecay’s few-shot framework transfers across CRISPR effector protein, we examined the Cas9-TREX2 fusion protein (short for TREX2), which shows different indel preferences but does not entirely deviate from the repair pathways that inDecay can cover (Fig. 3D). The 3‘→→5‘ exonuclease TREX2 shifts editing outcomes away from the short, cut-proximal deletions predicted by the NHEJ repair pathway and toward longer, asymmetric, one-sided deletions with enrichment around 9–15 bp (Fig. 3E). We set up similar fine-tuning experiments with increasing sample size and evaluated on 618 held-out targets to assess whether sample efficiency is maintained in a cross-editor scenario. Similar to the cell type transfer experiment, inDecay achieves meaningful improvement with as few as 30 samples (mean frameshift error = 0.06, KLD = 3.5) and continues to improve as the sample size increases. TREX2 shows a distinct indel size distribution from that predicted by the model trained on SpCas9 data (black vs. gray curve in Fig. 3E). The gap can be quickly filled with only 30 samples.

In summary, inDecay can effectively adapt to the repair landscapes of different cell types with limited fine-tuning data. Importantly, as few as 30 to 50 samples are sufficient to achieve alignment with the query cell line or a different editor. inDecay can quickly adapt to distinct mouse zygote editing patterns using few-shot learning

Template-free CRISPR/Cas editing has become a widely adopted technique for generating gene knockout animal models in biological research. Due to the limited availability of mammalian zygotes, it is essential to achieve efficient editing outcomes in order to maximize the yield of knockout embryos. However, embryonic cells exhibit unique repair preferences, and embryo editing normally involves different techniques, such as microinjection or electroporation, to deliver the CRISPR-gRNA complex, collectively shaping a distinct editing profile compared with somatic cell lines. Moreover, existing predictors, trained on self-targeting data, have been shown to be inaccurate for embryo editing [21, 22], underscoring the necessity of developing models trained on embryo-specific data.

To characterize the patterns of CRISPR-induced indel formation in mouse embryos and provide data for training embryo-specific models, we designed 52 gRNAs targeting 43 genes and performed editing experiments across a large cohort of embryos for each gRNA (Fig. 4A; guide sequences and read counts in Table S1). Given the efficiency of embryo editing, we collected around 11,700 mouse zygotes for sgRNA–Cas9 complex delivery to obtain a sufficient number of genome-edited embryos. The targeted loci were subsequently PCR-amplified and subjected to Sanger sequencing, followed by signal processing and filtering to derive the editing profiles of each gRNA–gene pair (see Methods). Notably, we conducted pilot experiments on 145 mouse zygotes to verify that the editing and electroporation pipeline does not compromise embryo viability (Table S3 and Additional file 1: Fig. S8). As a result, we compiled a dataset comprising editing outcomes from 1,878 successfully edited mouse embryos across 52 genomic loci, out of the 11,781 zygotes initially collected. As our method has demonstrated sample-efficient transferability (Fig. 3), we next employed few-shot learning using inDecay to capture the patterns of embryonic editing. To ensure fair evaluation, we applied leave-one-out cross-validation to split the fine-tuning and testing samples. In each fold, we evaluated the prediction results of one gRNA while using the remaining 51 gRNAs for training and validation. To quantify model performance, we aggregated results across 30 high-quality test gRNAs (indicated by stars in Fig. 4B), which were selected based on larger embryo sample sizes and higher sequencing signal quality.

Genome editing in mouse zygotes yields an indel distribution with a strong preference for small indels (observed, orange), differing from what the model learned from mESC data (pretrained, blue) (Fig. 4C). Remarkably, with only 52 samples, our method quickly addresses the discrepancy and surpasses our pre-trained inDecay model in all evaluated metrics (Fig. 4D, E, F), even though our pre-trained inDecay model has already outperformed current mainstream tools in embryonic editing prediction. More specifically, we highlighted the improvements as follows. In terms of the distribution of indel length, the mouse zygote editing data exhibits a heavy weighting toward 1-bp deletions and 1-bp insertions, a trend precisely captured by our fine-tuned inDecay model (see Fig. 4C, last panel for KL divergence details), while the model trained on mESC data shows additional peaks at 5- and 7-bp deletions. Moreover, when examining the major events (i.e., those with ratios >=>= 1/4), we found that our fine-tuned model yielded an average of 80% of major events, whereas inDelphi, Lindel, and FORECasT reached only 37–52% (Fig. 4D). Consistently, inDecay also achieved the lowest absolute error between predicted and observed frameshift ratios, enabling more realistic estimation of the chance of functional knockout in the zygote genome (Fig. 4D). When looking at the detailed event-level predictions per gRNA in the three examples (Fig. 4F), we observe that only the few-shot model reliably captures the true dominant editing patterns in embryonic cells. Notably, we chose mESC as the pre-trained model for demonstrating the most challenging scenario, and this improvement holds across different source cell lines (Additional file 1: Fig. S9).

Application of inDecay to predicting zygote editing in livestock species with scarce data

The CRISPR/Cas system is increasingly used to improve breeding and produce animal lines with desired traits in large mammals, especially for livestock [24, 25]. The emerging genetic modification tools, like variants of CRISPR/Cas systems, have predominantly been developed for and tested in model species. Despite the high value for agricultural studies and the field of large animal research, the preference for CRISPR-based editing and the subsequent repairing patterns in livestock zygotes still lack comprehensive characterization. Because livestock species are mostly monotocous animals with long gestation periods, it takes much longer to obtain genome-editing results from livestock zygotes, with far fewer embryo samples per donor than in mice. It would therefore be particularly helpful to optimize guide RNA design using accurate predictions of the editing outcome to reduce the number of trials. However, the time- and labor-intensive nature of editing livestock embryos makes such data scarce and possibly noisy, consequently restricting the training of standard machine learning models.

To investigate the gene editing and DNA repair preferences of livestock zygotes, we generated the first dataset suitable for model training by collecting a total of 3,632 zygotes (Fig. 5A) from major livestock species, including cattle, porcine, sheep, and goat. Zygotes were harvested via superovulation, followed by injection of RNA–Cas9 protein complexes for gene editing before the two-cell stage (Fig. 5B). After culturing to expand the cell population, targeted regions were amplified and sequenced via Sanger methods. Data preprocessing and quality filtering mirrored the protocols established for the mouse zygote dataset (see Methods). Ultimately, 434 embryos exhibiting successful genome editing were identified, shaping the indel outcome profiles for 12 distinct guide RNAs (guide sequences and read counts in Table S2). The total event-count distribution of the in-house mouse and livestock datasets is summarized in Additional file 1: Fig. S10.

Our in-house zygote editing results revealed distinctly different DNA repair activities in livestock species compared with somatic cell lines (Fig. 5C) and mouse zygotes. Notably, the indel length profiles in livestock embryos exhibited prominent peaks at 4- and 5-bp deletions, in contrast to the strong preference for smaller indels in mouse zygotes (Fig. 4C), likely suggesting higher activity of the MMEJ pathway in livestock embryos. Utilizing leave-one-out few-shot learning with as few as 12 guide RNAs, inDecay achieved high fidelity in matching indel length distributions (reducing KL divergence from 0.903 to 0.587) and recovering major editing events in approximately 80% of cases—yielding the highest top-event recall and the lowest frameshift prediction error observed (Fig. 5F). Discussion

The CRISPR-induced DSB repair procedure displays substantial cell type variability, leading to unique weighting of MMEJ, c-NHEJ deletion, and insertion events within the editing outcomes [10]. In this study, we introduced inDecay, a flexible CRISPR editing outcome prediction system that can capture the intricate variations across different cell states. Coupled with our unique MH-event detection system, inDecay specifically crafts features for each MMEJ and c-NHEJ deletion event with several highly informative decay terms. Through extensive benchmarking, we showed that inDecay outperforms other methods, and the predicted repair outcomes align with the observed outcomes in all aspects (see Fig. 2). The efficient design of inDecay allowed it to be adapted to a different cellular context in a highly data-efficient manner (see Fig. 3). The flexibility and lightweight model architecture make inDecay uniquely suitable to address the prediction problem for zygote editing, an important application of CRISPR editing where the sample size can hardly scale up. Indeed, the parameter-efficient design keeps both runtime and memory footprint low during few-shot fine-tuning, even on a consumer laptop (Additional file 1: Fig. S11). To comprehensively measure the embryonic DNA repair profiles of mouse and livestock, we collected more than 2,200 genetically modified embryos from more than 15,000 zygotes. Both zygote editing datasets show unique repair patterns compared with cell-line data, but the pre-trained inDecay can adapt to mouse data with 52 samples (Fig. 4) and improve livestock with no more than 4 data points per species (Fig. 5).

Our results demonstrate that embryo editing outcomes in mouse zygotes are characterized by a sharply peaked indel length distribution favoring minimal edits (1-bp insertions and deletions), distinct from the more varied indel profiles observed in somatic cell-derived models such as mESCs, which is an immortalized in vitro counterpart of the mouse zygote (see Fig. 4C). This biological specificity underscores the pitfalls of applying somatic-cell-trained editing-prediction tools directly to embryonic contexts. Notably, existing methods such as Lindel, inDelphi, and FORECasT exhibit substantial performance degradation in embryonic datasets, likely due to their limited training scope and failure to capture embryo-specific DNA repair preferences, reinforcing the need for data-driven adaptation in specialized settings. Furthermore, the inherently limited availability of mammalian zygotes—even with superovulation techniques—presents substantial challenges for collecting comprehensive training data, especially for non-model species such as livestock. This constraint amplifies the demand for modeling strategies that are both data-efficient and robust to sparse, noisy data.

One hurdle in developing a generalizable editing outcome prediction system lies in the under-characterization of cell types and species-level variability of repair outcome preferences. In early efforts, both FORECasT and inDelphi employed the self-targeting system in the context of several different cell lines. However, it remains unclear how representative these cell lines are for their species and their counterparts in the living organism (e.g., mESCs and mouse zygotes). While it seems challenging to conduct large-scale self-targeting assays in diverse cell types, gene-expression profiles of many cell types have been widely characterized and are readily accessible. Linking gene-expression patterns to repair preferences of uncharacterized cells offers a promising path. Recent technical advances by repair-seq [26] have demonstrated how the repair outcomes vary across different perturbed expression contexts, revealing genetic dependencies and fine-grained heterogeneity in DSB-repair pathways. We can leverage inDecay’s predicted event distribution as a prior. Given the activities of the core DSB repair pathways of a cell, we can reweight this prior according to the pathway to obtain the final repair outcome for an unseen cell type.

While inDecay demonstrates robust adaptability to new editing contexts, its architecture is specifically tailored to the cleavage preferences of CRISPR/Cas systems to maintain a compact, parameter-efficient design. The framework assumes that the editor (1) induces a double-strand break at a precise cleavage site (e.g., 3 bp upstream of the PAM for SpCas9) and (2) triggers standard MMEJ or NHEJ repair pathways. Consequently, the model is not directly applicable to non-CRISPR editors such as Transcription Activator-Like Effector Nucleases (TALENs) and Zinc-Finger Nucleases (ZFNs) [27], which would require redefining the cut geometry and the potential indel spectrum. Given that CRISPR/Cas systems currently dominate the field of genome editing, we anticipate that inDecay will remain applicable across editor variants.

Large DNA deletions are a class of unintended repair outcomes not typically captured by self-targeting systems. In our mouse embryo data, we observed large deletions ranging from hundreds to thousands of base pairs, extending far beyond the target site. Contradictory findings have been reported: Owens et al. [28] observed microhomology at large deletion breakpoints, implicating MMEJ, whereas Kosicki et al. [29] found that many large deletions lacked flanking microhomology, suggesting alternative mechanisms. A very recent study suggested that additional repair mechanisms were involved in producing large deletions, rather than solely relying on the MMEJ and c-NHEJ pathways [30]. Predicting the emergence of large deletions from the sequence alone remains challenging and requires further investigation.

As CRISPR-based gene therapy approaches clinical use, accurate estimates of editing efficiency in advance will be essential. Given concerns about the security of individuals’ genetic data and other ethical issues, repair-outcome data on actual human embryos or stem cells will foreseeably remain very limited. A flexible and parameter-efficient prediction system like inDecay will substantially benefit few-shot transfer to specific use cases with different cell types and limited training data. Looking forward, incorporating richer molecular features such as chromatin accessibility, epigenetic marks, and multi-omic data may further enhance prediction fidelity, especially for more divergent cell types or species. Additionally, integrating inDecay into experimental design workflows can accelerate gene editing projects by enabling in silico screening and ranking of gRNAs with optimized expected outcomes, reducing resource expenditure and improving experimental success rates.

Conclusions

CRISPR editing yields a complex distribution of indels that emerges from a competition among distinct DNA repair pathways, whose relative activities shift with cell state and cellular environment. inDecay is a parameter-efficient, cell-type-aware deep-learning model that represents this competition through interpretable event-level features, enabling it to learn from small datasets via few-shot transfer and to predict editing outcomes for rare samples such as embryos, where large training cohorts cannot be obtained.

Across five somatic cell lines, inDecay predicts repair profiles more accurately than inDelphi, Lindel, and FORECasT, and its compactness is precisely what makes it transferable. Through few-shot fine-tuning, it quickly recovers the editing patterns of unseen cell types and even a different editor, the Cas9–TREX2 fusion, with as few as 30–50 samples. Building on this, we generated the first large-scale embryo editing profile with 1,878 edited mouse and 434 edited livestock embryos which exhibit repair preferences distinct from somatic cell lines. On these data, inDecay recovers ∼∼80% of major editing events in mouse zygotes, compared with 37–52% for existing tools, and achieves comparable major-event recall (∼∼80%) in goat, cattle, and porcine embryos with no more than four guides per species.

As genome editing extends to embryos and non-model species where training data are inherently scarce, inDecay offers accurate prediction that can directly guide gRNA design for mouse-model generation, livestock engineering, and therapeutic editing.

Methods

Data collection

Embryos derived from murine, bovine (cattle), goat, porcine, and sheep species [31–34] were utilized. A ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex—comprising Cas9 protein (Invitrogen Truecut Cas9 protein V2 catalog: A36499) (80 ng/μμL) and sgRNA (TsingKe Biotechnology Co., Ltd) (20 ng/μμL) in 70 μμL Opti-MEM medium—was delivered into zygotes via electroporation (murine) or pronuclear microinjection (bovine, goat, porcine, and sheep). Electroporation parameters: Poring pulse:200 V, 2 ms duration, 50 ms interval, 4 pulses, + polarity; Transfer pulse:20 V, 50 ms duration, 50 ms interval, 5 pulses, ± polarity. Method: 60 μμL RNP complex was loaded into the electrode chamber (impedance: 0.48–0.52 kΩΩ). Post-electroporation, zygotes were cultured in KSOM medium (Merck Millipore, Cat. No. MR-107-D) to the blastocyst stage or transferred to recipients after overnight culture. For bovine, goat, sheep, and porcine embryos, pronuclear microinjection was performed using 5 pL of a solution containing 50 ng/μμL sgRNA and 100 ng/μμL Cas9 mRNA. Injected embryos were cultured to blastocyst for bovine, goat, and porcine. Genomic DNA from blastocyst-stage embryos or ear biopsies served as reference and experimental material. Whole-genome DNA extraction of pooled embryos was executed using the Single-Cell Whole Genome Amplification Kit (Vazyme, China; Cat# N603-02). The whole-genome DNA of ear biopsies was extracted using NP40 lysis buffer. Target regions were PCR-amplified and analyzed via Sanger sequencing. Resulting sequences were processed using ICE (Synthego) or DECODR (RGEN Tools) for editing efficiency quantification.

Data preprocessing

The prediction takes the target sequence as the input feature X and predicts the detailed editing outcomes Y, covering a diverse spectrum of events. The input sequence X was generally standardized, but the output events Y may have different annotation formats from method to method, depending on the coverage of the output events.

Transcode FORECasT data with the Lindel coding system

To convert FORECasT’s event identifiers into higher-resolution Lindel classes, we built an editing outcome transcoding system based on FORECasT’s indelmap results, in which every unique indel was coupled with an identifier. We incorporated two indel labeling functions from separate studies and corroborated their labeling results to ensure the reliability of the conversion.

Our conversion pipeline combined two complementary annotation functions: the CIGAR-string conversion function introduced by Liu et al. [35] and the IndelGen function from Chen et al. [15]. The transcoding procedure took three inputs — an intact target sequence, a FORECasT identifier, and a paired editing outcome sequence — and mapped them to a Lindel class through the following procedure. We first truncated the 79-bp target sequence to 60 bp, centering the cut site at position 30, and used IndelGen to simulate all possible outcome–class label pairs for the truncated sequence. We then matched the observed editing outcome sequence to one of these simulated outcomes, thereby retrieving its corresponding Lindel class label. In parallel, we used the Apindel CIGAR-string conversion function [35] to map the FORECasT identifier directly to a Lindel class. A Lindel class was output only when the two annotation methods agreed, ensuring reliable conversion. Finally, we aggregated the frequency vectors across all samples into an (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{\text {sample}} \times 557$$\end{document}nsample×557) matrix, which was used to construct an AnnData object: the name of each Lindel class (e.g., “-1+1-1+1”) was stored along the var axis, while guide-level metadata (target sequence, pam site, organism, strand, etc.) was stored along the obs axis. Overall, this transcoding system converts a large FORECasT-format data table into a frequency matrix with a second dimension of 557.

Fused-nuclease TREX2 editor data

TREX2 high-throughput editing data from the Cas9–TREX2 fusion editor [14] were obtained from the figshare repository (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.7312067). Two files were curated: (i) a reference file recording, for each sample (guide–target pair), the identifier, PAM-site coordinate, and genomic strand; and (ii) a read-count table listing every observed indel outcome per sample, with an allele identifier in SelfTarget notation and a raw count column. We then converted these files into the format required by the inDecay training pipeline. For each entry in the reference, the 20-bp protospacer was extracted as the last 20 unambiguous bases immediately 5‘ of the cleavage site (defined as cutsite = pamsite - 3), and candidate indels were simulated using the indelgentarget tool from the SelfTarget package. Approximately 21% of TREX2 reads correspond to one-sided deletions, for which either the left breakpoint lies to the right of the cut site or the right breakpoint lies to its left. These events fall outside the event space enumerated by indelgentarget, which requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {left} < \text {cutsite} \le \text {right}$$\end{document}left<cutsite≤right, and would be discarded by a conventional inner-join against the indelgen universe. To retain them as trainable labels, we merged each sample’s indelgen event table with its one-sided deletions in an outer join. For events absent from the indelgen universe, we set MH-related features to fixed defaults: (i) MH_n_coevent was set to 1, reflecting a deletion with no flanking MH ambiguity, and (ii) MH scores were set to zero by our alignmap feature extraction function. Code for downloading and reprocessing the TREX2 data is available in the rebuttal branch of the inDecay GitHub repository.

In-house embryonic data

For mouse and livestock embryonic editing data, we input the paired wild-type file and experimental file(s) of each gRNA into the Sanger analysis tool Decodr [36] to quantify editing efficiency from Sanger sequencing traces. We obtained each deletion sequence and its ratio directly from the analysis results. Given that the software only provided an aggregated proportion for all 1-bp insertions and similarly for all 2-bp insertions, we performed further processing on the insertion results. The 1-bp insertion ratio was split by the estimated relative contribution of the inserted base. For 2-bp insertions, we used the most frequent base pair event, with the ratio set to the sum of two insertion events. Each analyzed editing result was then mapped to the corresponding identifier by our transcoding system based on the final editing sequence. A small portion of the files failed to be analyzed and were therefore discarded. Subsequently, the remaining results for the same gRNA were aggregated for single-clonal data, while for bulk data, the ratios were adjusted by multiplying them by the total number of embryos used in the experiment.

Algorithmic design of inDecay

Detection of microhomology events

To compile the data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document}xi, we first needed to label whether an event was associated with microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). In this study, we used a pairwise sequence identity matrix to identify all possible microhomologies within our samples. The process involved several steps, as follows.

We started with a target sequence and truncated it if it exceeded 79 bp. At the designated cut site, we split the sequence into two fragments at the cut site. We then constructed a matrix where the left fragments occupied the rows (sequences before the cut site) and the right fragments filled the columns (sequences after the cut site). Each element in this matrix reflected the pairwise identity between nucleotides from the row and column fragments. We assigned a value of 1 for identical nucleotides and −1 for non-identical ones. Additionally, we can assign 1 to identical nucleotides and 0 as the penalty for mismatches. This setting will encourage the convolution filter to tolerate 1-bp mismatches within the MH-track. In theory, more sophisticated scoring methods could be employed to differentiate between purines and pyrimidines.

To detect the diagonal signal patterns indicating microhomologies, we applied kernel convolution, using a 2x2 identity matrix as the convolution kernel. We selected diagonal patterns that exceeded a predefined threshold. In this study, we set the threshold to 3 as the default value in our package, meaning that only MH-tracks longer than 3 bp were considered to induce MH deletion events. Each diagonal line in the matrix allowed us to identify its corresponding deletion start site and deletion length. Multistage model

inDecay model consisted of 2 modules, a ratio prediction module \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{ratio}$$\end{document}fratio and an event frequency prediction module \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{p}$$\end{document}fp. The ratio predictor takes in the one-hot encoded sequences and predicts two scalars: the deletion ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{del}$$\end{document}rdel and the insertion ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{ins}$$\end{document}rins. A linear model was used, which was the same as Lindel [15]. The backbone of inDecay can be flexible. One can use a linear regression model if interpretability is preferred. In this study, we chose to use a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with only two hidden layers to parameterize the event predictor. The size of hidden variables was set to 128 and 64 to maintain a small model size. Additionally, we tested the backbone KAN proposed in [38], which stands for a new line of neural networks. KAN achieved similar performance and enabled new functions like symbolic regression, but it required substantially more parameters and slowed training.

Train and test split

For somatic cell line training, we split the entire dataset into train-val and test sets. The test set was first held out to benchmark different models, and the remaining samples acted as a train-val set. A train-val split was performed with different random seeds for different training repeats to sample a unique training dataset. Specifically for the retraining of FORECasT, we included only guides with over 1,000 total counts that mapped to the forward strand in the training process, which was required by [14]. To find a set of samples whose editing profiles were well-represented across all cell lines, we selected test set samples with the following criteria: 1) the total count of the sample was greater than 1000 in all five cell lines; 2) both the insertion ratio and deletion ratio were not 0, which was to remove insertion-free guides and deletion-free guides. Finally, 1133 guides passed the above criteria, and they were withheld during the training process.

For cross-cell line transfer learning, in each sample size setting, we randomly selected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {sample}}$$\end{document}Nsample samples 10 times with different seeds, separately in the mESC and iPSC datasets. And 20% of the samples (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.2 N_{\text {sample}}$$\end{document}0.2Nsample) were used for validation. The test set for each cell line was the same as the dataset used in somatic cell lines.

For few-shot learning in embryonic data, we used a leave-one-out strategy, with one gRNA for testing and the remaining data for training, 20% of which were used for validation.

Model training

All the models were developed with PyTorch version 1.12.1. Our study employed two distinct loss functions in somatic cell line training. Also, in the last layer, we applied a temperature parameter in the softmax to control the randomness of predictions by scaling the logits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}$$\end{document}pi. The radar plot was generated using normalized performance metrics to allow direct and fair comparison of different evaluation criteria across multiple prediction methods. The normalized scores were then plotted on a radial coordinate system, with each axis representing one performance metric. The radar chart facilitates simultaneous visualization of multiple metrics, highlighting relative strengths and weaknesses of each prediction model.
