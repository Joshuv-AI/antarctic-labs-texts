Developing a typology of proximity healthcare interventions and system-level characteristics in Denmark: an exploratory qualitative interview study
Authors: Kathrine Carstensen, Astrid Fyrstenborg, Camilla Palmhøj Nielsen, Morten Deleuran Terkildsen, Iben Duvald, Cecilie Torp Lohse, Lisbeth Uhrskov Sørensen, Bettina Wulff Risør
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesProximity healthcare interventions aim to provide or support health services geographically close to, or in, citizens’ own homes, involving the actors needed to deliver appropriate care across sectors. However, the field remains conceptually ambiguous, and systematic knowledge of how such interventions are organised is limited. This study therefore aims to develop an empirically grounded typology of proximity healthcare interventions in Denmark and explore system-level characteristics that characterise their development and organisation.DesignExploratory qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews with key informants responsible for cross-sectoral healthcare planning and implementation.SettingThe Danish healthcare system, a publicly funded, decentralised system undergoing structural reform to strengthen proximity healthcare and cross-sectoral coordination.Participants28 informants across the 5 Danish regions, including health cluster coordinators, regional administrators and municipal representatives.MethodsData were collected through 20 semi-structured interviews and analysed using thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke’s approach. A typology was developed inductively from the data and iteratively refined through collaborative coding and analytical discussion.ResultsThe analysis revealed a heterogeneous field of proximity healthcare interventions differing in objectives, organisation and scope. The typology comprised healthcare delivery functions, including hospital outreach, delegation of treatment to municipal staff, shared healthcare models and self-administered healthcare supported by professionals, alongside supporting functions such as coordination roles, advisory partnerships, training and patient and family education. Across categories, three system-level characteristics were identified: substantial functional and organisational variation, including hospital anchoring of many interventions; reliance on short-term project funding and locally negotiated arrangements; and greater patient and relative involvement supported by digital infrastructure. These findings characterise proximity healthcare as both adaptive and fragmented within a decentralised system.ConclusionsThis study provides a system-level overview of proximity healthcare in Denmark through a typology that brings together healthcare delivery and supporting functions. By highlighting their interdependence and the characteristics shaping intervention development, the study contributes conceptual clarity in a policy field often ambiguously defined. The typology and identified system-level characteristics offer a practical tool for policymakers and planners to navigate a complex intervention landscape and a foundation for comparative and practice-oriented research on strengthening sustainable proximity healthcare in decentralised health systems.Trial registration numberNot applicable.Keywords: Health policy, Organisation of health services, Health Services, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Implementation Science

Introduction

The global rise in ageing populations, increasing prevalence of multimorbidity and ongoing workforce shortages are placing healthcare systems under increasing pressure. These demographic and structural challenges underscore the need for sustainable, person-centred care models that can alleviate pressure on acute hospital services while ensuring efficient delivery of high-quality care.1–6 In response, policymakers have increasingly promoted healthcare delivery ‘closer to home’ through policies and service models aimed at strengthening care in or near patients’ homes and communities. Drawing on previous policy analysis, we use proximity healthcare in this study to refer to ‘health services offered geographically close to, or in, citizens’ own homes, involving the actors necessary to deliver appropriate care, including municipalities, hospitals, general practitioners, privately practising specialists, civil society and private providers’.7 This definition overlaps with, but is broader than, related concepts such as integrated care and hospital-at-home. Integrated care primarily emphasises coordination and integration across providers and sectors, while hospital-at-home refers to a specific model of hospital-level treatment delivered in the patient’s home.8 9 In contrast, proximity healthcare is used here as a broader umbrella concept that focuses on where care is delivered and how relevant actors are mobilised to provide care close to citizens’ everyday lives. As such, it may encompass integrated care arrangements, hospital-at-home models and similar approaches, without being limited to any one of these. These different approaches are often discussed separately in the literature and policy arena, yet they are united by a common ambition to bring care closer to citizens’ everyday lives. Across contexts, they seek to improve access and continuity of care, reduce reliance on inpatient services and support people in remaining in their homes and communities whenever appropriate.2 7 A growing body of evidence supports proximity healthcare as a potentially effective and valuable approach to delivering patient care. Studies show that proximity care models may contribute to reducing hospital admissions and readmissions, improving patient satisfaction and supporting better long-term outcomes.2 10–15

In Denmark, proximity healthcare has been a political priority since the early 2010s,7 16 reflecting pressures similar to those driving reforms internationally. The concept emerged in Danish policy discourse in the years following the 2007 structural reform, which consolidated and specialised hospital services and later became increasingly prominent as part of policy debates about strengthening healthcare outside hospitals.16 The term is widely used across policy and political discourse but lacks a consistent definition, spanning interpretations from a primarily municipal focus to broader cross-sectoral models involving hospitals, general practitioners (GPs) and municipalities in the coordination of coherent care pathways. Rather than referring to a clearly delimited intervention category, proximity healthcare is generally framed in policy documents as a broad reform ambition. Concrete initiatives are often described through more specific terms, such as telemedicine, local health centres, expanded municipal acute services or hospital-at-home care, while being positioned as part of the wider ambition to provide care closer to citizens’ homes and everyday lives.16 Despite these variations, a common ambition is evident: to shift healthcare delivery away from hospital-based treatment towards services provided closer to home, thereby strengthening prevention, continuity and accessibility.7 16 However, systematic knowledge of how proximity healthcare is developing in practice remains limited. Although policy documents identify a range of initiatives associated with this agenda, the overall landscape appears fragmented, with limited insight into how initiatives are organised, connected or differentiated within the wider healthcare system.16 This lack of a comprehensive overview contributes to conceptual ambiguity and makes it difficult for policymakers and planners to assess how different interventions relate to one another and where opportunities for coordination, scaling or service development may exist.

The lack of overview in the Danish context reflects broader gaps in the international literature. Researchers have called for typologies of proximity healthcare interventions to enable systematic comparisons across types, considering their aims, delivery mechanisms and organisational settings.10 17 Existing work has largely concentrated on specific sectors or models, such as hospital-at-home or integrated care for defined conditions.10 17–19 These typologies offer valuable insights but are often narrow in scope, limiting their relevance for analysing broader system-level dynamics. Within hospital-at-home care, for example, the literature often distinguishes between early supported discharge, where patients are sent home sooner with continued treatment and admission avoidance, where patients receive treatment at home instead of being admitted.20 21 Although widely applied, this distinction captures only a fraction of the broader proximity healthcare landscape. Furthermore, most existing typologies focus narrowly on direct care delivery and give little attention to supporting aspects such as coordination, advisory roles and capacity-building, which are nonetheless essential to making proximity healthcare feasible and sustainable. This underscores the need for more comprehensive, empirically grounded typologies that encompass both the diversity of proximity healthcare interventions and the supporting functions that enable their implementation. Such typologies enable a more systematic understanding of how proximity healthcare interventions differ in their organisational arrangements, degree of integration and intended purposes. Beyond providing conceptual clarity, they may support policymakers and healthcare planners in navigating an increasingly complex intervention landscape, identifying areas of overlap and fragmentation and informing decisions regarding coordination, prioritisation and future service development. A systematic, empirically grounded typology may also facilitate comparison across interventions and regions, thereby supporting more coherent planning and evaluation of proximity healthcare initiatives.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a typology of proximity healthcare interventions in the Danish healthcare system, encompassing both direct healthcare delivery and supporting functions. Additionally, the study examines system-level characteristics across interventions that shape their development and organisation. The study contributes conceptual and empirical insights into how proximity healthcare is structured at the system level, providing a foundation for policymakers and healthcare planners internationally to design strategies that promote sustainable models of proximity healthcare.

Methods

Study design and methodological rationale

The study was designed as an exploratory qualitative interview study using thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke’s approach.22 23 It aimed at identifying and investigating proximity healthcare interventions implemented within the Danish healthcare system and examining system-level characteristics that shaped their development and organisation. This design was chosen because the written documentation of such interventions in the Danish context is often limited, uneven or locally embedded, particularly for interventions that are newly developed, temporary, hybrid or organised across sectoral boundaries. Qualitative interviews were therefore considered appropriate, as they provided access to informants’ overview knowledge of intervention types, organisational arrangements, cross-sectoral collaboration practices and broader system-level conditions that were not consistently captured in formal documents. Where relevant documents were available, these were collected and used as contextual and supplementary material in the analysis. However, interviews constituted the primary data source.

Study setting

Denmark has a universal healthcare system grounded in the broader principles of the Danish welfare model, which emphasises solidarity, equality and public responsibility.24 Health services are primarily tax-financed and provided by public institutions, ensuring access based on need rather than ability to pay.25 Overall health spending per capita in Denmark is among the highest in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries, reflecting the broad coverage and extensive public funding of healthcare.26 In comparative assessments across the European Union, Denmark performs well on indicators of accessibility, quality and health outcomes and consistently reports low levels of unmet healthcare needs.26

The Danish healthcare system is decentralised, with responsibilities divided between the state, 5 administrative regions and 98 municipalities. This organisational structure supports strong coordination across levels of care and a long-standing tradition of reform-driven development aimed at improving quality, efficiency and integration.27 28 Following the nationwide structural healthcare reform in 2007, hospital services were reorganised to improve efficiency and quality through consolidation and specialisation. As a result, Denmark has allocated a comparatively large share of health expenditure to hospitals, which continue to play a central role in health service delivery.7 27 In the years following the reform, demographic pressures, increasing multimorbidity and workforce shortages have added to the need for strengthening healthcare outside hospitals. Under the policy heading of proximity healthcare, this has involved efforts to expand primary and community-based treatment, improve cross-sectoral care pathways and prevent avoidable hospitalisation through earlier intervention and health promotion.7 16

In practice, these efforts depend on coordination between actors responsible for different parts of the healthcare pathway. Regions are responsible for hospitals, prehospital services and general practice, while municipalities manage health promotion, rehabilitation and social care. These distributed responsibilities make close cross-sectoral collaboration essential, particularly for patients with complex needs. As part of a national reform initiative, 21 healthcare clusters were established in 2022 to formalise local collaboration across sectors. The clusters constitute one of several governance structures designed to strengthen coordination between hospitals, municipalities and general practice, complementing broader regional health cooperation agreements and frameworks such as health agreements and health cooperation committees.7 25

Data collection

The empirical material consisted of 20 semi-structured interviews conducted with 28 informants across the five Danish regions. Some interviews were conducted one-on-one (n=12), while others included two participants (n=8). Participants were recruited through purposive, information-rich sampling. Eligible participants were individuals with a formal role in regional or cross-sectoral healthcare governance and broad system-level insight into the planning, coordination or implementation of proximity healthcare interventions across sectors. This included, for example, responsibility for health cluster coordination, regional strategic planning or cross-sectoral development activities. The study focused on actors involved in governance, planning and implementation because the aim was to map and conceptualise proximity healthcare interventions at a system level. Consequently, frontline healthcare professionals, patients and relatives were not included, as the study sought to capture perspectives on the development and organisation of proximity healthcare rather than lived experiences or clinical practice perspectives. These criteria were considered appropriate because the study aimed to develop a typology of proximity healthcare interventions and examine system-level characteristics across regions, rather than evaluate individual services or professional practices.

Recruitment was iterative. Each region was asked to identify informants with broad overview knowledge of proximity healthcare activities relevant to the study aim. We did not use a formal sampling frame or quotas for specific professional groups, as the purpose was not to compare professions but to capture variation in governance and organisational perspectives. Role diversity was therefore sought by including participants from all five Danish regions, different organisational levels and both regional and municipal perspectives. The final sample included health cluster coordinators, strategic staff in regional administrative units and, in two cases, municipal representatives invited by the regions.

Sample adequacy was assessed in line with the concept of information power,29 taking into account the focused study aim, the specificity of the sample and the analysis of organisational patterns. During data collection, we continuously assessed whether additional organisational perspectives were needed to ensure adequate coverage of intervention types and organisational arrangements across sectors and regions. Interview participants occasionally identified additional key informants with knowledge of organisational areas not yet represented in the sample, and these individuals were subsequently invited to participate. Recruitment was concluded when the interviews provided sufficient variation across regions, organisational levels and regional/municipal perspectives to support the development of the typology and the analysis of system-level characteristics. All participants received comprehensive written information about the study and gave oral, recorded consent before the interviews. Consent could be withdrawn at any time.

All interviews were conducted between June and November 2024 by KC, BWR and AF, and followed a semi-structured interview guide30 (displayed in online supplemental file 1). In recognition of our role in co-constructing the data, we engaged in ongoing reflexive discussions throughout the study, considering how our perspectives on proximity healthcare, healthcare organisation and implementation may have influenced the framing of interview questions, interactions with participants and interpretation of emerging organisational perspectives. The interview guide was developed based on existing literature, policy documents and the interdisciplinary expertise within the research team. This interdisciplinary research team contributed expertise in qualitative methodology, healthcare organisation and implementation, health economics and governance and public health, including cross-sectoral collaboration. The interview guide was structured around key aspects of proximity healthcare delivery and organisation, including types of healthcare and treatment interventions delivered outside hospitals, supporting functions such as outreach, coordination and bridging, advisory and shared functions, collaboration structures, financial arrangements and perceived potentials and challenges. Examples of guiding questions included: ‘What types of interventions and service delivery models exist within proximity healthcare in your region/hospital/cluster?’, ‘Which actors are involved, and how are roles and responsibilities distributed?’ and ‘How is collaboration organised between the involved actors?’. In line with the semi-structured format, the guide provided a common framework to ensure consistency across interviews while allowing flexibility for participants to elaborate on region-specific and local-specific organisational arrangements, implementation experiences and issues they considered particularly important. Interviewers used follow-up questions to clarify responses, explore concrete examples and examine issues of particular relevance to the informant’s organisational role and local context.

All interviews were conducted virtually via Microsoft Teams, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and anonymised in accordance with data protection regulations. Interviews lasted between 50 and 90 min (median 65 min).

Data analysis

We used thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach.27 28 Although reflexivity remained central throughout the analytical process, we adopted a pragmatic analytical approach that combined the flexibility and interpretive orientation of reflexive thematic analysis with a structured coding framework developed collaboratively across the research team. This approach was considered appropriate because the study aimed both to remain open to empirically generated patterns while systematically comparing intervention types and organisational arrangements across a large and heterogeneous dataset to develop a coherent typology and identify system-level characteristics.

To become familiar with the data, all transcripts were read thoroughly and initial notes were taken. A subset of interviews was discussed among KC, BWR and AF to develop an initial analytical coding framework, with particular attention to variations in intervention types, organisational arrangements, actor constellations, degrees of cross-sectoral integration and conditions shaping intervention development and organisation. Two interviews were independently test-coded in NVivo V.15.31 This was not done to assess coding reliability, but to facilitate analytical dialogue regarding the interpretation, scope and boundaries of the codes. On this basis, the coding structure was refined into a codebook with clear code descriptions, which was subsequently applied to the full dataset.

The final codebook comprised both descriptive and interpretive codes and was treated as an analytical tool rather than a fixed classification scheme. Throughout the analysis, the authors documented identified patterns and discussed preliminary interpretations, refining the analytical focus iteratively. As part of this reflexive process, emerging interpretations were discussed within the research team to question preliminary assumptions and ensure that the analysis remained grounded in the empirical material rather than in pre-existing policy or organisational categories. Where the coded material suggested overlaps or ambiguities between intervention types, organisational arrangements, actor constellations or system-level characteristics, the codebook was reconsidered and adjusted to better reflect the empirical material. Data unrelated to the design, implementation or coordination of proximity healthcare interventions were not pursued further in the analysis.

The analysis proceeded in two-related steps. First, analytical categories for the typology were developed by comparing intervention types, organisational arrangements and patterns of coordination across regions and sectors. Distinct intervention types were grouped into categories based on shared organisational features and purposes, which were then organised into two overarching domains: interventions that deliver healthcare directly and interventions that facilitate collaboration and healthcare delivery across sectors. Second, we explored coded material across intervention categories to identify broader patterns that cut across organisational forms and service areas. Rather than focusing on the characteristics of specific intervention types, this stage examined how participants described the processes, conditions and contextual factors influencing the development, organisation and implementation of proximity healthcare interventions. These cross-cutting patterns were developed into themes describing system-level characteristics across the interventions. Both the typology and the themes were refined through author discussions to ensure coherence and to reflect participants’ perspectives on how proximity healthcare interventions are developed, organised and implemented.

Results

The analysis identified a diverse and evolving landscape of proximity healthcare interventions in Denmark, characterised by substantial variation in purpose, organisation and cross-sectoral integration. The findings are presented in the two domains: healthcare delivery functions and supporting functions. Each domain comprises several categories that reflect recurring patterns of organisation, responsibility and collaboration across sectors. The categories are constructed as ideal types, capturing the core features of intervention types found across the five Danish regions. Some intervention types are widespread and well-developed, while others remain marginal or experimental. While individual interventions often blend elements from multiple categories and display internal variation, the typology serves to provide a structured, system-level overview of the main characteristics that define proximity healthcare interventions in practice. In addition to the typology of healthcare delivery and supporting functions, the analysis also identified several system-level characteristics that span intervention types and characterise the overall development and organisation of proximity healthcare in Denmark. These are presented at the end of the results section.

Healthcare delivery functions

This domain encompasses clinical services provided outside hospital settings, typically in patients’ homes or community environments. The organisation of these functions is determined by the level of integration and delegation between hospital, municipal and patient actors and in some cases also prehospital actors and GPs. Categories are distinguished based on the predominant model of healthcare delivery and described in terms of their purpose, organisational set-up and patterns of collaboration.

Hospital-delivered outreach functions

In this category, hospitals generally retain primary responsibility for initiating, delivering and overseeing treatment provided in patients’ homes or local environments, except in occasional cases where prehospital teams carry out short-term stabilising interventions that do not require subsequent hospital treatment. Interventions are delivered by hospital-employed physicians and/or nurses, or multidisciplinary teams. Municipal involvement, if present, is limited and informal, such as ad hoc assistance with practical tasks, without binding agreements or shared governance.

Acute outreach functions involve rapid assessment and, where necessary, treatment in the patient’s home or community as an alternative to hospital admission. They target patients with acute treatment needs across demographics, diagnoses and types of acute conditions. The core aim is to prevent unnecessary hospitalisation by stabilising patients on-site. These interventions are embedded in hospital emergency departments or prehospital systems and use mobile acute units, often staffed by paramedics, emergency physicians or nurses specialised in acute care. Collaboration with municipal acute nurses may occur and is typically initiated on a case-by-case basis. The functions are typically activated through emergency calls, GPs or out-of-hours services.

Specialised outreach functions are designed to deliver in-home healthcare to patients with complex medical conditions that require advanced and specialised clinical knowledge not typically available within municipal healthcare services. They are typically aimed at small or particularly vulnerable patient groups, such as children requiring palliative care or individuals with severe psychiatric disorders. The interventions are anchored in specialised hospital departments and healthcare is provided by doctors, nurses or multidisciplinary teams with relevant expertise.

Assertive outreach functions are applied in relatively few cases and target individuals with severe psychiatric or social needs who struggle to maintain contact with formal healthcare services. Multidisciplinary hospital teams, often including psychiatric, somatic and social care professionals, engage with patients in their local environments, such as supported housing, shelters or temporary accommodation. These interventions are carried out in collaboration with relevant local actors, such as municipal professionals, support contacts or residential facility personnel and focus primarily on stabilisation, motivation and engagement with appropriate services rather than assuming full treatment responsibility.

Hospital-led treatment delegated to municipal professionals

This category covers interventions in which hospitals retain clinical responsibility but delegate the practical delivery of treatment to municipal healthcare professionals. They target patients who can be safely treated or complete their treatment at home, across diagnoses, demographics and types of clinical needs. Treatment is provided in the patient’s home, with hospital clinicians prescribing the treatment plan, defining procedures and remaining available for consultation. Municipal acute or home nurses deliver the treatment on a daily basis according to agreed standards of competence, responsibility and communication. Municipal acute nurses are municipal home nurses with additional training and competencies in acute care, enabling them to manage more complex tasks in patients’ homes.

Hospital at home (admission avoidance) involves that the entire admission episode is delivered in the patient’s home rather than in hospital. Patients remain under hospital responsibility, while practical treatment is primarily provided by municipal acute nurses and in some cases by home nurses. The hospital physician initiates and prescribes the treatment and remains accountable, while the municipal nurses deliver treatment on a daily basis. The aim is to avoid hospital admission altogether by enabling complex treatment in the home environment.

Early discharge involves that patients are discharged from hospital earlier than usual and complete their treatment course at home. The hospital retains clinical responsibility for the treatment plan, while municipal home nurses provide the treatment, for example, intravenous antibiotics or fluid therapy. In some cases, municipal acute nurses may assist if specialised competences are required. The purpose is to shorten inpatient stays while ensuring continuity of treatment and safe recovery at home.

Transitional monitoring focuses on follow-up, monitoring and coordinated support for patients after discharge, particularly those at risk of deterioration or with ongoing clinical needs. The hospital remains clinically responsible but may act more as a consultant, while municipal home nurses and GPs take on proactive monitoring and support. The purpose is to ensure patient safety, maintain continuity and prevent avoidable readmissions.

Taken together, these three types illustrate different ways in which hospitals can delegate home-based treatment to municipal professionals: either as a full substitute for admission, as an early completion of treatment in the home, or as structured monitoring and support in the post-discharge period. Across all types, formal agreements between hospitals and municipalities define roles, competencies and communication pathways. The overarching goal is to provide treatment in the most appropriate setting while maintaining clinical oversight and reducing fragmentation.

Shared healthcare delivery

This type comprises interventions in which treatment is co-planned and co-delivered by hospital and municipal healthcare providers through integrated teams. It is characterised by shared responsibility, joint decision-making and a flexible distribution of tasks, allowing services to adjust to individual patient needs. Rather than maintaining a clear division of labour, hospital and municipal professionals collaborate within cross-sectoral teams that manage treatment collectively. Coordination is typically supported by shared treatment plans, joint meetings, collaborative supervision and real-time dialogue across sectors. Clinical roles may shift dynamically depending on the complexity of the case, the patient’s condition and available resources. Frequent patient contact enables teams to respond promptly to changing needs and avoid unnecessary transitions between sectors.

Although clearly distinct from hospital-led or delegated types, shared treatment delivery was only rarely identified in the analysis. It appears to represent an exceptional organisational form, where integration extends beyond co-location to encompass shared goals, responsibilities and accountability mechanisms, with potential applicability across a wide range of patient groups and treatment needs.

Self-administered healthcare with professional support

This category describes types of healthcare delivery in which patients or caregivers assume primary responsibility for treatment or monitoring, supported by comprehensive training, digital tools and ongoing hospital oversight. The aim is to increase patient autonomy, reduce hospital visits and admissions and ease pressure on outpatient services.

Self-administered medical treatment involves patients or caregivers performing specific procedures, such as intravenous antibiotic therapy, subcutaneous chemotherapy or blood transfusions, at home. Hospitals provide training, equipment and remote support while retaining overall responsibility. This approach is particularly suited to clinically stable patients requiring repetitive or long-term regimes.

Home monitoring requires patients or caregivers to undertake regular health measurements and transmit data digitally to the hospital, which supplies the necessary devices and instructions. The hospital reviews incoming data and makes timely adjustments to treatment as needed. Home monitoring is widely applied for chronic conditions such as heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and diabetes, and specific follow-up needs, such as those of families with newborns.

Supporting functions

The supporting functions include interventions that enable and support collaboration across organisational and sectoral boundaries. Rather than delivering healthcare directly, these functions promote the coherence, responsiveness and sustainability of proximity healthcare. Levels of integration and formalisation structure this domain, ranging from relational functions based on individual initiative to fully institutionalised system-level arrangements.

Relational and individual-based functions

This category comprises supporting functions that are activated in response to specific patient needs or requests from municipal healthcare professionals. These functions are not fully embedded in formal structures but operate as on-demand services shaped by local routines, interpersonal relationships and professional networks. They serve as mechanisms for ensuring timely communication, knowledge exchange and coordination across sectors when standard pathways are insufficient. Their activation depends on individual initiative and situational assessment rather than predefined procedures, allowing flexible problem-solving and responsiveness.

Designated healthcare coordinator roles involve appointing a named professional, typically a nurse or social worker, who follows an individual patient through their healthcare pathway. The coordinator is responsible for planning, communication and follow-up, primarily facilitating collaboration between sectors and involved healthcare professionals. Coordination is both relational and practical, often involving direct engagement with the patient and family to ensure continuity and avoid gaps during transitions.

Ad hoc clinical consultation involves on-demand specialist advice from hospital-based professionals to municipal healthcare professionals or GPs. Typically initiated by municipal healthcare professionals or GPs, these consultations address specific concerns such as wound assessment, treatment adjustment or referral needs. They may occur via telephone, video, digital tools or in person. Although some hospitals offer structured access points such as specialist hotlines, most consultations are informal and not governed by binding agreements, relying instead on personal contacts, familiarity and mutual trust between professionals across sectors.

Formally structured system-level functions

This category includes supporting functions embedded in formalised governance structures, service agreements or collaborative arrangements. These interventions typically serve populations with complex healthcare needs and seek to ensure consistency, accountability and system-wide coordination.

Formalised cross-sectoral coordination structures consist of system-level structures that are applied in complex or multidisciplinary cases, such as child psychiatry, adult and forensic psychiatry, geriatrics or rehabilitation. These structures are embedded in formal agreements that define roles, responsibilities and communication across sectors. They are activated based on a set criteria, with clear procedural guidance ensuring that professionals understand their respective tasks and collaborate effectively in joint case management.

Formalised advisory partnerships consist of structured collaboration between hospitals, municipalities and sometimes GPs, where regular consultation and shared decision-making are anchored in formal agreements. These partnerships focus on defined patient groups or clinical topics, such as palliative care or dementia, and they are maintained through scheduled meetings, designated contact persons and shared tools. Typically, the hospital provides expertise and guidance, while municipal professionals or GPs are responsible for carrying out the actions.

Structured training and capacity-building are hospital-led initiatives aimed at enhancing the competencies of municipal healthcare professionals and GPs. These initiatives support skill development, update clinical knowledge and foster interprofessional relationships. Activities include simulation exercises, targeted teaching sessions and webinars, either as stand-alone events or embedded within broader learning programmes. Beyond technical skills, these initiatives promote shared understanding and trust, which are essential for sustaining collaborative practice.

Patient and family-centred education and guidance

While not directly related to professional and sectoral collaboration, these functions support collaboration by empowering patients and families to engage actively in managing health conditions across settings. The educational initiatives aim to improve disease understanding, self-management and participation in healthcare. They include structured patient education sessions, group-based teaching and individualised guidance for chronic illness, rehabilitation or daily health challenges. The delivery is often coordinated between hospitals and municipal services and may involve both in-person and digital components. Examples include programmes for diabetes, COPD, cardiovascular disease and parental training for neonatal or paediatric home healthcare. By improving health literacy and confidence, these functions contribute to smoother healthcare transitions and more effective home-based management.

System-level characteristics across intervention types

Across domains and categories, the analysis identified several shared system-level characteristics that shape how proximity healthcare interventions are developed and organised in Denmark. These characteristics concern the functional differentiation of interventions, variation in organisational arrangements and institutional anchoring, the project-based and adaptive character of development and the increasing involvement of patients, relatives and digital infrastructure.

A prominent characteristic is the high degree of functional differentiation, with interventions spanning direct clinical healthcare, post-discharge follow-up, coordination, advisory support, capacity-building and patient education. This diversity indicates that proximity healthcare, as it emerges in practice, extends beyond delivering healthcare in physically closer settings and involves a reconfiguration of healthcare functions and relationships across sectoral boundaries to enhance continuity, responsiveness and integration. Alongside this functional differentiation, the interventions display considerable organisational variation. Some interventions are solely managed by hospital-based actors, while others involve formal delegation or structured collaboration with municipal professionals, and in some cases GPs. The typology thus captures a continuum from hospital-led models to delegated, jointly delivered and self-administered healthcare models. However, despite a broad policy focus on decentralised and collaborative healthcare, many interventions remain institutionally anchored in hospitals, particularly when they involve high clinical risk or specialised expertise. As one informant explained in relation to a specialised hospital-based outreach function:

When patient populations are small, and there are 29 municipalities, it becomes difficult to maintain the necessary specialist competencies to a sufficient degree. In such cases, it may be safer to have an outreach function. (Strategic staff member, regional administrative unit)

This suggests that proximity healthcare is developing at the intersection between ambitions for decentralisation and the continued centrality of hospital-based authority, responsibility and specialist knowledge.

A second system-level characteristic concerns the way interventions are initiated and institutionally anchored. Proximity healthcare interventions are frequently established as temporary, project-based initiatives rather than long-term system-level programmes. While some are embedded in formal structures with stable mandates and funding, many evolve as local responses to service gaps or coordination challenges, what several informants referred to as ‘troubleshooting’ interventions. This local problem-solving logic was reflected in one informant’s description of a pragmatic approach to development and problem-solving:

We are also somewhat pragmatic. It does not have to be complicated. We do not need a large project description to find out whether there is something we can do better. Sometimes we can just do it better. (Health cluster coordinator)

These interventions often operate as pilot projects supported by short-term funding and dependent on individual initiative, rather than being rooted in established or long-term organisational structures. However, informants also described how this mode of development could make interventions vulnerable when temporary funding ended:

Cluster funds can be useful for testing things, but when the funding is gone, we cannot continue the initiative. That becomes a major barrier to further development. (Strategic staff member, regional administrative unit)

This pattern suggests that proximity healthcare in Denmark currently has an adaptive and experimental character, enabling local actors to test and develop new solutions in response to concrete service gaps or coordination challenges. However, the reliance on temporary funding and locally negotiated arrangements also makes interventions vulnerable when project resources expire or when no stable mandate for continuation has been established. Consequently, development remains uneven and weakly institutionalised, with similar challenges addressed through diverse, locally determined approaches. This contributes to variation across local and regional settings and limits the extent to which proximity healthcare emerges as a coherent system-level model. A third system-level characteristic is the increasing involvement of patients and relatives, alongside the integration of digital infrastructure. Across intervention types, patients and relatives are often engaged in delivering healthcare, administering treatment or monitoring health status, supported by professional instruction and with access to professional guidance. These forms of involvement are not merely practical additions to service delivery but reflect a shift in the distribution of healthcare responsibilities. Digital technologies, including communication platforms, remote monitoring systems and virtual consultations, are embedded to varying degrees in both healthcare delivery and supporting functions, facilitating coordination and sustaining continuity when actors are not co-located. In this sense, digital infrastructure appears to function as an enabling condition for proximity healthcare. However, informants also described how existing digital systems were not always designed to support care delivery outside hospital settings. As one informant explained in relation to patients admitted in their own homes:

There is no ‘admitted at home’ module built into the system. It works, but not without blood, sweat and tears. There are many work-arounds to make it function. (Strategic staff member, regional administrative unit)

This illustrates how digital infrastructure may enable proximity healthcare, but also how its usefulness depends on whether systems are adapted to the distributed responsibilities, cross-sectoral coordination and care delivery outside hospital settings that these interventions require.

Taken together, these characteristics suggest that proximity healthcare is not simply a matter of relocating healthcare services closer to patients. Rather, it involves a broader reconfiguration of healthcare functions, responsibilities and relationships across the healthcare system. This reconfiguration remains uneven and partly experimental, shaped by local adaptation, continued hospital anchoring and emerging forms of distributed responsibility supported by digital infrastructure.

Discussion

This study developed an empirically grounded typology of proximity healthcare interventions in Denmark and suggests that proximity healthcare is best understood as a system of interdependent healthcare delivery and supporting functions shaped by project-based development and decentralised governance. The typology spans both healthcare delivery functions, ranging from hospital-led outreach to shared care and supporting functions such as coordination, capacity-building and patient and family involvement. Considerable variation was observed in the form and scope of interventions, reflecting local capacities and institutional arrangements rather than coordinated national policy. Three shared characteristics were identified across intervention types: reliance on temporary project-based funding, significant functional and organisational diversity and increasing involvement of patients and digital technologies. Taken together, the findings provide a system-level overview of how proximity healthcare is evolving within a decentralised public health system and contribute conceptual clarity to a policy area where the term remains broadly used but ambiguously defined.

The contribution of the typology

The findings of the study contribute to the international literature on proximity healthcare by offering a system-level typology that spans organisational levels, sectors and diverse care models. Previous typologies and reviews have predominantly focused on discrete intervention types or patient groups, such as hospital at home models or integrated care tailored to specific conditions.10 11 17–20 While these approaches provide valuable insight, they often lack a systemic perspective that captures the broader diversity and contextual embedding of proximity healthcare interventions. Our study addresses this gap by mapping a heterogeneous landscape across the Danish healthcare system, offering a typology that accounts for both healthcare delivery and supporting functions. This contributes to the international field of proximity healthcare, where the concept is frequently invoked in policy and practice but remains inconsistently defined and operationalised.7 16

A distinctive contribution of the typology lies in its explicit inclusion of supporting functions, such as coordination roles, advisory partnerships, training initiatives and patient and family education, alongside clinical care delivery models. These elements are often under-represented in existing typologies and frameworks, which tend to prioritise direct service delivery while overlooking the broader system of relational and organisational work that enables integration and continuity.11 17 20 Our analysis shows that these supporting functions are not supplementary but integral to how proximity healthcare operates in practice. They represent the connective infrastructure of proximity healthcare, comprising a diverse set of interventions that enable collaboration, coordination and continuity across sectors and services. These functions help link diverse care components that are often delivered across separate sectors or providers, contributing to more coherent, responsive and sustainable healthcare pathways. By conceptualising healthcare delivery and supporting functions as part of an interdependent ecosystem, our typology extends earlier frameworks and responds to calls in the literature on proximity healthcare and broader implementation literature to identify the enabling conditions that underpin effective and sustainable service provision.11 32 33

Project-based development and decentralised governance

The shared characteristics identified in our analysis reflect how proximity healthcare in Denmark is characterised by several system-level dynamics. These include the prevalence of temporary, project-based funding arrangements, substantial functional differentiation across intervention types, organisational variation in delivery models and increasing integration of digital technologies and patient involvement. Such patterns have also been noted in international studies on health system reform and integrated care.10 12 What our analysis adds is an illustration of how these characteristics manifest and combine within a decentralised healthcare system. Many interventions are developed as pilot projects, operating under short-term funding and driven by local initiative rather than embedded, system-wide mandates. Some informants characterised them as pragmatic ‘troubleshooting’ responses to coordination gaps, a description that reflects their adaptive and often short-term nature. This helps explain why there is marked regional variation in how similar challenges are addressed. Importantly, this project-based character should not only be seen as a symptom of fragmentation. Our analysis suggests that it also functions as an adaptive development logic that allows health systems to respond quickly, test solutions and adjust to context-specific needs. In the Danish context, this adaptability unfolds within a high-performing, well-functioning healthcare system rather than as a reaction to system failure.26 It reflects a form of responsive innovation shaped by local needs and opportunities within the broader frameworks of national reforms, legislation and strategic priorities. In this sense, project-based approaches can be understood as agile alternatives to more rigid, long-term programmes, a perspective also echoed in the implementation and health policy literature on adaptability and pilot-driven development.34–36 Thus, what might appear as ‘project logic’ also represents an institutionalised mode of improvement and experimentation embedded in the governance structures of a universal welfare system. At the same time, their reliance on temporary funding and individual initiative means they often lack the stability and incentives needed for system-wide sustainability and equitable diffusion, as highlighted in discussions on intervention sustainability and upscale.34 35 37 Thus, the findings illustrate a central duality in bottom-up, project-based arrangements. They enable agility, responsiveness and experimentation, but may also undermine stability, scalability and equity across regions. Finally, the project logic also represents a mode of improvement where overall strategic planning and development of a coherent proximity healthcare system is secondary to the bottom-up development. Our analysis suggests that a strengthened focus on integrating strategic planning with the current project logic could contribute to an even higher performance.

In summary, this study contributes to the literature on proximity healthcare by offering a system-level typology that integrates both healthcare delivery and supporting functions, while highlighting how project-based development logics and decentralised governance shape the balance between adaptability and long-term stability in the organisation of proximity healthcare.

Implications for practice and policy

The typology offers policymakers and planners a framework for navigating a fragmented field of proximity healthcare. By encompassing both healthcare delivery and supporting functions, it underlines their interdependence and provides a shared language for describing how different interventions relate to one another, and for mapping how existing initiatives are distributed across delivery and support functions. In a field characterised by considerable local variation, this may help identify overlap, duplication, fragmentation and unmet needs, while supporting dialogue about how collaboration across healthcare providers can be better integrated. The typology may also support more systematic planning and evaluation of proximity healthcare by enabling comparisons across intervention types, organisational models and regions. This can inform decisions about which models should be prioritised, scaled or adapted and how more coherent service configurations can be developed across sectors and levels of care. More broadly, the typology suggests that proximity healthcare is best understood not as a collection of isolated interventions, but as a system of interdependent healthcare delivery and supporting functions.

Furthermore, the typology highlights supporting functions such as coordination, advisory partnerships, training and patient education, which are often less visible than direct care delivery but appear essential for implementation and sustainability. These findings suggest that ambitions to deliver more healthcare outside hospitals rely not only on clinical delivery models but also on these organisational arrangements that enable coordination, knowledge exchange, competence development and patient involvement across organisational boundaries. Such arrangements may take the form of dedicated coordination roles, structured advisory functions, cross-sectoral training initiatives, patient education programmes and shared communication infrastructures and should be considered part of the healthcare infrastructure needed to implement and sustain proximity healthcare. This points to the importance of giving supporting functions clearer responsibility, resources and organisational anchoring, rather than treating them as temporary or peripheral additions to direct care delivery.

A further implication concerns the need to balance local flexibility with longer-term institutional anchoring. Many proximity healthcare interventions develop as local, project-based initiatives, which may support adaptation to local needs but can also contribute to fragmentation and inequity across regions if successful models are not sustained or integrated more systematically. Stronger strategic planning across sectors and governance levels may help ensure that local initiatives are assessed, prioritised and, where relevant, sustained, scaled or adapted within the wider healthcare system. Such planning may require attention to clearer mandates, more stable funding mechanisms and forms of coordination that support system-level coherence while preserving the capacity for local adaptation and innovation.

More broadly, these implications point to the relevance of the typology beyond the Danish case. Seen in an international perspective, the Danish case illustrates how a high-performing, universal and decentralised healthcare system experiments with proximity healthcare interventions within existing welfare state structures. The typology and identified system-level characteristics may therefore inform ongoing reforms in Denmark, while also offering a framework for understanding and comparing proximity healthcare interventions in other decentralised health systems seeking to strengthen local and cross-sectoral care.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its national scope, encompassing all five Danish regions and including respondents across key levels of the system involved in the planning and implementation of proximity healthcare. The purposive sampling strategy enabled the inclusion of diverse perspectives, particularly those responsible for cross-sectoral coordination, which allowed for a comprehensive mapping of healthcare delivery and supporting functions. Another strength lies in the inductive development of the typology based on qualitative data, which ensured that the categorisation reflected empirical realities rather than predefined frameworks. The inclusion of both core healthcare delivery functions and enabling support functions further enhances the applicability of the typology by capturing the interdependencies that shape proximity healthcare in practice.

A limitation is the absence of direct input from patients, caregivers and frontline professionals, which may restrict insight into lived experiences and practical enactment. In addition, given our informant composition, the mapping primarily captured interventions in which hospitals and regional actors play a central role. Other types of proximity healthcare interventions, particularly those initiated or led by municipalities or general practitioners, may therefore not have been fully represented. Future studies should therefore include these and other relevant perspectives to capture how proximity healthcare interventions are experienced and organised in everyday practice, and to refine the typology across different patient groups, care settings and organisational contexts. The study is situated in a specific national context characterised by a decentralised public healthcare system, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other health systems. However, we have provided detailed information about the institutional context and the types of interventions included, thereby enhancing transparency and enabling readers to consider the applicability of the findings to their own systems. Finally, as proximity healthcare remains an evolving policy field, some intervention types described in this study may develop further or change character over time. Ongoing research is therefore needed to assess how proximity interventions mature and interact with broader system reforms.

Conclusion

This study provides a system-level overview of proximity healthcare in Denmark by developing an empirically grounded typology that brings together both healthcare delivery functions and the supporting functions that enable, coordinate and sustain them. The typology and identified system-level characteristics show that proximity healthcare is emerging through a diverse mix of locally and regionally designed interventions, characterised by adaptability, continued hospital anchoring, weak institutionalisation and fragmentation. By drawing attention to the enabling role of supporting functions and to system-level characteristics in how interventions are developed and organised, the study offers conceptual clarity in the policy field of proximity healthcare, a term that is often used but seldom clearly defined. The typology and system-level characteristics can serve as a practical tool for policymakers and planners to navigate a complex intervention landscape, while also providing a foundation for comparative and practice-oriented research on strengthening sustainable proximity healthcare in decentralised health systems.
