Widespread Extended [C II] Emission in High-Redshift Galaxies: Insights from the FIRE-2 Cosmological Zoom-in Simulations Facilities: ALMA, HST, TACC
Lun-Jun Liu, Guochao Sun, Andreas L. Faisst, Claude-André Faucher-Giguère, Adam Lidz

Abstract
Recent ALMA observations reveal diffuse [C II] emission (“[C II] halos”) extending to ∼ 10 \sim 10\, kpc in galaxies at 4 < z < 6 4<z<6 . These measurements provide new insights into high-redshift galactic ecosystems and processes that drive metal enrichment on inner circumgalactic scales. To better understand the nature of [C II] halos, we analyze a suite of high-redshift FIRE-2 simulations at 5 ≤ z ≤ 6 5\leq z\leq 6 in the stellar mass range of 10 9 10^{9} – 10 10.5 ​ M ⊙ 10^{10.5}\,M_{\odot} . By post-processing these simulations with three-dimensional dust radiative transfer and photoionization modeling, we generate synthetic images of [C II] and UV continuum emission, from which we extract one-dimensional surface brightness profiles. Our results reproduce both the galaxy-integrated and spatial distribution of [C II] and UV emission, capturing in particular the more extended profile of [C II] emission. Comparing the time evolution of [C II] halos with bursty star formation histories of the simulated galaxies, we find that [C II] emission becomes more spatially extended following the decline of star formation rate in recent starburst episodes. This implies a strong correlation between extended [C II] emission and bursty star formation, consistent with a key role for star formation-driven outflows in producing [C II] halos—though the kinematics of [C II]-emitting gas suggest that inflows and turbulent motions are also significant contributors. We also find a modest contribution from satellite galaxies to extended [C II] emission. Our framework can be readily applied to predict the observability of [C II] halos at higher redshifts and extended to create spatially resolved synthetic observations of other important emission lines, such as [O III] and H α \alpha .

In this framework, we treat each star particle as a single stellar population defined by its initial mass,

II.3
Modeling [C II] with
Cloudy

We predict the [C II] luminosity of individual gas particles using the photoionization modeling code
Cloudy
9
9

9

https://gitlab.nublado.org/Cloudy/Cloudy
version C17
[
39
]
. Given the gas properties and incident radiation field,
Cloudy
solves for the thermal, ionization, and chemical state of the gas and predicts the resulting emission and absorption spectra. This allows us to reliably estimate the [C II] emissivity associated with the local physical conditions of each simulated gas particle.

Assuming the spherical symmetry of individual gas particles, the [C II] luminosity of these idealized spherical ‘gas clouds’ (
L_{\mathrm{[CII],cl}}
) can be computed based on its properties from the FIRE-2 galaxies, including gas mass of the cloud (
m_{\rm gas}
), gas-phase metallicity (
Z_{\rm gas}
), temperature of the gas cloud (
T_{\rm gas}
), and hydrogen nuclei number density (
n_{\rm H}
), and modeled
J_{\rm FUV}
property from the
skirt

1
). We adopt a fixed
T_{\rm gas}
taken from the FIRE simulations for each gas cloud rather than allowing it to vary with depth into the gas cloud as in
Cloudy
, but we have verified that this causes only minor differences in
L_{\mathrm{[CII]}}

[
89
, see also]
. Technically,
Cloudy
outputs the [C II] volume emissivity (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{[CII]}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{[CII]}}(r)\,[\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-3}}]
) at various radii of a gas cloud. Given that [C II] is optically thin in typical ISM condition with density spans
1-10^{3}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}

[
54
,
106
, e.g.,]
,
L_{\mathrm{[CII],cl}}
can be calculated by integrating
\epsilon_{\mathrm{[CII]}}
over the spherical volume of this gas cloud (with radius
R_{\rm cl}
), that is,

L_{\mathrm{[CII],cl}}=\int_{0}^{R_{\mathrm{cl}}}4\pi\epsilon_{\mathrm{[CII]}}r^{2}dr,

(1)

For FIRE-2 zoomed galaxies,
R_{\rm cl}
of each gas cloud can be approximated by the Sobolev-like length scale
[
126
,
53
,
52
,
85
]
using the density gradient near this gas cloud:

R_{\mathrm{cl}}\sim\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}}{2\,\langle\nabla\rho\rangle_{\mathrm{neighbors}}},

(2)

where
\langle\nabla\rho\rangle_{\mathrm{neighbors}}
is the average density gradient near a gas cloud with respect to its nearest 32 neighbors. The density gradient between a gas cloud and its
i
-th neighbor (
\nabla\rho_{\mathrm{cl},i}
) can be calculated using known properties of simulated galaxies:

\nabla\rho_{\mathrm{cl},i}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}-\rho_{i}}{\Delta r_{\mathrm{cl},i}},

(3)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
is the mass density of the gas particle,
\rho_{i}
is the mass density of the
i
-th neighboring gas particle, and
\Delta r_{\mathrm{cl},i}
is the distance in between.

Each gas cloud has its own unique set of parameters (
J_{\rm FUV}
,
Z_{\rm gas}
,

,
T_{\rm gas}
); however, it is computationally unaffordable to run
Cloudy

L_{\mathrm{[CII],cl}}

IV.2
Bursty SFHs and the Evolution of [C II] Halos

While visual inspection of 2D [C II] morphology provides a qualitative understanding of [C II] halo evolution, quantitative analysis of radial profiles is essential for (i) determining if a snapshot exhibits an extended [C II] halo and (ii) self-consistently comparing [C II] and UV radial profiles to study how [C II] halos evolve in relation to the UV continuum and SFH.

To perform a quantitative investigation of [C II] and UV radial profiles, we smooth both
\Sigma_{\rm[CII]}
and
\Sigma_{\rm UV}

First, it produces smoothly varying radial profiles that suppress small-scale features and noise (compared to smaller beam sizes) that may complicate the classification of [C II] halos. Second, it enables direct comparison between radial profiles of [C II] emission and UV continuum by applying identical smoothing to both tracers. Third, it allows us to define extended [C II] halos using observationally and physically motivated spatial scales.

We interpret this evolution as evidence that [C II] halos are physically connected to bursty star formation and the associated stellar feedback. During a starburst, strong accretion and/or the recycling of previously expelled gas can feed the dense central ISM, making the [C II] emission relatively more concentrated. Following the starburst, stellar feedback can disrupt the central gas reservoir and transport gas in physical conditions favorable for [C II] emission to larger radii. As a result, the [C II] morphology becomes more spatially extended during the post-burst phase. In this picture, different [C II] morphologies reflect different phases of the same feedback-regulated baryon cycle, rather than tracing physically distinct galaxy populations.

IV.4
[C II] from Inflows, Outflows, and Turbulent Gas

With the strong correlation between [C II] halos and starburst episodes revealed, it is crucial to understand the kinematics of [C II]-emitting gas and investigate the role of outflows and inflows in producing extended [C II] emission. The direction of gas radial motion can be determined by the radial component of the velocity vector
v_{\rm gas,rad}\hat{r}
with respect to the center of mass. We note that it may not be sufficient to use the sign of
v_{\rm gas,rad}\hat{r}
to classify a gas particle as outflowing/inflowing
[
102
, as in, e.g.,]
, given that some non-negligible fraction of the gas particles may be consistent with the intrinsic randomness in the gas motion, instead of coherent gas flows moving outward/inward. This is particularly true for the low-mass, bursty galaxies of interest here, whose gas reservoir is primarily supported by turbulent rather than rotational motion
[
133
,
55
,
137
, e.g.,]
. Therefore, in our analysis, a gas particle is regarded as outflowing/inflowing only when its radial velocity is greater than the
1\sigma
velocity dispersion in magnitude
[
103
]
, and as consistent with random motion if its radial velocity falls within the
1\sigma
velocity dispersion. We calculate these fractions using all [C II]-emitting gas without applying a flux limit. We can then investigate the fractional contribution,
f_{\rm[CII]}\equiv L_{\rm[CII]}({\rm outflow\,\,or\,\,inflow})/L_{\rm[CII],tot}
, of gas to the [C II] emission on inner-CGM scales (
3<R<10\,
kpc) of our simulated galaxies.

Figure
11
shows the evolution of
f_{\rm[CII]}(3<R<10\,{\rm kpc})
for the same example FIRE-2 galaxies as in Figure
8
. Overall, outflows and inflows account for a modest fraction of the inner-CGM scale [C II]-emitting gas, with the sum of their fractional contributions,
f_{\rm[CII],outflow}+f_{\rm[CII],inflow}
, typically remaining below
\sim 50\%
. The remaining [C II] emission resides in gas particles without statistically significant radial motion beyond the characteristic velocity dispersion. Notably,
f_{\rm[CII],outflow}
and
f_{\rm[CII],inflow}
exhibit a clear anti-correlation: when outflow contributions increase, inflow contributions decrease, and vice versa. The temporal evolution of these components is correlated with the [C II] halo evolution and starburst episodes. During the initial phase of a starburst, the inflow contribution typically increases as gas accretes to fuel star formation. Following the starburst peak, the [C II] halo becomes more extended (
R_{\rm 90,[CII]}
and
\Delta R_{\rm 90}
increase) as the outflow contribution rises. This outflowing gas may subsequently be recycled to replenish the ISM and initiate the next starburst episode if not gravitationally unbound.

V
Discussion

V.1
[C II] Halos in Various Theoretical Studies

In this work, we have modeled the spatial distribution of [C II] emission in high-
z
galaxies using the FIRE-2 cosmological zoom-in simulations. Our post-processing framework successfully reproduces the ubiquitous extended [C II] halos observed out to the inner CGM (
\sim 10\,
kpc) in high-
z
galaxies. We have investigated the temporal evolution of [C II] halos and revealed a strong correlation between the [C II] halo size and bursty SFH. Compared to several previous theoretical investigations of extended [C II] emission, our approach introduces a few notable improvements, which we discuss below.

First, compared to semi-analytical models, which rely on parameterized prescriptions and scaling relations, cosmological simulations provide more realistic and comprehensive representations of the environments and evolution in high-
z
galaxies. For example,
[
115
,
116
]
developed a semi-analytical model focused on the outflow scenario, demonstrating that outflows can produce extended [C II] emission. However, through detailed treatment of physical conditions and processes in FIRE-2 galaxies with resolved ISM, we find that outflows are not the only cause of [C II] halos on the inner-CGM scale—inflows and turbulent gas also play important roles (Section
IV.4
).

Second, given that [C II] originates from multiple gas phases, it is essential for simulations to resolve the phase structure of [C II]-emitting gas. The FIRE-2 simulations achieve sub-pc resolution in the densest gas (Section
II.1
). As demonstrated in Section
II.3
, the FIRE-2 simulations properly capture the phase structure of [C II]-emitting gas. In contrast, most previous simulation-based studies of [C II] halos do not achieve such high resolution required to fully resolve the multi-phase origin of [C II] emission. For example,
[
102
]
use TNG50 galaxies with
\sim 100\,
pc resolution, which rely on an assumed effective equation of state to describe the multi-phase ISM. This lack of adequate resolution may cause these simulations to miss some key aspects needed to accurately model [C II] emission. Additionally, different feedback prescriptions may impact the spatial extent of [C II] emission, given our demonstrated correlation between [C II] halos and starburst episodes. Weaker feedback strength can result in more compact [C II] distributions, potentially explaining why some prior studies underpredict [C II] halo sizes
[
108
,
109
,
110
,
123
, e.g.,]
. This may also connect to the CGM contribution to total
L_{\rm[CII]}
: for example,
[
123
]
found
\sim 10\%
of [C II] emission originates from the CGM, whereas we typically find
\gtrsim 50\%
(Section
III.3
). Moreover, we study the temporal evolution of spatially resolved [C II] emission in relation to the SFH (see Section
IV.2
), an aspect not addressed in previous work.

Lastly, our analysis employs carefully defined metrics and well-motivated classifications for [C II] halos. (i) We introduce the
\Delta R_{90}
parameter, which quantifies how much more extended [C II] emission is compared to UV continuum, following the observations
[
46
,
47
, e.g.,]
. While recent simulations
[
102
,
73
]
partially reproduce extended [C II] emission, they did not compare with the UV continuum profile for complete consistency with the observations. (ii) We more strictly define outflows/inflows as gas with radial velocity greater than the velocity dispersion, which can distinguish outflows/inflows with respect to the random motion along the radial direction. This contrasts with
[
102
]
, which classified gas solely by the sign of radial velocity. The simpler approach by
[
102
]
may significantly overestimate the outflowing/inflowing gas contribution by neglecting the strong random motion of gas in high-
z
bursty galaxies. (iii) Different definitions of satellite galaxies can lead to varying estimates of their contribution to [C II] emission on the inner-CGM scale. We define satellites as subhalos with
M_{\rm subhalo}\leq 0.2M_{\rm halo}
and measure their [C II] emission within
0.3R_{\rm vir,subhalo}
from each subhalo center, yielding a modest satellite contribution with
f_{\rm[CII],sat}
typically below
\sim 20\%
(Section
IV.6
). This contrasts with, e.g.,
[
73
]
, who found that satellites are the primary driver of extended [C II] emission.

V.3
Limitations and Future Work

Here we note several limitations of this work and discuss several avenues for improvements and future studies. Regarding the FIRE simulations, the new FIRE-3 model includes updated stellar evolution tracks, star formation criteria, low-temperature cooling and chemistry, and black hole physics
[
65
]
. These changes could affect the sub-kpc (or even
\sim 10\,
pc) scale properties of high-
z
galaxies. We defer the comparison between [C II] halos in FIRE-2 and FIRE-3 simulations to future work. Our sample is limited to massive galaxies (
M_{\star}\gtrsim 10^{9}\,M_{\odot}
) at
5\leq z\leq 6
. Extending this analysis to lower-mass galaxies would provide further insights into the nature of [C II] halos and bursty star formation. We defer to future work for a detailed analysis of [C II] halos in a wide range of high-
z
galaxy populations. In addition, the FIRE-2 simulations do not include black hole physics and AGN feedback (Section
II.1
), whereas recent observations have revealed evidence of AGN activity in a few ALPINE galaxies previously classified as star-forming galaxies
[
11
,
45
, e.g.,]
. We emphasize that these simulations offer a clean, controlled sample for the majority of ALPINE-like galaxies, which are likely primarily regulated by stellar feedback, and we defer an investigation of the impact of AGN on [C II] halos to future work.

Regarding the post-processing framework, parameters such as dust properties are calibrated to the Milky Way, which could deviate from high-
z
galaxies. We note that recent work has shown that the MW calibration is still valid, such as the MW extinction curve
[
40
]
, suggesting that the change in calibration will not significantly affect our post-processing results. When modeling the dust radiative transfer using
skirt
, we mainly consider the ISRF from star particles; therefore, the metagalactic UV background
[
37
, e.g.,]
is not included in our [C II] halo analysis. The UV background is subdominant to the local ISRF on the scales of interest in our FIRE-2 galaxies, while this background might become relevant at larger distances from the galaxy center (
>10\,
kpc). When simulating observed [C II] emission in
Cloudy
, while in this framework
Cloudy
takes a single number of
J_{\rm FUV}
, we note that the photoionization can also be affected by the SED shape. Since we just find small variations of SED shape in
6-13.6\,
eV, we consider it as a minor effect that does not significantly affect the
Cloudy
post-processing results. In addition, some state-of-the-art simulations now incorporate on-the-fly RT and non-equilibrium thermochemistry to accurately predict emission line diagnostics of high-
z
galaxies
[
72
,
24
, e.g.,]
, which serve as a useful cross check and can motivate modeling assumptions involved in the post-processing analysis.

We note that while the gas kinematics is mainly regulated by accretion and stellar feedback, it can be further complicated by mergers and galaxy interactions, where tidal stripping and ram pressure may also play significant roles. In our FIRE-2 sample, we have found a few snapshots that suggest that the extended [C II] emission might also be tied to major merger events, but with only a few instances, we do not have enough information to reliably characterize this connection. A detailed investigation of how mergers and galaxy interactions shape gas kinematics and [C II] halos is beyond the scope of this paper (but see, e.g.,
Di Cesare et al. 30
for recent simulations of extended [C II] emission in merging systems).

It should be noted that the framework used in this work is by no means unique to high-
z
[C II] emission. Other essential emission lines that trace galaxy evolution, such as Ly
\alpha
, [O III]
λλ4959,5007
, H
\alpha
, and [O III]
\,88\,\mu
m, can also be simulated on FIRE-2 galaxies to perform morphological investigations. Particularly,
[
133
]
found that Ly
\alpha
can be used to trace neutral gas in the inner CGM, given high optical depth and rapid cooling, while this work demonstrates that the [C II] halo traces the enrichment processes in the inner CGM. Recent observations also showed the extended nature of [O III]
λλ4959,5007
and H
\alpha

[
41
,
33
,
139
,
143
, e.g.,]
. Moreover, the [O III]
\,88\,\mu
m line is a primary target for forthcoming ALMA observation programs that aim to probe dust-obscured star formation in
z\gtrsim 8
galaxies, such as the PHOENIX program (ALMA#2025.1.01606.L, PI: Schouws; in conjunction with UV observations by JWST GO#9425, PI: Schouws). It is compelling to incorporate these lines in the post-processing analysis in future work.

In addition, if the extended [C II] halo is prevalent among high-
z
galaxies, this might impact the [C II] LIM signals in the one-halo regime. Recently,
[
152
]
studied the response of the [C II] power spectrum to variations of [C II] halo sizes. It would be interesting to investigate the population-level constraints of [C II] halos from the one-halo [C II] LIM signal in the FIRE simulations.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we explore the origin and evolution of extended [C II] emission that prevails in high-
z
bursty galaxies. We implement a post-processing framework on a sample of
5\leq z\leq 6
galaxies (
M_{\star}\sim 10^{9.5}
–
10^{10.5}\,M_{\odot}
) from the FIRE-2 cosmological zoom-in simulations, which involves simulating the three-dimensional dust radiative transfer using
skirt
and photoionization modeling using
Cloudy
. Our post-processing analysis not only enables direct comparisons with ALMA observations, but also allows us to investigate in detail how [C II] halos evolve in response to the bursty SFHs of high-
z
galaxies.

Our main findings are summarized as follows:

•

The predictions of our post-processing framework reproduce the observed galaxy scaling relations and statistics at high redshifts, including the
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
–
M_{\mathrm{halo}}
and
L_{\mathrm{[CII]}}
–SFR relations, along with the bright-end
\Phi_{\mathrm{[CII]}}
.

•

We generate synthetic images and extract 1D radial profiles of [C II] emission and UV continuum, from which we find broad agreement between simulated profiles and observations. In particular, a significant portion (typically
\gtrsim 50\%
) of the [C II] emission comes from the inner CGM, and thus [C II] is often more spatially extended than UV.

•

Based on our classification criteria, we find that the rate of occurrence of extended [C II] halos is
\gtrsim 80\%
. This result is broadly consistent with observations and confirms the widespread emergence of [C II] halos on inner-CGM scales (
\sim 10\,
kpc).

•

By analyzing the evolution of extended [C II] halos in the context of bursty SFHs, we find a strong correlation between the [C II] halo size and starburst episodes: the [C II] halo size decreases while the central star-forming region accretes gas and boosts SFR; the [C II] halo size significantly increases after the starburst peak, a process that expels [C II]-emitting gas to the inner CGM and suppresses SFR; [C II]-emitting gas can later be recycled to restart the next starburst episode. This is further quantified by the correlation coefficient between the time evolution of [C II] halo size and the bursty SFH, from which we find that the former correlates with the latter with a characteristic time delay of
\sim 60\,
Myr, likely due to the outflowing of [C II]-emitting gas driven by a recent starburst.

•

By studying the [C II] halo size in the context of the
{\rm SFR_{100Myr}/SFR_{10Myr}}
ratio, which traces variability of the recent SFH, we find that starburst-induced outflows drive the [C II] emission to be more extended. This provides additional support for the connection between [C II] halo size and the bursty SFH. Complementary H
\alpha
observations, together with [C II] and UV continuum morphologies, would be essential for further testing this prediction.

•

Using a stringent definition of gas outflow/inflow, we find that outflows and inflows contribute modestly to the inner-CGM-scale [C II] emission. Their contribution is strongly correlated with starburst episodes. We also find that most of the inner-CGM [C II]-emitting gas remains gravitationally bound.

•

The contribution of satellite galaxies to the [C II] halo is modest, with the fractional contribution typically remaining below
\sim 20\%
.

•

We predict that it is possible to measure inner-CGM-scale [C II] halos at
z\sim 7
with ALMA. Measuring the [C II] halo of a single massive star-forming galaxy at
z\sim 7
may require
\sim 5
hours of on-source integration time on ALMA.

In summary, this study reveals the correlation between the extended [C II] halo and bursty SFH in high-
z
galaxies, and highlights the complex nature of [C II] halos. It opens an avenue for using the spatial information of line emission to probe star formation activity and the phase of starburst episodes in early galaxies. It is compelling to perform detailed investigations of the impact of gas kinematics on [C II] halos and observations. Future observations will be able to measure [C II] halos in a wider mass and redshift range of high-
z
objects, which can help us better understand the interplay of star formation, the ISM, and the CGM. Moreover, the post-processing framework presented is by no means limited to modeling [C II]. It can be applied to other important emission line diagnostics, such as H
\alpha
and [O III], which can potentially offer further insight into high-
z
galactic ecosystems.
