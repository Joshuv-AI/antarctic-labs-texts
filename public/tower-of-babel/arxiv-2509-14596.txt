Efficient Importance Sampling for Wrong Exit Probabilities over Combinatorially Many Rare Regions
Yanglei Song, Georgios Fellouris

Abstract
We consider importance sampling for estimating the probability that a light-tailed d d -dimensional random walk exits through one of many disjoint rare-event regions before reaching an anticipated target. This problem arises in sequential multiple hypothesis testing, where the number of such regions may grow combinatorially and in some cases exponentially with the dimension. While mixtures over all associated exponential tilts are asymptotically efficient, they become computationally infeasible even for moderate values of d d . We develop a method for constructing asymptotically efficient mixtures with substantially fewer components by combining optimal tilts for a small number of regions with additional proposals that control variance across a large collection of regions. The approach is applied to the estimation of three probabilities that arise in sequential multiple testing, including a multidimensional extension of Siegmund’s classical exit problem, and is supported by both theoretical analysis and numerical experiments.

1
Introduction

Motivated by the problem of estimating error probabilities for sequential probability ratio tests (SPRT)
(
Wald 1947
)
,
Siegmund 1976
studied the use of Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate the probability that a one-dimensional, light-tailed random walk with negative drift exits a bounded interval, containing zero in its interior, through the upper boundary. An importance sampling estimator was proposed, and it was shown that its relative error remains bounded as the probability of interest tends to zero; see Section
1.2
for further discussion.

When testing multiple pairs of simple hypotheses, a natural generalization of Wald’s SPRT is to monitor each log-likelihood ratio statistic until all of them are simultaneously large in absolute value, and then to decide for each pair of hypotheses based on the sign of the corresponding test statistic
(
De and Baron 2012a
;
Song and
Fellouris 2017
)
. For this procedure, it is desirable to estimate the probability of at least one wrong decision. This consideration motivates an extension of Siegmund’s problem to the multidimensional setting.

To be specific, let
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
d
-dimensional random vectors defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Consider the associated random walk

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
for
n\geq 1
with
S_{0}=0
, and define the stopping time

T=\inf\left\{n\geq 0:\;S_{n,k}>bu\;\;\text{or}\;\;S_{n,k}<-b\ell,\;\;\text{for all }k\in[d]\right\},

(1)

where
\ell,u>0
,
b>0
,
S_{n}=(S_{n,1},\ldots,S_{n,d})^{\top}
, and
[d]:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
. If every coordinate of
X_{1}
has negative expectation, then with high probability all coordinates of
S_{T}
will be negative. We refer to the problem of estimating the probability that at least one coordinate of the random walk at time
T
is positive as the
multidimensional Siegmund problem
.

In this work, we consider a general formulation that encompasses the multidimensional Siegmund problem, as well as the estimation of a broad class of rare-event probabilities arising in the sequential multiple testing literature under various information structures and error metrics; see, e.g.,
De and Baron 2012a
;
De and Baron 2012b
;
Song and
Fellouris 2017
;
Song and Fellouris 2019
;
He and Bartroff 2021
. Although motivated by statistical applications, the proposed formulation is also relevant in queuing and insurance contexts; see, e.g.,
Asmussen 2003
;
Glasserman 2004
;
Hult and Lindskog 2006
.

1.1
Problem description

For any Borel subset
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b>0
, define the hitting time of the dilated set
bW
by the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
as

\displaystyle\tau_{b}(W):=\inf\{n\geq 0:S_{n}\in bW\},\quad\text{where}\quad bW:=\{bx:x\in W\}.

(2)

Fix disjoint open sets
W^{0},W^{1},\ldots,W^{J}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set

\displaystyle\tau_{b}^{j}:=\tau_{b}(W^{j}),\quad j=0,1,\ldots,J,\qquad\tau_{b}^{*}:=\min_{j\in[J]}\tau_{b}^{j}.

(3)

We are interested in estimating the probability that the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
hits
bW^{j}
for some
j\in[J]
before it hits
bW^{0}
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
, under the following assumptions: (i)
W^{1},\ldots,W^{J}
are convex; (ii) the random walk is light-tailed; and (iii) the drift
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]
is directed away from
W^{j}
for every
j\in[J]
. Note that
W^{0}
need not be convex. In fact, it may be empty, in which case the quantity of interest reduces to the
hitting probability

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty)
, namely the probability that the random walk eventually hits
bW^{j}
for some
j\in[J]
. In general, however,
W^{0}
will be nonempty and, although this is not required for our theoretical results, in all applications we consider the drift
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
points toward
W^{0}
. In view of this, we refer to
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
as the
wrong exit probability
.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
decays exponentially fast as
b
increases, plain Monte Carlo estimation becomes impractical for large values of
b
. It is therefore natural to appeal to importance sampling (I.S.) techniques. However, as we will discuss, existing efficient I.S. methods become computationally prohibitive when the number,
J
, of rare-event regions is very large (e.g., exponential in
d
), as it is typical in the motivating statistical applications.

Consider, for example, the multidimensional Siegmund problem. For each
A\subset[d]
, define

\displaystyle W^{A}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{k}>u\;\;\text{for}\;\;k\in A,\;\;x_{k^{\prime}}<-\ell\;\;\text{for}\;\;k^{\prime}\notin A\},

(4)

identify the empty set
\emptyset
with
0
, and index
\{A\subset[d]:A\neq\emptyset\}
by
[J]
with
J:=2^{d}-1
. Then the event that at least one coordinate of the random walk is positive at time
T
, as defined in (
1
), coincides with exiting through some
bW^{A}
for
A\neq\emptyset
before hitting
bW^{\emptyset}
, that is,

\{S_{T,k}>bu\text{ for some }k\in[d]\}\;\;=\;\;\{\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{\emptyset}\}.

See Figure
1
for an illustration of the exit regions when
d=2
. In this context,
J=2^{d}-1
grows exponentially with
d
and is already enormous for moderate dimensions (e.g.,
J>10^{12}
when
d=40
). Similar combinatorial complexity arises in events associated with other sequential multiple testing rules; see Sections
5
and
6
.

Thus, our main goal is the design of an efficient I.S. approach for the estimation of
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
,
which remains feasible even when
J
is very large
. Before describing the main contributions of this work, we first review the relevant literature.

Fig 1:
Exit regions of the multidimensional Siegmund problem with
d=2
. For general
d
, the number of disjoint regions is
2^{d}
, which grows exponentially with
d
.

1.2
Literature review

A wide range of variance reduction techniques have been developed for rare-event simulation; see
Bucklew 2004
;
Glasserman 2004
;
Asmussen and
Glynn 2007
for comprehensive textbook treatments, and
Asmussen and
Rubinstein 1995
;
Juneja and Shahabuddin 2006
;
Blanchet and Lam 2012
for survey articles. This work focuses on importance sampling (I.S.), a widely used method that simulates under alternative probability distributions and reweights samples using likelihood ratios to produce unbiased estimators; see Section
2
for a formal description.

Since our setting involves rare events associated with light-tailed random walks, we restrict our literature review to this class of problems. Importantly, I.S. techniques differ substantially between light-tailed and heavy-tailed settings; for the latter, see
Blanchet and
Glynn 2008
;
Blanchet and Liu 2008
;
Blanchet and
Liu 2010
. Moreover, although our focus is on random walks, we should also mention several related works that are developed in the setting of more general Markov processes; see, e.g.,
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
;
Ney and Nummelin 1987b
;
Sadowsky and
Bucklew 1990
;
Bucklew, Ney and
Sadowsky 1990
;
Lehtonen and
Nyrhinen 1992
;
Glasserman and
Kou 1995
;
Sadowsky 1996
;
Collamore 2002
;
Blanchet, Glynn and
Liu 2007
.

For light-tailed random walks, a powerful tool for designing I.S. proposals is
exponential tilting
. Specifically, let
\mu
denote the distribution of
X_{1}
, and define the cumulant generating function
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by
\Lambda(\theta):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\theta\cdot X_{1}}]
, where
\theta\cdot X_{1}
denotes the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Lambda(z)<\infty\}
, define the tilted measure
\mu_{\theta}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via the Radon–Nikodym derivative

\frac{d\mu_{\theta}}{d\mu}(x)=\exp\left(\theta\cdot x-\Lambda(\theta)\right),\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
denote the probability measure under which
X_{n},n\geq 1
are i.i.d. with law
\mu_{\theta}
.

As discussed earlier, in the one-dimensional setting (
d=1
)
Siegmund 1976
considered the problem of estimating
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{T}\geq bu)
, where
T
is the first exit time from
b[-\ell,u]
, i.e., as defined in (
1
) with
d=1
, where
\ell,u>0
are fixed and
b>0
is an index parameter. Assume that
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]<0
,
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}>0)>0
, and that the interior of
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
contains zero. Then there exists a unique
z>0
such that
\Lambda(z)=0
. If
X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, are generated under
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}
, the estimator
\exp(-zS_{T})\,\mathbbm{1}\{S_{T}>bu\}
is unbiased, and as shown in
Siegmund 1976
, its relative error—defined as the ratio of its standard deviation to the probability of interest—is uniformly bounded over
b>0
. Moreover, under mild conditions,
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}
is the unique I.S. proposal with this property in the family of exponential tiltings
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}:\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)\}
.

Collamore 1996a
;
Collamore 2002
considered the estimation of the hitting probability

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)
for a general convex open set
W
and a general light-tailed
d
-dimensional random walk, where
\tau_{b}(W)
is defined as in (
2
). Specifically,
Collamore 1996a
showed, under mild conditions, that this probability decays exponentially as
b\to\infty
, with exponent given by a rate function
I:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
(see Theorem
2.1
for a precise statement):

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}b^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)=-\inf_{x\in W}I(x).

(5)

Furthermore,
Collamore 2002
established the existence of a unique point
v\in\partial W
such that
I(v)=\inf_{x\in W}I(x)
. For this
v
, there exists a unique
\beta^{W}\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
satisfying
\Lambda(\beta^{W})=0
and
\nabla\Lambda(\beta^{W})=v
, and the tilted measure
\mathbb{P}_{\beta^{W}}
yields an
asymptotically efficient
(A.E.) I.S. proposal for estimating the hitting probability. Specifically, under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta^{W}}
, the estimator
\exp(-\beta^{W}\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)})\,\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}
is unbiased, and its second moment decays at the optimal exponential rate; see Definition
8
for the definition of asymptotic efficiency. Although this criterion is weaker than bounded relative error, it often provides sufficient accuracy in practice
(
Asmussen and
Glynn 2007
)
.

As seen in the previous two examples, large deviations theory
(
Dembo and Zeitouni 2009
;
Borovkov and
Mogulskii 2012
)
often guides the construction of efficient I.S. proposals. When the target set
W
is more complex than a single convex set, as shown in
Sadowsky and
Bucklew 1990
;
Collamore 2002
, a mixture of measures
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
can be effective, where
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
is finite. Specifically,
Collamore 2002
also considered the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty)
, where
\tau_{b}^{*}
is defined in (
3
), i.e., the probability that the random walk ever hits
bW^{j}
for some
j\in[J]
. Assuming that the sets
W^{1},\ldots,W^{J}
are disjoint, convex, and open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that the drift of the random walk,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
, is directed away from all of them, it was shown that a mixture of
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta^{j}}:j\in[J]\}
is asymptotically efficient for estimating
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty)
, where
\beta^{j}
is the exponential tilt corresponding to the set
W^{j}
, as discussed above. However, when
J
is very large, this mixture becomes computationally infeasible. Thus, even the efficient simulation of the
hitting
probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty)
remains an open problem in such regimes.

In a conference paper
(
Song and
Fellouris 2016
)
, we studied the simulation of two wrong exit probabilities, which are special cases of the setups considered in Sections
4
and
5
. However, that work requires
the coordinates of

X_{1}
to be log-likelihood ratios, independent, and “symmetric”; see Remark
4.7
and Example
5
. Moreover, it does not apply to probabilities such as the one in Section
6
.

Finally, state-dependent I.S. schemes have also been developed for light-tailed systems; see
Dupuis and Wang 2007
;
Dupuis, Sezer and
Wang 2007
. However, their applicability to the large
J
setting remains unclear.

1.3
Contributions and organization

We now outline our main contributions.

(i).
We characterize the exponential decay rate as
b\to\infty
of the wrong exit probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
. As this is bounded above by the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty\right)
, whose exponential decay rate is established in
Collamore 1996a
, our contribution lies in proving a matching lower bound, which we obtain through a pathwise large deviations analysis.

(ii).
We show that the mixture of the
J
tilted measures
\mathbb{P}_{\beta^{1}},\ldots,\mathbb{P}_{\beta^{J}}
, which is asymptotically efficient (A.E.) for the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty\right)
, is also A.E. for the wrong exit probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
under mild conditions on the set
W^{0}
. However, this mixture becomes computationally intractable when
J
is large. Accordingly, we focus on the design of feasible A.E. mixtures that require substantially fewer than
J
components.

(iii).
In view of (
5
),

\mathcal{C}_{*}:=\{j\in[J]:r_{j}=r_{*}\}
is the collection of regions most likely to be hit in the event of a wrong exit, where
r_{j}:=\inf_{x\in W^{j}}I(x)
for
j\in[J]
and
r_{*}:=\min_{j\in[J]}r_{j}
. We show that for a mixture of tilted measures
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
to be A.E., the parameter set
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
must include the tilts associated with
all
regions in
\mathcal{C}_{*}
, i.e.,
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}_{*}\}
. Consequently, if
\mathcal{C}_{*}
is too large (e.g., exponential in
d
), then no computationally feasible A.E. mixture of this form can exist. A concrete example is provided in Subsection
4.5
.

(iv).
Since computing all
\{r_{j}\}
is infeasible when
J
is large, the set
\mathcal{C}_{*}
and the value
r_{*}
are generally unknown. However, guided by problem-specific intuition, we can often identify a candidate subset
\mathcal{C}\subset[J]
that is likely to contain
\mathcal{C}_{*}
. Based on this insight, we propose a mixture of exponential tilts
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
, where
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
contains the set
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}

along with additional components designed to control the variance contributed by regions
W^{j}
for
j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
. The key idea is that, instead of assigning a separate tilt to each such region—which would be computationally infeasible—we identify a small set of tilts, not necessarily in
\{\beta^{j}:j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}\}
, such that each tilt simultaneously accounts for many regions. Most importantly, we provide a sufficient condition for the asymptotic efficiency of this mixture, which in turn guides the selection of the additional components. See Subsection
3.6
.

(v).
We illustrate the proposed strategy through the estimation of three wrong exit probabilities motivated by problems in sequential multiple testing; see Sections
4
–
6
. In each case, the number of regions
J
grows exponentially or combinatorially with the dimension
d
, yet we construct mixtures whose number of components grows only polynomially in
d
with a substantially lower order. These mixtures are computationally feasible, and we provide numerical results that demonstrate their practical performance.

Organization.
Section
2
reviews the basics of importance sampling and relevant results from
Collamore 1996a
;
Collamore 2002
. In Section
3
, we develop a general strategy for constructing A.E. proposals for wrong exit probabilities and present necessary conditions for asymptotic efficiency. Sections
4
–
6
present three applications of this strategy. Section
7
concludes the paper. Proofs and technical details are deferred to the appendices.

2
Importance Sampling for Hitting Probabilities

In this section, we introduce notation and review key concepts related to importance sampling. We also discuss the simulation of hitting probabilities for convex sets, following the foundational work of
Collamore 1996a
;
Collamore 1996b
;
Collamore 2002
. Our approach to simulating wrong exit probabilities builds upon these developments.

2.1
Basics of importance sampling

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n},n\geq 0
be a filtration of the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
.
Let
\mathbb{Q}
be a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that
{\mathbb{P}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
. We denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}
the expectation under
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
T
be an
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
-stopping time and denote by
\mathcal{F}_{T}
the associated
\sigma
-algebra. Define

{Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}}{d\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\mathbbm{1}\{T=n\},

where
\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}}{d\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{F}_{n})
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
{\mathbb{P}}
against
\mathbb{Q}
on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
for
n\geq 0
and
\mathbbm{1}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function of an event. For each
b>0
, let
A_{b}\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
, that is,
A_{b}\cap\{T=n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
for
n\geq 0
. By a change of measure, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[{Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{A_{b}\}\right].

That is, under
\mathbb{Q}
, the random variable
{Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{A_{b}\}
is an unbiased estimator of
{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right)
. We refer to
\mathbb{Q}
as a proposal measure. By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left({Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{A_{b}\}\right]\geq\left({\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right)\right)^{2},

which motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Assume
{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right)>0
for
b>0
.
We say that a proposal measure
\mathbb{Q}
is
asymptotically efficient (A.E.)
for estimating
{\mathbb{P}}(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\})
if

\displaystyle\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left({Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left\{A_{b}\right\}\right]\;\sim\;2\log{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right),

where
\sim
denotes that the ratio of the two sides converges to
1
as
b\to\infty
.

Thus, if
\mathbb{Q}
is an asymptotically efficient proposal, then as
b\to\infty
, the variance under
\mathbb{Q}
of the unbiased estimator

{Z}_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{A_{b}\right\}
is as small as possible on the logarithmic scale. For further discussion, see, e.g.,
Asmussen and
Glynn 2007
;
Glasserman and Wang 1997
.

2.2
Exponential tilting

In this work we focus on the setting where
X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, are i.i.d.
d
-dimensional random vectors with common law
\mu
, and define the associated random walk by
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
for
n\geq 0
, with
S_{0}=0
. Let
\Lambda
denote the cumulant generating function of
X_{1}
, and let
\Lambda^{*}
denote its Legendre–Fenchel conjugate, defined respectively by

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta):=\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\theta\cdot X_{1}\right)\right]=\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(\theta\cdot x)\,d\mu(x),\quad\text{for }\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\theta\cdot x-\Lambda(\theta)\right\},\quad\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We consider a special family of importance sampling proposals based on exponential tilting.
For each
n\geq 1
let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
the trivial
\sigma
-algebra.
For each
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
let
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
be a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\log\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}(\mathcal{F}_{n})=\theta\cdot S_{n}-n\Lambda(\theta),\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N},

(6)

and denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}
the expectation under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
. Under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
, the sequence
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
is i.i.d. with common distribution
\mu_{\theta}
, whose Radon–Nikodym derivative relative to
\mu
is given by

\frac{d\mu_{\theta}}{d\mu}(x)=\exp\left(\theta\cdot x-\Lambda(\theta)\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(7)

For any
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, the measures
{\mathbb{P}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
are mutually absolutely continuous on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-1}(T)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{b}\}
is an unbiased estimator of
{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{b}\cap\{T<\infty\}\right)
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
, where we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\theta}(T):=\exp\left(\theta\cdot S_{T}-T\Lambda(\theta)\right)\,\mathbbm{1}\{T<\infty\}.

(8)

2.3
Hitting probabilities

For a subset
W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
b>0
, recall from (
2
) that
\tau_{b}(W)
denotes the hitting time of the set
bW
by the random walk
\{S_{n}:n\geq 0\}
. Assuming that
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex and open, and that the random walk
\{S_{n}:n\geq 0\}
is light-tailed and directed away from
W
,

Collamore 1996a
characterizes the asymptotic exponential decay rate of the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\right)
as
b\to\infty
. To be specific, define
I:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
as the support function of the set
\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
, i.e., for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle I(x):=\sup\left\{\theta\cdot x:\;\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda\right\}=\sup\left\{\theta\cdot x:\;\log{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(\theta\cdot X_{1})]\leq 0\right\}.

(9)

Further, for each subset
W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define the minimal rate as follows:

\displaystyle r_{W}:=\inf_{x\in W}I(x).

(10)

Theorem 2.1
(From
Collamore 1996a
).

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex open set.
Assume that (1) there exists

\delta>0
such that
W
does not intersect
\operatorname{cone}(B({\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}],\delta))
,
and (2)
0
belongs to
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda))
and

W
intersects the relative interior of
cone(dom(\Lambda^{*}))
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)=-r_{W}.

Condition (1) is essentially geometric, as it requires that the drift of the random walk,
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]
, does not point toward
W
. This implies that
\mathbf{0}\notin W
and that the random walk is unlikely to hit the set
bW
when
b
is large. Condition (2) further guarantees both the positivity and the exponential decay of the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)
as
b\to\infty
. Indeed, if
\Lambda
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin
\mathbf{0}
, then by Cramér’s theorem
(
Dembo and Zeitouni 2009
, Corollary 6.1.6)
the sequence
\{S_{n}/n:n\geq 1\}
satisfies a large deviations principle with convex, lower semicontinuous rate function
\Lambda^{*}
. Consequently, the domain of
\Lambda^{*}
may be interpreted as the set of points that
S_{n}/n
can approach for large
n
, and the cone of rays through
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{*})
as the set of points that the random walk
\{S_{n}:n\geq 0\}
can potentially reach. Thus, the assumption that
W
intersects the relative interior of this set ensures that the random walk can hit
bW
for sufficiently large
b>0
with nonzero probability, but that the probability of such an event decays exponentially as
b
increases.

Finally, we note that condition (2) depends on both the random walk and the set
W
. In Appendix
A.1
(see Remark
A.2
), we show that it is satisfied for
any
convex open set
W
provided the random walk satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)\text{ is open \; and \; }\{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0\}\text{ is bounded.}

(11)

In Appendix
A.1
, we also provide a
self-contained
, elementary proof of a weaker version of Theorem
2.1
, which requires assumption (
11
) instead of (ii)
(see Theorem
A.1
).

2.4
Importance sampling for hitting probabilities

We next discuss the results in
Collamore 2002
regarding the estimation via importance sampling of the hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)
for large values of
b>0
, when
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a convex open set.
To present them, for each
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define the following function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\theta}(x):=\sup\left\{\alpha\cdot x:\;\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;\;\Lambda(\theta)+\Lambda(\alpha-\theta)\leq 0\right\},\;\text{ for }\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(12)

where the supremum of an empty set is
-\infty
. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle v(W;\theta):=\inf_{x\in\overline{W}}\mathcal{V}_{\theta}(x).

(13)

Moreover, we recall the definition of
r_{W}
in (
10
).

Theorem 2.2
(From
Collamore 2002
).

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex open set.
Assume that (1) there exists

\delta>0
such that
W
does not intersect
\operatorname{cone}(B({\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}],\delta))
, and (2)

\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
is open and

W
intersects the interior of
\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{*}))
. Then the following results hold.

(i)

There exists a unique
\beta^{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\Lambda(\beta^{W})=0
,

W\subset\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\;\beta^{W}\cdot x\geq r_{W}\},\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{L}_{r_{W}}I\subset\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\;\beta^{W}\cdot x\leq r_{W}\}.

(ii)

\beta^{W}
is the unique element in
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
such that

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta^{W}}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\beta^{W}}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]=-2r_{W}=2\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty).

Thus,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta^{W}}
is the unique asymptotically efficient proposal for estimating
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\right)
among all exponential tilting measures
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}:\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)\}
.

(iii)

For
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, if
v(W;\theta)>0
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]\leq-v(W;\theta).

Condition (1) in Theorem
2.2
is identical to that in Theorem
2.1
, whereas condition (2) is slightly stronger, which holds for any
W
provided the random walk satisfies (
11
).

Part (i) is established in
Collamore 2002
, with further details provided in Appendix
A.2
. The main idea of the proof is as follows. First, one shows that no point
x\in W
attains the value
r_{W}=\inf_{x\in W}I(x)
, which implies that
W
and the level set
\mathcal{L}_{r_{W}}I
are disjoint convex sets. By the separation theorem, these sets can be separated by a hyperplane. Lemma 3.2 of
Collamore 1996a
then identifies the normal vector and offset of this separating hyperplane using tools from convex analysis.

Given part (i), part (ii) (without the uniqueness result) follows immediately. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\beta^{W}}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\beta^{W}}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\beta^{W}}\left[\exp\left(-2\,\beta^{W}\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\right)\,\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}\right]\leq\exp(-2br_{W}),

where the first equality holds because
\Lambda(\beta^{W})=0
, and the second by the fact that on the event
\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}
we have
S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\in bW
, and hence
\beta^{W}\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\geq b\,r_{W}
by part (i).

Part (iii), which is critical for our purposes in this work, is independent of parts (i) and (ii). It establishes an asymptotic upper bound on the variance of the importance sampling estimator induced by an arbitrary exponential tilting.
In Appendix
A.2
,
we provide an
elementary
proof for this result (see Theorem
A.2
) under the stronger assumption (
11
).
In fact, in the proof of Theorem
A.2
, under the sole condition
v(W;\theta)>0
, we establish the following weaker version of part (iii):

\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]\leq-\sup_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\theta)+\Lambda(\alpha-\theta)\leq 0}\;\inf_{x\in\overline{W}}(\alpha\cdot x),

which however suffices for all applications in Section
4
,
5
and
6
.

Remark 2.1
.

Collamore 2002
investigates the simulation of hitting probabilities for Markov additive processes, which include random walks as a special case. Moreover, a corresponding lower bound to part (iii) of Theorem
2.2
is also established.

3
Importance Sampling for Wrong Exit Probabilities

In this section we fix open, convex sets
W^{1},\ldots,W^{J}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, together with an open (but not necessarily convex) set
W^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and focus on the estimation of the
wrong exit probability

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})
. Recall from (
3
) that
\tau_{b}^{j}
denotes the first hitting time of the set
bW^{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,J
, and that
\tau_{b}^{*}
denotes the first hitting time of the union
bW^{1}\cup\cdots\cup bW^{J}
.

We assume that the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
is directed away from the sets
W^{j}
,
j\in[J]
, in the sense that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{there exists }\;\;\delta>0\;\;\text{such that}\;\;\operatorname{cone}(B({\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}],\delta))\cap W^{j}=\emptyset\;\;\text{for every }j\in[J].\end{split}

(C1)

We do not assume that the random walk is directed toward
W^{0}
, although this holds in all applications we consider.
Furthermore, we assume the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)\text{ is an open set};\\
&\operatorname{int}\left(\operatorname{cone}\left(\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{*})\right)\right)\cap W^{j}\neq\emptyset,\quad\text{for every }j\in[J].\end{split}

(C2)

Conditions (
C1
) and (
C2
) impose the assumptions in Theorem
2.2
to each
W^{j}
,
j\in[J]
. In particular, part (i) of Theorem
2.2
motivates the following definition. Here, and in what follows, we recall the definition of
I(\cdot)
in (
9
), and we set

\displaystyle r_{j}:=r_{W^{j}}=\inf_{x\in W^{j}}I(x),\quad j\in[J].

(14)

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose conditions (
C1
) and (
C2
) hold. Then, for each
j\in[J]
there exists a unique vector
\beta^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(\beta^{j})=0
and

\displaystyle W^{j}\subset\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\beta^{j}\cdot v\geq r_{j}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{r_{j}}I\subset\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\beta^{j}\cdot v\leq r_{j}\}.

We refer to
\beta^{j}
as the optimal (exponential) tilting associated with
W^{j}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The computation of
\beta^{j}
and
r_{j}
is discussed in Subsection
3.7
.

Finally, we assume that

W^{0},W^{1},\ldots,W^{J}

lie within disjoint cones and are separated from the origin. Specifically, we assume

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{cone}(W^{j})\cap\operatorname{cone}(W^{j^{\prime}})=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }0\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq J;\\
&\text{there exists }\;\;\delta>0\;\;\text{such that}\;\;B(\mathbf{0},\delta)\cap W^{j}=\emptyset\quad\text{for each }j\in\{0\}\cup[J].\end{split}

(C3)

3.1
Characterization of exponential decay rates of wrong exit probabilities

For each
b>0
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})={\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty)-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{0}<\tau_{b}^{*}<\infty\right).

Thus, by applying Theorem
2.1
to each hitting probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{j}<\infty)
for
j\in[J]
, we immediately obtain an upper bound on the exponential decay rate of the wrong exit probability as
b\to\infty
. In the next theorem, we establish a
matching
lower bound, thereby characterizing its exponential decay rate.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that conditions (
C1
), (
C2
), and (
C3
) hold. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0},\;\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j})=-r_{j},\quad\text{ for each }j\in[J];

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})=-r_{*},\;\;\text{ where }r_{*}:=\min_{j\in[J]}r_{j}.

Moreover, we have
0<r_{j}<\infty
for each
j\in[J]
.

See Appendix
B.2
.
∎

When
W^{0}=\emptyset
, so that
\tau_{b}^{0}=\infty
, Theorem
3.1
follows directly from
Collamore 1996a
(see Theorem
2.1
above). In the general case, to derive a lower bound on the probability that the random walk hits
bW^{j}
before entering any other region,

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0},\;\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j})
,
for some
j\in[J]
, we apply
pathwise
large deviations results from
Borovkov and
Mogulskii 2012
.

3.2
Importance sampling for the probability of wrong exit from a specific region

Fix
j\in[J]
. For any
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}^{j}(\theta):=\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-1}(\tau_{b}^{*})\,\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0},\;\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j}\right\},

where we recall the definitions of the tilted measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
in (
6
) and the likelihood ratio
\mathbb{L}_{\theta}(\cdot)
in (
8
). By a change of measure, under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
it is an unbiased estimator of the probability that the random walk hits
bW^{j}
before any
bW^{i}
,
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,J\}\setminus\{j\}
, that is,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0},\;\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}^{j}(\theta)\right].

The following lemma, an immediate consequence of Theorems
3.1
and
2.2
, shows that
the exponential tilting
\beta^{j}
associated with
W^{j}
in Definition
3.1
is the choice of
\theta
that leads to an A.E. proposal for estimating the above probability. Recall the definition of
r_{j}
in (
14
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume conditions (
C1
), (
C2
) and (
C3
) hold. For each
j\in[J]
, we have

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta^{j}}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}^{j}(\beta^{j})\right)^{2}\right]=-2r_{j}.

Further, for each
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, if
v(W^{j};\theta)>0
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}^{j}(\theta)\right)^{2}\right]\leq v(W^{j};\theta):=v_{j}(\theta),

(15)

where we recall the definition of
v(\cdot;\cdot)
in (
13
).

See Appendix
C.1
.
∎

3.3
Importance sampling for the wrong exit probability

For the estimation of the wrong exit probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)
, we use the mixture measure

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}:=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}

as an importance sampling proposal, where
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
is a
finite
subset and
|\Theta|
denotes its cardinality. We denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}
the expectation with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
.

Remark 3.2
.

For a fixed
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, the distribution of
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots

under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
is the product measure of the distribution
\mu_{\theta}
in (
7
). On the other, for
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
with
|\Theta|\geq 2
,
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are not independent under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
.

Recall
\mathbb{L}_{\theta}(\cdot)
from (
8
).
For any choice of finite
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
and
b>0
, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta):=\left(\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{L}_{\theta}(\tau_{b}^{*})\right)^{-1}\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\},

(16)

which is an unbiased estimator for the wrong exit probability under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
, that is,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right].

Remark 3.3
.

To simulate under the mixture measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
, we proceed as follows: first, sample
\theta\in\Theta
uniformly at random; then generate a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
with distribution
\mu_{\theta}
defined in (
7
), and compute a realization of the estimator
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)
defined in (
16
). Repeating this procedure multiple times and averaging the resulting values yields an estimate of the wrong exit probability.

We aim to choose the set
\Theta
such that (i) the mixture
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is asymptotically efficient, and (ii) both constructing
\Theta
and evaluating
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)
are computationally tractable, so that the resulting I.S. estimator is feasible.

3.4
Sufficient conditions for asymptotic efficiency

In view of Definition
2.1
and Theorem
3.1
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is an A.E. proposal for estimating
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\right]\leq-2r_{*}.

(17)

The next lemma shows that this is always the case if
\Theta
contains the optimal tilting corresponding to each region. In what follows, for any

\mathcal{C}\subset[J]
we set

r_{\mathcal{C}}:=\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}}r_{j}.

(18)

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that conditions (
C1
), (
C2
) and (
C3
) hold. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset[J]

and
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
be a finite set.
If
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}\subset\Theta
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j}\}\right]=-2r_{\mathcal{C}}.

(19)

Consequently, if
\{\beta^{j}:j\in[J]\}\subset\Theta
, then (
17
) holds, and as a result,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is an asymptotically efficient proposal for estimating the wrong exit probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})
.

See Appendix
C.2
.
∎

Lemma
3.3
establishes the asymptotic efficiency of the mixture
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
for
\Theta=\{\beta^{j}:j\in[J]\}
. However, when
J
is extremely large (e.g., exponential in the dimension
d
), it is computationally prohibitive both to compute
\{\beta^{j}:j\in[J]\}
and to evaluate
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)
in (
16
), since the latter involves a sum over
J
likelihood ratios. Thus, in what follows, our goal is to construct a
feasible
A.E. mixture
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
, whose cardinality
|\Theta|
is
much smaller
than
J
. For this, note that for any
\mathcal{C}\subset[J]
the limit in (
19
) holds provided that
\Theta
contains the tilts corresponding to the sets indexed by
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}\subset\Theta
.
If this is the case, and if additionally

\displaystyle\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{C}}\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j}\}\right]\leq-2r_{\mathcal{C}},

(20)

then by Lemma
3.3
and Theorem
3.1
it follows that
r_{*}=r_{\mathcal{C}}
and that
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is an A.E. proposal.

Condition (
20
) requires that (i)
\mathcal{C}
is large enough so that
r_{\mathcal{C}}=r_{*}
, and (ii) the second moment of
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)
can be controlled when
\{S_{n}\}
exits through one of the regions
bW^{j}
with
j\notin\mathcal{C}
.
The following theorem provides a sufficient condition for (
20
), and hence for the asymptotic efficiency of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
.
Recall
r_{\mathcal{C}}
from (
18
) and
v_{j}(\cdot)
from (
15
).

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that conditions (
C1
), (
C2
) and (
C3
) hold. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset[J]
and
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
be a finite set. Suppose that
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}\subset\Theta
and

\displaystyle\text{for each }\;j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}\;\text{ there exists }\;\gamma\in\Theta\;\text{ such that }\;\;v_{j}(\gamma)\;\geq\;2r_{\mathcal{C}}.

(21)

Then,
r_{*}:=r_{\mathcal{C}}
,
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is an asymptotically efficient proposal for estimating the wrong exit probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})
; that is,

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\right]=-2r_{*}=2\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}).

See Appendix
C.3
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
\Theta=\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}
, then condition (
21
) reduces to

\displaystyle\text{for each }j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}\;\text{there exists }i\in\mathcal{C}\;\text{such that}\;\;v_{j}(\beta^{i})\;\geq\;2r_{\mathcal{C}}.

The construction of
\Theta
for applying Theorem
3.4
is discussed in Subsection
3.6
.

3.5
A necessary condition for asymptotic efficiency

In this subsection, we establish a necessary condition for
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
to be A.E.. Let
\mathcal{C}_{*}
denote the set of indices corresponding to the regions with the smallest exponential rates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{*}:=\left\{j\in[J]:\;\;r_{j}=r_{*}\right\}.

(22)

By Theorem
3.1
,
\mathcal{C}_{*}
indexes the regions from which the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
is most likely to exit on the event of a wrong exit, i.e.,
\{\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0}\}
. It is thus natural to expect that an efficient choice of
\Theta
should include
the optimal tiltings (recall Definition
3.1
) associated with these most likely exit regions, namely that
\left\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}_{*}\right\}\subset\Theta
. We next establish the
necessity
of this inclusion, under the additional assumption stated below:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\overline{\operatorname{cone}(W^{j})}\;\cap\;\overline{\operatorname{cone}(W^{j^{\prime}})}=\{\mathbf{0}\},\;\;\;\text{ for }\;0\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq J.\\
&\text{ Either }\;(i).\;W^{0}=\emptyset,\quad\text{ or }\;\;(ii).\;W^{0}\text{ is convex and }\operatorname{int}\left(\operatorname{cone}\left(\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{*})\right)\right)\cap W^{0}\neq\emptyset.\end{split}

(C4)

The first condition in (
C4
) is slightly stronger than the first part of (
C3
). The second allows
W^{0}
to be either empty or to satisfy assumptions analogous to those imposed on
W^{j}
for
j\in[J]
in condition (
C2
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume that conditions (
C1
), (
C2
), (
C3
) and (
C4
) hold.
Let
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
be finite.
If
\beta^{j}\not\in\Theta
for some
j\in\mathcal{C}_{*}
, then

{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
fails to be asymptotically efficient for estimating
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{0})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\right]>-2r_{*}.

See Appendix
C.4
.
∎

Theorem
3.5
can be used to derive negative results: if the size of
\mathcal{C}_{*}
grows exponentially with
d
, then it becomes
computationally infeasible
to construct any asymptotically efficient proposal of the form
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
for some finite
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, since
\Theta
must include
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}_{*}\}
.

Remark 3.5
.

As demonstrated by the counterexamples in
Glasserman and Wang 1997
, the condition
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}_{*}\}\subset\Theta
does not, in general, suffice for
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
to be A.E.

3.6
Strategy for constructing
\Theta

Theorem
3.4
provides a strategy for selecting
\Theta
so that the mixture
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is A.E.:

Step 1.

Select a subset
\mathcal{C}\subset[J]
and compute
r_{j}
and
\beta^{j}
for
j\in\mathcal{C}
.

Step 2.

Choose a subset
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
such that (i)
\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}\subset\Theta
, and (ii) we can verify that condition (
21
) holds.

For Step 1, we note that
r_{j}
and
\beta^{j}
do not in general admit closed-form expressions, and their computation typically requires solving a convex optimization problem (see Subsection
3.7
). Hence, Step 1 is computationally feasible provided that
|\mathcal{C}|
is not too large.

Moreover, Theorem
3.5
suggests that
\mathcal{C}
should contain those regions most likely to produce a wrong exit, i.e., the set
\mathcal{C}_{*}
defined in (
22
). However, when
J
is large, it is generally not feasible to compute all values in
\{r_{j}:j\in[J]\}
, and consequently
\mathcal{C}_{*}
will not be known. In practice, therefore,
\mathcal{C}
should be selected based on
application-specific intuition
about where wrong exits are most likely.

For Step 2, the more components we include in
\Theta
, the easier it is for condition (
21
) to hold. In the extreme, condition (
21
) is always satisfied if
\Theta
includes the exponential tilting that corresponds to region
W^{j}
,
\beta^{j}
, defined in Definition
3.1
,

for every
j\notin\mathcal{C}
. However, this is computationally infeasible when
J
is large. To ensure that
|\Theta|
remains much smaller than
J
, we aim to include in
\Theta
components that
simultaneously
accommodate multiple indices
j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
, rather than being tailored to a single region. That is, the goal is that for each
\gamma\in\Theta\setminus\{\beta^{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}
there exist multiple
j\notin\mathcal{C}
such that
v_{j}(\gamma)\geq 2r_{\mathcal{C}}
. We stress that such
\gamma
need not belong to
\{\beta^{j}:j\notin\mathcal{C}\}
.

Given a finite
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, there are two ways to verify condition (
21
). The first is analytical: for each
j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
, we need to find some
\gamma\in\Theta
, construct an
analytical
lower bound for
\nu_{j}(\gamma)
, and verify that it is at least
2r_{\mathcal{C}}
. In Theorems
4.2
,
5.2
, and
6.2
, we adopt this approach to derive sufficient conditions for three different problems. The second way is computational and requires the numerical evaluation of
v_{j}(\gamma)

for
all

j\in[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
and some
\gamma\in\Theta
. If
[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
is large, this
is generally infeasible. However, under
special
structures, such as independence or homogeneity, it may suffice to perform this computation for only a small subset of
[J]\setminus\mathcal{C}
. This is the case in Example
1
and Subsection
4.3
.

3.7
Discussion of computation

To implement the above strategy, we need to be able to (i) compute
r_{j}
and
\beta^{j}
for a given
j\in[J]
, and (ii) obtain a
lower bound
on
v_{j}(\gamma)
defined in (
15
) for some
\gamma\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
and
j\in[J]
.
We now provide further details on each of these two tasks. Fix
j\in[J]
and
\gamma\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
.

We first consider task (i). As established in Lemma 3.2 of
Collamore 2002
(see also Lemma
B.1
for a detailed explanation), we have

\displaystyle r_{j}=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda}\left(\inf_{x\in\overline{W^{j}}}\theta\cdot x\right).

(23)

If
W^{j}
is defined via constraints, and if the
strong duality
holds, then the inner optimization is equal to its convex dual for each
\theta
, and the overall problem becomes a convex optimization and can typically be solved using efficient numerical algorithms. For example,
in applications discussed below, each region
W^{j}
is of the form

\displaystyle W^{j}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(A^{j})^{\top}x>b^{j}\right\},

(24)

for some integer
n^{j}\geq 1
,
A^{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times n^{j}}
and
b^{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n^{j}}
. In this case, the inner optimization is a linear programming for each
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and when the
strong duality
holds, we have

\displaystyle r_{j}=\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n^{j}}}

\displaystyle b^{j}\cdot y,\;\;\text{ subject to }\;\;\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\;\;A^{j}y=\theta,\;\;y\geq 0,

where the constraint
y\geq 0
is interpreted coordinatewise.

Next, we consider task (ii). By the definition of
v(\cdot;\cdot)
in (
13
), and the max-min inequality,

\displaystyle v_{j}(\gamma)\geq\sup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\gamma)+\Lambda(\theta-\gamma)\leq 0}\left(\inf_{x\in\overline{W}^{j}}\theta\cdot x\right).

(25)

If
W^{j}
is of the form (
24
) and if
\Lambda(\gamma)\leq 0
, due to weak duality, we
always
have

\displaystyle v_{j}(\gamma)\geq\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n^{j}}}b^{j}\cdot y,\;\;\text{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta-\gamma)\leq 0,\;\;A^{j}y=\theta,\;\;y\geq 0.

It is important to note that the convex optimization problem on the right-hand side is typically not solved exactly due to computational complexity. Instead, we construct a feasible solution, which provides a
lower bound
for
v_{j}(\gamma)
.

4
Multidimensional Siegmund Problem

In this section, we consider the multidimensional Siegmund problem. Specifically, assume that
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1,k}]<0
for each
k\in[d]
, where
X_{1}=(X_{1,1},\ldots,X_{1,d})^{\top}
. Let
\ell,u>0
be fixed. For each
A\subset[d]
, recall the definition of
W^{A}
in (
4
).
Further, denote by
\tau^{A}_{b}
the first hitting time of
bW^{A}
for
A\subset[J]
and define

\mathcal{D}_{b}:=A\;\;\text{ if and only if }\;\;\tau_{b}(W^{A})\leq\tau_{b}(W^{\tilde{A}})\,\text{ for any }\tilde{A}\subset[d].

We expect the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
to first hit
bW^{\emptyset}
, and the goal is to use importance sampling to estimate the probability of it reaching other regions first.

First, we make connections with the setup in Section
3
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{d}
all subsets of
[d]
and by
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}=\mathcal{P}_{d}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
the power set with the empty set removed. We identify
\emptyset
with
0
and
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}
with
[J]
, where
J=2^{d}-1
. For example,
W^{0}
and
\tau_{b}^{0}
is identified with
W^{\emptyset}
and
\tau_{b}^{\emptyset}
, while

\{\tau_{b}^{A},W^{A}:A\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}\}
with
\{\tau_{b}^{j},W^{j}:j\in[J]\}
. Similarly,
\{r_{j},v_{j}(\gamma),\beta^{j}:j\in[J]\}

in (
14
), (
15
), and Definition
3.1
are identified with
\{r_{A},v_{A}(\gamma),\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}\}
, and

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{b}\neq\emptyset\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{\emptyset}\right),\;\;\text{ with }\tau_{b}^{*}=\min\{\tau_{b}^{A}:A\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}\}.

(26)

Note that conditions (
C1
) and (
C3
) always hold in this setup. In addition, throughout this section, we assume that (
C2
) holds.

4.1
A general proposal

In this example, we have
J=2^{d}-1
. Thus, a mixture of
J
components, as suggested in Lemma
3.3
, is not computationally feasible even for moderate values of
d
; for instance,
2^{d}>10^{12}
when
d=40
. Instead, we apply the strategy proposed in Theorem
3.4
and show that, under suitable conditions, mixtures with significantly fewer components remain asymptotically efficient. For each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}
, we begin by characterizing the pair
(r_{A},\beta^{A})
and lower bounding
v_{A}(\gamma)
, following the discussions in (
23
) and (
25
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{-}
,
r_{A}
and
\beta^{A}
are the optimal value and solution of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;u\sum_{k\in A}\theta_{k}-\ell\sum_{k^{\prime}\in A^{c}}\theta_{k^{\prime}},\quad\textup{ subject to }

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

Further, for each
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(\gamma)\leq 0
,
v_{A}(\gamma)
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;u\sum_{k\in A}\theta_{k}-\ell\sum_{k^{\prime}\in A^{c}}\theta_{k^{\prime}},\quad\textup{ subject to }

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta-\gamma)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions characterizing
\beta^{A}
, given in Appendix
D.1
, provide a basis for analyzing
r_{A}
and
\beta^{A}
. In addition,
a feasible solution to the second optimization problem in Lemma
4.1
provides a lower bound for
v_{A}(\gamma)
,
as required by Theorem
3.4
.

We next apply the strategy from Section
3.6
to construct a subset
\Theta\subset\operatorname{dom}\Lambda
such that the resulting measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
is asymptotically efficient. We begin with guessing
\mathcal{C}_{*}
in (
22
): if the coordinates of
X_{1}
are not highly correlated and
u
is not far smaller than
\ell
, then we expect that on the event
\{\mathcal{D}_{b}\neq\emptyset\}
, exactly one coordinate is most likely to be positive, that is,
|\mathcal{D}_{b}|=1
. This suggests that
r_{*}
in Theorem
3.1
is most likely attained by regions indexed by singleton subsets, i.e.,
\{W^{\{k\}}:k\in[d]\}
. Motivated by this intuition, we set
\mathcal{C}=\{\{k\}:k\in[d]\}
and include the corresponding optimal tiltings
\{\beta^{\{k\}}:k\in[d]\}
, as defined in Definition
3.1
, in a candidate set
\Theta
, for which the associated mixture measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
serves as a potential proposal.

Furthermore, we include additional tilting parameters in
\Theta
to ensure that condition (
21
) is satisfied.
To this end, we consider two collections of parameters in
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
.
First, for each
k\in[d]
, let
\gamma^{k}
be the solution of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;u\theta_{k},\quad\text{ subject to }\quad\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}=0\text{ for }k^{\prime}\neq k.

(27)

Clearly, if we denote by
z_{k}
the unique positive number such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(z_{k}X_{1,k})]=1
, then

\displaystyle\gamma^{k}_{j}=z_{k}\;\;\text{ if }j=k,\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma^{k}_{j}=0\;\;\text{ otherwise},

which implies that the optimal value of (
27
) is given by
uz_{k}
.

Second, for each
m\neq m^{\prime}\in[d]
, denote by
s_{m,m^{\prime}}
and
\gamma^{m,m^{\prime}}
the optimal value and maximizer of the following convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;u(\theta_{m}+\theta_{m^{\prime}}),\quad\textup{ subject to }\\
&\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{m}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{m^{\prime}}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{k}=0\;\text{ for }\;k\not\in\{m,m^{\prime}\},\end{split}

(28)

which essentially only involves two variables
\theta_{m},\theta_{m^{\prime}}
, since other variables are fixed to zero.

The motivation for considering the parameter collections in (
27
) and (
28
) is twofold. First, their sizes (
d
and
d(d-1)/2
, respectively) grow polynomially in
d
, in contrast to the exponential growth of all subsets of
[d]
. Second, they enable coverage of multiple subsets
A\subset[d]
satisfying (
21
). Specifically, fix
k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]
. For any
A\subset[d]
containing
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
,
\gamma^{k}+\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}
is a
feasible
solution to the second optimization problem in Lemma
4.1
with
\gamma=\gamma^{k}
, and thus

\displaystyle v_{A}(\gamma^{k})\geq uz_{k}+s_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(29)

In other words,
uz_{k}+s_{k,k^{\prime}}
provides a lower bound for
v_{A}(\gamma^{k})
whenever
k\neq k^{\prime}\in A
, and thus
\gamma^{k}
can effectively cover all subsets
A\subset[d]
containing
k
, whose total number is
2^{d-1}
. Similarly, if
A
contains
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
, then
\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}+\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}
is a
feasible
solution to the same problem with
\gamma=\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}
, and hence

\displaystyle v_{A}(\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}})\geq 2s_{k,k^{\prime}},

(30)

so that
\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}
covers all
A\subset[d]
with
\{k,k^{\prime}\}\subset A
, of size
2^{d-2}
.

Based on the preceding analysis, we derive sufficient conditions for constructing asymptotically efficient proposals.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the following two conditions.

\displaystyle(H1):\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]}\left(uz_{k}+s_{k,k^{\prime}}\right)\geq\min_{k\in[d]}2r_{\{k\}}.

\displaystyle(H2):\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]}2s_{k,k^{\prime}}\geq\min_{k\in[d]}2r_{\{k\}}.

Under either condition,
r_{*}=\min_{k\in[d]}r_{\{k\}}
. Further, define
\Theta^{(0)}:=\{\beta^{\{k\}}:k\in[d]\}
and

\displaystyle\Theta^{(1)}:=\Theta^{(0)}\cup\{\gamma^{k}:k\in[d]\},\quad\Theta^{(2)}:=\Theta^{(0)}\cup\{\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}:k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]\}.

If
(H1)
holds, then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta^{(1)}}
is asymptotically efficient. If
(H2)
holds, then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta^{(2)}}
is asymptotically efficient.

Let
A\subset[d]
be arbitrary such that
|A|\geq 2
, and let
k\neq k^{\prime}\in A
. First, under condition
(H1)
and due to (
29
), we have
v_{A}(\gamma^{k})\geq uz_{k}+s_{k,k^{\prime}}\geq 2\min_{k\in[d]}r_{\{k\}}
. Then the claims under
(H1)
follow immediately from Theorem
3.4
.

Similarly, the claims under
(H2)
follow directly from (
30
) and Theorem
3.4
.
∎

Theorem

4.2
suggests a procedure that solves
d+d(d-1)/2
numerical optimization problems for
\{r_{\{k\}},\beta^{\{k\}}:k\in[d]\}
and
\{s_{k,k^{\prime}},\gamma^{k,k^{\prime}}:k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]\}
. If
(H1)
(resp.
(H2)
) holds, then a mixture with
2d
(resp.
d(d+1)/2
) components is asymptotically efficient.

Remark 4.2
.

Since
\gamma^{k}
in (
27
) is a feasible solution to the problem in (
28
), we have
uz_{k}\leq s_{k,k^{\prime}}
, and thus condition
(H2)
is weaker than
(H1)
. Adding more elements from
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
to
\Theta^{(2)}
allows for constructing asymptotically efficient proposals under conditions weaker than (H2), at the cost of increased computation. For instance, one could consider
\gamma\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
with three nonzero coordinates.

Remark 4.3
.

It is not always necessary to add components to
\Theta^{(0)}
to achieve A.E. For example, by Remark
3.4
,
if for each
A\subset[d]
with
|A|\geq 2
there is a

such that

\displaystyle v_{A}(\beta^{\{j\}})\geq 2\min_{k\in[d]}r_{\{k\}},

(31)

then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta^{(0)}}
is asymptotically efficient. However, this requires solving the second optimization in Lemma
4.1
for all

with

. While computationally prohibitive in general, this becomes feasible under special structures, as we will see in the following examples.

Example 1
(correlated normals).

Assume that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{d},\quad\text{Cov}(X_{1})=\Sigma_{\rho},\;\;\text{ where }\;\;\Sigma_{\rho,k,k^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }k=k^{\prime}\in[d]\\
\rho,\text{ if }k\neq k^{\prime}\in[d]\end{cases},

(32)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{d}

d

1

\Lambda(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\top}\theta+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\top}\Sigma_{\rho}\theta,\;\;\text{ for }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Furthermore, for each

4.2
Independent coordinates

In this subsection, we assume that the coordinates of
X_{1}
are
independent
. In this case,
\gamma^{k}
in (
27
) has special interpretations as discussed below. Further, if, in addition, the coordinates of
X_{1}
have the same distribution, then under mild conditions, a mixture of
d
components is asymptotically efficient; see Subsection
4.3
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. We say
\tilde{\nu}
is Siegmund exponential tilting of
\nu
if there exists
z\neq 0
such that
\frac{d\tilde{\nu}}{d\nu}(x)=\exp(zx)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that when such a
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
exists, it must be unique.

Remark 4.4
.

To justify the terminology, we consider
d=1
and assume that the distribution of
X_{1}
is given by
\nu
and
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]<0
. Denote by
\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}>b\text{ or }S_{n}<-a\}
for some
a,b>0
. To simulate the probability that
\{S_{\tau}>b\}
, as shown in
Siegmund 1976
, the unique asymptotically efficient exponential-tilting distribution is
\tilde{\nu}
.

Remark 4.5
.

Siegmund exponential-tilting pairs appear naturally in hypothesis testing problems.
Let
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
be two probability distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and denote by
\ell(x):=\log(d\nu_{1}/d\nu_{0}(x))
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
the log-likelihood ratio function. Denote by
\nu
(resp.
\tilde{\nu}
) the distribution of
\ell(Z)
if
Z
has the distribution
\nu_{0}
(resp.
\nu_{1}
). Then
\tilde{\nu}
is Siegmund exponential-tilting distribution of
\nu
(and vice versa).

Denote by
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
the distribution of
X_{1,k}
and by
\Lambda^{k}(t):=\log{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(tX_{1,k})]
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
the cumulant generating function of
X_{1,k}
under
{\mathbb{P}}
. Denote by
\mu_{k}^{(1)}
the distribution of
X_{1,k}
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\gamma^{k}}
, where
\gamma^{k}
is defined in (
27
) and recall that
z_{k}>0
is the unique positive number such that
\Lambda^{k}(z_{k})=0
. For
k\in[d]
,
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\gamma^{k}}
, the coordinates of
X_{1}
remain independent and

X_{1,k^{\prime}}\sim\mu^{(0)}_{k^{\prime}}\;\;\text{ if }k^{\prime}\neq k,\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{1,k}\sim\mu^{(1)}_{k}.

That is, under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\gamma^{k}}
, the distribution of the
k
-th coordinate is tilted from
\mu^{(0)}_{k}
to its Siegmund exponential tilting
\mu^{(1)}_{k}
, while the other coordinates retain their original distributions.

Next, we show that under certain conditions,

\beta^{\{k\}}
, as defined in Definition
3.1
, is in fact equal to
\gamma^{k}
in (
27
), for
k\in[d]
.
Denote by

\kappa_{k}^{(0)}:=-(\Lambda^{k})^{\prime}(0)>0,\quad\kappa_{k}^{(1)}:=(\Lambda^{k})^{\prime}(z_{k})>0.

Note that
-\kappa_{k}^{(0)}
and

4.5
Negative results

In this subsection, we consider a

must include exponentially many components in
d
, making it computationally infeasible.

Specifically, assume that
d
is
even
, and that
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1,k}]<0
for each
k\in[d]
, where
X_{1}=(X_{1,1},\ldots,X_{1,d})^{\top}
. Let
\ell,u>0
be fixed. Denote by
\mathcal{S}=\{A\subset[d]:|A|=d/2\}
all subsets of
[d]
with size
d/2
. Suppose the goal is to use importance sampling to estimate the probability that the random hits

bW^{A}

for some

A\in\mathcal{S}
before
bW^{\emptyset}
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{b}^{*}<\tau_{b}^{\emptyset}\right),\quad\text{ where }\;\tilde{\tau}_{b}^{*}:=\min\{\tau_{b}^{A}:\;A\in\mathcal{S}\},

(34)

and
W^{A}
is defined in (
4
). Compared to (
26
), the formulation in (
34
) involves fewer regions, yet still fits within the framework developed in Section
3
. Specifically, we let

and identify
W^{\emptyset}
with
W^{0}
and
\{W^{A},r_{A},\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{S}\}
with

. Further, define

are exchangeable, that is, for any permutation

of

5
Gap Rule from Sequential Multiple Testing

Let
2\leq m\leq d-2
be an integer, and recall that
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Assume that the first
m
coordinates of the random walk have positive drift and the remaining ones negative, that is,

{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1,k}]>0\quad\text{ if }1\leq k\leq m,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1,k}]<0\quad\text{ if }m+1\leq k\leq d.

In this section, we consider the first time that the top
m
coordinates of the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
exceed the remaining ones by at least
b>0
, and we are interested in estimating the probability that, at the stopping time, at least one of these top
m
coordinates does not belong to the set
[m]
, that is, the coordinates with positive drift
(
Song and
Fellouris 2017
)
.

To be specific, let
\mathcal{G}_{m}
denote the collection of subsets of
[d]
with cardinality
m
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}
denote the subsets of cardinality
m
excluding
[m]
, i.e.,

\mathcal{G}_{m}:=\{A\subset[d]:|A|=m\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}:=\mathcal{G}_{m}\setminus\{[m]\}
. For each
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}

let
\tau_{b}^{A}
denote the first time the random walk hits the set
bW^{A}
, where

W^{A}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{k}-x_{k^{\prime}}>1\text{ for every }k\in A,\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}\},

(35)

For large
b>0
, we expect the random walk to exit from
bW^{[m]}
, and we are interested in estimating the probability that it hits
bW^{A}
for some

A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}
before
bW^{[m]}
, i.e.,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}}\tau_{b}^{A}<\tau_{b}^{[m]}\right).

To connect with the notation in Section
3
,
we identify
\{W^{[m]},\tau_{b}^{[m]}\}
with
\{W^{0},\tau_{b}^{0}\}
and
\{W^{A},\tau_{b}^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\}
with
\{W^{j},\tau_{b}^{j}:j\in[J]\}
; in particular,
J=\binom{d}{m}-1
. Similar identification applies to
\{r_{A},v_{A}(\gamma),\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\}
.

Note that conditions (
C1
) and (
C3
) always hold in this setup. In addition, throughout this section, we assume that (
C2
) holds.

Remark 5.1
.

In general, if there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{1,k}]>a
for all
k\in[m]
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{1,k^{\prime}}]<a
for all
k^{\prime}\not\in[m]
, then with high probability, the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
exits through the region
bW^{[m]}
. In this case, one may instead consider the centered random vector
X_{1}-a
, where the subtraction is understood coordinatewise.
Furthermore, the gap is assumed to be
1
in the definition (
35
) of
W^{A}
. This is without loss of generality, as any constant gap can be absorbed into the scaling parameter
b
.

5.1
A general proposal

If
d
and
m
are moderately large, the full mixture in Lemma
3.3
with
\binom{d}{m}-1
components
is computationally infeasible; for example,
\binom{50}{20}>10^{13}
. We again apply Theorem
3.4
to derive sufficient conditions under which mixtures with significantly fewer components are asymptotically efficient.

The following lemma characterizes
\{r_{A},\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\}
and lower bounds
v_{A}(\gamma)

for
\gamma\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, following (
23
) and (
25
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}
. Then
r_{A}
and
\beta^{A}
are the optimal value and solution of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;\sum_{k\in A}\theta_{k},\quad

\displaystyle\textup{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\sum_{i\in[d]}\theta_{i}=0,

\displaystyle\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

Further, for each
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(\gamma)\leq 0
,
v_{A}(\gamma)
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;\sum_{k\in A}\theta_{k},\;

\displaystyle\textup{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta-\gamma)\leq 0,\quad\sum_{i\in[d]}\theta_{i}=0,

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

See Appendix
E
.
∎

Following the strategy in Subsection
3.6
, we begin with guessing
\mathcal{C}_{*}
in (
22
). As discussed above, with high probability, the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
exits from
bW^{[m]}
.
When this is not the case, we typically expect the exit to occur through
bW^{A}
for some

A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}
that is
not
too different from
[m]
. Thus define

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}:=\{([m]\setminus\{k\})\cup\{k^{\prime}\}:\;\;k\in[m],\;\;k^{\prime}\not\in[m]\},

which are subsets that have size
m
and differ from
[m]
in two indexes. Based on this intuition, we set
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}
, and include the corresponding optimal tiltings
\{\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}\}
, as defined in Definition
3.1
, in a candidate set
\Theta
, for which
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
serves as a potential proposal.

Further, we include in
\Theta
the following collections of parameters in
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
. First,
for
\ell\in[m]
and
\ell^{\prime}\not\in[m]
, denote by
\tilde{z}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
the optimal value and solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}\quad&\textup{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell}+\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}=0,\\
&\quad\theta_{\ell}\leq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{k}=0\;\text{ for }\;k\not\in\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}.\end{split}

(36)

Second, for
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in[m]
and
\ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{2}^{\prime}\not\in[m]
, denote by
\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
the optimal value and solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;\theta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}+\theta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\quad\textup{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell_{1}}+\theta_{\ell_{2}}+\theta_{\ell^{\prime}_{1}}+\theta_{\ell^{\prime}_{2}}=0,\\
&\theta_{\ell_{1}}\leq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell_{2}}\leq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\geq 0,\quad\theta_{k}=0\;\text{ for }\;k\not\in\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}\}.\end{split}

(37)

Let
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}
. That is,
A
has size
m
and differs from
[m]
in more than two elements. Thus there exist

\displaystyle\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[m]\setminus A,\quad\text{ and }\quad\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}\in A\setminus[m].

(38)

For the second optimization problem in Lemma
5.1
with
\gamma=\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}
, a feasible solution is given by
\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}+\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
, and as a result, we have

\displaystyle v_{A}(\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}})\geq\tilde{z}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}+\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}.

(39)

In other words,
\tilde{z}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}+\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
provides a lower bound for
v_{A}(\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}})
for all
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}
such that (
38
) holds. In this sense,
\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}
can be used to cover all such
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}
satisfying
\ell_{1}\in[m]\setminus A
and
\ell_{1}^{\prime}\in A\setminus[m]
.

Similarly, for the second optimization problem in Lemma
5.1
with
\gamma=\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
, a feasible solution is given by
2\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
, and as a result,

\displaystyle v_{A}(\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}})\geq 2\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}.

(40)

Thus,
2\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
is a lower bound for
v_{A}(\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}})
for all
A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{-}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}
such that (
38
) holds, and in this sense,
\gamma^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
can be used to cover this collection of
A
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider the following two conditions.

\displaystyle(H1^{\prime}):\;\;\min_{\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in[m],\ \ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{2}^{\prime}\not\in[m]}\left(\tilde{z}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}+\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\right)\geq\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}}2r_{A}.

\displaystyle(H2^{\prime}):\;\;\min_{\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in[m],\ \ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{2}^{\prime}\not\in[m]}2\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\geq\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}}2r_{A}.

Under either condition,
r_{*}=\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}}r_{A}
. Further, define
\widetilde{\Theta}^{(0)}:=\{\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}\}
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}^{(1)}:=\Theta^{(0)}\cup\{\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:\ell\in[m],\ell^{\prime}\not\in[m]\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}^{(2)}:=\Theta^{(0)}\cup\{\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}:\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in[m],\ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{2}^{\prime}\not\in[m]\}.

If
(H1^{\prime})
holds, then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\widetilde{\Theta}^{(1)}}
is asymptotically efficient. If
(H2^{\prime})
holds, then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\widetilde{\Theta}^{(2)}}
is asymptotically efficient.

The claims under condition
(H1^{\prime})
(resp.
(H2^{\prime})
) follow directly from equation (
39
) (resp. (
40
)) and Theorem
3.4
.
∎

The above theorem suggests solving
m(d-m)
optimization problems to compute
\{r_{A},\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{m}^{1}\}
, in addition to
m(d-m)+\binom{m}{2}\binom{d-m}{2}
optimizations problems to obtain

\{\tilde{z}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}},\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:\ell\in[m],\ell^{\prime}\not\in[m]\}
and

\{\tilde{s}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}},\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}:\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in[m],\ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{2}^{\prime}\not\in[m]\}
. Then if
(H1^{\prime})
(resp.
(H2^{\prime})
) holds, a mixture of
2m(d-m)
(resp.
m(d-m)+\binom{m}{2}\binom{d-m}{2}
) components is asymptotically efficient.

Remark 5.2
.

Since
\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}
in (
36
) is a feasible solution to the problem in (

), we have
\tilde{z}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime}}\leq s_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}
, and thus
condition
(H2^{\prime})
is weaker than
(H1^{\prime})
. By adding more elements from
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
to
\widetilde{\Theta}^{(2)}
,
(H2^{\prime})
can be further relaxed, at the cost of additional computation.

In Appendix

.
Due to Remark

6
Sum-intersection Rule

In this section, we consider the
sum-intersection rule
proposed in
Song and Fellouris 2019
. Let
1\leq L\leq d-1
be a fixed integer, and suppose that all coordinates of the random walk have negative expectation, that is,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1,k}]<0
for
k\in[d]
. Consider the first time the sum of the
L
smallest (in absolute value) coordinates of the random walk exceeds
b>0
. We are interested in estimating the probability that upon stopping at least
L
coordinates are positive. When
L=1
, this reduces to the multidimensional Siegmund problem in Section
4
with
\ell=u=1
. Thus, we focus on the case that
L\geq 2
.

To formalize this, denote by
\mathcal{S}_{L}:=\{A\subset[d]:|A|\geq L\}
the collection of all subsets with size
at least

L
. For each
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
, denote by
\tau_{b}^{A}
the first time the random walk hits the set
bW^{A}
, where we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}W^{A}:=\Big\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\;\;&x_{k}>0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\;\;x_{k^{\prime}}<0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c},\;\;\\
&\sum_{k\in B}|x_{k}|>1\;\text{ for every }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\Big\},\end{split}

(42)

and as before
\mathcal{G}_{L}=\{B\subset[d]:|B|=L\}
denotes the collection of subsets of size
L
. We are interested in estimating

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\min_{A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\tau_{b}^{A}<\min_{A\notin\mathcal{S}_{L}}\tau_{b}^{A}\right)

To connect with the problem formulation in Section
3
, we let

W^{0}=\cup_{A\not\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}W^{A}

and index
\{W^{A}:A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}\}
with
[J]
, where
J:=2^{d}-\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\binom{d}{i}
. Observe that
W^{0}
is an open, yet non-convex set, while
W^{j}
is open and convex for every
j\in[J]
. Moreover, conditions (
C1
) and (
C3
) always hold in this setup, and we additionally assume that condition (
C2
) holds.

As before, we first characterize
\{r_{A},\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}\}
and lower bound
v_{A}(\gamma)

for
\gamma\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, following (
23
) and (
25
).

Lemma 6.1
.

For each
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
,
r_{A}
and
\beta^{A}
are the optimal value and solution of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\;\;\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=L-\ell+1}^{d}|\theta|_{(i)},\quad\textup{ subject to }

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c},

where
|\theta|_{(1)}\geq|\theta|_{(2)}\geq\ldots\geq|\theta|_{(d)}
denotes the decreasing rearrangement of the absolute values
\{|\theta_{i}|:i\in[d]\}
for any
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Further, for each
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(\gamma)\leq 0
,
v_{A}(\gamma)
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\;\;\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=L-\ell+1}^{d}|\theta|_{(i)},\quad\textup{ subject to }

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta-\gamma)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}\leq 0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

See Appendix
F
.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

As shown in Appendix
F
(see (
61
) and (
62
)), the above optimization problems are both convex and can be formulated with
\binom{d}{L}+d
decision variables with one non-linear convex constraint and
\binom{d}{L}+2d
linear constraints.

We follow the strategy in Section
3.6
.
Since each coordinate of the random walk
\{S_{n}\}
has a negative drift, on the rare event that
it exists from
bW^{A}
for some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
, intuitively the number of coordinates having negative values is mostly likely to be as small as possible, which is
L
. Thus, it is reasonable to set
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{G}_{L}
, and include the optimal tiltings
\{\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
, as defined in Definition
3.1
, in a candidate set
\Theta
, for which the associated mixture measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta}
serves as a potential proposal.

Next, we show that a mixture, containing
\{\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
and some additional components, is asymptotically efficient. First, for
A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}
, define
z_{A}
and
\gamma^{A}
to be the optimal value and solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\;|\theta|_{(L)},\;\;\textup{ subject to }\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in A,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}=0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in A^{c},\end{split}

(43)

where
|\theta|_{(1)}\geq|\theta|_{(2)}\geq\ldots\geq|\theta|_{(d)}
denotes the decreasing rearrangement of the absolute values
\{|\theta_{i}|:i\in[d]\}
for any
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In particular, a feasible solution contains at most
L
non-zero coordinates.

Second, for
B\in\mathcal{G}_{L+1}
, define
s_{B}
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{B}
to be the optimal value of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\;\;\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=L-\ell+1}^{L+1}|\theta|_{(i)},\quad\textup{ subject to }\\
&\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0,\quad\theta_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in B,\quad\theta_{k^{\prime}}=0\;\text{ for }\;k^{\prime}\in B^{c}.\end{split}

(44)

In particular, a feasible solution contains at most
L+1
non-zero coordinates.

Fix any
A\subset[d]
such that
|A|\geq L+1
. Let
\tilde{A}\subset A
such that
|\tilde{A}|=L
, and let
\tilde{k}\in A\setminus\tilde{A}
. For the second optimization problem in Lemma
6.1
with
\gamma=\gamma^{\tilde{A}}
, we have that
\gamma^{\tilde{A}}+\tilde{\gamma}^{\tilde{A}\cup\{\tilde{k}\}}
is a feasible solution, and in the proof of Theorem
6.2
, we show that this implies

\displaystyle v_{A}(\gamma^{\tilde{A}})\geq\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\;\;\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=L-\ell+1}^{d}\left|\gamma^{\tilde{A}}+\tilde{\gamma}^{\tilde{A}\cup\{\tilde{k}\}}\right|_{(i)}\geq z_{\tilde{A}}+s_{\tilde{A}\cup\{\tilde{k}\}}.

In other words,
z_{\tilde{A}}+s_{\tilde{A}\cup\{\tilde{k}\}}
is a lower bound for
v_{A}(\gamma^{\tilde{A}})
for all
A\subset[d]
such that
\tilde{A}\cup\{\tilde{k}\}\subset A
, and in this sense
\gamma^{\tilde{A}}
covers for all
A\subset[d]
that contains
\tilde{A}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that

\displaystyle(H\text{-}SI):\;\;\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{L},k\in A^{c}}z_{A}+s_{A\cup\{k\}}\geq\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}}2r_{A}.

Then
r_{*}=\min_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}}r_{A}
. Further, define

\Theta^{(SI)}:=\{\beta^{A}:\;A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}\;\cup\;\{\gamma^{A}:\;A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}.

Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta^{(SI)}}
is asymptotically efficient.

See Appendix
F.1
.
∎

The above theorem suggests solving
\binom{d}{L}
optimization problems to compute
\{r_{A},\beta^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
, in addition to
\binom{d}{L}+\binom{d}{L+1}
optimizations problems to obtain

\{z_{A},{\gamma}^{A}:A\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
and

\{s_{B}:B\in\mathcal{G}_{L+1}\}
. Then, if the condition
(H\text{-}SI)
holds, a mixture of
2\binom{d}{L}
components is asymptotically efficient. As before, we can weaken the condition by adding more components to the mixture.

7
Conclusions

We develop a strategy for efficiently estimating rare-event probabilities involving many disjoint exit regions. By combining optimal tilts for a small number of regions with carefully selected additional components, the method remains practical even when the number of regions grows combinatorially or even exponentially with dimension
d
. Its effectiveness is demonstrated through several sequential multiple testing problems.

Future directions include extending the random walk model to Markov additive processes, as in
Collamore 2002
, which may enable the estimation of error probabilities in sequential testing procedures with controlled sensing or composite hypotheses. Another direction is to develop computationally feasible proposals in settings where any asymptotically efficient mixture of exponential tilts provably requires too many components.

A.1
A weaker version of Theorem

In this appendix, we present a self-contained, elementary proof of a weaker version of Theorem
2.1
, under the stronger assumption (
11
).

Remark A.1
.

Since
\Lambda
is lower semi-continuous
(
Brown 1986
, Theorem 1.13)
,
we have
\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
is closed, and thus condition (
11
) implies that
\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
is compact.

Theorem A.1
(Weaker version of Theorem
2.1
).

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex open set.
Assume that (
11
) holds, and that

\operatorname{cone}(B({\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}],\delta))\cap W
is empty for some
\delta>0
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)=-r_{W}.

Upper bound.
For each
\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
, by a change of measure, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\exp(-\theta\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)}+\tau_{b}(W)\Lambda(\theta));\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\exp(-\theta\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)});\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\inf_{x\in W}\theta\cdot(bx)),

where the first inequality is because
\Lambda(\theta)\leq 0
, and the second is because on the event
\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}
, we have
S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\in bW
.
Optimizing the upper bound over
\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)\leq-\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda}(\inf_{x\in W}\theta\cdot x).

Since
\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
is compact due to condition (
11
) (see Remark
A.1
), by Sion’s minimax theorem
(
Sion 1958
)
we have

\displaystyle r_{W}=\inf_{x\in W}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda}\theta\cdot x=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda}\inf_{x\in W}\theta\cdot x,

which establishes the upper bound:
b^{-1}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)\leq-r_{W}
.

Lower bound.
Fix an arbitrary
x\in W
. We recall from (
9
) that:

\displaystyle I(x)=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda}\theta\cdot x.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
is compact due to condition (
11
) (Remark
A.1
),
this optimization problem
must have a solution, denoted as
\theta_{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\Lambda
.

Due to condition (
11
),
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
is open, and thus
\nabla\Lambda(\mathbf{0})={\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]
(see, e.g., Theorem 2.2 in
Brown 1986
). Since
W\cap\operatorname{cone}(B({\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}],\delta))=\emptyset
for some
\delta>0
, we have that
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]\neq\mathbf{0}
, and that
\mathbf{0}\not\in W
, which also implies that
x\neq\mathbf{0}
since
x\in W
. As a result, there exists some
\tilde{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(\tilde{\theta})<0
, which implies that Slater’s Condition
(
Bertsekas, Nedic and
Ozdaglar 2003
, Asumption 6.4.3)
holds, and thus the KKT condition is necessary for
\theta_{x}
. Since
x\neq\mathbf{0}
, we must have

\displaystyle I(x)=\theta_{x}\cdot x,\quad\Lambda(\theta_{x})=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho\nabla\Lambda(\theta_{x})=x\text{ for some }\rho>0.

(45)

Since
W
is open, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
B(x,\epsilon)\subset W
. Fix such an
\epsilon
.
By a change of measure from
{\mathbb{P}}
to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{x}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon)<\infty\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{x}}\left[\exp\left(-\theta_{x}\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))}+\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))\Lambda(\theta_{x})\right);\;\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))<\infty\right]

\displaystyle\geq-b(\theta_{x}\cdot x+\epsilon\|\theta_{x}\|)\;{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{x}}\left(\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))<\infty\right),

where the final inequality holds because
\Lambda(\theta_{x})=0
and

S_{\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))}\in bB(x,\epsilon)
on the event

\{\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))<\infty\}
. Since
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
is open,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{x}}[X_{1}]=\nabla\Lambda(\theta_{x})=\frac{x}{\rho}.

Due to the Strong Law of Large Numbers,
\lim_{b\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{x}}\left(\tau_{b}(B(x,\epsilon))<\infty\right)=1

\displaystyle\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{b}(W)<\infty)\geq-I(x)-\epsilon\|\theta_{x}\|.

Since

The proof is complete.

A.2
Discussions for Theorem
2.2

We first provide more details about the proof of Theorem
2.2
based on
Collamore 2002
.

The proof is due to
Collamore 2002
; we provide further details below. In
Collamore 2002
, the process
\{(X_{n},S_{n}):n\geq 0\}
is modeled as a Markov additive process, where
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
and
\{(X_{n},\xi_{n}):n\geq 0\}
is a time-homogeneous Markov process taking values in
\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with transition kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x,E\times\Gamma):={\mathbb{P}}\left((X_{n+1},\xi_{n+1})\in E\times\Gamma\mid X_{n}=x\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{S},;E\in\mathcal{S},;\Gamma\in\mathcal{R}^{d},

where
\mathcal{S}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{S}
, and
\mathcal{R}^{d}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the special case of random walks considered in our setting, the state space
\mathbb{S}
can be taken as a singleton. Hence, the dependence on
{X_{n}}
and the structure of
\mathbb{S}
in
Collamore 2002
can be disregarded.

For
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, we identify the proposal
\mathbb{Q}
in
Collamore 2002
with
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
, and then
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{Q}}
in Equation (2.4) of
Collamore 2002
is a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that for
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\frac{d\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{Q}}}{d\mu}(v)=\frac{d\mu}{d\mu_{\theta}}(v)=\exp\left(-\theta\cdot v+\Lambda(\theta)\right).

\displaystyle=

As a result, the rate function

as given in (

As discussed around equation (3.6) and in Section 3.2 of

We next establish a

Theorem A.2
(Weaker version of Part (iii) of Theorem
2.2
).

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex open set.
Assume that (
11
) holds.
For
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
, if
v(W;\theta)>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]\leq-v(W;\theta).

Fix arbitrary
\theta\in\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda)
such that
v(W;\theta)>0
.
Since
v(W;\theta)>0
, it implies that the following subset is non-empty:

\widetilde{D}:=\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\theta)+\Lambda(\alpha-\theta)\leq 0\}.

For each
\alpha\in\widetilde{D}
,
by a change of measure from
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha-\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha-\theta}\left[\exp\left(-\alpha\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)}+\tau_{b}(W)(\Lambda(\theta)+\Lambda(\alpha-\theta))\right)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}<\infty\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha-\theta}\left[\exp\left(-\alpha\cdot S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-b\inf_{x\in W}(\alpha\cdot x)),

where, in the final step, we use the fact that on the event
\{\tau_{b}(W)<\infty\}
, we have
S_{\tau_{b}(W)}\in bW
. Optimizing the upper bound over
\alpha\in\widetilde{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\theta}^{-2}(\tau_{b}(W))\right]\leq-\sup_{\alpha\in\tilde{D}}\left(\inf_{x\in W}(\alpha\cdot x)\right).

Finally, by the convexity of
\Lambda(\cdot)
,

\widetilde{D}\subset\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\alpha/2)\leq 0\}.

Since
\{\tilde{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\Lambda(\tilde{\theta})\leq 0\}
is bounded,

\widetilde{D}
is bounded. Since
\Lambda
is lower-semicontinuous (see Remark
A.1
),
\widetilde{D}
is compact. Then, by Sion’s minimax theorem
(

, we have

\displaystyle v(W;\theta)=\inf_{x\in\overline{W}}\sup_{\alpha\in\widetilde{D}}\alpha\cdot x=\sup_{\alpha\in\widetilde{D}}\inf_{x\in\overline{W}}\alpha\cdot x.

The proof is then complete since
\inf_{x\in W}(\alpha\cdot x)=\inf_{x\in\overline{W}}(\alpha\cdot x)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Recall that for each

\displaystyle\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}(\tau^{*}_{b}<\tau^{0}_{b},\;\;\tau_{b}^{*}=\tau_{b}^{j})\geq-r_{j},

(47)

on which we now focus.

Let
\mathbb{C}^{d}[0,1]
be the space of continuous functions from
[0,1]
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which is endowed with the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, that is, for

\|f-g\|_{\infty}=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|f(t)-g(t)\|
. For

\epsilon

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(f,\epsilon):=\{g\in\mathbb{C}^{d}[0,1]:\|g-f\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\}.

. By Lemma
B.2
, there exists a small enough

such that
for each

\displaystyle B(v,2\epsilon)\subset W^{j},\;\;\text{ and }\;\;B(tv,2\epsilon)\cap W^{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset\;\text{ for }\;0\leq t\leq 1,

(48)

where recall that

denotes the open ball centered at

with radius

. Denote by

Fix an arbitrary
\tau>0

, where

for

and the sample-path of

is piece-wise linear. Note that for any

.

Lemma C.1

\displaystyle\Lambda^{j,*}(x)\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\beta^{j}}(\tau_{b}^{j})=\exp(\beta^{j}\cdot S_{\tau_{b}^{j}})\leq\exp\left(b(r_{j}+\delta/4)\right),

\displaystyle\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\Theta}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{b}(\Theta)\right)^{2}\right]\geq-2r_{*}+\delta/2+\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta^{j}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{b,\epsilon,\delta,\Theta}^{(j,*)}\right).

4.4

\frac{1}{d}v_{1}^{+}+\frac{d-1}{d}v_{1}^{-}\geq 0

Now we show the second claim. Note that
\Lambda^{1}(\cdot)
is differentiable on its
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{1})

\Lambda^{1}(t)<0

t<0
or

. Finally, denote by
\iota\in(0,z)
is the unique root of
\Lambda^{1}
. Then on the
\operatorname{dom}(\Lambda^{1})

\iota

\iota
. We consider two cases.

Case 2:

In this case, for each

58

\displaystyle\begin{split}&a\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{+})+(d-a)\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{-})=0,\quad\iota<v_{a}^{+}<z_{1},\quad v_{a}^{-}<0,\\
&-\frac{1}{\ell}(\Lambda^{1})^{\prime}(v_{a}^{-})=\frac{1}{u}(\Lambda^{1})^{\prime}(v_{a}^{+}).\end{split}

In particular,

and

Now, assume the contrary that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle(a+1)\Lambda^{1}(v_{a+1}^{+})+(d-a-1)\Lambda^{1}(v_{a+1}^{-})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle(a+1)\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{+})+(d-a-1)\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{+})-\Lambda^{1}(v_{a}^{-}).

Recall the discussion at the beginning of this subsection. Due to homogeneity,

&\lambda_{B}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L},\quad\sum_{B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\text{ and }k\in B}\lambda_{B}\leq|\theta_{k}|\;\text{ for }\;k\in[d].\end{split}

(62)

∎

\gamma^{\tilde{A}}_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }k\in\tilde{A},\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma^{\tilde{A}}_{k}=0\;\text{ for }k\not\in\tilde{A},

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\overline{W^{{A}}}}\gamma^{\tilde{A}}\cdot x=\inf\left\{\sum_{k\in[d]}|\gamma^{\tilde{A}}_{k}|x_{k}\;:\;x_{k}\geq 0\;\text{ for }\;k\in[d],\quad\sum_{k\in B}x_{k}\geq 1\;\text{ for }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}\right\}.

Then due to Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\overline{W^{{A}}}}\gamma^{\tilde{A}}\cdot x=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=L-\ell+1}^{d}|\gamma^{\tilde{A}}|_{(i)}=z_{\tilde{A}},

F.2
Supporting lemmas: sum-intersection rule

Lemma F.1
.

Let
2\leq L<d
, and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\theta_{1}\geq\theta_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\theta_{d}\geq 0
. Consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\theta\cdot x,\quad

\displaystyle\text{ subject to }\;x_{k}\geq 0\;\;\text{ for }\;k\in[d],\quad\sum_{k\in B}x_{k}\geq 1\;\;\text{ for }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}.

Denote by
\tilde{\theta}_{L}=\sum_{k=L}^{d}\theta_{k}
. Then the optimal value is given by

\displaystyle\min\left\{\tilde{\theta}_{L},\;\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\theta}_{L}+{\theta}_{L-1}),\;\frac{1}{3}(\tilde{\theta}_{L}+{\theta}_{L-1}+\theta_{L-2}),\;\ldots,\;\frac{1}{L}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{m}+{\theta}_{L-1}+\ldots+\theta_{1}\right)\right\}

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a feasible solution, and assume for some
1\leq k\leq d-1
,
x_{k}\geq x_{k+1}
. Define
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as follows:

\tilde{x}_{j}=x_{j}\;\text{ for }\;j\not\in\{k,k+1\},\quad\tilde{x}_{k}=x_{k+1},\quad\tilde{x}_{k+1}=x_{k}.

Then
\tilde{x}
is also a feasible solution and

\theta\cdot x-\theta\cdot\tilde{x}=(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k+1})(x_{k}-x_{k+1})\geq 0.

That is,
\tilde{x}
is at least as good as
x
. Then, by an inductive argument,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\theta\cdot x,\quad\text{ s.t. }\;x_{k}\geq 0\;\;\text{ for }\;k\in[d],\quad\sum_{k\in B}x_{k}\geq 1\;\;\text{ for }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\theta\cdot x,\quad\text{ s.t. }\;0\leq x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{d}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{L}x_{i}\geq 1.

Further, since
\theta_{k}\geq 0
for
k\in[d]
, for the latter optimization problem, we may assume
x_{d}=x_{d-1}=\ldots=x_{L+1}=x_{L}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{L}x_{i}=1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\theta\cdot x,\quad\text{ s.t. }\;x_{k}\geq 0\;\;\text{ for }\;k\in[d],\quad\sum_{k\in B}x_{k}\geq 1\;\;\text{ for }\;B\in\mathcal{G}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}}\theta_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\theta_{L-1}x_{L-1}+\tilde{\theta}_{L}x_{L},\;\;\text{ s.t. }\;0\leq x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{L}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{L}x_{i}=1.

Then the proof is complete by Lemma
F.2
.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

Let
L\geq 2
, and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
such that
\theta_{1}\geq\theta_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\theta_{L-1}\geq 0
and
\theta_{L}\geq 0
. Consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}}\theta\cdot x,\quad

\displaystyle\text{ subject to }\;0\leq x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{L},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{L}x_{k}=1.

Then the optimal value is given by

\displaystyle v^{*}:=\min\left\{{\theta}_{L},\;\frac{1}{2}({\theta}_{L}+{\theta}_{L-1}),\;\ldots,\;\frac{1}{L}\left({\theta}_{L}+{\theta}_{L-1}+\ldots+\theta_{1}\right)\right\}.

The KKT conditions are as follows. There exist
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
for
k\in[L]
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\sum_{\ell=k}^{L}(\theta_{\ell}-\lambda)\;\;\text{ for }\;k\in[L],\quad 0\leq x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{L},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{L}x_{i}=1,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}x_{1}=0,\quad\lambda_{k}(x_{k-1}-x_{k})=0\;\text{ for }2\leq k\leq L.

Denote by
k^{*}\in[L]
an index that attains
v^{*}
, that is,

v^{*}=\frac{1}{L-k^{*}+1}{\sum_{\ell=k^{*}}^{L}\theta_{\ell}}.

It is elementary to verify that the following triplet
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\{\lambda_{k},k\in[L]\}
satisfies the KKT conditions:

\displaystyle x_{1}=\ldots=x_{k^{*}-1}=0,\quad x_{k^{*}}=\ldots=x_{L}=1/(L-k^{*}+1),\quad\lambda=v^{*},

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\sum_{\ell=k}^{L}(\theta_{\ell}-v^{*})\;\text{ for }\;k\in[L].

In particular, by the definition of
k^{*}
and
v^{*}
,
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
for
k\in[L]
and
\lambda_{k^{*}}=0
As a result, the optimal value is
v^{*}
.
∎
