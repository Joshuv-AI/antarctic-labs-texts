Abstract
In this paper, we finally prove the well-posedness of the linearized R13 moment model, which describes, e.g., rarefied gas flows. As an extension of the classical fluid equations, moment models are robust and have been frequently used, yet they are challenging to analyze due to their additional equations. By effectively grouping variables, we identify a 2-by-2 block structure, allowing us to analyze well-posedness within the abstract LBB framework for saddle point problems. Due to the unique tensorial structure of the equations, in addition to an interesting combination of tools from Stokes’ and linear elasticity theory, we also need new coercivity estimates for tensor fields. These Korn-type inequalities are established by analyzing the symbol map of the symmetric and trace-free part of tensor derivative fields. Together with the corresponding right inverse of the tensorial divergence, we obtain the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions. This result also serves as the basis for future numerical analysis of corresponding discretization schemes.

3
Coercivity and Korn-type inequalities

Korn inequalities play a fundamental role in the study of variational principles in linear elasticity or fluid mechanics. In their most famous version, they show that the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-norm of the full gradient of a vector field can be controlled by the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-norm of only its symmetric part under suitable additional conditions, such as zero boundary values (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
36
,
46
]
). More precisely, let us denote by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a bounded Lipschitz domain, then there exists a constant
c=c(\Omega)>0
such that for all
u\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c\,\left\lVert\operatorname{sym}\mathrm{D}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(Korn-1)

The complete classification of differential operators that might appear on the right-hand side of such types of inequalities is already contained in
[
5
]
. We will refer to such coercivity estimates when zero boundary conditions are present as
Korn inequalities of the first type
and include Aronszajn’s characterization for completeness (only focusing on first-order differential operators):

Lemma 2
(Aronszajn
[
5
]
, Korn inequalities of the first type).

Let
d\geq 2
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
q\in(1,\infty)
,
V
and
\tilde{V}
two finite-dimensional real inner product spaces, and
\mathbb{A}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{A}_{j}\partial_{j}
a linear homogeneous differential operator of first order with constant coefficients
\mathbb{A}_{j}\in L(V,\tilde{V})
. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

There exists a constant
c=c(\mathbb{A},q,\Omega)>0
such that the inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{D}u\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\Omega)}\leq c\,\left\lVert\mathbb{A}u\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\Omega)},

(K-1)

holds for all
u\in\operatorname{W}^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega;V)
.

2.

\mathbb{A}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-elliptic differential operator, meaning that
fix latexml on arxiv

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}:\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}\mathbb{A}_{j}v\overset{!}{=}0\,\Rightarrow\,v=0\Bigg).

(
\mathbb{R}
-ellipt.
)

Set
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\tilde{V}=\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and consider the symmetrized gradient for vector fields, given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\bm{u}\coloneqq\operatorname{sym}\mathrm{D}\bm{u}.

(22)

It is well known that the symmetrized gradient is

Korn-1

Example 4

In many applications, the differential operator of interest has more structure and is given by applying an orthogonal projection on the gradient of a vector field as

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\bm{u}=\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{u}]\quad\text{with}\quad\mathscr{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathscr{A}^{2}=\mathscr{A}.

Classical examples are the symmetric part

Later in

Remark 5
(Legendre–Hadamard ellipticity).

The projection
\mathscr{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
induces an elliptic differential operator
\mathbb{A}\coloneqq\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\nabla}]
if and only if for all
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, the corresponding symbol map
\mathbb{A}[\bm{\xi}]\coloneqq\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\xi}]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is injective. In that case, since
\mathscr{A}
is continuous and
\partial{B_{1}(\bm{0})}\times\partial{B_{1}(\bm{0})}
is compact, we find a
\lambda>0
such that for all
\bm{\eta},\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\left\lvert\mathscr{A}\left[\frac{\bm{\eta}}{\lvert\bm{\eta}\rvert}\otimes\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\lvert\bm{\xi}\rvert}\right]\right\rvert\quad\text{by just taking}\quad\lambda\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lvert\bm{z}\rvert=1\\
\lvert\bm{y}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\lvert\mathscr{A}[\bm{z}\otimes\bm{y}]\rvert,

(24)

while
\lambda>0
holds by the injectivity of
\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\xi}]

\bm{\eta},\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\lambda\lvert\bm{\eta}\rvert\lvert\bm{\xi}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathscr{A}[\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}]\rvert.

(25)

Since
\mathscr{A}
is an orthogonal projection, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}[\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}]\bm{:}\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}=\mathscr{A}[\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}]\bm{:}\mathscr{A}[\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}]=\lvert\mathscr{A}[\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\xi}]\rvert^{2}\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:inject}}}{\geq}\lambda^{2}\lvert\bm{\eta}\rvert^{2}\lvert\bm{\xi}\rvert^{2},

(26)

which is the Legendre–Hadamard ellipticity condition.

\displaystyle\bm{T}|\bm{\xi}|^{2}+(\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}})\otimes\bm{\xi}+\bm{\xi}\otimes(\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}})=0.

(42)

Hence, a multiplication with
\overline{\bm{\xi}}
from the right gives

\displaystyle 2\,\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}}\,|\bm{\xi}|^{2}+\bm{\xi}\,((\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}})\bm{\cdot}\overline{\bm{\xi}})=0.

(43)

Multiplying by
\overline{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}
from the left, we deduce

\displaystyle 3\,((\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}})\bm{\cdot}\overline{\bm{\xi}})\,|\bm{\xi}|^{2}=0\quad\overset{\bm{\xi}\neq\bm{0}}{\Rightarrow}\quad(\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}})\bm{\cdot}\overline{\bm{\xi}}=0.

(44)

Thus, by
eq.

43
, it holds
\bm{T}\overline{\bm{\xi}}=0
, and, returning to
eq.

42
, we conclude that
\bm{T}=0
. Summarizing, we have shown that for a symmetric trace-free tensor
\bm{T}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{3}\left(T_{ij}\xi_{k}+T_{ik}\xi_{j}+T_{jk}\xi_{i}\right)-\tfrac{2}{15}\Big(\sum_{l}T_{lk}\xi_{l}\delta_{ij}+\sum_{l}T_{lj}\xi_{l}\delta_{ik}+\sum_{l}T_{li}\xi_{l}\delta_{jk}\Big)\overset{!}{=}0,

(45)

for
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
implies that
\bm{T}=\bm{0}
, meaning that the symmetric trace-free gradient is
\mathbb{C}
-elliptic on symmetric trace-free tensor fields.

3.2
Right inverse of the matrix-valued divergence operator

The ellipticity property of the previous section also yields the second main theoretical tool regarding the inversion of the matrix-valued divergence. But first, we recall the classical result about vector fields (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Lem. 11.2.3]
or
[
7
, Satz 6.3]
).

Lemma 14
.

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then there exists a constant
c=c(\Omega)>0
, such that for all
\kappa\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, we find a
\bm{t}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\bm{t}=\kappa\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\bm{t}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c\,\left\lVert\kappa\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(53)

Now, we extend this result to higher-order tensors. More precisely, we construct a right inverse of the matrix-valued divergence operator that depends continuously on the data. Since the divergence acts row-wise in

\Div\bm{\tau}=\bm{u},,

(54)

one could solve the problem row by row using
Lemma

14
. However, the resulting matrix field would generally not satisfy any additional structure. The key point of the next lemma is that we construct a right inverse of the matrix divergence that still solves the equation while producing a matrix field with the desired structural constraints (e.g., symmetry or symmetry and vanishing trace).

Lemma 15
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and
\mathscr{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be an orthogonal projection, such that
\mathbb{A}\coloneqq\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\nabla}]
is an elliptic differential operator from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let us further denote by
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathscr{A}}\coloneqq\{\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\mathscr{A}[\bm{\tau}]=\bm{\tau}\}
. Then there exists a constant
c=c(\mathscr{A},\Omega)>0
, such that for all
\bm{u}\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we find a
\bm{\tau}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathscr{A}})
satisfying

\displaystyle-\Div\bm{\tau}=\bm{u}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c\,\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(55)

Proof.

Consider the bounded linear form

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\varphi})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(56)

and the bounded and symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle B(\bm{v},\bm{\varphi})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\bm{:}\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{\varphi}]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(57)

for
\bm{v},\bm{\varphi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By the ellipticity of
\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\nabla}]
, we can apply
Lemma

2
to conclude the coercivity of
B
. Using Lax–Milgram, we then find a unique solution
\bm{v}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that

\displaystyle B(\bm{v},\bm{\varphi})=\ell(\bm{\varphi})\quad\forall\,\bm{\varphi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(58)

Thus, in particular, for all
\bm{\varphi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\bm{:}\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{\varphi}]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\overset{\mathclap{\mathscr{A}\text{o.p.}}}{=}\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\bm{:}\mathrm{D}\bm{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(59)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\bm{\varphi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}}}{=}-\int_{\Omega}\Div\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\bm{\cdot}\bm{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

meaning that
\bm{v}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a weak solution of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Div\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]&=\bm{u}\quad&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\hphantom{-\Div\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}]}\bm{v}&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(60)

Since
\mathscr{A}[\bm{\cdot}\otimes\bm{\nabla}]
is elliptic, we can apply classical elliptic regularity theory (cf.
[
22
, Thm. 10.3, 3.18]
with the Legendre–Hadamard condition from
remark

5
and also the literature mentioned after
[
24
, Prop. 2.11]
) to conclude that for the unique solution, we moreover have

\displaystyle\bm{v}\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{c}\,(\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}).

(61)

Furthermore, by the Poincaré and Korn inequalities, we estimate that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{1}\left\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{2}\left\lVert\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(62)

Now, we show that
\bm{\tau}\coloneqq\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]
does the trick. Since
\bm{v}\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
is the unique solution of
eq.

60
, with this choice, we have
\bm{\tau}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathscr{A}})
and
\Div\bm{\tau}=-\bm{u}
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:LM}}}{=}\int_{\Omega}\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\overset{\mathclap{\text{CBS}}}{\leq}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

(63)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:norm_v}}}{\leq}c_{2}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\mathscr{A}[\mathrm{D}\bm{v}]\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=c_{2}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

(64)

and we can estimate the
\operatorname{H}(\Div)
-norm of
\bm{\tau}
as

\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}(\Div)(\Omega)}=\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\lVert\Div\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{c_{2}^{2}+1}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

(65)

But we can also estimate the full
\operatorname{H}^{1}
-norm using
eq.

64
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\mathscr{A}\text{o.p.}}}{\leq}\left\lVert\mathrm{D}^{2}\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:elliptreg}}}{\leq}\tilde{c}\left(\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:norm_v}}}{\leq}\tilde{c}\left(\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+c_{2}\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:normtau}}}{\leq}\tilde{c}\left(1+c_{2}^{2}\right)\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Hence, a combination of
eq.

64
and
eq.

66
concludes the proof.

4
Existence of unique weak solutions for the linear R13 equations

We are now ready to prove the existence of a unique weak solution to the linear R13 equations. Namely, given some
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\in\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{Q}^{\prime}
, there exists a unique
(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{P}})\in\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{V}})+\mathcal{B}(\bm{\mathcal{V}},\bm{\mathcal{P}})&=\mathcal{F}(\bm{\mathcal{V}})\quad&\forall\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}{}&\in\mathrm{V},\\
\mathcal{B}(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{Q}})&=\mathcal{G}(\bm{\mathcal{Q}})\quad&\forall\,\bm{\mathcal{Q}}{}&\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{aligned}\right.

(67)

To this end, we follow the classical strategy, which we briefly recall below.

Theorem 16
(
[
9
, Thm. 1.1]
).

Let
\mathrm{V}
and
\mathrm{Q}
be two Hilbert spaces, and
a(\cdot,\cdot):\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{V}\to\mathbb{R}
and
b(\cdot,\cdot):\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
be two continuous linear forms. Let us suppose that the range of the operator
B
, associated with
b(\cdot,\cdot)
, is closed in
\mathrm{Q}^{\prime}
; that is, there exists a constant
k_{0}>0
such that

\sup_{v\in\mathrm{V}}\frac{b(v,q)}{{\|v\|}_{\mathrm{V}}}\geq k_{0}{\|q\|}_{\mathrm{Q}/\ker B^{\top}}=k_{0}\Big(\inf_{q_{0}\in\ker B^{\top}}{\|q+q_{0}\|}_{\mathrm{Q}}\Big).

(68)

If, moreover,
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is invertible on
\ker B
, that is, there exists
\alpha_{0}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\inf_{u_{0}\in\ker B}\sup_{v_{0}\in\ker B}\frac{a(u_{0},v_{0})}{{\|u_{0}\|}_{\mathrm{V}}{\|v_{0}\|}_{\mathrm{V}}}\geq\alpha_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{v_{0}\in\ker B}\sup_{u_{0}\in\ker B}\frac{a(u_{0},v_{0})}{{\|u_{0}\|}_{\mathrm{V}}{\|v_{0}\|}_{\mathrm{V}}}\geq\alpha_{0},

(69)

then there exists a solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} a(u,v)+b(v,p)&=f(v)\quad&\forall\,v&\in\mathrm{V},\\
b(u,q)&=g(q)\quad&\forall\,q&\in\mathrm{Q},\end{aligned}\right.

(70)

for any
f\in\mathrm{V}^{\prime}
and any
g\in\operatorname{im}B
. The first component
u
is unique, and
p
is defined up to an element of
\ker B^{\top}
. Moreover, one has the bounds

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{V}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{\|a\|}{\alpha_{0}}+1\right)\frac{1}{k_{0}}\|g\|_{\mathrm{Q}^{\prime}}

(71)

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\mathrm{Q}/\ker B^{\top}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k_{0}}\left(1+\frac{\|a\|}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\|f\|_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}}+\frac{\|a\|}{k_{0}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\|a\|}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\|g\|_{\mathrm{Q}^{\prime}}.

(72)

4.1
Coercivity on the kernel

To continue proving the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}
on the kernel of
B
(i.e., the linear map associated with
\mathcal{B}
; see
section

2
), we first need the following characterization.

Lemma 17
(Kernel of
B
).

We have

\ker B=\left\{(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s},p)\in\mathrm{V}:\Div\bm{\sigma}=-\bm{\nabla}p,\operatorname{div}\bm{s}=0\right\}.

(73)

Proof.

By the definition of the kernel, we have for all
\bm{\mathcal{U}}\in\ker B
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{Q}})

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Q}

(74)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow-e(\bm{v},\bm{\sigma})-g(p,\bm{v})-b(\kappa,\bm{s})

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,(\bm{v},\kappa)

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Q}.

(75)

Since this holds for any
(\bm{v},\kappa)\in\mathrm{Q}
, we extract two separated conditions, i.e.,
e(\bm{v},\bm{\sigma})+g(p,\bm{v})=0
and
b(\kappa,\bm{s})=0
. We insert the explicit expressions of these bilinear forms from
section

2
and directly conclude that
\Div\bm{\sigma}=-\bm{\nabla}p
and
\operatorname{div}\bm{s}=0
define the kernel of
B
.

Lemma 18
(Coercivity of
\mathcal{A}
on
\ker B
).

The bilinear form
\mathcal{A}
is coercive on
\ker B
, i.e., there exists a constant
\alpha_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{U}})\geq\alpha_{0}\left\lVert\bm{\mathcal{U}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}
for all
\bm{\mathcal{U}}\in\ker B
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

17
, with
\bm{\mathcal{U}}=(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s},p)\in\ker B
, we have
\Div\bm{\sigma}=-\bm{\nabla}p
. Thus, we estimate that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\overset{\mathclap{(\ast)}}{\geq}\left\lVert\Div\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}p\right\rVert_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

(76)

using, in the lower bound (
\ast
), the CBS inequality for the Frobenius inner products in
\int_{\Omega}\|\Div\bm{\sigma}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\|[\langle(\delta_{kl})_{k,l=1}^{3},(\partial_{k}\sigma_{il})_{k,l=1}^{3}\rangle_{\textnormal{F}}]_{i=1}^{3}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\leq\|\bm{1}_{3}\|_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathrm{D}\bm{\sigma}\|_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
. Then, we conclude with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{U}},\bm{\mathcal{U}})

\displaystyle=a(\bm{s},\bm{s})+\bar{d}((\bm{\sigma},p),(\bm{\sigma},p))

(77)

The results in

Remark 26
(Regularity).

theorem

16

and
\mathcal{G}
in the dual spaces
\mathrm{V}^{\prime}
and

5
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we derived the well-posedness for the weak R13 equations. The first important step was to reformulate the problem as a grouped mixed problem within the abstract LBB framework. Due to the unique tensorial structure of the equations, which also involve matrix-valued differential equations, we derived new theoretical tools, including a tensor-valued Korn inequality for the symmetric and trace-free parts of matrix derivatives. Proving the ellipticity of such operators then led to the existence of a right inverse of the matrix-valued divergence as the second crucial ingredient needed to analyze the equations. These estimates could also be used in the context of linear elasticity if, e.g., nonstandard material laws are involved, and we presented them in a general form.

Our analysis is the first step towards a numerical analysis of discretization schemes for the equations. For example, in the context of mixed finite element methods – although promising numerical progress has been made, e.g., using generalized Taylor–Hood elements in
[
60
]
– no known stable element pairing is yet available. In fact, as a mixed problem, continuous results do not directly transfer to the discrete case. Especially the discrete sup-condition and the handling of the discrete kernel of
B
are unclear for now, since the symmetry and trace-free constraints on the tensor
\bm{\sigma}
are challenging; see, e.g., the discussion in
[
6
, Sec. 9.1]
.

Further aspects include the extension to the time-dependent case, the construction of efficient preconditioned iterative solvers, and, eventually, the solution and analysis of the complete nonlinear system. On the theoretical side, reformulating the system as a variational optimization problem, conducting alternative analyses using different groupings, using augmented formulations, and extending to higher-order moment systems are natural next steps. Such systems will require coercivity estimates for symmetric and trace-free tensor derivatives of even higher order, as well as suitable right inverses of the corresponding divergence operators.
