Spectral Certificates and Sum-of-Squares Lower Bounds for Semirandom Hamiltonians
Nicholas Kocurek nichok6@cs.washington.edu University of Washington

Abstract
The k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} problem is one of the most well-studied problems in classical complexity. We study a natural quantum analogue of k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} , the problem of computing the ground energy of a certain subclass of structured local Hamiltonians, signed sums of k k -local Pauli operators, which we refer to as k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} Hamiltonians. As an exhibition of the connection between this model and classical k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} , we extend results on refuting k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} instances to the Hamiltonian setting by crafting a quantum variant of the Kikuchi matrix for CSP refutation, instead capturing ground energy optimization. As our main result, we show an n O ⁡ ( ℓ ) n^{O(\ell)} -time classical spectral algorithm certifying ground energy at most 1 2 + ε \frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon in (1) semirandom Hamiltonian k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} instances or (2) sums of Gaussian-signed k k -local Paulis both with O ⁡ ( n ) ⋅ ( n ℓ ) k / 2 − 1 ​ log ⁡ n / ε 4 O(n)\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1}\log n/\varepsilon^{4} local terms, a tradeoff known as the refutation threshold. Additionally, we give evidence this tradeoff is tight in the semirandom regime via non-commutative Sum-of-Squares lower bounds embedding classical k k - 𝖷𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{XOR} instances as entirely classical Hamiltonians.

The

-

[

]

-

]

]

In light of the grim outlook of worst-case complexity, the study of random CSPs was initiated to potentially subvert these hardness results. As random

-

The state-of-the-art algorithms provide spectral refutations, certificates in the form of a spectral norm of a matrix, particularly through the novel Kikuchi matrix method which has seen a flurry of applications arise recently

In the setting of quantum complexity theory, the

Each “constraint”

1.1
Our results

In this paper, we investigate the
random
and
semirandom
complexity of Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
. It is folklore that random systems are not highly entangled as entanglement is a property requiring structure, an intuition that is captured in quantum error correction and the construction of NLTS Hamiltonians. Despite this, the ground spaces of these Hamiltonians should be robust to small additive noise, which can be captured succinctly by simple eigenvalue/eigenvector perturbation theory. Rather than external noise, we instead look at noise internal to the structure, namely, in the semirandom model through isolated randomness in the sign of each term.

Our main result is the following spectral algorithm for bounding the ground state energy of such a semirandom
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonian. Our algorithm is derived from a new quantum variant of the Kikuchi hierarchy, built as the signed adjacency matrix of a graph on (degree-
\ell
) Pauli operators.

Theorem 1.2

(Spectral refutation of semirandom
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians)
.

Fix
n/2\geq\ell\geq k/2
. There is a classical algorithm taking as input a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
describing an
n
-qubit Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
and outputs a number
\mathrm{algval}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\in[0,1]
in time
n^{O(\ell)}
with the following two guarantees:

1.

\mathrm{algval}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})
for every instance;

2.

If
{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq O(n)\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1}\cdot\log(n)\cdot\varepsilon^{-4}
and
\mathcal{I}
is drawn from the semirandom Rademacher and Gaussian distributions described in
Definition

2.9
, then with high probability over the draw of the semirandom instance, i.e., the randomness of the interactions
\{b_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}}
, it holds that
\mathrm{algval}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
.

First and foremost, we highlight the algorithm of
Theorem

1.2
is entirely classical, so the result can be interpreted as saying the ground energy of
semirandom
,
dense

k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians is
classically
easy to approximate. Intuitively, this provides a strong formalism of the idea that the entanglement structure of many-body systems is not robust to internal noise.

More explicitly, the existential version of
Theorem

1.2
(following from the Matrix Chernoff bound) immediately implies that the ground energy of semirandom
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians concentrates around
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
and therefore can be approximated by a product state, since a random product state achieves value
\frac{1}{2}
. As a result, a simple product state ansatz serves to show the low energy space of these Hamiltonians has an
\mathsf{NP}
witness with high probability. Nonetheless, since maximizing over product states is not believed to be efficient, this fails to give an algorithm. Our ansatz is instead the spectral norm of a Kikuchi matrix that counts a class of Pauli operator walks, computable classically efficiently for dense instances, giving us an efficient certificate of low ground energy. Another interpretation of
Theorem

1.2
is then as a testable verifier that such a random product state is in fact a permissible approximation for the ground state. We can compare this to works like
[
BH13
,
AJT19
]
, which show Hamiltonians whose interaction hypergraph is a sufficient spectral expander can have their ground state approximated by a product state. Our result has no such assumption on the underlying graph structure, but instead has the assumption swapped for randomness in the signs, allowing it to succeed for certain families of semirandom Hamiltonians on potentially high-threshold rank graphs that otherwise seem to trick Sum-of-Squares.

Since the model here is average-case, this has no direct implications for worst-case approximation, but draws a tight comparison between the Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
and classical
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
refutation models, for which the analogous result of
Theorem

1.2
holds. It also provides an interesting classical certificate or ansatz for Hamiltonians generally.

As a special case of our analysis, we also obtain the following refutation algorithm for Gaussian signed Boolean polynomials, akin to refutation for semirandom classical
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}

Overall, this provides a satisfying explanation for the tradeoff observed in

Theorem

provides a systematic way to derive matching non-commutative Sum-of-Squares lower bounds from their classical counterpart by embedding hard instances of

\mathsf{XOR}

While

1.2
serves as evidence of a no-go for stronger semirandom certification, it falls short of blocking certification for random Hamiltonians due to the one-basis assumption, and this assumption is important in constructing the perfect completeness integrality gaps ruling out weak refutation, coming from the fact that anti-commuting Paulis cannot be frustration-free, and truly random

-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians have many anti-commuting pairs. Moreover, non-commutative Sum-of-Squares “knows of” this fact (see

) so can detect unsatisfiability in low-degree this way, a phenomenon not present in classical CSPs. In other words, non-commutativity actually helps refutation, which is why the hard instances for non-commutative Sum-of-Squares actually come from commuting Hamiltonians. This allows it to “trivially” break the refutation threshold (the observed tradeoff in
Theorem

1.2
) by taking advantage of non-commutativity in the fully random setting, showing semirandom is a harder task than random for non-commutative Sum-of-Squares in the Hamiltonian case, in a departure from the classical version. An interesting question we leave open is to show the correct threshold for refutation of truly random Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
or semirandom models with guaranteed amounts of non-commutativity, like the SYK model.

.

Semirandom Hamiltonians.
In a separate vein,
[
CDB+24
]
studies randomly signed sparse Hamiltonians, with their main model essentially corresponding to
Definition

1.1
with
k=n
for both Rademacher and Gaussian series, giving evidence that this regime is classically hard but quantumly easy. In statistical physics, typically concrete dense or geometric models resembling Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
are often studied in the spin glass literature to understand energy configurations of (potentially quantum) interacting particles. In the standard
p
-spin Ising model, where one has
p=k
, the interaction coefficients
b_{C}
typically represent the ferromagnetism or antiferromagnetism of the interaction in the Rademacher case, and it is not uncommon to study semirandom models where these interactions are random Rademacher or Gaussian and the interacting particles are often chosen particularly on a lattice to model physically relevant systems. One popular model to study, the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, can be seen as a dense classical
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
like Hamiltonian with i.i.d. Gaussian interactions

. The goal in these settings is usually to nail down exact behavior (down to constants) of these models, and it has been shown even

levels of Sum-of-Squares is known to fail at certifying the energy of this model
[
GJJ+20
]
precisely. Another common system called the SYK model has received similar attention in
[

,
ACKK25
]
shows a similar failure of classical ansatz to capture the exact maximum energy of the associated Hamiltonian. Our approach, which is roughly captured by Sum-of-Squares, is similarly not amenable to exact results of this form. This is to say, while our ansatz provides intuition that semirandom interactions can destroy entanglement in constant-sized energy gaps, our results do not extend down to subconstant gaps, which remain an interesting avenue of study in understanding the behavior of ground and thermal states of quantum many-body systems and potential quantum supremacy.

[

]
shows that a problem called

2.1.1

Definition 2.3

Fact 2.4
.

The Pauli operators are involutory,
X^{2}=Y^{2}=Z^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
, Hermitian, i.e.
X^{\dagger}=X
, and pairwise anti-commute, i.e.
XZ=-ZX
.

The most important fact of the Pauli operators is that they form a basis for Hermitian operators, which allows us to decompose any Hamiltonian as a sum of Paulis.

Definition 2.5

(Pauli basis)
.

The Pauli operators
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
under scalar field
\mathbb{C}
form an orthogonal basis for operators in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
under the inner product
\langle P,Q\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mbox{\rm Tr}(P^{\dagger}Q)
. As a consequence, we can write
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
as

\mathbf{H}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot P\,.

The
\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle
are called the Pauli coefficients and we shorthand them as
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)
. If all Pauli coefficients are real then
\mathbf{H}
is Hermitian and generally
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
form a basis for Hermitian operators in
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
when over
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.6

(Operator weight)
.

A Pauli operator
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
has
\deg(P)=k
if it acts with a non-identity operator on exactly
k
qubits. In general a matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
has
\deg(M)=k
if its non-zero Pauli coefficients
\widehat{M}(P)
are all on operators with
\deg(P)\leq k
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{k}(n)
the set of degree-
k
Pauli operators on
n
qubits. Note that a
k
-local Hamiltonian decomposes into degree-
k
Pauli operators by above. Given two operators
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, we say
P\sqsubseteq Q
if whenever
Q
is a non-identity operator on qubit
i
,
P
acts the same on that qubit.

2.2
Sum-of-Squares hierarchy

In order to build some intuition for non-commutative Sum-of-Squares, we briefly recall the setup of the standard Sum-of-Squares algorithm over the Boolean hypercube. Suppose we have a real polynomial
p
that we are interested in maximizing over. A kind of odd way to write this is as:

\displaystyle\max_{\mu}\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\mu}{\left[p\right]}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mu(x)\cdot p(x)

subject to

\displaystyle\mu\text{ a distribution over $\{0,1\}^{n}\,.$}

Note this indeed matches the max of
p
by taking
\mu
to be supported on
p
’s optimizers. The Sum-of-Squares algorithm relaxes the notion of distribution to pseudo-distribution, which are not true distributions but roughly look like them on low degree moments.

Definition 2.7

(Pseudo-expectations/distributions over the hypercube)
.

A degree-
d
pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}
over a pseudo-distribution
\mu
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is a linear operator that maps degree
\leq d
real polynomials on
\{0,1\}^{n}
into real numbers with the following three properties:

1.

(Normalization)
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}[1]=1
.

2.

(Booleanity) For any
x_{i}
and any polynomial
p
of degree
\leq d-2
,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}[p(x_{i}^{2}-x_{i})]=0
.

3.

(Positivity) For any polynomial
p
of degree at most
d/2
,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}[p^{2}]\geq 0
.

The degree-
d
Sum-of-Squares relaxation is then:

\displaystyle\max_{\mu}\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}{\left[p\right]}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mu(x)\cdot p(x)

subject to

\displaystyle\mu\text{ a degree-$d$ pseudo-distribution over $\{0,1\}^{n}\,.$}

2.2.1
Non-commutative Sum-of-Squares

If we are instead interested in computing the maximum energy state of a Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}
, we generalize from probability distributions to maximization over density operators:

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\rho)\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}})=\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{i\sim\lambda(\rho)}{\left[\bra{\psi_{i}}\mathbf{H}\ket{\psi_{i}}\right]}

subject to

\displaystyle\rho\text{ a density operator on $(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\,.$}

In the same vein as typical Sum-of-Squares, the non-commutative Sum-of-Squares hierarchy relaxes the notion of density operator to pseudo-density operator.

Definition 2.8

(Pseudo-density operators)
.

A degree-
d
pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
operator defined for pseudo-density operator
\rho
on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
is the linear operator
\mathbf{H}\mapsto\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}\rho)
satisfying the properties:

1.

(Normalization)
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}[\mathbbm{I}]=1
.

2.

(Positivity) For any operator
\mathbf{H}
acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
of degree at most
d/2
,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}]\geq 0
.

The degree-
d
non-commutative Sum-of-Squares relaxation is then written as

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\;\;\;

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\rho)\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}})=\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{i\sim\lambda(\rho)}{\left[\bra{\psi_{i}}\mathbf{H}\ket{\psi_{i}}\right]}

subject to

\displaystyle\rho\text{ a degree-$d$ pseudo-density operator on $(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\,.$}

2.3
Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}

In this section we define in greater depth Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
and prove a few auxiliary facts about the resulting family of Hamiltonians.

Definition 2.9

(Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
)
.

An instance of Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}

\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
where
\mathcal{H}
is a
k
-uniform hypergraph on
[n]
, each
P_{C}
is a succinctly described
n
qubit Pauli operator acting non-trivially only on
C
, and each
b_{C}
is a
\pm 1
-interaction coefficient. The instance
\mathcal{I}
defines a Hamiltonian on
n
qubits given by

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathbbm{I}+b_{C}P_{C}}{2}\,.

When
\mathcal{I}
is drawn from a distribution where each
b_{C}
is drawn independently and uniformly from
\{\pm 1\}
we say
\mathcal{I}
is (Rademacher) semirandom. If additionally
\mathcal{H}
is a uniformly random hypergraph of fixed size, we say that
\mathcal{I}
is random. If each
b_{C}
is drawn independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, we say
\mathcal{I}
is Gaussian semirandom. Finally, if all
P_{C}
are
Z
-type operators, i.e.
P_{C}=\bigotimes_{i\in C}Z_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in[n]\setminus C}\mathbbm{I}_{i}
, then we say
\mathcal{I}
is in the
Z
-basis, and analogously for
X
and
Y
. More generally, if the Hamiltonian is diagonal in any basis on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
we call it a single-basis or one-basis Hamiltonian.

Fact 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits defining
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Then
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Note
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}=\frac{\mathbbm{I}}{2}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}P_{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\mathbbm{I}}{2}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}
, so
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})=\frac{1}{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}})
. We show the fact by probabilistic method via a distribution
\mu
over states
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
such that
\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\mu}{\left[\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}\right]}=0
. In particular, let
\mu_{\text{Haar}}
be the single qubit Haar measure and
\mu=\mu_{\text{Haar}}^{\otimes n}
. It follows that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\psi\sim\mu}{\left[\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H},i\in C}b_{C}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\varphi\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}{\left[\bra{\varphi}(P_{C})_{i}\ket{\varphi}\right]}=0\,.

Here
(P_{C})_{i}
means the Pauli operator on qubit
i
. The last equality follows from the fact that a uniform Haar state has expectation
0
over any Pauli operator.
∎

Fact 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a semirandom Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits defining
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Then if
{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq 2(n+1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log(1/\delta)
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

The result requires the following matrix concentration bound.

Lemma 2.12

(Matrix Rademacher/Gaussian series
[
Tro15
]
)
.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
be a sequence of Hermitian complex matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and

A=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}A_{i}\,,

where
b_{i}
are i.i.d. from
\{\pm 1\}
or
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for
i=1,...,m
. Let
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(A)=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}^{2}\right\rVert}_{2}
. Then

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(A)\geq t\right]}\leq 2d\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(A)}\right)\,.

Considering the sequence
\{P_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}}
and
\mathbf{H}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}P_{C}
,
Lemma

2.12
yields

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H})\geq\varepsilon{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\right]}\leq 2^{n+1}\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}{2}\right)\,.

We use here that the Pauli operators are involutory to get
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(P)=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\right\rVert}_{2}={\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}
. By letting
{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq 2(n+1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log(1/\delta)
for any
\delta>0
of our choosing, this probability becomes at most
\delta
.
∎

2.4
Binomial coefficient inequalities

Fact 2.13
.

Let
n,\ell,q
be positive integers with
\ell\leq n
. Let
q
be constant and
\ell,n
be asymptotically large with
\ell\leq n/2
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{{n\choose\ell-q}}{{n\choose\ell}}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{\ell}{n}\right)^{q}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{{n-q\choose\ell}}{{n\choose\ell}}=\Theta(1)\,.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\frac{{n\choose\ell-q}}{{n\choose\ell}}=\frac{{\ell\choose q}}{{n-\ell+q\choose q}}\,.

Using that
\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{b}\leq{a\choose b}\leq\left(\frac{ea}{b}\right)^{b}
finishes the proof of the first equation.

We also have that

\displaystyle\frac{{n-q\choose\ell}}{{n\choose\ell}}=\frac{(n-q)!(n-\ell)!}{n!(n-\ell-q)!}=\prod_{i=0}^{q-1}\frac{n-\ell-i}{n-i}=\prod_{i=0}^{q-1}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n-i}\right)\,,

and this is
\Theta(1)
since
\ell\leq n/2
and
q
is constant.
∎

3
Certifying Semirandom
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians for even
k

In this section, we prove the case of
Theorem

1.2
where
k
is even using the Kikuchi matrix method. Our certification algorithm largely follows the framework of
[
GKM22
,
HKM23
]
for refuting semirandom
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instances, with our main new ingredient being a spectral certificate in the form of a novel Kikuchi matrix built for Hamiltonians as opposed to classical CSPs, which we introduce here. The Kikuchi matrix we give here is crafted for Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
, but it can be defined more generally for any Hamiltonian, and we give some intuition and a derivation of such in
Appendix

A
.

Definition 3.1

(Even-arity Kikuchi matrix for Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
even and
k/2\leq\ell\leq n/2
. Let
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
be a weight-
k
Pauli operator. We define the following adjacency matrix on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
:

A_{P}(Q,R)=\begin{cases}1&Q^{\dagger}R=P\text{ and }{\left|\mathrm{supp}(Q)\cap\mathrm{supp}(R)\right|}=\ell-k/2\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits. The level-
\ell
Kikuchi matrix of
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
is then defined by

K_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}=\frac{1}{{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}A_{P_{C}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A^{*}_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\,.

We call the graph built from
A_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}{\left|b_{C}\right|}A_{P_{C}}
the Kikuchi graph.

It is important, to apply the trace moment method, that our Kikuchi matrix is Hermitian, which we establish here.

Observation 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits describing
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Then
K_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}
is symmetric and the underlying graph is undirected.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
A_{P}
is symmetric for any
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
. To see this, we argue that each pair
Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
with
Q^{\dagger}R=P
commute. The main observation is since
{\left|\mathrm{supp}(Q)\cap\mathrm{supp}(R)\right|}=\ell-k/2
, there must be
k
qubits for which only one of
Q
and
R
are non-trivial. To fulfill
Q^{\dagger}R=P
, it must be the case these qubits are exactly those in
\mathrm{supp}(P)
, half from
Q
and half from
R
. The remaining parts of
Q
and
R
must be identical in order to cancel, giving us
R^{\dagger}Q=P
as well.
∎

3.1
Step 1: Expressing
\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}
as a quadratic form of a Kikuchi matrix

As the core of the Kikuchi matrix method, we show how the Kikuchi matrix captures Hamiltonian optimization directly in its quadratic forms.

Observation 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits describing
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Let
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
be a pure state. Consider
\psi^{\odot\ell}
in
((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n})^{\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}
defined by
\psi^{\odot\ell}_{P}=P\ket{\psi}
. Then

\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\Delta}(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}K_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}\psi^{\odot\ell}\,,

where
\Delta={k\choose k/2}{n-k\choose\ell-k/2}3^{\ell-k/2}
.

Proof.

For a Kikuchi matrix
K=A^{*}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
of a basis Pauli operator
P
we can write

\displaystyle(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}K\psi^{\odot\ell}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\rm Tr}((A^{*}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\cdot\psi^{\odot\ell}(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}\mbox{\rm Tr}((A^{*}_{Q,R}\cdot\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\cdot(\psi_{Q}^{\odot\ell}(\psi_{R}^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}\mathbbm{1}(Q^{\dagger}R=P)\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\cdot Q\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}\mathbbm{1}(Q^{\dagger}R=P)\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}(P\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})

\displaystyle=\Delta\bra{\psi}P\ket{\psi}\,.

In the second line, we are using the fact that the trace of a product is the sum of the Hadamard product to rewrite
\mbox{\rm Tr}(K\cdot\psi^{\odot\ell}(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger})
as a sum over submatrix products. In the fourth line, we use the cyclicity of the trace and the fact
Q
and
R
commute. We get the count
\Delta
by counting how many pairs
(Q,R)\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}^{n}\times\mathcal{P}_{\ell}^{n}
have
Q^{\dagger}R=P
. As in
3.2
, we note that
Q
and
R
must both act non-trivially on
\ell-k/2
qubits and disjointly on the
k
qubits
P
acts on. We choose from (1)
{k\choose k/2}
choices of which qubits of
P
that
Q
acts non-trivially on, (2)
{n-k\choose\ell-k/2}
choices of which qubits not of
P
that
Q
and
R
both act non-trivially on, and (3)
3^{\ell-k/2}
which non-trivial operator
\{X,Y,Z\}
each of the latter operators use. We finish by computing

\displaystyle(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}K_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}\psi^{\odot\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}\cdot(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}K_{P_{C}}\psi^{\odot\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}\bra{\psi}b_{C}P_{C}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\Delta\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}\ket{\psi}\,.

∎

Given this relationship, it suffices to provide a spectral norm bound for the Kikuchi matrix when the underlying Hamiltonian instance is semirandom, which we do using the trace moment method. In order to do so, we need to establish a few more basic facts about the Kikuchi matrix. For starters, it turns out to be much easier to get a tight bound on the Hamiltonian’s energy after applying a particular regularization process to the Kikuchi graph, which we define here.

Definition 3.4

(Degree-regularized Kikuchi matrix)
.

Let
K=A^{*}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
be a level-
\ell
Kikuchi matrix. Let
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)\times\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}
be defined as
\Gamma=D+d\mathbbm{I}
where
D
is the diagonal degree matrix of the unsigned
A
and
d=\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{P\sim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}{\left[\deg(P)\right]}
is the average degree. The degree-regularized Kikuchi matrix is then

\tilde{K}=\Gamma^{-1/2}A^{*}\Gamma^{-1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\,.

To use this regularization effectively, we want the following bound on the average degree (or number of non-zero entries) in a row/column in
A
.

Observation 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits describing
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
and let
K_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}
be the Kikuchi matrix. For
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
we define the graph degree according to the Kikuchi graph
A_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}}
. Then
\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{P\sim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}[\deg(P)]\geq\left({\frac{\ell}{3n}}\right)^{k/2}\cdot{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}
.

Proof.

Each
C\in\mathcal{H}
contributes
\Delta
to the total degree, so the average degree is
\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{P\sim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}[\deg(P)]=\frac{{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta}{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)\right|}}
. We then have

\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{P\sim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}[\deg(P)]=\frac{\Delta}{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)\right|}}\cdot{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}=\frac{3^{\ell-k/2}{k\choose k/2}{n-k\choose\ell-k/2}}{3^{\ell}{n\choose\ell}}\cdot{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq\left({\frac{\ell}{3n}}\right)^{k/2}\cdot{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\,,

where the last inequality follows from
2.13
.
∎

Previously it was known how to build Kikuchi matrices for any finite Abelian group
[
KM25
]
. Our Kikuchi matrix on the other hand is built on the non-Abelian group of the Pauli operators. Nonetheless, the above shows we only ever take edges between commuting operators.

3.2
Step 2: Bounding the spectral norm of
\tilde{K}
via the trace moment method

We are now ready to state our main technical component, which is a spectral bound on the underlying Kikuchi adjacency matrix using the trace moment method, and use it to prove the even case of
Theorem

1.2
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance on
n
qubits and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
the associated Hamiltonian. Define
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}-\frac{\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}}{2}
. Let
\tilde{K}_{\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}}=\Gamma^{-1/2}A^{*}\Gamma^{-1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
be the level-
\ell
degree-regularized Kikuchi matrix, as defined in
Definition

3.1
, for
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Suppose additionally that the interaction coefficients
\{b_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}}
are drawn independently and uniformly from
\{\pm 1\}
or they are drawn as independent standard Gaussians, i.e., the instance
\mathcal{I}
is semirandom as in
Definition

2.9
. Then, with probability
\geq 1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, it holds that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma^{-1/2}A^{*}\Gamma^{-1/2}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\ell\log n}{d}}\right)\,.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
for even
k
.

For any unit norm state
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
we have
\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}
. Let
\tilde{A}=\Gamma^{-1/2}A^{*}\Gamma^{-1/2}
. Note by
3.3
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta}\cdot(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}K_{\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}}\psi^{\odot\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta}\cdot(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})(\tilde{A}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})(\Gamma^{1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\psi^{\odot\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta}\cdot\lVert\tilde{A}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\rVert_{2}\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(\Gamma^{1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\psi^{\odot\ell}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta}\cdot\lVert\tilde{A}\rVert_{2}\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}(\Gamma)\,.

The last step follows from the multiplicativity of spectral norm across tensor product and the fact

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(\Gamma^{1/2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\psi^{\odot\ell}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}=(\psi^{\odot\ell})^{\dagger}(\Gamma\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\psi^{\odot\ell}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}(\psi^{\odot\ell}_{P})^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{P,P}\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}})\psi_{P}^{\odot\ell}=\mbox{\rm Tr}(\Gamma)\,.

Here we use the fact that
P^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
. Now we note
\mbox{\rm Tr}(\Gamma)=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}\deg(P)+d=2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\Delta
which is just twice the total degree. Putting it all together and invoking
Lemma

3.6
we have

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})=\max_{\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}}\bra{\psi}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\ket{\psi}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\lVert\tilde{A}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\ell\log n}{d}}\right)\,.

Finally, we recall
3.5
that
d\geq\left(\frac{\ell}{3n}\right)^{k/2}\cdot{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}
and observe that if
{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq O(1)\cdot n\log n\left(\frac{3n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1}\varepsilon^{-2}
for a sufficiently large universal constant, the bound becomes

∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.6
.

By
3.2
, we have that
\lVert\tilde{A}\rVert_{2}\leq\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})^{1/2r}
for any positive integer
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. We view
A
as a random matrix and by Markov’s inequality establish

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})\geq N\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})]\right]\leq\frac{1}{N}\,.

Let
N={\left|\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)\right|}
. We note this event is the same as
\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})^{1/2r}\geq N^{1/2r}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})]^{1/2r}
, and for
2r\geq\log N
we have
N^{1/2r}\leq O(1)
. This immediately gives us that with probability
\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}
,
\lVert\tilde{A}\rVert_{2}\leq O\left(\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})]^{1/2r}\right)
. We then have that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\left(\Gamma^{-1}A\right)^{2r}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\left(\Gamma^{-1}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}A_{P_{C}}\right)^{2r}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}}\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}\cdot b_{C_{i}}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}\cdot b_{C_{i}}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2r}b_{C_{i}}\right]\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)\,.

Now we make the following observation. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}
be a term in the above sum. Fix
C\in\mathcal{H}
and count the number of times
r
that
C=C_{i}
in the sequence above. Assuming we are in the
\{\pm 1\}
case, observe that if
m
is even, then
\operatorname{{\bf E}}{\left[b_{C_{i}}^{m}\right]}=1
in the product above when
b_{C_{i}}\sim\{\pm 1\}
. If
m
is odd, we instead have
\operatorname{{\bf E}}{\left[b_{C_{i}}^{m}\right]}=\operatorname{{\bf E}}{\left[b_{C_{i}}\right]}=0
. Similarly, in the Gaussian case the moments look as follows.

Fact 3.7

(Moments of a standard Gaussian)
.

Let
b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then for any
r\geq 1

\operatorname{{\bf E}}{\left[b^{m}\right]}=\begin{cases}(m-1)!!&\text{$m$ is even}\\
0&\text{$m$ is odd}\end{cases}

Once again the odd moments are
0
, but in this case the even moments increase with
(m-1)!!
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.8

(Trivially closed sequences)
.

Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}
. We say that
C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}
is trivially closed with respect to
C
if
C
appears an even number of times in the sequence. We say that the sequence is trivially closed if it is trivially closed with respect to all
C\in\mathcal{H}
. Further, we say the sequence is a closed
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walk if
q
distinct
C_{i}
appear and after collating their non-zero multiplicities
m_{i}
we get the set
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
. A trivially closed sequence is one in which the walk is closed and all
m_{i}
for
i\in[q]
are even.

With the above definition in hand, we argue in the Gaussian case:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r\\
\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\text{a }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2r}b_{C_{i}}\right]\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r\\
\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\text{a }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)\,.

In the first line, we observe if any
C_{i}
appears without even multiplicity, the whole term contributes
0
. We then categorize the walks by the (indistinguishable) repetitions of each hyperedge, which tells us the corresponding Gaussian moments across the entire walk. From here, observe the trace term is now unsigned, so all terms are positive, so the
\{\pm 1\}
coefficient case is yields the exact same expression with the Gaussian moments reduced to
1
. For this reason, it suffices to bound this term to finish both cases. The following lemma yields the desired bound.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r\\
\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\text{a }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)\leq 8^{2r}\cdot N\left(\frac{2r}{d}\right)^{r}\,.

With
Lemma

3.9
, we bound
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mbox{\rm Tr}((\Gamma^{-1}A)^{2r})]
. Taking

to be

for a sufficiently large universal constant and applying Markov’s inequality finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.9
.

We bound the sum as follows. First, note that the number of ways to choose even
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r
is the same as choosing ways to add positive integers to
r
, which is called the integer partition
p(r)
. A classical bound gives
p(r)\leq(e^{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}})^{\sqrt{r}}
which is simply less than
4^{2r}
, so we can pay this factor and look to bound the maximum sum term across all even choices of
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
.

Now, we observe that for a closed sequence
C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}
, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)=\sum_{Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{2r-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)}\prod_{i=0}^{2r-1}\Gamma^{-1}_{Q_{i},Q_{i}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left(Q_{i}\cdot Q_{i+1}=P_{C_{i}}\right)\,,

where we define
Q_{2r}=Q_{0}
. Thus, the sum we wish to bound in
Lemma

3.9
simply counts the total weight of closed
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walks
Q_{0},C_{1},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{2r-1},C_{2r},Q_{2r}
(where
Q_{2r}=Q_{0}
) in the Kikuchi graph
A
, where the weight of a walk is simply
\prod_{i=0}^{2r-1}\Gamma^{-1}_{Q_{i},Q_{i}}
, and then multiplies the whole thing by
\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!
. Intuitively, when the
m_{i}
are large, the prefactor
(m_{i}-1)!!
grows and gives the corresponding walk a higher weight than we see in the Rademacher case. Our hope is to offset this by showing walks with such a larger
m_{i}
are less frequent.

Let us now bound this total weight by uniquely encoding a
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walk
Q_{0},C_{1},\dots,C_{2r},Q_{2r}
as follows.

•

First, we choose the template for the walk, which is the way in which the indices
1,\dots,2r
are related such that the multiplicities indeed fulfill a
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walk. Formally, we can let a template be a partition
T
of the indices
1,\dots,2r
into indistinguishable buckets of sizes
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
.

•

Second, we write down the start vertex
Q_{0}
.

•

For
i=1,\dots,2r
, if
i
is the first index in its bucket, we choose an edge
C_{i}
from the neighbors of
Q_{i-1}
to walk along. If
i
is not the first index in its bucket, then it is determined completely by whatever
C
we chose previously, since the template condition enforces equality.

With the above encoding, we can now bound the total weight of closed
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walks as follows. First, let us consider the total weight of walks for some fixed choice of template
T
. We have
N
choices for the start vertex
Q_{0}
. For each
i=1,\dots,2r
if
i
is the first index in its bucket, we have
\deg(Q_{i-1})
choices for
Q_{i}
, and we multiply by a weight of
\Gamma^{-1}_{Q_{i-1},Q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\deg(Q_{i-1})}
, so the contribution to the product is
1
. For each
i=1,\dots,2r
where
i
is not the first in its buckets, its value is predetermined, but we still multiply by a weight of
\Gamma^{-1}_{Q_{i-1},Q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{1}{d}
. Hence, the total weight for walks coming from a set template is at most
N\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2r-q}
, since there are
2r-q
indices that are not first in one of the
q
buckets.

Now let
{2r\choose\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}}
count the number of ways to partition
[2r]
into indistinguishable buckets
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
. We aim to bound the maximum of

\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\cdot{2r\choose\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}}\cdot N\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2r-q}\,,

across all even choices for
\{m_{i}\}_{i\in[q]}
. Observe that
\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!
counts exactly the number of perfect matchings within the
q
buckets, so in total the first two terms count how to partition
[2r]
in the buckets and then match them. Another way to count this is to first perfectly match
[2r]
and then partition the
r
edges into
\{m_{1}/2,\dots,m_{q}/2\}
buckets. The standard
(2r-1)!!
count for number of perfect matchings on
[2r]
yields

(2r-1)!!\cdot{r\choose\{m_{1}/2,\dots,m_{q}/2\}}\cdot N\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2r-q}\,.

We aim to bound
{r\choose\{m_{1}/2,\dots,m_{q}/2\}}\leq 2^{r}(2r)^{r-q}
regardless of the choice of
\{m_{i}\}_{i\in[q]}
. To do this, we encode a partition as follows. Scanning through
i=1,\dots,r
construct a string
z\in\{0,1\}^{r}
by letting
z_{i}=0
if
i
is the first element in its bucket, and
z_{i}=1
otherwise. Now for each
z_{i}=1
, we specify the first element with
z_{i}=1
whose bucket it shares. Assuming there are
q
buckets, we can specify this element uniquely with only
q
symbols. Thus there are at most
2^{r}
choices for
z
and only
q^{r-q}
choices for how the remainder sort in. Since
q\leq r
,
2^{r}(2r)^{r-q}
serves as an upper bound. Putting it all together we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r\\
\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\text{a }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{2r}\max_{\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1},\dots,C_{2r}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\text{a }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma^{-1}A_{P_{C_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{2r}(2r-1)!!\cdot{r\choose\{m_{1}/2,\dots,m_{q}/2\}}\cdot N\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2r-q}

\displaystyle\leq 8^{2r}\cdot N\left(\frac{2r}{d}\right)^{2r-q}\,.

In the last line we use
(2r-1)!!\leq(2r)^{r}
and
{r\choose\{m_{1}/2,\dots,m_{q}/2\}}\leq 2^{r}(2r)^{r-q}
. To conclude we just notice
q\leq r
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}})^{2}

\ket{\psi}\text{ pure state}\end{subarray}}{\left|\mathcal{U}\right|}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\bra{\psi}P_{U}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}_{U}}b_{C}P_{\widetilde{C}}\ket{\psi}^{2}

\ket{\psi}\text{ pure state}\end{subarray}}{\left|\mathcal{U}\right|}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{U}}b_{C}b_{C^{\prime}}\bra{\psi}P_{\widetilde{C}}P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}\,.

Since the output guarantees of

It suffices to prove the auxiliary lemmas,

Proposition 4.8

For any pair

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\left(\Gamma_{t}^{-1}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}^{(t)}}\sum_{C\neq C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{(t)}_{U}}b_{C}b_{C^{\prime}}A^{U}_{C,C^{\prime}}\right)^{2r}\right)\right]

Definition 4.14

(Trivially closed sequence)
.

Lemma 4.15

•

•

\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,(C_{2r},C_{2r}^{\prime})\\

\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2r}b_{C_{i}}b_{C_{i}^{\prime}}\right]\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma_{t}^{-1}A^{U_{i}}_{C_{i},C_{i}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2r+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r\\
\forall i\in[q],m_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,(C_{2r},C_{2r}^{\prime})\\
\in{\left\{(\mathcal{H}^{(t)}_{U})^{\otimes 2}\right\}}_{U\in\mathcal{U}^{(t)}}\\
\text{top }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma_{t}^{-1}A^{U_{i}}_{C_{i},C_{i}^{\prime}}\right)\,.

To introduce the term
2^{2r}\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!
, we argue the following. By the top
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}
-walk assumption, we know the multiplicities of half of the
b_{C}
, and let
\{m_{1}^{\prime},\dots,m_{q^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
be the multiplicities of the remaining bottom half. The natural prefactor should perhaps be
\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}-1)!!\prod_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}(m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!
, but this fails to account for the fact that some groups of coefficients may
repeat
across the top and bottom half. Thinking adversarial, we can increase the value of the prefactor by merging groups of size
m_{i}
and
m_{j}^{\prime}
for
i\in[q]
and

, thus trading
(m_{i}-1)!!(m_{j}^{\prime}-1)!!
for
(m_{i}+m_{j}^{\prime}-1)!!
. We make two observations now: (1) merging groups is always advantageous as long as we assume in the worst case
m_{i}+m_{j}^{\prime}
is always even and (2) we can only ever merge pairs, since by assumption the types are always distinct within the top and bottom. The real prefactor is then
\prod_{i=1}^{\max(q,q^{\prime})}(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!
up to reordering the indices, which we bound with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
m_{1},\dots,m_{q}
and
m_{1}^{\prime},\dots m_{q^{\prime}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}m_{i}^{\prime}=2r
. Then,

\prod_{i=1}^{\max(q,q^{\prime})}(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!\leq 2^{2r}\cdot\max\left(\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!,\prod_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}m_{i}^{\prime}!\right)\,.

Here if
m_{i}
has
i>q
then we interpret it to be
0
and vice versa.

Applying the lemma immediately gives the inequality exhibited above, so we give a proof here.

Proof of
Lemma

4.17
.

Without loss of generality, assume
q\leq q^{\prime}
, then we write
\prod_{i=1}^{\max(q,q^{\prime})}(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!=\prod_{i=1}^{q}(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!\cdot\prod_{i=q+1}^{q^{\prime}}(m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!
. Fix
m_{i},m_{i}^{\prime}
for
i\in[q]
such that
m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}
is even. In this case we have

(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!\leq(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime})!!=2^{\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}}\left(\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}\right)!\leq 2^{\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}}\sqrt{m_{i}!m_{i}^{\prime}!}\,.

In the last line we appeal to the weak log-convexity of the factorial function. Similarly, if
m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}
is odd we have

(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!\leq 2^{\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1}{2}}\left(\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1}{2}\right)!\leq 2^{\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}}\sqrt{m_{i}!m_{i}^{\prime}!}\,.

Finally, we observe for any
i>q
, we have
(m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!\leq\sqrt{m_{i}^{\prime}!}
by comparing terms. Combining these bounds, we write

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\max(q,q^{\prime})}(m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}-1)!!

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\max(q,q^{\prime})}2^{\frac{m_{i}+m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}}\sqrt{m_{i}!m_{i}^{\prime}!}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}m_{i}^{\prime}}{2}}\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!\prod_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}m_{i}^{\prime}!}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2r}\cdot\max\left(\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!,\prod_{i=1}^{q^{\prime}}m_{i}^{\prime}!\right)\,.

∎

We now turn our attention to bounding the sum in its entirety. As before, we can appeal to the
p(r)\leq(e^{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}})^{2r}\leq 16^{2r}
bound on the number of integer partitions
p(r)
to focus on the maximum of the inner term. To finish, we prove the following.

Lemma 4.18
.

For any choice of
m_{1},\dots,m_{q}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=2r
,

2^{2r+1}\prod_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,(C_{2r},C_{2r}^{\prime})\\
\in{\left\{(\mathcal{H}^{(t)}_{U})^{\otimes 2}\right\}}_{U\in\mathcal{U}^{(t)}}\\
\text{top }\{m_{1},\dots,m_{q}\}\text{-walk}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\Gamma_{t}^{-1}A^{U_{i}}_{C_{i},C_{i}^{\prime}}\right)\leq N\cdot 2^{6r+2}\cdot\left(\frac{4\eta_{t}r}{d}\right)^{r}\,.

Taking

to be
O(\log N)
for a sufficiently large universal constant and applying Markov’s inequality finishes the proof. ∎

Proof of
Lemma

.

As before, we have for any trivially closed sequence

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,(C_{2r},C_{2r}^{\prime})\\
\in{\left\{(\mathcal{H}^{(t)}_{U})^{\otimes 2}\right\}}_{U\in\mathcal{U}^{(t)}}\\

•

To see the upper bound then, note we can encode such a object as follows. Scanning through

∎

-
\mathsf{XOR}

Lemma 4.19
.

, this bound follows more immediately from the fact that
{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(t)}_{U}\right|}\leq\tau_{t}
. This allows us to bound the number of terms in the inner sum above nicely as

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{(Q,R)\sim E_{(C,C^{\prime})}}[(Q,R)\text{ deleted}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\eta}\cdot{k-t\choose\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{s=0}^{k-t}\sum_{{\left|I\right|}=s+t,I\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(C^{\prime})}\tau_{s+t}\min\left(1,\left(\frac{\ell}{3n}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-s}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2{k-t\choose\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor}{k\choose k/2}}{\eta}\sum_{s=0}^{k-t}\tau_{s+t}\min\left(1,\left(\frac{\ell}{3n}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-s}\right)\,.

Standard binomial estimates give that for some constant
D>0
the leading coefficient may be bound by
\frac{D^{k}}{\eta}
, which is exactly the bound we sought. We finish by proving
Lemma

4.20
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.20
.

For arbitrary
(C,C^{\prime})
we let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
be any vertex such that
Q\xrightarrow{P_{\widetilde{C}},P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}}R
. We are interested in the probability that there exists
R^{\prime}
with
Q\xrightarrow{P_{\widetilde{C}},P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}}R^{\prime}
for a fixed
C^{\prime\prime}
.

Taking a look at the structure of
(Q,R)
in
E_{C,C^{\prime}}
the conditions of
Q\xrightarrow{P_{\widetilde{C}},P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}}R
guarantee we have
Q^{(1)}
matches
P_{\widetilde{C}}
on either
\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor
or
\lceil\frac{k-t}{2}\rceil
of the tensored operators and
Q^{(2)}
matches
P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}
similarly. Additionally, it guarantees that
Q^{(1)}
and
Q^{(2)}
are
\mathbbm{I}
on all remaining operators intersecting supports with
P_{\widetilde{C}}
and
P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}
respectively. In total, this fixes
2(k-t)
of the operators in
Q
. Since
{\left|Q^{(1)}\right|}+{\left|Q^{(2)}\right|}=\ell
, there remains
\ell-k-t
free non-identity operators outside of these. Note these may appear anywhere that is not already fixed, the only condition is they match in
Q
and
R
to cancel out. We can view the process of sampling an edge, conditioned on these
2(k-t)
fixed operators, as simply sampling the free operators from
{[2n-2k-2t]\choose\ell-k-t}
and random operators from
\{X,Y,Z\}
for each one.

In order for
Q\xrightarrow{P_{\widetilde{C}},P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}}R^{\prime}
to occur, we necessarily have that
Q^{(2)}
matches
P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}
on at least
\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor
operators. Any matches within
P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}
we get for free since
Q^{(2)}
must already match, at least in the worst case. The
\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-{\left|P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}\right|}
remaining must be randomly chosen as per our sampling. Note the number of such terms is
\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-{\left|P_{C^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{C^{\prime\prime}}\right|}+t
since
{\left|P_{C^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{C^{\prime\prime}}\right|}={\left|P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}}\right|}+t
. Fix any choice of terms
T
, of which there are at most
{k-t\choose\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor}
options. We union bound over all possible
T
. The desired condition is met given (1)
T
is contained in the set of free operators and (2) all operators match those in
P_{C^{\prime\prime}}
. The probability of the latter is clearly
\frac{1}{3}^{\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-{\left|P_{C^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{C^{\prime\prime}}\right|}+t}
. The former can be seen as
{2n-2k-2t-\gamma\choose\ell-k-t-\gamma}/{2n-2k-2t\choose\ell-k-t}
letting
\gamma=\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-{\left|P_{C^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{C^{\prime\prime}}\right|}+t
and we can bound this as
\left(\frac{\ell}{3n}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{k-t}{2}\rfloor-{\left|P_{C^{\prime}}\sqcap P_{C^{\prime\prime}}\right|}+t}
given our binomial estimates
2.13
.
∎

Non-commutative Sum-of-Squares Lower Bounds for Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.5
, transforming Sum-of-Squares
\mathsf{XOR}
lower bounds to non-commutative Sum-of-Squares Hamiltonian
\mathsf{XOR}
lower bounds. Given the definition for non-commutative Sum-of-Squares Hamiltonian optimization in
Section

2.2
, all that is needed to show the algorithm fails is some instance
\mathcal{I}
for which
\text{val}(\mathcal{I})\approx\frac{1}{2}
and a pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
under which the Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}
has value

5.1
Warmup: Random one-basis
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians

We start with a warmup in proving non-commutative Sum-of-Squares lower bounds via
Theorem

1.4
. Our proof generalizes the low-width resolution framework of
[
Gri01
,
Sch08
]
for proving Sum-of-Squares lower bounds to the non-commutative setting.

Since random (one-basis)
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians typically have
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H})\approx\frac{1}{2}
, we just need a pseudo-expectation for such instances. More explicitly, we show:

Theorem 5.1

(
Theorem

1.4
restated)
.

Fix
k\geq 3
and
n\geq\ell\geq k
. Let
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}
be a random one-basis
n
-qubit
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonian described by
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
with
\Omega(n)\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\geq{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}\geq O(n)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}
. Then with large probability over the draw of
\mathcal{I}
it holds that:

1.

\mathrm{val}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
;

2.

There exists an
n
-qubit degree-
\frac{\ell\varepsilon^{2}}{2\log n}
pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
for which
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\right]}=1
.

This immediately implies that the algorithm of
Section

2.2
fails, giving
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof.

Item

1
follows immediately from the lower bound on
{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}
and
2.11
. To prove
Item

2
, we construct a pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
based on the principle of max-entropy. Since
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
must have
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\right]}=1
in the end, it requires
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[P_{C}\right]}=b_{C}
for each
C\in\mathcal{H}
, which intuitively corresponds to
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
believing that each
b_{C}P_{C}
simultaneously has value
1
under
\rho
. The principle of max-entropy says that we should enforce exactly
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[P_{C}\right]}=b_{C}
and any “hard” constraints that immediately follow from this and leave everything else unspecified (hence inducing the maximum entropy). For us, hard constraints are derived through the following fact.

Fact 5.2
.

Suppose
P,Q
be Pauli operators and
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
be a pure state. If
\bra{\psi}P\ket{\psi}=1
and
\bra{\psi}Q\ket{\psi}=1
then
\bra{\psi}PQ\ket{\psi}=1
.

Proof.

From
\bra{\psi}P\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}Q\ket{\psi}=1
we derive
P\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}Q
has an eigenvalue
+1
, and since it is rank
1
,
\mbox{\rm Tr}(P\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}Q)=1
and so
\bra{\psi}PQ\ket{\psi}=1
.
∎

After enforcing
\bra{\psi}b_{C}P_{C}\ket{\psi}=1
for all
C\in\mathcal{H}
in our pseudo-expectation, we propagate via
5.2
to all low-degree combinations of
b_{C}P_{C}
. Our pseudo-expectation is then defined through the following algorithm.

\widetilde{\Omega}(\ell)
-degree max-entropy pseudo-expectation for
\mathcal{I}

Input:
A Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
.

Output:
A valid degree-
d
pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}
with
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\right]}=1
.

Algorithm:

1.

Let
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}[\mathbbm{I}]=1
.

2.

For every
C\in\mathcal{H}
set
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}[P_{C}]=b_{C}
.

3.

Repeat the following until no progress can be made:

(a)

Choose
Q
and
R\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq d}(n)
with
\mathrm{weight}(Q^{\dagger}R)\leq d
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q\right]}\neq 0
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[R\right]}\neq 0
.

(b)

Set
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q^{\dagger}R\right]}=\overline{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q\right]}}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[R\right]}
.

(c)

If the value was previously something else, throw an error.

4.

For any remaining
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq d}(n)
not set, let
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q\right]}=0
.

Given that this algorithm does not error, it is clear that the max-entropy pseudo-expectation for
\mathcal{I}
is a normalized operator defined on all degree-
d
Pauli monomials, which can be extended by linearity to all Hamiltonians in light of the Pauli basis decomposition of
Definition

2.5
, and moreover
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}}\right]}=1
by construction. It remains to show (1) the algorithm does not error for random instances
\mathcal{I}
and (2)
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}
is indeed a pseudo-expectation, which reduces to showing the positivity condition of
Definition

2.7
.

We start by showing the max-entropy pseudo-expectation is well-defined. To do so, we need to define the following notion of high-dimensional expansion in hypergraphs.

Definition 5.3

(Boundary expansion in hypergraphs)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypergraph on
[n]
. Then
\mathcal{H}
is a
(\beta,d)
-small-set boundary expander if for every subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with
{\left|S\right|}\leq d
,
{\left|\bigoplus_{C\in S}C\right|}\geq\beta{\left|S\right|}
.

Boundary expanders, or more precisely one-basis Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instances with boundary expanding underlying hypergraphs, are the exact instances for which the max-entropy pseudo-expectation works.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
be a one-basis Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance such that
\mathcal{H}
is a
(\beta,d)
-small-set boundary expander. Then the degree-
\frac{\beta d}{2}
max-entropy pseudo-expectation for
\mathcal{I}
is well-defined.

Intuitively, expansion allows us to say that in order to find a contradiction in the max-entropy construction, we need to go through many variables and exceed the degree threshold, therefore the pseudo-expectation misses these in low degree and believes the instance is satisfiable. We would then like to show that random hypergraphs underlying our random instances are small-set boundary expanders.

Fact 5.5

(Theorem 4.12
[
KMOW17
]
; Theorem 7.12
[
KM25
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a random
k
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices and
O(n)\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1-\beta}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}
edges. Then
\mathcal{H}
is a
(\beta,\ell\varepsilon^{-2})
-small-set boundary expander with large probability.

Finally, once we have that the max-entropy pseudo-expectation is well-defined, we need that it is indeed a pseudo-expectation.

Lemma 5.6
.

Given an
n
qubit one-basis Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
, if the max-entropy operator
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}
for
\mathcal{I}
is well-defined, then
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}
satisfies positivity, that for any degree at most
d/2
operator
\mathbf{H}
acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\right]}\geq 0
.

Putting everything together, we have by
5.5
with
\beta=\frac{1}{\log n}
that random one-basis Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instances with at most
O(n)\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{k/2-1}
terms have
(\frac{1}{\log n},\ell\varepsilon^{-2})
-small-set boundary expansion with large probability. By
Lemma

5.4
, the degree-
\frac{\ell\varepsilon^{-2}}{2\log n}
max-entropy pseudo-expectation is well-defined, and by
Lemma

5.6
it is a valid pseudo-expectation.
∎

It remains to prove
Lemma

5.4
and
Lemma

5.6
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

Suppose that the degree-
\frac{\beta d}{2}
max-entropy pseudo-expectation is not well-defined for
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{(P_{C},b_{C})\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
, then there exists some Pauli
P
which we try to set
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P\right]}=1
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P\right]}=-1
, or rather there is a derivation in the algorithm of both. More explicitly, we say a derivation is the following.

Definition 5.7

(Derivations)
.

A degree-
d
derivation of an operator
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
is a sequence of operators
(Q_{1},...,Q_{t})\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
Q_{t}=P
with the properties:

1.

For all
i\in[t]
,
\mathrm{weight}(Q_{i})\leq d
.

2.

For all
i\in[t]
,
\exists j,k<i
such that
Q_{i}=Q_{j}\cdot Q_{k}
or
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
.

We call
t
the length of the derivation.

Remark 5.8
.

A degree-
d
derivation
D
of
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
inductively shows the existence of
S(D)\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with
\prod_{C\in S(D)}P_{C}=P
. We call
{\left|S(D)\right|}
the width of the derivation.

Observe that every step of the algorithm can be written as a new term in a derivation. Erroring implies there are two derivations
D,D^{\prime}
such that
\prod_{C\in D}P_{C}=\prod_{C\in D^{\prime}}P_{C}
and
\prod_{C\in D}b_{C}=-\prod_{C\in D^{\prime}}b_{C}
. Multiplying them together then yields a derivation
D^{\prime\prime}
for
\mathbbm{I}
such that
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbbm{I}\right]}=-1
. Since
D
and
D^{\prime}
are distinct (due to their opposite signs), this yields a non-trivial set
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
\prod_{C\in S}P_{C}=\mathbbm{I}
. Since all
P_{C}
are of the same type, this is equivalent to
\bigoplus_{C\in S}C=\varnothing
.

The upshot is that, by the assumption of
(\beta,d)
-small-set expansion, such an
S
must have
{\left|S\right|}>d
, otherwise this violates the expansion. Now our goal becomes to find a subsequence of
D
or
D^{\prime}
that has width in the interval
[d/2,d]
. By
(\beta,d)
-small-set expansion, such a derivation produces an operator of weight at least
\frac{\beta d}{2}
, which contradicts the assumption that the algorithm never looks at operators of weight above
\frac{\beta d}{2}
.

To see this then, just note that since
{\left|S\right|}>d
, one of
D
or
D^{\prime}
must have width at least
d/2
, since
S
is just the symmetric difference
S(D)\oplus S(D^{\prime})
. If it falls in
[d/2,d]
we are done, otherwise it must be larger than
d
, in which case we recurse by looking at the maximal subderivation. Since all derivations start at width
k\leq d
, we must eventually cross the interval
[d/2,d]
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.6
.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be an operator acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
with degree at most
d/2
. We define the following relation
\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}
on
\mathcal{P}_{\leq d/2}(n)
.

Definition 5.9

(Max-entropy equivalence on
\mathcal{P}_{\leq d/2}(n)
)
.

Let
Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq d/2}(n)
be related under
\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}
if
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q^{\dagger}R\right]}\neq 0
.

We prove that this is an equivalence relation here.

Proof.

Since
Q^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
, and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbbm{I}\right]}=1
,
Q\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}Q
. Similarly,
Q\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}R
results in
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q^{\dagger}R\right]}\neq 0
and by commuting/anti-commuting
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[R^{\dagger}Q\right]}\neq 0
and
R\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}Q
. For transitivity, observe that if
Q\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}R
,
R\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}T
then
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q^{\dagger}R\right]}\neq 0
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[R^{\dagger}T\right]}\neq 0
. Moreover, the algorithm must derive
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q^{\dagger}T\right]}\neq 0
and in degree at most
d
, and so
Q\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}T
.
∎

Now let
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[r]}
be the set of induced equivalence classes under
\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}
and write
\mathbf{H}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq d/2}(n)}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot P
and further partition based on the equivalence classes to get
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot P
. Observe that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\right]}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot P\right)^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot P\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\\
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}\end{subarray}}\overline{\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle}\cdot\langle\mathbf{H},P^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\overline{\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle}\cdot\langle\mathbf{H},P^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}\,,

with the last line following directly from the definition of
\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}
. We finish by choosing some representative element
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
for each equivalence class and observing that for any
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}=\overline{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}
. This follows since by the definition of
\sim_{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}}
, both
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}
are defined, so the algorithm uses them to derive
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}
. This allows us to rewrite

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\overline{\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle}\cdot\langle\mathbf{H},P^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\overline{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P^{\prime}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P\right]}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\langle\mathbf{H},P\rangle\cdot\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P\right]}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

We use here that
\overline{\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}}=\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[Q_{i}^{\dagger}P\right]}
. Since the only non-zero values are
\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
, this is equivalent to saying that
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}
is real if and only if
P
and
Q_{i}
commute. Suppose
P^{\dagger}Q_{i}
is a
\pm 1
-sign of some operator in
\{\mathbbm{I},X\}^{\otimes n}
. Note that this is equivalent to commutation. The algorithm only sets these operators to real values, so
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}
is real. Assume now that
P^{\dagger}Q_{i}
is a
\pm i
-sign of a
\{\mathbbm{I},X\}^{\otimes n}
. Since the base operator is real-valued,
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}{\left[P^{\dagger}Q_{i}\right]}
is then complex-valued.
∎

Doing a post-mortem of the above proof elucidates why random
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
Hamiltonians do not fool non-commutative Sum-of-Squares. Suppose some
P=b_{C}P_{C}
and
Q=b_{C^{\prime}}P_{C^{\prime}}
anti-commute. Multiplying
PQPQ=-\mathbbm{I}
and applying
5.2
yields that any ground state with energy
1
must have
-\bra{\psi}\mathbbm{I}\ket{\psi}=1
, a contradiction. Moreover, non-commutative Sum-of-Squares “knows” this, as the following shows.

Fact 5.10
.

Suppose
P,Q
be
k
-local Pauli operators that anti-commute. There is no degree-
2k
pseudo-expectation
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}
with
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[P\right]}=1
and
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[Q\right]}=1
.

Proof.

Consider
\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{3}(-P+Q+PQ)
. By positivity, any valid pseudo-expectation of degree-
2k
has
\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\right]}\geq 0
. However,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{9}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[3\mathbbm{I}-PQ-Q-QP+QPQ-Q+QPQ\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{9}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[3\mathbbm{I}-2P-2Q\right]}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{9}\,.

∎

5.2
Lifting Sum-of-Squares lower bounds

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.5
, lifting Sum-of-Squares lower bounds to non-commutative Sum-of-Squares lower bounds. The previous section showed how we build consistent pseudo-expectations for one-basis Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instances. Now, we show how to take a classical
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance and associate a one-basis Hamiltonian instance.

Theorem 5.11

(
Theorem

1.5
restated)
.

Fix
k\geq 2
. Given a classical
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{H},\{b_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{H}})
, we can compute in polynomial-time a description of the Hamiltonian
k
-
\mathsf{XOR}
instance

The mapping is from

It then suffices to show

Let

X^{\otimes n}

\ket{+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\

\ket{0}

This allows us to see that

Thus, the energy under

by putting all weight on some maximal

3
. We conclude
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}})=\mathrm{val}(\mathcal{I})
.
∎

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{J}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[\mathbbm{I}\right]}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\rho}{\left[Z_{C}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}{\left[x_{C}\right]}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf\tilde{E}}}_{\mu}{\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2{\left|\mathcal{H}\right|}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{H}}b_{C}\prod_{i\in C}x_{i}\right]}\,.

The last value is exactly the Boolean Sum-of-Squares relaxation for

It remains to show that

is a valid pseudo-expectation. Let

be an operator on

.

Definition 5.13

(
Z
-basis equivalence on

)
.

Let

\{\mathbbm{I},X\}^{\otimes n}
.

We prove that

\mathcal{P}_{\leq d/2}(n)

Proof.

Q^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
so
Q\sim Q
. If
Q\sim R

and

To see the bijection with

Appendix A
Deriving the Hamiltonian Kikuchi Matrix

In this section, we give some intuition on the Hamiltonian Kikuchi matrix. We build up to our construction by relating the matrix to the typical trace moment method analysis for bounding the maximum energy of a Hamiltonian.

Given a Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, we write its Pauli decomposition as
\mathbf{H}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)\cdot P
and apply the trace moment method, which allows us to bound
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{H})\leq\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2r})^{1/2r}
for some choice
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Computing this trace out gives the following expression

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2r})

\displaystyle=\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\left(\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)\cdot P\right)^{2r}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P_{1},\dots,P_{2r}\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P_{i})\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}P_{i}\right)\,.

In general, we can compute
\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}P_{i}\right)
efficiently using the stabilizer formalism for Pauli operators, so as long as the Hamiltonian has a sparse Pauli spectrum, a criteria satisfied by local Hamiltonians, the number of sequences to enumerate over is
\mathsf{poly}(n)^{2r}
. The only reason this fails to give an algorithm is that we must take
2r\gg n
for a
2^{n}
-dimensional operator in order for the trace moment method to succeed, meaning an exponential number of sequences.

To get around this issue, we relate this analysis to performing a random walk on the set of
n
-qubit Pauli operators, generalizing the heuristic level-
n
Kikuchi matrix seen in
[
WAM19
]
.

Definition A.1

(Level-
n
Kikuchi matrix for
k
-local Hamiltonians)
.

Let
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
be a Pauli operator. We define the following adjacency matrix on
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.

A_{P}(Q,R)=\begin{cases}1&Q^{\dagger}R=P\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We further extend the Kikuchi matrix of any operator
\mathbf{H}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
through linearity, yielding the level-
n
Kikuchi matrix

K_{\mathbf{H}}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)\cdot A_{P}\,.

Our Kikuchi matrix
K_{\mathbf{H}}
is still
2^{n}
-dimensional, and we attempt to fix this problem by counterintuitively defining an
even bigger
operator.

Proposition A.2
.

For a given Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, consider the matrices
K_{\mathbf{H}}
,
K_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
, and
\widehat{K}_{\mathbf{H}}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)\cdot A_{P}\otimes P
. The maximum eigenvalue of all three are the same.

Proof.

K_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
has the same eigenvalues as
K_{\mathbf{H}}
up to multiplicity.
\widehat{K}_{\mathbf{H}}
is obtained from
K_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
conjugating by the block-diagonal unitary
U\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2n}\times 2^{2n}}
given by
U_{P,P}=P
.
∎

Applying trace moment method to
\widehat{K}_{\mathbf{H}}
, which now has dimension
2^{n}{\left|\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)\right|}
, finally yields something that looks like a random walk on
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{K}_{\mathbf{H}}^{2r}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P_{1},\dots,P_{2r}\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\prod_{i=1}^{2r}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P_{i})\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}P_{i}\right)\cdot\mbox{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r}A_{P_{i}}\right)\,.

Observe this last term counts the number of walks on the graph
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
with a given Pauli sequence, which is actually invariant since all walks are isomorphic. This means, up to proportionality, the trace moments of our (modified) Kikuchi matrix exactly match that of the underlying Hamiltonian! Unfortunately, our operator is still huge. Our fix is two-fold: (1)
Proposition

A.2
allows us to drop the tensored
\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}
and just look at the operator norm of the Kikuchi graph and (2) as the key power of the Kikuchi matrix, we may restrict our adjacency matrix from the Pauli group
\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
to the Pauli slice,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
, all weight-
\ell

n
-qubit operators, yielding a hierarchy of matrices trading dimensionality for approximation quality.

Definition A.3

(Kikuchi matrix for
k
-local Hamiltonians)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
even and
k/2\leq\ell\leq n/2
. Let
P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
be a weight-
k
Pauli operator. We define the following adjacency matrix on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(n)
:

A_{P}(Q,R)=\begin{cases}1&Q^{\dagger}R=P\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The level-
\ell
Kikuchi matrix of
\mathbf{H}
is then defined by

K_{\mathbf{H}}=\left(\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)\cdot A_{P}\right)\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A^{*}_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2^{n}}\,.

We call the graph
A_{\mathbf{H}}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathbbm{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}|\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(P)|\cdot A_{P}
the Kikuchi graph.

What we are left with is an energy bound for
\mathbf{H}
depending entirely on the operator norm of
A_{\mathbf{H}}^{*}
, an operator of subexponential dimension, which is what allows us to compute the spectral norm without incurring an exponential cost.
