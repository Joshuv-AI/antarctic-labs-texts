All solid rings
Jaime Benabent Guerrero

Abstract
. We give an explicit characterization of the solid rings of the fourth type, finishing a work by Bousfield and Kan in the 1970s. As a consequence of our methods, we give criteria to find the core of a ring and explicitly compute the core of some rings.



1.
Introduction

In
[
BK71
, Definition 2.1]
, Bousfield and Kan introduced the concept of a
solid
ring
1
1

1

Our rings are always assumed to be commutative, associative and unital.
, with the observation that the completion of a space with respect to a ring
R
, in the sense of
[
BK72
]
, is homotopically equivalent to the completion of the space with respect to the maximal solid ring contained in
R
, denoted
cR
.

In the same work, Bousfield and Kan classified solid rings into four distinct types. Although the first three types are well understood, the fourth type has remained comparatively obscure. The purpose of this article is to revisit this classification and shed new light on the nature of this lesser-known class of solid rings.
The first three types of solid rings are as follows:

(1)

Cyclic rings
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
n\geq 1
is a positive integer.

(2)

Subrings of the rationals
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]
, where
J
is any set of primes.

(3)

Product rings
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
J
is any set of primes that contains all prime factors of
n\geq 2
.

while the fourth type was given by any of the following two equivalent definitions:

(4a)

Any colimit given by a diagram of rings of type (1), (2), or (3) that does not fall into any of the three types defined previously.

(4b)

The
core
of the infinite product rings
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
K\subseteq J
are any two nested infinite sets of primes and the exponents
\{e_{p}\}_{p\in K}
are positive integers.

Despite their origins, solid rings have broader significance in mathematics, particularly in algebraic geometry, and are of independent interest.
They form the foundation for the classification of subterminal schemes, see
[
Gue25
]
.

In this note, after briefly recalling the definition of solid rings and establishing key structural lemmas, we introduce the concept of
basic
solid rings.
This leads to a comprehensive classification of all solid rings as coproducts of these fundamental building blocks.

From this perspective, solid rings naturally decompose into two categories:
On one side, we have the finite
basic
solid rings, given by
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
for a positive integer
n
, which account for all solid rings of type (1).
On the other side, we have coproducts of infinite
basic
solid rings, which take the form
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}
for some prime
p
and a nonnegative integer
a
.
In such coproducts, every prime appears at most once.
These rings encompass all solid rings of type (2), (3), or (4), depending on whether the number of positive exponents is zero, finite, or infinite, respectively.

Moreover, such a coproduct admits a natural interpretation as the colimit of a tower of inclusions, offering a concrete and structured description of solid rings of type (4).

The coproduct interpretation provides a systematic way to compute the core of any ring as the coproduct of all the basic solid rings it contains.
For a ring
R
with
c:={\textup{char}}(R)\not=0
, the core is the finite basic solid ring
{\mathbb{Z}}/c{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In contrast, for a ring of characteristic zero, the core is determined by the infinite basic solid rings contained within it.

We apply this framework to compute the core of several rings, including an explicit determination of the core of an infinite product ring of the form described in (4b), viewed as a subring of the original ring.
Specifically, the ring described in (4b) is the subring of

{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}

consisting of precisely those elements
(r/s,\{a_{p}\}_{p\in K})
such that only finitely many of the
{a_{p}}^{\prime}s
differ from
r/s
modulo
{\mathbb{Z}}/p_{i}^{e_{p_{i}}}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Note that this condition is well-defined, as the quotient is undefined in only finitely many factors; specifically, those corresponding to the prime divisors of
s
, which are finite in number.

2.
Solid rings

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring.

•

R
is called
solid
if for any other ring
S
there exists at most one morphism
R\to S
, i.e. the unique morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}\to R
is an epimorphism.

•

An element
x\in R
is called
absolute
if for any other ring
S
the image of
x
under any morphism
R\to S
is fixed, i.e.
x\otimes 1=1\otimes x
in
R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R
.

•

The
core
of
R
, denoted
cR
, is the subring of absolute elements, i.e. the maximal solid subring of
R
.

A ring is solid if and only if all of its elements are absolute.

Solidity
can also be defined in other categories.
In particular, the following two lemmas are categorical in nature and apply more generally beyond the category of rings.
These lemmas establish fundamental properties of colimits when the underlying objects are solid.

Lemma 2.2
.

Any colimit of solid rings is solid.

Proof.

Given a colimit diagram of solid rings, any morphism from the colimit to another ring
S
is uniquely determined by the universal property of colimits. Since each object in the diagram admits at most one morphism to
S
, their uniqueness is preserved in the colimit, ensuring that it remains solid.
∎

The next lemma states that in a diagram of solid rings we can ignore or rearrange arrows between objects without affecting the colimit.
This follows from the fact that, due to solidity, morphisms between the objects of our diagram do not add new structural information.

Lemma 2.3
.

The colimit of a diagram of solid rings is isomorphic to the coproduct of the underlying rings.
Furthermore, if an object appears multiple times in the coproduct diagram, we may consider only one copy.

Proof.

The colimit of a diagram captures the data of morphisms from each object in the diagram.
Since each solid ring has at most one morphism to any other ring, the colimit structure is determined purely by the objects themselves rather than by the arrows between them.
Consequently, we can discard these arrows and work directly with the coproduct of the underlying objects.

Moreover, if a solid ring appears more than once in a coproduct diagram, the only possible morphism between two such copies is the identity. Hence, we only need to consider one copy in the coproduct.
∎

2.4.
Coproducts of solid rings of types (1), (2), and (3)

We now recall the characterization of solid rings of types (1), (2), and (3) from the introduction and compute their coproducts explicitly. In each case, we will see that a finite coproduct of rings of a given type remains within the same type.
The proofs of the following lemmas are straightforward calculations of finite coproducts.
In the last case, recall that in the category of rings, finite coproducts distribute over finite products.

Lemma 2.5
.

The coproduct of any set
\{{\mathbb{Z}}/n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}\}_{i\in I}
of solid rings of type (1) can be written as
{\mathbb{Z}}/g{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
g=\gcd\limits_{i\in I}n_{i}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The coproduct of any set
\{{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{i}^{-1}]\}_{i\in I}
of solid rings of type (2) can be written as
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]
, where
J=\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}J_{i}
.
In particular,
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]
can be thought as the coproduct of the rings
\{{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\}_{p\in J}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Any solid ring of type (3),
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
where
J
is any set of primes that contains all prime factors of
n
, can be written as the coproduct of

{\mathbb{Z}}[(J\setminus K)^{-1}]\textup{ and }\{{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\}_{p\in K},

where
K
is the finite set of primes that divides
n
and
e_{p}:=v_{p}(n)
is the exponent of
p
in
n
.

These lemmas, together with the fact that colimits commute with colimits, allow us to further simplify any colimit diagram of solid rings of types (1), (2), and (3).
Specifically, such a colimit contains at most one solid ring of type (1),
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
, at most one solid ring of type (2),
{\mathbb{Z}}[J\setminus K]
for any two nested set of primes
K\subseteq J
, and all remaining rings are of the form
\{{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\}_{p\in K}
where the exponents are positive integers.

At this stage, one may observe that solid rings of types (2) and (3) can be unified into a broader framework.
Both types can be viewed as coproducts of rings of the form

\{{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\}_{p\in J},

where only finitely many exponents are positive integers and the rest are zero.
When all exponents
e_{p}
are zero, we recover rings of type (2), while allowing a finite non-zero amount of them to be positive corresponds to rings of type (3).

This insight suggests a natural way to decompose solid rings into more fundamental building blocks.
In the following subsection, we introduce
basic
solid rings, which serve as the essential components from which all solid rings can be constructed.
These will provide a conceptual foundation for understanding the structure of solid rings in full generality.

We conclude this section with a key structural result, already present in the literature, which ensures that every solid ring can be built from these fundamental cases.
The proof relies on the fact that any ring can be written as a colimit of its finitely generated subrings.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
BK71
, Proposition 3.1]
)
.

Every solid ring can be written as a colimit of solid rings of types (1), (2), and (3).

3.
Basic solid rings

At the foundation of this unified perspective lie the
basic
solid rings, which serve as the fundamental building blocks of all solid rings.
These are divided into two types:

•

Finite basic solid rings
, given by the rings

{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}

for a positive integer
n
, which correspond to solid rings of type (1).

•

Infinite basic solid rings
, given by the rings

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}

for a prime
p
and a nonnegative integer
a
.

As proven in the previous section, coproducts of infinite basic solid rings where at most a finite amount of the exponents are non-zero generate all solid rings of types (2) and (3).
This, together with the fact that every solid ring can be described as a colimit of rings of types (1), (2), and (3), means that every solid ring can ultimately be expressed in terms of these basic building blocks as a colimit.

Furthermore, for any prime
p
and any nonnegative integers
b\leq a
, there is a natural epimorphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{b}{\mathbb{Z}}.

This implies that, each prime contributes at most one infinite basic solid ring to the coproduct decomposition.

Taking into account that a coproduct of solid rings is of type (1) whenever at least one of its components is of type (1), we arrive at the following immediate consequence of the results established in this section:

Theorem 3.1
.

Every solid ring is either of type (1) or an arbitrary coproduct of infinite basic solid rings, where each prime contributes at most once.

Additionally, it is useful to determine when a morphism exists between any two basic solid rings.
Beyond (
3
), we have the following two cases:

•

There is an epimorphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/n,

if and only if
a\geq v_{p}(n)
.

•

There is an epimorphism

{\mathbb{Z}}/m\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/n,

whenever
n\mid m
.

3.2.
Maps from basic solid rings

In this subsection, we analyze how the existence of a morphism from a basic solid ring to a given ring
R
constrains the structure of
R
.
Since every solid ring is a coproduct of basic solid rings (Theorem
3.1
), understanding the morphisms from these building blocks is equivalent to understanding morphisms from arbitrary solid rings.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists a morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}/m{\mathbb{Z}}\to R
if and only if the characteristic of
R
divides
m
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R
if and only if there exists an element
r_{p,a}\in R
satisfying

pr_{p,a}^{2}=r_{p,a}\textup{ and }p^{a+1}r_{p,a}=p^{a}.

Proof.

One direction is immediate.
For the other direction, consider the morphism given by

(m/p^{t},n)\mapsto pr_{p,a}^{t+1}m+(1-pr_{p,a})n.

∎

These observations immediately determine the core of any ring with nonzero characteristic:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
R
be a ring with characteristic
{\textup{char}}(R)\not=0
.
Then the core of
R
is given by

cR={\mathbb{Z}}/{\textup{char}}(R){\mathbb{Z}}.

Now we consider the case where
R
has characteristic zero.
In this setting, we establish when an infinite basic solid ring can be embedded into
R
:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
R
be a ring of characteristic zero, and assume there exists a morphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R

for some prime
p
and a nonnegative integer
a
.
Then there exists a nonnegative integer
b\leq a
such that there is an injective morphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\hookrightarrow R.

Proof.

Any morphism can be factored as a quotient map followed by an inclusion.
If an element whose first coordinate is nonzero belongs to the kernel, then
{\textup{char}}(R)\not=0
, which contradicts our assumption.
Hence, the kernel must be contained in the
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}
component, ensuring the factorization of this component through a subring
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{b}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
∎

In the next section, we leverage the tower of inclusions perspective to provide a precise characterization of solid rings of type (4).

4.
Solid rings of type (4)

By Theorem
3.1
, it remains to analyze coproducts of infinite basic solid rings in which each prime appears at most once and an infinite number of the exponents are positive.
In this section, we begin by introducing the tower of inclusions perspective, which provides a concrete description of solid rings of type (4).
We then verify that these rings are indeed genuinely new.
Finally, we examine how these rings naturally embed as subrings within the original rings proposed by Bousfield and Kan in their foundational work.

4.1.
The tower of inclusions perspective

Consider a family of infinite basic solid rings

\{{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\}_{p\in J},

where
J
is an infinite collection of primes, and let
K:=\{p\in J:e_{p}\geq 1\}
be an infinite subset of
J
.
Without loss of generality, assume
K=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\dots\}
is ordered by increasing magnitude, so that we may define
K_{n}:=\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
J_{n}:=(J\setminus K)\cup K_{n}
.

Because colimits commute with colimits, we can first take the coproduct of all rings corresponding to primes in
J_{0}=J\setminus K
as a single step, obtaining
{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{0}^{-1}]
.
Then, by sequentially taking coproducts with one additional prime at each step, we obtain the following directed system of inclusions:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{0}^{-1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{n}^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K_{n}}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots,}

where each inclusion is induced by the universal property of coproducts.

Let
S
be the colimit of this sequential system, its elements can be understood as elements of some
{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{n}^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K_{n}}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}
in their minimal form, i.e. those that do not belong to the image of any earlier morphism.
The ring operations in
S
are inherited from the colimit structure, meaning that addition and multiplication between any two elements take place in the largest ring containing both.

4.2.
Classification of solid rings of type (4)

We refer to the data given by these infinite basic solid rings as
(J,\{e_{p}\}_{p\in J})
where
J
is an infinite set of primes and
\{e_{p}\}_{p\in J}
is a family of nonnegative exponents, with the condition that infinitely many of them are positive.

To confirm that these data define genuinely new rings, distinct from those of type (1), (2), or (3), as well as from any other type (4) ring described by different data, we examine their torsion subring and the corresponding quotient:

•

The torsion subring:

S^{t}=\bigoplus\limits_{p\in K}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}.

•

The quotient by torsion:

S/S^{t}={\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}].

These invariants show that different choices of
(J,\{e_{p}\})
yield non-isomorphic rings, completing the classification.

4.3.
The infinite product
{\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}

Let

R:={\mathbb{Z}}[J^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}.

In this subsection, we give a precise description of the core
cR
.

Since
{\textup{char}}{R}=0
, no finite basic solid ring embeds into
R
, wo we only need to determine which rings of the form

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}

for some prime
p
and
a\geq 0
, are contained in
R
.

For a morphism from such a ring to
R
to exist, the map must be defined coordinate-wise, which required
p\in J
and
e_{p}\leq a
.
The minimal such embedding occurs when
a=e_{p}
, and the coproduct of these minimal embeddings gives precisely the largest solid subring of
R
, namely
cR
.

To determine the explicit inclusion
cR\subseteq R
, consider the sequential diagram of inclusions defined in (
4.1
) above and, for any nonnegative integer
n
, the inclusion

{\mathbb{Z}}[J_{n}^{-1}]\times\prod\limits_{p\in K_{n}}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{e_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\hookrightarrow R

defined coordinate-wise in the only possible way.

r/s
modulo
{\mathbb{Z}}/p_{i}^{e_{p_{i}}}{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is well defined in all but finitely many of the factors since
s
has a finite amount of prime factors.

5.
All solid rings

As noted in Lemma
3.5
, the core of a ring with nonzero characteristic is fully determined by its characteristic.
On the other hand, as established in the previous section, the core of a characteristic zero ring
R
is precisely determined by the set of rings of the form

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}

for some prime
p
and
a\geq 0
, that embed into
R
.
More generally, the core of any ring
R
is completely determined by the basic solid rings that embed into it.
Indeed:

•

For a ring with nonzero characteristic, no infinite basic solid ring embeds into it.

•

For a ring of characteristic zero, no finite basic solid ring embeds into it.

Nevertheless, for a ring with nonzero characteristic, it is still meaningful to consider morphisms from infinite basic solid rings into it, even though these morphisms are not embeddings, as the following lemma shows:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with characteristic
c:={\textup{char}}{R}\not=0
.
Then for any prime
p
, there exists a morphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R

if and only if
a\geq v_{p}(c)
.

Proof.

Since we have a tower of surjections

\cdots\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n+1}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow\cdots\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]

it suffices to verify that:

1.

A morphism exists for
a=v_{p}(c)
.

2.

No morphism exists for
a=v_{p}(c)-1
whenever
p\mid c
.

The existence of a morphism for
a=v_{p}(c)
follows directly from the composition

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{v_{p}(c)}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/c{\mathbb{Z}}\hookrightarrow R.

To show that no morphism exists for
a=v_{p}(c)-1
when
p\mid c
, suppose otherwise.
Then, by Lemma
3.4
, such a morphism would imply the existence of an element
r\in R
such that

p^{v_{p}(c)}r=p^{v_{p}(c)-1}.

Writing
c=p^{v_{p}(c)}d
with
\gcd(p,d)=1
, we obtain

0=cr=dp^{v_{p}(c)}r=dp^{v_{p}(c)-1}\not=0,

a contradiction.
∎

This information leads to a new classification of solid rings, which we now summarize in terms of an exponent function and a binary parameter:

5.2.
A classification of solid rings

A solid ring is given by the following data:

(1)

An exponent function
e:{\mathbb{P}}\to\{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{+\infty\}
.

(2)

A binary parameter
q\in\{0,1\}
, where
q=0
if either:

•

There exists a prime
p
with
e(p)=+\infty
, or

•

There are infinitely many primes
p
with
e(p)>0
.

These data uniquely specify a solid ring, where the parameter
q
distinguishes between finite and infinite constructions:

•

If
q=0
, the ring is obtained as a coproduct of infinite basic solid rings, and the exponent function determines which rings contribute to this structure.

•

If
q=1
, the solid ring is finite, and the exponent function provides the prime factorization data.

6.
Limits and colimits of solid rings

We can represent the core of a ring
R
as a pair
(cR,q)
where:

•

The function

cR:{\mathbb{P}}\to\{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{+\infty\},

assigns to each prime
p
the minimal exponent
a_{p}
such that there exists a morphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R.

•

The binary parameter
q
indicates whether the characteristic of
R
is zero or nonzero.

6.1.
Limits

The following lemma describes how the function associated with a limit relates to the functions of the underlying objects in the diagram.
Notably, in this first result, the parameter
q
does not play a role.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of rings forming a diagram whose limit is
R
.
Then

cR=\sup\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}.

Proof.

Fix a prime
p
.
We prove that

cR(p)=\sup\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}(p).

Since
R
is the limit of the
R_{i}
, there are natural projections
R\to R_{i}
, and for any
i\in I
, we have

cR(p)\geq cR_{i}(p),

because a morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R
induces a corresponding morphism into each
R_{i}
.
This gives one side of the inequality.

Let
M:=\sup\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}(p)
.
If
M=+\infty
, the equality follows immediately.
Otherwise, since
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{M}{\mathbb{Z}}
maps to every
R_{i}
in a compatible way with the diagram we are working with since
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{M}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a solid ring, it must factor through the limit
R
, giving a morphism

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{M}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R.

This shows the other side of the equality,

cR(p)\leq\sup\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}(p),

completing the proof.
∎

We now turn our attention to the parameter
q
, which distinguishes between the cases of characteristic zero and positive characteristic.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of rings forming a diagram whose limit is
R
.
Then
q=1
if and only if every ring
R_{i}
in the diagram satisfies
q_{i}=1
,
cR(p)<+\infty
for any prime
p
, and there are at most finitely many primes with
cR(p)>0
.

Proof.

Suppose
q=1
.
Then there exists a morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}/{\textup{char}}(R){\mathbb{Z}}\to R\to R_{i}
for each
i\in I
, which implies that
R_{i}
has nonzero characteristic.
Thus
q_{i}=1
for every
i\in I
.
Furthermore, the conditions
cR(p)<+\infty
for all primes
p
, and the finiteness of
\{p\in{\mathbb{P}}:cR(p)>0\}
follow directly, since otherwise, we would have
q=0
, contradicting our assumption.

For the converse, assume that every ring in the diagram satisfies
q_{i}=1
.
Since we are given that
cR(p)<+\infty
for all
p
and that only finitely many primes satisfy
cR(p)>0
, we can construct the solid ring

\prod\limits_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{cR(p)}{\mathbb{Z}},

which maps in a compatible way into every
R_{i}
, and thus it factor through
R
by the universal property of limits.
This ensures that
q=1
, completing the proof.
∎

Both lemmas together provide a complete description of how the exponent function and the parameter
q
behave under limits, allowing us to determine the core of a limit of rings explicitly in terms of the cores of the underlying objects in the diagram.

6.4.
Colimits

In this subsection we focus on the core construction for arbitrary colimits.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of rings forming a diagram whose colimit is
R
.
Then

cR\leq\min\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}.

Proof.

Let
m_{p}:=\min\limits_{i\in I}cR_{i}(p)
.
Then there exists
i\in I
such that we have a morphism
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{m_{p}}{\mathbb{Z}}\to R_{i}
. Composing with the canonical map
R_{i}\to R
induced by the colimit gives a morphism into
R
, and hence the inequality follows.
∎

Notably, for arbitrary colimits, this inequality can not be made into an equality.
Consider the following pushout diagram, where the arrows are the projections onto the even and odd-indexed components, respectively:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}^{2{\mathbb{N}}+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}^{2{\mathbb{N}}}}

The colimit of this diagram is
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2{\mathbb{N}}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2{\mathbb{N}}+1}=0
, because the image of the element
(0,1,0,1,\dots)
forces any ring
A
making the square commute to satisfy
0=1
, and hence
A=0
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of rings forming a diagram whose colimit is
R
.
Then

\max\limits_{i\in I}q_{i}\leq q.

Proof.

If any
R_{i}
has characteristic not equal to zero (i.e.,
q_{i}=1
), then a cyclic ring maps into
R
after composition with the natural map induced by the colimit, implying
q=1
.
∎

Notably, for arbitrary colimits, this inequality can not be made into an equality.
The counterexample is the same as above.
It shows a diagram of rings of characteristic zero whose colimit is the characteristic one ring (i.e., the zero ring).

7.
Computations

We conclude our work by computing the cores of some well-known characteristic zero rings.
Recall that the case of rings with nonzero characteristic has already been settled in Lemma
3.5
.

7.1.
A characteristic zero field

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero.
Since
{\mathbb{Q}}\hookrightarrow K
, we have
q_{K}=0
and
cK\equiv 0
.
Therefore, the core of
K
is:

cK={\mathbb{Q}}.

7.2.
The
p
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

Recall that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
is the inverse limit of the system:

\cdots\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n+1}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow\cdots\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}.

For each
n
, the core of
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
is given by:

c({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})(q)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $q\not=p$}\\
n,&\text{if $q=p$}\end{cases}.

Applying Lemma
6.2
, we obtain:

c{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}(q)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $q\not=p$}\\
+\infty,&\text{if $q=p$}\end{cases}.

Hence,
c{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}={\mathbb{Z}}_{(p)}
, the localization of
{\mathbb{Z}}
at the prime ideal
(p)
.

7.3.
The profinite completion of the integers
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}

Recall that
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the product over all primes:

\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}:=\prod_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}.

A direct application of Lemma
6.2
yields:

c\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}(q)=+\infty,\text{ for all }q\in{\mathbb{P}},

which means:

c\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}={\mathbb{Z}}.

7.4.
A polynomial ring over a characteristic zero ring

Let
R
be a ring of characteristic zero, and let
R[\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
be a polynomial ring over
R
in any collection of indeterminates.

Suppose there exists a morphism from
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]\times{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{a}{\mathbb{Z}}
to
R[\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
. By Lemma
3.4
, this would imply the existence of a polynomial
F
satisfying
pF^{2}=F
. Since
R
has characteristic zero, such an identity forces
F
to be a degree zero polynomial. Thus, the image of any basic solid ring must lie entirely in the subring
R\subseteq R[\{X_{i}\}]
.

We conclude that

c(R[\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}])=cR.
