Well-posedness, long-time behavior, and discretization of some models of nonlinear acoustics in velocity–enthalpy formulation
Herbert Egger, Marvin Fritz, Egger H, Fritz M

Abstract
We study a class of models for nonlinear acoustics, including the well-known Westervelt and Kuznetsov equations, as well as a model of Rasmussen that can be seen as a thermodynamically consistent modification of the latter. Using linearization, energy estimates, and fixed-point arguments, we establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions that, for sufficiently small data, are global in time and converge exponentially fast to equilibrium. In contrast to previous work, our analysis is based on a velocity-enthalpy formulation of the problem, whose weak form reveals the underlying port-Hamiltonian structure. Moreover, the weak form of the problem is particularly well-suited for a structure-preserving discretization. This is demonstrated in numerical tests, which also highlight typical characteristics of the models under consideration. \jnlcitation \cname , and . \ctitle Global well-posedness and long-time stability of some models of nonlinear acoustics.

Introduction

Models for nonlinear acoustics are usually derived via certain approximations of the compressible Euler equations; see
9
,
20
for instance. As a general model governing the velocity potential
\psi
, we consider the equation

\displaystyle(1-a\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c_{0}^{2}\Delta\psi=b\Delta\psi_{t}+c\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}+d\psi_{t}\Delta\psi.

(1.1)

Here,
c_{0}
denotes the speed of sound in the unperturbed medium,
b
is the coefficient of thermoviscous damping, and
a,c,d
are further model parameters describing the nonlinear effects in the medium.
Several well-established models of nonlinear acoustics are covered by (
1.1
), e.g., the Westervelt equation (
a=(\gamma+1)/c_{0}^{2}
,
c=d=0
), the Kuznetsov equation (
a=(\gamma-1)/c_{0}^{2}
,
c=2
,
d=0
), or its modification by Rasmussen (
a=(\gamma-2)/c_{0}^{2}
,
c=2
,
d=1
);
see
11
,
29
for a comparison and detailed derivations.

Motivation and related work.

Due to its seemingly superior accuracy
3
, the Kuznetsov equation has been widely used in the engineering community; see
1
,
5
,
10
,
21
and the references given there.
In contrast to the underlying compressible Euler system and the Westervelt equation, it,
however, lacks thermodynamic consistency, that is, nonphysical energy production cannot be ruled out rigorously.
This was the motivation for Rasmussen et al. to propose a modification that has similar accuracy, but provably encodes the passivity of the system; see
29
,
30
,
31
for details.
In our numerical tests, we observe that this model also has somewhat superior stability properties.
The mathematical analysis of the Westervelt and Kuznetsov equations has been comprehensively addressed by Kaltenbacher et al.
12
,
13
,
14
,
18
, who employed the velocity potential formulation.
Further analytical results based on maximal
L^{p}
-regularity were obtained in
24
,
25
for analytical results , and
34
addressed the Cauchy problem for a related model.
The well-posedness of other models of nonlinear acoustics, such as the Blackstock equation, the third-order Moore–Gibson–Thompson equation, and models with nonlocal-in-time dissipation were considered in
8
,
16
,
22
,
35
.
In
4
,
27
, an apriori error analysis for appropriate discretization schemes was presented,
23
explored a second-order mixed formulation,
15
studied a different first-order system,
19
,
17
considered the convergence of time discretization methods, and
26
investigated asymptotic-preserving finite element methods for the Westervelt equation.

Scope and main contributions.

In this paper, we investigate the velocity-enthalpy formulation of (
1.1
), see Section
2
, and study the global existence and uniqueness of solutions, as well as their long-time behavior.
For parameters
c=2d
, which includes the Westervelt and Rasmussen equations, the weak form of this model reveals an underling port-Hamiltonian structure, thus guaranteeing passivity and thermodynamic consistency of the system.
In addition, the weak form of the velocity–enthalpy formulation turns out to be very well suited for a structure-preserving discretization which inherits the elementary dissipation mechanism and basic stability properties from the continuous problem. Some preliminary computational results will be presented for illustration of these findings.

Outline.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows:
In
Section

2
, we briefly derive and formally state the velocity-enthalpy formulation of (
1.1
), introduce our notation, and present our main theoretical results, whose proof is elaborated in the following sections.
A linearized problem is investigated in
Section

Section

. The global existence and decay of solutions are finally established in
Section

5
.
In
Section

6
, we discuss the structure-preserving discretization of the problem through mixed finite elements and an implicit time-stepping scheme. We further present some numerical tests which illustrate the passivity and the exponential decay of solutions on the discrete level.

Preliminaries and main results

Let us start with giving a complete definition of the model problem to be considered in the rest of the manuscript.
We assume that
c_{0}^{2}=1
in the sequel, which can always be achieved by appropriate scaling of space and time. We further introduce the velocity
v=-\nabla\psi
and the total specific enthalpy
h=\psi_{t}
. Then (

) can be rewritten equivalently as

\displaystyle v_{t}+\nabla h

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\ t\geq 0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle(1-ah)\,h_{t}+\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v

\displaystyle=b\Delta h-c\,v\cdot\nabla h-d\,h\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\ t\geq 0.

(2.2)

The system is complemented by appropriate boundary and initial conditions. For ease of notation, we choose

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,\ t\geq 0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0},\qquad v(0)=v_{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

(2.4)

Other types of boundary conditions could be treated with minor modification of our arguments below.
Throughout the manuscript, we make the following assumptions about the problem data required for our analysis.

.
Throughout the text, we use standard notation for function spaces and norms; see, for instance,

Weak formulation and energy dissipation

As a first step of our analysis, we present a weak characterization of solutions, which allows us to establish the basic power balance of the problem and which serves as the starting point for the design of structure-preserving numerical schemes later on.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(v,h)
be a sufficiently smooth solution of (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) on
[0,T]
for some
T>0
. Then

\displaystyle(v_{t},w)_{\Omega}+(\nabla h,w)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

(2.5)

\displaystyle((1-ah)h_{t},q)_{\Omega}-(v,\nabla q)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\,b(\nabla h,\nabla q)_{\Omega}-(c-d)(v\cdot\nabla h,q)_{\Omega}+d(h,v\cdot\nabla q)_{\Omega}

(2.6)

for all
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}
and
q\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, and all
0\leq t\leq T
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathcal{H}(v,h)

\displaystyle=-b\|\nabla h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(2d-c)(v\cdot\nabla h,h)_{\Omega},

(2.7)

where
\mathcal{H}(v,h):=\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2}-\tfrac{a}{3}h^{3}\,\textup{d}x
is the Hamiltonian, i.e., the total energy of the system.
For
c=2d
, the energy thus decreases monotonically over time.

Proof 2.2
.

We note that the last term in (
2.2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\,d\,h\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v=d\,v\cdot\nabla h-d\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}(h\,v).

The variational identities (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) are then obtained in the usual manner, that is, by multiplying the equations (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) with appropriate test functions and integration over the domain
\Omega
. For the second term on the left-hand side of (
2.6
), we used integration-by-parts. The corresponding boundary term vanishes due to the conditions on the test function
q
.
By formal differentiation of the energy functional, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathcal{H}(v,h)

\displaystyle=(v_{t},v)_{\Omega}+((1-ah)h_{t},h)_{\Omega}.

(2.8)

The power balance (
2.7
) then follows immediately from (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) by testing with
(w,q)=(v,h)
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let us emphasize that the power balance (
2.7
) is a direct consequence of the particular structure of the variational identities (
2.5
)–(
2.6
), which can be preserved under Galerkin projection.
We will use this fact in Section
6
below.
Let us further note that the energy functional
\mathcal{H}(v,h)
is convex provided that
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}
is sufficiently small.

Well-posedness and exponential decay

The natural energy
\mathcal{H}(v,h)
of the problem is not strong enough to provide uniform bounds for
h
in
L^{\infty}
in space-time, which are required to demonstrate the well-posedness of the problem.
For our analysis, we therefore
utilize the stronger energy

\mathcal{E}(v,h):=\|h\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}^{2}:=\|h(t)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|v(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(2.9)

Using standard convention, we write
H^{\ell}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}(\Omega):=\{v\in H^{\ell}(\Omega)^{n}:\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v\in H^{\ell}(\Omega)\}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
for the space of regular velocity fields and denote by
\|v\|_{H^{\ell}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}=(\|v\|_{H^{\ell}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v\|_{H^{\ell}}^{2})^{1/2}
its natural norm.
We search for a solution
(v,h)
in the space
\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{T}

\displaystyle=\{v\in C([0,T];H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega)^{n}):\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{T}

\displaystyle=\{h\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))\cap C([0,T];H^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)):h=\Delta h=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

Here
C([0,T];X)
and
L^{p}(0,T;X)
denote the spaces of functions
f:[0,T]\to X
of time with values in some normed space
X
; see
37
for details and properties of these spaces.
If not stated otherwise, the norm for the intersection of two Banach spaces is defined by
\|\cdot\|_{X\cap Y}:=\max\{\|\cdot\|_{X},\|\cdot\|_{Y}\}
.
We are now in a position to state our main results.

Theorem 2.4

(Well-posedness and energy decay)
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then for any
T>0
there exists an
\varepsilon(T)>0
such that for all initial values

\displaystyle(v_{0},h_{0})\in H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Omega)\quad\text{with}\quad\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}=h_{0}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon(T),

there exists a unique (local) solution
(v,h)\in\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}
of problem (
2.1
)–(
2.4
).
Furthermore, if
\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon_{\infty}
with
\varepsilon_{\infty}>0
sufficiently small, then the solution exists (globally) for all
t>0
and there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|h(t)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq C_{0}\,e^{-C_{1}t}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(2.10)

The detailed proof of these assertions is presented in the following three sections.
For orientation of the reader, let us briefly mention the main ingredients for the analysis already here:
The smallness condition on the initial data allows to guarantee that the coefficient
(1-\eta h)
in front of the time derivative of (
2.2
) remains bounded and uniformly positive.
The existence of a unique solution local in time can then be established using Galerkin approximations, energy estimates, and a fixed-point argument.
The latter is based on a linearization of the problem, discussed in Section
3
, while existence of a fixed-point is established in Section
4
.
A careful derivation of additional energy estimates finally allows us to derive global bounds on the solution and the decay estimate follow via the Haraux–Lagnese inequality; see Section
5
for details.

A linearized problem

In a first step of our analysis, we study the linear problem that arises from replacing
h
in some of the terms in (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) by a known function

. We additionally add a source function

Throughout this section, we thus consider the linear system

\displaystyle v_{t}+\nabla h

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\ t>0,

\displaystyle h_{t}+\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v

\displaystyle=b\Delta h+a\eta h_{t}-c\nabla\eta\cdot v-d\eta\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v+f\qquad

\displaystyle\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{b}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}

In addition, we used
\|\Delta h^{k}\|\simeq\|h^{k}\|_{H^{2}}
on
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
, since
\Omega
was assumed to have a
C^{3,1}
-regular boundary.

Step 2e.

As a final step, we test (
3.6
) with
g^{k}=L\Delta^{2}h^{k}
and integrate by parts in space as before. This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{L}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}+Lb\|\nabla\!\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=L(\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k},\nabla\!\Delta h^{k})_{\Omega}-La(\nabla\eta h_{t}^{k}+L\eta\nabla h_{t}^{k},\nabla\!\Delta h^{k})_{\Omega}+Lc(\nabla^{2}\eta\cdot v^{k}+\nabla\eta:\nabla v^{k},\nabla\!\Delta h^{k})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+Ld(\nabla\eta\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k}+\eta\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k},\nabla\!\Delta h^{k})_{\Omega}-L(\nabla f,\nabla\!\Delta h^{k})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{L}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k}\|^{2}+L\delta\|\nabla\!\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{La^{2}}{\delta}\big(\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|h_{t}^{k}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C_{5}\big(\|\nabla f\|^{2}+\|\nabla v_{0}\|^{2}\big)+C_{5}^{\prime}\big(1+1/\delta+\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}\eta\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\big)\,E^{*}(t).

For the second inequality, we used very similar arguments as in the previous step.
The third term on the right-hand side can be further estimated by
\frac{C^{\prime}La^{2}}{\delta}\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,4}}^{2}\big(\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}\big)
using Sobolev embeddings.

Step 3. Uniform bounds.

By adding up the estimates derived in the previous steps, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}

\displaystyle\Big(\|h^{k}\|^{2}+\|v^{k}\|^{2}+Kb\|\nabla h^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla h^{k}\|^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k}\|^{2}+b\|\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}+L\|\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}+L\|\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v^{k}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Big(b\|\nabla h^{k}\|^{2}+K\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+b\|\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+Lb\|\nabla\!\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\Big(K(a\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}+\delta)\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\delta\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\delta\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+(a\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}+\delta)\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\tfrac{C^{\prime}a^{2}L}{\delta}\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,4}}^{2}\big(\|h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla h_{t}^{k}\|^{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{*}\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C_{2}^{*}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C_{3}^{*}\big(1+1/\delta+\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}\eta\|_{L^{4}}\big)\,E^{*}(t).

We now choose
\delta>0
sufficiently small,
K
sufficiently large, and finally
L
sufficiently small, such that the terms in the third line can be absorbed into the second line of the left-hand side.
In order to be able to do so, we used
a\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}<1
.
Together with the definition of
E^{*}(t)
, see (
3.8
), we then see that

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E^{*}(t)+c^{*}\big(\|h_{t}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\nabla\!\Delta h^{k}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{*}\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C_{2}^{*}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C_{3}^{*}\big(1+1/\delta+\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}\eta\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\big)\,E^{*}(t).

Note that the constants
c^{*},C_{i}^{*}
depend solely on the bounds for the parameters, and
C_{i}^{*}
additionally depends on the final time
T
.
We then integrate this inequality in time and use the definition of
E^{*}(t)
as well as
\|\Delta h^{k}\|\simeq\|h^{k}\|_{H^{2}}
and
\|\nabla\!\Delta h^{k}\|\simeq\|h^{k}\|_{H^{3}}
, where we employed the boundary conditions.
We further rewrite the resulting estimate in the form
z(t)\leq z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)z(s)\,\textup{d}s

with

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\|h^{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|v^{k}(t)\|_{H^{0}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}^{2}+\|h^{k}_{t}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|h^{k}\|_{L^{2}_{t}(H^{2})}^{2},

(3.9)

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle=\|h_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}^{2}+\hat{C}_{1}\|f\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}^{2},

(3.10)

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle=\hat{C}_{2}+\hat{C}_{3}\big(\|\nabla\eta(s)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}\eta\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\big).

(3.11)

Note that
\hat{C}_{i}
are uniform constants and
g\in L^{1}(0,T)
according to our assumptions on
\eta
.
Applying the Grönwall inequality, see
2
Lemma II.4.10
, we thus conclude that

z(t)\leq z_{0}\exp\!\big(\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,\textup{d}s\big)
. Together with the definition of
v^{k}
, this already yields

\displaystyle\|h^{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|v^{k}(t)\|_{H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}^{2}+\|h^{k}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}^{2}+\|h^{k}\|_{L^{2}(H^{3})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{C}_{1}\,\exp\!\big(\hat{C}_{2}T+\hat{C}_{3}\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})\cap H^{1}(L^{4})}^{2}\big)\cdot\big(\|h_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}^{2}\big),

which holds with constants
\hat{C}_{i}
that are independent of the dimension of
W^{k}
.

Step 4: Limit process, existence, and uniqueness.

The uniform bounds on the solution
(v^{k},h^{k})
allow us to extract a sub-sequence which converges in an appropriate sense to a limit function
(\bar{v},\bar{h})
satisfying the same bounds.
Due to the high regularity of the solutions, one can pass to the limit in all terms of (
3.6
) and show that
(\bar{v},\bar{h})
satisfies the weak form of (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) as well as the boundary and initial conditions (
2.3
)–(
2.4
); see
33
for details of the arguments involved.
Uniqueness finally follows from the linearity of the equations and employing the energy estimates once again.

4
Local solvability

We can now establish the local existence of a unique solution to problem (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) by means of Banach’s fixed-point theorem.
To do so, we introduce the balls
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}_{T}:\|v\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}}\leq R\}
and
\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{T}:\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}\leq R\}
of radius
R
in
\mathcal{V}_{T}
and
\mathcal{H}_{T}
, respectively.
The parameter
R
will be adapted several times below as needed.
We will prove the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then for any
T>0
, there exist
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(T)>0
and
R=R(T)>0
, such that for all initial values
(v_{0},h_{0})\in H^{1}_{\textup{div}}\mspace{2mu}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Omega)
with
h_{0}=\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v=0
on
\partial\Omega
and such that
\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon
, the system (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) has a unique local solution
(v,h)\in\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
. Moreover,
\|(v,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}\leq C(T)\,\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})
with constant
C(T)
depending only on
T
.

Proof 4.2
.

We consider the mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T},\qquad(\tilde{v},\tilde{h})\mapsto(v,h),

(4.1)

where
(v,h)
is the solution to (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) and (
2.3
)–(
2.4
), with parameter functions
\eta=\tilde{h}
, and
f=0
.
We note that
\mathcal{F}(\tilde{h},\tilde{v})
does not depend on the velocity
\tilde{v}
.
Any fixed-point
(\bar{v},\bar{h})\in\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
of
\mathcal{F}
can be considered a solution (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) and to additionally satisfy the initial and boundary conditions (
2.3
)–(
2.4
).
By the regularity of the solutions to the linearized problem (
3.1
)–(
3.2
), one can see that any fixed-point
(\bar{v},\bar{h})
in fact also solves the original equations (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) in strong form.
It thus remains to verify the assumptions of Banach’s fixed-point theorem, which is what we do in the following.

Step 1.

For any
T>0
and
R>0
, the set
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
is non-empty and closed in the topology of
\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}
.
Moreover, for
R
sufficiently small, any function
\eta=\tilde{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
, and hence
\mathcal{F}
is well-defined.

Step 2. Self-mapping.

Let
(\tilde{v},\tilde{h})\in\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
with
R
sufficiently small. In addition, let
(v,h)=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v},\tilde{h})
be the unique solution of the linearized problem (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) with (
2.3
)–(
2.4
),
\eta=\tilde{h}
, and
f=0
.
From the estimates of Proposition
3.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\|(v,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}^{2}\leq C_{L}\cdot\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0}).

(4.2)

Using
\|\tilde{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}\leq R
and
\eta=\tilde{h}
, we can bound

\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})\cap H^{1}(L^{4})}\leq CR
by using appropriate Sobolev embeddings
7
,
33
, and hence

C_{L}=\hat{C}_{1}\exp(\hat{C}_{2}T+\hat{C}_{3}CR)
.
We may thus choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(R,T)
sufficiently small, such that
\|(v,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}\leq R
for all initial values satisfiying
\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon
.
Under this restriction,
\mathcal{F}
is a self-mapping on
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
.

Step 3. Contraction.

Let the two tuples
(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{h}_{1}),(\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{h}_{2})\in\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
be given,
R>0
be sufficiently small, and set

\displaystyle(v_{1},h_{1}):=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{h}_{1}),\qquad(v_{2},h_{2}):=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{h}_{2}).

We abbreviate the differences by
(\tilde{v},\tilde{h}):=(\tilde{v}_{1}-\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{h}_{1}-\tilde{h}_{2})
and
(v,h):=(v_{1}-v_{2},h_{1}-h_{2})
, and note that
(v(0),h(0))=(0,0)
, since the functions
(v_{i},h_{i})
satisfy the same initial conditions (
2.4
).
From the definition of
\mathcal{F}
via (
3.1
)–(
3.2
), one can further see that

\displaystyle(v_{t},u)_{\Omega}+(\nabla h,u)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.3)

\displaystyle((1-a\tilde{h}_{1})h_{t},g)_{\Omega}+b(\nabla h,\nabla g)_{\Omega}-(v,\nabla g)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=a(\tilde{h}h_{2,t},g)_{\Omega}-c(\nabla\tilde{h}_{1}\cdot v,g)_{\Omega}-c(\nabla\tilde{h}\cdot v_{2},g)_{\Omega}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\quad-d(\tilde{h}_{1}\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v,g)_{\Omega}-d(\tilde{h}\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{2},g)_{\Omega},

for any
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}
,
g\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and all
0\leq t\leq T
.
This amounts to the weak form of the linearized system (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) with

\displaystyle\eta=\tilde{h}_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f=a\tilde{h}h_{2,t}-d\tilde{h}\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{2}-c\nabla\tilde{h}\cdot v_{2}.

(4.5)

In order to apply Proposition
3.1
, we estimate
f
in the norm of
L^{2}(H^{1})
.
We treat the three terms independently. First, note that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}h_{2,t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}\leq\|\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\|h_{2,t}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}+\|\nabla\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|h_{2,t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{4})}\leq C(T)R\|\tilde{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}},

where we applied Hölder and Sobolev inequalities, and the uniform bounds for
\tilde{h}_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
.
For the second term, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{2}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}\leq\|\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{2}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}+\|\nabla\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{2}\|_{L^{2}(L^{4})}\leq C(T)R\|\tilde{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}},

using similar arguments as before. The third term can finally be bounded by

\displaystyle\|\nabla\tilde{h}\cdot v_{2}\|_{L^{2}(H^{1})}\leq\|\nabla\tilde{h}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}\|v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(H^{1})}+\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\|v_{2}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}\leq C(T)R\|\tilde{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}.

By applying Proposition
3.1
, we thus immediately obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{h}_{1})-\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{h}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}=\|(v,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}\leq C_{L}C(T)R\|(\tilde{v}_{1}-\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{h}_{1}-\tilde{h}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}.

(4.6)

By choosing
R
sufficiently small, we get a contraction, and we define
\varepsilon(T)=\varepsilon(T,R)
to retain the self-mapping property.
In summary, we have shown that
\mathcal{F}
is a contractive self-mapping on
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
for this choice of
R
, whenever
\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon(T)
.
This yields the existence of a unique fixed-point
(v,h)
of
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{R}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}^{R}
, and hence a local solution to (
2.1
)–(
2.4
).

Remark 4.3
.

With the same arguments, one can show that the unique local solution
(v,h)
of problem (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) also depends continuously on the (initial) data. Hence, the problem is (locally) well-posed. The details are left to the reader.

5
Global solutions and decay estimate

As a next step of our analysis, we now derive a-priori estimates for the solution of (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) that are independent of the time horizon
T
. As a consequence, the local solution can be extended to infinity. Moreover, the estimates will allow us to establish exponential decay to equilibrium.
Let us start with stating and proving the time-independent a-priori bounds.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then there exist constants
\varepsilon_{\infty}
,
C_{\infty}>0
, such that for all
T>0
and initial values satisfying
\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})\leq\varepsilon_{\infty}

(v,h)\in\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}

\displaystyle\|h(t)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|h\|_{H^{3}}^{2}+\|h_{t}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\,ds\leq C_{\infty}\big(\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|h_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\big)\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

(5.1)

Moreover, the energy at a time
t
can be estimated by

\mathcal{E}(v(t),h(t))\leq C_{0}\mathcal{E}(v_{0},h_{0})
for all
t\in(0,T]
.

Proof 5.2
.

Fix
T>0
and let
(v,h)\in\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}
denote a local solution of (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) on the time interval
[0,T]
.
From the a-priori estimates of Proposition
4.1
, we know that
\|(v,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{T}}^{2}:=\|v\|_{\mathcal{V}_{T}}^{2}+\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}^{2}\leq R^{2}
, with
R>0
depending monotonically on the norm of the initial conditions.
In particular, we may assume
R=R(\varepsilon,T)
sufficiently small by conditions on the initial values.
From the regularity of the solution, we deduce validity of

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla h

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)^{n}),

(5.2)

\displaystyle(1-ah)h_{t}-b\Delta h

\displaystyle=-\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v-cv\cdot\nabla h-d\,h\,\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

(5.3)

By formal differentiation of these equations, one can further deduce validity of the additional equations

\displaystyle\Delta v_{t}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\!\nabla h

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}),

(5.4)

\displaystyle\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\!\Delta h

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}),

(5.5)

\displaystyle(1-ah)\nabla h_{t}-a\nabla hh_{t}-b\nabla\!\Delta h

\displaystyle=-\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v-c\nabla(v\cdot\nabla h)-d\nabla(h\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v)

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}).

(5.6)

By formal testing of these equations, similar to the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we will now
establish
T
-independent a-priori bounds.

Step 1.

We multiply (
5.2
) with
-\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v
and (
5.3
) with
-\Delta h
, integrate over
\Omega_{t}=\Omega\times(0,t)
, and sum up to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\nabla h(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+b\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\nabla h_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)-a(hh_{t},\Delta h)_{\Omega_{t}}+c(\nabla h,v\Delta h)_{\Omega_{t}}+d(\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v,h\Delta h)_{\Omega_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\nabla h_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+a\|h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{\infty})}\|h_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+c\|\nabla h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{4})}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}+d\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\|h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{\infty})}\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}.

Here and below, we use
(u,u^{\prime})_{\Omega_{t}}=\int_{0}^{t}(u,u^{\prime})_{\Omega}\,ds
and write
\|u\|_{L^{p}_{t}(X)}=\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,t;X)}
and
\|u\|_{L^{p}(X)}=\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}
for abbreviation.
By Sobolev embeddings
7
,
33
, the uniform bounds for the solution
(v,h)
on
[0,T]
, and
\|h\|_{H^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\nabla h(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+b\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\nabla h_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+CR\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2},

with a constant

The last term in this estimate can be absorbed into the right-hand side, provided that

In particular, we may assume

and multiply the inequalities by

to obtain the following

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\Big(\|\nabla h(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+2\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{b}\Big(\|\nabla h_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big).

(5.7)

Step 2.

We test (
5.5
) with
\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v
and (
5.6
) with
-\nabla\!\Delta h\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
In a similar manner to before, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\Delta h(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)+b\|\nabla\!\Delta h\|_{L^{2}_{t}(L^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\Delta h_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\textup{div}\mspace{2mu}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)-a(h\nabla h_{t},\nabla\!\Delta h)_{\Omega_{t}}-a(\nabla hh_{t},\nabla\!\Delta h)_{\Omega_{t}}

with constants

Numerical illustration

In this section, we discuss the structure-preserving discretization of the nonlinear acoustic wave propagation models under investigation. After this, we also present some simulation results for the Westervelt, the Kutsnezov, and the Rasmussen equation, illustrating their similarities and differences.

6.1
Structure-preserving discretization

For the space discretization, one can use a conforming Galerkin approximation by mixed finite elements to approximate the functions
(v,h)
. In view of our analysis, a natural choice of spaces is

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}=P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{H}_{h}=P_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(6.1)

where

is the space of piecewise polynomials of order
\leq k
defined over some conforming simplicial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega

The semi-discretization of (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) then reads as follows: Find
(v_{h},h_{h}):[0,T]\to\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{H}_{h}
such that
v_{h}(0)=v_{0,h}
,
h_{h}(0)=h_{h,0}
given, and such that

\displaystyle(v_{h,t},w_{h})_{\Omega}+(\nabla h_{h},w_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

(6.2)

\displaystyle((1-ah_{h})h_{h,t},q_{h})_{\Omega}-(v_{h},\nabla q_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\,b(\nabla h_{h},\nabla q_{h})_{\Omega}-(c-d)(v_{h}\cdot\nabla h_{h},q_{h})_{\Omega}+d(h_{h},v_{h}\cdot\nabla q_{h})_{\Omega}

(6.3)

for all
w_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
and
q_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{h}

.
Assuming

As a direct consequence of the particular structure of the variational identities that define the discrete solution, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 6.1

(Structure-preservation, discrete power balance)
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}
,
\mathbb{H}_{h}
be chosen as above, and let
(v_{h},h_{h})
denote a discrete solution of (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(v_{h},v_{h})=-b\|\nabla h_{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+(2d-c)(v_{h}\cdot\nabla h_{h},h_{h})_{\Omega}.

In particular, for

the discrete energy is monotonically decreasing over time.

Proof 6.2
.

Similarly to the continuous level, the result follows by formally differentiating the energy, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(v_{h},h_{h})

\displaystyle=(v_{h,t},v_{h})_{\Omega}+((1-ah_{h})h_{h,t})_{\Omega}.

The right-hand side amounts to the first terms in (
6.2
)–(
6.3

and
q_{h}=h_{h}

The result then follows immediately from the two discrete variational identities.

Remark 6.3
.

The power balance and its proof for the continuous problem are inherited automatically by conforming Galerkin discretization in space.
Noting that the energy
\mathcal{H}(v,h)
is convex whenever
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}
is sufficiently small, one can employ variational time-discretization strategies to obtain corresponding results as well for fully-discrete schemes; see
6

36

7
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we analyzed a class of models for nonlinear acoustics, including the Westervelt and Kuznetsov equations, as well as a thermodynamically consistent modification of the latter proposed by Rasmussen.
By employing linearization, energy estimates, and fixed-point arguments, we have demonstrated the existence and uniqueness of solutions for these models.
Our results show that, for sufficiently small initial data, the solutions are global in time and converge exponentially fast to equilibrium.
While the Kuznetsov and Rasmussen models require similar implementation effort and yield very similar results in simulations, the latter enjoys thermodynamic consistency, allowing to guarantee energy-dissipation on the continuous and discrete level. This key property is directly encoded in the port-Hamiltonian structure of the weak form of the velocity-enthalpy formulation.

We showed that the geometric structure, encoded in the weak form of the problem, can be preserved by conforming Galerkin approximation, e.g., by appropriate mixed finite elements. We further indicated that fully–discrete structure-preserving discretization schemes can be obtained by variational time-discretization schemes.
Energy-decay was, however, also observed for simulations based on the implicit midpoint rule. For one- and two-dimensional problems, the fully-implicit time-stepping schemes turned out to be very stable and computationally effective. The Newton method converged consistently within a few iterations, demonstrating both the efficiency and reliability of the overall discretization approach.

In closing this section, we mention some directions for future research.
A natural extension of the models would be to include dispersive effects, which are critical to accurately capturing the behavior of acoustic waves in various media.
Apart from establishing mathematical well-posedness, the guarantee of thermodynamic consistency should be a particular focus.
Another direction would be a thorough analysis of discretization schemes for (
2.1
)–(
2.4
), including variational time-discretization and a full error analysis.
Establishing
L^{\infty}
bounds for the discrete solutions and studying the long-time behavior would be further topics of interest.
