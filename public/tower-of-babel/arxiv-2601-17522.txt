Resolvent, spectrum and resonances for the acoustic operator with piecewise constant coefficients
A. Mantile, A. Posilicano

Abstract
. We study the acoustic operator A v , ρ := v 2 ρ ∇ ⋅ ρ − 1 ∇ A_{v,\rho}:=v^{2}\rho\nabla\!\cdot\rho^{-1}\nabla with transmission conditions at the boundary of Ω = Ω 1 ∪ ⋯ ∪ Ω n \Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Omega_{n} , where the Ω ℓ \Omega_{\ell} ’s are connected disjoint open bounded Lipschitz domains, the positive functions v v and ρ \rho are constant on each connected component of Ω \Omega and v = ρ = 1 v=\rho=1 on ℝ 3 \ Ω ¯ \mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega} . Through a formula for the resolvents difference ( − A v , ρ + z ) − 1 − ( − Δ + z ) − 1 (-A_{v,\rho}+z)^{-1}-(-\Delta+z)^{-1} , we provide a Limiting Absorption Principle, determine the spectrum, which turns out to be purely absolutely continuous, and, in the case the connected components of Ω \Omega are of class 𝒞 1 , α \mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha} , characterize the resonance set. The second part of the paper is devoted to the case where Ω = Ω ⁡ ( ε ) \Omega=\Omega(\varepsilon) is connected with a small size ε \varepsilon and the ε \varepsilon -analytic functions v = v ⁡ ( ε ) v=v(\varepsilon) and/or ρ = ρ ⁡ ( ε ) \rho=\rho(\varepsilon) converge to 0 + 0_{+} inside Ω ⁡ ( ε ) \Omega(\varepsilon) as ε ↓ 0 \varepsilon\downarrow 0 ; there, we provide the analytic ε \varepsilon -expansions of the resonances of A v , ρ A_{v,\rho} according to different choices of the rate of convergence towards zero of the material parameters.



1.
Introduction

We consider acoustic operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(1.1)

A_{v,\rho}:=v^{2}\!\rho\nabla\!\!\cdot\!\rho^{-1}\nabla

whose material coefficients
v
and
\rho
, defining the speed of propagation of the waves and the density of the medium respectively, are piecewise constants and positive-valued
functions. More precisely, given
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n}
open, connected and bounded Lipschitz domains in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

\overline{\Omega}_{i}\cap\overline{\Omega}_{j}=\varnothing\,,\quad i\not=j\,,

and given the strictly positive constants
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, the material coefficients are

(1.2)

v:=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}v_{\ell}\,\chi_{\Omega_{\ell}}\,,\qquad\rho:=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\rho_{\ell}\,\chi_{\Omega_{\ell}}\,,

where
\Omega_{{\rm ex}}
is the connected domain
\Omega_{{\rm ex}}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
with
\Omega\equiv\Omega_{{\rm in}}:=\Omega_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Omega_{n}
;
\chi_{S}
denotes the characteristic function of a set
S
.

At the interfaces
\Gamma_{\ell}
’s given by the boundaries of the
\Omega_{\ell}
’s, we require the transmission conditions

(1.3)

\gamma_{0,\ell}^{\mathrm{ex}}u=\gamma_{0,\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}u\,,\qquad\rho_{\ell}\,\gamma_{1,\ell}^{\mathrm{ex}}u=\gamma_{1,\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}u\,,\qquad\ell=1,...n\,,

where
\gamma_{0,\ell}^{{\rm in}/{\rm ex}}
and
\gamma_{1,\ell}^{{\rm in}/{\rm ex}}
denote the lateral Dirichlet and Neumann traces (we refer to Sections 2 and 3 for the more detailed definitions in the appropriate functional spaces). Such boundary conditions ensure the symmetry of
A_{v,\rho}
in the weighted Hilbert space (equivalent, in the Banach space sense, to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
) given by

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},b^{-1})
, where
b:=v^{2}\!\rho=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}v_{\ell}^{2}\rho_{\ell}\,\chi_{\Omega_{\ell}}
. Further, see Theorem
3.1
,
A_{v,\rho}
turn out to be, on an appropriate domain, a not positive self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},b^{-1})
. This latter property entails the existence and uniqueness of the solutions of the Cauchy problem for the wave equation

(1.4)

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{t}^{2}}=A_{v,\rho}u

which models the dynamics of the acoustic waves in a medium with bulk modulus
b
. Indeed, by, e.g.,
[
7
, Proposition 3.14.4, Corollary 3.14.8 and Example 3.14.16]
, the unique (mild) solution of the Cauchy problem for (
1.4
) with initial data
u_{0}
and
\dot{u}_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given by

u(t)=\cos(t(-A_{v,\rho})^{1/2})\,u_{0}+(-A_{v,\rho})^{-1/2}\sin(t(-A_{v,\rho})^{1/2})\,\dot{u}_{0}\,,

where the sine and cosine operator functions are defined through the functional calculus for the self-adjoint
A_{v,\rho}
. Moreover, by the inversion of the Laplace-Stieltjes transform for operator valued functions (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.4]
), the above solution can be expressed in terms of the resolvent
(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}
. Such a resolvent, or better, a formula for the resolvent difference
(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}-(-\Delta-\kappa^{2})^{-1}
turns out to be our main tool in the study of the spectrum and resonances. This difference formula is obtained in Section 4 proceeding as follows: the heuristic
re-writing

A_{v,\rho}=v^{2}\rho\rho^{-1}\Delta u+v^{2}\rho\nabla\rho^{-1}\!\cdot\!\nabla u=\Delta u+({v^{2}}-1)\chi_{\Omega}\Delta+v^{2}\rho\nabla\rho^{-1}\!\cdot\!\nabla u

suggests that
A_{v,\rho}
correspond to the free Laplacian plus a
perturbation made of a regular component, supported on
\Omega
, and a
singular one (due to the jumps of
\rho^{-1}
), supported on its boundary
\Gamma
. This
picture emerges more precisely in a distributional framework; using the Green identities
and the boundary conditions, one gets

(1.5)

A_{v,\rho}u={\Delta}u+(v^{2}-1)1_{\Omega}^{\ast}\Delta_{\Omega}^{\text{{max}}}1_{\Omega}u+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{\ell}-1}{\rho_{\ell}+1}\,\gamma_{0,\ell}^{\ast}\,\gamma_{1,\ell}u\,,

where the
\gamma_{0,\ell}^{*}
’s denote the distributions supported on
\Gamma_{\ell}
corresponding to the duals of the full Dirichlet trace operators (we refer to Section 2 for more notation and definitions). By (
1.5
),
A_{v,\rho}
belongs to the class of operators of the kind

(1.6)

\Delta+B_{1}+(\gamma_{0}^{*}\oplus\gamma_{1}^{*})B_{2}(\gamma_{0}\oplus\gamma_{1})

where
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
denote the full Dirichlet and Neumann trace operators for the boundary
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Gamma_{n}
and
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are bounded operator in suitable function spaces. Operators of the kind (
1.6
), in the case
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are symmetric (this is not the case for
A_{v,\rho}
), were built and studied in depth in
[
24
]
on the basis of the abstract trace maps method for singular perturbations introduced in
[
32
]
and developed in
[
23
]
; this method produces self-adjoint operators
A_{\mathsf{B}}
which are mixed (i.e., regular plus singular) perturbations of a pivot self-adjoint
operator
A_{0}
(see
[
24
, Section 2]
. Let us notice that in the present case, contrarily to the ones studied in
[
24
]
,
A_{v,\rho}
and the free Laplacian
\Delta
are self-adjoint in different Hilbert spaces. In
[
24
]
, operators
A_{\mathsf{B}}
are built through a resolvent formula of the kind

(-A_{{\mathsf{B}}}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}=(-A_{0}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}+G_{\kappa}Q_{\kappa}^{-1}(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})G_{-\bar{\kappa}}^{*}\,,

where

Q_{\kappa}:=1-(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})\tau G_{\kappa}\,.

The
Q
-function
\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}
has a key role
in
[
24
]
: it encodes all the spectral properties of the
perturbation, it is naturally involved in the derivation of the Limiting
Absorption Principle (LAP for short) and in the proof of the asymptotic completeness of the
wave operators for the scattering couple
(A_{{\mathsf{B}}},A_{0})
.
In particular, an explicit formula for the scattering
amplitude (see
[
24
, Theorem 3.10]
) allows to
identify the resonances of
A_{{\mathsf{B}}}
with the poles in unphysical sheet Im
(\kappa)<0
of the meromorphic map
\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
.

In the first part of our work, we adapt the construction in
[
24
]

to the case of the acoustic operator
A_{v,\rho}
treated as a mixed perturbation of the free Laplacian
\Delta
. The main
outcome is the definition of the acoustic
Q
-function

(1.7)

Q_{\kappa}=\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}+({v^{2}}-1)\kappa^{2}N_{\kappa}&({v^{2}}-1)\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
-\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\kappa}&1-\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}\,,\qquad\widetilde{\rho}:=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{\ell}-1}{\rho_{\ell}+1}\,\chi_{\Gamma_{\ell}}

which is used to build a formula for the
resolvents difference
(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}-(-\Delta-\kappa^{2})^{-1}
,
holding for any
\operatorname{Im}\kappa>0
(see Theorems
4.3
and

4.6
). This provides a
direct framework for the study of the resonances. By the mapping properties of traces and by the abstract results from
[
34
]
, a Limiting Absorption Principle for the acoustic resolvent is
derived in Theorem
5.3
. The continuity of the map
(v,\rho)\mapsto A_{v,\rho}
is discussed in Section 6.

For the convenience of the reader, we summarize the main results of the first part of the paper in the following Theorem, referring to Sections 3 to 6 for more details and the proofs and to Section 2 for the definitions of the involved functional spaces and operators.

Theorem 1.1
.

i)
The self-adjoint representation in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},{b}^{-1})
of the
acoustic operator is given by

A_{v,\rho}=(\Delta_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}}^{\mathrm{max}}\oplus v^{2}\Delta_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{max}})|{\mathscr{D}}_{\rho}\,,

where

{\mathscr{D}}_{\rho}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{2}_{\Delta}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Gamma)\,:\,\rho_{\ell}\,\gamma_{1,\ell}^{\mathrm{ex}}u=\gamma_{1,\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}u\,,\ \ell=1,\dots,n\right\}\,.

Its spectrum is

\sigma(A_{v,\rho})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(A_{v,\rho})=(-\infty,0]\,.

ii)
For any

\kappa\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
there holds

(1.8)

(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}-(-\Delta-\kappa^{2})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{\ast}&S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}Q_{\kappa}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}({v^{2}}-1)\Delta_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}\\
\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix},\,

where
Q_{\kappa}
defined in (
1.7
).

iii)
(LAP) The limits

\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}(-A_{v,\rho}+\lambda\pm i\delta)^{-1}\,,

exist in
{{\mathscr{B}}}(L_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L_{-\alpha}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for any
\alpha>1/2
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
; the same hold true in the case
\lambda=0
whenever

\alpha>3/2
. The two
{{\mathscr{B}}}(L_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L_{-\alpha}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
-valued extended resolvents defined through such limits are continuous maps on
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
and on
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
respectively.

iv)
If, for any
1\leq\ell\leq n
, the sequence
\{v_{\ell}(j),\rho_{\ell}(j)\}_{j=1}^{+\infty}\subset(0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)
converges to
(v_{\ell},\rho_{\ell})\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)
as
j\to+\infty
, then
\{A_{v(j),\rho(j)}\}_{j=1}^{+\infty}
converges in norm resolvent sense to
A_{v,\rho}
. If
\rho_{\ell}\to+\infty
, then the norm resolvent limit still exits as a well defined closed operator
A_{v,+\infty}
.

The second part of the paper is devoted to resonances. The resonance problem for acoustic operators in discontinuous materials has
been addressed in previous works where the scattering resonances are defined
in terms of a transmission problem whose solutions are the quasi-modes of the
system, see
[
29
]
and references therein. The common approach
relies on the microlocal analysis of the transmission problem allowing to
construct high-energy quasimodes and the asymptotic characterization the
corresponding resonances, which is achieved by the quasi-modes to resonances
result, see e.g. in
[
30
, Proposition 2.1]
. When
\Omega
is
strictly convex with a smooth boundary, the acoustic operators
(
1.1
) fit into the scheme of such works. In particular, when

v^{2}\rho\in\left(0,1\right)

[
30
, Theorem 1.1]
predicts
the existence of an infinite sequence of resonances
\kappa_{j}^{2}
tending rapidly to the real axis.
In the opposite case,
v^{2}\rho>1
, there exists a resonance-free region of the form

\{\kappa^{2}:|\operatorname{Re}(\kappa)|>C_{1}\,,\ \operatorname{Im}(\kappa)\leq-C_{2}/|\kappa|\}
, see
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
. Such results have been
extended to the case of Lipschitz star-shaped obstacles,
[
29
]
.

We elaborate a direct approach to resonances which
relies on the resolvent difference formula (
1.8
). The framework is provided
in Section 7, where, under the hypothesis that
\Omega
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
, it is shown that
\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
is an operator-valued meromorphic map with poles of finite rank

\kappa_{\circ}\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
fulfilling the condition
\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}
. Then, in view of the results of
[
24
]
, where the
inverse
Q
-function determines the scattering amplitude, we are lead to identify the
resonances as those
\kappa_{\circ}^{2}
such that
\operatorname{Im}\kappa_{\circ}<0
and
\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}
. This definition turns out to be equivalent to the ones given by the blackbox formalism, see
[
36
]
,
[
13
, Chapter
4]
. Indeed, from (
1.8
) there follows that

\kappa\mapsto(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}
, outside the poles of

\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
, is analytic as a map from

L_{comp}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to
L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The link between these two equivalent definitions of resonances has been exploited in
[
15
]
as regards
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}

purely singular perturbation supported on a boundary. Furthermore, by Lemma
7.2
, the poles of
\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
can not lie on the real line and so our definition of the resonance set simplifies to

(1.9)

\text{$\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}$ is a resonance of $A_{v,\rho}$}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}\,.

A scale-dependent material design is introduced in Sections 8 where it is assumed

(1.10)

\Omega_{\ell}(\varepsilon)=y_{\ell}+\varepsilon\Omega_{\circ}\,,\qquad\varepsilon\Omega_{\circ}:=\{\varepsilon x,\ x\in\Omega_{\circ}\}\,,\qquad\Omega_{i}(\varepsilon)\cap\Omega_{j}(\varepsilon)=\varnothing\text{ if }i\not=j\,,

\Omega_{\circ}
is open, bounded, connected and of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
,
y_{1},\dots y_{n}

a collection of distinct points and the material coefficients
v=v(\varepsilon)
and

\rho=\rho(\varepsilon)
depend on
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. By dilating and translating, each
\Omega_{\ell}(\varepsilon)
is transformed into the pivot domain
\Omega_{\circ}
and one gets a recipe for calculating the resonances involving the volume
and surface operators of
\Omega_{\circ}
(see
(
8.1
)). This prepares the framework for the asymptotic regimes
considered in Section 9. Here, we consider the simpler medium containing a single
inhomogeneity
\Omega(\varepsilon)=y+\varepsilon\Omega
fulfilling the scaling (
1.10
), while the
material parameters belong to one of the following four cases:

case 1)

(1.11)

v^{2}(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon^{2}\big(v^{2}+v_{1}^{2}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,,\quad\rho(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\left(1+\rho_{1}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,;

case 2)

(1.12)

v^{2}(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\left(1+v_{1}^{2}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,,\qquad\rho(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon^{2}\big(\rho+O(\varepsilon)\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,;

case 3)

(1.13)

v^{2}(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon\big(v^{2}+v_{1}^{2}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,,\qquad\rho(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon\big(\rho+\rho_{1}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,;

case 4)

(1.14)

v^{2}(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon^{2}\big(v^{2}+v_{1}^{2}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,,\quad\rho(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\Omega_{{\rm ex}}(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon\big(\rho+\rho_{1}\,\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\big)\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}\,.

We denote by
A(\varepsilon)
the
\varepsilon
-dependent operator corresponding to such cases.
The results of our analysis are
resumed in the next theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
\varepsilon
small enough, there exist resonances

\kappa_{\pm}^{2}(\varepsilon)
of
A(\varepsilon)
such that the
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
-valued functions

\varepsilon\mapsto\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})
are analytic, where:

case 1)

\kappa_{0}=\frac{v}{\lambda^{1/2}}\,,\qquad\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2v\lambda^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{1}v{\lambda^{1/2}}\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)-i\,\frac{v^{2}}{8\pi\lambda^{2}}\,\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

\lambda>0
is a simple eigenvalue of the Newton potential operator
N_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
e_{\lambda}
is the corresponding eigenfunction;

case 2)

\kappa_{0}=\rho^{1/2}\,\omega_{M}\,,\qquad\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2}\,\rho^{1/2}\omega_{M}-i\,\frac{|\Omega|}{8\pi}\,\rho\,\omega_{M}^{4}\,,

\omega_{M}>0
the ”Minnaert resonance” defined by

\omega_{M}^{2}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\,\langle S_{0}^{-1}1,1\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\,;

case 3)

\kappa_{0}=\rho^{1/2}\,\omega_{M}
, the expression for
\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}
can be computed but for the sake of brevity is not written explicitly;

case 4)

\kappa_{0}=v\,\nu^{1/2}\,,\qquad\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\,\frac{1}{2}\,{\nu}^{1/2}\left(\rho\,v\,\langle\gamma_{0}u_{\nu},\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}-v_{1}^{2}v^{-1}\right),

\nu>0
is a simple eigenvalue of the positive Neumann Laplacian
-\Delta_{\Omega}^{N}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u_{\nu}
the corresponding normalized eigenfunction and

\phi_{\nu}:=S_{0}^{-1}\gamma_{0}({\nu}^{-1}-N_{0})u_{\nu}\,.

Furthermore, there exist resonances
\kappa^{2}_{\pm}(\varepsilon^{1/2})
of
A(\varepsilon)
, emerging from the zero eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\Omega}^{N}
, such that the
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
-valued function

\varepsilon^{1/2}\mapsto\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon^{1/2})=\pm\rho^{1/2}\omega_{M}(1+\kappa_{1}\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{1/2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

is analytic; the constant
\kappa_{1}
is explicitly computed.

Some of the acoustic resonances detected in Theorem
1.2
have
counterparts in the corresponding scattering problems at a fixed frequency. We refer to
those localization phenomena of scattering of waves with a fixed frequency close to small inhomogeneities which are observed in different systems where the
material parameters at the inhomogeneity sites are highly contrasted with
respect to the background, see
[
12
]
. Such behaviors, roughly
corresponding to quasi-modes, occur at specific incoming frequencies referred
to as ’resonances’ of these micro-resonators. In acoustics, specific asymptotic
assumptions can excite localized quasi-modes with support concentrated on the
volume or on the surface of the resonator. The stationary acoustic wave
equation in the regime (
1.11
) has been considered, among others, in

[
11
]
(see also
[
3
]
for an equivalent
dielectric problem), while the regime (
1.12
) was considered in
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
(with applications to the acoustic
imaging) and
[
28
]
(where the localization effect was
interpreted in terms of the behavior of a frequency-dependent resolvent).
In the first case, volume
quasi-modes are observed at frequencies
\lambda^{-1/2}
corresponding to the
eigenvalues
\lambda
of the Newton potential operator
N_{0}
. In the
second case, a surface quasi-mode is detected at the Minnaert frequency

\omega_{M}
. The results for cases 1 and 2 in Theorem
1.2

confirm the common wisdom that localization phenomena in
micro-resonator systems correspond to resonances of the
generator of the dynamics: at the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1.2

provides a first proof of this correspondence.

Several works on the stationary analysis of micro-resonator quasi-modes follow
the Gohberg-Sigal scheme based on a generalized Rouché theorem for
meromorphic operator-valued functions. In Section 9 we adopt a different
strategy: after recasting the resonance problem in terms of a suitable
functional equation, the localization of resonances rests on implicit function
arguments and, in the case (
1.14
), to detect the resonances emerging from
0\in\mathbb{R}
, on a further Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction. This approach presents some novelties. At first, it provides solutions which are analytic functions with respect to the small parameter
\varepsilon
(or
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
in
the (
1.14
) case with zero energy); moreover, it provides a recursive algorithm for computing
the coefficients of the analytic expansion of the solution at any order. It is worth
noticing that although the computation of the higher-order corrections may be a difficult task, such a difficulty is only due to the length of the computations and no technical difficulty beyond the ones already overcame at the first-order level appears.

Our results, in cases (
1.11
) and (
1.14
), require a simple spectrum for the Newton operator
N_{0}
and for the Neumann Laplacian
\Delta_{\Omega}^{N}
respectively. It is known that this is a generically true hypothesis as regards
\Delta_{\Omega}^{N}
and we conjecture that the same is true as regards
N_{0}
. We expect that the same kind of results as in Theorem
1.2
hold even without such an assumption on the spectra, by using a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction and proceeding along the lines of our proof of case 4 for the zero energy-case (also see the approach to the branching of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Fredholm operators in
[
39
, Section 32]
).

A specific feature of micro-resonator regimes is the emergency of finite-energy
resonances asymptotically close to the continuous spectrum. It is worth
noticing that this does not exclude the existence of other resonances. In
particular, as recalled above,
[
30
]
predicts the existence of a
family of acoustic resonances
\kappa_{j}^{2}
such that

\operatorname{Re}(\kappa_{j}^{2})\to+\infty
and
|\text{Im}(\kappa_{j})|\leq C_{N}|\kappa_{j}|^{-N}
for any
N\gg 1
. Such result applies to each of the cases
considered in Theorem
1.2
. However, due to the scaling, the
energy-range of such resonances shifts at
+\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Due to this fact, we
argue that only the micro-resonator finite-energy resonances are expected to
have relevant contributions to the dynamics in the asymptotic limit.

The spectral and scattering theory for operators of the kind
A_{v,1}

were considered in
[
41
, Sections II.5 and III.6]
, while a corresponding
micro-resonator setting with
v^{2}(\varepsilon)=\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Omega(\varepsilon)}+\varepsilon^{2}\chi_{\Omega(\varepsilon)}
and
\rho=1
was investigated in
[
25
]
(there, no hypothesis on the simplicity of the spectrum of
N_{0}
was assumed).
The configurations (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) generalize the
ones considered in several previous works on acoustic quasi-modes, while we
underline that neither resonances nor scattering-resonances in the settings
(
1.13
) and (
1.14
) have been considered before. In particular, the
emergence of a system of resonances converging to
\sigma(-\Delta_{\Omega}^{N})
when
v(\varepsilon)
and

\rho(\varepsilon)
converge to zero with the same rate was not
observed so far. We are not aware of other contributions to
resonance problems in micro-resonator settings beside the preprint

[
22
]
dealing with a simplified
version of the Minnaert regime (
1.12
); comparing (
1.7
) with the operator in
[
22
, Section 5]
, the scattering resonances defined there coincide with the resonances defined by (
1.9
).

\bullet

w_{v}:=v^{2}(v^{2}-1)^{-1}\,,\qquad w_{+\infty}:=1\,,\qquad z_{\rho}:=\widetilde{\rho}^{\,-1}\qquad z_{+\infty}:=\frac{1}{2}\,.

Since

(Q^{\flat,\natural}_{0})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{\flat}^{-1}&0\\
w_{\flat}^{-1}\left(z_{\natural}-\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)^{-1}\!\!\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}&\left(z_{\natural}-\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

by the continuity of
\kappa\mapsto Q^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}
, the inverse
(Q^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa})^{-1}
exists and is a bounded operator for any
\kappa
in a sufficiently small complex neighborhood of the origin
U_{0}
. Hence,

(6.1)

R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}:=R_{\kappa}+\begin{bmatrix}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{*}&S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}(Q_{k}^{\flat,\natural})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}

is a well defined bounded operator for any
\kappa\in U_{0}\cap{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
.
Since

\|(-A_{v(j),\rho(j)}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}-R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}\|\to 0
as
(0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)\ni(v_{\ell}(j),\rho_{\ell}(j))\to(\flat,\natural)
, the map
-\kappa^{2}\mapsto R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}
is a pseudo-resolvent. It is the resolvent of a closed operator
A_{\flat,\natural}
whenever
R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}
is injective for some
\kappa\in U_{0}\cap{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
\flat\not=+\infty
, then
R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}
is injective for any
\kappa\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
such that
Q^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}
has a bounded inverse.

Proof.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be such that
R^{\flat,\natural}_{\kappa}u_{0}=0
, i.e.,

R_{k}u_{0}=-\begin{bmatrix}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{*}&S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}(Q_{k}^{\flat,\natural})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

By

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}\\
\gamma_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{*}&S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}N_{k}&\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
\gamma_{1}N_{k}&\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1-\kappa^{2}N_{k}&-\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
\gamma_{1}N_{k}&\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}w_{\flat}-1&0\\
0&z_{\natural}\end{bmatrix}-Q_{k}^{\flat,\natural}\,,

there follows

\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\left(1-\begin{bmatrix}w_{\flat}-1&0\\
0&z_{\natural}\end{bmatrix}(Q_{k}^{\flat,\natural})^{-1}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\end{bmatrix},

equivalently,

(6.2)

\begin{bmatrix}w_{\flat}-1&0\\
0&z_{\natural}\end{bmatrix}(Q_{k}^{\flat,\natural})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}u_{0}\end{bmatrix}=0\,.

If
w_{\flat}\not=1
, then, by (
6.2
),
\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}u\\
\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}u\end{bmatrix}=0
; this entails
R_{\kappa}u=0
and so
u=0
.
∎

Consequently, one has the following

Theorem 6.3
.

If, for any
1\leq\ell\leq n
, the sequence
\{v_{\ell}(j),\rho_{\ell}(j)\}_{j=1}^{+\infty}\subset(0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)
converges to
(v_{\ell},+\infty)
,
v_{\ell}\not=1
, as
j\to+\infty
, then
\{A_{v(j),\rho(j)}\}_{j=1}^{+\infty}
converges in norm resolvent sense to the closed operator
A_{v,+\infty}
with resolvent
R^{v,+\infty}_{\kappa}
given by (
6.1
).

Remark 6.4
.

The analogous of Theorem
6.3
for the case
v_{1}=\dots=v_{n}=1
follows by using the resolvent in Example
4.2
. One has that
A_{1,\rho(j)}
converges in norm resolvent sense to
A_{1,+\infty}
, with

(A_{1,+\infty}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}=R_{\kappa}+S\!L_{\kappa}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\right)^{\!\!-1}\!\!\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}\,.

The latter is injective by the same kind of reasonings as in the proof of Lemma
6.2
.

Remark 6.5
.

Looking at the resolvent in Example
4.1
, one gets that
v_{\ell}(j)\to+\infty
, implies the norm convergence of
(-A_{v(j),1}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}
to the pseudo-resolvent

R_{\kappa}+R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{*}(1+\kappa^{2}N_{\kappa})^{-1}\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}\,.

However, the latter is not injective and hence is not the resolvent of a closed operator. Indeed, one can easily check that any function of the kind
1_{\Omega}^{*}u_{\Omega}
,
u_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
belongs to its kernel.

Remark 6.6
.

The closed operator
A_{v,+\infty}
is relevant in acoustic modeling only if it is the generator of a operator cosine function, so that the Cauchy problem for the wave equation
\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{t}^{2}}=A_{v,+\infty}u
is solvable. In the not self-adjoint case (as for
A_{v,+\infty}
), this can be a not trivial problem, even if one has at disposal the resolvent (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 2.1, Chapter II]
).

7.
Resonances

From now on, we suppose that the boundary
\Gamma
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
, i.e., it can be represented locally as the graph of a
function having Hölder-continuous, with exponent
\alpha
, first-order derivatives (see, e.g.,
[
21
, page 90]
).

Lemma 7.1
.

The map

{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}:=\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}+({v^{2}}-1)\kappa^{2}N_{\kappa}&({v^{2}}-1)\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
-\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\kappa}&1-\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}

is a
\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
-valued analytic map and
{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
is a
\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
-valued meromorphic map with poles of finite rank. Such poles are the points
\kappa_{\circ}\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
such that
\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Rewriting
Q_{\kappa}
as

Q_{\kappa}=\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\left(\,\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}v^{-2}({v^{2}}-1)\kappa^{2}&0\\
0&-\widetilde{\rho}\,\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}N_{\kappa}&1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\kappa}&\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}\,\right),

by meromorphic Fredholm theory (see, e.g.,
[
33
, Theorem XIII.13]
) it is enough to show that

(7.1)

{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}N_{\kappa}&1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\kappa}&\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}

is a
\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
-valued analytic map.

By, e.g.,
[
25
, Lemma 3.1]
,
N_{\kappa}
is Hilbert-Schmidt and hence compact. One gets
1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(H^{-1/2}(\Gamma),L^{2}(\Omega))

by
1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\kappa}\in{\mathscr{B}}(H^{-1/2}(\Gamma),H^{1}(\Omega))
and by the compact embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
.
It is known that the Neumann-Poincaré operator
K_{0}
belongs to
\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(H^{1/2}(\Gamma))
(here we need the
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
hypothesis on
\Gamma
, see, e.g.,
[
18
]
),
\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}=K_{0}^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
. The same argument (see, e.g.,
[
18
, equation (15)]
) gives

\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\kappa}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
.

Finally,
\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{\kappa}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))
by
\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{\kappa}\in{\mathscr{B}}(L^{2}(\Omega),H^{1/2}(\Gamma))
and by the compact embedding
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
.
∎

The analogy with the case of Schrödinger operators
suggests to define the resonances of
A_{v,\rho}
as the poles of the
meromorphic extension of the map
z\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}(-A_{v,\rho}-z)^{-1}1^{*}_{B_{1}}
in the
non-physical sheet where
\text{Im}(\sqrt{z})<0
; here,
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are any two open bounded sets. As is well known, the map
{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}R_{\kappa}1^{*}_{B_{1}}

is analytic. This entails the analyticity of the maps

{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}S\!L_{\kappa}
,
{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto\Delta_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{max}}1^{*}_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}1_{B_{1}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}1_{B_{1}}
. Therefore, taking into account the resolvent formula in Theorem
4.6
and Lemma
7.1
, the analytic
{\mathscr{B}}(L^{2}(B_{1}),L^{2}(B_{2}))
-valued function
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\ni\kappa\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})1^{*}_{B_{1}}
has a
meromorphic
{\mathscr{B}}(L^{2}(B_{1}),L^{2}(B_{2}))
-valued extension

(7.2)

{\mathbb{C}}\ni\kappa\mapsto 1_{B_{1}}R_{\kappa}1^{*}_{B_{2}}+1_{B_{1}}\begin{bmatrix}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega}^{\ast}&S\!L_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}Q_{\kappa}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}({v^{2}}-1)\Delta_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{max}}1_{\Omega}R_{\kappa}\\
\widetilde{\rho}\,\gamma_{1}R_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}1^{*}_{B_{2}}\,.

Since the operator (for brevity we denote it by
L
) on the right of
Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
in (
7.2
) has range which is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
and the one on the left is injective, the poles of the map in (
7.2
) coincides with the points
\kappa_{\circ}
such that
\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}
. Otherwise, denoting by
\text{\rm ran}(L)_{\|}
the orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace
\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})
, we would have
\text{\rm ran}(L)_{\|}\cap\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})=\{0\}
which would imply
\text{\rm ran}(L)\subseteq\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})^{\perp}
, in contradiction with the density of
\text{\rm ran}(L)
.

Therefore, we define the set of resonances of
A_{v,\rho}
as the discrete set

(7.3)

\mathsf{e}(A_{v,\rho}):=\{\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}:\kappa_{\circ}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\ \text{and}\ \ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\},\ \}\,.

This definition of resonance set is in agreement with the one given in
[
1
, Appendix B]
.
To avoid any misunderstanding, let us remark that in some literature the term ”resonances” refers to
\kappa_{\circ}
instead of
\kappa_{\circ}^{2}
, see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.

The definition (
7.3
) can be somewhat simplified because the condition
\kappa\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
is automatically satisfied by the points
\kappa_{\circ}
for which
Q_{\kappa_{\circ}}
is not injective:

Lemma 7.2
.

\mathsf{e}(A_{v,\rho})=\big\{\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ker(Q_{\kappa_{\circ}})\not=\{0\}\big\}\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
,
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\ni\kappa\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}1^{*}_{B_{1}}
has a continuous
{\mathscr{B}}(L^{2}(B_{1}),L^{2}(B_{2}))
-valued extension to
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Since the meromorphic
{\mathscr{B}}(L^{2}(B_{1}),L^{2}(B_{2}))
-valued function (
7.2
)
coincides with
\mapsto 1_{B_{2}}(-A_{v,\rho}-\kappa^{2})^{-1}1^{*}_{B_{1}}
when restricted to
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, it is continuous on
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Hence, the only possible real pole of
\kappa\mapsto Q_{\kappa}^{-1}
is
\kappa=0
. However,
Q_{0}
has a bounded inverse (see the proof of Lemma
4.5
) and the proof is done.
∎

8.
The
\varepsilon
-dependent case

Given the
(0,+\infty)
-valued functions
\varepsilon\mapsto v_{\ell}^{2}(\varepsilon)=v^{2}(\varepsilon)
,
\varepsilon\mapsto\rho_{\ell}(\varepsilon)=\rho(\varepsilon)
, we consider the family of acoustic operators

A(\varepsilon):={v^{2}(\varepsilon)\rho(\varepsilon)}\nabla\!\cdot\!{\rho(\varepsilon)}^{-1}\nabla\,,\qquad 0<\epsilon\leq 1\,,

with domain

{\text{\rm dom}}(A(\varepsilon))=\big\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L_{\Delta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Gamma(\varepsilon))\,:\,\rho(\varepsilon)\,\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\varepsilon)u=\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}(\varepsilon)u\big\}\,.

Here
\gamma^{{\rm in}/{\rm ex}}_{1}(\varepsilon)
denote the inner/exterior Neumann traces at
\Gamma(\varepsilon):=\partial\Omega(\varepsilon)
, where

\Omega(\varepsilon)=\Omega_{1}(\varepsilon)\cup\dots\cup\Omega_{n}(\varepsilon)\,,\qquad\Omega_{\ell}(\varepsilon)=y_{\ell}+\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)\,,\qquad y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon):=\{\varepsilon x,\ x\in\Omega_{\circ}\}

and the parameter
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small so that
\Omega_{i}(\varepsilon)\cap\Omega_{j}(\varepsilon)=\varnothing
whenever
i\not=j
.

By Example
4.3
and Lemma
7.2
, the resonance set of
A(\varepsilon)
is

\mathsf{e}(A(\varepsilon))=\big\{\kappa^{2}(\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ker(\widetilde{Q}_{\kappa(\varepsilon)}(\varepsilon))\not=\{0\}\,\big\}\,,

where

\widetilde{Q}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon):\big(\!\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)\big)\!\oplus\!\big(\!\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ}(\varepsilon)\big)\to\big(\!\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)\big)\!\oplus\!\big(\!\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ}(\varepsilon)\big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\text{mat}(v^{2}(\varepsilon)+(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}^{*})&({v^{2}(\varepsilon)}-1)\kappa^{2}\text{mat}(1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon))\\
\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\text{mat}(\gamma^{\circ{\rm in}}_{1}(\varepsilon)1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}^{*})&\text{mat}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma^{\circ}_{1}(\varepsilon)\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon))\end{bmatrix}

with
S\!L^{\circ}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)
and
\gamma^{\circ}_{1}(\varepsilon)
denoting the operators corresponding to the domain
\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)
.

Given the map
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon x
such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\Omega_{\circ}})={\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}
, we define

\Psi_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\qquad\Psi_{\varepsilon}u:=u\circ\varphi_{\varepsilon}

\Phi_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon))\to L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ})\,,\qquad\Phi_{\ell}u:=u\circ(\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\Omega_{\circ})

and

\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\Gamma_{\circ}(\varepsilon))\to L^{2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\,,\qquad\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\phi:=\phi\circ(\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\Gamma_{\circ})\,.

One has the identities

1_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}=\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\,,\qquad 1^{*}_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}=\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}1^{*}_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,,

\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega_{\circ}(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon^{-2}\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,,\qquad R_{\kappa}=\varepsilon^{2}\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}R_{\varepsilon\kappa}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\,,

S\!L^{\circ}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}S\!L^{\circ}_{\varepsilon\kappa}\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\,,\qquad\gamma_{1}^{\circ}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\gamma^{\circ}_{1}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\,.

These relations give, by
\Psi_{\varepsilon}\Psi_{\ell m}\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}=\Psi_{\ell m}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{\varepsilon}&0\\
0&\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\times

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}\text{mat}(v^{2}(\varepsilon)+(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\varepsilon\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}^{*})&({v^{2}(\varepsilon)}-1)\varepsilon\kappa^{2}\text{mat}(1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\varepsilon\kappa})\\
\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\varepsilon\,\text{mat}(\gamma^{\circ{\rm in}}_{1}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\varepsilon\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}^{*})&\text{mat}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma^{\circ}_{1}\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\varepsilon\kappa})\end{bmatrix}\times

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{\varepsilon}&0\\
0&\Phi^{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\end{bmatrix}.

Noticing that for a block operator matrix
\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}
there holds

\ker\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\not=\{0\}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\ker\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-a}A&\varepsilon^{-b}B\\
\varepsilon^{-c}\,C&\varepsilon^{-d}D\end{bmatrix}\not=\{0\}

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
a,b,c,d
such that
a+d=b+c
,
we get, by Lemma
7.2
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{e}(A(\varepsilon))=

\displaystyle\{\kappa^{2}(\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ker\big(M^{(a,b,c)}_{\kappa(\varepsilon)}(\varepsilon)\big)\not=\{0\}\,\}\,,

where

M^{(a,b,c)}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon):\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ})\big)\oplus\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\big)\to\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ})\big)\oplus\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\big)\,,

\displaystyle M_{\kappa}^{(a,b,c)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\text{mat}(v^{2}(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{-a}+(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\varepsilon^{-a+2}\,\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\varepsilon\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}^{*})&({v^{2}(\varepsilon)}-1)\varepsilon^{-b+1}\,\kappa^{2}\text{mat}(1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\varepsilon\kappa})\\
\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\varepsilon^{-c+1}\,\text{mat}(\gamma^{\circ{\rm in}}_{1}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}\Psi_{\ell m}R_{\varepsilon\kappa}1_{\Omega_{\circ}}^{*})&\varepsilon^{-(b+c-a)}\,\text{mat}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma^{\circ}_{1}\Psi_{\ell m}S\!L^{\circ}_{\varepsilon\kappa})\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, if there exists a map

\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}L^{2}(\Omega_{\circ})\big)\oplus\big(\oplus_{\ell=1}^{n}H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\big)

such that

(8.1)

u(\varepsilon)\not=0\,,\quad\phi(\varepsilon)\not=0\,,\qquad M^{(a,b,c)}_{\kappa(\varepsilon)}(\varepsilon)\begin{bmatrix}u(\varepsilon)\\
\phi(\varepsilon)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},

then

\kappa^{2}(\varepsilon)\in\mathsf{e}(A(\varepsilon))\,.

9.
Resonances
\varepsilon
-expansions

In this Section, we look for the resonance of
A(\varepsilon)
in the case
n=1
; hence, we set
\Omega_{\circ}=\Omega
and avoid the use of the apex
{\,}{}^{\circ}
in denoting operators. We study the four cases, differing by the behavior for
\varepsilon\ll 1
of the analytic functions
\varepsilon\mapsto v^{2}(\varepsilon)\chi_{\Omega}
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\rho(\varepsilon)\chi_{\Omega}
, indicated in (
1.11
)-(
1.14
) in the Introduction. We denote by
A_{j}(\varepsilon)
,
j=1,2,3,4
, the corresponding acoustic operators.

Inspired by
[
35
]
, in order to solve (
8.1
), we use the following implicit function argument: let

F:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\to\Xi

be an analytic map, where
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is an open neighborhood of
0
and
\Xi
is a complex Hilbert space with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|
. We look for an analytic map

f:U_{0}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\left(\Xi\backslash\{0\}\right)\,,

where
U_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a small open neighborhood of
0
, which uniquely solve the equation

{F}(z,f(z))=0\,.

Suppose there exists
(\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\left(\Xi\backslash\{0\}\right)
such that
{F}(0,\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})=0
. Then, given such a
(\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})
, we define the map

\Phi:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi,\qquad\Phi(z,\zeta,\xi):=(\langle\xi_{0},\xi-\xi_{0}\rangle,{F}(z,\zeta,\xi))\,.

Obviously,
\Phi(0,\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})=(0,0)
. Let us denote by
D_{0}F:{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\to\Xi

the Frechet differential at
(0,\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})
of the map
(\zeta,\xi)\mapsto F(0,\zeta,\xi)
. By the implicit function theorem for Banach space-valued analytic functions (see, e.g.,
[
40
]
), if the bounded linear map

D_{0}\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\,,\qquad D_{0}\Phi(\zeta,\xi):=(\langle\xi_{0},\xi\rangle,D_{0}F(\zeta,\xi))

is a bijection (and hence has a bounded inverse by the open mapping theorem), then there exists
U_{0}\subseteq U
, an open neighborhood of
0
, and an unique analytic function

f:U_{0}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\Xi\,,\qquad f(z)\equiv(\zeta(z),\xi(z))\,,

such that
\Phi(z,f(z))=(0,0)
. Therefore, by the definition of
\Phi
,

\begin{cases}\langle\xi_{0},\xi(z)\rangle=\|\xi_{0}\|^{2}\\
F(z,f(z))=0\,.\end{cases}

Hence, by
\xi_{0}\in\Xi\backslash\{0\}
, one gets
\xi(z)\in\Xi\backslash\{0\}
. Furthermore,

f(z)=f(0)-(D_{0}\Phi)^{-1}(0,\partial_{0}F(\zeta_{0}))\,z+O(|z|^{2})\,,

where
\partial_{0}F:{\mathbb{C}}\to\Xi
denotes the partial derivative with respect to the first variable
z
, calculated at the point
(0,\zeta_{0},\xi_{0})
. Let us remark that all the coefficients of the expansion of
f
beyond the first one can be computed recursively, as in the finite dimensional case.

9.1.
Case 1.

Let us define the map

{F}_{1}:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times X\to X

\displaystyle{F}_{1}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi):=M^{(2,1,1)}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{-2}+(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\kappa^{2}N_{\varepsilon\kappa}&({v^{2}(\varepsilon)}-1)\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\varepsilon\kappa})\\
\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\varepsilon\kappa}&\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\varepsilon\kappa}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix},

where
X=L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
,

U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a small open neighborhood of the origin and
\varepsilon\mapsto v^{2}(\varepsilon)
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\rho(\varepsilon)
are analytic function as in (
1.11
).

We look for a solution
\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\times(X\backslash\{0\})
of the equation

(9.1)

{F}_{1}(\varepsilon,\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))=0\,.

By the analyticity of the operator-valued maps
z\mapsto N_{z}
and
z\mapsto S\!L_{z}
(see, e.g.,
[
28
]
), writing

N_{z}=N_{0}+N_{(1)}z+O(|z|^{2})\,,\qquad S\!L_{z}=S\!L_{0}+S\!L_{(1)}z+O(|z|^{2})\,,

and by

\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}=-\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}\,\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,,

one gets

\displaystyle{F}_{1}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}-\kappa^{2}N_{0}&-\kappa^{2}\,1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}^{2}-\kappa^{3}N_{(1)}&-\kappa^{3}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{(1)}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\,{\rho_{1}}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}&-\frac{1}{2}\,{\rho_{1}}\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})

Therefore,

\displaystyle{F}_{1}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=0\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}(v^{2}-\kappa^{2}N_{0})u-\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi=0\\
\frac{1}{2}\,\phi=0\end{cases}\!\!\!\!\!\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}(v^{2}-\kappa^{2}N_{0})u=0\\
\phi=0\,.\end{cases}

Hence,

{F}_{1}(0,\pm{v}{\lambda^{-1/2}},e_{\lambda}\oplus 0)=0\,,

whenever
e_{\lambda}
is a normalized eigenfunction, with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda>0
, of the Newton operator
N_{0}
corresponding to the connected domain
\Omega
.

Now, we introduce the map

(9.2)

\Phi_{1}:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times X\to{\mathbb{C}}\times X\,,\qquad\Phi_{1}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi):=(\langle e_{\lambda},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-1,{F}_{1}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi))\,.

Obviously,

\Phi_{1}(0,\pm{v}{\lambda^{-1/2}},e_{\lambda}\oplus 0)=(0,0)\,.

Let us
denote by

D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}:{\mathbb{C}}\times X\to{\mathbb{C}}\times X

the Frechet differential at
(0,\pm{v}{\lambda^{-1/2}},e_{\lambda}\oplus 0)
of the map
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\mapsto\Phi_{1}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)
, i.e.,

D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=\left(\langle e_{\lambda},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\big(\mp 2v\lambda^{1/2}\kappa e_{\lambda}+v^{2}(1-\lambda^{-1}N_{0})u-v^{2}\lambda^{-1}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi\big)\oplus\frac{1}{2}\,\phi\right)\,.

Now, for any given
(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\in{\mathbb{C}}\times X
, we prove
the existence of a unique solution
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\in{\mathbb{C}}\times X
to the equation

(9.3)

D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\,.

Setting

w:=u_{*}+2v^{2}\lambda^{-1}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{*}\,,

equation (
9.3
) recasts as

(9.4)

\begin{cases}\langle e_{\lambda},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=z_{*}\\
\mp 2v\lambda^{1/2}\kappa e_{\lambda}+v^{2}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda-N_{0})u=w\\
\frac{1}{2}\,\phi=\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

Introducing the orthogonal decompositions

u=\widehat{u}_{\lambda}\,e_{\lambda}+u_{\perp}\,,\quad w=\widehat{w}_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}+w_{\perp}\,,

where

\widehat{u}_{\lambda}:=\langle e_{\lambda},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,\quad\widehat{w}_{\lambda}:=\langle e_{\lambda},w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,\qquad\langle e_{\lambda},u_{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle e_{\lambda},w_{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\,,

(
9.3
) rewrites as

\begin{cases}\widehat{u}_{\lambda}=z_{*}\\
\mp 2v\lambda^{1/2}\kappa=\widehat{w}_{\lambda}\\
v^{2}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda-N_{0})u_{\perp}=w_{\perp}\\
\frac{1}{2}\,\phi=\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

We define the linear operator

(\lambda-N_{0})_{\perp}:\ker(\lambda-N_{0})^{\perp}\to\ker(\lambda-N_{0})^{\perp}\,,\quad(\lambda-N_{0})_{\perp}:=(\lambda-N_{0})|\ker(\lambda-N_{0})^{\perp}\,;

since
N_{0}
is compact and self-adjoint (hence,
\ker(\lambda-N_{0})^{\perp}=\text{\rm ran}(\lambda-N_{0})
), it is a bounded bijection.

If
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue then
w_{\perp}\in\ker(\lambda-N_{0})^{\perp}
and the unique solution of (
9.3
) is given by

(9.5)

\begin{cases}\kappa=\mp(2v\lambda^{1/2})^{-1}\langle e_{\lambda},u_{*}+2v^{2}\lambda^{-1}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
u=z_{*}+{\lambda}{v^{-2}}\,(\lambda-N_{0})_{\perp}^{-1}\big(u_{*}+2v^{2}\lambda^{-1}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{*}\big)_{\perp}\\
\phi=2\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

Therefore, whenever
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue,
D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}
is a bounded bijection and, by the implicit function argument recalled at the beginning of the Section, in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a
{\mathbb{C}}\times(X\backslash\{0\})
-valued, analytic function
\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon),u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon))
,

\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm\frac{v}{\lambda^{1/2}}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\kappa_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=e_{\lambda}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}u_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\phi_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}

which uniquely solves (
9.1
). One can explicitly compute all the coefficients appearing in the expansions. In particular,

\big(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u_{\pm}^{(1)}\oplus\phi_{\pm}^{(1)}\big)=-\left(D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}\right)^{-1}\left(\left.\frac{d\ }{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\Phi_{1}(\varepsilon,\pm v\lambda^{-1/2},e_{\lambda},0)\right).

By

N_{(1)}u:=\left.\frac{d\ }{d\kappa}\right|_{\kappa=0}N_{\kappa}u=\frac{i}{4\pi}\,\langle 1,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,1\,,

one obtains

\displaystyle D_{\pm}\Phi_{1}(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u_{\pm}^{(1)}\oplus\phi_{\pm}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0,\big(-{v_{1}^{2}e_{\lambda}\pm i\,(4\pi)^{-1}v^{3}}{\lambda^{-3/2}}\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}1\big)\oplus\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{1}\lambda\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\right)\,.

Hence, by (
9.5
) and by

\langle e_{\lambda},1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\lambda\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,,

there follows

\displaystyle\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\mp\,\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2v\lambda^{1/2}}-i\,\frac{v^{2}}{8\pi\lambda^{2}}\,\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mp\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{1}v{\lambda^{1/2}}\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,.

This gives

\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm\left(\frac{v}{\lambda^{1/2}}+\!\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2v\lambda^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{1}v{\lambda^{1/2}}\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)\!\varepsilon\!\right)-i\,\frac{v^{2}}{8\pi\lambda^{2}}\,\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})

and so
k^{2}_{\pm}(\varepsilon)\in\mathsf{e}(A(\varepsilon))
, where

k^{2}_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda}-\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\lambda}+\rho_{1}v^{2}\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)\varepsilon\mp i\,\frac{v^{3}}{8\pi\lambda^{5/2}}\,\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

Let us notice that, in the case where
v=1
and
\rho_{1}=v_{1}=0
, the above expansion coincides with the one provided in
[
25
]
.

Remark 9.1
.

Since the eigenfunction
e_{\lambda}
is real-valued, the scalar products
\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
and
\langle 1,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
are real-valued as well.

Remark 9.2
.

Regarding our hypothesis on the simplicity of the eigenvalue
\lambda
, since
N_{0}
is the inverse of a Laplacian in
L^{2}(\Omega)
with some kind of (not local) boundary conditions (see
[
17
]
), we conjecture, by the results in
[
16
, Chapter 6]
, that such an assumption holds true for a generic domain
\Omega
.

The study of Case 2 and of the successive ones requires some preliminaries.
Setting
{K}_{z}:=\gamma_{0}D\!L_{z}
, the map
z\mapsto{K}_{z}
is
{\mathscr{B}}(H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ}))
-valued analytic and (see
[
28
, Lemma 5.3]
)

{K}_{z}={K}_{0}+{K}_{(2)}\,z^{2}+{K}_{(3)}\,z^{3}+O(|z|^{4})\,.

Defining the scalar product
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\frac{1}{2}}
in
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
by

[\phi,\varphi]_{\frac{1}{2}}:=\langle{S_{0}}^{-1}\phi,\varphi\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where

S_{0}:H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\to H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\circ})\,,\qquad S_{0}:=\gamma_{0}S\!L_{0}\,,

we introduce the orthogonal (with respect
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\frac{1}{2}}\,
) projector

P_{0}\phi:=c_{\Omega}^{-1}\,[1,\phi]_{\frac{1}{2}}\,1\,,\qquad c_{\Omega}:=[1,1]_{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

It is known that
-\frac{1}{2}
is a simple eigenvalues of
K_{0}
with the constant function
1
as the corresponding eigenfunction, so that

P_{0}\left(\frac{1}{2}+{K}_{0}\right)P_{0}=0\,.

Furthermore (see,
[
28
, Lemmata 2.3 and 2.4]
),

P_{0}{K}_{(2)}P_{0}=-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{M}}\,P_{0}\,,\qquad\omega_{M}^{2}:=\frac{c_{\Omega}}{|\Omega|}\,,

and

P_{0}{K}_{(3)}P_{0}=-i\,\frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi}\,P_{0}\,.

By the identity,

(9.6)

{K}_{0}S_{0}=S_{0}{K}_{0}^{*}\equiv S_{0}\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\,,

-\frac{1}{2}
is a simple eigenvalue of
\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}
with eigenfunction
{S_{0}}^{-1}1
and

P_{0}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)P_{0}^{*}=0\,.

Here
{\,}{}^{*}
denotes the adjoint with respect to the
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
-
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
duality; therefore,

P_{0}^{*}\phi=c_{\Omega}^{-1}\,\langle 1,\phi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\,{S_{0}}^{-1}1\,.

Furthermore,

(9.7)

P_{0}^{*}K^{*}_{(2)}P_{0}^{*}=-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{M}}\,P_{0}^{*}\,,\qquad P_{0}^{*}K^{*}_{(3)}P_{0}^{*}=i\,\frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi}\,P_{0}^{*}\,.

Defining the scalar product
[\cdot,\cdot]_{-\frac{1}{2}}
in
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
by

[\phi,\varphi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}:=\langle S_{0}\phi,\varphi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\,,

we introduce the orthogonal (with respect
[\cdot,\cdot]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\,
) projector

P\phi:=\widehat{c}_{\Omega}^{\ -1}\,[{S_{0}}^{-1}1,\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\,{S_{0}}^{-1}1\,,\qquad\widehat{c}_{\Omega}:=[{S_{0}}^{-1}1,{S_{0}}^{-1}1]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

One has

\widehat{c}_{\Omega}=\langle 1,{S_{0}}^{-1}1\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=\langle{S_{0}}^{-1}1,1\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}=c_{\Omega}

and hence

P\phi=c_{\Omega}^{\ -1}\,[{S_{0}}^{-1}1,\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\,{S_{0}}^{-1}1=c_{\Omega}^{\ -1}\,\langle 1,\phi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\,{S_{0}}^{-1}1=P_{0}^{*}\phi\,.

By (
9.6
),

\displaystyle[\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\phi,\varphi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle\langle S_{0}\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\phi,\varphi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=\langle\phi,K_{0}S_{0}\varphi\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S_{0}^{-1}S_{0}\phi,K_{0}S_{0}\varphi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=[\phi,S_{0}^{-1}K_{0}S_{0}\varphi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\phi,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\varphi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}

and thus
\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}
is self-adjoint in the Hilbert space given by
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
equipped with the scalar product
[\cdot,\cdot]_{-\frac{1}{2}}
; such a Hilbert space will be denoted by

\widehat{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
. Since
\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}
is self-adjoint and compact (hence,

\text{\rm ran}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)=\ker\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)^{\perp}
),

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}:\ker\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)^{\!\!\perp}\to\ker\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)^{\!\!\perp}

is a bounded bijection, where we introduced the shorthand notation

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}:=P_{\perp}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)P_{\perp}\,,\qquad P_{\perp}:=(1-P)\,.

Finally, we introduce the Hilbert space

\widehat{X}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\oplus\widehat{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)

with scalar product

\langle u_{1}\oplus\phi_{1},u_{2}\oplus\phi_{2}\rangle_{\widehat{X}}:=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

9.2.
Case 2.

Let us define the map

{F}_{2}:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}

\displaystyle{F}_{2}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi):=M^{(0,1,1)}_{\kappa}(\varepsilon)\begin{bmatrix}u\\
P\phi+\varepsilon^{2}P_{\perp}\phi\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}(\varepsilon)+(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\kappa^{2}N_{\varepsilon\kappa}&(v^{2}(\varepsilon)-1)\kappa^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{\varepsilon\kappa}\\
\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{\varepsilon\kappa}&\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{\varepsilon\kappa}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
P\phi+\varepsilon^{2}P_{\perp}\phi\end{bmatrix},

where
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a small open neighborhood of the origin and
\varepsilon\mapsto v^{2}(\varepsilon)
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\rho(\varepsilon)
are analytic function as in (
1.12
).

We look for a solution
\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\times(\widehat{X}\backslash\{0\})
of the equation

(9.8)

{F}_{2}(\varepsilon,\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))=0\,.

By
\gamma_{1}S\!L_{z}={K}_{-\bar{z}}^{*}
, one gets

\gamma_{1}S\!L_{z}=\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}+K^{*}_{(2)}\,{z}^{2}-K^{*}_{(3)}\,{z}^{3}+O(|z|^{4})\,.

Hence, by

\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}=1-2\rho\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

one obtains

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+\rho(\varepsilon)}\ \gamma_{1}S\!L_{\varepsilon\kappa}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}+\big(\kappa^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\big)-\kappa^{3}K_{(3)}^{*}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

This gives (notice that
\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{(1)}=0
)

\displaystyle{F}_{2}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}&\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}+\big(\kappa^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\big)P\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}v^{2}&\kappa^{2}v_{1}^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}P\\
0&-\kappa^{3}K_{(3)}^{*}P\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

Therefore,
{F}_{2}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=0
if and only if
(\kappa,u,\phi)
solves

\begin{cases}u=0\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}\phi+(\kappa^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0})P\phi=0\,.\end{cases}

This is equivalent to

\begin{cases}u=0\\
P(\kappa^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0})P\phi=0\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}\phi+P_{\perp}(\kappa^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0})P\phi=0\,,\end{cases}

i.e.,

\begin{cases}u=0\\
\left(\rho-\frac{\kappa_{0}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{M}}\right)P\phi=0\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}\phi+\kappa^{2}P_{\perp}K_{(2)}^{*}P\phi=0\,.\end{cases}

Thus,
{F}_{2}(0,\pm\kappa_{\circ},0\oplus\phi_{\circ})=0
, whenever

\kappa_{\circ}=\sqrt{\rho}\ \omega_{M}\,,\qquad\phi_{\circ}={S_{0}}^{-1}1-{\rho\,\omega^{2}_{M}}\varphi_{\circ}^{\perp}\,,

\varphi_{\circ}^{\perp}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}P_{\perp}K_{(2)}^{*}{S_{0}}^{-1}1\,.

Now we introduce the map

(9.9)

\Phi_{2}:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\,,\qquad\Phi_{2}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi):=([\phi_{\circ},\phi-\phi_{\circ}]_{-\frac{1}{2}},{F}_{2}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi))\,.

Obviously,

\Phi_{2}(0,\pm\kappa_{\circ},0\oplus\phi_{\circ})=(0,0)\,.

Let us denote by

D_{\pm}\Phi_{2}:{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}

the Frechet differential at
(0,\pm\kappa_{\circ},0\oplus\phi_{\circ})
of the map
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\mapsto\Phi_{2}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\pm}\Phi_{2}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=\bigg([\phi_{\circ},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}},u\oplus

\displaystyle\bigg(\pm 2\kappa_{\circ}\kappa K_{(2)}^{*}P\phi_{\circ}+\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}\phi+\big(\kappa_{\circ}^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\big)P\phi\bigg)\bigg)\,.

Now, for any given
(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}
, we prove
the existence of a unique solution
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}
to the equation

(9.10)

D_{\pm}\Phi_{2}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\,.

By
P\big(\kappa_{\circ}^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\big)P=0
, (
9.10
) is equivalent to the system

\begin{cases}[\phi_{\circ},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=z_{*}\\
u=u_{*}\\
P\big(\pm 2\kappa_{\circ}\kappa K_{(2)}^{*}P\phi_{\circ}+\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u\big)=P\phi_{*}\\
P_{\perp}\big(\pm 2\kappa_{\circ}\kappa K_{(2)}^{*}P\phi_{\circ}+\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)P_{\perp}\phi+\kappa_{\circ}^{2}K_{(2)}^{*}P\phi\big)=P_{\perp}\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

The latter re-write as

\begin{cases}[\phi_{\circ},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=z_{*}\\
u=u_{*}\\
\mp 2\omega_{M}^{-2}\kappa_{\circ}\kappa\,P\phi_{\circ}=P\big(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*}\big)\\
P\phi=c_{\phi}\,{S_{0}}^{-1}1\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}\phi=P_{\perp}\big(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*}-(c_{\phi}\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\pm 2\kappa_{\circ}\kappa)K_{(2)}^{*}{S_{0}}^{-1}1\big)\end{cases}

and so the unique solution of (
9.10
) is given by

(9.11)

\begin{cases}u=u_{*}\\
\kappa=\mp\,\omega_{M}^{2}(2\kappa_{\circ})^{-1}\,[\phi_{\circ},P\phi_{\circ}]_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{-1}\,[\phi_{\circ},P\big(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*}\big)]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\,\,\,\,\,=\mp\,({2\sqrt{\rho}\,\omega_{M}|\Omega|}\,)^{-1}\,\langle 1,\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\phi=c_{\phi}\,({S_{0}}^{-1}1-\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\varphi_{\circ}^{\perp})+\varphi_{\perp}=c_{\phi}\phi_{\circ}+\varphi_{\perp}\,,\quad c_{\phi}=c_{\Omega}^{-1}\big(z_{*}-[\phi_{\circ},\varphi_{\perp}]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)\\
\varphi_{\perp}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}P_{\perp}\big(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*}\mp 2\kappa_{\circ}\kappa K_{(2)}^{*}{S_{0}}^{-1}1\big)\,.\end{cases}

Therefore, by the implicit function argument recalled at the beginning of the Section, in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a
{\mathbb{C}}\times(\widehat{X}\backslash\{0\})
-valued, analytic function
\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon),u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon))
,

\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm\sqrt{\rho}\,\omega_{M}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\kappa_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}u_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\phi_{\circ}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\phi_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}

which uniquely solves (
9.8
). One can explicitly compute all the coefficients appearing in the expansions. In particular,

\big(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u_{\pm}^{(1)}\oplus\phi_{\pm}^{(1)}\big)=-\left(D_{\pm}\Phi_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\left.\frac{d\ }{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\Phi_{2}(\varepsilon,\pm\sqrt{\rho}\,\omega_{M},0\oplus\phi_{\circ})\right).

equivalently,

D_{\pm}\Phi_{2}\big(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u_{\pm}^{(1)}\oplus\phi_{\pm}^{(1)}\big)=\left(0,-\kappa_{\circ}^{2}\left(\left(v_{1}^{2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}P\phi_{\circ}\right)\oplus\left(\mp\kappa_{\circ}K_{(3)}^{*}P\phi_{\circ}\right)\right)\right).

Hence, by (
9.11
) and (
9.7
),

\displaystyle\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=

D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}:{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}

the Frechet differential at
(0,\pm v\nu^{1/2},u_{\nu}\oplus\phi_{\nu})
of the map
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\mapsto\Phi_{4}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=\bigg(

\displaystyle\langle u_{\nu},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[\phi_{\nu},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}},\big(\mp 2v{\nu}^{1/2}\kappa(N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\big)

\displaystyle+v^{2}(1-{\nu}N_{0})u-v^{2}{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi\big)\oplus\left(\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)\phi\right)\bigg)

Now, we study the invertibility of
D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}
. Since the case
\nu=0
is somehow more involved, we begin considering the case
\nu\not=0
.

Lemma 9.6
.

Suppose that the Neumann eigenvalue
\nu\not=0
is simple. Then, for any given
(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}
, the equation

(9.27)

D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})

has a unique solution
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\in{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}
. In particular,

(9.28)

\kappa=\mp\,\frac{1}{2}\,\nu^{1/2}\|u_{\nu}\|^{-2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(v^{-1}\langle u_{\nu},u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+v\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\phi_{*}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\,

where
u_{\nu}
denotes the eigenfunction corresponding to
\nu
.

Proof.

The equation (
9.27
) is equivalent
to the system of equations

\begin{cases}\langle u_{\nu},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[\phi_{\nu},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=z_{*}\\
\mp 2v{\nu}^{1/2}\kappa(N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu})+v^{2}(1-{\nu}N_{0})u-v^{2}{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi=u_{*}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0})\phi=\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

By (
9.25
), the latter rewrites as

(9.29)

\begin{cases}\langle u_{\nu},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[\phi_{\nu},\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=z_{*}\\
\mp 2v{\nu}^{-1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+v^{2}(1-{\nu}N_{0})u-v^{2}{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi=u_{*}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0})\phi=\phi_{*}\,.\end{cases}

Let us now suppose that (
9.29
) has at least one solution
(\kappa,u,\phi)
. By the relation in the second line,
u_{*}-v^{2}u\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\Omega)
and so its Neumann trace is well-defined as a distribution in
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
. Hence, by taking the Neumann trace in the second line and by adding the result to the third one, one obtains

\frac{1}{2}\,\phi+\frac{1}{2}\,[\gamma_{1}]S\!L_{0}\phi=\nu^{-1}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}(v^{-2}u_{*}-u)+\phi_{*}\,.

Thus, by
[\gamma_{1}]S\!L_{0}\phi=-\phi
,

\nu^{-1}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}(u-v^{-2}u_{*})=\phi_{*}\,.

Then, by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}\big(\mp 2v^{-1}{\nu}^{-3/2}\kappa u_{\nu}-N_{0}u-1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi\big)=

\displaystyle\mp 2v^{-1}{\nu}^{-3/2}\kappa\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}u_{\nu}+u

\displaystyle=\pm 2v^{-1}{\nu}^{-1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+u=

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}(\nu^{-1}(u-v^{-2}u_{*}))\,,

the function

w_{*}:=\nu^{-1}(u-v^{-2}u_{*})

solves the boundary value problem

(9.30)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}-\nu)w_{*}=f_{*}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}w_{*}=\phi_{*}\,,\end{cases}

where

f_{*}=v^{-2}u_{*}\pm 2v^{-1}{\nu}^{-1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}\,.

By
[
21
, Theorem 4.10]
, (
9.30
) is solvable if and only if

\langle u_{\nu},f_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\phi_{*}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=0\,.

The latter entails (
9.28
). Furthermore, the set of solutions of (
9.30
) is

\{w_{*}=c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*}\,,\quad c_{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}\}\,

where
\widetilde{w}_{*}
is any fixed particular solution. Hence,
u
is of the kind

(9.31)

u=\nu(c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*})+v^{-2}u_{*}\,,\quad c_{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}\,.

If
u
is as in (
9.31
), then

\displaystyle P\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u=

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{-1}\langle 1,\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}S_{0}^{-1}1=-c_{\Omega}^{-1}\langle 1,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}S_{0}^{-1}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{\Omega}^{-1}\big(\langle 1,\nu\widetilde{w}_{*}+v^{-2}u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)S_{0}^{-1}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{\Omega}^{-1}\big(-\langle 1,\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}\widetilde{w}_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle 1,v^{-2}u_{*}-f_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)S_{0}^{-1}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{-1}\langle 1,\Delta^{{\rm max}}_{\Omega}\widetilde{w}_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=c_{\Omega}^{-1}\langle 1,\phi_{*}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\phi_{*}\,.

Notice that the latter also gives
P\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u_{\nu}=0
, i.e.,

\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u_{\nu}=P_{\perp}\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u_{\nu}
.
Therefore, by the third line in (
9.29
),

P_{\perp}\phi=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}P_{\perp}(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u)=\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}-\nu c_{*}\varphi_{\nu}^{\perp}\,,

where

\varphi_{\nu}^{\perp}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{)}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{\!-1}\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u_{\nu}

and

\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}P_{\perp}(\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}(\nu\widetilde{w}_{*}+v^{-2}u_{*}))\,.

By (
9.21
),
\varphi_{\nu}^{\perp}=-\phi_{\nu}^{\perp}
and so

(9.32)

P_{\perp}\phi=\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}+\nu c_{*}\phi_{\nu}^{\perp}\,.

Writing

(9.33)

P\phi=c_{\phi}S_{0}^{-1}1

and considering the scalar product with
1\in L^{2}(\Omega)
in the second line in (
9.29
), one gets, using (
9.31
),

\displaystyle\langle 1,u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=

\displaystyle v^{2}\langle 1,(1-\nu N_{0})(\nu c_{*}u_{\nu}+\nu\widetilde{w}_{*}+v^{-2}u_{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-v^{2}\nu c_{\phi}|\Omega|

\displaystyle-v^{2}\nu\langle 1,1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}(\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}+\nu c_{*}\phi_{\nu}^{\perp})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

Combining this relation with the one in the first line of (
9.29
), the couple
(c_{*},c_{\phi})
solves the system of equations

(9.34)

\begin{cases}\nu\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}c_{*}+[\phi_{\nu},S_{0}^{-1}1]_{-\frac{1}{2}}c_{\phi}=z_{*}-\langle u_{\nu},\nu\widetilde{w}_{*}+v^{-2}u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-[\phi_{\nu},P_{\perp}\phi]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\nu\langle 1,N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}^{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,c_{*}+|\Omega|\,c_{\phi}=\langle 1,(1-\nu N_{0})\widetilde{w}_{*}-v^{-2}N_{0}u_{*}-1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.\end{cases}

By (
9.25
), writing
\phi_{\nu}=c_{\nu}S_{0}^{-1}1+\phi^{\perp}_{\nu}
,

N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi^{\perp}_{\nu}=\nu^{-1}u_{\nu}-c_{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}S_{0}^{-1}1=\nu^{-1}u_{\nu}-c_{\nu}1\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\det\begin{bmatrix}\nu\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&[\phi_{\nu},S_{0}^{-1}1]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\nu\langle 1,N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}^{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&|\Omega|\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\left(|\Omega|\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-[\phi_{\nu},S_{0}^{-1}1]_{-\frac{1}{2}}\langle 1,\nu^{-1}u_{\nu}-c_{\nu}1\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\left(|\Omega|\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+c_{\Omega}|c_{\nu}|^{2}\right)>0

and the system (
9.34
) is uniquely solvable. In conclusion, if the system (
9.29
) is solvable, then there is an unique solution
(\kappa,u,\phi)
given by (
9.28
), (
9.31
) and by (
9.32
), (
9.33
), with
(c_{*},c_{\phi})
solving (
9.34
).

Conversely, given
(z_{*},u_{*},\phi_{*})
let us define
(\kappa,u,\phi)
by (
9.28
), (
9.31
) and by (
9.32
), (
9.33
), with
(c_{*},c_{\phi})
solving (
9.34
). Then,

D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},(\mp 2v{\nu}^{-1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+v^{2}(1-{\nu}N_{0})u-v^{2}{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi)\oplus\phi_{*})\,.

Since

u=\nu(c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*})+v^{-2}u_{*}\,,

where
\widetilde{w}_{*}
solves (
9.30
), one gets

D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},(u_{*}+\widetilde{u}_{*})\oplus\phi_{*})\,,

where

\widetilde{u}_{*}:=\mp 2v{\nu}^{-1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+v^{2}\nu(1-\nu N_{0})(c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*})-{\nu}N_{0}u_{*}-v^{2}{\nu}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi\,.

Thus, to conclude the proof we need to show that
\widetilde{u}_{*}=0
. At first, let us notice that
\widetilde{u}_{*}
is harmonic. Indeed,

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}\widetilde{u}_{*}=

\displaystyle\mp 2v{\nu}^{1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+v^{2}\nu^{2}c_{*}u_{\nu}-v^{2}\nu\Delta_{\Omega}^{{\rm max}}\widetilde{w}_{*}-v^{2}\nu^{2}(c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*})-\nu u_{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mp 2v{\nu}^{1/2}\kappa u_{\nu}+v^{2}\nu f_{*}-\nu u_{*}=0\,.

Furthermore, by
\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}\widetilde{w}_{*}=\phi_{*}
and by
[\gamma_{1}]S\!L_{0}\phi=-\phi
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}\widetilde{u}_{*}=

\displaystyle v^{2}\nu\big(\phi_{*}-\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u-\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\nu\left(\phi_{*}-\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u-\frac{1}{2}\,\phi-\frac{1}{2}\,[\gamma_{1}]S\!L_{0}\phi-\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\nu\left(\phi_{*}-\left(\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u+\frac{1}{2}\,\phi+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\phi\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

Therefore,
\widetilde{u}_{*}
is a constant; let us set
\widetilde{u}_{*}=\widetilde{c}_{*}
and write
P\phi=c_{\phi}S_{0}^{-1}1
. Then, by the second line in (
9.34
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{*}|\Omega|=

\displaystyle\langle 1,\widetilde{u}_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\nu\big(\langle 1,\widetilde{w}_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle 1,N_{0}(\nu(c_{*}u_{\nu}+\widetilde{w}_{*})+v^{-2}u_{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle 1,1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\nu\big(\langle 1,(1-\nu N_{0})\widetilde{w}_{*}-v^{-2}N_{0}u_{*}-1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\psi_{\nu}^{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\nu c_{*}\langle 1,N_{0}u_{\nu}+1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}^{\perp}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

Thus
\widetilde{u}_{*}=0
and the proof is done.
∎

Remark 9.7
.

Let us notice that our hypothesis on the simplicity of the Neumann eigenvalue
\nu
, holds true for a generic domain
\Omega
, see
[
16
, Example 6.4]
.

By the previous Lemma and by the implicit function argument recalled at the beginning of the Section, in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a
{\mathbb{C}}\times(\widehat{X}\backslash\{0\})
-valued, analytic function
\varepsilon\mapsto(\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon),u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon))
,

\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm v\sqrt{\nu}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\kappa_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad u_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=u_{\nu}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}u_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}\,,\qquad\phi_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\phi_{\nu}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\phi_{\pm}^{(n)}\varepsilon^{n}

which uniquely solves
{F}_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa(\varepsilon),u(\varepsilon)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon))=0
. One can explicitly compute all the coefficients appearing in the expansions. In particular,

\big(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u_{\pm}^{(1)}\oplus\phi_{\pm}^{(1)}\big)=-\left(D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}\right)^{-1}\left(\left.\frac{d\ }{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\pm v\nu^{1/2},u_{\nu}\oplus\phi_{\nu})\right)\,,

equivalently,

D_{\pm}\Phi_{4}(\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)},u^{(1)}\oplus\phi^{(1)})=\big(0,\widehat{u}_{\nu}\oplus\widehat{\phi}_{\nu}\big)\,,

where

\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{u}_{\nu}\\
\widehat{\phi}_{\nu}\,\end{bmatrix}:=-\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}^{2}\mp v^{3}\nu^{3/2}N_{(1)}&\mp v^{3}\nu^{3/2}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{(1)}\,\\
-2\rho\,\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}&-2\rho\,\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\nu}\\
\phi_{\nu}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, by (
9.28
),

\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\,\frac{1}{2}\,{\nu}^{1/2}\|u_{\nu}\|^{-2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(v^{-1}\langle u_{\nu},\widehat{u}_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+v\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\widehat{\phi}_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

By

N_{(1)}u=\frac{i}{4\pi}\,\langle 1,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}1\,,\qquad S\!L_{(1)}\phi=\frac{i}{4\pi}\,\langle 1,\phi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}1\,,

there follows

\displaystyle v^{-1}\langle u_{\nu},\widehat{u}_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+v\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\widehat{\phi}_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v_{1}^{2}v^{-1}\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\rho v\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}-2\rho v\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Since both
(\nu^{-1}-N_{0})u_{\nu}=0
and
1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}
are harmonic and

\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}=\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{0}S\!L_{0}S_{0}^{-1}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{0}(\nu^{-1}-N_{0})u_{\nu}=\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{0}(\nu^{-1}-N_{0})u_{\nu}\,,

one gets

1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}=(\nu^{-1}-N_{0})u_{\nu}\,.

Furthermore,

\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}+\gamma_{1}^{{\rm ex}})S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}=\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\,[\gamma_{1}]S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}=\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\,\phi_{\nu}\,.

Green’s formula gives

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle\langle u_{\nu},\Delta_{\Omega}N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\Delta_{\Omega}u_{\nu},N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\nu\langle u_{\nu},N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\gamma_{1}^{{\rm in}}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}=\langle u_{\nu},\Delta_{\Omega}1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\Delta_{\Omega}u_{\nu},1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\langle u_{\nu},1_{\Omega}S\!L_{0}\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle u_{\nu},(1-\nu N_{0})u_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\nu\langle u_{\nu},N_{0}u_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

Thus

\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\,\frac{1}{2}\,{\nu}^{1/2}\|u_{\nu}\|^{-2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\rho v\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}-v_{1}^{2}v^{-1}\|u_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

By (
9.19
),
\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}
does not depend on
\|u_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
; hence, by taking
\|u_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
,

\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\,\frac{1}{2}\,{\nu}^{1/2}\left(\rho v\,\langle\gamma_{0}^{{\rm in}}u_{\nu},\phi_{\nu}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}-v_{1}^{2}v^{-1}\right).

and, writing
\kappa_{\pm}^{(1)}=\pm\,\kappa_{\nu}
,
\kappa_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\kappa_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\pm(v\nu^{1/2}+\kappa_{\nu}\varepsilon)+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,;

then

\kappa^{2}_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=v^{2}\nu+2v\nu^{1/2}\kappa_{\nu}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})

belongs to
\mathsf{e}(A(\varepsilon))
.

We conclude by considering the case
\nu=0
, where the implicit function argument does not apply directly and a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Sections 3 and 4, Chapter 5]
) has to be used.

Let us denote by

D_{0}\Phi_{4}:{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}

the Frechet differential at
(0,0,0\oplus c_{0}S_{0}^{-1}1)
(see Remark (
9.3
)) of the map
(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)\mapsto\Phi_{4}(0,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)
, i.e., writing
P\phi=c_{\phi}S_{0}^{-1}1
,

\displaystyle D_{0}\Phi_{4}(\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=\left(c_{\Omega}\overline{c}_{0}c_{\phi},v^{2}u\oplus\left(\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)P_{\perp}\phi\right)\right).

One has

\ker(D_{0}\Phi_{4})=\{(\kappa,u\oplus\phi):u=0\,,\ \phi=0\}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}\,.

Writing

\phi_{*}=c_{\phi_{*}}S_{0}^{-1}1+\phi_{*}^{\perp}\,,\qquad u=c_{u_{*}}1+u_{*}^{\perp}\,,

the system

\begin{cases}c_{\Omega}\overline{c}_{0}c_{\phi}=z_{*}\\
v^{2}u=u_{*}\\
\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)P_{\perp}\phi=\phi_{*}\end{cases}

is solvable if and only if

P(v^{2}\phi_{*}-\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*})=(v^{2}c_{\phi_{*}}+c_{\Omega}^{-1}|\Omega|c_{u_{*}})S_{0}^{-1}1=0

and in that case the solution is given by

(9.35)

\begin{cases}c_{\phi}=c_{\Omega}^{-1}{\overline{c}}_{0}^{\,-1}z_{*}\\
u=v^{-2}u_{*}\\
P_{\perp}\phi=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}(\phi_{*}-v^{-2}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*})\,.\end{cases}

This entails

\text{\rm ran}(D_{0}\Phi_{4})={\mathbb{C}}\oplus\widehat{X}_{0}\,,

where

\widehat{X}_{0}:=\big\{u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*}\in\widehat{X}:\langle 1,u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+v^{2}[S_{0}^{-1}1,\phi_{*}]_{-\frac{1}{2}}=0\big\}=\{1\oplus v^{2}S_{0}^{-1}1\}^{\perp}\,.

Therefore, denoting by
\widehat{P}
and by
\widehat{P}_{\perp}
the orthogonal projectors onto the closed subspaces
{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\widehat{X}_{0}
and
({\mathbb{C}}\oplus\widehat{X}_{0})^{\perp}=\{0\}\oplus\text{span}(1\oplus v^{2}S_{0}^{-1}1)
respectively, one has
\widehat{P}=1-\widehat{P}_{\perp}
with

\widehat{P}_{\perp}(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*}):=\left(0,(|\Omega|+v^{4}c_{\Omega})^{-1}\left(\langle 1,u_{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+v^{2}\langle 1,\phi_{*}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\right)(1\oplus v^{2}S_{0}^{-1}1)\right)\,.

Obviously,

(9.36)

\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\widehat{P}\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=0\\
\widehat{P}_{\perp}\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u\oplus\phi)=0\,.\end{cases}

Defining

\widehat{\Phi}_{4}:U\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}_{0}\,,\qquad\widehat{\Phi}_{4}:=\widehat{P}\Phi_{4}\,,

and denoting by
\widehat{D}_{0}\widehat{\Phi}_{4}
the Frechet differential at
(0,0,0\oplus c_{0}S_{0}^{-1}1)
of the map
u\oplus\phi\mapsto\Psi(0,0,u\oplus\phi)
, one gets

\widehat{D}_{0}\widehat{\Phi}_{4}=\widehat{P}D_{0}\Phi_{4}\,.

By (
9.35
),
\widehat{D}_{0}\widehat{\Phi}_{4}:\widehat{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}\times\widehat{X}_{0}
is a bounded bijection, and

\widehat{D}_{0}\widehat{\Phi}_{4}(u\oplus\phi)=(z_{*},u_{*}\oplus\phi_{*})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}u=v^{-2}u_{*}\\
\phi=(c_{\Omega}\overline{c}_{0})^{-1}z_{*}S_{0}^{-1}1+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\gamma_{1}S\!L_{0}\right)_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}(\phi_{*}-v^{-2}\gamma^{{\rm in}}_{1}N_{0}u_{*})\,.\end{cases}

Since

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{4}(0,0,0\oplus c_{0}S_{0}^{-1}1)=\widehat{P}\Phi_{4}(0,0,0\oplus c_{0}S_{0}^{-1}1)=0\,,

the implicit function theorem applies to
\widehat{\Phi}_{4}
and therefore, in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists a
\widehat{X}_{0}
-valued analytic map
(\varepsilon,\kappa)\mapsto u(\varepsilon,\kappa)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon,\kappa)
such that

\widehat{P}\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u(\varepsilon,\kappa)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon,\kappa))=0\,,

equivalently,

(9.37)

\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u(\varepsilon,\kappa)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon,\kappa))=\widehat{P}_{\perp}\Phi_{4}(\varepsilon,\kappa,u(\varepsilon,\kappa)\oplus\phi(\varepsilon,\kappa))\,.

Defining
