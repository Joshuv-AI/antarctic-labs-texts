Relationship Between Electrical Parameters and Cardiac Synchrony in Patients Underwent Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing
Authors: Manxin Lin, Shufen Huang, Xinyi Huang, Jincun Guo, Linlin Li, Simei Chen, Guiyang Li, Qiang Li, Dong Chang, Maolong Su, Binni Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe study aims to assess cardiac synchrony under different left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) and evaluate the relationship between different surface or intracardiac electrical parameters and mechanical synchrony.MethodsEighty‐two patients with successful LBBAP were recruited. The electrical synchrony, evaluated by paced QRS duration (pQRSD) and Stim‐LVAT (stimulus to left ventricular activation time), and mechanical synchrony, evaluated by the standard deviation of the time‐to‐peak contraction velocity in 12 left ventricular segments (Tsd‐12‐LV), were compared between groups in patients underwent LBBAP. To investigate the relationship between different electrical parameters with mechanical synchrony under LBBAP, patients were divided into subgroups according to left ventricular activation time (LVAT, < 60, 60–70, and > 70 ms), presence of left bundle branch (LBB) potential (positive, negative), QRS axis (normal, left axis deviation [LAD]), and potential to ventricular interval (PVI, < 20 and > 20 ms). Mechanical synchrony was compared among the subgroups respectively 3 days post LBBAP procedure.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were documented in electrical synchrony, evaluated by pQRSD, and mechanical synchrony, evaluated by Tsd‐12‐LV among the subgroups divided by the stim‐LVAT, LBB potential, PVI duration, or paced QRS axis in the LBBAP group.ConclusionsLBB potential, PVI, or normal paced QRS axis is not the prerequisite for successful LBBAP and optimal cardiac synchrony. Adopting a Stim‐LVAT value of less than 75 ms to attain ideal electrical and mechanical synchrony during the LBBAP procedure may be applicable.Trial registration http://www.chictr.org.cn/index.aspx. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: ChiCTR1800021104

Introduction

The number of patients with bradycardia requiring pacing has increased over the past several decades. Right ventricular apex (RVA) pacing has been recommended for a long time due to its safety, effectiveness, and simplicity [1, 2]. Nevertheless, the adverse effects of RVA pacing have long been realized including the electrical and structural asynchrony in the left ventricle, which can lead to a higher risk of heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), and mortality [3, 4, 5]. As a promising physiological pacing technique, left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has demonstrated reliability and parameter stability during short‐term or medium‐term follow‐up in previous studies [6, 7, 8], and a limited number of studies have confirmed the achievement of cardiac synchrony under LBBAP [9, 10]. Recent studies have demonstrated the relationship between the electrical parameters and mechanical synchrony, as well as the related cardiac function after pacemaker implantation. For example, electrical synchrony, evaluated by paced QRS duration (pQRSD), could exhibit positive effects on mechanical synchrony in patients after His bundle pacing (HBP) [11]. The paced QRS axis has been suggested as a predictor of left ventricular (LV) dysfunction in patients who underwent right ventricular pacing (RVP) [12]. Reduction in the deviation of ventricular activation time has been shown to correlate with cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) response under biventricular pacing, while this metric has not yet been proven appropriate in patients who underwent LBBAP or HBP [13]. Potential to ventricular interval (PVI) was deemed as a surrogate in predicting the location of the lead in the distal or proximal segment of the left His‐Purkinje system. However, no study has ever investigated the relationship between PVI and cardiac synchrony under LBBAP to our knowledge. In this article, we assessed the cardiac synchrony under LBBAP and evaluated the relationship between different surface or intracardiac electrical parameters and cardiac mechanical synchrony.

Methods

Patient Selection

This was a prospective observational study. Consecutive patients with symptomatic bradycardia and indications for pacing who underwent successful pacemaker implantation in Xiamen Cardiovascular Hospital, Xiamen University from January 2018 to May 2019, were recruited. Patients were excluded if (i) diagnosed with dilated cardiac disease, myocardial infarction, or structural heart disease, (ii) previously implanted with any cardiac device, (iii) prosthetic valve replacement has been conducted, and (iv) poor acoustic window limited the assessment of cardiac structure or function. The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Cardiovascular Hospital, Xiamen University was obtained before enrollment, and all patients signed the informed consent form.

Lead Implantation

LBBAP was achieved by a trans‐ventricular septal method in the basal ventricular septum as described in detail elsewhere [14]. Briefly, the Select Secure (model 3830, 69 cm, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) pacing lead was delivered through a fixed curve sheath (C315 HIS, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) and screwed into ventricular septum gradually until the paced QRS morphology showed a “QR/Qr” pattern in V1 lead. A successful LBBAP was defined as paced QRS of a right bundle branch block (RBBB) pattern and either of the following: (a) confirmation of selective LBBP; (b) the stimulus to left ventricular activation time (Stim‐LVAT) shortening abruptly with increasing output (≥ 10 ms); and (c) Stim‐LVAT remaining constant and less than 75 ms. In patients with sinus rhythm, the right atrial lead would be implanted in the right atrial appendage, connecting to the atrial port, with the LBBAP lead connecting to the ventricular port. A dual‐chamber pacemaker (Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) was implanted subsequently. For patients with persistent/permanent AF, only the LBBAP lead would be implanted and connected to a single‐chamber pacemaker. The location of the lead is determined by the nine‐partition method reported by Jiang et al. [15].

Electrocardiographic Measurements

QRS duration (QRSD) and stim‐LVAT were measured to evaluate the electrical synchrony under LBBAP. Twelve‐lead surface electrocardiography (ECG) was recorded using the GE CardioLab Electrophysiology recording system (GE Healthcare Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA) at 100 mm/s. The intracardiac electrogram (IEGM) from the tip electrode of leads was recorded in a unipolar fashion. Left bundle branch (LBB) potential was defined as a discrete potential before the QRS complex was recorded during intrinsic rhythm. The intrinsic QRSD, pQRSD, PVI, and stim‐LVAT were sequentially measured. The PVI was measured from the LBB potential to the onset of the QRS complex. The intrinsic QRSD was measured under intrinsic rhythm from the earliest onset of the QRS complex in any leads to the latest offset in any leads, while pQRSD was measured from the stimulus to the latest offset in any leads. Stim‐LVAT was measured from the stimulus to the peak of R‐wave in lead V5 or V6. QRS axis was measured and rounded down to the nearest 10°. The axis between −30° and +90° was defined as the “normal electrical axis” (NEA) while the axis between −90° and −30° was defined as “left axis deviation” (LAD). The electrical measurements were evaluated under working voltage so that all the measurements would be recorded under non‐selective LBBAP.

Mechanical Synchrony Evaluation

Echocardiographic data for evaluation of mechanical synchrony were collected by tissue Doppler image (TDI) using color Doppler ultrasonic diagnostic apparatus (EPIQ 7C, Philips Medical Systems) 3 days after the procedure and analyzed on an independent workstation (QLAB version 10.8, Philips Medical Systems) subsequently. In brief, the time‐to‐peak contraction velocity (Ts) of each 12 LV segments (except for the apex segments) was measured, and the standard deviation of the time‐to‐peak contraction velocity in 12 left ventricular segments (Tsd‐12‐LV) was calculated to evaluate the LV systolic synchrony. The average data obtained from three cardiac cycles were used for statistical analysis. Tsd‐12‐LV > 33 ms was defined as intra‐ventricular dyssynchrony.

Patient Assignment

Patients who underwent successful LBBAP were enrolled and assigned to different subgroups according to different electrical parameters to investigate the relationship between different electrical parameters and mechanical synchrony. Said electrical parameters include stim‐LVAT (< 60, 60–70, and > 70 ms), presence of LBB potential (positive, negative), QRS axis (normal, left deviation), and PVI (< 10, 10–20, and >20 ms). Electrical and mechanical synchrony were compared among the subgroups, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Differences between two groups were compared using the t‐test and differences among three or more groups were compared using the single‐factor analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA). The Chi‐square test was used to compare the differences for categorical data. The correlation between the two variables was analyzed using the Pearson correlation. A value of p < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 3.6.3.

Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony in Patients Underwent LBBAP

Of the 82 patients with successful LBBAP implantation, pQRSD (123.6 ± 10.6 ms) and stim‐LVAT (63.4 ± 6.8 ms) were documented during the procedure, with pQRSD slightly longer than baseline QRSD (123.6 ± 10.6 ms vs. 102.9 ± 26.7 ms, p < 0.001). LBB potentials were recorded in 50 patients (61.0%). Fifty‐seven patients (69.5%) exhibited NEA, while 24 patients (29.3%) manifested LAD, and 1 patient (1.2%) manifested right axis deviation. Stim‐LVAT With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony in Patients Underwent LBBAP

Of all 82 patients with successful LBBAP, Stim‐LVAT shortening abruptly with increasing output was documented in 29 patients (35.4%). The other 53 patients (64.6%) had Stim‐LVAT remaining constant and less than 75 ms. Confirmation of selective LBBP was found in 11 patients. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean Stim‐LVAT between patients with abrupt shortening of Stim‐LVAT and those without (62.8 ± 8.0 ms vs. 63.7 ± 5.7 ms, p = 0.54). The mean stim‐LVAT was 63.3 ± 6.6 ms in patients who underwent LBBAP. The patients were divided into three groups by the values of stim‐LVAT: long stim‐LVAT (70–75 ms) was recorded in 12 patients, medium stim‐LVAT (60–70 ms) was recorded in 48 patients and short stim‐LVAT (< 60 ms) was recorded in the other 22 patients. No statistically significant difference was documented in the mechanical synchrony, evaluated by Tsd‐12‐LV (24.5 ± 16.9 ms vs. 24.9 ± 11.3 ms vs. 29.0 ± 11.5 ms, p = 0.40) among all three subgroups. However, statistically significant differences were found in pQRSD (120.2 ± 7.2 ms vs. 123.3 ± 11.2 ms vs. 130.8 ± 11.3, p = 0.02), with post hoc analysis (Scheffe) showing statistically significant difference between subgroups short stim‐LVAT and long stim‐LVAT (p = 0.021). No statistically significant differences were found between subgroups short stim‐LVAT and medium stim‐LVAT (p = 0.521) or between subgroups medium stim‐LVAT and long stim‐LVAT (p = 0.087).

LBB Potential With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony in Patients Underwent LBBAP

LBB potentials were recorded in 50 patients (61.0%), which were more statistically frequent in patients with sick sinus syndrome (SSS) than in those with atrioventricular block (AVB) (71.1% vs. 52.3%, p = 0.20). In the 21 AVB patients without LBB potential, 12 patients presented with ventricular escape rhythm with complete left bundle branch block (LBBB) morphology or extremely slow escape rate, in whom a temporary pacemaker had been implanted. In the subgroup analysis classified according to the presence of LBB potential, no statistically significant differences were found in pQRSD (122.6 ± 10.0 ms vs. 125.2 ± 11.8 ms, p = 0.31), stim‐LVAT (62.5 ± 6.3 ms vs. 65.2 ± 7.3 ms, p = 0.09), and Tsd‐12‐LV (25.8 ± 12.0 ms vs. 26.7 ± 12.6 ms, p = 0.77) between LBB potential (+) group and LBB potential (−) group (Figure 2).

PVI With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony in Patients Underwent LBBAP

When assigning patients to different subgroups according to PVIs, long PV intervals (LPVI, 20–30 ms) were recorded in 26 patients and short PV intervals (SPVI, < 20 ms) were recorded in 24 patients. No statistically significant differences were found in pQRSD (122.7 ± 11.2 ms vs. 122.6 ± 8.7 ms, p = 0.98), stim‐LVAT (62.1 ± 6.0 ms vs. 62.9 ± 6.8 ms, p = 0.72), and Tsd‐12‐LV (27.4 ± 13.9 ms vs. 24.2 ± 9.7 ms, p = 0.34) between the two subgroups (Figure 2).

Paced QRS Axis With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony in Patients Underwent LBBAP

Fifty‐seven patients (69.5%) presented NEA while 24 patients (29.3%) manifested LAD and 1 patient (1.2%) manifested right axis deviation in this cohort under LBBAP. There were no differences in pQRSD (123.8 ± 9.0 ms vs. 121.8 ± 12.1 ms, p = 0.48) and stim‐LVAT (62.5 ± 7.0 ms vs. 65.0 ± 5.5 ms, p = 0.09) between the NEA and LAD subgroups. Meanwhile, no differences were documented in Tsd‐12‐LV (25.3 ± 11.5 ms vs. 28.1 ± 13.7 ms, p = 0.39) between the two subgroups (Figure 2).

Patients who underwent LBBAP were divided into six subgroups according to whether PVI was > 20 ms, LBB potential was recorded, or the paced QRS axis was deviated. Discussion

The present study explored the relationship between mechanical synchrony and different electrical parameters among different subgroups of patients who underwent LBBAP. The main findings of this study are the following: (1) With a criterion of the duration of stim‐LVAT less than 75 ms in V5 or V6 during the LBBAP procedure, the mechanical synchrony will hardly be affected by the left ventricular activation time (LVAT). (2) In patients who underwent successful LBBAP, cardiac synchrony can be maintained irrespective of the presence of LBB potential, duration of PVI, or paced QRS axis. The findings from this study confirmed that electrical and mechanical synchrony can be maintained under the LBBAP procedure based on the current standardized method and stim‐LVAT is the most important indicator for subsequent cardiac synchrony which shall be focused on during future procedures.

LBBAP as a Promising Method for Physiological Pacing

LBBAP, characterized with a low and stable pacing capture threshold and relatively narrow QRSD, has emerged as a promising method for physiological pacing [8, 14, 16]. Recently small sample studies have confirmed the effectiveness of LBBAP in HF patients complicated with LBBB [17]. Chen et al. reported that LBBAP can achieve relatively narrow pQRSD and stable lead parameters during short‐term follow‐up [18]. Vijayaraman et al. confirmed that LBBAP was feasible in a high percentage of patients with low threshold during acute follow‐up. The lead parameters remained stable at median follow‐up of 3 months in this cohort [19]. LBB capture is the cornerstone in maintaining electrical and mechanical synchrony. However, as far as LBBAP is concerned, the relationship of electrical parameters with electromechanical synchrony and its potential benefits remained uncertain. It has long been recognized that electrical synchrony can be reflected by pQRSD and translated to mechanical synchrony.

Stim‐LVAT With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony

Stim‐LVAT has been proven to be a suitable surrogate evaluation for LV activation [14, 19]. It may serve as a predictor for both LBBAP capture and LV electrical synchrony [20]. In Huang's opinion, if stim‐LVAT shortened abruptly with increasing output or remained shortest and constant at both low and high outputs during the procedure, LBB capture could be confirmed [20]. Therefore, the pattern of dynamic changes of stim‐LVAT at different output would be essential. But is there a certain value of stim‐LVAT that could predict successful LBB capture or ensure cardiac synchrony? The answer is still controversial. The LVAT is measured from the onset of QRS complex to the peak of R wave on leads V5 or V6, and 45 ms is considered the upper limit of the normal range, which could be deviated with cardiac hypertrophy, enlargement and fibrosis or conduction abnormalities [21]. The stimulus spreading from the bundle branch to Purkinje system might consume 20–30 ms, so the normal range of PVI is considered 20–30 ms in patients with non‐LBBB during intrinsic rhythm [20]. Therefore, stim‐LVAT should be less than 75 ms theoretically, since it is approximately the sum of PVI and LVAT. Similarly, in Qian's study, stim‐LVAT < 76 ms was deemed as a practical parameter for defining LBB capture which might lead to better LV mechanical synchrony, evaluated by single photo emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging [22]. In our cohort, the stim‐LVAT was less than 70 ms in average. Meanwhile, subgroup analysis of Stim‐LVAT is conducted in order to further evaluate if different Stim‐LVAT (< 60 ms, 60–70 ms, > 70 ms) may lead to differences in cardiac mechanical synchrony. And our results showed that no statistically significant differences were documented in the mechanical synchrony among these subgroups. This result supported the applicability of adopting a Stim‐LVAT value of less than 75 ms to attain ideal electrical and mechanical synchrony during LBBAP procedure, since the value of stim‐LVAT hardly affected LV mechanical synchrony when less than 75 ms. As for electrical synchrony, statistically significant differences were found between subgroups short stim‐LVAT and long stim‐LVAT (p = 0.021), which may have resulted from the differences in baseline QRSD (see Table S1). Nevertheless, subgroup analysis showed that the differences in electrical synchrony did not result in the differences in mechanical synchrony. In conclusion, current results supported using stim‐LVAT as an indicator for cardiac synchrony with LBBAP, and 75 ms might be a suitable reference value of stim‐LVAT for patients with a heart of normal size and structure, which still needs to be verified in future randomized control studies.

LBB Potential With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony

Existence of LBB potentials may be considered an evidence of lead position within the periphery of LBB [23] and might indicate greater opportunity of LBB capture. But in real‐world practice, the identification of LBB potential may be affected by many factors. For example, LBB potential may not be visualized in patients with complete LBBB or escape rhythm from a non‐LBB fascicle without retrograde activation of the LBB [20]. It may also be affected by systematic disturbance or electrical interference, as LBB potential may be too small or blurry to be recognized in such cases. This may account for the fact that LBB potentials could not be recorded in 39% of the patients in this study, and that the LBB potentials documented in the subgroup of patients with SSS in this cohort were less than that in our previous study (71.1% vs. 82.7%) [24]. Hou's study showed that LBB potential (+) group has shorter stim‐LVAT duration and better mechanical synchrony, compared to that in the LBB potential (−) group [9]. Inconsistent with their results, our study showed that no differences were observed in electrical and mechanical synchrony between LBB potential (+) group and LBB potential (−) group. It should be noted that the stim‐LVAT in our study was much shorter in LBB potential (−) group than those in Hou's, which was comparable to that in LBB potential (+) group. In our opinion, LBB potential is not a prerequisite for LBB capture. Stim‐LVAT, rather than the presence of LBB potential, was a better indicator for evaluating electrical synchrony under LBBAP.

PVI With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony

Up to the present, it is unclear whether pacing at the proximal or distal part of the left His‐Purkinje system may affect cardiac synchrony. PVI reflects the wavefront conduction within the His‐Purkinje system toward the exit in the ventricle and appeared at approximately 20 ms under LBBAP since the normal range of PVI is considered 20–30 ms in patients with non‐LBBB during intrinsic rhythm [20, 25]. A PVI too short may indicate a fixed position in the relatively distal part or even fascicular of the left bundle. In our study, no differences were observed in the duration of stim‐LVAT, pQRSD, or LV mechanical synchrony in subgroups divided according to different PVIs. It is noteworthy that the patients were relatively healthy and the activation propagated rapidly in the Purkinje system in this cohort. This may hint that pacing on whether the proximal or distal part of LBB might not be pivotal with a healthy left His‐Purkinje system under LBBAP in which the activation wavefront propagated relatively fast, resulting in effective electrical and mechanical synchrony. However, using PVI as a surrogate in predicting the level of LBBAP also has its limitations, for it may be lengthened by reduced conduction velocity of a diseased His‐Purkinje system, which should be noted in future studies in the selection of patients. Using PVI along with the anatomical position of the pacing lead and paced QRS morphology in leads II and III may help better determine the level of conduction system pacing [25].

Paced QRS Axis With Electrical and Mechanical Synchrony

The typical lead fixation regions of LBBAP are localized in the left bundle trunk or proximal part of left anterior and posterior fascicular area. Pacing at the distal part of LBB or left posterior fascicular branch may lead to LAD under LBBAP. LAD was documented in about 30% of patients in this cohort. It has been reported that abnormal paced QRS axis may lead to impaired cardiac function in RVP. In Kim's study, the LV ejection fraction decreased in patients with an abnormal paced QRS axis during RVP, but not in those with a normal axis [12]. It is not yet clear whether the paced QRS axis deviation affects cardiac synchrony in LBBAP. LAD subgroup had similar LV mechanical synchrony compared to that with NEA in our study. Further studies with larger sample sizes and long‐term follow‐up may be needed to verify these effects and delineate the role of axis deviation on the long‐term effect of cardiac function after LBBAP.

Limitations

This was an observational study with a relatively small sample size in a single center. The non‐randomized and non‐controlled nature of this study limited its evidentiary value. The current manuscript verified the value of stim‐LVAT in evaluating LV electrical synchrony in hearts with normal size and structure, yet this conclusion still needs to be investigated in different populations since it varies with cardiac enlargement, hypertrophy, or rotation. In addition, these findings were short‐term results, with mechanical synchrony measured 3 days post‐LBBAP procedure. Long‐term follow‐up regarding the safety and efficacy of LBBAP may help reveal a correlation with hard endpoints such as mortality and improvement of clinical and echocardiographic parameters. Multi‐center studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up periods may be beneficial for evaluating the further potential impact of electrical parameters on cardiac synchrony and function in patients who underwent LBBAP.

Conclusions

LBBAP exhibits better electrical and mechanical synchrony when stim‐LVAT is less than 75 ms during the procedure. In patients with normal‐sized heart that underwent LBBAP, cardiac mechanical synchrony will not be affected by paced QRS axis deviation, the duration of PVI, or the presence of LBB potential. The results supported the applicability of adopting a Stim‐LVAT value of less than 75 ms to attain ideal electrical and mechanical synchrony during the LBBAP procedure.
