Magnitude of human papillomavirus vaccination uptake and its determinants among adolescent girls in Lesotho: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tsion Mulat Tebeje, Kassahun Animut Metkie, Yordanos Sisay Asgedom, Amanuel Yosef Gebrekidan, Seblewongel Gebretsadik Sertsewold, Ermias Bekele Enyew, Mesfin Abebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem, the WHO recommends that human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines should be included in national immunisation programmes and should reach 90% of all girls by age 15 as primary prevention. Lesotho reintroduced the national HPV vaccine programme in 2022.ObjectiveTo investigate the prevalence and associated factors of HPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls in Lesotho. Design A population-based cross-sectional study design. Setting Lesotho. Participants Girls aged 15–17.Primary and secondary outcome measuresA multilevel Poisson regression model with robust variance was applied to identify factors associated with HPV vaccination uptake. An adjusted prevalence ratio with a 95% CI was reported.ResultsThe uptake of HPV vaccination among adolescent girls in Lesotho was 52.15% (95% CI 48.43 to 55.84). Girls aged 16 and 17 and those residing in the Maseru district were significantly associated with a lower proportion of HPV vaccination uptake. While, ever heard of cervical cancer and ever heard of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) were found to be linked with a higher proportion of HPV vaccination uptake.ConclusionHPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls in Lesotho was far behind the WHO 2030 target. Raising awareness about cervical cancer and HPV vaccination via targeted education is vital. A comprehensive sexual health education programme that includes information on STIs and HPV can improve vaccination uptake, especially in districts with low uptake.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a sexually transmitted infection (STIs) primarily spread through sexual contact.1 While most HPV infections are asymptomatic and resolve simultaneously, around 10% of individuals develop persistent infections, substantially raising their risk of developing carcinomas.2 HPV infection has been linked to multiple cancers, with cervical cancer being the most common HPV-related cancer. Worldwide, approximately 80% of the HPV-attributable cancer burden is due to cervical cancer, with women accounting for roughly 90% of cancers related to the virus.3 HPV types 16 and 18 are most virulent and responsible for 70% of cervical cancers and precancerous lesions.4

Cervical cancer is the fourth most frequent cancer among women worldwide, and its incidence and mortality are disproportionately high in low-income and middle-income countries, including those in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).5 Cancer caused by HPV results in a high infection-related cancer burden in SSA, making the region the highest in age-specific incidence rate of HPV cancer.3

Vaccination remains the best way to prevent HPV infection as it prevents new HPV infections and HPV-associated diseases. The HPV vaccination is recommended to be administered to girls in the 9–14 age range before they engage in sexual activity.6 Depending on the recipient’s immune status, the vaccine may be given in one, two or three doses.5 Reducing HPV infection rates among girls to over 80% also helps to decrease the risk of infection for boys.6 In line with this, the WHO has set a goal for 2030 to achieve a 90–70–90 target, eliminating cervical cancer as a major public health issue. The target includes 90% of girls to be fully vaccinated against HPV by the age of 15, 70% of women to be screened with a high-performance HPV test by the age of 35 and 45, and 90% of women with cervical disease to obtain care and treatment.7

As a primary prevention, adolescent girls’ HPV vaccination is a crucial long-term intervention for reducing cervical cancer risk, with high coverage leading to herd immunity and community protection.7 8 More than 85% of high-income countries have incorporated HPV vaccination into their national programmes, compared with fewer than 25% of low-income and less than 30% of lower middle-income countries.7

Only 27 of the 47 countries in the African Region have integrated HPV vaccination into their routine immunisation programmes, with a primary focus on girls aged 9–14. As of 2022, coverage of the first dose in the African Region was only 33%, falling behind the global target of 90%.9 Most SSA countries have adopted school-based HPV vaccination strategies, with a systematic review showing a pooled uptake of 28.53% among adolescent schoolgirls, though this varies significantly by country.10

In Lesotho, cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer, comprising 45.9% of all cancer cases among females of all ages. Additionally, it is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women.11 The incidence of cervical cancer per 100 000 women in Lesotho was 56.8 compared with the global incidence of 13.3, which is one of the highest rates.12 13 To address cervical cancer, which remains a significant health issue in the country, Lesotho successfully reintroduced the national HPV vaccine programme in April 2022 for all adolescent girls aged 9–14, 7 years after the initial campaign was discontinued.14 The HPV vaccination is mainly delivered through the school health curriculum in a new package of age-appropriate health services in the country.15

Understanding the factors influencing HPV vaccination uptake can provide valuable insights for designing targeted interventions. Various factors, such as age,16 educational attainment, wealth quintile,17 family size,18 marital status, health services utilisation,19 working status,20 knowledge of cervical cancer, urban–rural residence21 and proximity to health facilities22 influence the uptake of HPV vaccine. The current study focused on the magnitude of HPV vaccination uptake and its determinants among adolescent girls in Lesotho, utilising the latest demographic and health survey (DHS) data.

Methods

Source of data and population

We used the 2023–2024 nationally representative Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey (LDHS), the fourth DHS conducted in the country following the 2004, 2009 and 2014 LDHS. These cross-sectional data provide estimates at the national level, for urban and rural areas, and for the country’s 10 districts.23 In this study, we used the Individual Women’s Record dataset (IR file). A weighted sample of 699 girls aged 15–17 was included in the analysis.

Variables of the study

Outcome variable

The dependent variable was HPV vaccination uptake. Girls aged 15–17 were asked if they had ever received a vaccination against HPV, that is, an injection in the left upper arm to protect against cervical cancer.23 It was dichotomised as a binary variable, and those who responded yes were coded 1 and otherwise 0.

Independent variables

Considering the hierarchical nature of the DHS data, we obtained the independent variables at the individual and community levels. Age, education status, literacy, household wealth index, marital status, health insurance, hearing about cervical cancer, engaging in sexual activities, working status and media exposure were individual-level variables. Region, residence and distance to a facility were community-level variables.

Data management and analysis

Before any statistical analysis, the data were weighted to restore the survey’s representativeness by accounting for the chance of unequal sample selection and potential variations in response rates. Weighting was calculated by dividing the variable women’s individual sample weight (v005) by 1 000 000. The software we used for statistical data analysis was STATA V.17. We displayed descriptive findings as frequencies and percentages after extraction, cleaning and recoding.

Due to the hierarchical nature of the DHS data, the adolescent girls are nested within a cluster, and the participants in the same cluster are likely to share similar characteristics than those in another cluster. This suggests a proper methodological approach that accounts for the clustering/hierarchical nature of the DHS data, a multilevel analysis. Additionally, considering the data were collected cross-sectional and the high prevalence of HPV vaccination uptake, which was 52%, reporting the OR could exaggerate the relationship between the dependent and independent variables. Because of the above reasons, a multilevel Poisson regression with robust variance was used, and a prevalence ratio was reported.24 25

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess a clustering effect and it revealed a significant clustering effect with an ICC of 0.108. This implies differences between clusters accounted for 10.8% of the total variability for HPV vaccination uptake. To identify suitable variables for multilevel Poisson regression models with robust variance analysis, we conducted a bi-variable multilevel Poisson regression analysis. Independent variables with a p value below 0.2 in the bi-variable analysis were selected for the multivariable analysis. We built four models for the multivariable multilevel Poisson regression analysis: the null model (with no independent variables), model 2 (with individual-level variables), model 3 (with community-level variables) and model 4 (with both individual and community-level model). The best-performing model was identified using deviance (−2 log-likelihood) and a model with the lowest deviance was considered the best-fit model. In addition, the information criteria, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were also used as model selection method to provide complementary approaches, favouring the model with the lowest information criteria.26 In this multivariable multilevel Poisson regression model with robust variance, variables with p values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. The adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) and a 95% CI were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed and quantified by the variance inflation factor (VIF). All variables demonstrated VIF values below 5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a significant concern in the analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plan of the research. This is because we have conducted a secondary data analysis based on already available DHS data.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

Among the participants, three-quarters (75.4%) have not yet completed secondary education. The majority of respondents (69.67%) reported that they had not initiated sexual activity. Additionally, more than one-third were followers of Roman Catholic religion, 17.75% came from the poorest households and only 2.43% had health insurance coverage. Less than half of the girls (44.33%) have ever heard about STIs and more than two-thirds (68.73%) of them have ever been tested for HIV. About three-quarters (75.43%) of the girls were exposed to media, with 42.41% using the internet almost every day. Distance to health facilities was a significant concern for less than one-fourth of the participants. Most respondents (59.57%) were from rural areas (table 1).

Prevalence of HPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls in Lesotho

The HPV vaccination uptake among teenage girls in Lesotho stands at 52.15% (95% CI 48.43 to 55.84), with 21.65% having received only one dose and 30.5% having completed two doses (figure 1). Most of these youths received HPV vaccinations at school (94.3%), whereas approximately 5.7% were vaccinated at other locations. Among those who were vaccinated, 45% received an HPV vaccination card, while the remaining 55% did not receive this card. The prevalence of HPV vaccination uptake differs across districts of Lesotho ranging from 34.82% in Maseru to 75.41% in Butha-Buthe (figure 2).

Factors associated with HPV vaccination uptake

The models were compared and the final model with both individual and community-level variables was chosen as the best-fitted model since it had lowest deviance (686.74), AIC (729.21) and BIC (801.32) values. In the final multivariable multilevel robust Poisson regression model; girl’s age, ever heard of cervical cancer, ever heard of STI and district were found to be statistically significant factors of HPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls aged 15–17 in Lesotho with a p value ≤0.05. The proportion of receiving HPV vaccination among girls aged 16 and 17 years was decreased by 14% (aPR=0.86; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.99) and 49% (aPR=0.51; 95% CI 0.41 to 0.65) compared with girls aged 15 years. Adolescent girls who had ever heard of cervical cancer demonstrated a 58% higher proportion of HPV vaccination uptake (aPR=1.58; 95% CI 1.28 to 1.95) compared with those who had not. Additionally, the proportion of HPV vaccination was 18% higher (aPR=1.18; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.37) among adolescent girls who had ever heard of STIs compared with those who had never heard of them. Furthermore, adolescent girls from the Maseru region had a 48% (aPR=0.52, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.82) lower proportion of receiving HPV vaccination compared with adolescent girls residing in Butha-Buthe (table 2).

Discussion

This study revealed that the magnitude of HPV vaccination uptake in Lesotho was 52.15% (95% CI 48.43 to 55.84). Even though the uptake of HPV vaccination is below the WHO 2030 target of 90%, it was higher than a meta-analysis in SSA among schoolgirls (28.53%).10 This aligns with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses in high-income and low-income countries, which was 41.5%.27 However, this was lower than nationwide studies conducted in Brazil,28 South Africa29 and Tanzania,30 where the uptake was 71.1%, 75% and 84.7% respectively. Potential reasons for the lower HPV vaccination uptake in Lesotho might be concerns over side effects, societal attitudes and cultural beliefs, incorrect information, difficulty in accessing the HPV vaccine and a general lack of understanding regarding HPV, the vaccine and cervical cancer.10 31 The observed prevalence in the country highlights the importance of ongoing monitoring to assess the effectiveness of current vaccination programmes and pinpoint areas that need further intervention to achieve the WHO target.

The findings of this study underscore the factors influencing HPV vaccination uptake, ranging from individual-level to contextual. In the final model, we found that age, ever heard of cervical cancer, ever heard of STIs and district were statistically significant determinants of HPV vaccination uptake. The relationship between age and HPV vaccination uptake likely shows programme eligibility and the timing of the vaccine rollout than by age itself. Nevertheless, girls aged 16 and 17 years were found to be less likely to receive HPV vaccination than girls aged less than 15 years. However, this is inconsistent with a finding reported in Ethiopia,20 where the increasing age of girls was associated with a higher likelihood of vaccination. This is because, in Lesotho, the HPV vaccine is intended for girls between the ages of 9 and 14, offering protection against HPV types, which are responsible for genital warts and the majority of cervical cancers,23 in accordance with WHO recommendations.6 However, girls aged 16 and older may no longer qualify for these programmes, particularly if they missed the initial rollout or dropped out of school. This issue is especially significant in Lesotho, where high dropout rates among adolescent girls are often driven by early marriage, pregnancy and socioeconomic challenges.32 33 Additionally, the HPV vaccination is cost-effective for girls aged below 16, with health benefits, but declines as they age up, indicating a need for increased vaccination coverage.34

Similar to a study done in Ethiopia,20 having heard of cervical cancer positively influences the uptake of HPV vaccination. Compared with their counterparts, those who had ever heard of cervical cancer were more likely to receive HPV vaccination. A possible explanation might be the relationship between knowledge and health-seeking behaviours; those who are informed about cervical cancer are more likely to view the HPV vaccine as a preventive measure, resulting in higher uptake rates. Moreover, research shows that a lack of knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV affects the uptake of HPV vaccination. To enhance vaccination rates, educational interventions on cervical cancer and its prevention are essential.35 36

Having ever heard about STIs was found to be associated with increased uptake of HPV vaccination. This could be because awareness of STIs, including HPV, enhances understanding of the vaccine’s role in preventing infections that may lead to severe health outcomes, such as cervical cancer. In addition to this, the experience of STIs may cause a change in risk perception.37 For instance, in a cohort study, women who get vaccinated have higher rates of STIs before being vaccinated compared with those who did not get vaccinated. Because women who have previously had STIs are at a greater risk of contracting HPV, it is crucial to be vaccinated against HPV earlier, a measure which in some cases may have been prompted by a preceding STI diagnosis.38 Moreover, in contexts where adolescent sexual activity is stigmatised, individuals who have received counselling or treatment for STIs may be more open to preventive measures such as HPV vaccination.39 However, this situation also highlights concerns about missed opportunities for primary prevention among girls who are not yet sexually active but lack access to age-appropriate, non-judgmental health education.

Furthermore, adolescent girls who resided in Maseru district were less likely to receive HPV vaccination compared with those from Butha-Buthe district. This was consistent with the descriptive statistics, which showed that Maseru district had the lowest HPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls. This may be attributed to misinformation about the efficacy and safety of HPV vaccine as well as vaccine hesitancy.40 Educating healthcare professionals to convey information effectively to parents regarding the safety and advantages of the vaccine is essential. Open communication can also help ease concerns and promote vaccination. However, to understand district-level disparities in HPV vaccination coverage, we recommend future studies with qualitative or mixed-methods approach.

The strength of this study lies in its use of recent, nationally representative data. However, the key limitation is that the LDHS interviewed 15–17-year-old females about HPV vaccination, reflecting retrospective reporting, which may not reveal the current coverage since Lesotho’s HPV campaign targets girls aged 9–14. The study cannot establish a causal relationship between HPV vaccination uptake and associated factors due to its reliance on cross-sectional data. As HPV vaccination cards were not available for everyone, information was also obtained through self-reports, which might introduce social desirability biases and subsequent over-reporting. This could lead to differential misclassification, as such over-reporting is likely concentrated among girls without cards, potentially resulting in an overestimation of the total vaccine coverage reported. Additionally, the data provided by the DHS lack important variables, such as knowledge and awareness of HPV, attitudes towards HPV vaccination, and perceptions of cervical cancer.

Conclusion

This study highlights the current state of HPV vaccination coverage, revealing critical insights into its prevalence and the factors influencing its uptake. HPV vaccination uptake among adolescent girls in Lesotho is behind the WHO 2030 target of 90% of girls being fully vaccinated. Age, ever heard of cervical cancer, ever heard of STIs and district were significant predictors of HPV vaccination uptake.

To improve HPV vaccination rates among adolescent girls in Lesotho, it is essential to implement community-based outreach initiatives to reach out-of-school girls. Additionally, conducting district-level planning will help address geographic disparities, particularly in Maseru, by identifying local challenges and performing door-to-door follow-ups in cases of parental refusal. Integrating education about cervical cancer, HPV and STIs into national curricula and youth programmes is crucial. Furthermore, fostering collaboration among health, education and gender ministries will ensure coordinated service delivery. Finally, establishing robust monitoring systems to track vaccination coverage by age, school status and district will inform targeted interventions and measure progress towards the WHO’s 2030 target.
