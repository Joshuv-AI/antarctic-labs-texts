Abstract
. Software increasingly shapes the infrastructures of daily life, making requirements engineering (RE) central to ensuring that systems align with human values and lived experiences. Yet, current popular practices such as CrowdRE and AI-assisted elicitation strategies risk detaching requirements work from the cultural, social, and political contexts that shape lived experiences, human values, and real user needs. In this paper, we introduce REConnect that re-centers RE on the human connection as central to the understanding of lived experiences where impact is sought. REConnect advocates for a human-centered participatory approach “that matters" to the communities and beneficiaries involved, ensuring alignment with their values and aspirations. Drawing on three case studies of societal impact: BloodSync in rural Nepal, Herluma supporting women at risk of homelessness in Canada, and BridgingRoots to revitalize Indigenous languages in the Canadian Arctic. REConnect argues that three key principles and enablers: building trusting relationships, co-designing with and alongside stakeholders, and empowering users as agents of change, can yield requirements that are culturally grounded, socially legitimate, and sustainable beyond system delivery. REConnect also proposes a set of actionable practices (REActions) that embed relationality and ongoing stakeholder engagement throughout requirements elicitation, analysis, and validation of solution development. Finally, we situate REConnect in the era of Generative AI. While AI can accelerate and scale certain RE tasks, its integration must be guided by participatory practices that not only preserve human agency but also empower humans’ roles to become guardians of values and ethics, inclusion amplifiers, curators of AI outputs, and co-reflectors in iterative review cycles.

to transportation networks

, from classroom tools

)
to social institutions

)
, software shapes how we live, work, and interact
(
Dodge and Kitchin 2011
)
. It influences the resources we access
(
Noble 2018
)
, the decisions we make
(
Floridi 2015
)
, and the identities we form
(
Floridi 2015
)
. As the boundaries between digital, physical, and social spaces are blurring
(
Bennaceur et al. 2024
)
, software has become a dominant driver of our lived experiences. The decisions organizations make in designing these socio-technical systems bear growing ethical, legal, and societal weight
(
Weiser 1999
)
. Researchers
(
Shahin et al. 2022
;
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
;
Hussain et al. 2022
)
and recent movements such as digital humanism argue that human values and needs, rights, and dignity should be placed at the center of technology development.

Requirements Engineering (RE), as the field pertinent to the elicitation, representation, and validation of requirements for software-intensive applications
(
Sommerville and Sawyer 1997
)
, has a unique opportunity but also a greater than ever responsibility to identify, reason and specify users’ values and the context of their lived experiences.
Human values such as privacy, fairness or transparency have become more important than ever in system adoption
(
Whittle et al. 2019b
)
, underscoring the greater than ever importance of requirements engineering for a diverse society.
Pure technical functionality is no longer seen as the sole measure of software success.
In reality, user values, goals, and requirements are deeply shaped by social, economic, and political contexts
(
Le Dantec and Fox 2015
)
.
Trust and relationships influence adoption, and power structures affect not only the voices whose needs are represented but also those who can influence software adoption in communities or societies.
Recent approaches argue for participatory approaches involving a diversity of stakeholders and teams to identify and operationalize human values in software development; however, authors also recognize the outstanding challenges in eliciting human values as they are highly contextual: they are
subjective
as they depend on diverse stakeholders’ viewpoints and their lived experiences, and
uncertain
as they are better understood once the software solution is deployed
(
Bennaceur et al. 2024
)
.

The increasing scope and scale of today’s software bring additional significant challenges to the elicitation, analysis, and validation of human values and requirements
in context
: deciphering users’ lived experiences and latent needs is no longer possible through traditional participatory design methods
(
Macaulay 2012
)
.
Many software organizations have turned to automating the requirements elicitation process and to ‘listening’ to product users on online product forums or product reviews; organizations then turn this sheer volume of user input towards fixing bugs or developing new features
(
Maalej and Nabil 2015
;
Pagano and Maalej 2013
)
. The field of CrowdRE has contributed methods and practices that leverage semi-automated techniques for mining app reviews
(
Groen et al. 2017
)
, sentiment analysis
(
Guzman and Maalej 2014a
)
, and clustering user feedback with large language models
(
Maalej et al. 2025
)
. While such methods are “participatory" in nature, given their intention to engage with users and their voices, they favour efficiency at the compromise of user-facing interactions.
This risks detachment from the users’ context-dependent lived experience and needs.
Incomplete user input can lead to the loudest voices overshadowing quieter issues
(
Tizard et al. 2024
)
; underrepresentation of certain user voices leads to incomplete requirements elicitation and biased input in the software engineering process
(
Tizard et al. 2024
)
.
Design decisions, increasingly based on output from AI algorithms, can be biased due to an incomplete understanding of users’ characteristics
(
Buolamwini and Gebru 2018
)
, affecting users’ lived experiences (e.g., biased medical care decisions in English councils
(
Rickman 2024
)
).
These risks amount as AI agents provide emergent behavior at runtime from automatically generated requirements
(
Mitchell et al. 2025
)
.

In this paper, we call for revisiting the possibilities and enablers of participatory approaches in requirements engineering by re-centering its efforts on stakeholder relationships and trust, co-design, and user empowerment throughout the requirements engineering activities during system development and post-delivery, through what we term REConnect.
Drawing on value-based requirements engineering
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
and community-engaging participatory approaches to system design
(
Whittle and et al. 2014
)
, REConnect positions the human connection as central to understanding of lived experiences where impact is sought, and advocates for a human-centered participatory RE approach ’that matters’ – to the communities and beneficiaries involved, ensuring
alignment with their values and aspirations. It goes beyond capturing functional needs to ensuring that principles of digital humanism, such as fairness,
inclusiveness, and human agency, are accounted for in the context of lived experiences, in ongoing engagement with relevant stakeholders. REConnect embeds a deep understanding of the cultural, socio-economic, and political
contexts in which communities operate, recognizing that these factors influence both the needs expressed and the
feasibility of solutions. We draw on three case studies of socially impactful projects that describe requirements as social artifacts negotiated through stakeholder relationships and shared understanding, and shaped by cultural contexts and influencing social dynamics.
From these case studies, we introduce the three principles of the REConnect method: (1) building trusting relationships with stakeholders; (2) co-designing with and alongside users; and (3) empowering users as agents of change.
In what follows, we first briefly review user-centered and value-based requirements engineering as a precursor to the era of “automated, AI-assisted RE," and as a way of explaining the limitations of the more recent CrowdRE research and practices. In particular, we highlight the growing disconnect between scaled RE practices and their ability to address the needs of diverse user populations. We then describe the three case studies before we introduce REConnect, its three main principles, and associated REConnect Actions for actionable insight. We conclude by reflecting on the role of such extensive human involvement in the era of Generative AI.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Inclusiveness in Software Engineering

Today’s complex software systems must account for diverse user experiences and human contexts
(
Autili et al. 2025
;
Bennaceur et al. 2024
)
. Requirements engineering plays a critical role in understanding user needs and characteristics, yet organizations often struggle to identify
who
to involve and
how
to capture their perspectives. This gap can have serious consequences: for example, object-detection systems in self-driving vehicles exhibit higher error rates for pedestrians with darker skin tones, illustrating how insufficiently diverse data can lead to real-world harm
(
Wilson et al. 2019
)
.

Addressing these challenges requires attention to human values
(
Friedman et al. 2013
)
and user characteristics such as age
(
Mannheim et al. 2019
)
, ability, and socioeconomic status
(
Burnett et al. 2021
)
. Other authors argue for an even more deeply intertwined relationship between inclusive software and human values. Inclusive software is designed to respect, reflect, and accommodate the diversity of human needs, abilities, and contexts
(
Nuseibeh 2025
)
, while human values provide the ethical and social principles that guide its design and development
(
Ferrario et al. 2016
)
. Human values such as equity, fairness, accessibility, privacy, autonomy, and respect define what inclusivity means in practice. Without explicitly considering these values, software introduces bias that can unintentionally marginalize certain groups or create barriers to participation. For example, accessibility features support equity for users with disabilities, multilingual interfaces reflect cultural respect, and privacy mechanisms uphold autonomy and trust.

Concerns about system biases go back to Friedman and Nissenbaum
(
Friedman and Nissenbaum 1996
)
, who detailed how computer systems can perpetuate societal prejudice. Sutcliffe
(
Sutcliffe 2002
)
emphasized user diversity early in user-centered requirements methods, while later frameworks such as Value-Sensitive Design
(
Friedman et al. 2013
)
aimed to systematically account for stakeholder values and ethics or participatory approaches to ensure values are grounded in real user needs and their context. In the following sections, we briefly review methods for user-centered and participatory requirements engineering that aim to elicit and operationalize human values through direct interaction with users and communities.

2.2.
User-centered and Participatory RE

The role played by requirements engineering in responsible software engineering is not new.
What is continually evolving is our understanding of
context
necessary to understand diverse users’ values, requirements, and lived experiences in a world increasingly driven and dependent on software
(
Nuseibeh 2025
)
. Since the early 2000s, authors such as Sutcliffe
(
Sutcliffe 2002
)
have advocated for the importance of RE activities that focus on understanding the human element in software engineering, how people think, communicate, and make decisions. Later frameworks for requirements analysis accounted for individual and personal goals and the effect of time and context on personal requirements
(
Sutcliffe et al. 2005
)
. Context emerged as a driver of requirements engineering, as user needs could not be fully understood without their situational context, such as physical setting, workflow, organizational or ethnic culture, and domain-specific constraints.

Building on the same premise that systems should be shaped around users’ needs and contexts, Linda Macaulay’s work
(
Macaulay 2012
)
advocates for direct user involvement in requirements elicitation, analysis, and validation. Participation through group-based and interactive techniques (like JAD, focus groups, cooperative prototyping) becomes a way to capture stakeholders’ context so the requirements reflect real use situations.
In fact, collaboration with the users not as “data sources" but active participants and co-creation of understanding is an element shared also by Holtzblatt’s contextual inquiry
(
Beyer and Holtzblatt 1998
)
that puts the user’s environment front and center through immersive field studies, while Whittle and colleagues
(
Whittle and et al. 2014
)
expand the circle of participation to include communities, especially undeserved or civic groups, in co-production of technology. All three approaches underscore the importance of understanding and embedding context, whether it is workflow, social dynamics, or moral values, into the design process. More recently, Whittle and colleagues argue that human values (like fairness, transparency, responsibility) must be explicitly surfaced and integrated into requirements and development cycles
(
Whittle et al. 2019a
)
, which we discuss in the next section.

Across multiple studies, evidence consistently shows that active user participation in software development—particularly during requirements engineering—supports adoption, system usability and project success. Early studies by Zand and Sorensen
(
Zand and Sorensen 1975
)
showed that involving users can help overcome resistance to organizational change. In the context of requirements engineering, user engagement has been found to increase system acceptance
(
Kujala 2003
)
, enhance the likelihood of project success
(
Kujala et al. 2005
)
, and improve users’ understanding of system functionality
(
Damodaran 1996
)
. Moreover, participatory practices contribute to stronger customer loyalty and broader market reach by ensuring the software meets real user needs
(
Kabbedijk et al. 2009
)
.

2.3.
Value-based RE

Awareness of human values in software engineering has grown through the work by Ferrario et al.
(
Ferrario et al. 2016
)
and Mougouei et al.
(
Mougouei et al. 2018
)
, drawing on Schwartz’s framework of human values
(
Schwartz 2012
)
. Whittle argues that traditional requirements engineering (RE) often overlooks the values layer, producing systems that may be functionally sound but misaligned with users’ ethical expectations. By integrating human values, software can do more than operate correctly — it can align ethically with the needs, priorities, and well-being of its users and communities
(
Whittle et al. 2019a
)
.

Later value-based RE frameworks
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
;
Harbers et al. 2015
)
emphasize the explicit elicitation of stakeholder values, motivations, and emotions, highlighting that failing to distinguish between user and system requirements can create “soft issues" in RE, such as politics, feelings, motivations, and ethical concerns
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
. Methods including interviews, workshops and ethnography are proposed to elicit stakeholder values, motivations, and emotions. VBRE also accounts for process management implications, such as prioritization conflicts, trade-offs, and stakeholder negotiation, enabling teams to systematically address ethical and social concerns throughout the development lifecycle
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
. Critical systems thinking has been proposed alongside VBRE as a complementary approach to integrate nontechnical aspects—ethics, power, politics, and values—into early RE activities
(
Duboc et al. 2019
)
.

Additional studies have explored ways to model and represent emotional goals for gender-inclusive design
(
Hassett et al. 2023
)
emotions and values in RE, including specialized modeling languages
(
Miguéis et al. 2019
;
Pedell et al. 2015
)
, while Perera et al.
(
Perera et al. 2021
)
provide evidence that considering human values—such as freedom, fairness, and social responsibility—early in RE prevents violations that could result in financial, reputational, or societal harm. Scenario-based values elicitation, as proposed in VBRE, operationalizes these insights, providing a practical method for capturing and applying values in requirements and design decisions.

2.4.
Automated techniques for RE: CrowdRE and GenAI approaches

Defined as “an umbrella term for automated or semi-automated approaches to gather and analyze information from a crowd to derive validated user requirements"
(
Groen et al. 2017
)
, Crowd-based requirements engineering (CrowdRE) has emerged as a strategy to address the scale challenges of modern software projects by leveraging insights from large and diverse user populations. Techniques in CrowdRE commonly involve mining user feedback from sources such as app reviews, social media, forums, and issue trackers to identify requirements-relevant information
(
Groen et al. 2017
)
. For example, Pagano and Maalej
(
Pagano and Maalej 2013
)
demonstrated how app store reviews can provide rich insights into features, usability, and quality issues, serving as both requirements and bug reports.

Researchers have further investigated automated classification to extract requirements insights from the crowd. Guzman et al.
(
Guzman and Maalej 2014b
)
applied sentiment analysis to extract user opinions, while Kurtanović and Maalej
(
Kurtanović and Maalej 2017
)
used machine learning to classify reviews into functional and non-functional requirements, supporting large-scale requirements analysis. More recently, Generative AI (GenAI) techniques, such as large language models and text generation tools, have been applied to summarize, categorize, and synthesize crowd feedback, enabling faster and some understanding of user needs
(
Ghosh et al. 2024
;
Sami et al. 2024
;
Almeida and Silva 2024
;
Ataei et al. 2024
;
Ronanki et al. 2023
;
InfoWorld 2025
)
. GenAI can also help generate hypothetical scenarios, simulate user perspectives, and identify potential gaps in representation, enhancing the inclusivity of requirements elicitation.

However, CrowdRE and GenAI approaches raise ethical and inclusivity considerations. While they scale well, less vocal stakeholders may be underrepresented, and AI models can inadvertently propagate biases present in the training data
(
Tronnier and et al. 2024
;
Al-kfairy and et al. 2024
;
Ferrara 2023
)
. From a value-sensitive perspective, this highlights the risk of systematic exclusion, where software may neglect underrepresented groups or misalign with users’ values
(
Aizenberg and van den Hoven 2020
;
Bano and et al. 2023
)
. Gender minorities and other marginalized populations, for example, may be overlooked if datasets or training procedures are not inclusive
(
UNDP 2024
;
Ramos Salazar and et al. 2024
)
. Integrating CrowdRE with GenAI in a way that actively considers human values, stakeholder diversity, and ethical principles is therefore essential for building software that is both functional and socially responsible
(
Anonymous 2024
)
.

Inspiration for REConnect: Participatory RE that matters

First, we describe three cases that have inspired our proposal for REConnect: BloodSync, Herluma, and BridgingRoots projects. We present them as cases that illustrate and highlight the role and influence of the human connection during the requirements and software engineering processes, and without which, the subtle elements that mattered for the communities and users of the systems in question would not have surfaced, likely leading to a lack of system adoption. The projects were initiated and managed through a program of community-based research and innovation at the University of Victoria
1
1

1

inspireuvic.org
, and the project team initially included senior students (in Canada and Nepal) trained in industry-mentored experiential learning approaches. In Section
4
we present and discuss our proposed REConnect approach by drawing on examples from these cases.

3.1.
Case Study 1: BloodSync

Nepal’s healthcare landscape is characterized by a stark urban–rural divide: more than 70% of the population cannot afford private care, and remote districts like Rukum lack essential infrastructure
(
Karkee and Comfort 2016
)
. Chaurjahari Hospital Rukum (CHR) faces chronic blood shortages due to the absence of a local transfusion center. The BloodSync project developed an ICT-driven blood management platform tailored to the region’s low-literacy, low-connectivity, and socio-cultural realities, with an emphasis on maternal and emergency care.

Shared presence and lived empathy
in the Rukum community were foundational to building trust and relationships in every step of the BloodSync project. In remote places such as Rukum, medical care depends more on local relationships than formal systems
(
Panday et al. 2017
)
. When CHR faced ongoing blood shortages, especially during maternal emergencies, families were left to source blood donors themselves, often with tragic consequences. Rather than assuming solutions from the outset, the team prioritized learning from lived experiences on the ground. In doing so, they found that developing trusting relationships was essential before design ideas could be discussed. The BloodSync team journeyed over 600 kilometers across landslides and earthquake-damaged terrain to be physically present in the community. The team spent hours at the hospital, engaging and walking through hospital wards with the medical director and participating in donating blood in its emergency ward.

The BloodSync team initiated informal dialogues with hospital staff, village elders, and health volunteers to understand local beliefs surrounding blood donation. Misinformation and historic institutional neglect had fostered fears that donation weakens the body or shortens life. Becoming aware of the influence of political figures, the team, now trusted by the hospital, engaged with the Mayor of Rukum and Red Cross representatives, who have been involved in many other development efforts that engaged and impacted the community. The team learned that the community valued humility, integrity, and credibility, values that significantly boost public trust and are essential to trustworthy governance in Nepal
(
Subedi and Bhandari 2025
;
Kathayat 2024
)
, which were often lacking in previous failed development efforts. The Mayor emphasized the importance of involving credible stakeholders such as ward leaders (local political figures reporting to the mayor) in helping with local awareness campaigns and donor mobilization. The team engaged in repeated meetings with the community and was able to uncover details of the political and cultural dynamics shaping trust, carefully mapping relationships among elected officials, ward networks, and health institutions. This strategy secured early buy-in from critical actors who later became co-design partners.

Co-designing with the politicians and hospital leaders
allowed a solution to emerge with active participation of community members, politicians, and hospital leaders; these stakeholders were central to the design itself, not merely feedback providers. Initially, the team intended to develop an application where blood donors registered themselves and received notification and an invitation to donate when a medical case requiring blood emerged. Early feasibility discussions, however, directly challenged these intuitive design assumptions by highlighting barriers like limited digital literacy and, more importantly, superstition-driven fear of donating blood. Design decisions had to be anchored in the realities of rural health coordination. An alternate design emerged whereby the hospital staff would automatically add any new patients visiting the hospital as a potential donor, thereby eliminating the need for donor registration. Furthermore, the deeply rooted resistance to blood donations was approached by leveraging the locals’ trust in their ward leaders and who were now to be notified of eligible donors in their respective wards, and phone them personally to donate blood.

From the start, community stakeholders co-led the design process; together, they shaped a people-centered system that routed hospital blood requests through ward leaders, the local trusted figures, who would reach out to donors through personal communication rather than automated alerts. This approach was co-built through immersive workshops involving hospital doctors, ward leaders, and Red Cross staff. Simulated emergency scenarios surfaced key accessibility requirements such as language, iconography, and office use. Trust, usability, and cultural alignment were integral to the design. By embedding community voices in every iteration, the solution design gained legitimacy and sustainability.

Empowering ward leaders
during solution design and implementation turned a technical platform into a sustainable practice of care. It emerged as an opportunity for community-driven action rooted in local trust and shared responsibility, an important cultural element of the blood donation workflow. As discussions unfolded between the BloodSync team, the Mayor, hospital, and ward leaders, it became clear that giving the ward leaders autonomy to coordinate donor mobilization aligned with both their roles as elected officials and their intrinsic motivation to support community welfare. The decision to integrate them into the system was not imposed, but evolved as a natural extension of their influence and standing in the community.

Similarly, Red Cross staff and ward leaders, initially unfamiliar with digital tools, gained confidence through simulation-based training coordinated by the Mayor. As they began leading donor outreach, their roles evolved from passive participants to active owners, culminating in the formation of a formal blood coordination committee. This committee, comprising ward leaders, medical staff, and volunteers, helped refine the blood donation workflow, improve donor verification, and adapt outreach strategies to local realities. Real-time dashboards enabled the hospital and Red Cross staff to monitor requests and blood stock, strengthening coordination and fostering a cultural shift toward shared responsibility. Community leaders later proposed expanding these digital workflows to maternal health and emergency transport. The outcome was more than a tech solution; it was a locally embedded care infrastructure grounded in trust, participation, and sustained leadership.

3.2.
Case Study 2: Herluma

Improving access to shelters and basic care for women fleeing intimate partner violence and facing homelessness has been the goal of the Herluma project in collaboration with the Greater Victoria Coalition to End Homelessness (GVCEH) organization in Victoria, British Columbia. Through a number of pivots, the project developed a web-based “fleeing-violence navigator" that aggregates up-to-date bed availability and more effective operations in local shelters.

Forging authentic connections
with the women experiencing homelessness and the homeless shelters’ staff required more than technical readiness; it demanded an understanding of the deeply rooted systemic problems, intergenerational trauma, and stress within shelter staff. The Herluma team started building relationships in sessions led by people with lived homelessness experience. Powerful, emotionally charged sessions enabled the team to understand the stigmatization around homelessness
(
Burrows et al. 2019
)
, and the complexity behind how an individual, as a child, is destined to be homeless as a result of generational abuse and poverty. A key stakeholder, the GVCEH facilitated focus-groups with women experiencing domestic violence, some of whom had later experienced homelessness, in hopes of gathering pain points of how a technical solution may help them find resources and shelters. The authenticity of the team in understanding the problem at its core helped build trust and a relationship with the homeless women and GVCEH.

The trusting relationship with GVCEH was key to understanding that the homelessness problem is a multifaceted, socially, and politically complex issue
(
Shinn 2007
)
, and to navigate a complicated shelter support ecosystem. With the guidance of GVCEH, the team engaged during the course of several meetings with BC Housing, an important government organization and stakeholder who later expressed strong support for the project, recognizing their collaborative approach as distinct from previous short-term interventions. This endorsement helped to further strengthen credibility across the shelter ecosystem. Meanwhile, at the shelters, frontline workers were navigating daily emotional tolls of anxiety and frustration from trauma disclosures, safety planning, and system navigation, often while spending hours phoning other shelters in search of vacancies. Any technology-based external intervention, no matter how well-intentioned, risked being dismissed unless it first proved capable of recognizing that burden. With BC Housing’s backing, the project team’s efforts gained added authenticity, paving the way for deeper relationships with other shelter organizations that had previously been hesitant to engage.

Co-designing with people with lived experience
shaped the design outlook in the Herluma project. The project team initially assumed that women fleeing domestic violence and experiencing homelessness lacked a tool, namely a mobile application to manage important contact information, access to shelter overnight, harm-reduction zones, or even maternity care. However, during the focus group sessions with the women, the team realized that most of them do not possess a cell phone or have access to the internet to be able to view the resources from a mobile application. Especially in the case of domestic abuse, women did not want to leave a digital footprint but rather maintain anonymity. These insights led to a major overhaul in the system design requirements. Although there was a limitation in creating prototyping artifacts due to differences in level of understanding, the team was able to identify the pain points of women facing abuse and not having a shelter for the night.

Subsequent working sessions with GVCEH and BC Housing allowed the team to co-design a solution for organizations and shelters working towards helping women at risk of homelessness. Together with the shelter staff, the team iterated over multiple prototypes of a web-based application tailored to creating efficient shelter bed management tools, while protecting the anonymity of the women who walk into the shelters by using unique identifiers while using pseudonyms. Conversations around privacy risks for shelters led to the system offering both public and private visibility settings to protect shelters serving undocumented or high-risk populations. The co-design process in Herluma unfolded not as a series of scheduled feedback cycles but as a deeply embedded collaboration shaped by the realities of women fleeing violence and front-line shelter workers.

Empowering shelter operators, most of them former homeless women
was crucial in finding the solution that worked. The ongoing engagement of shelter operators, most of them having shared homelessness in the past, in learning about the homeless women’s lived experience proved empowering to them. It brought them a sense of giving back, along with hopes that their friends and family on the streets can have easy access to emergency shelters, later transitioning to working at these shelters. The team collaborated with people inside and outside these shelters to develop a design that helped the homeless shelters adopt the system. For instance, early adopters like the Victoria Native Friendship Centre quickly integrated the tool into routine operations. Front-line workers demonstrated their use, answered questions, and encouraged other shelters to adopt. As the system gained traction, staff-led feedback informed key adaptations. Flexibility with privacy tools increased confidence among staff, reinforcing a sense of control over how and when information was shared. The shift from passive adoption to ownership meant the project empowered women towards safety and sovereignty while boosting efficiency in the shelters by streamlining communications and bed management.

3.3.
Case Study 3: Bridging Roots

“How do we teach a lost Indigenous language in a remote community in Canada’s Arctic with scarce resources in a way that respects community participatory action?" is a quote by M. Tomasino, Vice-Principal of Mangilaluk School in Tuktoyaktuk (Tuk), a remote Inuit hamlet of about 950 inhabitants on the Western Canadian shores of the Arctic Ocean. “The impact of residential schools in Canada is that our language and culture were disallowed in schools after 1871, leaving Indigenous people without the means to communicate their stories." The Bridging Roots project co-designed, in an authentic partnership with the Tuktoyaktuk community, a game-based language revitalization tool to teach Inuvialuktun (the language of the Western Canadian Inuit) to students in grades 2-12 at the Mangilaluk School. When using the tool, the students engage in class activities in the form of games that use the Inuvialuktun language; they can also add to the information about their community and land (stories, photos), actively contributing to a growing body of knowledge about their language and community.

A long-term collaboration
built on cultural awareness and high regard for authentic relationships rather than technical project details has contributed uniquely to the success of this project. Foundational to a successful interaction with Indigenous communities is respect for their people, culture, and ways of living
(
Wilson-Raybould 2024
)
. The project evolved from the high goal of involving Inuit youth in learning about technology design to a more specific goal of supporting their community in its language revitalization efforts. Over two years, the project team developed a trusting relationship with the community and the Mangilaluk School in Tuk that proved essential to the discovery, refinement, development, and validation of tool features. This was only possible because of the relationship developed with the youth and respected Elders in the community. The long-term engagement – in-person interaction (three visits of one week at a time) followed by ongoing online communication – led to cultural sensitivity and identifying the community’s value of authentic relationships and community-participatory action
(
Wilson-Raybould 2024
)
, rooted in historical trauma due to extensive extractive relationships. In-person visits facilitated consultations with the Indigenous elders on ways to teach the Inuit language and traditions. The interactions with the Inuit youth were informal in the first few months, mostly texting about the youth’s life on the shores of the Arctic Ocean and which, on the surface, was not contributing to the project’s success. Remarkable, however, are the friendships that developed between the Indigenous youth and the non-Inuit team members: authentic and deep, in line with the Indigenous culturally-informed ways of living and being. The Inuit youth became more engaged in the project with every visit to Tuktoyaktuk, leading to meaningful co-design of the language teaching tool.

Co-designing with the community
was essential to understanding the intended impact for language revitalization and to developing the system features. In-person workshops during immersive visits to Tuk engaged community Elders, language teachers from the school, and students in various 2-12 grades. Initial brainstorming sessions clearly demonstrated the limited understanding that the project team had about the Indigenous ways of teaching their native language and how embedded that was within the Indigenous ways of living on their traditional lands, and the role of community participation. Without directly engaging the youth and members of the Inuit community during brainstorming, design, and feature validation activities, the project would have been destined to failure due to a lack of acceptance from the language teachers and the community as a whole.

First, Indigenous languages carry deep meaning rooted in cultural traditions and ways of interacting with the environment, informing important functional and non-functional requirements for the language tool. The community consultations revealed that in oral history cultures like the Inuit’s, traditions have been preserved and transmitted through drawings, or orally through stories, from generation to generation. As such, useful features included youth being able to record Inuvialuktun words, phrases, and stories from elders; moreover, these stories would relate to traditional activities on their lands, such as fishing or hunting. Second, games emerged as the most effective way to keep students engaged in using the tool during language classes. Low attendance to classes and feelings of depression is not something we consider when designing educational tooling and yet is typical of Indigenous communities where students deal with a significant amount of personal, family-related trauma, resulting in depression and long-term absence from the school. The co-design workshops also facilitated discussions around tradeoffs necessary due to conflicting community values: timely language teaching vs. teaching the correct spelling and pronunciation; the conflict was due to the rapidly diminishing expert knowledge from community members knowledgeable of the language and traditions (only four out of about 100 families still speaking Inuvialuktun in Tuk). The collective resolution was to enable the school language teachers to verify the language content in the app.

Empowering the youth and the community
in creating and growing the language content has been a key success factor in the adoption, uptake, and sustainability of the tool in the classrooms. Motivated by a desire to learn tech design, the Inuit youth used Figma to design games, often in collaboration with grade two students who drew game elements that would appeal to the younger students. The sense of engagement further motivated the older students to interview community Elders and record Inuit stories about traditional skills, including hunting or fishing, or simply surviving on the ice, in a place surrounded by ice for ten months in a year. New content being taught in the language app was now being created by their own community members! Finally, the youth empowerment extended beyond actively participating in the technology-enabled language revitalization in their community: at the time of writing, three of the Inuit youth, inspired to pursue higher education “down south", are submitting applications for undergraduate degrees at the University of Victoria. They declared a higher confidence to live outside their close-knit Indigenous community, and about 4,000 km away from their families.

3.4.
Summary

In summary, we see the main salient points of these three projects:

The BloodSync project illustrates how trust-building was possible through physical presence and was key to understanding and navigating the political landscape; trust hinged on values of credibility, humility, and aligning with political authority and influence. It revealed how, through trusting relationships, stakeholders both within and outside the system’s formal boundaries can be engaged meaningfully as influencers during requirements elicitation and validation. By embedding design within lived realities, this project demonstrated how systems can emerge organically from within a community, sustained by shared ownership and cultural resonance.

In the Herluma project, through a longer, meaningful interaction with the lesser represented users of technology, their true pain-points were identified, and co-design flipped the initial tech vision entirely when women revealed practical and safety barriers. The solution focused on shelter operators as the primary users, with privacy and anonymity built in — reflecting a “design-for-operators-to-protect-clients" model.

The BridgingRoots project offered a profound example of what long-term, respectful engagement and empowerment look like when collaborating with Indigenous communities, historically subjects of knowledge extraction rather than owners of impactful solutions. Here, relationships were not instrumental to design; they were the design. Trust was built through informal youth engagement, authentic relationships, and cultural sensitivity, rather than extracting requirements; the team earned them through presence, patience, and emotional attunement. Empowerment was about youth and community cultural leadership; Inuit students designed system features by taking active roles as knowledge keepers and agents of continued community engagement for a sustainable language learning application.

These three cases highlight the crucial role of human connection in shaping solutions that align with stakeholders’ visions of societal impact. Human connection emerged as the primary means for deeply understanding, analyzing, and validating technologies that supported their lived experiences.

4.
REConnect: Participatory RE that Matters

Drawing on the three case studies (BloodSync, Herluma, and BridgingRoots), we introduce REConnect, a method that embeds users, community members, and other stakeholders throughout the requirements engineering activities during system development as well as post-delivery. In the era of data-driven automation techniques for understanding user needs, feedback, and requirements
(
Groen et al. 2017
;
Hosseini et al. 2014
)
, there is a risk of overlooking the individual behind each data point. While stakeholder values such as trust, collaboration, morals, ethics, and such
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
are frequently cited as design imperatives, they are embedded, driven by, and possibly changing through lived experiences
in context
, making their identification and understanding increasingly challenging.

Drawing on value-based requirements engineering
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
, REconnect is a call to revitalize RE from a process that aims to discover, analyze, and validate requirements, to re-centering its efforts on the human element – namely the sustained stakeholder relationships that allow not only value recognition, but foster value navigation, the act of uncovering and co-constructing values through real-time, relational engagement. By grounding solution design in an understanding of lived experiences where impact is sought, REConnect advocates for a human-centered participatory RE approach ’that matters’ to the communities and beneficiaries involved, ensuring alignment with their values and aspirations. It goes beyond capturing functional needs to ensuring that these values, embedded and shaped by lived experiences, are aligned with the principles of digital humanism, such as fairness, inclusiveness, and human agency. REConnect embeds a deep understanding of the cultural, socio-economic, and political contexts in which communities operate, recognizing that these factors influence both the needs expressed and the feasibility of solutions.

REConnect is based on three principles:

•

building trusting relationships
, and which enables the identification of stakeholder roles (formal or informal influencers), values, motivations, and emotions

•

co-designing with and alongside users
, where key stakeholders contribute to shaping the design from personal lived experience, and

•

empowering users as agents of change
, changing their role from being passive recipients of decisions to active shapers of outcomes

We describe these principles in more detail below using examples from the three case studies. To make REConnect actionable, we also distilled recurring practices from the three case studies into a set of REActions.

4.1.
Building Trusting Relationships with Stakeholders

Trust is the foundation of effective requirements engineering, and developing trust takes time. Requirements do not emerge in a vacuum; they are negotiated, socially-constructed artifacts
(
Potts and Newstetter 1997
)
. Yet, trust is not built in short-lived interviews, workshops, or transactional field visits
(
ur Rehman et al. 2013
)
; it is cultivated through sustained, reciprocal engagement over time. It requires that stakeholders believe their concerns will be heard, share sensitive information, as well as that software engineers regard this information as credible and actionable
(
Burnay and Snoeck 2017
)
. Prioritizing the human connection and building relationships to learn about the users, their cultural context, and lived experiences, rather than one-off or short-lived interactions, leads to trust and to users being more likely to open up about local social, societal, and political dynamics. When stakeholders perceive authentic commitment and relationships, they are more likely to share unspoken needs and
values
, their
motivations
and
emotions
for changes in their lived experiences, and participate in uncovering the real-world implications of technical solutions. Relationships developed over a longer time period also lead to increased
stakeholders’ buy-in into the design process
since their input is seen as valuable and impactful to the project’s success. We highlight some of these details below, before outlining concrete REConnect actions towards building relationships and trust with stakeholders.

In the BridgingRoots project, understanding that Indigenous partners
valued authentic relationships and friendships, and community engagement
in decision-making was only possible through sustained interaction over 18 months and several in-person visits.
Through this longer engagement, trust developed, and community members became open about personal trauma.
In particular, these partners shared the impact that family trauma has on students’ attendance in classes and patterns of learning behavior.
A game-based approach to language learning, vetted by the community elders, emerged as essential to keeping the learners engaged and interested in the language despite personal hardships.
The friendships developed with the Indigenous youth were key to their engagement in the design of the online games.
Similarly, in the BloodSync project, through extended time engaging with the community and hospital staff, the project team demonstrated genuine
humility
and
credibility
.
These are core values to the Rukum rural community, and represent an authentic desire to learn about the current practices and barriers to blood donation in rural Nepal.
This deep yet delicate engagement set the stage for collaborative requirements workshops, where stakeholders gradually revealed politically motivated interests or constraints, or deeply rooted superstitions.
Gaining the trust and commitment of the local trusted leaders was key to their active participation in co-designing a solution that would otherwise have been doomed to failure due to misalignment with the contextual, culturally-informed community values.
To help the homeless women, in the Herluma project, any acceptable solution had to target inefficiencies in the shelter operations while protecting women’s
dignity
through
anonymity
, an insight possible only through the extended engagement and trust built with the women with lived homeless experience, as well as the burdened, emotionally drained shelter staff.

While trust may depend on how much one party knows about the other participant’s needs, goals, or constraints
(
Luna-Reyes et al. 2008
)
, understanding alone does not build a relationship. Below, we include several concrete REConnect actions that facilitate relationship and trust building during requirements engineering activities:

REConnect Actions
for
Building Trusting Relationships with Stakeholders:

•

Contextual Stakeholder Mapping
: Identify both formal roles and informal influencers in the community (e.g., local ward leaders in BloodSynch). To ensure requirements respect cultural beliefs and practices (e.g., BridgingRoots’ respect for Indigenous way of living and being), co-create stakeholder maps/charts to identify stakeholder relations and their implication to the system
(
Alidoosti et al. 2023
)
. Understand the cultural and political landscape by considering the values and influences of relevant stakeholders (e.g., mayors in BloodSynch).

•

Immersive Field Presence
: Commit to field visits and practice cultural immersion to understand cultural, social or organizational details in context
(
Beyer and Holtzblatt 1999
)
(e.g., BloodSync team’s journey to Rukum and the BridgingRoots visits to remote Tuktoyaktuk). Use ethnographic methods such as storytelling sessions or informal focus groups to build empathy, trust from influential community members, as well as trust in authentic working relationships.

•

Community-engaging facilitated Workshops
: Co-host problem-framing workshops
(
Simonsen and Robertson 2013
)
involving related local organizations knowledgeable of the problem context (e.g., local organizations concerned with ending homelessness in Herluma) and stakeholders identified during contextual stakeholder mapping. Conduct story-sharing sessions to surface cultural beliefs and historical mistrust (e.g., addressing blood donation superstitions with community members in BloodSynch).

4.2.
Co-Designing With and Alongside Users

Engaging stakeholders in solution co-design positions them as co-producers, not just information providers. While collaborative design is not a novel concept in itself, its integration with formal RE processes and validation criteria definition is still underutilized. Developing authentic working relationships with key stakeholders leads to identifying their motivations and values, and facilitates their meaningful engagement in co-designing solutions that not only cater to individual-level requirements but also leverage their motivations and emotional response in building socio-technical solutions
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
. This is compelling and particularly important in contexts of historical marginalization where communities have long been excluded from shaping technologies that affect them.
In BridgingRoots, for example, working with Indigenous teachers and Elders demanded more than inclusive meetings; it required epistemic humility and mutual learning, and resulted in adopting a game-based design to mitigate the effects of personal trauma in the school. Co-design in these settings yielded more than prototypes; it produced requirements that were not only functionally valid but socially legitimate and culturally grounded: Game design workshops with students and teachers shaped the content of culture-aware games and aligned meaningfully with the ways of language teaching in the Inuit community. As oral histories, Indigenous traditions are taught through stories, making language a central culture-preserving mechanism
(
Battiste 2002
)
: stakeholders helped prototype the content interface and content of the language-teaching games to include information around fishing and hunting practices central to their culture.
In the BloodSync project, initial assumptions that the hospital’s challenges stemmed from the absence of a formal blood banking or donation management system were reconsidered through collaborative workshops with political leaders and medical staff. These sessions revealed that the root issue was not a technical gap, but a human coordination problem. Through sustained dialogue, the team and stakeholders co-developed a model in which community leaders, rather than a centralized digital system, would be mobilized to identify and contact eligible donors whenever the hospital sent a case notification to the Mayor’s office. This shift led to a clearly defined hierarchical request workflow, from which formal system requirements were derived.

The following concrete REConnect actions allow embedding user expertise into the structural stages of RE, requirement decomposition, scenario simulation, and artifact creation, and lead to co-design efforts that produce actionable, context and culture-aware, and formally specifiable requirements and outcomes.

REConnect Actions
for
Co-Designing With and Alongside Users:

•

Iterative Prototyping and Simulation
: Use role-playing scenarios to simulate workflows with diverse stakeholders and identify usability gaps (e.g., prototyping workshops in BloodSync and Herluma). Employ "Wizard of Oz" prototypes
(
Segura and Barbosa 2013
)
for rapid user feedback on incomplete features

•

Community-engaged Requirement Mapping
: Conduct joint workshops to decompose requirements with attention to cultural, economic, and technical constraints. Incorporate community feedback loops to validate assumptions (e.g., moving from one-way SMS broadcasts to a multi-tier workflow in BloodSync).

•

Co-Design Artifacts within socio-economic Context
: Develop contextual artifacts (paper sketches, mockups, dashboards) suitable for the users’ literacy and environment (e.g., icon-based designs for BloodSync, desktop-first prototypes for Herluma). Involve users in iterating these artifacts until they clearly reflect local needs.

4.3.
Empowering Users as Agents of Change

Engaging stakeholders in co-design leads to empowerment because it shifts them from being passive recipients of decisions to active shapers of outcomes. Their input and unique perspectives rooted in role-based expertise, lived experiences, or influence within their communities are being recognized in the process. This shifts their participation from passive beneficiaries to active agents of change. Seeing how their input directly influences outcomes shows stakeholders that their voices matter and leads to a sense of agency and autonomy, key drivers of empowerment
(
Deci and Ryan 2013
)
. From our case studies, we learned that in building relationships with stakeholders, an understanding of stakeholders’ motivation and emotions helps capture deeply rooted needs; validating them through co-design activities ultimately empowers the stakeholders, resulting in higher adoption rates, more transparent negotiation, and systems that evolve sustainably after deployment. Through a reciprocal, reinforcing relationship
(
Deci and Ryan 2013
;
Ryan and Deci 2000
)
, this engagement further boosted their motivation and agency and contributes to ongoing civic engagement and advocacy for the envisioned impact.

In the BloodSync project, empowerment began the moment we invited local ward leaders and Red Cross representatives into mayor-led training sessions. By undertaking clear coordination and decision-making roles, these stakeholders not only learned new skills but also took charge of monitoring awareness training attendance and blood banking stocks. Medical staff, often stressed by the unpredictability of donor availability, valued reliability and transparency in the solution-building. Their sense of responsibility motivated them to engage deeply in interactions, while political leaders, driven by power and accountability, sought governance structures that would ensure awareness in the general public. In BridgingRoots, community teachers and Elders took the lead in shaping how language content would be created, curated, and maintained. Inuit youth designed games from an understanding of patterns of peer engagement in the classrooms. Teachers, anxious about misrepresenting histories, insisted on a process rooted in integrity and community-engagement and empowered youth in creating language content through interviews with the community Elders. In Herluma, empowerment emerged from months of in-person engagement with shelter staff who previously experienced homelessness; motivated by their own desire to help women with similar experience, their engagement fuelled their agency and understanding of the actual influence and ability to help these women at risk of homelessness.

REConnect Actions
for
Empowering Users as Agents of Change:

•

Motivation Mapping
: Map Selected Stakeholder Motivations to the solution capabilities. Conduct explicit motivation analysis to understand why stakeholders care and how they might act (e.g. mayors were motivated by public accountability and a sense of achievement to use the system as a governance tool to demonstrate service delivery; Inuvialuktun language teachers and Inuit youth were driven by cultural preservation and community participatory practices).

•

Engage Empowered Stakeholders
: Engaging stakeholders in the design of the respective solution capabilities (e.g. youth co-designed game workflows in BridgingRoots; shelter workers shaped system visibility features, ensuring anonymity of homeless individuals in Herluma)

•

Capacity Building and Training
: Provide training for local stakeholders as part of co-design workshops (e.g., mayor-led ward leader training in BloodSync).

5.
Discussion

5.1.
REConnect: Participatory RE that Matters

REConnect, a deliberate play on words, re-centers human connection in requirements engineering activities. Inspired by projects with tangible societal impact, it emphasizes developing trusting relationships and a sustained engagement with technology end users and stakeholders. This stands in contrast to recent automation-driven approaches that, while efficient, risk overlooking the lived experiences and values of the communities where impact is sought.

At its core, REConnect treats stakeholders as collaborators, not informants. Drawing on value-based requirements engineering
(
Thew and Sutcliffe 2018
)
and community-engaging software engineering
(
Whittle and et al. 2014
)
, REConnect grounds solution design in an understanding of lived experiences where impact is sought, and advocates for a human-centered participatory RE approach “that matters" – to the communities and beneficiaries involved, ensuring alignment with their values and aspirations. It ensures that systems address immediate functional needs while also supporting cultural affinity, dignity, equity, and well-being over time. Participation is elevated from episodic feedback to continuous dialogue and shared authorship, drawing on Participatory Design to expand RE competencies toward cultural awareness, active listening, and synthesis of diverse perspectives.

It is reasonable, however, to ask whether such extensive emphasis on human involvement remains necessary in an era of rapid advances in AI. After all, automated tools are increasingly capable of generating and analyzing RE artifacts, designs, and even implementing solutions. REConnect does not reject automation; it embraces it as a complement. AI can uncover patterns and scale analysis, but it cannot replace the depth of understanding that comes from lived experience, trust-building, and co-creation. By embedding ongoing participation, REConnect moves beyond recognizing values to making them actionable. By leveraging AI for efficiency, it reframes requirements as evolving entities, shaped by the interplay of human insight and technological capability. We will discuss the integration of GenAI within REConnect next.

5.2.
Towards AI-Enhanced Participatory RE: Integrating Generative AI within REConnect

Advancements in machine learning models have afforded increased automation in requirements engineering over the past decades. Interestingly enough, automation support has been and continues to be a moving target for RE. Early automation efforts provided some coarse insight into stakeholder needs. For example, basic NLP techniques like keyword extraction, topic modeling, and simple sentiment analysis could analyze app reviews or support tickets to reveal common themes
(
Maalej and Nabil 2015
;
Pagano and Maalej 2013
)
, but these approaches were often limited in context. By the late 2010s, researchers began exploring deep learning that could interpret requirements text, enabling tasks like automated classification of requirements
(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
or matching user feedback to feature requests
(
Johann et al. 2017
)
.

Today, the AI and LLM revolution represents a significant leap forward in automation for RE activities. State-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., GPT-5) can produce human-like requirement artifacts, from drafting user stories and use case scenarios to summarizing stakeholder feedback in grammar mistake-free artifacts. Recent studies have even demonstrated that ChatGPT-generated user stories can approach the quality of human-written ones under certain conditions
(
Ronanki et al. 2022
)
. Looking further ahead, AI tools are emerging that leverage such models to transform software requirement specifications into user stories or to interactively elicit needs via conversational agents. The concept of agentic AI, autonomous AI systems capable of performing specific
(
Mukherjee and Chang 2025
)
, goal-directed tasks without human intervention, foreshadows a direction in which some RE activities might be automated. It is conceivable that an AI agent could someday handle routine requirements elicitation by engaging with stakeholder inputs or draft requirements documents with minimal human intervention.

However, we surmise that the rise of generative AI should not replace the human-driven, participatory nature of RE, but rather enhance it. Within this quickly evolving technology landscape, REConnect provides a set of principles and actionable methods that empowers human users as co-designers of an AI-assisted RE process, allowing them an influence over the design of the process itself not just system output. This influence is core to REConnect’s third principle of
Empowering users as Agents of Change
. Progressing through the RECOnnect steps, the users are able to co-define AI goals, boundaries and evaluation criteria, so that AI output aligns with stakeholder values. Instead of managing LLMs as replacements for stakeholder engagement, REConnect challenges software organizations to leverage LLMs as supportive agents, embedded within a participatory approach that prioritizes stakeholder values and is rooted in trust, co-design, and stakeholder empowerment. Step 1. Human-Centered Groundwork: Relationship building and Stakeholder Mapping through Context Immersion

By enacting the REActions of Building Relationships and Trust with Stakeholders, practitioners first identify key stakeholder roles, community influencers, power dynamics, and cultural or political factors that shape the project environment and stakeholders’ values and motivations. They may create rich stakeholder maps or personas in collaboration with domain experts. An organization should consider not only formal roles, such as a hospital administrator, but also informal influencers, such as a community volunteer, a trusted leader, or a respected elder. Personas and contextual understanding are critical because, depending on the software project, the influencers could be decisively different. Crucially, this stage involves in-person presence and participation in the stakeholders’ environment, observing daily routines, and engaging in open-ended conversations. Through these interactions, requirement practitioners can glean subtle contextual details. For example, important details could revolve around community norms and personal trauma related to community events, which would unlikely surface from automated or online-based analysis alone.

This step remains in the human domain because trust and cultural understanding are inherently social and experiential. Based on current technology, AI cannot replace the experience of a requirements practitioner drinking tea around a village fire to hear concerns, or a team shadowing homeless shelter staff through a busy afternoon shift to feel their stress. In this step, humans articulate realities of their lived experiences, and remain
meaning-makers
.
They decide what matters in their community or organization. The result of this step is a richer understanding of ’who to listen to’ (formal and informal stakeholder roles and influencers) and ’why’ (their values, motivations) in the unique context of the project. Similar to how guardrails provide DevOps engineers with predefined policies and checks to improve safety and security
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
, the insights from this step allow the humans to
define ethical guardrails
(e.g., fairness, cultural alignment, inclusiveness). In an elevated role as
guardians of values and ethics
, stakeholders can later use these guardrails during requirements definition or co-design activities by ensuring requirements and design reflect stakeholders’ moral and social priorities. Furthermore, the relationships developed through this initial groundwork lead to key stakeholder buy-in in and engagement in later co-design stages in REConnect.

Step 2. AI-Assisted Knowledge Discovery of Stakeholder Input

After developing an understanding of the stakeholder lived and organizational ecosystem, the REConnect process can leverage generative AI to improve the efficiency of the discovery of requirements-relevant knowledge. In practice, this step might involve aggregating and analyzing large volumes of unstructured stakeholder input that could be overwhelming for manual analysis, for example, thousands of user reviews, support tickets, social media discussions, or transcripts from stakeholder workshops. LLM models can be used to sift through this data and identify patterns, emerging concerns, and latent needs that stakeholders have expressed.

For example, a generative model can summarize user feedback, automatically highlight frequent grievances, or suggest an underlying theme. Unlike earlier topic modeling approaches that might yield top words associated with a topic, an LLM-based analysis can produce human-readable summaries and draft user stories. The model could also perform role-based analysis, filtering insights by stakeholder segment.

Moreover, this AI-assisted knowledge discovery is not limited to online feedback insights. It can be leveraged to handle stakeholder input from other avenues such as interviews, workshops, and surveys. After automated transcription of stakeholder input, organizations can use generative models to generate insights from the data. This AI-assisted knowledge discovery aligns with CrowdRE’s goals of listening to the crowd at scale, but with more semantic consideration. We envision tools where an RE team can prompt an LLM to focus on distinct concerns from various user groups. If properly prompted, the model might reveal both the loud, common themes and some quieter themes.

It is important to highlight that the AI’s role in this step is supportive and exploratory. The goal is not to have the AI make final decisions about requirements, but to enhance RE practitioner awareness. By automating the synthesis of large datasets, requirements practitioners can focus more on the interpretation of the data collected from stakeholders.

Step 3. Human Interpretation and sense-making in Context

After the AI-assisted mining step, the next step involves
interpreting the AI outputs with contextual knowledge
gained in Step 1 (Relationship building) and in light of domain expertise and lived experience.
In collaborative workshops, stakeholders and requirements practitioners review the emerging needs and insights.
Humans retain interpretive authority as they can challenge, refine, or contextualize AI-generated artifacts. Stakeholders become
inclusion amplifiers
, ensuring diverse voices are integrated, by
identifying missing perspectives and guiding targeted AI-assisted mining
. For example, a workshop might present an AI-created persona or scenario to the group and ask. “What might we be missing?" Practitioners would like to induce stakeholders to correct any misinterpretations or fill contextual gaps. For instance, an AI summarization may fail to capture a community’s historical mistrust of certain institutions, or that a trusted community leader could be more important for delivering notifications than an impersonal app notification; then, a stakeholder can flag these omissions as critical. Such details would not become an explicit design consideration without the stakeholders’ knowledge of the underlying context. This human-centric synthesis step acts as a crucial quality control between AI’s interpretation and the formulation of requirements. While AI’s role can be considered as input to the brainstorming table, humans retain their role as decision-makers rather than passive recipients.

Step 4. Aligning Stakeholder Motivations to Solution Capabilities

A critical aspect of REConnect is the emphasis on empowering stakeholders as agents of change, which entails aligning the software’s design with the intrinsic motivations of the people who will use it. After co-designing the preliminary requirements, the next step is to map stakeholder motivations to envisioned system capabilities. This is a creative activity collaboratively conducted by requirements practitioners and stakeholders together, drawing on the trust and understanding developed earlier. Each influential stakeholder or group (identified back in Step 1) is considered. Key questions that a requirements practitioner should answer include: What drives the stakeholder? How could the system enable them, address their concerns, or unlock new possibilities to solve their problems?

For example, in the BloodSync case, we recognized that ward leaders were motivated by community duty and a desire to prove themselves as champions of public welfare. Correspondingly, a software feature was designed to allow ward leaders to coordinate local blood donors. This feature harnessed their motivation for leadership into a feature of the system. This mapping process requires empathy and often a negotiation of value trade-offs.

In this step, AI plays a little direct role because, as of this writing, it cannot determine stakeholders’ motivations rooted in cultural, political, or socio-economic contexts. The output of this step is a list of stakeholders and how they might contribute to solution co-design activities because of their motivation to changes in the problem domain; most importantly are those who are identified as informal influencers in their organization or community. People are more likely to champion a system if they see their intentions and values integrated into its software capabilities.

Step 5. Co-Designing with AI: Iterative Prototyping

With a validated set of requirements and understanding of stakeholder values and motivations, REConnect engages the humans in co-design activities, which can be enhanced by AI-generated prototypes. This step extends our earlier REActions of “Iterative Prototyping and Simulation" to rapidly produce design artifacts, and involve stakeholders as
co-reflectors in iterative review and refinement of solution designs
.

Using the requirements and context as input, a generative AI tool could generate initial prototypes of the solution. For a user interface, this might involve creating wireframe ideas or even visual mock-ups by leveraging generative visual models or UI-specific generation tools. Additionally, a generative AI model may draft workflow diagrams or storyboards of user interactions. For textual artifacts, an LLM could generate sample user stories, use case descriptions, or even pseudo-code demonstrating how a requirement might be implemented.

One advantage of generative AI in this step is speed. Since new prototypes or variants can be generated in minutes, the practitioners can iterate through feedback loops much faster. In a workshop, a group of stakeholders might reject an AI-generated interface as too complex; the facilitator can immediately adjust the prompt for the relevant generative AI model and produce a revised version on the spot for the group to consider. Generative AI tools may potentially allow stakeholders to directly shape the evolving design without needing specialized skills in drawing or coding.

6.
Conclusion

REConnect seeks to revitalize requirements engineering from a technical practice into a profoundly human-centred one.
It re-centres the process on trust, co-design, and empowerment, through a fundamentally relational engagement with users and stakeholders.
This approach enables the understanding and careful analysis of their lived experiences, values, motivations, and aspirations, while situating these insights within the broader cultural, socio-economic context, and considers the potential impacts of technology.
Yet as AI increasingly automates elements of requirements engineering, we arrive at a pivotal crossroad.
The challenge will no longer be about efficiency, but about shaping the nature of human-AI collaboration so that they amplify human agency and protect the essential role of humans in upholding human and societal values.
If participatory approaches are to safeguard human agency in this evolving collaboration, drawing on cultures with relational worldviews
(
Lewis et al. 2018
)
offers a way to ensure requirements engineering redefines technology design around trust, reciprocity, and empowerment.
