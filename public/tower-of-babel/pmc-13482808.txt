Endovenous thermal ablation combined with foam sclerotherapy is a safe and effective method for the treatment of lower extremity varicose veins: a multicenter retrospective analysis
Authors: Huanyu Chen, Wei Qiu, Wuhao Li, Bangjian Zhou, Xingyu Ge, Hong Zhang, Kewu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To compare the safety, efficacy, and perioperative outcomes of radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and endovenous microwave ablation (EMA), each combined with foam sclerotherapy, versus traditional high ligation and stripping (HL/S) for symptomatic lower extremity varicose veins. The primary endpoint was the change in Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) from baseline to 12 months postoperatively. This multicenter retrospective study analyzed 305 patients from two hospitals, divided into three groups: RFA + sclerotherapy (n = 102), EMA + sclerotherapy (n = 101), and HL/S (n = 102). Perioperative outcomes (operation time, blood loss, hospital stay), postoperative pain (Visual Analog Scale, VAS), inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6), dorsal foot venous pressure, coagulation parameters, complication rates, Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS), and Chronic Venous Insufficiency Quality of Life Questionnaire (CIVIQ-20) scores were assessed and compared up to 12 months postoperatively. Both minimally invasive groups demonstrated superior perioperative outcomes compared to HL/S, including shorter operation times, less blood loss, and reduced hospital stays. Postoperative pain scores were significantly lower in the RFA and EMA groups, with RFA showing the lowest early pain levels. Inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) and coagulation activation (D-dimer, fibrinogen) were significantly lower in the ablation groups, with RFA values being the lowest. Complication rates were lower in the RFA (34.3%) and EMA (41.6%) groups versus HL/S (58.8%). However, no significant differences were observed among the three groups in long-term VCSS, CIVIQ-20 scores, or recurrence rates at 12 months. RFA and EMA combined with sclerotherapy are safe and effective minimally invasive alternatives to HL/S, offering advantages in faster recovery, less pain, reduced inflammatory response, and fewer complications. While perioperative benefits favor thermal ablation, all three techniques provide comparable long-term symptomatic relief and quality-of-life improvement.

Introduction

Lower extremity varicose veins (VVs) are a common vascular surgical disease characterized by incompetence of the superficial venous system, venous wall dilation, and tortuosity. Clinical manifestations include heaviness, pain, edema, skin pigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis, and even venous ulceration, significantly impairing patients’ physical and mental health and quality of life1. Global epidemiological studies indicate a high prevalence of chronic venous disease (CVD) in adults, with a substantial proportion exhibiting visible varicose veins (CEAP class C2 or above), and the prevalence increases with age, typically higher in women than in men2. In China, with the aging population and lifestyle changes, the disease burden of VVs is increasingly heavy, creating an urgent clinical need for efficient, safe, and rapid-recovery treatment options.

Traditional high ligation and stripping (HL/S) has long been regarded as the “gold standard” for treating VVs, with proven long-term efficacy. However, this procedure is highly invasive, requiring multiple incisions in the groin and lower limb, leading to considerable intraoperative bleeding, significant postoperative pain, prolonged hospitalization, and risks of complications such as nerve injury, hematoma, and wound infection3,4. The recovery time for patients to return to normal work and life is also significantly delayed. In recent years, the rapid development of endovenous thermal ablation techniques has revolutionized the management of VVs. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and endovenous microwave ablation (EMA), as representative minimally invasive treatments, have been widely recommended in national and international vascular surgery guidelines as first-line options for treating truncal vein reflux5,6.

Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively compare the short-term efficacy and safety of the aforementioned three procedures through a retrospective analysis of 305 VVs patients from Hefei First People’s Hospital and the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. While previous studies have largely confirmed the non-inferiority of thermal ablation to HL/S in terms of vein closure and symptom relief, this multicenter Chinese cohort study provides a more integrated evaluation by simultaneously assessing perioperative inflammatory stress markers (CRP, IL-6), coagulation activation parameters (D-dimer, FIB), and dorsal foot venous hemodynamic changes across the three modalities7–8. These data provide a more comprehensive assessment of the values for varicose veins of the lower extremities, and offer more substantial and targeted evidence-based medical support for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei First People’s Hospital (Approval No: 2026-010-01) and the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval No: KYXM-202601-013). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Hefei First People’s Hospital and the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University.

As this was a retrospective observational study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. All eligible consecutive patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period were included. The primary outcome measure was the Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) change from baseline to 12 months postoperatively. Secondary outcome measures included perioperative parameters (operation time, blood loss, hospital stay), postoperative pain scores, inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6), coagulation parameters, complication rates, CIVIQ-20 scores, and target vein occlusion rates at 12 months.

Patient selection

Consecutive patients with symptomatic lower extremity VVs who underwent surgical treatment in the Interventional Vascular Surgery departments of the two hospitals between January 2021 and January 2024 were included. Inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 80 years; primary great saphenous vein (GSV) reflux confirmed by color Doppler ultrasound (reflux time ≥ 0.5 s); Clinical-Etiological-Anatomical-Pathophysiological (CEAP) clinical class C2 to C6; treatment with one of the three procedures: RFA combined with foam sclerotherapy, EMA combined with foam sclerotherapy, or traditional HL/S; and complete clinical and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria included: presence of lower extremity deep vein thrombosis or obstructive disease; history of ipsilateral lower limb venous surgery or sclerotherapy; severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal insufficiency or active malignancy; pregnancy or lactation; and active infection in the surgical area or congenital immune system disease. Finally, 305 patients meeting the criteria were included and divided into three groups: RFA + sclerotherapy group (n = 102), EMA + sclerotherapy group (n = 101), and HL/S group (n = 102).

Surgical methods

All surgical operations were performed by attending physicians or above with rich experience in interventional vascular surgery. Patients were treated with RFA, EMA, or HL/S according to clinical assessment and patient preference after discussion with the treating vascular surgeon.

RFA combined with sclerosing agent group: RFA combined with sclerosing agent group: The patient was placed in the supine position, and the affected limb was disinfected and draped. Under ultrasound guidance, the great saphenous vein or small saphenous vein was punctured at the level below the knee or above the ankle. After successfully introducing a 0.018-inch guide wire, a 6 F vascular sheath was inserted. Subsequently, a radiofrequency ablation catheter (Hengrui) was introduced through the sheath, and its tip was precisely positioned 2.0–2.5 cm below the saphenofemoral junction or saphenopopliteal junction under real-time ultrasound monitoring. This safety distance was consistently maintained to minimize the risk of thermal-induced deep vein thrombosis. Then, a large amount of tumescent anesthetic solution was infused into the subcutaneous tissue around the target vein to compress the vein, isolate and protect the surrounding tissues, and achieve analgesia. After confirming satisfactory anesthesia and correct catheter position, the radiofrequency generator was activated, and the ablation and closure were performed segment by segment until the entire target vein was ablated and closed. The proximal segment of the treated vein received a single ablation cycle per manufacturer recommendations. After the treatment of the main trunk vein, the residual varicose vein clusters below the knee were punctured and injected with foam sclerosing agent under ultrasound guidance. Residual varicose tributaries were treated with ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy using 1% polidocanol prepared by the Tessari method (liquid-to-air ratio 1:4), with a maximum total foam volume of 15 mL. The foam volume was determined according to the diameter, number, and distribution of residual varicose tributaries under ultrasound guidance. No patient required additional sclerotherapy sessions during the 12-month follow-up period.

EMA combined with sclerosing agent group: EMA combined with sclerosing agent group: The patient‘s position and preoperative preparation were the same as those in the RFA group. After successful puncture, a vascular sheath was inserted, and then an ECO-100 F microwave ablation catheter (Nanjing Yigao Microwave System Engineering Co., Ltd., China) was sent to the target position. The catheter tip was positioned 2.0–2.5 cm distal to the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction to maintain a safety margin comparable to that used in the RFA group. On the basis of sufficient infusion of the same formula of tumescent anesthetic solution, the microwave emission power was set at 50 watts, and the single emission time was 6–8 s. The target vein was ablated segment by segment at an interval of 1.0 cm per retraction. The sclerosing agent treatment method is the same as that of the RFA group.

HL/S group: The patients in this group underwent standard high-ligation and stripping of the great saphenous vein. An oblique incision was made in the groin area, and the main trunk of the great saphenous vein and its main tributaries were dissected and ligated. Subsequently, a vein stripper was inserted through an incision at the ankle or below the knee, and the main trunk of the great saphenous vein was stripped in a retrograde or antegrade manner. For the obvious varicose vein clusters in the calf, a varicose vein stripping procedure was performed through punctiform incisions.

Postoperative management

All patients received standardized postoperative care following institutional protocols. Elastic compression stockings (23–32 mmHg, thigh-length) were applied immediately after the procedure and patients were instructed to wear them continuously for the first 48 h, followed by daytime wear only for a total duration of 4 weeks. Early ambulation was encouraged within 2–4 h post-procedure for all three groups, though patients in the HL/S group had a relatively longer bed rest period due to general or regional anaesthesia recovery requirements. Postoperative analgesia was provided using oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (ibuprofen 400 mg every 6–8 h as needed) in all groups, with no routine use of opioid analgesics. Routine pharmacological thromboprophylaxis was not administered unless patients had additional risk factors for venous thromboembolism; in such cases, low-molecular-weight heparin was used for 3–5 days postoperatively according to institutional guidelines.

Observation indicators

The observation indicators of the study cover multiple dimensions to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety. Peri-operative indicators include the operation time (the time elapsed from the start of anesthesia to the completion of lower - limb compression dressing), the intraoperative blood loss (estimated by the gauze weighing method and the volume of fluid in the suction bottle), and the number of postoperative hospital days. The visual analog scale (VAS, with 0 representing no pain and 10 representing severe pain) was used to systematically evaluate the pain degree of patients at rest 1 day before surgery and 1, 3, and 7 days after surgery. Regarding laboratory inflammation indicators, fasting cubital venous blood was collected from patients early in the morning 1 day before surgery and on the 7th day after surgery to detect the levels of serum C - reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin—6 (IL-6). Meanwhile, a digital venous pressure monitor (Mindray CVP-2000) was used to measure the dorsal venous pressure of the affected limb at the same time points. The internationally recognized Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) and the Short-form Chronic Venous Insufficiency Quality of Life Questionnaire (CIVIQ-20) were used to evaluate the disease severity and the improvement of quality of life before surgery and 1, 6, and 12 months after surgery respectively. To minimize assessment bias, VCSS and CIVIQ-20 evaluations were performed by independent investigators who were not involved in the surgical procedures. The assessors were blinded to the specific treatment modality received by each patient during the follow-up assessment. All kinds of complications that occurred within 48 h after surgery and within 1 month after surgery were closely recorded, including incision bleeding, local swelling, incision infection, ecchymosis, paresthesia, induration, superficial thrombophlebitis, skin burns, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), etc. At 12 months after surgery, color Doppler ultrasound was used to evaluate the recanalization of the great saphenous vein or newly - developed hemodynamically significant reflux, and the recurrence rate was calculated accordingly.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 26.0). The measurement data conforming to the normal distribution were expressed as Mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison between groups. If the difference was statistically significant, LSD-t test was further used for pairwise comparison. The measurement data with non-normal distribution were expressed as the median (interquartile range) [M(IQR)], and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for comparison between groups. Counting data were expressed as the number of cases (percentage) [n(%)], and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact probability method. All statistical tests were two-sided tests. A difference was considered statistically significant when P < 0.05, *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01 ***P < 0.001. Data visualization plotting is carried out using Origin 2018. The patient, rather than the treated limb, was considered the statistical unit for all analyses. Patients undergoing bilateral procedures were included only once in the statistical analysis.

Comparison of perioperative indicators

In terms of perioperative indicators reflecting surgical trauma and recovery efficiency, both minimally invasive ablation techniques showed significant advantages. The operation time of the RFA combined with sclerosing agent group was the shortest, which was 65 ± 12 min. The EMA combined with sclerosing agent group came second, with an operation time of 75 ± 15 min. The traditional HL/S group had the longest operation time, reaching 95 ± 20 min. The differences among the three groups were statistically significant. It is worth noting that the operation time of the RFA group was slightly shorter than that of the EMA group. This may be because the ablation heating element of the RFA catheter is longer than that of the EMA catheter, resulting in a shorter operation time in the RFA group than in the EMA group (Fig. 1A). In terms of intraoperative blood loss, both the RFA group and the EMA group controlled it at a very low level (10 ± 5 mL vs. 12 ± 6 mL), which was significantly less than that of the HL/S group 45 ± 15 mL (Fig. 1B). In terms of hospital stay, the minimally invasive groups also showed the characteristics of rapid recovery. The hospital stay days of the RFA group and the EMA group were 2.0 ± 0.5 days and 2.5 ± 0.8 days respectively, which were significantly shorter than that of the HL/S group (5.0 ± 1.2 days) (Fig. 1C).

Comparison of postoperative pain scores

Postoperative pain is an important factor affecting patients’ experience and early mobilization. There was no difference in the baseline level of VAS scores among the three groups of patients before surgery. At each time point after surgery, the analgesic effects of the two minimally invasive surgical procedures were significantly better than those of the traditional surgery. On the first day after surgery, the VAS scores of the RFA group and the EMA group were significantly lower than those of the HL/S group. In addition, on the first and third days after surgery, the VAS scores of the RFA group were lower than those of the EMA group, indicating that RFA may have more advantages in early postoperative pain control (Fig. 2).

Comparison of postoperative inflammatory indicators and dorsal foot venous pressure

To objectively evaluate the stress response caused by surgical trauma and the improvement of hemodynamics, we measured the inflammatory indicators and dorsal foot venous pressure. On the 7th day after surgery, the levels of CRP and IL-6, which reflect the level of acute inflammation, were significantly lower in the two minimally invasive surgical groups than in the HL/S group. Further pairwise comparisons showed that the levels of CRP and IL-6 in the RFA group were also significantly lower than those in the EMA group, suggesting that RFA was associated with lower postoperative inflammatory marker levels in this cohort. However, because of the retrospective design and the different anaesthetic modalities used, a causal relationship between the surgical technique and systemic inflammatory response cannot be established (Fig. 3A,B). In terms of hemodynamics, on the 7th day after surgery, the dorsal foot venous pressure in the RFA group and the EMA group decreased significantly compared with that before surgery, and the decrease was significantly greater than that in the HL/S group (Fig. 3C).

Comparison of complications

Analysis of postoperative complications demonstrated a more favorable safety profile for the minimally invasive procedures than for HL/S. The proportions of patients experiencing at least one postoperative complication were 34.3% (35/102), 41.6% (42/101), and 58.8% (60/102) in the RFA, EMA, and HL/S groups, respectively. These overall complication rates represent the number of patients with one or more postoperative complications rather than the sum of individual complication events. Because some patients experienced multiple complications, the frequencies of individual complications are not mutually exclusive. Specifically, the incidences of ecchymosis, paresthesia, and surgical-site infection were significantly lower in both minimally invasive groups than in the HL/S group, whereas no significant differences were observed in superficial phlebitis, pigmentation, or deep vein thrombosis. No cases of pulmonary embolism occurred in any group. Detailed complication data are summarized in Table 2.

Comparison of postoperative VCSS and CIVIQ -20 scores

At 12 months postoperatively, duplex ultrasound examination was performed to evaluate target vein occlusion status and recurrence. The occlusion rate of the treated great saphenous vein was 96.1% (98/102) in the RFA group, 95.0% (96/101) in the EMA group, and 96.1% (98/102) in the HL/S group, with no statistically significant difference among the three groups (P = 0.923). Recanalization was observed in 4 patients in the RFA group, 5 in the EMA group, and 4 in the HL/S group. There were no cases of pulmonary embolism or symptomatic deep vein thrombosis during follow-up. No reinterventions were required during the 12-month follow-up period in any group. Discussion

Lower extremity varicose veins are a major manifestation of chronic venous insufficiency. The development of its treatment concept has undergone an important transformation from simply pursuing anatomical venous closure to emphasizing functional restoration and improvement of quality of life. With the rapid development of minimally invasive techniques, endovascular thermal ablation has gradually replaced traditional open surgery and become the preferred method for treating symptomatic varicose veins9,10. Against this background, radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (EMA), as two main endovascular thermal ablation techniques, have attracted much attention due to their characteristics of minimal trauma and rapid recovery. However, systematic comparative studies on the peri-operative stress response, inflammation level, coagulation function, and hemodynamic improvement between these two minimally invasive techniques and the classic high ligation and stripping (HL/S) are still relatively limited11,12. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the characteristics of the comprehensive impact of different surgical procedures on the body is of great significance for guiding the selection of individualized treatment plans in clinical practice.

This multicenter retrospective study systematically compared the efficacy and safety of RFA combined with foam sclerotherapy, EMA combined with foam sclerotherapy, and conventional HL/S in 305 patients with lower extremity varicose veins. Importantly, the primary endpoint of this study was the change in VCSS at 12 months after treatment. Although all three treatment modalities resulted in significant improvements in VCSS and CIVIQ-20 scores compared with baseline, no significant differences were observed among the three groups at any follow-up time point, indicating that they achieved comparable medium-term clinical efficacy in symptom relief and quality-of-life improvement. These findings suggest that all three treatment strategies are effective for the management of symptomatic varicose veins. Therefore, although no superiority was demonstrated for the primary endpoint, the observed perioperative advantages may still be clinically meaningful when treatment selection is individualized13.

In this study, the HL/S group was at a disadvantage in terms of operation time, blood loss, and length of hospital stay, which directly reflects the characteristics of open surgery, such as large trauma and slow recovery. In contrast, RFA and EMA avoid long incisions in the groin area and multiple punctate incisions, and achieve closure of the main trunk vein through percutaneous puncture, greatly reducing tissue damage. It is worth noting that the operation time of the RFA group was slightly shorter than that of the EMA group, which may be related to the device design and operation procedures of the two techniques. The RFA catheter can treat a 7-centimeter vein segment at one time, while the withdrawal interval of the EMA catheter used in this study is 1 centimeter, theoretically requiring more operation cycles. However, the more crucial advantage lies in postoperative recovery. The lower postoperative VAS scores observed in the minimally invasive groups may have contributed to earlier ambulation and shorter hospitalization. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because differences in perioperative anaesthetic management may also have influenced postoperative pain perception and discharge timing14. In addition, the relatively high incision infection rate (7.8%) in the HL/S group also highlights the advantage of minimally invasive techniques in infection control without destroying the integrity of the skin.

Although both RFA and EMA are thermal ablation techniques and there were no significant differences in the main endpoints (vein closure rate, long-term symptom improvement, recurrence rate) of this study, the subtle differences observed in some secondary endpoints are worthy of in-depth discussion. This study found that in the early postoperative period (1–3 days), the VAS pain scores of the RFA group were significantly lower than those of the EMA group, and the levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) on the 7th day after surgery were also significantly lower. This phenomenon may be due to the different thermal action mechanisms of the two techniques. RFA mainly relies on the resistive heating effect, and its thermal energy is concentrated in a very limited area of tissue around the catheter electrode. It mainly heats and shrinks the vein wall evenly through heat conduction. Modern RFA devices are integrated with precise temperature feedback and impedance control, which can stably maintain the operating temperature at 120 °C, avoiding tissue carbonization and steam bubble formation caused by overheating, and thus may cause less irritation to the nerve endings around the blood vessels15. In contrast, EMA generates heat through the friction of water molecules in the tissue by microwave radiation. Its active heating range is relatively wider, with stronger thermal penetration and is not easy to be precisely controlled at one point16. This stronger thermal field may cause more extensive irritation or damage to the adventitia of blood vessels and surrounding tissues (including the accompanying cutaneous nerves), resulting in a slightly more severe early postoperative pain reaction and systemic inflammatory reaction than RFA. However, it is important to note that most of these differences are transient and self-limiting and do not affect the long-term symptom relief and quality-of-life scores of the two groups. The detection results of coagulation indicators were highly consistent with the changes in inflammatory markers, further confirming that there are significant differences in the impact of different surgical methods on the body’s stress response. As important indicators reflecting the activation of the coagulation system, the postoperative elevation of FIB and D-D directly reflects the degree of coagulation dysfunction caused by surgical trauma.

In addition, dorsal foot venous pressure is an important indicator reflecting the surgical effect. In terms of hemodynamic improvement, all three surgical procedures can effectively reduce the dorsal foot venous pressure. However, the two minimally invasive procedures show a better improvement amplitude in the early postoperative period than the traditional surgery. This may be related to the fact that minimally invasive techniques can more thoroughly close the reflux source of the main trunk without affecting the function of the deep fascia pump.

In terms of complications, the overall safety of the minimally invasive group is good. The relatively high rate of pigmentation in the EMA group may be related to the thermal penetration characteristics of microwave, which leads to the extravasation of red blood cells into the subcutaneous tissue. However, most of them can fade over time. The incidence of serious complications such as deep vein thrombosis in the three groups is extremely low and there is no difference, which confirms the safety of minimally invasive techniques. This strongly suggests that for patients with indications, choosing any modern minimally invasive technique can achieve long-term efficacy no less than that of the traditional gold-standard surgery, while enjoying many peri-operative benefits brought by minimally invasive procedures.

Notably, the HL/S procedure in this study was performed under general or regional anaesthesia, whereas both minimally invasive procedures were routinely performed under tumescent local anaesthesia. This inherent difference in anaesthetic modality should be considered an important potential confounding factor when interpreting the perioperative findings. Specifically, postoperative pain scores may have been influenced not only by the extent of surgical trauma but also by differences in perioperative anaesthetic and analgesic management. Likewise, the higher postoperative CRP, IL-6, D-dimer, and fibrinogen levels observed in the HL/S group may partly reflect the greater systemic stress response associated with general or regional anaesthesia in combination with more invasive surgery. The longer postoperative hospital stay in the HL/S group may also have been influenced by the recovery requirements following general or regional anaesthesia, rather than the surgical procedure alone. Therefore, the perioperative advantages observed for RFA and EMA should not be attributed solely to the endovenous techniques themselves. Future prospective studies using standardized anaesthetic protocols or appropriate statistical adjustment for anaesthetic modality are warranted to better distinguish the independent contribution of surgical technique from that of perioperative anaesthesia. Nevertheless, the consistently lower complication rates together with the favorable perioperative recovery observed in the minimally invasive groups suggest that these techniques may still provide additional clinical benefits beyond differences in anaesthetic management.

Limitations and prospects of this study

This study also has some limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design may have selection bias that cannot be completely avoided, particularly as treatment allocation was based on shared decision-making rather than randomization. Propensity score matching or multivariable adjustment for potential confounders was not performed, which may limit the validity of intergroup comparisons. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings. Secondly, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months, which may not fully reflect late recurrence or long-term durability after treatment. Thirdly, a detailed cost-benefit analysis has not been carried out. Future studies can include this to provide a more comprehensive basis for decision-making. In addition, because different anaesthetic strategies were routinely used for HL/S and endovenous procedures, residual confounding related to perioperative anaesthesia cannot be completely excluded despite comparable baseline characteristics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RFA and EMA combined with foam sclerotherapy, as well as HL/S, achieved comparable improvements in clinical symptoms and quality of life at the 12-month follow-up. Compared with HL/S, RFA and EMA demonstrated advantages in perioperative recovery, including reduced postoperative pain, lower inflammatory response, and shorter hospital stay. However, due to the retrospective design and limited follow-up duration, further studies with longer follow-up are required to confirm the long-term durability of these treatment strategies. Treatment selection should be individualized according to patients’ clinical characteristics, preferences, and economic considerations.
