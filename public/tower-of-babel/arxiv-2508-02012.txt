The Financial Connectome: A Brain-Inspired Framework for Modeling Latent Market Dynamics
Yuda Bi, Vince D. Calhoun

Abstract
We propose the Financial Connectome —a new scientific discipline that models financial markets through the lens of brain functional architecture. Inspired by the foundational work of group independent component analysis (groupICA) in neuroscience, we reimagine markets not as collections of assets, but as high-dimensional dynamic systems composed of latent market modules . Treating stocks as functional nodes and their co-fluctuations as expressions of collective cognition, we introduce dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC) : the financial analogue of dynamic functional connectivity (dFNC). This biologically-inspired framework reveals structurally persistent market subnetworks, captures phase transitions, and uncovers systemic early-warning signals—all without reliance on predictive labels. Our results suggest that markets, like brains, possess modular, self-organizing, and temporally evolving architectures. This work inaugurates the field of financial connectomics —a principled synthesis of systems neuroscience and quantitative finance, aimed at uncovering the hidden logic of complex economies.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation: Towards a Functional Connectomics of Financial Markets

Financial markets, like the brain, are complex adaptive systems: they exhibit non-linear interactions among heterogeneous agents, and their global states—such as crashes, bubbles, or periods of calm—emerge from distributed dynamics rather than individual behaviors
Bouchaud 2013
;
Farmer and Foley 2009
. Traditional econometric models, often grounded in equilibrium assumptions and representative agents
Fama 1970
;
Kahneman 2011
, struggle to capture such emergent phenomena, particularly during structural transitions or crises.

In contrast, network neuroscience has pioneered methods to model distributed brain activity through
functional connectivity
analysis of fMRI signals
Bassett and Sporns 2017
;
Bullmore and Sporns 2009
. A key insight is that cognitive states arise not from isolated neural units, but from temporally coordinated patterns among brain regions
Friston 1994
. These patterns are often extracted using blind source separation techniques, such as independent component analysis (ICA), and further tracked through dynamic functional connectivity (dFNC)
Calhoun et al. 2014
.

We propose a conceptual bridge:
stocks can be modeled as functional units analogous to brain regions
, where their co-movements reflect latent market states—collective phenomena such as fear, greed, or liquidity shifts. Like fMRI signals, stock time series are high-dimensional mixtures of overlapping influences. Just as ICA reveals underlying neural sources, we posit that
groupICA
can uncover interpretable, systemic components from financial time series.

This analogy is supported by shared organizing principles:

•

Systemicity
: Global market behaviors emerge from the interaction of many correlated yet non-identical agents or assets.

•

Modularity
: Subsets of assets (e.g., sectors) show strong intra-module coupling, echoing neural subnetworks like the DMN or VIS
Mantegna 1999
.

•

Self-organization
: Both brains and markets generate coherent macroscopic patterns without centralized control.

•

Temporal dynamics
: Regime shifts, metastability, and critical transitions arise in both systems in response to endogenous and exogenous perturbations
Helbing 2012
.

Despite these parallels, financial modeling rarely employs advanced tools from network neuroscience. Techniques such as groupICA, sliding-window correlation, and latent temporal clustering remain underutilized in finance. To bridge this gap, we propose the
Financial Connectome
: a new modeling framework that adapts groupICA and associated tools to reveal latent dynamic structure in financial markets. This approach enables the identification of stable market modules, the characterization of structural phase shifts, and potentially new early-warning signals of systemic instability.

To address this gap, we introduce the concept of the
Financial Connectome
: a modeling framework that adapts tools from network neuroscience to uncover latent structure and dynamics in financial markets. By treating stocks as functional nodes and capturing their co-evolution using techniques such as
groupICA

Calhoun et al. 2001
,
sliding-window correlation
, and
contrastive representation learning
, this approach enables the identification of market modules, tracking of systemic phase transitions, and potentially new indicators of systemic risk. This framework lays the foundation for a new interdisciplinary field of
financial connectomics
, which we elaborate on in the following sections.

1.2
Overview of Brain Connectomics

The field of brain connectomics seeks to model the brain as a complex network of interacting regions, where nodes represent functional units and edges quantify statistical or causal interactions between them
Sporns et al. 2005
;
Bassett and Sporns 2017
. A central modality for studying such interactions is functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which measures BOLD signals at high spatial resolution over time
Ogawa et al. 1990
. The resulting data form a four-dimensional array—3D brain volumes sampled across successive time points—yielding a dynamic map of whole-brain activity
Smith 2004
;
Logothetis 2008
. Modeling the brain as a complex network allows researchers to move beyond localized activation patterns and instead study the principles of global coordination, modular organization, and integration–segregation dynamics that underlie cognition and behavior
Telesford et al. 2011
.

To reduce dimensionality and enhance interpretability, the brain is often parcellated into discrete
functional regions of interest (ROIs)
. These functional parcellations are typically data-driven, defined by clustering of spontaneous or task-related BOLD activity to identify functionally homogeneous units
Sporns 2013
. Examples include the Schaefer atlas
Schaefer et al. 2018
and Yeo networks
Yeo et al. 2011
, which partition the brain based on intrinsic connectivity profiles. In many studies, functional parcels are not predefined anatomically but are instead derived using
data-driven decomposition methods
, such as spatial independent component analysis (ICA), which extracts spatially independent and functionally coherent networks from fMRI data. ICA is particularly well-suited to fMRI because it does not require prior assumptions about signal timing or spatial structure, making it ideal for discovering intrinsic networks from resting-state data
Jafri et al. 2008
;
Calhoun and Adali 2012
;
Erhardt et al. 2011
.

Importantly,
functional networks do not necessarily align with anatomical boundaries
. A single network may span multiple anatomical areas, and a given area may participate in multiple networks depending on the cognitive state. This dynamic, overlapping organization reflects the distributed nature of brain function. By averaging voxel-level BOLD signals within each functional unit, a multivariate time series is obtained for each subject, representing the activation dynamics of distinct brain networks.
Functional network connectivity (FNC)
refers to the statistical dependence between these network-level time series
Friston 1994
;
Smith et al. 2011
. The most common form is the
static FNC
, computed as a symmetric correlation matrix summarizing average pairwise connectivity over the entire scan. While this provides a global view of brain organization, it assumes stationarity and does not explicitly model transient states or temporal reconfigurations. To address this limitation, the concept of
dynamic functional network connectivity (dFNC)
has been introduced
Calhoun et al. 2014
;
Allen et al. 2014
. The simplest form of dFNC involves estimating connectivity within short, sliding time windows, revealing how network interactions evolve over time. This temporal lens has exposed state transitions, metastable dynamics, and time-varying modular configurations that are invisible to static models.

To extract meaningful latent structure from high-dimensional fMRI data,
independent component analysis (ICA)
is commonly used
McKeown et al. 1998
. In spatial ICA, the brain is decomposed into maximally independent spatial components and associated temporal signals. Each component typically reflects a functionally coherent network—such as the visual, sensorimotor, or default-mode network—while the associated time series encode their activation profiles. At the group level,
group ICA

Calhoun et al. 2001
;
Erhardt et al. 2011
—a pioneering framework introduced by Calhoun and colleagues—enables population-level decomposition by temporally concatenating individual datasets, reducing dimensionality via principal component analysis (PCA), and applying ICA to extract shared components. This methodology not only permits group-level statistical analysis, but also maintains individual specificity through subject-level back-reconstruction. The component time series extracted from group ICA are used to compute dFNC matrices, representing how inter-network interactions evolve across time
Rashid et al. 2014
. These matrices can be clustered into recurring connectivity states, embedded for representation learning, or linked to behavioral phenotypes and clinical traits.

Multimodal Fusion in Connectomics.

While fMRI provides rich temporal information about functional dynamics, it is often complemented by structural imaging modalities—such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) or structural MRI—to offer a more comprehensive picture of brain organization. These modalities capture different aspects of neural architecture: DTI maps white matter tracts to reveal physical connectivity, while structural MRI characterizes cortical thickness, volume, and morphology. To integrate these complementary views, researchers have developed
multimodal fusion frameworks
, such as joint ICA
Sui et al. 2011
and mCCA
Sui et al. 2013
, which jointly decompose multiple data types into shared latent components. These techniques aim to uncover consistent patterns across modalities that co-vary meaningfully across individuals or time
Sui et al. 2012
. For example, a fused component might reflect both increased gray matter density and heightened functional activation in a given network. Such fusion strategies have proven particularly useful in clinical neuroscience, where combining structural and functional signatures enhances sensitivity to disease phenotypes. The success of multimodal fusion in capturing shared latent representations motivates analogous approaches in financial domains, where integrating heterogeneous data—such as price, volume, options, sentiment, or macro indicators—may reveal deeper structures than single-modality models can offer.

In summary, brain connectomics provides a hierarchical modeling framework—from ICA-derived functional parcellations to dynamic connectivity matrices—that captures the latent architecture of large-scale brain function
Bassett and Sporns 2018
. The combination of fMRI, ICA, and dFNC offers a mature toolkit for modeling high-dimensional dynamic systems—one that we argue can be fruitfully adapted to financial markets. We explore these parallels in the next section through the lens of
financial connectomics
.

1.3
Methodological Translation: From Brain to Market

Having established the conceptual parallels between neural and financial systems, we now detail the methodological translation from brain connectomics to financial analysis. Table
1
summarizes this mapping, which forms the foundation of our proposed framework.

Step 1: Mapping Financial Assets to ROIs.

In neuroscience, brain regions of interest (ROIs) are the fundamental units for analyzing regional activity and connectivity. In our financial analogue, we map
exchange-traded funds (ETFs)
—rather than individual stocks—to ROIs. This abstraction is motivated by the fact that stock-level universes are highly heterogeneous, with inconsistent listing durations, varying liquidity, and fluctuating market capitalizations. ETFs, by contrast, represent aggregated exposures to sectors, regions, or asset classes, offering a more stable and interpretable basis for system-level modeling. Each ETF is associated with a multivariate time series comprising features like price, return, trading volume, or volatility, analogous to the BOLD signal of a brain region. These serve as the basic functional units for decomposition and connectivity analysis. Similar network-based modeling has been explored in econophysics
Mantegna 1999
;
Tumminello et al. 2010
, where financial instruments are treated as nodes in evolving correlation networks.

Step 2: Interpreting the Market as a Mixed Signal System.

Just as voxel-level fMRI signals are modeled as weighted mixtures of latent neural sources—rendering direct observation of individual components impossible—we posit that financial time series similarly reflect overlapping latent market forces (e.g., macro shocks, sector sentiment, liquidity flows) that drive observable co-movement patterns. This interpretation justifies the use of blind source separation techniques like ICA, which have been successful in isolating interpretable neural components from high-dimensional signals. The use of ICA in financial contexts has been explored sporadically
Oja et al. 2000
, but rarely in conjunction with dynamic network modeling.

Step 3: Extracting Latent Market Modules via Group ICA.

In brain imaging, group independent component analysis (group ICA) is a standard approach for identifying spatially independent yet functionally coherent networks shared across individuals
Calhoun et al. 2001
;
Erhardt et al. 2011
. We extend this methodology to financial markets by applying group ICA across rolling time windows of multivariate asset data. Each time window—spanning a fixed historical period—is treated as a
pseudo-subject
, analogous to an fMRI session in neuroscience. This formulation enables higher temporal granularity and accommodates non-stationarity in market dynamics, akin to temporal segmentation in event-related fMRI designs.

This setup allows ICA to extract a set of latent components that capture temporally recurring patterns of coordinated asset behavior. We refer to these components as
market modules
, which represent functionally specialized co-movement structures within the market. These modules may align with known sectoral groupings (e.g., technology, energy), thematic patterns such as environmental, social, and governance (ESG) stocks—which emphasize sustainable and ethical investment principles—or reveal emergent latent structures that elude predefined classification schemes.

The resulting components are spatially distributed across the asset space and temporally localized to each window, resembling canonical resting-state brain networks in their coherence and interpretability. This approach enables a data-driven parcellation of the market into dynamically interacting subsystems.

Step 4: Modeling Dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC).

To characterize the temporal evolution of interactions among market modules, we introduce
dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC)
—a financial analogue to dynamic functional connectivity in brain science
Allen et al. 2014
;
Calhoun et al. 2014
. Specifically, we track the activation time course of each market module (i.e., ICA component) across rolling windows and compute the pairwise correlation between their trajectories.

The resulting dMNC matrices reflect evolving patterns of co-fluctuation among latent market forces. For example, synchronized activation may correspond to periods of systemic coupling (e.g., market crashes), whereas decorrelated modules may indicate fragmentation (e.g., divergence between growth and value sectors or regional decoupling) or sectoral divergence. These time-varying networks can be clustered into discrete connectivity states, visualized to identify regime transitions, or analyzed to extract early-warning signals preceding structural breaks.

Step 5: Identifying Market Regimes and Structural Transitions.

The dMNC matrices can be summarized across time via unsupervised clustering to reveal recurring
market states
, analogous to brain connectivity states in cognitive neuroscience. By tracking the temporal sequence of these states, we can characterize the market’s trajectory through phases of stability, volatility, recovery, or collapse.

Additionally, by analyzing the patterns preceding known events—such as the 2020 COVID-19 crash or the 2022 inflation-driven bear market—we can identify characteristic pre-transition signatures (e.g., rising inter-module synchronization, component reorganization) that may serve as early indicators of systemic stress. This enables a purely structural, data-driven lens for monitoring market health
without requiring any predictive labels
.

Summary.

Taken together, this methodological pipeline—from asset-level time series and group ICA decomposition to dynamic network modeling—mirrors the computational architecture of modern brain connectomics. Crucially, our approach does not rely on black-box forecasting or deep learning models, but instead derives interpretable, modular, and dynamic representations of market structure from the data itself.

The resulting “financial connectome” offers a principled and flexible framework for uncovering latent market modules, detecting regime shifts, and extracting structural indicators of systemic transitions. We envision this approach as a foundational tool for financial researchers, quantitative analysts, and policy makers interested in the complex dynamical behavior of financial systems.

1.4
How Financial Connectomics Differs from Prior Approaches

While prior research has explored various modeling frameworks for financial systems—including statistical, graph-theoretic, and signal-processing approaches—our proposed
Financial Connectome
framework introduces a unique methodological synthesis that departs from prior works in three key ways: (i) it infers latent drivers through group-level decomposition, (ii) models evolving system-wide structure via dynamic connectivity, and (iii) embeds high-level states via modular system dynamics. We highlight key distinctions as follows:

Beyond Traditional Econometric Models.

Conventional time series models such as ARIMA, GARCH, and VAR focus on short-term forecasting using handcrafted lag structures and assume stationarity or linear interactions
Parot et al. 2019
;
Kinateder and Wagner 2014
. While effective for local fluctuations, these models lack the capacity to uncover system-wide latent patterns or detect market regime shifts. In contrast, our framework prioritizes
latent structural decomposition
and
modular temporal dynamics
, aiming to characterize evolving market architecture rather than pointwise predictions.

Beyond Instantaneous Graph-based Methods.

Graph-theoretic approaches—such as correlation networks, minimum spanning trees
Mantegna 1999
, and rolling correlation matrices—are often limited to shallow, pairwise structures that do not capture higher-order latent organization. Our use of
group ICA
instead reveals interpretable
market modules
, which function as latent network-level drivers of asset co-movements—analogous to functional subnetworks in the brain.

Integration of ICA and Connectivity.

Independent component analysis (ICA) and network connectivity have each been sporadically applied to financial data
Oja et al. 2000
;
Isogai 2017
, but typically in isolation. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically applied a full connectomics pipeline—combining
group ICA
,
dynamic connectivity
, and latent module interactions—to financial markets. By explicitly drawing structural parallels with neuroscience, our framework provides a cohesive and interpretable system-level view of market behavior.

Dynamic Module-Level Interaction.

Unlike traditional factor models (e.g., Fama-French
Fama and French 1993
), which assume fixed or slow-moving latent factors, our framework models the dynamic evolution of
market modules
—groups of assets that exhibit coherent, yet transient co-activation. Using dynamic connectivity patterns, we track how these modules interact, decouple, or synchronize over time. This provides a richer, unsupervised lens on structural transitions and systemic instability.

From Prediction to Structural Regime Analysis.

While much of the existing literature focuses on forecasting prices or returns
Shahi et al. 2020
;
Gülmez 2025
;
Ge 2025
, our approach shifts attention toward modeling systemic organization and temporal regimes. The resulting outputs—time-varying network states and latent module interactions—enable market regime characterization, anomaly detection, and stress monitoring, even in the absence of predictive objectives or exogenous labels.

Methodological Framework: Constructing the Financial Connectome

2.1
Overview of the Pipeline

Our proposed
Financial Connectome
framework mirrors the data processing pipeline of modern brain connectomics, adapted to the domain of financial market analysis. As illustrated in
Figure 1
, the framework involves a sequence of modular steps, each with direct correspondence to established neuroimaging operations. Importantly, our approach is fully grounded in classical statistical modeling—eschewing black-box deep learning in favor of transparent and interpretable methods. Below we outline each component of the pipeline.

•

Input data
: Multivariate time series comprising asset-level signals (e.g., price, volume, volatility) are extracted across historical time windows. These serve as the financial counterpart to voxel-level BOLD signals in fMRI, capturing raw temporal dynamics of individual stocks or sectors.

•

Group ICA decomposition
: Inspired by spatial ICA in neuroimaging, we apply
group independent component analysis
across rolling time windows of financial data to extract latent co-movement structures. These latent factors, termed
market modules
, represent functionally coherent asset clusters whose synchronized behavior resembles canonical brain networks (e.g., DMN, VIS).

•

Dynamic connectivity modeling
: We compute pairwise correlations between the temporal profiles of the market modules within each window to construct
dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC)
matrices. These matrices capture evolving interaction patterns among latent market structures and offer insight into the systemic organization of financial markets over time.

•

Dimensionality reduction and clustering
: Instead of deep learning-based embeddings, we employ classical methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering, and multidimensional scaling (MDS) to summarize the structure of dMNC matrices. These statistical techniques preserve interpretability while enabling visualization and downstream analysis.

•

Downstream tasks
: The extracted structures support multiple analyses, including market regime identification (e.g., crisis onset), connectivity-based anomaly detection, and discovery of
market markers
—i.e., characteristic connectivity patterns associated with major transitions or systemic stress, analogous to neural biomarkers.

This end-to-end pipeline provides a neuroscience-inspired, interpretable, and statistically principled framework for understanding financial dynamics. It emphasizes modularity and systemic structure, offering diagnostic insights without relying on predictive supervision or opaque machine learning models.

2.2
Group ICA over Rolling Windows: Defining Market Modules

Why Decompose? Group ICA as the Structural Backbone

Constructing meaningful networks in financial systems requires a principled choice of
nodes
. It is neither interpretable nor stable to directly construct connectivity graphs over individual stocks or raw high-dimensional price signals. The resulting graphs are typically sparse, noisy, and lack consistent topological structure—hindering both interpretability and generalizability.

In brain connectomics, this challenge is addressed by first identifying latent spatial networks—such as the visual network (VIS) or default mode network (DMN)—via group ICA. These functionally coherent components serve as the canonical building blocks (nodes) for defining functional connectivity (FNC) and dynamic FNC (dFNC). Without such modular decomposition, downstream analysis of network dynamics would be ill-defined.

Analogously, we posit that uncovering latent
market modules
is a necessary first step toward constructing meaningful dynamic market networks. These modules—representing functional clusters of co-moving assets—constitute the foundational “units” from which higher-order market structure and dynamics can be inferred.

Method: ICA over Rolling Windows as Pseudo-Subjects

To extract these modules in a data-driven, temporally aware manner, we propose a group ICA framework over rolling historical windows, treating each time window as a
pseudo-subject
. The procedure is as follows:

1.

Divide the historical financial dataset into
T
fixed-length overlapping windows (e.g., 30 trading days), where each window reflects a short-term market configuration.

2.

For each window
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, collect multivariate time series from
N
assets (e.g., stocks), each represented by
F
features (e.g., normalized price, return, volume, volatility).

3.

For each window, compute a summary statistic per asset-feature pair—e.g., average, standard deviation, or trend coefficient—yielding a
T\times N\times F
tensor.

4.

The resulting tensor is reshaped into a
(T\cdot N)\times F
matrix, where each row captures a snapshot of an asset in a given time window. PCA is applied to remove redundancy, followed by ICA to extract interpretable market modules that consistently recur across windows.

5.

The resulting
K
spatial components define latent market modules (analogous to brain networks), with each component expressing a weighted spatial pattern over the
N
assets. An associated timecourse can also be derived for each component by back-projecting the component weights into the original window space.

Interpretation: Data-Driven Market Modules

The learned components typically correspond to coherent groups of assets that exhibit correlated behaviors over specific periods. In some cases, the modules align with conventional sectoral classifications (e.g., technology, energy, financials), while in others they may reveal latent clusters such as:

•

Small-cap high-volatility stocks during speculative bubbles

•

Cross-sector themes like ESG-focused or momentum-driven portfolios

•

Temporally transient clusters tied to macroeconomic or geopolitical events

Unlike predefined sector labels, these modules emerge from the data without supervision, capturing the intrinsic co-movement structure that evolves with market conditions. They represent
functional units
of market dynamics - similar to emerging brain networks - rather than static categorizations.

Comparison with Fixed Sector Classification

Traditional financial models often rely on static sectoral partitions (e.g., GICS or NAICS) to organize asset behavior. These classifications are human-defined, rigid, and slow to adapt to structural changes or cross-sectoral dynamics. In contrast, our ICA-based decomposition is:

•

Dynamic
: Modules are re-estimated over rolling windows, allowing for regime-dependent reconfiguration.

•

Unsupervised
: No manual sector labels or human intervention is required.

•

Latent
: Modules reflect statistical co-movement patterns, potentially capturing hidden structures not observable through standard classifications.

This enables a more flexible and adaptive understanding of market structure—one that can respond to emergent trends, hidden interactions, and evolving risk exposures in real time.

2.3
Extracting Module Dynamics and Building dMNC

Motivation: Why Group ICA is Essential for Market Dynamics

Group ICA is not merely a dimensionality reduction tool—it offers a principled statistical decomposition of the financial market into latent, functionally coherent subsystems. Similar to its role in brain imaging, where group ICA extracts reproducible neural networks such as the visual (VIS) or default mode network (DMN), we utilize group ICA to identify
market modules
—clusters of assets that exhibit statistically independent co-movement patterns over time.

Attempting to analyze interactions directly at the asset level is often problematic: high
-dimensionality, noise, and volatility render the resulting networks unstable and uninterpretable. Group ICA addresses this challenge by isolating latent drivers of collective behavior, enabling a lower-dimensional yet functionally meaningful representation of market dynamics.

From ICA to Temporal Module Activations

Each component produced by group ICA represents a spatial pattern—a weighted combination of assets—that characterizes a latent market module. Crucially, ICA also yields an associated timecourse for each component, indicating how strongly that module is expressed over time.

By applying group ICA to a series of rolling or segmented historical windows, we extract a temporal activation matrix:

A\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T},

where
K
is the number of ICA components (market modules) and
T
is the number of time windows. The entry
A_{k,t}
denotes the strength of module
k
during time window
t
. This matrix captures the dynamic expression of underlying structural forces in the market—e.g., sectoral flows, macroeconomic cycles, or thematic trading patterns.

Dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC)

To capture the evolving interdependencies among modules, we construct dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC) matrices via a sliding-window Pearson correlation approach. Specifically:

1.

For each temporal window of width
\Delta
, extract a submatrix
A_{\cdot,t:t+\Delta}
.

2.

Compute the Pearson correlation matrix among the
K
module activations within this window.

3.

The result is a symmetric matrix
C_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
, reflecting the functional connectivity among market modules at time
t
.

Sliding this window across the entire timeline produces a sequence of matrices
\{C_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-\Delta}
, which collectively characterize the dynamic topological evolution of the market’s internal structure.

Optional Preprocessing: Enhancing Stability and Interpretability

To mitigate the impact of short-term noise and enhance signal quality, the module activation series can optionally undergo smoothing or normalization steps prior to correlation computation. Common techniques include:

•

Moving average or exponential smoothing to suppress high-frequency volatility.

•

Seasonal adjustment or detrending to remove repetitive cycles.

•

Z-scoring across modules to ensure comparability and scale invariance.

These procedures are not strictly required but can significantly improve the robustness and interpretability of dMNC matrices, especially during turbulent market regimes.

Interpretation and Applications of dMNC

Each dMNC matrix serves as a snapshot of the latent systemic architecture of the market at a given point in time. Temporal transitions or shifts in the structure of these matrices can reveal:

•

Crisis synchronization:
Heightened connectivity across all modules during systemic shocks.

•

Regime bifurcation:
Diverging behaviors among traditionally correlated modules.

•

Emergent themes:
Spontaneous formation of novel inter-module correlations driven by external events.

Such insights enable a wide range of downstream analyses, including market regime detection, systemic risk monitoring, and unsupervised event discovery.

Summary

Group ICA provides a statistically grounded and interpretable way to uncover the latent building blocks of financial systems. Combined with dynamic correlation modeling, the resulting dMNC matrices offer a compact, modular, and temporally aware representation of market structure. Crucially, this framework operates entirely in an unsupervised and non-parametric fashion, avoiding assumptions about regime boundaries or predictive targets—making it particularly well-suited for structural market modeling.

2.4
Embedding and Utilizing dMNC for Downstream Analysis

From ICA Modules to dMNC Construction

Once group ICA is performed, each market module is associated with a timecourse of activations over rolling windows. These module timecourses are stacked into a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T}
, where each row corresponds to a specific component (IC1, IC2, …, ICK), and each column to a time window.

To ensure consistency across samples, we enforce a fixed component ordering (e.g., IC1 always corresponds to the same latent module across all timepoints), leveraging symbol alignment techniques during ICA aggregation. This consistency is crucial: since Pearson correlation is sensitive to ordering, any permutation would lead to semantic inconsistency in downstream representations.

For each timepoint
t
, we define a temporal window of width
\Delta
and extract the corresponding submatrix
A_{\cdot,t:t+\Delta}
. A Pearson correlation matrix is then computed among the
K
module timecourses within this window:

C_{t}=\text{corr}(A_{\cdot,t:t+\Delta})\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}

This yields a dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC) matrix
C_{t}
for each window
t
, capturing the pairwise relationships among latent market modules during that specific period. The sequence
\{C_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-\Delta}
characterizes the temporal evolution of the market’s internal architecture.

Using dMNC Matrices for Structural Analysis

Each dMNC matrix represents a high-level, noise-robust snapshot of systemic market connectivity at a given time. These matrices can be used directly or vectorized (e.g., flattening upper triangles) to support various downstream tasks:

•

Market Regime Clustering
: By computing pairwise similarity (e.g., Frobenius norm, cosine distance) between dMNC matrices, one can group structurally similar periods into distinct regimes—such as bullish, bearish, or transitional.

•

Anomaly Detection
: Abrupt shifts in dMNC structure may indicate unusual market activity, policy shocks, or early-warning signs of systemic risk.

•

Visualization
: Dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., PCA, UMAP, t-SNE) applied to vectorized dMNC matrices can reveal low-dimensional trajectories of market evolution and transitions between states.

•

Regression and Forecasting
: The dMNC matrices or their embeddings can serve as inputs to models predicting downstream variables—such as volatility, drawdown probability, or macroeconomic indicators.

Advantages of the dMNC-Based Representation

•

Latent and Modular
: dMNC operates on modules learned from data, not pre-defined sectors, allowing adaptation to evolving market structure.

•

Unsupervised and Interpretable
: Unlike black-box predictive models, dMNC offers human-interpretable snapshots of system-wide co-dependencies.

•

Resilient to Asset-Level Noise
: Operating at the module level aggregates away asset-specific fluctuations and reveals higher-order dynamics.

Summary

dMNC matrices serve as a powerful representation of latent market states, capturing the dynamic interrelationships among functional modules uncovered by ICA. By consistently aligning module order and using statistically grounded connectivity computation, we obtain a compact yet expressive description of market structure that is well-suited for clustering, visualization, and risk monitoring—without relying on deep learning or supervised labels.

2.5
Downstream Use: Regime Discovery and Systemic Signals

Unsupervised Market Regime Discovery via dMNC Clustering

The sequence of dMNC matrices
\{C_{t}\}
captures the evolving interdependencies among latent market modules. To identify structural regimes, we first vectorize each
C_{t}
by flattening its upper triangle (excluding the diagonal), producing a time-indexed set of feature vectors
\{v_{t}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K(K-1)/2}
.

These vectors are then optionally reduced in dimensionality using PCA or UMAP, followed by unsupervised clustering (e.g.,
k
-means or HDBSCAN) to detect recurring patterns. Typical regimes include:

•

Synchronized growth regimes
, where module correlations are uniformly high and stable.

•

Stress or disintegration regimes
, marked by reduced or negative correlations across modules.

•

Transition regimes
, where connectivity patterns shift rapidly over time.

•

Neutral or resting regimes
, characterized by low variance in connectivity structure.

This provides a structure-centric taxonomy of market phases—independent of return volatility or macroeconomic labels—and enables visualization of system evolution in a network-aware manifold.

Systemic Risk and Structural Anomaly Detection

Abnormal or unstable market states can be detected by monitoring abrupt changes in the dMNC sequence. Two strategies are proposed:

•

Similarity jump detection
: Compute the cosine or Frobenius similarity between
C_{t}
and
C_{t-1}
; sharp drops may indicate regime transitions or exogenous shocks.

•

Distance to baseline
: Define a baseline regime from historically stable dMNC patterns and compute distance (e.g., Mahalanobis) between current
C_{t}
and the baseline cluster.

These methods parallel approaches in neuroscience for detecting disruptions in brain functional connectivity under stress or disease.

Structural Volatility via dMNC Statistics

We define the
structural volatility signal

S(t)
as:

S(t)=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{Var}_{\tau}[\mathrm{dMNC}_{ij}(t-\tau,\ldots,t)]

where
\mathrm{Var}_{\tau}
denotes the temporal variance over a sliding window of past
\tau
days. This measures the degree of instability in inter-module coordination.

High values of
S(t)
indicate turbulent reconfigurations in market structure, often preceding or accompanying macroeconomic crises, policy shocks, or liquidity events. Analogous signals have been observed in pathological brain states where network coherence breaks down.

Summary

By modeling the market as a dynamic network of latent functional modules, the Financial Connectome framework provides a principled basis for structural regime discovery, anomaly detection, and systemic risk monitoring. Unlike price- or return-based methods, these analyses emphasize internal market architecture—opening a path toward interpretable, real-time financial diagnostics inspired by network neuroscience.

Functional–Module Extraction on the Stock-brain

We analogize the 50-dimensional stock matrix to a pseudo-brain and extract its latent connectivity structure via multi-scale group Independent Component Analysis (ICA), mirroring the functional connectome extraction pipeline used in human neuroimaging. Having defined the 50–node

Step 1: Multi-scale group ICA.

For each window length

Step 2: Cross-window component alignment.

1.

2.

|\rho|

3.

3.1

Step 4: Dynamic factor index construction.

For each trading day
t

\mathbf{r}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{50}

\mathbf{r}_{t}

\rho_{252}(t)=\text{Corr}\left(\{z^{\text{On}}_{\tau}\},\{z^{\text{Off}}_{\tau}\}\right)_{\tau=t-251}^{t},

risk-shift curve

Step 5: Coherence diagnostics and validation.

Cross-era similarity

|\rho|
between different

\rho_{252}(t)

4.1
Stock Market Modularity via Group-Level ICA

We begin by evaluating whether latent modular structures—analogous to functional brain networks—can be robustly extracted from financial markets using group-level Independent Component Analysis (ICA). A pilot study was conducted on 50 high-liquidity U.S. equities using rolling-window log-return features as input. The analysis reveals interpretable components that consistently capture contrasting market behaviors, particularly along polarity axes such as cyclical versus defensive stocks. These latent modules exhibit remarkable recurrence across distinct economic eras, suggesting the presence of stable functional structures within market co-movement dynamics. This echoes how cognitive systems express reliable patterns across individuals and states.

4.2
Cross-Era Stability of Market Modules

To quantify temporal robustness, we evaluate the stability of ICA-derived components across different macroeconomic periods. Matched components between non-overlapping eras exhibit high correlations (often exceeding 0.6), even when significant structural shifts occurred in the market. Notably, this stability is preserved across varying ICA configurations—including different numbers of input assets and decomposition dimensions—highlighting the inherent regularity of market connectivity structure.

These results support a fundamental hypothesis: financial markets, like the brain, exhibit modular organization that is both persistent and adaptable. Market shocks or transitions may alter the composition of modules, but the core latent patterns—reflecting the functional architecture of the system—are preserved. This property of structural recurrence provides a compelling foundation for further modeling, including dynamic tracking of modular state shifts or predictive applications in systemic risk monitoring.

One alternative is to use high-frequency intraday data to improve granularity, which could enable a voxel-like approach while maintaining temporal depth.

Discussions

5.1
Stable Market Modules as Functional Networks

Our results reveal that the financial market exhibits latent modular structures reminiscent of functional brain networks. Among these, a “Risk-On / Risk-Off” axis consistently emerges from group-level ICA across both stock and ETF data, reflecting a polarity between cyclical and defensive assets. Notably, these modules show greater temporal and cross-era stability at the ETF level, suggesting that higher-order asset aggregates may encode more robust structural patterns than individual securities.

These findings motivate a broader interpretation of market behavior: rather than viewing co-movement as noise or temporary correlation, we suggest that markets possess intrinsic, evolving network architectures—patterns that can be meaningfully decomposed and studied using neuroscience-inspired tools.

5.2
Toward Downstream Representations: dMNC and Regime Clustering

While this study focuses on uncovering stable latent modules through static group-level ICA, a natural next step is to exploit the temporal dynamics of these components. Drawing inspiration from dynamic Functional Network Connectivity (dFNC) in neuroscience, we propose to build a dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC) framework that characterizes how inter-module relationships evolve over time. By sliding a temporal window over historical returns and extracting component time courses, we can compute moment-to-moment correlation matrices between ICA components, revealing fluctuating market states and transitions.

These dMNC matrices can serve as input to downstream tasks such as market regime clustering, anomaly detection, and temporal segmentation. For instance, we anticipate that certain dMNC states—marked by strong anti-correlation between risk-on and risk-off networks—will coincide with classical “flight to safety” episodes, while synchronized activation may signal momentum-driven bull markets. Clustering these dMNC states using unsupervised techniques (e.g., k-means, HMMs, self-supervised contrastive learning) offers a data-driven way to define latent financial regimes without relying on arbitrary macro labels.

However, several challenges must be addressed to ensure reliable downstream modeling. First, the ICA component signs and order may fluctuate across runs or datasets. We have partially addressed this by using sign alignment and Hungarian matching across windows, but further improvements—such as robust initialization, ensemble ICA, or ICASSO-style bootstrapping—may enhance temporal consistency. Second, market components are inherently non-stationary; modules may reconfigure during extreme events. This necessitates flexible matching strategies and embedding-based representations that allow “soft” module tracking across time. Finally, integrating multi-timescale or multi-modal inputs (e.g., combining 1-day and 30-min return ICA) requires architectural decisions regarding feature fusion, synchronization, and interpretability.

5.3
Bridging Neuroscience and Finance: A Cross-Disciplinary Perspective

The methodological parallels between neuroscience and financial systems are deeper than surface analogies. Both domains deal with high-dimensional, noisy, and temporally evolving signals generated by interacting subunits—neurons or stocks—embedded within complex networks. In neuroscience, the field of
connectomics
has flourished by analyzing brain activity through latent network decomposition, revealing structured dynamics that underlie cognition and behavior. Similarly, financial systems exhibit emergent patterns—such as coordinated sector rotations or regime shifts—that resist reduction to single-variable predictors.

By extending connectome analysis to markets, we do not simply borrow tools—we also invite reciprocal inspiration. For instance, financial systems may serve as testbeds for validating models of emergent synchronization, modular resilience, or regime transitions under controlled shocks (e.g., interest rate hikes), which are difficult to observe in biological systems. Conversely, financial applications can drive new demands in scalability, real-time analysis, and causal modeling, feeding back into connectomics research.

In this light, we envision a two-way bridge: neuroscience methods bring structure-awareness to financial modeling, while finance offers dynamic, interpretable systems that challenge and extend neuro-inspired frameworks. This mutual dialogue could catalyze new advances in both fields.

5.4
Group ICA as a General-Purpose Decomposition Framework

Originally developed within neuroimaging, group-level Independent Component Analysis (ICA) offers a powerful and domain-agnostic framework for uncovering latent structure in complex, high-dimensional systems. By assuming that multiple observations (e.g., fMRI subjects, financial time windows) share a common set of independent sources, group ICA facilitates unsupervised structure discovery in systems where distributed yet overlapping signals dominate.

In finance, this paradigm is particularly compelling. Traditional models often rely on fixed assumptions—linearity, stationarity, or pre-specified factor structures—that may miss emergent interactions among market entities. In contrast, group ICA identifies latent modules directly from empirical returns, without reliance on handcrafted features like “value” or “sector.” These components, such as our discovered Risk-On/Risk-Off axis, capture functional co-movement patterns that evolve over time while retaining coherent macro interpretations. This reframes market structure not as a static taxonomy, but as a set of dynamic, distributed processes—offering new ways to characterize capital flow, systemic stress, or thematic rotation.

Beyond equities, this framework opens promising directions for macroeconomic and digital asset modeling. In macroeconomics, ICA can be applied to multivariate indicators (e.g., inflation, interest rates, unemployment) across countries to uncover hidden global forces—such as synchronized policy cycles or latent stagflationary dynamics—that transcend traditional national boundaries. Similarly, in the cryptocurrency space, ICA may identify thematic modules (e.g., DeFi tokens, L1 ecosystems, stablecoins) that co-move based on shared narratives, liquidity constraints, or regulatory shocks—offering an interpretable lens into contagion dynamics and behavioral regimes in non-traditional markets. Notably, this framework may be particularly suited for markets characterized by regime instability and narrative-driven reorganization, such as digital assets and emerging frontiers.

Unlike black-box machine learning models, ICA offers interpretable, modular representations with immediate relevance to portfolio construction, scenario analysis, and policy intervention. Identifying persistent “liquidity stress modules” or “macro volatility ensembles” could inform hedging strategies, asset allocation, or even real-time macro risk indicators. In this light, group ICA emerges not merely as a neuroscience tool—but as a general-purpose decomposition engine for understanding the architecture of complex, evolving systems.

5.5
Implications for Quantitative Finance and Real-World Applications

In an era where black-box alpha models increasingly suffer from performance decay, lack of interpretability, and overfitting to high-frequency noise, there is growing interest in structural and explainable frameworks that uncover persistent market organization. Our study offers a proof-of-concept for such a
structure-first
approach—modeling the financial system as a latent network of dynamic, self-organizing modules, analogous to functional subnetworks in the brain.

While our current implementation is intentionally simplified—based on a limited set of ETFs, medium-frequency return data, and fixed-length sliding windows—it demonstrates that interpretable latent components can be consistently extracted across regimes and matched across time. We emphasize that this is not yet a production-ready model, but rather a “functional atlas” of market dynamics meant to inspire further development.

This framework departs from traditional approaches in several key ways. Whereas factor models focus on static, often linear exposures (e.g., Fama-French styles, PCA eigenportfolios), and price-prediction models chase short-horizon alpha (e.g., statistical arbitrage, trend following), our method seeks to uncover the latent
architecture
that governs asset co-movement and risk propagation. Just as cognitive neuroscience first builds a brain atlas before studying behavior, we advocate for first mapping the modular financial structure before drawing predictive conclusions.

Importantly, the latent modules revealed through ICA and dynamic Market Network Connectivity (dMNC) can support a variety of downstream financial applications:

•

Alpha Generation:
Regime-aware trading signals can be derived by monitoring transitions between ICA components—for instance, the activation of risk-on modules or synchronization between funding-sensitive clusters.

•

Systemic Risk Monitoring:
dMNC metrics may function as real-time stress indicators, flagging potential contagion pathways or volatility build-ups when certain modules become unstable or hyper-correlated.

•

Portfolio Construction:
ICA-based components offer a novel diversification axis: investors can build portfolios that span orthogonal functional subnetworks, potentially reducing exposure to latent co-activation shocks.

•

Strategy Decomposition:
Quantitative strategies can themselves be projected onto ICA-defined subspaces to assess redundancy, regime sensitivity, or behavioral overlap.

Looking ahead, several important extensions could bring this framework closer to real-world deployment:

•

Online ICA and Real-Time Monitoring:
Implementing real-time, adaptive ICA decomposition (e.g., using online optimization or recursive ICA algorithms) could allow dynamic updating of market modules as new data arrives, enabling on-the-fly regime detection.

•

Multi-Modal Fusion:
By incorporating news sentiment, macro indicators, or investor positioning data, one could build richer hybrid representations and begin disentangling exogenous drivers from endogenous market structure.

•

Causal Inference within Modules:
Overlaying event-driven data—such as central bank announcements or geopolitical shocks—onto latent module activity may allow us to reverse-engineer causal influence graphs across market subnetworks.

•

Scalable Infrastructure:
To transition from white-paper to production system, significant engineering work is required: low-latency data ingestion pipelines, GPU-accelerated ICA computation, online matching algorithms, and robust visualization tools for monitoring latent regimes in real time.

Ultimately, we view the
financial connectome
not as a static model, but as a conceptual foundation—one that bridges neuroscience, complex systems, and quantitative finance. Our hope is that this framework encourages further work on structure-aware representations, dynamic modular inference, and interpretable abstractions of market behavior. The present work represents a first step toward this vision: a call to explore the hidden geometry of markets, not just their observable outputs.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a novel framework for modeling financial markets through the lens of functional network decomposition, inspired by methodologies originally developed in neuroscience. By applying group-level Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to multi-year stock and ETF return data, we uncover interpretable, temporally persistent modules that reflect latent regimes of market behavior. Notably, we identify a recurring “Risk-On / Risk-Off” axis that recapitulates intuitive macroeconomic themes and demonstrates structural continuity across time and instruments.

Beyond the discovery of stable market components, we propose a roadmap for extending this framework to dynamic representations via the construction of Market Network Connectivity (dMNC), enabling regime detection, systemic risk tracking, and structure-aware portfolio design. We argue that this connectome-inspired modeling paradigm provides a fundamentally new way of understanding financial systems—not merely as collections of assets or factors, but as evolving modular networks with intrinsic functional organization.

While our present implementation serves as a proof of concept using tractable datasets and offline analysis, the broader implications are far-reaching. From a scientific perspective, this work expands the domain of group ICA beyond neuroscience into complex economic systems. From a practical standpoint, it offers interpretable, structure-first abstractions that may complement or enhance existing quantitative workflows. We hope this study stimulates further exploration at the intersection of financial modeling, systems neuroscience, and unsupervised learning.
