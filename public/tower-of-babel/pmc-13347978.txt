Artificial intelligence-assisted integrated care to promote colonoscopy uptake (AICC) in China: study protocol for a cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xin Wang, Huilan Zhou, Nan Zhang, Yan Liu, Haipeng Wang, Jiewei Tang, Zhuoran Sun, Zhiyuan Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) leads to heavy disease and economic burdens globally. Early screening such as colonoscopy has been demonstrated to reduce both CRC incidence and mortality. However, uptake of colonoscopy among high-risk individuals is low in China, limiting screening efficacy. To improve screening uptake, it is necessary to ensure the engagement of both specialist and general practitioners. And recently, AI techniques show considerable promise in delivering personalized health education. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of AI-assisted integrated care, compared with specialty care in improving uptake of colonoscopy among CRC high-risk individuals in China.MethodsA two-arm, parallel cluster randomized controlled trial will enroll 400 high-risk residents for CRC aged 40–64 years from 40 communities/villages across one urban district and two rural counties in Shandong Province, China. Communities/villages as clusters will be randomized within counties to either a specialty care group or an AI-assisted integrated care group. Participants will be those initially identified as high risk for CRC through a risk assessment questionnaire or fecal immunochemical test. The primary outcomes are colonoscopy uptake at 3 and 6 months post-intervention and the time to colonoscopy. Secondary outcomes encompass CRC screening literacy, beliefs, and intention to undergo colonoscopy. Additionally, we will also conduct a process evaluation and health economic evaluation.DiscussionThis study proposes a novel strategy integrating a multidisciplinary team with digital health solutions. We expect that this strategy will provide a feasible approach to improve colonoscopy uptake. The findings could offer practical insights for informing and advancing cancer prevention and control initiatives in China.Trial registrationClinicalTrial.gov NCT07261059. Registered on December 2, 2025.

Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the second most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in China [1]. Driven by lifestyle changes and aging population, the increasing incidence and mortality of CRC highlight its growing significance as a public health challenge. Most CRC cases develop from polyps to CRC over 10 to 15 years, offering a critical window for early detection. The 5-year survival rate for early-stage CRC exceeds 90%, compared to less than 14% for metastatic CRC [2]. However, over 85% of Chinese patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, which substantially contributes to the overall poor survival outcomes [3].

Colonoscopy, the gold standard for CRC screening, enables direct visualization and removal of precancerous polyps [4]. Evidence shows that colonoscopy can reduce CRC incidence by 56% and and mortality by 57% [3]. China has launched national initiatives such as the “Action Plan for Cancer Prevention and Control (2023–2030)” and the Protocol for Colorectal Cancer Screening and Early Detection (2024) to promote the expansion of CRC screening, particularly targeted at high-risk individuals aged 40–74 [5, 6]. It adopts initial risk stratification using questionnaires and fecal immunochemical tests (FIT), followed by referral to colonoscopy for those testing positive on either measure [7]. Despite these efforts, screening coverage remains critically low at approximately 1%, far below that in the USA (70%) and the Republic of Korea (35%), underscoring a substantial gap in current national programs [8]. Moreover, the uptake of colonoscopy among high-risk individuals in China is also low, ranging from 14 to 19% [9, 10]. These challenges are compounded by multiple barriers, including limited public awareness, negative perceptions regarding colonoscopy, and insufficient patient-provider communication [11–13].

Improving the uptake of colonoscopy is critical to its effectiveness as a screening tool. Multiple strategies to enhance participation have been explored globally, including patient reminders, health education, patient navigation, and clinician recommendation [14–17]. While reminders and education are readily scalable, their impact is often limited to poor information dissemination. Patient navigation, though effective, is constrained in large-scale implementation by high costs and human resource demands. Clinicians are regarded as highly authoritative source of health information, and their recommendation remains one of the most influential drivers of screening uptake. Systematic reviews have confirmed that clinician recommendations significantly improve uptake of cancer screening [18]. Correspondingly, US guidelines endorse clinician recommendation as an evidence-based intervention to increase CRC screening uptake [19]. In China, however, CRC screening rely mainly on specialists from hospitals, with limited involvement of general practitioners (GPs) [20]. As gatekeepers, GPs have a crucial responsibility to provide health management for community residents. Therefore, it is necessary to explore an integrated care model centered on collaboration between GPs and specialists to improve colonoscopy uptake.

In addition, artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly applied in health care fields, serving as a transformative tool for health education. Specifically, AI-powered chatbots can provide real-time and personalized health educational interactions, which may enhance public knowledge of CRC screening and ultimately improve uptake [21]. Compared with traditional health education approaches, chatbots have many advantages, including the ability to simplify specialized medical knowledge, low costs, and instant response for easy public access [22, 23]. Recent studies on ChatGPT and other large language models (LLM) have shown high accuracy, clarity, and user acceptance for CRC screening; however, those studies remain in early-stage development or pilot trials, with evaluations focusing primarily on model performance and lack of rigorous evaluation on CRC screening behavior in real world [24–27].

To address these research gaps, this study aims to develop and evaluate an AI-assisted integrated care model designed to improve uptake of colonoscopy for CRC screening in China. The model leverages an AI-powered chatbot for personalized patient education and enables seamless collaborative recommendation between GPs and specialists. Our goal is to generate evidence for implementing an effective and scalable CRC screening strategy in China and other settings facing high burden of CRC.

Explanation for the choice of comparator {9b}

In line with the most common practice for CRC screening in China, participants in the control group will receive a colonoscopy recommendation from a specialist.

Objectives {10}

The study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of AI-assisted integrated care for colonoscopy (AICC), compared to specialty care, for improving uptake of colonoscopy among high-risk individuals in China. We hypothesize that the AICC will significantly increase uptake of colonoscopy by improving screening-related literacy, beliefs, and behavioral intention.

Methods: patient and public involvement, and trial design

Patient and public involvement {11}

Two rounds of public engagement guided the intervention design. First, five public representatives co-designed the chatbot’s educational functions. Subsequently, ten contributors evaluated the integrated GP-specialist model from a recipient perspective. Their involvement optimized the overall intervention design.

Trial design {12}

This pragmatic, two-arm, parallel, cluster randomized controlled trial will be conducted in community settings. It is designed to test the hypothesis that an AI-assisted integrated care model leads to higher colonoscopy uptake compared with specialty care alone. A total of 40 communities/villages as clusters will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control group. Individuals at high risk for CRC will be identified using a risk assessment questionnaire and a fecal immunochemical test (FIT). Eligible participants will then receive the corresponding care pathway based on the allocation of their communities/villages (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Design and flowchart of the study

Inclusion criteria

(1) High-risk status defined as a score of ≥ 4 points on the above risk assessment scale or a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT) result; (2) aged 40–64 years; (3) possessing smartphone literacy and the ability to engage with the digital intervention; (4) willingness to consent to participate.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Previous diagnosis of CRC; (2) contraindications to colonoscopy (e.g., severe cardiac pulmonary, cerebral, or renal conditions).

Eligibility criteria for sites and those delivering interventions {14b}

The selection of counties in Shandong Province under the “Urban Cancer Screening and Early Detection” program is based on their prior engagement in CRC screening initiatives, willingness to participate, and regional representativeness. Eligible counties had a record of consistent and active involvement in population-based cancer screening, which has enabled them to accumulate substantial experience, develop mature implementation procedures and management teams. These capacities provide essential support for the large-scale participants recruitment and implementation of complex interventions required by this trial.

Specialists delivering the interventions must have prior experience in CRC screening care within organized screening program. GPs are required to have at least 5 years of clinical practice experience. All personnel (both specialists and GPs) involved in the implementation must complete and pass standardized training before the trial begins.

Who will take informed consent? {32a}

Informed consent will be obtained from all eligible participants prior to enrollment. Trained research team members from county hospital will administer the consent process, which includes providing a detailed information sheet, explaining the study procedures thoroughly, and addressing participant inquiries. Those who agree to participate will sign the consent form, retain a copy of the signed documents, and be explicitly advised of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {32b}

Access to research data requires formal request by interested researchers. All requests will be subject to review and approval in compliance with applicable ethical standards and data protection regulations. No biological specimens will be collected in this study.

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

Usual specialty care group

Participants in this group will receive usual specialty care, colonoscopy recommendation by specialists of county hospitals. For ethical considerations, these participants will be offered access to the chatbot upon completion of the trial.

AI-assisted integrated care group

Development of CRC chatbot

The proposed chatbot for CRC screening education will be developed through a structured systematic process and deployed on the WeChat platform for ease to access. Initial functional requirements will be defined through interviews with healthcare providers and target users. To ensure reliability, a domain-specific knowledge base will be constructed using verified medical literature and subjected to clinical peer review. The system architecture leverages Alibaba’s Qwen LLM, enhanced by retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) technology to minimize hallucinations. Following iterative optimization for accuracy and user experience, the chatbot will provide a holistic educational module, including CRC pathology, screening methods, procedural details, and navigation to local medical resources.

Intervention implementation

In addition to usual recommendation of specialists, participants in this group will receive GPs’ follow-up and health education supported by the CRC chatbot. The intervention procedure is as follows:

Firstly, participants and family members will receive an on-site colonoscopy recommendation from county-hospital specialists, who will explain the importance and examination procedure of colonoscopy. Concurrently, the specialists will introduce the dedicated chatbot, assist with registration, and conduct an initial 5-min structured learning session. For those who consent to colonoscopy, an appointment will be scheduled immediately. The same protocol will be applied to FIT-positive individuals, with GPs delivering the recommendation once results are available.

Beginning 1 month after baseline, participants who have not completed colonoscopy will receive monthly follow-up from a GP for three consecutive months. At each follow-up, the GPs will deliver monthly health education to participants and families, accompanied by a reminder to use the chatbot, and help make an appointment for those consent for colonoscopy.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention/comparator {15b}

As this study evaluates a low-risk behavioral intervention (health education and recommendation), the allocated interventions are not subject to modification. Participants retain the right to withdraw from the study at any time, which would automatically cease their involvement in the intervention.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

GPs will proactively provide face-to-face health education at each follow-up visit. To further enhance user engagement, the chatbot will be accessed through WeChat platform, leveraging its widespread popularity and user-friendly extensibility in China. Considering that older adults may be less familiar with digital technology, we will also invite their adult children to use the chatbot, thereby promoting its utilization.

Concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

There is no specific concomitant care administered nor prohibited during the trial.

Ancillary and post-trial care {34}

There is no anticipated harm or compensation for participation.

Harms {17}

Due to the low risk of the interventions (health education and chatbot use), no specific adverse events are anticipated. Any spontaneously reported adverse events will be recorded and reported to the ethics committee. Colonoscopy is a routine clinical procedure with known risks (e.g., bleeding, perforation). These risks are managed to follow the hospital’s standard clinical procedures and are not within the scope of this trial’s monitoring, as colonoscopy is not part of the study intervention.

Recruitment {20}

County-hospital specialists will visit villages/communities to conduct CRC risk assessment using questionnaires and FIT, thereby reducing barriers such as time constraints and transportation difficulties and maximizing enrollment. The questionnaire will be completed on-site via QR-code scanning, providing immediate results, and enabling rapid identification of high-risk individuals. Participants will also receive a FIT kit (collection tube, sampling paper, instructions, and return bag) for stool sampling. After returning the collection tube within the prescribed timeframe, screening staff will centrally collect and mail them to the Shandong Province Cancer Center for free testing. The results will be available for participants within 1 week.

Individuals who tested positive on the initial questionnaire will be recruited on site, whereas those with positive FIT results (but negative questionnaire) will be enrolled by GPs after the FIT results are available. Ultimately, eligible participants will be allocated to groups based on their community of residence.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

The random allocation sequence will be generated by an independent biostatistician who is not involved in participant recruitment, enrollment, or intervention delivery. Villages/communities within each county will be assigned to study arms using a computer-generated random number table.

Sequence generation: type of randomization {21b}

In one urban district and two rural counties in Shandong Province, 10–20 villages will be randomly chosen from each selected county/district. A simple randomization approach will be employed at the cluster level. Within each county/district, villages will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group, resulting in 20 villages per group across the three sites.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

The random allocation sequence will be stored in a secure, password-protected central database, accessible solely to the independent allocation coordinator. Group assignments will be released centrally at the time of allocation, ensuring that neither enrollment staff nor intervention implementers have prior access, thereby maintaining full allocation concealment and minimizing selection bias.

Implementation {23}

Personnel enrolling participants will receive the cluster-level allocation sequence only before initiating baseline assessments and delivering the intervention in each village/community. The master allocation list will be kept concealed by an independent biostatistician throughout the planning and village-enrollment phases to prevent selection bias.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and healthcare providers could not blind to assigned group. Outcome assessors, data managers, and analysts will remain blinded to group assignments throughout the trial.

How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

Blinding of outcome assessors, data managers, and data analysts will be ensured by using unique study IDs during data collection and analysis. All datasets provided for statistical analysis will encode group assignments with neutral labels (e.g., group A/B).

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

Unblinding of data collectors, managers, and analysts is not permissible in order to minimize subjective bias and ensure the objectivity of the data.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

Participants’ colonoscopy completion status will be obtained through unique identifiers from the county-hospital information systems. For individuals with no colonoscopy record, telephone follow-ups will be conducted to ascertain whether the procedure is performed at other hospitals.

Literacy, belief, and intention regarding CRC screening will be assessed via a self-developed questionnaire. This instrument will be developed by the research team to ensure alignment with the study aims and intervention.

CRC screening literacy comprises of 7 items, covering the basic knowledge of CRC, screening methods, benefits, and common misconceptions. Each item is answered with “yes,” “no,” and “not sure,” with one point for a correct answer and no points for “not sure” and incorrect answers. The total literacy score ranges from 0 to 7, and the higher the score, the higher the literacy level of the participants about CRC screening.

CRC screening belief will be assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (from “completely disagree” to “completely agree”). A higher score indicates a stronger belief.

Colonoscopy behavioral intention will be measured using a 5-point Likert scale (from “completely disagree” to “completely agree”). Those who agree or completely agree are defined as having colonoscopy intention.

The usability of the AICC model will be assessed via a mixed-methods approach entailing a questionnaire and semi-structured interviews. The questionnaire covers two sections with 9 entries: Evaluation of Clinician-Delivered Health Education (4 entries) and Evaluation of the Chatbot (5 entries). The Likert 5-point scale is used, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 on a scale of “completely disagree” to “completely agree.” The total usability score ranges from 9 to 45 points, with higher scores indicating greater perceived usability of the AICC intervention. The semi-structured interview guide will be used for interviews which ask both participants and intervention facilitators questions about their experiences with receiving or delivering the AICC intervention, perceived facilitators and barriers to undergoing colonoscopy or implementing the intervention, and suggestions of how (if anything) the intervention could be improved.

The costs of the two group interventions will be calculated, including expenses related to chatbot development and maintenance, personnel training, and implementation activities. Moreover, user engagement with chatbot will be obtained directly from the backend system.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

At the 1-month follow-up, survey data will be collected via home visits conducted by GPs and investigators. Colonoscopy completion status will be ascertained from the county-hospital information system, with telephone confirmation for participants without a record in the system.

Data management {26}

To ensure participant anonymity, the analytical dataset will be de-identified by removing all direct and indirect identifiers. We will retain data for a period of at least 5 years after the project has been completed.

Confidentiality {33}

Participant confidentiality will be strictly protected by strong personal identifiers separately with restricted access. All data will be pseudonymized for analysis, and only aggregated, non-identifiable results will be published. All researchers will complete confidentiality training prior to involvement.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

At baseline, characteristics of participants will be summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), and continuous variables as either mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), based on their distribution.

Effect evaluation

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis will be used in this trial where all randomized participants are analyzed. For the primary outcome analysis, between-group differences in colonoscopy uptake will be initially assessed using the Chi-square (χ2) test. Subsequently, a cluster-adjusted generalized estimating equations (GEE) models will be employed to quantify the intervention effect while accounting for clustering and potential confounders. Time-to-colonoscopy will be analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, with between-group comparison performed by the log-rank test. The intervention’s effect on the time-to-colonoscopy will be further quantified using a Cox proportional hazards regression model, adjusted for potential confounders.

For the secondary outcome analysis, between-group comparisons of literacy and belief scores (using independent t-tests) and behavioral intention (using the χ2 test) will be conducted. GEE models will assess intervention effects on secondary outcomes, adjusting for relevant covariates. All tests will be two-tailed, with a significance level (α) of 5%.

Economic evaluation

We will conduct a cost-effectiveness analysis of the AICC intervention versus usual specialty care from the perspective of healthcare system. The analysis will estimate the cost per lesion detected and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), defined as the cost per additional quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained. Costs will include screening program costs (from project records), direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, and indirect costs (sourced from the study in Shandong). QALYs will be calculated by applying health utility weights, derived from existing literature, according to patients based on their diagnostic states.

Process evaluation

Quantitative data on the usability of the AICC intervention and user engagement with chatbot, available only for the intervention group, will be summarized using descriptive statistics. As for the qualitative data, all interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. We will adopt the thematic framework approach for analysis, which will involve systematic coding of data, development of thematic categorization, and an in-depth synthesis of key themes to identify barriers and facilitators influencing intervention implementation.

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

All statistical analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, mandating the inclusion of all randomized participants from every cluster in their originally assigned groups.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

The approach to handling missing data will be determined by its extent. For datasets with minimal missing value (< 5%), a complete case analysis will be employed. When missing data is substantial (5–20%), multiple imputation will be applied, followed by sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {27d}

Subgroup analyses for the primary and secondary outcomes will be conducted to elucidate the effect heterogeneity across populations stratified by baseline characteristics, including region, sex, age, and education.

Interim analyses {28b}

Not applicable. All analyses will be conducted after completion of the study.

Protocol and statistical analysis plan {5}

The statistical analysis plan will be developed ahead of the analysis and publicly available. It will be reviewed by the trial steering committee and the data monitoring committee.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {3d}

Day-to-day operations and data coordination will be handled by the Coordinating Centre at the Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute. Overall trial oversight will be the responsibility of an independent Trial Steering Committee (TSC). The TSC, which includes the principal investigator, clinical specialists, and a biostatistician, will meet monthly to review trial progress and oversee key decision-making.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure {28a}

The data monitoring committee comprises three research assistants conducting on-site monitoring under the supervision of the project managers from Sun Yat-sen University and Fudan University. The committee, independent from sponsors and funders, will monitor data integrity and participants safety.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {29}

The research team will conduct monthly reviews of implementation progress. These reviews are internal quality control measures and are not independent from the investigators.

Protocol amendments {31}

Any change to the protocol constituting a substantial amendment will be submitted as a formal amendment to the Ethic Committee of the School of Public Health, Fudan University, for prior approval before implementation.

Dissemination policy {8}

Results of this study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed international publications.

Discussion

This two-arm cluster randomized trial aims to leverage a chatbot-assisted integrated care model to improve colonoscopy uptake among high-risk individuals in Chinese communities. Positive findings would provide a promising transformative solution for population-based CRC screening in resource-limited settings. It is potential to enhance CRC screening coverage across China’s diverse settings.

This study’s strength is its pragmatic trial of the AICC model. Although clinician recommendation is well documented, strong evidence in China is limited. Likewise, previous research on CRC screening chatbots have focused on technical feasibility rather than real-world integration [28], and GP-specialist collaboration has not been utilized in this context. Our AICC model converges these elements, integrating GPs with specialist services and using AI to support recommendations, offering a scalable solution within existing screening workflows.

We anticipate several challenges in implementing the AICC model. First, maintaining intervention fidelity across different communities may be difficult. We will implement standardized operating procedures and uniform training programs to ensure consistency in content and quality of recommendations. Second, although all participants use smartphones, low chatbot acceptability among the elderly remains a concern. We plan to iteratively refine the user interface based on users’ feedback and encourage involvement of family members to facilitate engagement.

Several limitations deserve attention. Given the multi-component intervention, which integrates recommendations from both specialists and GPs with chatbot assistance, it will not be feasible to disentangle the individual contribution of each component to improvements in colonoscopy uptake. In addition, as the study is conducted exclusively in Shandong Province, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. In this trial, we included three district/counties with diverse economic development status. Regions with different healthcare resources, economic conditions, and cultural characteristics may respond differently, underscoring the need for future validation in broader and more diverse settings.
