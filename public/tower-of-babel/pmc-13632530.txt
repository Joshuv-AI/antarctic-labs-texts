Phylogeny constrains and environment modulates glycoside hydrolase architecture
Authors: Alison E. Hughes, Andrea M. Castillo-Sevilla, Renaud Berlemont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Domain adjacency networks quantify multidomain GH organization at scale.•GH architectures remain highly conserved within bacterial genera.•Environmental filtering shapes multidomain GHs in cosmopolitan lineages.•Mammalian gut-associated microbes exhibit elevated multidomain GH ratios.•Phylogeny dominates while environment fine-tunes GH domain architecture.

Introduction

Glycoside hydrolases (GHs) are carbohydrate active enzymes (CAZymes) catalyzing the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds in poly- and oligo-saccharides. GHs support the microbial degradation of cellulose, hemicellulose, chitin, and other complex carbohydrates across ecosystems, from soil and marine ecosystems to the human gut. The short metabolizable sugars released through hydrolysis fuel heterotrophic growth and thus facilitate the overall biogeochemical cycles (Berlemont and Martiny, 2016; López-Mondéjar et al., 2022). The ubiquity of GHs in metagenomic datasets highlights the central function of GHs. Many GH families have been identified (Drula et al., 2022), and they frequently occur as multidomain proteins the GH domain is associated with some accessory domains (Nguyen et al., 2019). However, predicting the functions of GH sequences remains a major challenge (Zheng et al., 2023; Erdody et al., 2025) that is further amplified in multi-domain enzymes where the presence of accessory domains can affect the enzyme function (Boraston et al., 2004; Liberato et al., 2022).

Protein domains are compact evolutionary units that serve as building blocks that combine in various arrangements (i.e., protein architectures). The origin of multidomain proteins is rooted in gene duplication, fusion, and fission processes that enable the assembly of complex proteins from a limited set of ancestral components (Apic et al., 2001; Vogel et al., 2005; Berlemont et al., 2022). Generally, new protein architectures arise slowly as domain fusion and fission events are rare (Bashton and Chothia, 2007). As a result, most proteins exhibit deep conservation of their architecture across large evolutionary distances (Neduva and Russell, 2005; Worth et al., 2009; Forslund et al., 2019). GHs are frequently cited as examples of proteins in which accessory domains have undergone repeated gain, loss, and rearrangement during evolution, generating considerable architectural diversity across bacterial lineages (Boraston et al., 2004; Gilbert et al., 2013; Talamantes et al., 2016). However, the extent to which this apparent plasticity is constrained by phylogeny versus shaped by ecological adaptation has not been quantitatively evaluated. For example, Streptomyces, Bacteroides, and fibrolytic Bacillota have extensive strain-level variation in the architecture of GHs even when the genus-level distribution of core GH domains remains mostly unchanged (Berlemont and Martiny, 2015; Grondin et al., 2017; Sidar et al., 2020). In these clades, the dynamic assembly and reshuffling of accessory domains is thought to facilitate adaptation to heterogeneous, polysaccharide-rich environments where different glycans require distinct catalytic and targeting functions (Boraston et al., 2004; Gilbert et al., 2013). Such ecological specialization may also promote the emergence of ecotypes, i.e., genetically and functionally differentiated subpopulations adapted to specific environmental niches.

Important domains associated with GHs include carbohydrate binding modules (CBMs), fibronectins, and lectin domains that are typically linked to extracellular enzymes such as cellulases. Many bacteria also rely on S-layer homology (SLH) domains as molecular anchors to bind GH proteins to the surface of the cell (Mesnage et al., 2000; Kern et al., 2011) while others produce multienzyme complexes to concentrate GHs and other CAZymes (Artzi et al., 2016; David et al., 2025). All these strategies reflect distinct evolutionary responses to similar biophysical constraints, enhancing enzyme-substrate interactions while reducing enzyme loss due to diffusion (Guillén et al., 2010; Hervé et al., 2010; Várnai et al., 2014)

Thus, environments where diffusion is high (e.g., aquatic systems, gut) are expected to favor increased multidomain architectures in GHs, whereas environments with reduced diffusion (e.g., soils, biological crusts) may favor simpler GH enzymes. However, as the GH repertoires are strongly conserved among closely related taxa, often at the genus level in bacteria (Talamantes et al., 2016; Berlemont and Martiny, 2015, 2013), disentangling patterns of GH domain complexity across environments is complicated. In addition, sampling bias further complicates the ability to test such predictions. Metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) provide a way to directly evaluate the microbial genomic diversity from environmental samples. Here, we relied on the GEM catalog (Nayfach et al., 2020) rather than aggregating genomes from heterogeneous sources to minimize biases inherent to isolate genome databases (Berlemont and Martiny, 2013; Nayfach et al., 2019). GEM provides a standardized collection of MAGs generated from thousands of environmental samples. This consistency enables more robust comparisons of GH architectures across taxa. In addition, GEM includes harmonized environmental metadata and predefined ecosystem categories, facilitating cross-environment comparisons.

Thus, using GEM, we systematically investigated the multidomain architecture in the major GH families involved in the processing of structural polysaccharides (e.g., cellulose), oligosaccharides, and starch. We first hypothesized that microbial genera exhibit distinct but phylogenetically coherent multidomain protein architectures, and that these lineage-specific patterns can be identified by analyzing bootstrapped sets of environmental MAGs rather than individual genomes. To quantitatively compare multidomain organization across lineages, we represented GH architectures as domain–domain adjacency networks (DANs), where nodes correspond to protein domains and edges represent their ordered associations within proteins. We predicted that these networks should cluster tightly across bootstrapped replicates, reflecting low within-genus variability. In contrast, DANs from different genera should display distinct weighted architectures. Additionally, we predicted that unrelated microbial lineages occupying the same environmental categories would produce overlapping DANs highlighting convergent structural patterns in their GH architecture.

Finally, focusing on cosmopolitan genera, we hypothesized that various ecotypes exhibit divergent evolutionary trajectories in their multidomain protein architectures, driven by varying ecological niches. Such processes would lead to increased architectural plasticity, producing greater heterogeneity in DANs.

Despite the ecological importance of GHs, most large-scale studies have focused on the distribution and abundance of individual catalytic domains, while largely overlooking multidomain organization (Berlemont and Martiny, 2016; López-Mondéjar et al., 2022). However, multidomain organization can strongly influence enzyme targeting, substrate binding, retention, and catalytic efficiency (Boraston et al., 2004; Gilbert et al., 2013), suggesting that GH architecture itself may represent an important but overlooked dimension of microbial functional adaptation. Understanding how multidomain GH architectures vary across lineages and environments is therefore essential for moving beyond GH inventories toward a more mechanistic understanding of microbial carbohydrate degradation. Such knowledge may ultimately improve our ability to predict how microbial and enzymatic processes respond to environmental perturbations, including shifts in substrate availability, ecosystem structure, and climate-driven environmental changes.

Materials and methods

GH identification

Analyzed genomes were selected from the GEM to ensure consistency in MAG reconstruction and environmental annotation across samples (Nayfach et al., 2020). Protein sequences from GEM MAGs were retrieved from the JGI server (https://portal.nersc.gov/GEM/), and GH-containing proteins from nearly complete MAGs (i.e., ≥90% completeness) were reannotated using GeneHunt (Nguyen et al., 2019) in order to identify their predicted multidomain architecture. The analyzed proteins included GH families targeting oligosaccharides (GH1PF00232 and GH3PF00933) as well as extracellular GHs involved in major structural polysaccharides (i.e., GH5PF00150, 6 PF01346, 8PF01270, 9PF00759, 10PF00331, 11PF00457, 12PF01670, 18PF00704, 19PF00182, 30PF02055, 44PF12891, 45PF02015, and 48PF02011). In addition, we included the mostly amylolytic GH13PF00128 domain (Nguyen et al., 2018).

Proteins lacking additional annotated domains may nevertheless contain non-domain regions (e.g., linkers, intrinsically disordered regions, signal peptides, transmembrane helices, or currently uncharacterized structured regions), which are not represented as discrete Pfam domains.

Multidomain architecture

To obtain robust estimates of GH multidomain architecture, analyses were restricted to genera represented by at least 50 nearly complete MAGs. The repertoire of GHs and their associated domains was used to construct protein domain–domain adjacency networks (DANs). First, proteins of interest annotated with their ordered domains were converted into directed domain–domain adjacency pairs used to construct networks in which nodes represent domains and edges represent observed N→C associations. Single-domain proteins were also retained as isolated nodes to preserve information on both GH-domain abundance and isolated architectures.

For each network, we computed four structural metrics. Edge density represents the fraction of possible domain–domain links realized within a network. The clustering coefficient measures the tendency of domains to form local tripartite motifs. Degree-distribution entropy quantifies the diversity of interaction partners associated with each domain. And finally, the Louvain modularity identifies groups of domains with dense within-module associations and sparse between-module associations (Bornberg-Bauer et al., 2005; Bagowski et al., 2010; Newman, 2010).

Networks comparisons

As most genera exhibited highly uneven environmental distributions, with MAGs typically concentrated in one or a few dominant environmental categories (e.g., Ruminococcus occurring only in mammalian digestive systems), related environments were combined into 13 broader categories to standardize comparisons: animal digestive system (ADGS), city (CITY), freshwater (FW), human reproductive system (HRpS), human respiratory system (HRsS), human skin (HS), mammal digestive system (MDGS), marine water (MW), other aquatic (OAQ), plant (PL), sediment (SD), soil (SL), and wastewater (WW) (Table S2). In addition, as DAN from individual genomes were sensitive to idiosyncratic variation and assembly noise, and to more accurately compare multidomain architecture across lineages, we implemented a bootstrapped sampling strategy in which MAGs were randomly drawn from each genus to construct multiple replicate domain networks at the genus level. This approach standardized sampling effort across genera, stabilized estimates of network properties (i.e., density, clustering, modularity, entropy), and captured within-lineage variability through the generation of replicate networks.

To quantitatively compare the DANs, we evaluated both network complexity and weighted similarity across replicate samples. First, we computed a suite of graph-theoretic metrics listed above. However, because these metrics differ in scale and distribution, each was standardized across replicate samples using Z-score transformation, enabling direct comparison of effect sizes among metrics and across genera.

Next, to assess similarity among DANs, we compared networks using the quantitative Jaccard diversity index (i.e., Ružička) computed from the frequencies of directed domain-domain adjacencies. This weighted formulation emphasized recurrent multidomain architectures while reducing the influence of rare, idiosyncratic domain combinations. Pairwise similarities were then converted to dissimilarities (1 − Jaccard) and subjected to non-metric multidimensional scaling. NMDS was selected because domain-domain adjacency overlap produces sparse, non-Euclidean dissimilarity structures for which rank-based ordination is more appropriate compared to metric methods. Whereas the Z-score metrics quantify the structural complexity of each individual network, the quantitative Jaccard-NMDS framework captures similarity in domain-connectivity patterns among networks.

Finally, to quantitatively evaluate the relative contributions of taxonomy and environment to variation in DANs, genus-level weighted DAN profiles were compared using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) implemented in the R-vegan package. However, to avoid pseudoreplication arising from the bootstrap procedure, the 50 replicate DANs generated for each genus were first summarized into a single weighted genus-level consensus DAN profile by averaging edge frequencies across replicates. Pairwise dissimilarities were computed among consensus DAN profiles using the quantitative Jaccard index, preserving the frequencies of directed domain-domain adjacencies. Phylum-level taxonomy was used as a coarse proxy for phylogenetic affiliation, while the dominant aggregated environmental category represented the ecological predictor. Marginal tests (by = "margin") were used to estimate the unique contribution of each factor after accounting for the other. Because taxonomic and environmental distributions were partially confounded and unevenly represented across lineages, these analyses were interpreted as estimates of relative explanatory association rather than a strict partitioning of phylogenetic and environmental effects.

Cosmopolitan genera

Cosmopolitan genera were defined as lineages with nearly complete MAGs (i.e., ≥90% completeness), represented by at least 50 MAGs, and occuring across at least three of the aggregated environmental categories. For the cosmopolitan lineages, and for each GH family (GHx), we computed the MAG-specific GHx-frequency calculated as the number of GHx divided by the total number of protein encoding genes (i.e., [GHx] / [PEG]) and the MDGHx-ratio as the number of multidomain GHx over the total number of GHx (i.e., [MDGHx] / [GHx]). For example, in the genus Stenotrophomonas, the MAG # 3300012846.10 (96.63% complete) derived from the animal digestive system (ADGS), GH3-frequency was calculated using the number of GH3 (i.e., 5) and the total number of PEGs (i.e., 2,933), yielding 5/2933 ∼ 0.0017. The MDGH3-ratio was calculated using the number of MDGH3 genes (i.e., 4) and the total number of GH3 genes (i.e., 5), resulting in 4/5 = 0.80. These calculations were performed independently for each MAG and GHx combination.

Several taxa are reported using Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) placeholder designations (e.g., Ruminococcus C, Pseudomonas E, RC9) that reflect unresolved polyphyletic lineages or provisional genome-based classifications (Parks et al., 2026). These labels were retained to preserve consistency with the original GEM/GTDB taxonomic annotations used for MAG classification. Taxa derived from GTDB provisional classifications or unresolved genome-based lineages are indicated throughout the manuscript with an asterisk (*) following the taxon name.

Finally, the effect of aggregated environmental categories on the GHx-frequency and MDGHx-ratio within cosmopolitan genera was tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test [Metric ∼ Environment] implemented in R followed by Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise comparisons with adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg).

Statistics and data visualization

Statistics and data visualization were conducted in R (v4.5.2) using tidyverse (v2.0.0),data.table (v1.17.8), reshape2 (v1.4.4), FSA (v0.10.0), rcompanion (v2.5.2), stats (v4.5.2), ggplot2 (v4.0.0), patchwork (v1.3.2), and gplots (v3.2.0).

Results

Identification of GHs architecture

Focusing on 17,108 nearly complete MAGs (i.e., ≥90% completeness) from the GEM dataset with taxonomic identification up to the genus level, we identified 158,829 proteins with at least one GH domain of interest for a total of 302,630 protein domains derived from 437 domain families. More specifically, 56% of these proteins were multidomain, indicating that complex multidomain architecture is common among GH proteins (Supplementary data 1, Figure S1-S16). Regarding the proteins classified as containing only a single annotated GH domain, the annotated domain accounted for ∼71% of the protein sequence length on average. The remaining sequence likely reflects a combination of linker regions, intrinsically disordered segments, signal peptides, terminal tails, and potentially structured regions not currently represented by Pfam models. Consequently, although we cannot exclude the presence of currently unrecognized domains, incomplete domain annotation is unlikely to substantially affect the broad patterns of multidomain organization described here.

The most abundant GH domains were associated with starch and oligosaccharide degradation, particularly GH13 (n = 54,152), GH3 (n = 44,075), and GH1 (n = 16,453). Among GHs targeting structural polysaccharides, GH5, GH18, GH10, and GH9 were most prevalent, whereas GH6, GH11, GH12, GH44, GH45 and GH48 were comparatively rare. On average, 8.9 proteins of interest were identified per MAG. Although, across lineages, the GH-repertoire fluctuated broadly both in composition and abundance with some MAGs harboring up to 69 GH domains of interest in an unidentified Catenulispora (Ga0207671_10023321_5) derived from a rhizosphere sample.

Besides GH-specific subdomains (e.g., GH3_Cterminal domain, PF01915), the partner domains associated with GHs included many domains involved in substrate targeting and enzyme retention. Among others, we identified many CBMs such as CBM26PF16738 (n = 4137), CBM48PF02922 (n = 3915), CBM4_9PF02018 (n = 2934), and CBM2PF00553 (n = 2669). Many lectin/fibronectin-like domains such as FN3-like domain (PF14310, n = 25,459), FN3PF00041 (n = 1927), Ricin-type_beta-trefoil_lectin_domain-like (PF14200, n = 907), and Concanavalin_A-like_lectin_glucanases_superfamily (PF13385, n = 434) were also detected. Next, 3018 dockerinsPF00404 for the cellulosome assembly were also identified. In addition, we identified many domains involved in cell-surface anchoring such as 1939 Secretion_system_C-terminal_sorting_domain (PF18962), 1216 Bacterial_SH3_domain (PF08239), and 961 SLHPF00395.

We also identified a long list of less abundant domains including several domains of unknown function (e.g., DUF3459, DUF5011) and a few domains encoding other CAZymes (e.g., GT2PF00535, GH16PF00722) and other catalytic domains (e.g., SulfatasePF00884). Although collectively diverse, these domains each represented only a small fraction of the observed domain associations. Overall, when excluding the GH-specific subdomains, partner domains involved in substrate targeting and enzyme retention accounted for ∼61% of the accessory domains, suggesting that enzyme localization and retention are dominant architectural features of GHs.

GH-complement across genera

We next investigated the GH repertoire of individual MAGs (Fig. 1A,C) to evaluate the diversity, co-occurece, and arrangement of catalytic and accessory GH domains. Although these representations were useful for identifying rare architectures or unusual domain fusion, analyzing individual MAGs only captured a fraction of the lineage diversity and was influenced by genome completeness, strain-level variation, and stochastic genome-specific gene gain and loss. These genome-level architectures emphasized idiosyncratic differences rather than consistent structural patterns, and were poorly suited for systematic comparisons. To overcome these limitations, the GH-architectural signatures per bacterial genus was captured by integrating directional domain-domain transitions of multiple MAGs. Domain-domain adjacency networks (DANs) (Fig. 1B,C) were constructed to quantitatively describe domain organization. We then compared GH architechure across lineages using network metrics, including density, clustering, entropy, and modularity. This framework revealed genus-specific patterns that ranged from highly interconnected networks to more modular organizations. For example, Bacteroides (n = 76 MAGs) exhibited a highly interconnected network with few isolated components and consequently low modularity, indicating a broadly shared set of GH-associated domain combinations (Fig. 1B). Across lineages, although many domain-domain associations were observed in absolute terms, they occupied only a small fraction of the theoretically possible combinations (i.e., low density), indicating an low overall edge density (Fig. 2A). The genera with higher density (e.g., Ruminococcus E, UBA3125) had reduced numbers of mostly interconnected DoIs. Despite low density across genera, domain associations exhibited significant local clustering (aka triad formation) beyond the expected given networks size and degree distribution consistent with recurrent multi-domain motifs, especially in Bacillota (Fig. 2B). Next, entropy was computed per domain and summarized for each replicate DAN to assess whether domains tended to associate with a narrow or diverse set of partners relative to a degree-preserving null model. Positive Z-entropy reflected more promiscuous domain associations whereas negative Z-entropy reflected more specialized and constrained domain associations.

Near-zero entropy values suggested that the domain connectivity is largely explained by network degree and size alone. Entropy and clustering tended to vary inversely across DANs, consistent with the expectation that diversified domain connectivity limited the closure of local tripartite motifs, while constrained connectivity promoted tripartite motif formation (Fig. 2B,C). At the network level, Louvain modularity analysis revealed, a structured domain-community (i.e., a group of domains significantly interconnected compared to the network). Because modularity values were score-normalized, higher modularity indicated lineages with domain assemblies that are more internally cohesive and more clearly separated. Finally, across genera, the low variability of density, clustering, entropy, and modularity across bootstrapped DAN replicates suggested that both the GH repertoire and its patterns of association with accessory domains were conserved within most bacterial genera investigated, reflecting stable domain-architecture constraints.

Within genera, most replicate DANs clustered tightly, demonstrating high internal consistency and reproducibility of weighted architectural similarity. In contrast, networks from different lineages showed clear separation, reflecting lineage-specific patterns of domain connectivity. Notably, several lineages commonly associated with mammalian digestive systems (e.g., Ruminococcus, Parabacteroides) occupied a similar region of the ordination, whereas genera typically associated with marine, soil, or other open environments were positioned more distantly.

In addition, while the majority of genera displayed highly consistent DANs across bootstrap replicates, few (e.g., Lachnospira, Solobacterium, Brevundimonas, Capnocytophaga) exhibited pronounced dispersion in NMDS space (Fig. 3). The dispersion of the bootstrap replicates corresponded to variation in GH repertoires and accessory-domain associations among MAGs. More specifically, dispersed lineages (e.g., Solobacterium and Streptococcus) were associated with variable GH repertoir whereas thightly clustered genera (e.g., Ruminococcus C*) displayed stable GH repertoires (Figure S17). In addition, the prevalence of partner domains associated with the DoIs showed no consistent pattern across genera: it remained uniformly low in some lineages (e.g., Solobacterium), scaled with the number of DoIs in others (e.g., Ruminococcus C*), or fluctuated substantially among MAGs within some genera (e.g., Streptococcus)(Figure S17).

Finally, to evaluate the relative contributions of taxonomy and environment to DAN structure, we performed a PERMANOVA using phylum-level taxonomy and dominant environmental category as explanatory variables. After accounting for environmental effects, taxonomy explained 18.4% of the variation in weighted DAN structure (P < 0.001), whereas environment explained 10.5% after accounting for taxonomy (P = 0.013). These results highlight the predominance of phylogenetic constraints while indicating that environmental affiliation also contributes significantly to variation in multidomain GH architecture.

GH architecture in cosmopolitan genera

We next focused on testing the hypothesis that the variability among bootstrap replicates mirrored heterogeneity in genera derived from various environments. To this end, we identified genera spanning multiple environments and examined how the GH repertoire and the frequency of multidomain GHs varied across ecological contexts. Most genera displayed highly uneven environmental distributions, with MAGs typically concentrated in one or a few dominant habitats (e.g., Ruminococcus_C in mammalian digestive systems; Figures S1, S2). Consequently, many genus × environment combinations were represented by only a limited number of MAGs, reducing the ability to compare GH architectures across fine-scale environmental categories. Related environments were therefore aggregated into broader ecological classes (Table S2), increasing representation within categories while reducing ecological resolution. Using these aggregated categories, we identified 20 cosmopolitan genera represented across at least three environments (n = 2583 MAGs; Figure S18).

We next computed the GHx-frequency and MDGHx-ratio for each GHx family across MAGs and tested the effect of the environment on the GH repertoire using Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s post hoc tests. Although most cosmopolitan genera had a rich GH repertoire (#GH), few genera, such as Acinetobacter and Porphyromonas, exhibited reduced GH content which remained stable across aggregated environmental categories. For lineages with rich GH repertoire, the environmental categories had variable effects on the GH frequency (Table S3). For example, in Stenotrophomonas (n = 164 MAGs), the frequency of GH3, GH5, GH13, and GH18 was affected by the environment whereas GH8, GH9, GH10, and GH19 remained unaffected (Fig. 4A-B, Table S3). Specifically, GH3 and GH5-frequencies increased in ADGS, while GH13-frequency decreased in FW compared to HS, MW, and SL. Additionally, GH18-frequency was reduced in ADGS compared to most other environments. Interestingly, in some lineages, such as Bacteroides (Fig. 4B, Table S3), the GHx-frequency was not affected by the environment. Across cosmopolitan genera, the GHx-frequencies of generally abundant GH3, GH5, GH13, GH18, and GH30 were affected by the environment whereas GH8, GH9, GH10, and GH19 remained stable (Fig. 4B). Finally, we tested how the aggregated environment-categoires affect the prevalence of multidomain GHs by computing the MDGHx-ratio for each GHx family across lineages (Fig. 4C-D). The MDGHx-ratio was affected (p < 0.001) by the environment in eight genera (i.e., Brevundimonas, Corynebacterium, Cutibacterium, Enterobacter, Pseudomonas E*, RC9*, Stenotrophomonas, and Streptococcus). More specifically, in Stenotrophomonas, MDGH3, MDGH13, and MDGH18 ratios were found to be affected by the environment. For example, MDGH3-ratio was reduced in CITY relative to SL whereas MDGH13-ratio was increased in ADGS, CITY, and MW, while reduced in FW and HS. Furthermore, MDGH18-ratio was increased in ADGS compared to CITY and MW. In contrast, the MDGHx-ratios of GH5, GH8, GH10, and GH19 showed little variation. Interestingly, although the GHx-frequency remained stable in Bacteroides, MDGH9 and MDGH18-ratios fluctuated significantly across aggregated environmental categories, with increased ratios observed in ADGS and MDGS compared to other environments (Table S4). Across lineages, the architectures of GH3, GH13, and GH18 were most affected by the environment whereas GH8, GH10, and GH30, when found, remained unaffected by the environment (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

Across all analyzed genera, domain–domain adjacency networks (DANs) exhibited low variability across bootstrapped replicates. Because similarity was evaluated using weighted domain adjacencies, the resulting DAN comparisons primarily reflect the recurrent architectural signatures characteristic of each lineage rather than rare domain combinations observed in individual genomes. Overall, this indicates that both GH repertoires and their associated domain architectures are highly conserved within lineages. This pattern is consistent with the expectation that domain architectures evolve under strong structural and functional constraints, even in modular systems such as GHs (Apic et al., 2001; Bashton and Chothia, 2007; Forslund et al., 2019). Despite the known modularity of GHs, particularly in the gain and loss of accessory domains (Boraston et al., 2004; Gilbert et al., 2013; Talamantes et al., 2016), this variability operates within lineage-specific boundaries.

The low variance in network metrics (density, clustering, entropy, modularity), together with the tight clustering of replicate DANs in NMDS space, indicates that each genus maintains a stable architectural signature. These findings extend previous observations of phylogenetic conservation in GH repertoires (Berlemont and Martiny, 2015; Berlemont and Martiny, 2013) to the level of domain organization, demonstrating that GH multidomain architectures retain strong phylogenetic inertia (Neduva and Russell, 2005; Worth et al., 2009). Overall, DANs provide a complementary framework to traditional abundance-based analyses because they capture how catalytic and accessory domains are organized within proteins rather than simply quantifying domain content. By representing multidomain architectures as networks of domain associations, this approach enables comparisons of enzyme organization across lineages and environments and reveals structural patterns that would be overlooked by inventories of GH families alone.

DANs exhibited low edge density, indicating that only a limited subset of possible domain combinations is realized, consistent with strong selective filtering of viable architectures (Bashton and Chothia, 2007; Forslund et al., 2019). However, significant local clustering, particularly in Bacillota, reveals the recurrence of specific multidomain motifs. The inverse relationship between DAN entropy and clustering further highlights a trade-off between architectural flexibility and motif specialization (Bornberg-Bauer et al., 2005; Bagowski et al., 2010), with some genera exhibiting more promiscuous domain associations and others relying on limited but conserved assemblies. Notably, accessory domains involved in substrate targeting and enzyme retention (e.g., CBMs, FN3-like domains, dockerins, SLH) represented approximately 61% of all non-catalytic partner domains, underscoring that maintaining enzyme-substrate proximity is a dominant organizing principle of GH architecture (Boraston et al., 2004; Mesnage et al., 2000; Artzi et al., 2016; Guillén et al., 2010; Hervé et al., 2010; Várnai et al., 2013). Although our analyses did not directly measure diffusion constraints, this enrichment of binding, anchoring, and cell-surface retention domains is consistent with the hypothesis that multidomain organization has evolved, at least in part, to reduce enzyme loss and improve substrate capture in environments where diffusion may limit hydrolytic efficiency. Although accessory domains involved in substrate targeting and enzyme retention dominated the observed architectures, all identified domains, including DUFs, were incorporated into the DANs. These less abundant domains likely contribute to lineage-specific or rare multidomain architectures. However, because they occur infrequently relative to the dominant accessory domains, their influence on the overall network structure is limited. Indeed, the bootstrap strategy implemented here was specifically designed to emphasize recurrent architectural patterns while minimizing the impact of rare or idiosyncratic domain combinations, allowing robust comparisons of multidomain organization across bacterial lineages.

Interestingly, the GH families analyzed here differ in the breadth of their catalytic specificities. Families such as GH10 and GH11 are mostly xylanase, whereas others including GH8 and GH13 are polyspecific and target multiple substrates (Erdody et al., 2025). Altough, differences in multidomain organization could reflect differences in substrate specificity. previous analyses of biochemically characterized GHs indicate that the relationship between multidomain architecture and substrate specificity is not one-to-one. For example, GH9 proteins display considerable architectural diversity despite sharing predominantly cellulase activity, whereas GH8 proteins remain largely single-domain despite encompassing multiple specificities. Together, these observations suggest that accessory-domain evolution doesn’t mirror the GH specificity (Erdody et al., 2025). Therefore, although catalytic specialization may contribute to architectural evolution within individual GH families, it is unlikely to account for the strong lineage-level conservation of multidomain organization observed across the diverse GH families analyzed here. Yet, experimental studies of individual GHs have demonstrated that accessory domains can substantially influence enzyme performance without altering catalytic chemistry. For example, CBMs frequently enhance degradation of insoluble polysaccharides by increasing substrate affinity and prolonging enzyme-substrate interactions, whereas cell-surface anchoring domains (e.g., SLH) and dockerins promote enzyme retention and organization into multienzyme complexes (Boraston et al., 2004; Gilbert et al., 2013; Mesnage et al., 2000; Kern et al., 2011; Artzi et al., 2016; David et al., 2025; Guillén et al., 2010; Hervé et al., 2010; Várnai et al., 2014). These studies establish the functional importance of specific accessory domains at the enzyme level. Our analyses complement these biochemical observations by showing that such domains are not randomly distributed across microbial genomes but instead follow lineage-specific organizational patterns that are themselves modulated by environmental context.

Beyond phylogenetic constraints, we tested whether environmental pressures drive convergence in GH architecture. NMDS ordination revealed qualitative clustering of some DANs associated with similar environments, particularly among lineages from mammalian digestive systems, suggesting that shared ecological conditions can influence domain connectivity patterns (Berlemont and Martiny, 2016; López-Mondéjar et al., 2022). However, this convergence was not dominant: the primary structure of the ordination remained phylogenetically driven, and network complexity metrics did not show consistent convergence across environments. This conclusion is further supported by PERMANOVA, which attributed a larger proportion of variation in weighted DAN structure to taxonomic affiliation than to environmental category. Although taxonomic affiliation and environment are not completely independent across the available dataset, both factors retained significant unique associations with DAN topology, supporting a hierarchical model in which lineage establishes the principal architectural framework while environment modulates it.

A key result of this study is the decoupling between GH abundance and architecture. Across cosmopolitan genera, environmental effects on GHx-frequency were inconsistent and lineage-dependent, with some genera showing significant variation and others remaining stable. This is consistent with previous observations that GH gene content is largely phylogenetically structured (Berlemont and Martiny, 2016, 2013; López-Mondéjar et al., 2022). In contrast, multidomain architecture (MDGHx-ratio) showed clearer and more consistent environmental sensitivity, particularly for GH3, GH13, and GH18 families. For example, in Stenotrophomonas, the multidomain organization of several GH families varied significantly across environments, and in Bacteroides, architectural variation was observed even when GHx-frequency remained unchanged. This suggests that environmental selection may act differently on enzyme frequency and protein organization, with architecture representing a more responsive dimension of functional adaptation (Berlemont et al., 2022; Gilbert et al., 2013). While these patterns are consistent with ecotype differentiation and the enrichment of domains involved in enzyme retention is consistent with diffusion-related constraints, our data do not directly resolve which environmental variables drive these patterns, suggesting that multiple ecological mechanisms may be involved. Specifically, an increase in accessory binding domains as a result of the environment, may be reflective of diffusion dynamics, substrate availability (i.e., composition and abundance), and community structure.

We further observed that while most genera exhibit stable DAN structure, a subset—particularly cosmopolitan lineages—display increased dispersion in NMDS space, indicating greater within-genus variability in domain connectivity. The increased dispersion observed in some cosmopolitan genera is consistent with the emergence of ecotypes that retain a conserved core GH repertoire while diverging in accessory-domain composition. Such diversification may facilitate adaptation to local substrate landscapes without requiring major changes in catalytic potential. In these genera, both GH frequency and multidomain architecture vary across environments, demonstrating that GH repertoires are not fixed but shift across ecological contexts. However, the direction of causality remains unresolved. One possibility is that exposure to diverse environments promotes diversification of GH architectures. Alternatively, genera with inherently flexible GH repertoires may be pre-adapted to occupy a broader range of ecological niches. These hypotheses are consistent with broader theories linking genomic flexibility to ecological breadth (Talamantes et al., 2016; Nayfach et al., 2019), but our results do not distinguish cause from consequence.

While these results reveal consistent patterns in GH architecture across lineages and environments, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although GEM reduces biases associated with isolate genome databases through standardized assembly and environmental annotation, the dataset remains partially skewed toward human-associated and well-sampled environments, potentially limiting the generality of the observed patterns across underrepresented ecosystems (Nayfach et al., 2020, 2019). Beyond GEM, the use of MAGs in metagenomic alayses is affected by assembly bias, with a reduced representation of environmentally hard to assemble genomes (e.g., Streptomyces). Second, our analysis focuses on a subset of well-characterized GH families, primarily those involved in the degradation of common polysaccharides, and may therefore overlook less abundant or poorly annotated GHs that could contribute to alternative degradation strategies. Third, multidomain architectures were inferred from MAG-derived protein predictions, which may be affected by assembly fragmentation or annotation uncertainty, potentially impacting domain adjacency inference. Finally, while DAN-based metrics capture structural properties of domain organization, they do not directly reflect enzyme activity, regulation, or in situ functional performance. As such, the patterns described here should be interpreted as proxies of functional potential rather than enzymatic activities.

Together, these findings support a hierarchical model in which phylogeny establishes a conserved framework of GH multidomain organization (Berlemont and Martiny, 2015, 2013), while environmental pressures fine-tune specific architectures. More broadly, our results suggest that protein domain architecture represents an additional dimension of microbial functional potential that complements gene-content analyses (Berlemont and Martiny, 2016; López-Mondéjar et al., 2022). Although the functional consequences of individual domain combinations remain to be experimentally validated, incorporating multidomain organization into comparative studies may improve our understanding of how microbial carbohydrate-degrading capabilities evolve across lineages and environments. More broadly, the DAN-based framework provides a scalable approach to quantify enzyme modularity, detect evolutionary constraints, and link molecular architecture to ecological function (Bornberg-Bauer et al., 2005; Newman, 2010).

Although numerous biochemical studies have established functional roles for individual accessory domains, systematic links between multidomain architecture and enzyme performance across diverse GH families remain limited. Future work integrating comparative genomics with biochemical characterization will be essential to determine how specific domain combinations influence substrate utilization, enzyme retention, and overall performance under ecologically relevant conditions.
