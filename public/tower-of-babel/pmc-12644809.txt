Inherent surface heterogeneity of carbon black by one-shot structure analysis using hybrid reverse molecular dynamics simulation
Authors: Takumi Watanabe, Rin Chinen, Rieko Kobayashi, Takuya Maie, Yuji Kubota, Jun-ichi Ozaki, Tomonori Ohba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnalyses of the surface properties of carbon materials have been limited despite their importance for adsorption, catalysis, and electrochemical reactions. In this study, the bulk and surface structures of carbon black were comprehensively evaluated by one-shot structure analysis using the hybrid reverse molecular dynamics (HRMD) simulations, in which X-ray or electron diffraction was evaluated by the simulations. The one-shot structure analysis agreed with observations obtained by other experimental methods such as transmission electron microscopy, Raman scattering spectra, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The carbon black surface had less crystallinity and stability than its bulk with smaller graphene unit sizes. Peeling carbon black with an electron beam exposed the bulk structure to the surface, which was immediately reconstructed into structural defects. These findings highlight the importance of understanding the surface-layer structures of carbon materials to facilitate applications in catalysis and sensing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25349-x.Subject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The interfaces between gases and solids, liquids and solids, and gases and liquids play a critical role in adsorption and sensing1,2, catalytic reactions3,4, and electrochemical reactions5,6. Crystalline solids have a periodic arrangement, but this periodicity is disrupted at or near their surfaces owing to the rearrangement of their atomic structure. This was attributed to minimizing the energy loss associated with surface formation, which is known as surface relaxation and reconstruction. Metal surfaces exhibit considerable levels of surface relaxation and reconstruction. For instance, the Cu, Au, Pt, and TiO2 surfaces exhibited an inward relaxation of less than 1% within the first several layers7,8.

Carbon materials have an amorphous and complex structure despite their simple components, and understanding carbon surfaces is thus insufficient. The density functional theory calculations of graphite predicted that the surface relaxation and reconstruction were hardly observed without corrugation of the basal plane9, while those appeared owing to dangling bonds at their edges10. Armchair edges should be more contracted on the surface due to their higher stability. Meanwhile, surface reconstruction depresses the surface energy of graphene sheets11 and graphitizes diamond surfaces12,13. The properties of nanocarbons are dominated by the presence of oxygen groups and structural defects such as non-six-membered rings on their surface because of their high surface-to-volume ratio14. Thus, surface properties are essential for understanding multiple reaction mechanisms and advancing surface sciences. However, analyses of carbon materials with heterogeneous structures, especially their surfaces, are limited.

In general, carbon structure analyses have been conducted through X-ray diffraction (XRD), Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Raman scattering spectroscopy. However, those techniques still find it hard to reveal the comprehensive and detailed structure of carbons. Simulation-assisted carbon structure analysis is a technique to reveal carbon structure at the molecular level. Rigden and Newport demonstrated the three-dimensional atomic structure of amorphous diamond-like carbon using the reverse Monte Carlo simulation, which combined X-ray and neutron diffraction with simulated diffraction15. The hybrid reverse Monte Carlo simulation for evaluating carbon structures was proposed by combining the conventional reverse Monte Carlo method with an energy-based constraint, which improves the stability of the carbon structure16,17. Those constructed models succeeded in reproducing nano-structure features of amorphous-like carbon, while it was hard to demonstrate large-scale structures. A hybrid reverse molecular dynamics (HRMD) simulation has the advantage of demonstrating large-scale structures, such as graphitic structures18. Carbon black (CB) is a representative carbon material with a heterogeneous structure for which the surface is amorphous and the bulk is graphitic19. Both the surface and bulk structure analyses are thus inevitable in which XRD and electron diffraction (ED) become powerful tools despite similar observational techniques. The X-ray and electron beams in XRD and ED differ in the penetration depth, which can be defined as the reciprocal of the mass attenuation coefficient; under the assumption that CB has a density of 2.0 g cm− 3, X-rays have a penetration depth of 1.2 mm at CuKα20, while electron beams have a penetration depth of 20 μm at 120 kV21. Consequently, XRD patterns observe the bulk, while ED patterns focus on the surface layer and edges. In this study, we propose a new one-shot structure analysis method using the HRMD simulation18 and investigate the differences between CB bulk and surface structures by combining XRD and ED observations associated with HRMD simulations.

Results

Structural observations

Figure 1 shows the structural evaluation of three types of CB with differing levels of crystallinity: low-crystalline CB, medium-crystalline CB, and high-crystalline CB. TEM showed that the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs had particle sizes of 31 ± 12, 36 ± 13, and 80 ± 19 nm, respectively (Figs. S1a–S1c). CBs had a graphitic bulk region surrounded by an amorphous surface region (Fig. 1a). The CBs had distinct XRD peaks at scattering factors s (= 4π sin (θ)/λ) of 18, 30, 36, and 51–53 nm− 1 attributed to the (002), (10), (004), and (11) diffraction peaks, respectively (Fig. 1b, d). The (002), (10), and (11) XRD peaks were used to determine the average crystallite sizes of the low-, medium, and high-crystalline CBs, which were 1.3 ± 0.2, 3.3 ± 0.3, and 12.1 ± 1.6 nm, respectively (Table S1). Thus, the high-crystalline CB was confirmed to have the highest crystallinity, followed by the medium- and low-crystalline CBs, which agreed with the TEM images (Fig. 1e–g). The wrinkled graphene was observed in the low-crystalline CB image (Fig. 1e), whereas the planar graphene structure was obviously seen in the high-crystalline CB image (Fig. 1g). In the medium-crystalline CB image (Fig. 1f), the graphene plane was smaller than that of the high-crystalline CB, while it was more planar than that of the low-crystalline CB. The ED patterns (Fig. 1b and d) were weaker and broader than the XRD patterns, which indicates that the surfaces were less crystalline. The average crystallite sizes for the surfaces of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs were 1.1 ± 0.1, 1.8 ± 0.2, and 2.2 ± 0.3 nm, respectively. The reduced crystallite sizes in the ED patterns relative to the XRD patterns can be attributed to differences in the surface structures. The differences were most apparent for the high-crystalline CB, whereas there was almost no change for the low-crystalline CB. The XRD and ED patterns indicated nearly no differences in the interlayer distance; the XRD patterns showed that the interlayer distances for the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs were 0.36, 0.35, and 0.34 nm, respectively. In contrast, the ED patterns indicated that the interlayer distances were 0.37, 0.35, and 0.35 nm, respectively, which were larger than those obtained from the XRDs.

Characterization of the surface and bulk structures

The Fourier transform was applied to the experimentally obtained XRD and ED patterns to obtain electron radial distribution functions (RDFs), which were used to get the simulated RDFs calculated by HRMD simulations. As shown in Fig. 2, the simulated RDFs agreed well with the experimental electron RDFs. Slight differences in the experiments and the simulations at the nearest- and second-nearest neighbor peaks and their valleys were attributed to the structural model used in this work, which assumed the fundamental unit of carbon to be a benzene molecule. Despite the disadvantage of using the benzene unit in this work, large-scale graphene/graphite structures could be easily treated during the HRMD simulations. In addition, a CB is also mostly composed of a sp2 honeycomb carbon structure, and a benzene unit is adaptable to express the graphitic structure of a CB. The other peaks mostly coincided in the experiments and simulations. The electron RDFs from the ED patterns (Fig. 2d, f) were more quickly attenuated than those from the XRD patterns (Fig. 2a, c), which indicated a shorter correlation length between carbon atoms. In other words, the surface region had lower crystallinity than the bulk region. For the low-crystalline CB, the electron RDF was immediately attenuated beyond the C–C distance of 1 nm. In contrast, the correlation between carbon atoms persisted up to 2 nm in the medium- and high-crystalline CBs. The insets in Fig. 2 (see also Fig. S3) show the graphene structures obtained from HRMD simulations, which provide large-scale carbon structures in contrast with Monte Carlo simulations and derivative techniques16,17,22. The apparent graphene-like structures differed from previous Monte Carlo-based analyses22,23. The most significant amount of crystalline graphene was observed in the high-crystalline CB. The ED patterns similarly indicated defective graphene structures in the CBs.

Correlation between carbon atoms evaluated from experimental and simulated RDFs. (a–c) RDFs obtained from XRD patterns and HRMD simulations (black and colored curves, respectively). (d–f) RDFs obtained from ED patterns and HRMD simulations (black and colored curves). Insets show snapshots of the graphene structures obtained from HRMD simulations based on the XRD and ED patterns. The bulk structures observed in the XRD patterns have higher crystallinity than the surface structures observed in the ED patterns.

The simulated in-plane crystallite sizes and the graphene size obtained from HRMD simulations were compared with the experimental La of the (10) and (11) diffraction peaks in the XRDs and EDs, and the graphene sizes from the TEM images, respectively (Fig. 3). The simulated in-plane crystallite size was assessed from a region of relative carbon stability exceeding 80%. The in-plane crystallite size distributions were well fit to the Gaussian-like distributions, although slight differences were observed, especially for the XRD pattern of the high-crystalline CB, owing to the limitation of the graphene number. On average, the simulated in-plane crystallite sizes agreed well with the experimental in-plane crystallite sizes (Fig. 3a–c). The simulated in-plane crystallite sizes based on the XRD and ED patterns were 1.3 ± 0.8 and 1.1 ± 0.5 nm, respectively, for the low-crystalline CB (Fig. 3a), 3.4 ± 1.3 and 1.4 ± 0.7 nm, respectively, for the medium-crystalline CB (Fig. 3b), and 12 ± 3 nm and 1.5 ± 1.0 nm, respectively, for the high-crystalline CB (Fig. 3c). Those values agreed well with the experimental in-plane crystallite sizes (depicted in black lines in Fig. 3a–c) observed from the (10) and (11) peaks, shown in Table S1, although only the averaged ones were obtained from the experiments. The simulated in-plane crystallite sizes based on the XRD patterns were larger with broader distributions than those based on the ED patterns, which can be attributed to the increased crystallinity in the bulk region. The in-plane crystallite sizes based on the ED patterns were similar regardless of the crystallinity of the bulk region, which is reasonable because they reflect the characteristics of the less crystalline surface region. Thus, the crystallite sizes showed apparent heterogeneity between the bulk and surface regions, which agrees with previous reports24–27. The simulated graphene sizes L (Fig. 3d–f) obtained from the snapshots corresponded with the graphene sizes observed in the TEM images (Fig. S3), although slight discrepancies were observed in the low-crystalline CB owing to the difficulty of size assessment in the TEM images. The graphene sizes in the XRD-based HRMD simulation and TEM images were 6.6 ± 2.6 and 4.9 ± 2.0 nm, respectively, for the low-crystalline CB, 8.0 ± 2.9 and 7.0 ± 2.7 nm, respectively, for the medium-crystalline CB, and 14.0 ± 4.4 and 12.0 ± 4.4 nm, respectively, for the high-crystalline CB. Conversely, the graphene sizes of all CBs in the ED-based HRMD simulation and TEM images of the surface were 3.5 ± 2.0 and 3.3 ± 1.4 nm, respectively, smaller than for the bulk region. These results indicate that the crystallinity of the surface region was surprisingly independent of the CB crystallinity.

(a–c) In-plane crystallite sizes calculated from XRD (dashed curves and open symbols) and ED patterns (solid curves and filled symbols) of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs compared with experimental crystallite sizes La obtained from (10) and (11) peaks of the XRD and ED patterns (black dashed and solid lines, respectively). (d–f) Graphene sizes L calculated from HRMD simulations based on the XRD patterns (dashed curves and open symbols) and ED patterns (solid curves and filled symbols) compared with graphene sizes evaluated from TEM images of the bulk region (black dashed curves and open symbols) and surface region (black solid curves and filled symbols).

The relative stability of the CBs, which was defined as the rate of stability in comparison with the ideal graphite stabilized energy, was calculated from snapshots of the HRMD simulations (Fig. S4). The stability analyses relate to the performance of catalytic reactivity, sensing, and electrochemical activity; that is, an unstable surface leads to higher reactivity and activity. The average stabilities obtained from the XRD- and ED-based simulations were 66% and 59%, respectively, for the low-crystalline CB, 74% and 68%, respectively, for the medium-crystalline CB, and 77% and 73%, respectively, for the high-crystalline CB, respectively. The stability was always higher in the XRD-based simulations than in the ED-based simulations, which indicates that the surface region was less stable than the bulk region for all CBs because of graphene bending, distortion, and edges. The armchair and zigzag edges had 75% and 63% stabilities, respectively, relative to ideal graphene. Thus, the graphene defects were presumed to be less stable than 80%. The stability analyses based on the XRD and ED patterns indicated that the structural defect ratios were 20% and 22%, respectively, for the low-crystalline CB, 18% and 18%, respectively, for the medium-crystalline CB, and 8% and 11%, respectively, for the high-crystalline CB. The surface region had slightly more structural defects than the bulk region for all CBs, which agrees with experimental observations of the surface C–C and C–O components obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in Fig. 4a–c. The carbon amounts forming sp3-bonding and surface oxygen groups were 35%, 33%, and 25% for the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs, respectively, which showed good agreement with the structural defects predicted by the HRMD simulations. The Raman scattering spectroscopy also supported these results as the ID/IG ratios were 0.9, 1.0, and 0.3 for the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs, respectively (Fig. S4d). Therefore, the one-shot structure analysis of the HRMD simulation with a single XRD/ED pattern agreed with multiple experimental analysis results, offering a promising approach that may improve the efficiency of structure analyses, particularly for heterogeneous carbons.

Preparation of fresh surfaces and structural observation

The above HRMD simulations indicated that the surface region was more amorphous with structural defects leading to a less-stabilized structure than the bulk region for all CBs. Electron beam irradiation was used to remove the graphene layer on the CB surface and create a fresh surface. Although the TEM images showed little difference before and after electron beam irradiation, the particle size decreased from 31 ± 12 to 29 ± 7 nm for the low-crystalline CB, 36 ± 13 to 31 ± 9 nm for the medium-crystalline CB, and 80 ± 19 to 64 ± 14 nm for the high-crystalline CB (Fig. S1). These results indicate that the electron beam irradiation peeled the particles and shifted the particle size distributions to smaller sizes. Figure 5 shows the ED patterns of the CBs before and after electron beam irradiation, which were mainly unchanged except for the high-crystalline CB. In this case, the ED pattern of the fresh surface exhibited obvious 004 and 11 peaks, indicating that the crystalline structure of the bulk region was exposed to the surface and maintained. However, the fresh surfaces of the low- and medium-crystalline CBs tended to revert toward a less crystalline structure just after electron beam irradiation than their XRD patterns in the bulk regions (Figs. 1) and S1.

Surface structure changes of CBs by electron beam treatment. (a-c) Comparison of ED patterns between the pristine and electron-beam-treated three CBs (black and colored curves). The ED pattern of high-crystalline CB differed above 30 nm− 1. The diffraction peaks of (002), (10), (004), and (11) are assigned in (c).

Figure 6 shows structural observations of the fresh surface for the high-crystalline CB. HRMD simulations were performed to obtain the in-plane crystallite size, graphene size, and stability distribution after electron beam irradiation (Fig. S5). Here, the RDF patterns in the experiment and the HRMD simulation coincided with each other, except for slightly different first- and second peaks due to the limitation of the simulated structure model. The sharp peaks in the electron RDF after irradiation suggest a more crystalline structure in the surface region (Fig. 6a). Both the average in-plane crystallite size and graphene size increased from 1.5 to 1.8 nm and 3.9 to 5.1 nm, respectively, after irradiation (Fig. 6b, c). This may be attributed to the 10 and 11 peaks (Fig. 5c). The stability also increased from 73% to 75%, while the total amount of defects decreased from 11% to 9% (Fig. 6d). These results indicate that the crystalline structure of the bulk region was maintained by some degree even after being exposed to the surface by electron beam irradiation.

(a) Electron RDFs, (b) in-plane crystallite size La distributions, (c) graphene size L distributions, and (d) stability distributions before irradiation (black) and after irradiation (green).

Discussion

We demonstrated that XRD- and ED-based HRMD simulations were useful as one-shot structure analyses to reveal the multiple physical structures of CBs. The structure analyses showed that the interlayer distance was unchanged, while the average crystallite sizes of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs were 1.3 ± 0.2, 3.3 ± 0.3 nm, and 12.1 ± 1.6, respectively, in the bulk region and 1.1 ± 0.1, 1.8 ± 0.2, and 2.2 ± 0.3 nm, respectively, in the surface region indicating that the latter was less crystalline. The electron RDFs of the bulk region had a short-range correlation until 1 nm for the low-crystalline CB and a long-range correlation until 2 nm for the medium- and high-crystalline CBs. The electron RDFs of the surface region were more quickly attenuated, indicating less crystallinity than in the bulk region. The in-plane crystallite and graphene sizes of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs demonstrated good agreement between the experimental results and HRMD simulations. The in-plane crystallite sizes of the high-crystalline CB were 12 ± 3 nm in the bulk region and 1.5 ± 1.0 nm in the surface region, indicating substantial structural differences. The graphene sizes demonstrated a similar trend for the bulk and surface regions, which agreed well with TEM images. Therefore, the HRMD simulations with the experimental diffraction data revealed the comprehensive structures of the CBs. The differences in stability in the bulk and surface regions support the observations of the crystallinity. The surface region consistently showed lower crystallinity than the bulk region, which can be attributed to surface reconstruction and the formation of defects induced by air exposure to minimize surface energy11, which increased the sp3-bonding rate and oxygen functional groups28. Electron beam irradiation exposed the fresh surface, but almost immediately reverted to an amorphous structure in the low- and medium-crystalline CBs. In contrast, the fresh surface of the high-crystalline CB retained a relatively crystalline structure. The electron beam irradiation caused the exposed bulk region to undergo partial atomic rearrangement, forming an amorphous structure including sp3 bonds.

Structure evaluation of carbon materials is normally hard, because carbon materials have a less crystalline structure. Various techniques, including XRD, TEM, and Raman scattering spectroscopy, have been used to reveal the complex structure. The HRMD simulation associated with X-ray and electron diffractions could be a powerful tool to evaluate the detailed structure involving the analysis data from experimental techniques. The HRMD technique has the potential to be an easier way to analyze the structure than the normal experimental analysis, as well as being available for various materials. The bulk and surface structures of CB were comprehensively evaluated by one-shot structure analysis using the HRMD simulations, and it was proposed that the CB surfaces had more structural defects than the internal structure. Understanding of the surface structure is useful for various applications in surface chemistry and reactions.

Methods

Experiment

The CBs #32b and #4010b were obtained from Mitsubishi Chemical Co., Tokyo, Japan. Acetylene black was obtained from Denka Co., Tokyo, Japan. The CBs #32b, acetylene black, #4010b were named as the low-crystalline CB, medium-crystalline CB, and high-crystalline CB, respectively. The CBs were used as they are in this work. TEM and ED (JEM-2100 F, JEOL Co., Tokyo, Japan) were performed at 120 kV and below 10− 5 Pa, which was a moderate condition for observing carbon materials without any damage to the structures. The samples were dispersed in ethanol by ultrasonication and dropped on a copper grid prior to the TEM measurement. Forty ED patterns of each CB were used to obtain the averaged pattern. XRD and small-angle X-ray scattering were performed using CuKα radiation with a wavelength of λ = 0.1541 nm at 40 kV and 40 mA (SmartLab, Rigaku Co., Tokyo, Japan). The interlayer distance and in-plane crystallite size La were evaluated by using the Scherrer equation with Lorentzian curve fitting of the (002), and (10) and (11) diffraction peaks, respectively (Scherrer constant = 1.05)29. The particle size was measured from the TEM images and calculated from a hard-sphere model fitting of the small-angle X-ray scattering30,31. The graphene size L was evaluated from 60 to 80 plateaus in the TEM images. Raman scattering spectra on the three spots of each CB were obtained using an Nd: YAG laser at a power of 7.0 mW (NRS-3000; JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). Electron beam irradiation was performed using the TEM apparatus (JEM-2100 F, JEOL Co., Tokyo, Japan). The samples were irradiated at 120 kV over 8 h. Carboxyl groups were considered to indicate the edge sites of the CBs and were observed by deconvolution of the C1s XPS peaks obtained at 10 mA and 10 kV Mg Kα radiation (JPS-9030, JEOL Co., Tokyo, Japan) using Gaussian–Lorenzian mixed functions. The He buoyancy measurements were used to determine the true densities of the CBs at 300 K (BELLSORP MAX, MicrotracBEL Co., Osaka, Japan). The true densities of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs were 1.2, 1.5, and 1.8 g cm− 3, respectively.

Simulation

HRMD simulations were conducted by using a cubic simulation box with dimensions of 5.0 × 5.0 × 5.0 nm³ under environment-dependent interatomic potential in an MD scheme32,33. The total pseudo-calculation time was more than 40 ps for time steps of 0.1–0.01 fs. The temperature was determined to be set to 0.1 K, because the graphene structure was maintained and moved enough within the calculation time, and the MD simulation of graphene was previously conducted from 0.1 to 1000 K34. The true carbon densities of the low-, medium-, and high-crystalline CBs were controlled at 1.2 ± 0.2, 1.5 ± 0.2, and 1.8 ± 0.2 g cm−3, respectively. The detailed algorithm and conditions of the HRMD simulations are presented in the supporting information and were taken from our previous study18. The graphene size was estimated directly from more than 80 graphene in the snapshots. The simulated stabilities of the CBs were calculated from the sum of all interaction potentials. The simulated crystallite size was defined as a region with more than 80% of the stability of ideal graphite. The structural defect rate was defined as the rate of carbon atoms bonding to fewer than two carbons within 0.2 nm.
