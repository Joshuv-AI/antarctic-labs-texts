Subtle presentation of complete uterine rupture in a scarred uterus: a case report
Authors: Reem Yousef, Razan Kasem, Ola Khalouf, Shifaa Al Suleiman, Osama Haj Osman, Ashraf Olabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Uterine rupture is a rare but serious obstetric complication, most often associated with a scarred uterus following cesarean delivery. Although uterine rupture classically presents with acute abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and fetal distress, atypical manifestations may delay recognition and heighten maternal and fetal risk.Case presentation:We report a 25-year-old gravida 2 para 1 woman with a prior lower-segment cesarean section who presented with a 14-hour history of vague abdominal discomfort and minimal vaginal bleeding, accompanied by cessation of fetal movements. Ultrasonography demonstrated placental remnants within the uterus and a deceased fetus in the abdominal cavity, consistent with complete uterine rupture. Hemodynamic and laboratory parameters remained stable. Emergency laparotomy confirmed rupture of the previous cesarean scar with extrusion of the fetus and placenta. The uterine defect was repaired, and the patient recovered uneventfully.Discussion:Uterine rupture may present with atypical findings such as mild abdominal pain or scant bleeding, which can complicate diagnosis. Evaluation should consider differential diagnoses including placenta previa, placental abruption, or spontaneous abortion. Ultrasonography can reveal uterine wall disruption, free intraperitoneal fluid, or extrauterine fetal parts, thereby aiding confirmation in stable patients. Prompt laparotomy with uterine repair or hysterectomy remains the cornerstone of management, underscoring the need for vigilance even in hemodynamically stable presentations.Conclusion:This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of uterine rupture in hemodynamically stable patients with atypical symptoms and highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion while ensuring timely surgical intervention to mitigate maternal morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Uterine rupture is a rare but serious obstetric complication characterized by a partial or complete disruption of the uterine wall. It may occur spontaneously during pregnancy or labor, with or without extrusion of the fetus into the abdominal cavity. This event can develop in either an unscarred uterus or a previously scarred one, most commonly following surgical procedures such as cesarean section, myomectomy, or reconstructive uterine surgery[1]. A complete uterine rupture is defined as a full-thickness tear involving all layers of the uterine wall, including the overlying serosa (peritoneum), and may result in the expulsion of the fetus and placenta into the abdominal cavity[2]. Although uterine rupture typically presents with acute and evident clinical manifestations, it can occasionally occur with subtle or nonspecific symptoms, making timely diagnosis difficult[3]. In general, uterine rupture is considered an obstetric emergency that carries significant maternal and fetal risks, requiring prompt diagnosis and immediate surgical intervention.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old gravida 2, para 1 woman presented to the obstetrics and gynecology clinic with a 14-hour history of abdominal discomfort. The pain was described as vague, dull, and nonlocalized; mild to moderate in intensity; and initially not impeding her daily activities. Approximately 7 hours after the onset of symptoms, she noted a small amount of vaginal bleeding, estimated at 15 mL, without associated leakage of clear fluid. Blood loss was determined using an objective method rather than subjective patient recall. The collected sanitary pads were weighed, and the known dry weight of the pads was subtracted to determine the net weight of the blood loss. A standard clinical conversion ratio was applied, in which a 1 g increase in weight corresponds to approximately 1 mL of blood loss. This quantification was further validated clinically using a pictorial blood loss assessment chart. She also reported cessation of fetal movements since the morning of admission. Her obstetric history was notable for a previous lower-segment cesarean section performed during her first pregnancy due to fetopelvic disproportion, with an uneventful postoperative course. The interpregnancy interval (IPI) was approximately 1 year. The current pregnancy was a singleton intrauterine gestation estimated at 28 weeks based on the last menstrual period. She had no additional surgical history and denied any personal or family history of chronic illnesses, such as hypertension or diabetes mellitus. The patient was a non-smoker and had not taken any medications during the current pregnancy. Upon presentation, her vital signs were stable, with a pulse rate of 107 beats per minute, a blood pressure of 100/60 mmHg, and an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. On gynecologic examination, the external cervical os was slightly open, while the internal os remained closed. The cervix was firm, and no active vaginal bleeding or amniotic fluid leakage was observed. Transabdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged uterus containing placental remnants measuring approximately 1.5 cm in thickness, with no intrauterine fetus visualized (Fig. 1). A demised fetus was identified within the abdominal cavity with no cardiac activity (Fig. 2). Fetal biometric parameters corresponded to 28 weeks and 6 days for the biparietal diameter and 24 weeks and 1 day for the femur length. Additionally, a small volume of free fluid was detected in Morrison’s pouch and the pouch of Douglas, consistent with leakage of amniotic fluid into the peritoneal cavity. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of complete uterine rupture was established. A prior ultrasound performed 5 days earlier had confirmed an intrauterine pregnancy, suggesting that uterine rupture occurred subsequently. Laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 12.6 g/dL, a platelet count of 145 000/μL, and a white blood cell count of 7500/μL. Unfortunately, because the patient did not receive regular prenatal care, prior complete blood count (CBC) results (baseline Hb/Hct) were unavailable for comparison. An emergency laparotomy was performed. The decision-to-incision time interval was exactly 20 minutes. This period encompassed the rapid preparation of the operating theater and the immediate mobilization of the anesthesia team. Upon entering the peritoneal cavity, a moderate amount of amniotic fluid mixed with blood was encountered. A deceased fetus was retrieved from the abdominal cavity, followed by manual removal of the placenta. Inspection of the uterus demonstrated a complete rupture of the previous lower uterine segment cesarean scar extending along its entire length, without active or profuse bleeding. The uterine cavity was cleared of residual tissue and thoroughly irrigated. The rupture was repaired using continuous 2-0 Vicryl sutures, and the uterus was repositioned to its anatomical position within the pelvis. A drain was placed in the pouch of Douglas to facilitate the drainage of residual fluids, and the abdominal wall was closed in layers. The surgery was completed without intraoperative complications. The surgery lasted 50 minutes. Unfortunately, high-resolution intraoperative images are unavailable due to the acute, life-threatening nature of the emergency, in which immediate surgical intervention took precedence over photography. Furthermore, lighting constraints in the emergency operating theater prevented adequate image quality. The complete rupture involving the serosa was confirmed via direct visualization by the surgical team. Postoperatively, the patient was closely monitored, with attention to vital signs and vaginal bleeding. Six hours postoperatively, the patient’s hemoglobin level was 11.9 g/dL, and she remained completely hemodynamically stable. She stayed in the hospital for 72 hours. However, due to the remote location of her primary village and the potential risk of delayed complications, the patient remained in temporary accommodations near the hospital for the following week to ensure immediate access to emergency care if required (Fig. 3). Discussion

An accurate epidemiological appraisal of uterine rupture requires a rigorous distinction between the baseline risk inherent to an unlabored scarred uterus and the active risk precipitated by a trial of labor after cesarean (TOLAC). In the absence of labor, the incidence of catastrophic (complete) uterine rupture during an elective repeat cesarean delivery (ERCD) is exceedingly rare, estimated at approximately 0.02%[5]. Conversely, the exposure of a scarred myometrium to the mechanical forces of active labor increases this vulnerability. For individuals with a history of a single prior low-transverse uterine incision undergoing TOLAC, large-scale contemporary cohort data and consensus guidelines establish the incidence of complete uterine rupture at 0.5% to 0.9%[5,6]. Furthermore, while a history of multiple prior cesarean deliveries introduces cumulative structural changes to the lower uterine segment, modern multi-center registries demonstrate that the risk of rupture remains substantially lower than historical estimates of 3% to 4%. In contemporary obstetric cohorts, the true rate of complete uterine rupture among candidates with two prior cesarean deliveries attempting TOLAC ranges between 0.9% and 1.8%, failing to approach the inflated thresholds frequently reported in older literature or smaller, single-center datasets that fail to differentiate between true catastrophic rupture and asymptomatic scar dehiscence[5].

Several factors have been identified in the medical literature as contributing to uterine rupture. These include a history of previous uterine rupture, multiple cesarean sections, a short IPI, and attempts at vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC). Other factors contributing to rupture include incomplete wound healing due to ischemia or fibrosis, uterine trauma, uterine overdistension (as seen in multiple pregnancies, polyhydramnios, or macrosomia), multiparity, obstructed labor, abnormal fetal presentation, excessive uterine contractions, congenital uterine anomalies, and advanced maternal age. In the current case, the main contributing factor was a previous lower-segment cesarean section performed just 1 year prior[7–9].

The timing of the uterine rupture at 28 weeks' gestation underscores a critical intersection between impaired tissue remodeling and escalating mechanical stress, with the short 1-year IPI serving as the primary pathophysiological driver. A standard healing cascade following a cesarean section requires adequate time for myofibroblast activity, collagen cross-linking, and revascularization. A truncated interval of only 1 year severely limits this window, preventing proper collagen reorganization and the restoration of normal myometrial tensile strength. Consequently, the previous hysterotomy site remains structurally compromised – replaced by rigid, poorly vascularized, and unyielding collagenous scar tissue rather than flexible, fully reconstructed smooth muscle.

This cellular and structural vulnerability directly explains why the structural failure occurred mid-pregnancy. As the pregnancy transitions into the early third trimester at 28 weeks, the uterus undergoes rapid volume expansion due to accelerating fetal growth and peak amniotic fluid accumulation. While the normal myometrium hypertrophies and stretches to accommodate this volume, the rigid scar tissue lacks the necessary elasticity and compliance. Exacerbated by poor local vascularity that limits tissue repair under tension, the scar undergoes progressive, silent dehiscence. Ultimately, the continuous, escalating intrauterine pressure outpaces the compromised tensile threshold of the unreconstructed scar, culminating in complete full-thickness rupture at 28 weeks.

Prior to the patient’s acute presentation, there was no documented clinical or radiological evidence of a preceding partial uterine rupture or scar dehiscence. Uterine scar dehiscence is frequently asymptomatic and clinically silent, often causing no specific signs or symptoms until catastrophic failure occurs. In this case, the structural failure went undetected until it rapidly progressed to a complete, full-thickness uterine rupture with fetal extrusion. It did not extend superiorly into the active uterine body or laterally into the broad ligament, thereby completely sparing the major uterine vascular bundles and uterine arteries.

Although some cases of uterine rupture may remain clinically silent, the condition typically presents with a characteristic triad of vaginal bleeding, acute abdominal pain, and fetal heart rate abnormalities. Patients often report a sudden onset of severe abdominal pain, frequently described as a “tearing” or “ripping” sensation, followed by intensely painful contractions. In instances where intraperitoneal hemorrhage occurs, diaphragmatic irritation may lead to referred pain in the shoulder or chest, occasionally mimicking ischemic cardiac pain. Importantly, the presence of a labor epidural can obscure these symptoms, thereby delaying recognition and diagnosis of uterine rupture[10].

On clinical examination, abdominal palpation may elicit midline pain. The majority of women experiencing uterine rupture demonstrate abdominal tenderness, even in the presence of labor epidural analgesia. Careful palpation can also reveal alterations in uterine shape or contraction dynamics. In such cases, contraction amplitude often diminishes, and uterine activity may cease entirely[11,12].

Fetal bradycardia represents the most frequently observed abnormality associated with uterine rupture[5,13]. However, no specific fetal heart rate tracing pattern is pathognomonic for this condition[14]. The complete absence of fetal heart sounds constitutes a serious clinical finding and necessitates ultrasonographic evaluation to confirm the absence of cardiac activity.

In a cohort of 97 028 deliveries, Giuliano et al documented 52 cases of uterine rupture (0.05%). The vast majority (89%) occurred in women with a history of cesarean section. Fetal heart rate (FHR) abnormalities represented the most common clinical manifestation (n = 22, 45.8%), followed by abdominal pain (n = 13, 25%), intrapartum vaginal bleeding (n = 12, 23.1%), loss of fetal station (n = 8, 15.4%), third-stage hemorrhage (n = 7, 13.5%), and hematuria (n = 2, 3.8%). The classic triad of FHR abnormalities, abdominal pain, and vaginal bleeding was observed in only 9% of complete ruptures[13].

In our case, despite the occurrence of a complete uterine rupture with extrusion of both the fetus and placenta into the abdominal cavity, the clinical presentation was atypical, delayed, and nonspecific. The patient exhibited only mild, diffuse abdominal discomfort and minimal vaginal bleeding while remaining hemodynamically stable. The delay identified in this case did not stem from an institutional or initial triage failure; upon presentation to the facility, the patient was triaged promptly and appropriately according to established obstetric protocols. Instead, the delay was entirely pre-hospital, driven mainly by the absence of sharp abdominal pain or intense vaginal bleeding and by a profound lack of health literacy and knowledge regarding obstetric danger signs. In developing regions, and specifically within the socio-politically complex healthcare landscape of Syria, there is a documented lack of structured antenatal education. Community awareness regarding the critical urgency of gross vaginal bleeding, the necessity of monitoring maternal-fetal movement (fetal kick counts), and the potential sequelae of these symptoms for maternal-fetal outcomes remains low.

The differential diagnosis of uterine rupture encompasses other etiologies of second- and third-trimester vaginal bleeding accompanied by acute abdominal pain, including spontaneous abortion, bloody show associated with normal labor, placenta previa, and placental abruption[11]. In our case, these differential diagnoses were excluded using ultrasound.

The diagnosis of rectus sheath hematoma was excluded due to the absence of localized abdominal wall pain, a palpable abdominal mass, or ultrasonographic evidence of blood accumulation confined within the rectus sheath.

Acute appendicitis was also discounted due to the absence of classic constitutional or gastrointestinal symptoms, such as anorexia, nausea, vomiting, or migratory abdominal pain. Moreover, the WBC count was within normal limits. Most importantly, the definitive ultrasonographic finding of a complete uterine rupture with extrauterine fetal extrusion provided an immediate, indisputable diagnosis that fully explained the clinical presentation.

Radiographic and laboratory investigations can be helpful in the diagnosis of a minor uterine rupture. However, imaging is not appropriate in cases of major rupture due to the urgent need for surgical delivery and hemorrhage control. In hemodynamically stable patients with suspected minor rupture, ultrasonography can be valuable in excluding alternative causes of vaginal bleeding, such as placenta previa, placental abruption, or spontaneous abortion. Sonographic findings supportive of uterine rupture include disruption of the uterine wall, hematoma adjacent to a hysterotomy scar, intraperitoneal free fluid, anhydramnios, or visualization of fetal parts outside the uterine cavity[15,16]. Ultimately, definitive diagnosis is most often established intraoperatively, when hemoperitoneum and extrauterine fetal parts are identified during laparotomy.

In our case, preoperative transabdominal ultrasonography demonstrated an enlarged uterus containing placental remnants, with no intrauterine fetus identified. A demised fetus was visualized within the abdominal cavity without evidence of cardiac activity. These findings established the diagnosis of uterine rupture prior to surgery. Based on the documented intrauterine pregnancy 5 days earlier, the rupture was believed to have occurred after the most recent ultrasound examination.

Uterine rupture necessitates immediate intervention, as any delay in delivery, resuscitation, or surgical management significantly heightens maternal and fetal risks[11]. The cornerstone of treatment is an emergent cesarean section, with or without a concurrent exploratory laparotomy. Hemorrhage control must be performed simultaneously with delivery[17]. Initial resuscitation typically involves rapid infusion of lactated Ringer’s solution, while substantial or brisk blood loss should prompt the early initiation of blood transfusion.

In our case, a precise estimation of intraoperative blood loss was challenging because the blood was heavily admixed with amniotic fluid and surgical irrigation saline. However, by combining suction canister volume measurements and the gravimetric method (weighing surgical sponges), the total volume of admixed fluid was estimated at approximately 1200 mL. No blood transfusions were required during the perioperative period. This was due to successful intraoperative hemostasis, continuous hemodynamic stability, and a minimal drop in the patient’s hemoglobin levels.

When intraperitoneal bleeding is suspected, a midline abdominal incision should be favored over the Pfannenstiel approach. The midline incision affords superior surgical exposure, facilitating identification of the bleeding source and potentially reducing the time interval between incision and delivery[18]. In cases of limited rupture, uterine repair may be feasible[14]. However, in the presence of hemodynamic instability or extensive uterine injury, hysterectomy becomes necessary. Notably, approximately one-third of women with uterine rupture ultimately require hysterectomy[19,20].

In the present case, the decision to perform a Pfannenstiel incision was based on the patient’s hemodynamic stability and the preoperative ultrasound findings, which did not show evidence of active, large-volume intra-abdominal bleeding. Given these findings, the surgical team determined that a Pfannenstiel incision would provide adequate access to the pelvis while minimizing surgical trauma and postoperative morbidity associated with a midline laparotomy.

Importantly, the surgical team remained fully aware of the potential need for broader exposure. Therefore, a contingency plan was in place to extend the incision to a midline laparotomy if intraoperative findings revealed significant hemorrhage or any other circumstance requiring enhanced access for bleeding control and surgical management. Fortunately, the operative findings allowed successful management through the initial Pfannenstiel approach without compromising patient safety or surgical outcomes.

Prior to discharge, the patient received comprehensive future fertility counseling. She was educated on the significantly increased risk of recurrent uterine rupture and advised to delay subsequent conception for 18–24 months. Furthermore, she was informed that any future pregnancy would be classified as high-risk, requiring strict antenatal surveillance and a scheduled cesarean delivery. Family planning, contraceptive options, and the vital importance of preconception counseling were also thoroughly discussed. The patient expressed complete satisfaction with the management she received and personally thanked the hospital staff for their care and support.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of considering uterine rupture in hemodynamically stable patients who present with atypical symptoms, such as mild abdominal pain or minimal vaginal bleeding. In such cases, accurate diagnostic assessment using ultrasonography or magnetic resonance imaging is crucial for confirmation. Women with a prior cesarean section warrant close surveillance, particularly during early pregnancy following a cesarean delivery. The case also highlights the profound health literacy gap in developing countries such as Syria regarding short interpregnancy intervals and obstetric danger signs, such as vaginal bleeding and decreased fetal movement, thereby posing an urgent need for public health initiatives to improve community-level maternal education.
