Clinical trial landscape and translational prospects of natural killer cell-based immunotherapy: an analysis based on the INFORMA database
Authors: Han Sun, Yanni Zhang, Ye Yuan, Dehua Liu, Jinhan Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural killer (NK) cell-based immunotherapy is an increasingly important cancer treatment strategy because of its innate cytotoxicity, allogeneic potential, and compatibility with off-the-shelf manufacturing. We analyzed 287 clinical trials identified in the INFORMA database to characterize the global development of NK-cell therapies across phases, regions, indications, combinations, cell sources, product origins, and engineering platforms. Most trials were planned or early-phase, and combination regimens were more frequent than monotherapy. The USA, China, and South Korea led clinical activity. Hematologic malignancies remained the principal testing ground, while lung, colorectal, and other solid tumors reflected broader expansion. Platform-level analysis showed a predominance of allogeneic products and rapid growth of CAR-engineered NK-cell trials after 2020. These findings indicate a transition from proof-of-concept adoptive transfer toward standardized, genetically programmable, off-the-shelf platforms. The principal challenge is shifting from manufacturing feasibility alone to durable biological activity. Future development should align cell engineering, combination partners, translational endpoints, and biomarker-guided patient selection with specific barriers involving persistence, tumor delivery, microenvironmental fitness, and immune escape.

Natural killer (NK) cells recognize and eliminate malignant cells without prior antigen sensitization, supporting allogeneic, banked, and off-the-shelf therapeutic development.1 2 Products can be generated from peripheral blood, umbilical cord blood, cell lines, or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), while chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), cytokine support, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and genome editing have made NK cells programmable immunotherapy platforms. Early CAR-NK studies showed activity with limited cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, or graft-versus-host disease,3 and iPSC-derived products have demonstrated the feasibility of standardized gene-modified therapy.4 Nevertheless, development remains heterogeneous in cell source, engineering, expansion, lymphodepletion, cytokine support, combination partner, indication, and endpoint selection. We therefore interpret the trial landscape through a central thesis: NK-cell therapy is entering a platform-optimization phase in which manufacturing is increasingly achievable, but durable biological activity remains limiting. The question is no longer simply whether an NK-cell product can be produced and administered, but how the product, patient population, and treatment context should be co-designed to overcome a defined mechanism of failure.

We analyzed NK-cell cancer immunotherapy trials in INFORMA Trialtrove. On February 3, 2026, we exported all records returned by “Drug Type is Biological>Cellular > Cell type>Natural killer cell,” without phase, status, country, sponsor, date, or registry restrictions. Two reviewers manually included interventional oncology trials in which an NK-cell product was a central intervention and excluded non-oncology, non-interventional, preclinical, and diagnostic studies. The 287 eligible trials were analyzed as unique protocols rather than registrations. Records sharing a registry identifier or sponsor protocol number were merged; otherwise, identity was adjudicated from title, sponsor, intervention, indication, phase, enrollment, start date, and design. Separate protocols, independently enrolled cohorts, and materially different interventions were retained. The most complete, recently updated record served as the anchor, supplemented with non-conflicting information from linked records. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and unresolved variables were coded as not reported rather than inferred.

Primary registries were prioritized for identity, phase, status, enrollment, dates, and design; sponsor information and publications were prioritized for product composition, cell source, engineering, and treatment details. Combination therapy required protocol-planned use of an NK-cell product with another therapeutic or conditioning modality. Partners were grouped as chemotherapy (including protocol-specified lymphodepletion), targeted therapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, or other modalities. Supportive care and cytokines used only ex vivo were excluded. Each trial was counted once in the monotherapy-versus-combination comparison but could contribute to multiple partner or indication categories when distinct protocol-defined modalities, cohorts, or arms were present. Trials were additionally coded for NK-cell source, autologous versus allogeneic origin, and CAR class. Multiproduct trials could contribute to multiple source categories. Product-specific cytokine engineering and genome editing were recorded only when explicitly reported. The unique trial was the primary analytical unit, and no cross-trial efficacy comparison was attempted.

INFORMA was selected a priori because its linked trial, sponsor, product, mechanism, status, and geographic fields support consistent classification of heterogeneous global platforms. However, access to the underlying commercial database is restricted, and the analysis is a database-specific landscape rather than an exhaustive registry-based systematic review. Formal cross-validation against ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, or national registries would require a separately prespecified search, field harmonization, record linkage, and duplicate-resolution workflow. Public records were therefore used for targeted field verification, not as a second independently assembled cohort.

Most trials were planned or early phase, reflecting continued emphasis on safety, dose, feasibility, manufacturing reproducibility, and preliminary efficacy (figure 1A,B). The USA, China, and South Korea accounted for 134, 95, and 28 trials, respectively (figure 1C). Combination therapy predominated (163 trials), most often with chemotherapy (63), targeted therapy (43), or immunotherapy (43) (figure 1D). Hematologic malignancies, particularly leukemia, remained the principal indications, whereas lung and colorectal cancers indicated expansion into solid tumors (figure 1E,F). Cancer-stage and perioperative patterns also suggest that NK-cell approaches are being explored beyond advanced disease for immune consolidation, recurrence prevention, and combination-based disease control, although these uses remain investigational.

Platform-level analysis showed that NK-cell line-derived products were most frequently represented across trial-platform occurrences (n=272), followed by iPSC-derived (n=75), umbilical cord blood-derived (n=61), and peripheral blood-derived products (n=5); categories were not mutually exclusive because some trials evaluated multiple products (figure 1G). At trial level, 47 products were autologous (16.4%), 189 allogeneic (65.9%), and 51 unreported (17.8%), demonstrating the predominance of allogeneic development. CAR-NK trials increased from 3 in 2016–2020 to 95 in 2021–2025, with 1 registered for 2026–2027. CAR-haNK trials numbered 4 and 2, and CAR-TaNK trials 3 and 3, in 2016–2020 and 2021–2025, respectively (figure 1H). The incomplete 2026–2027 interval should not be interpreted as declining activity. These counts describe development activity rather than comparative clinical efficacy. Because trial-platform and multilabel categories can overlap, they are signals of technological direction rather than independent product totals. Nevertheless, the allogeneic predominance and post-2020 rise in CAR-NK registration support convergence toward renewable, engineered products that can be manufactured in advance, quality-controlled in batches, and deployed across patients.

Together, early-phase predominance, hematologic concentration, and frequent combinations indicate a transition from proving that NK-cell products can be manufactured and administered to determining how they should be optimized. The first developmental stage established ex vivo expansion, allogeneic administration, repeatable manufacturing, and early clinical feasibility. The current stage requires co-design of the product, patient population, and combination partner around a specified mechanism of treatment failure. Hematologic malignancies provide accessible targets, measurable circulating exposure, and cellular-therapy precedent for rapid platform validation, whereas solid tumors require coordinated solutions for lymphodepletion, targeting, trafficking, microenvironmental suppression, and immune escape. Combination partners should therefore be selected by mechanism rather than added empirically: chemotherapy can provide lymphodepletion or debulking, tumor-directed antibodies can enhance antibody-dependent cytotoxicity, immunomodulators can relieve suppressive signaling, and cytokine or microenvironment-directed strategies can improve persistence and functional fitness.

The major barriers form a sequence of exposure, delivery, fitness, recognition, and selection. Greater expansion and persistence correlated with response to cord blood-derived CAR19/IL-15 NK cells,5 whereas systemic IL-15 can promote recipient T-cell-mediated rejection,6 supporting regulated cell-intrinsic cytokine signaling, rational lymphodepletion, repeat dosing, and cellular pharmacokinetic monitoring rather than indiscriminate cytokine exposure. Chemokine-receptor mismatch can restrict tumor entry; CXCR2 engineering increased NK-cell migration toward renal-cell-carcinoma chemokines.7 Paired blood and tumor sampling, or molecular imaging where feasible, could distinguish failed homing from functional failure after tumor entry. Within tumors, hypoxia, nutrient competition, lactate, adenosine, and transforming growth factor (TGF)-beta impair function; lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)-dependent lactate suppressed T-cell and NK-cell activation experimentally.8 Translational endpoints should therefore measure metabolic state and microenvironmental conditions, not only immune-cell abundance. Target heterogeneity, inhibitory ligands, and shedding of activating ligands enable escape, while preventing MICA/MICB shedding restored NK-cell-driven immunity in preclinical models.9 Multi-antigen targeting, checkpoint-resistant engineering, and combinations should be matched to the documented escape phenotype rather than applied uniformly.

Predictive biomarkers should integrate tumor features (target density and heterogeneity, ligands, chemokines, and metabolism), product features (source, receptor repertoire, potency, fitness, and persistence), and host features (immune reconstitution, baseline NK-cell status, and allorejection risk). Tumor-infiltrating NK-cell density and HLA class I expression predicted pathological response to antibody therapy in HER2-positive breast cancer,10 while early expansion, persistence, and donor attributes were associated with response to CAR19/IL-15 NK cells.5 Future trials should prospectively link serial blood and tumor measurements to a prespecified resistance mechanism, distinguish predictive from prognostic markers, and align the engineering feature, combination partner, enrichment strategy, and correlative endpoint with the biological step expected to limit efficacy.

Several limitations apply. INFORMA may have update lag, incomplete fields, or differences from primary registries. Cell source, engineering, lymphodepletion, cytokine support, endpoints, and status were inconsistently reported in some records. Planned and early-phase studies may not mature, and multiproduct categories can overlap. Cytokine support and genome-editing status were not consistently structured, so non-reporting was not treated as absence and prevalence was not estimated. Because the database is proprietary, readers without licensed access cannot reproduce the raw export. The absence of formal cross-validation against public registries means that estimates may reflect database-specific coverage, update schedules, and classification. Targeted verification reduced but could not eliminate omission or misclassification.

In conclusion, NK-cell immunotherapy is moving from proof-of-concept transfer to platform optimization. Allogeneic predominance and rapid CAR-NK growth indicate convergence toward standardized, genetically programmable, off-the-shelf products. Manufacturing remains essential, but sustained benefit will require mechanism-informed engineering, rational combinations, and biomarker-guided selection aligned with persistence, tumor delivery, microenvironmental fitness, and immune escape. Future trials should determine not only whether a product can be manufactured and safely administered, but whether its exposure, delivery, function, recognition, and patient selection are biologically aligned to produce durable benefit.
