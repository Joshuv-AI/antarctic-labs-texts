Hybrid spherical designs
Martin Ehler

Abstract
. Spherical t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t -designs are finite point sets on the unit sphere that enable exact integration of polynomials of degree at most t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t via equal-weight quadrature. This concept has recently been extended to spherical t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t -design curves by the use of normalized path integrals. However, explicit examples of such curves are rare. We construct new spherical t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t -design curves for small t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t based on edge-transitive convex polytopes. We then introduce hybrid t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t -designs that combine points and curves for exact polynomial integration of higher degree. Our constructions use the edges and vertices of dual pairs of convex polytopes and polynomial invariants of their symmetry group. A notable result is a hybrid t \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t -design for t = 19 \color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t=19 .

1  Introduction

Integration on the unit sphere serves as a fundamental tool in mathematics, physics, and engineering for analyzing and modeling phenomena with spherical symmetry. An effective approach for such integration is the concept of spherical designs. These are sets of points on the sphere that enable exact integration of polynomials up to a specific degree by using equal-weight quadrature formulas. Quadrature in general is widely studied in numerical analysis, approximation theory, and signal processing, and although equal weights are not always necessary, they do simplify the formulas and make them more convenient and easier to use for practitioners.

Points:

Formally, for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
denote the space of all polynomials of total degree at most
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d+1
variables, restricted to the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\|x\|=1\}
.
A spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design is a finite set of points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
with cardinality
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}|X|
, such that the normalized surface integral on the sphere can be exactly determined by the equal-weight quadrature formula

(1)

\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}p(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd.

This concept dates back to work by McLaren in 1963
[
37
]
, although the term ‘spherical design’ was coined later by Delsarte, Goethals, and Seidel in the 1970s
[
14
]
. Since then, spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-designs have inspired a rich body of research investigating their explicit construction through analytic, algebraic, and numerical methods
[

Curves:

While spherical designs have been extensively studied in the discrete setting, their continuous counterparts, spherical design curves, have been introduced only recently in
[
16
]
. These closed curves define a continuous path on the sphere and achieve the same exact integration property via normalized path integrals, i.e., a continuous function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma:[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d}
with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma(0)=\gamma(L)
is a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curve if

\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd\,,

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\ell(\gamma)
denotes the arc-length of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
.

Although constructions of families of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curves are provided in
[

,

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t

Curves for mobile sampling:

The concept of spherical design curves in
[
16
]
is motivated by mobile sampling, which studies the reconstruction of a function from its values along a curve
[
49
,
48
,
22
]
. Unlike discrete point sampling, such as with spherical design points that require multiple sensors, mobile sampling utilizes a single sensor that moves along a curved trajectory. An illustrative case of mobile sampling on the sphere involves gathering environmental data along an airplane flight path or the trajectory of a satellite, with the objective of reconstructing global information over the entire globe
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

From a practical perspective, it makes sense to combine both sampling approaches by using a mobile device alongside static sensors where available.
However, point-based and curve-based methods have traditionally been studied separately, and their combined use has remained largely unexplored within sampling theory and designs.

Hybrid designs:

We propose the new notion of hybrid designs, where we consider both, a curve
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma:[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d}
and finitely many points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
. We call the pair

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(\gamma,X)
a hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design if there is a balancing constant
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0\leq\beta\leq 1
such that

\frac{\beta}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}p(x)+\frac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd\,.

The benefit of hybrid designs in comparison to a ‘pure’ curve is increase in strength. Given a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curve
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
, we may add a set of points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X
to enhance the integration strength to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Pol_{\leq s}
for some
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s>t
, see Figures
4
,
5
, and

2  Curves, designs, and group orbits

We begin by introducing the concept of spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curves, as originally proposed in
[
16
]
. Next, we discuss integration along orbits of spherical arcs, which forms the foundation of our further investigations.

2.1  Spherical design curves

Here, a curve is defined as a continuous, piecewise smooth mapping
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma:[0,L]\to\mathbb{S}^{d}
that is closed in the sense that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma(0)=\gamma(L)
. Given a continuous function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p:\mathbb{S}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the path integral along
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
is

\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{0}^{L}p(\gamma(\alpha))\|\dot{\gamma}(\alpha)\|\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\,,

and its arc-length is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\ell(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}1
. For
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t\in\mathbb{N}
, recall that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
denotes the space of all polynomials of total degree at most
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d+1
variables, restricted to the sphere. We follow
[
16
]
and call
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
a spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curve if

\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd\,.

Integration over the entire sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
is always taken with respect to the normalized, orthogonally invariant measure, and
the parameter
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
is referred to as the strength of the design (curve). Unlike
[
16
]
, we permit arbitrary self-intersections, so that the curve may traverse sections multiple times.

A piecewise smooth curve can be constructed by connecting finitely many points with geodesic arcs. To explicitly parametrize a geodesic arc, recall that the geodesic distance between points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}y
on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
is given by

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\dist(x,y)=\arccos\langle x,y\rangle\in[0,\pi]\,.

For
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}y\neq\pm x
, the distance is neither
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0
nor
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\pi
, and the geodesic arc is parametrized as
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{x,y}:[0,\dist(x,y)]\to\mathbb{S}^{d}
,

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\gamma_{x,y}(s)=\frac{\sin(\dist(x,y)-s)}{\sin(\dist(x,y))}\,x+\frac{\sin(s)}{\sin(\dist(x,y))}\,y\,.

Integration along a geodesic arc is direction-independent, so that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\gamma_{x,y}}p=\int_{\gamma_{y,x}}p
.

By connecting points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
with such geodesic arcs, one obtains a geodesic chain, analogous to a polygonal chain in Euclidean space. For closed chains, called cycles, we set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{n+1}:=x_{0}
. The integral over a cycle
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma=(\gamma_{x_{j},x_{j+1}})_{j=1}^{n}
is

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\int_{\gamma}p=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\gamma_{x_{j},x_{j+1}}}\!\!\!p\,,

and the length of the cycle is the sum of the arc lengths
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\ell(\gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dist(x_{j},x_{j+1})
.

Every geodesic cycle
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
induces a curve and is called a spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycle (or
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curve) if

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\quad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd\,.

In the following example we construct spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycles derived from a set of great circles.

Each line through antipodal points serves as the normal of a hyperplane, whose intersection with the sphere

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}n

To define this curve, we construct a graph whose vertices are the intersection points of these great circles, and whose edges are segments of the great circles connecting the vertices. An Euler cycle in this graph is a cycle that visits every edge exactly once. By Euler’s Theorem on undirected, connected graphs, such a cycle exists if and only if each vertex has an even degree
[
50

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{2}

[

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t

To expand exact integration from point orbits to orbits of geodesic arcs, we consider the space of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
-invariant polynomials of degree at most
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
given by

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\PoltdG:=\{p\in\Poltd:p\circ g=p\text{ for all }g\in G\}\,.

It is already observed in
[
19
,
13
]
that the group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous if and only if
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
consists solely of constant functions. In other words,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
is one-dimensional, commonly denoted by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG=\mathbb{R}
.

This formulation enables us to derive exact integration for orbits of geodesic arcs.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G\subseteq\mathcal{O}(d+1)
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
is a geodesic arc, then the orbit
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(g\gamma_{0})_{g\in G}
induced by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
satisfies the exact integration condition

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p,\,\quad\text{for all $p\in\Poltd$.}

If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
is a constant curve, so that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}(s)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s\in[0,1]
, then the integration reduces to a point evaluation
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}p=p(gx_{0})
. Thus, each orbit
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}Gx_{0}
is a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design, as mentioned above and used in
[
37
]
.

Proof.

For
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p\in\Poltd
, we denote its average over
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
by the Reynolds operator

(2)

p^{G}:=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}p\circ g\,.

This average leads to the compact expression

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p\circ g=\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p^{G}\,.

Since
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p^{G}
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
-invariant and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p^{G}
is constant and we obtain

\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p^{G}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}\,.

The orthogonal invariance of the surface measure on the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
leads to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\circ g=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p
, for all
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\in G
, and hence
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p
, which concludes the proof.

Starting with an arbitrary spherical arc
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
, the orbit of geodesic arcs
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Gamma=\{g\gamma_{0}:g\in G\}
is generally not a connected set. As a result, it does not form a geodesic cycle. To overcome this issue, we will soon introduce additional structure to the orbit
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Gamma
. This structure is provided by convex polytopes, which are discussed next.

3  Spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycles from convex polytopes

A convex polytope
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as the convex hull of a finite set of points in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
is centered at the origin and scaled such that it lies within the unit ball. By projecting outward, each edge of the polytope defines a geodesic arc on the unit sphere. On the two-sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{2}
, for instance, those arcs yield a spherical tiling as studied in
[
24
]
in more generality.

To construct a geodesic cycle on the sphere, we use that the vertices and edges of the polytope define a graph. An Euler cycle traverses each edge of the graph exactly once, and according to Euler’s theorem, a connected graph has an Euler cycle if every vertex is incident with an even number of edges, cf. Example
2.1
. By outward projection, such an Euler cycle translates directly into a geodesic cycle on the sphere.

If not all vertices are incident to an even number of edges, we double all edges of the graph. This modified graph ensures that all vertices now meet the conditions for admitting an Euler cycle.

3.1  Euler cycles of convex polytopes induce spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycles

The symmetries of a convex polytope
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the elements
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\in\mathcal{O}(d)
that leave
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
invariant, i.e.,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}
. These elements constitute the (full) symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
, which also acts on the geometric features of the polytope.

One such feature is a face and defined as the intersection of the polytope with a hyperplane, so that the polytope lies on one side of the hyperplane. Faces can range from vertices (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0
-dimensional faces) and edges (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}1
-dimensional faces) all the way up to facets (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d-1
-dimensional faces) and the polytope itself (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d
-dimensional face).

A (proper) flag is a tuple
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d-1})
of nested faces of the polytope, so that each
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f_{k}
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k
-dimensional, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f_{k-1}\subseteq f_{k}
for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k=1,\ldots,d-1
. A convex polytope in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called regular if its symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
acts transitively on its flags. That is, for any two flags
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f^{\prime}
, there exists
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\in G
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}f^{\prime}=gf
.

In particular, regular convex polytopes are
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k
-face-transitive for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k=0,\ldots,d-1
, i.e., the symmetry group acts transitively on the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k
-dimensional faces. Consequently, each set of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k
-dimensional faces forms a single orbit under the action of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
.

If the symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
of a convex polytope is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous, vertex-transitivity (transitivity on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0
-dimensional faces) ensures that the vertices, when projected onto the sphere, form a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design. We now extend this observation from vertices to edges.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
is an edge-transitive convex polytope in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. If its symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G\subseteq\mathcal{O}(d+1)
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous, then every Euler cycle projected onto the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
is a spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycle.

If the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
are satisfied but
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
does not admit any Euler cycles, then as mentioned before we may double each edge and every Euler cycle in this new configuration yields a geodesic
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycle.

Proof.

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(\mathrm{e}_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
denote all edges of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}
ordered according to an Euler cycle and let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma=(\gamma_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
be the induced geodesic cycle on the sphere, so that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{i}
is the geodesic arc induced by projecting the edge
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathrm{e}_{i}
onto the sphere.

Since
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
acts transitively on the edges, the edges form a single orbit
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\{\mathrm{e}_{0},\ldots,\mathrm{e}_{m}\}=\{g\mathrm{e}_{0}:g\in G\}
. For every
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\in G
, the edge
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\mathrm{e}_{0}
corresponds to the geodesic arc
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g\gamma_{0}
. Thus,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
also acts transitively on the arcs. Since the cardinality of an orbit divides the group order, we deduce

\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{i})}\int_{\gamma_{i}}p=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}p\,.

Lemma
2.2
implies the integration condition

\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{0=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{i})}\int_{\gamma_{i}}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all $p\in\Poltd$,}

so that the Euler cycle projected onto the sphere is a spherical
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design cycle.

4  Hybrid spherical designs

The
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curves discussed in the previous sections are based on edge-transitive polytopes. This approach utilizes their
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-homogeneous symmetry groups and produces several new
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curves. However, the achievable range of the degree
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
is limited. The highest value of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
in Table
4
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}11
and corresponds to the symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}H_{4}
of the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}120
-cell and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}600
-cell. The examples in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{3}
are restricted to the degrees
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t=2,3,5
.

To achieve higher degrees, we introduce the concept of hybrid designs that combine a spherical (design) curve with additional (design) points.

4.1  Introduction to hybrid designs

In sampling theory, the path of a curve can model a mobile sensor, while individual points correspond to static sensors. To improve coverage and integration capabilities, we now combine curves and points. In particular, we may balance the contributions of static and mobile sensors by an additional factor
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta
.

Definition 4.1

(hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-designs)
.

Given a curve
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a finite set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we call the pair
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\left(\gamma,X\right)
a hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design if there is a constant
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0\leq\beta\leq 1
such that

(3)

\frac{\beta}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}p(x)+\frac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\qquad\text{for all }p\in\Poltd\,.

Evaluation for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p\equiv 1
implies that the contributions of the points and the curve need to be balanced by an affine combination, which leads to the factors
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}1-\beta
in (
3
). In the context of numerical quadrature, it is reasonable to require that both, the points and the curve, contribute positively. This leads to the condition
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0<\beta<1
. If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
is a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design curve and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
are
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design points, then
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\left(\gamma,X\right)
is a hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design for every
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta
. The artificial choice
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=0
or
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=1
is associated to pure designs that consist of either a curve or points exclusively. The balancing factor offers a degree of freedom not available in pure designs.

The integration formula can be interpreted as being induced by a probability measure on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
whose support consists of points and the trajectory of a curve. For general probability measures, we now derive a necessary covering property that provides insight into how the points should be positioned relative to the curve. The covering radius of a closed subset
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}M\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
is defined as

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\delta(M):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min_{y\in M}\operatorname{dist}(x,y),

representing the radius of the largest open ball in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
that does not intersect
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}M
.

While explicit upper bounds on the covering radius of spherical design points, particularly for small
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
, are derived in
[
46
]
, the following result illustrates that the curve and the points of a hybrid design must also cover the sphere sufficiently well.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a constant
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}C_{d}>0
, depending on the dimension
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d
, such that the following holds: If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
is a probability measure on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that

(4)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all $p\in\Poltd$,}

then the covering radius of the support of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
satisfies

\delta(\supp(\mu))\leq C_{d}t^{-1}\,.

For probability measures with finite support, this result is contained in
[
40
]
and shows that the upper bounds in
[
46
]
do not have the optimal asymptotics for large
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
. The more general claim in Lemma
4.2
might also be well-known to the experts (see
[
35
,
36
]
for related results). For instance, it can derived from the lines of the first part of the proof in
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
, and we provide the details in the Appendix
A
for the sake of completeness.

Since the constant
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}C_{d}
is not specified here, Lemma
4.2
does not offer a direct bound on the covering radius for specific hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-designs. It rather suggests that a smaller covering radius is preferable, meaning points should be placed to fill gaps where the curve has large voids. We continue with a few elementary hybrid designs in Figures

Proof.

It is sufficient to check the integration conditions for the monomials. We used the computer algebra system Mathematica

The orthogonal invariance of the surface measure of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{2}
yields that the integral
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}x^{k}y^{l}z^{m}
vanishes if at least one of the exponents
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}
is odd. Moreover, the identity
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1
leads to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}x^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}y^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}z^{2}=\frac{1}{3}

(i) A circle at height
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0\leq r\leq 1

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{r}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
,

(5)

\gamma_{r}(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha)\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\\
\sin(\alpha)\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\\
r\end{pmatrix}\,.

We observe
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\gamma_{r}}x=\int_{\gamma_{r}}y=0
and the respective point evaluations at the south pole also vanish. We now let the balancing factor
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta(r)
depend on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}r

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{r})}\int_{\gamma_{r}}z=r

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}z=0

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}-\beta(r)+(1-\beta(r))r=0

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta(r)=\frac{r}{1+r}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}1
-design.

For
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}r=\frac{1}{3}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta(r)=\frac{1}{4}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{r})}\int_{\gamma_{r}}z^{2}=\frac{1}{9}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}z^{2}
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{4}+(1-\frac{1}{4})\frac{1}{9}=\frac{1}{3}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}z^{2}=\frac{1}{3}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1
eventually imply that the analogous evaluation for the monomials

and

is odd. It remains to check the monomials

, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x^{2},y^{2},z^{2}
. Direct calculations based on symmetries reveal that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(\gamma_{0},\{u_{N},u_{S}\})

4.2  Hybrid designs from pairs of dual polytopes

To improve the covering radius of a geodesic

5  Proof of Theorem
4.4

Our proof of Theorem
4.4
is guided by the approach in
[
19
]
, where suitable group orbits are derived to get strong
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}t
-design points. The approach consists of two steps and is based on invariant theory. First, we derive an integration result for two group orbits, where one consists of points and the other of spherical arcs. Second, we analyze spherical polynomials that are invariant under the symmetry groups of distinct pairs of dual polytopes. The combination and balancing of these steps will lead to the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

5.1  Combining orbits of points with orbits of geodesic arcs

Lemma
2.2
enables exact integration on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
using orbits of arcs provided that the space of invariant elements
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
is one-dimensional.
If we identify
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s>t
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathrm{Pol}_{\leq s}^{G}
is two-dimensional, then we can still derive exact integration of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Pol_{\leq s}
by adding another orbit of points.

Assume the orthogonal decomposition

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\PolsdG=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}

is valid, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}
is a suitable
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
-invariant polynomial. If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
is a root of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}
, then the orbit
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}Gx_{0}
forms an
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s
-design, see
[
44
]
. The subset of the sphere where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}
vanishes has measure zero, so that the random choice of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}
with respect to the surface measure of the sphere fails with probability one.

We overcome this limitation by introducing an additional orbit of arcs that effectively reverses the situation, making the construction work for almost all points, except for a set of measure zero. Our method parallels McLaren’s strategy in
[
37
]
, see also
[
19
]
, which employs multiple point orbits for weighted quadrature formulas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G\subseteq\mathcal{O}(d+1)
is a finite group such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG
is two-dimensional, so that there is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}\in\PolsdG
satisfying the orthogonal decomposition

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}\,.

(a)

Then for all arcs
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
and all points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}(x_{0})\neq\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}
, the following integration identity holds,

(8)

\frac{\beta}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}p(gx)+\frac{1-\beta}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}p=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\Pol_{\leq s}\,.

(b)

Moreover, for all arcs
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}(x_{0})
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}
have opposite signs and then (
8
) holds with a balancing factor that satisfies
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0\leq\beta<1
.

Proof.

(a) Since
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}\neq 0
, there is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta\in\mathbb{R}
that satisfies

(9)

\beta\Big(p_{\invariant}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}\Big)=-\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}\,.

The polynomial
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}
is orthogonal to the constants, so that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p_{\invariant}=0
, and (
9
) may be stated as

\beta p_{\invariant}(x_{0})+\frac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p_{\invariant}\,.

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p\in\Polsd
. Since the space of invariant polynomials
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}
has dimension two, the invariant polynomial
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p^{G}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}p\circ g
can be written as a linear combination
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p^{G}=r_{0}+r_{1}p_{\invariant}
for some
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}r_{0},r_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. The summation over the group leads to

\displaystyle\tfrac{\beta}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}p(gx_{0})+\tfrac{1-\beta}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{g\gamma_{0}}\!\!p

\displaystyle=\beta p^{G}(x_{0})+\tfrac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p^{G}

\displaystyle=\beta r_{0}+\beta r_{1}p_{\invariant}(x_{0})+(1-\beta)r_{0}+\tfrac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}r_{1}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}

\displaystyle=r_{0}+r_{1}\Big(\beta p_{\invariant}(x_{0})+\tfrac{1-\beta}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}\Big)

\displaystyle=r_{0}+r_{1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p_{\invariant}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\,,

where the last equality follows from the orthogonal invariance of the surface measure of the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
. This completes the proof of the first part of the theorem.

(b) To verify the second part, we first assume that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}\neq 0

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}
is orthogonal to the constants, it necessarily assumes both positive and negative values. Thus, there is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}(x_{0})
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}
have opposite signs. The choice

(10)

\beta=\frac{-\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}}{p_{\invariant}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}}

implies
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0<\beta<1
and leads to (
9

If
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{0})}\int_{\gamma_{0}}p_{\invariant}=0

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=0
and all

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{\invariant}

5.2  Hybrid designs and invariant polynomials on the sphere

To apply Lemma
5.1
, we must check that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}
. In particular, we seek the maximal
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG
is two-dimensional.

For preparation, we recall the harmonic decomposition of polynomials on the sphere, cf.
[
47
]
. Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{H}_{l}
be the vector space of spherical harmonics of degree
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}l\in\mathbb{N}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
of the homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}l
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}d+1
variables.
Each space
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{H}_{l}
is invariant under the orthogonal group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{O}(d+1)
, and
the polynomials
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Polsd
on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
are orthogonally decomposed into

\Polsd=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{s}\mathcal{H}_{l}\,.

To find the invariant polynomials
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PolsdG=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{s}\mathcal{H}^{G}_{l}
, we can analyze each component
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{H}_{l}^{G}
individually for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}l=0,\ldots,s
.

The dimensions of the spaces of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
-invariant polynomials
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{H}^{G}_{l}
are the coefficients of the (harmonic) Molien series

(11)

M(\omega):=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\dim(\mathcal{H}^{G}_{l})\omega^{l}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1-\omega^{2}}{\det(\mathrm{I}_{d+1}-\omega g)}\,,

cf.
[
19

38
]
, where the second equality is due to
Molien’s Theorem as derived in
[
38
]
for the spherical harmonics.
The factor
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}1-\omega^{2}
appears because we are not dealing with general homogeneous polynomials on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
but spaces of spherical harmonics, cf.
[
38

.

For the tetrahedral group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}A_{3}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}B_{3}

\displaystyle M_{A_{3}}(\omega)

\displaystyle M_{B_{3}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\omega^{4})(1-\omega^{6})}=1+\omega^{4}+\omega^{6}+O(\omega^{8})\,,

\displaystyle M_{H_{3}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\omega^{6})(1-\omega^{10})}=1+\omega^{6}+\omega^{10}+O(\omega^{12})\,,

cf.
[

19

Remark 5.3

If the polytope
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{P}

For the (full) octahedral group

7  Moving beyond transitivity

In this section, we review exact integration via invariants from a broader perspective and derive hybrid spherical designs without transitivity assumptions. It enables us to verify the hybrid designs in Figures
5(d)
and
6(d)
.

Previously, we used invariants for exact integration of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
by assuming
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\dim(\PoltdG)=1
in Lemma
2.2
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\dim(\PoltdG)=2
in Lemma
5.1
. We now explain the basic idea behind using invariants to derive exact integration formulas, which holds regardless of the dimension
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\dim(\PoltdG)
. This idea becomes even more evident when we explore it in a broader, more general setting. Instead of restricting ourselves to points or curves, we start with a signed measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
on the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
. The support of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
could include points, arcs, curves, or other structures.

The averaging process makes also sense for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
by defining
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu^{G}
as the average over the push-forwards
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g_{*}\mu
, i.e.,

(16)

\mu^{G}:=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}g_{*}\mu\,.

This produces a
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
-invariant measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu^{G}
and leads to the following key observation about the connection between invariant theory and exact integration.

Lemma 7.1
.

The signed measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
integrates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
exactly if and only if
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu^{G}
integrates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
exactly.

Proof.

The push-forward measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}g_{*}\mu
satisfies the transformation formula

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}(g_{*}\mu)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\circ g(x)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,,

so that the definition (
16
) leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}g_{*}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\circ g(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,.

Since the surface measure of the sphere is orthogonally invariant, we also have
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p
. Hence, the equality
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p^{G}
holds if and only if the identity
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{G}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p
is satisfied.

Our goal is to achieve exact integration of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
. Lemma
7.1
is useful because finding a measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
that integrates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
exactly is easier than doing so for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Poltd
, as
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG
is a smaller space of polynomials. In the extreme case where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\PoltdG=\mathbb{R}
, every probability measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
suffices.

To make the connection to Lemma
5.1
, take
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a geodesic arc
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
. Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\delta_{x_{0}}
denote the point measure concentrated on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x_{0}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\nu_{\gamma_{0}}
the normalized Hausdorff measure on the arc
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
. If the probability measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu
is defined by

\mu=\beta\delta_{x_{0}}+(1-\beta)\nu_{\gamma_{0}}\,,

then the integration
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{G}(x)
coincides with the left-hand-side of (
8
) in Lemma
5.1
. Thus, Lemma
7.1
can be viewed as the general framework in which Lemmas
2.2
and
5.1
operate.

Polytopes were used in the previous sections to control the support of the invariant measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu^{G}
, so that the one-dimensional part of the support did not consist of multiple disconnected arcs but was the connected path of a continuous closed curve.

We now eliminate the vertex- and edge-transitivity assumptions and consider points
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a geodesic cycle
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
that are unions of orbits under a common symmetry group
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G
be a finite subgroup of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathcal{O}(d+1)
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Pol_{\leq s}^{G}=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}
is two-dimensional.
Assume that the geodesic cycle
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
consists of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}m

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G\gamma_{1}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\ldots
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}G\gamma_{m}
, and some finite set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
consists of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}n
distinct orbits
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}Gx_{1}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\ldots

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}Gx_{n}

If the probability measures

\mu_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|Gx_{i}|}{|X|}\delta_{x_{i}}\,,\qquad\mu_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|G\gamma_{j}|\ell(\gamma_{j})}{\ell(\gamma)}\nu_{\gamma_{j}}

satisfy the compatibility requirement that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}
have opposite signs, then
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(\gamma,X)
is a hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s
-design.

Proof.

If either
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}=0
or
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}=0
, we can choose
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=1
or
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=0

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+(1-\beta)\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}=0
. Otherwise, we define
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta
analogously to (
10
) as

(17)

\beta=\frac{-\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}-\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}\,,

and also obtain
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+(1-\beta)\int p_{\invariant}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}=0
. The opposite sign ensures that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}0<\beta<1
. Therefore,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta\mu_{0}+(1-\beta)\mu_{1}
integrates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Pol_{\leq s}^{G}=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}p_{\invariant}
exactly, and Lemma
7.1
implies that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta\mu_{0}^{G}+(1-\beta)\mu_{1}^{G}
integrates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Pol_{\leq s}
exactly.

To verify that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu_{0}^{G}
corresponds to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}X
, we compute

\displaystyle\int p\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{G}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|Gx_{i}|}{|X|}p(gx_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in Gx_{i}}p(x)=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}p(x)\,.

Similarly, we verify that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu_{1}^{G}
corresponds to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma
due to

\displaystyle\int p\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{G}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|G\gamma_{j}|\ell(\gamma_{j})}{\ell(\gamma)}\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma_{j})}\int_{g\gamma_{j}}p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|G\gamma_{j}|}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\int_{g\gamma_{j}}p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{G\gamma_{j}}p=\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}p\,.

Thus,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}(\gamma,X)
is a hybrid
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}s
-design.

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu:=\delta_{e_{1}}+\nu_{\gamma_{0}}
. Since
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p_{4,B_{3}}(e_{1})=\frac{2}{5}
, the defining equation (

) leads to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=\frac{-1/10}{-1/10-2/5}=\frac{1}{5}
.

(iii) The
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}32
vertices of the rhombic triacontahedron when projected onto the sphere, consist of the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}12

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}20

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\gamma_{0}
is the geodesic arc corresponding to an edge of the icosidodecahedron. As above, we calculate

so that (
17
) leads to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=\frac{45}{49}
.

(iv) The vertices are again a single orbit, so that we put
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mu=\delta_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\phi^{2}}}(\phi,1,0)}+\nu_{\gamma_{0}}
. We use

and obtain
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\beta=\frac{5}{21}
.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
4.2

We follow the first part of the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
.
According to the definition of the covering radius
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\delta:=\delta(\supp(\mu))
, there is
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that the closed ball
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{B}_{\delta/2}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{d}:\dist(x,y)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\}
of radius
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\delta/2
centered at
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}x
does not intersect
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\supp(\mu)
, i.e.,

(18)

\mathbb{B}_{\delta/2}(x)\cap\supp(\mu)=\emptyset.

Let us denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{S}^{d}
by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\Delta
and the identity operator by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}I
.
According to
[
10
, Lemma 5.2]
,
see also
[
8
]
for the original idea, there is a constants
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}c_{d}>0
and a
function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}p
supported on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\mathbb{B}_{\delta/2}(x)
such that

(19)

\|(I-\Delta)^{d}p\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\leq 1,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\delta^{2d}\leq c_{d}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p.

Since (
18
) implies
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
, the exact integration (
4
) and
[
8
, Theorem 2.12]
lead to

(20)

\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p\right|\leq c^{\prime}_{d}t^{-2d}\|(I-\Delta)^{d}p\|_{L^{1}}\,,

for some constant
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}c^{\prime}_{d}>0
.
By combining (
20
) with (
19
), we deduce

\delta^{2d}\leq c_{d}c_{d}^{\prime}t^{-2d}\|(I-\Delta)^{d}p\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\leq c_{d}c_{d}^{\prime}t^{-2d},

so that we conclude
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}\delta\leq C_{d}t^{-1}
, for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.3}C_{d}=(c_{d}c_{d}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2d}}
.
