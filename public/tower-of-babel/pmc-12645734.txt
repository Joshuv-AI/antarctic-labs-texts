A social ecological model mapping of factors shaping health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation: a scoping review
Authors: Huilin Zhou, Xiong Zhang, Mei Li, Rong Li, Jinhui Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough organ donation is a life-saving procedure, it faces significant challenges owing to the shortage of available organs. Health personnel play a vital role in promoting organ donation. However, their attitudes toward organ donation vary greatly. Understanding the factors that influence these attitudes is essential for developing effective strategies to enhance organ donation.MethodsIn accordance with the Arksey and O’Malley framework, a comprehensive scoping review was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CNKI, VIP and Wanfang. A systematic search was conducted from 1 January 2015 to 6 July 2025.Results22 cross-sectional studies of health personnel examining determinants of organ donation attitudes were retained for further analysis. According to the social ecological model, the factors influencing attitudes of health personnel toward organ donation can be divided into five categories: Individual level: demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitude, behavior, and experience; Interpersonal level: family and social relationships, professional activities; Organizational level: professional characteristics, training and education, work experience; Community level: cultural and religious factors, social participation; Public policy level: law and systems, medical policy.ConclusionsHealth personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation are shaped by a complex interplay of factors. Addressing these factors through targeted education, cultural sensitivity training, and policy reforms can increase the support for organ donation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13665-x.

Introduction

Organ transplantation has emerged as a highly effective treatment for end-stage organ failure, saving thousands of lives and significantly improving the quality of life of the recipients. This is evidenced by the rising number of global donors and improved post-transplant survival rates [1]. With advancements in organ donation and transplantation technologies, most countries now have related programmes in place [2]. However, a critical global shortage of organs persists, characterized by a severe imbalance between demand and supply [3, 4]. According to global data, only 10% of transplant candidates ultimately receive donor organs [5]. Despite having the highest deceased donor rate globally, Spain still has significant transplant waiting lists, with 17.4 lung transplant candidates per million people in 2023 [6]. China’s data further illustrates this crisis. According to the China Organ Transplant Development Report (2022), waiting lists for transplantation have grown steadily from 2015 to 2022. By 2022, 135,242 patients were awaiting liver, kidney, heart, and lung transplantation. However, only 20,244 transplant surgeries were performed (14.97%), leaving 96,706 individuals on the waiting list by the end of the year [7].

Multiple factors contribute to the low organ donation rates. Crucially, the attitudes and behaviors of health personnel directly influence the willingness of potential donors and their families to donate [8, 9]. They identify potential donors, transmit information, communicate with families, implement donation procedures, and influence public education regarding organ donation. Research confirms that positive attitudes among health personnel are essential for promoting organ donation [10]. Studies have demonstrated a dose-response relationship: stronger professional endorsement correlates with more active persuasion of potential donors, thereby increasing donation rates [11]. In clinical practice, these professionals are responsible for identifying donors and acquiring family consent, and their attitudes and knowledge directly determine the success of the donation [12]. Notably, the involvement of nurse coordinators significantly increases donor numbers and family consent rates [13]. Nevertheless, the attitudes of health personnel toward donations are influenced by multiple factors. Consequently, as the recent literature emphasizes, it is crucial to understand these determinants to improve donation rates and enhance clinical commitment to this life-saving endeavor. Extensive research has examined the attitudes of health personnel toward organ donation and the factors that influencing them [14]. These attitudes are recognized as multifactorial constructs influenced by interacting variables across socio-behavioral dimensions [14].

The social-ecological model (SEM) provides a comprehensive framework for systematically organizing this complex literature [15], and has been shown to be effective in analyzing the intricate health behaviors surrounding organ donation [16, 17]. This study used SEM to map the multifactorial landscape that shapes attitudes toward organ donation, establish a structured taxonomy for heterogeneous evidence, and identify critical knowledge gaps through its hierarchical architecture. The study also aimed to inform multilevel interventions targeting individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy domains [15].

Notably, no existing review has synthesized these determinants through the lens of SEM. Therefore, we conducted a systematic analysis to establish theoretical foundations for improving clinical donation practices and developing evidence-based strategies to enhance organ donation rates.

Methods

Study design

This review employs the methodology framework of Arksey and O ‘Malley [18] using the PCC strategy to identify the overarching research question. Through literature search based on keywords, two reviewers independently conducted title, abstract, and full-text screening. The included literature underwent systematic extraction of key elements, with evidence mapping performed using a socio-ecological model. This approach systematically synthesized evidence regarding factors influencing healthcare personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation. Comprehensive literature searches and screening of the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry confirmed that no scoping reviews had been published or were ongoing that specifically addressed these factors. The study protocol was registered with OSF (https://osf.io/ypb7s/). The review strictly adhered to PRISMA-ScR [19], ensuring methodological rigor and maintaining scientific integrity.

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of ten published study databases: PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CNKI, VIP and Wangfang. The search was conducted from January, 1 2015 to July, 6 2025, combining medical subject headings and their synonyms using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ to identify factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation. The PubMed search strategy is presented in Fig. 1. Only records in English and Chinese were retained and imported into EndNote 20 for deduplication and screening.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met all the following criteria: (a) participants were practicing health personnel, (b) findings explicitly addressed factors influencing attitudes toward organ donation, (c) the design was cross-sectional, excluding qualitative research and (d) the full text was published in English or Chinese. Studies were excluded if they were duplicates or if full text could not be obtained.

Study selection

After importing all records into EndNote and removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text assessment. Any disagreements were resolved through discussions between the two reviewers or by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data were systematically extracted from the full text following the final selection of eligible studies. The extraction protocol was divided into two components. Component 1 calculated the frequency of research level characteristics (language of publication, sample size, number of years of publication, research object) and captured bibliographic characteristics such as the first author, publication year, study population, sample size, and measurement instruments. Component 2 extracted the primary findings reported in the results section of each study’s report. Two independent reviewers conducted thematic Content Analysis: line-by-line coding of extracted factors, categorizing similar codes as descriptive themes. These themes were subsequently mapped to the five tiers of the socio-ecological model (individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy).

These elements enable methodological rigor and relevance to be critically evaluated. All studies were published between 2015 and 2024 and involved hospital-based physicians and nursing staff members. Seven studies specifically sampled intensive care unit (ICU) professionals. The sample size ranged from 150 to > 1,000 respondents. Attitudinal assessment instruments include researcher-developed questionnaires incorporating five items from validated scales or fully validated instruments with established psychometric properties.

Synthesized findings

Guided by the social-ecological framework, multilevel determinants of health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation were categorized into five interconnected levels (Table 2). The two reviewers independently extract each factor to identify the primary SEM levels. If the original author explicitly states that a factor operates across multiple dimensions (e.g., listing “work income” as both a personal and occupational characteristic), this influencing factor is coded with dual dimensions. In case of disagreements, other team members will discuss and resolve the coding through collaborative discussion.

Synthesis of determinants

Individual level factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation

Seventeen studies identified the individual-level determinants of health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation. Demographic characteristics have emerged as predominant influences, with age and gender demonstrating complex relationships [22, 23, 26, 28, 30, 34, 35, 40]. While most studies reported more positive attitudes among women [22, 40], others indicated greater male acceptance [30, 34] and advanced age consistently correlated negatively with donation-related fear [23, 26, 28, 34, 35]. Chinese investigations specifically revealed the impact of family status (children/residence) [38, 40], political affiliation [40], marital status [38, 40], and income level [30, 38, 39], whereas chronic illness was identified as a potential factor in only one study [21]. Knowledge variations regarding brain death protocols and donation procedures significantly shape attitudes [21, 22, 25, 32], with value-based convictions, including beliefs that donation saves lives [21], aligns with divine will (e.g., “does not violate God’s will”) [21], and addresses organ shortages [34], enhancing receptiveness. Conversely, concerns about life support withdrawal [32], perceived medical risks [34], and bodily integrity violations [35] diminish willingness. Positive motivators include professional pride in donation work [21], willingness to discuss [25], perception of bodily preservation [21], self-recognized transplant needs [34], and acknowledgment of donation necessity [25]. Crucially, personal experiences such as family discussions, blood donation history [24], donor card possession [36], experience persuading donors [28], acceptance of cremation [25, 34], and personal/family donation involvement [27, 41] collectively shaped favorable attitudes.

Interpersonal level factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation

Interpersonal dynamics significantly affected the attitudes of the health personnel toward organ donation. Family discussions regarding organ donation [21, 24, 25, 27, 32, 34], family members’ attitudes [21, 24], and partners’ stances [34] collectively influence the propensity for personal donation. Furthermore, prior experience with relative consultations [27], collaboration with non-governmental organizations [25], and professional recommendations for tissue/organ donation [24, 32] were significantly associated with enhanced attitudinal receptiveness.

Organizational level factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation

Organizational determinants significantly shaped health personnel’ donation attitudes, encompassing professional category [26, 29, 31–34], hierarchical title [38, 39, 41], workplace setting [23, 35], clinical experience duration [27, 29], employment status [39], clinical specialty [20, 35], and nurse-to-patient ratios [39]. Higher professional titles [38, 39, 41], extended work experience [27, 29], and permanent employment status [39] were consistently correlated with enhanced willingness to donate. Personnel in hospitals with active donation programs, particularly those regularly managing brain death cases in the critical care (ICU) or neurosurgical departments [20, 23, 35], demonstrated more positive attitudes than their counterparts in other units. Conversely, an inadequate nurse-to-patient ratio predicted a reluctance to donate. Educational attainment [21, 30, 35, 37–40], specialized training in donation protocols [20, 32], and direct experience with brain death determination or donation procedures [20, 27] emerged as significant positive influences.

Community level factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation

Cultural backgrounds and religious beliefs exert significant community-level influences on donation attitudes, with notable variations across ethnic groups. Available evidence indicates that health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation differ significantly across racial and ethnic groups [30]. Non-affiliated individuals and adherents of donation-supportive faith exhibit heightened receptivity [25, 36]. Engagement with organizations such as the Red Cross [29] and participation in related social initiatives further cultivate favorable dispositions toward donations [25].

Public policy level factors influencing health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation

Legal and policy frameworks significantly influenced health personnel’s attitudes toward donations. Concerns regarding presumed consent legislation permitting non-voluntary organ retrieval [25] and illegal organ trade practices [21] substantially diminish the willingness to donate. Paradoxically, employment in higher-tier medical institutions [20, 27] was correlated with enhanced donation propensity. Most significantly, robust knowledge dissemination regarding organ donation coordination protocols has emerged as a critical positive attitudinal determinant [41].

Discussion

Individual level: the core role of cognition, belief and personal experience

This synthesis shows that health personnel’s attitudes toward organ donation are fundamentally shaped by individual-level determinants, particularly cognitive frameworks, belief systems, and lived experiences. Gender effects are shaped by the intersection of biological altruism, knowledge-mediated risk appraisal and culturally scripted roles: emotional, family-mediated framing amplifies women’s empathy and favourable attitudes [42], whereas technical-heroic framing aligns with male self-efficacy [43], reversing the gender gradient. Patriarchal versus equal-citizen contexts account for cross-national heterogeneity, underscoring the need to treat gender as a dynamic, culturally moderated variable. Age-mediated differences reflect non-linear intersections of cohort-specific bodily-integrity scripts [44], trust trajectories toward brain-death technologies [45], and micro-contextual interactions, producing heterogeneous patterns shaped by uneven occupational distributions across age bands. Attitudes are uniquely influenced by marital status, parenthood, and political affiliation, potentially reflecting Confucian cultural imperatives [30, 38]. The interplay of kinship-network obligations and Confucian norms—where filial piety and body-integrity ideals subordinate individual autonomy to family consensus—renders marital status a key determinant of medical staff’s organ-donation attitudes [46]. Communist Party members demonstrated a greater willingness to donate, possibly due to increased ideological indoctrination emphasizing social responsibility [12, 31]. And what we found the variable effect of Party affiliation arises from divergent operational definitions, sample heterogeneity, and dual socialization. Educational interventions targeting brain-death criteria and donation procedures are essential for cultivating the right attitude [47]. Donation-positive behavior is motivated by a combination of altruism and self-interest. A history of blood donation, possession of a donor card, and successful persuasion by others reinforced prosocial identity [48]. Specifically, blood donation redefined physical ‘loss’ as an extension of internalized altruism in the context of the pandemic [49]. Carrying a donor card makes a public commitment, aligning future actions with an established identity [50]. Persuading others can enhance self-efficacy and consolidate normative identity. Crucially, these mechanisms can collectively explain why a conviction to save lives, the experience of donating, and persuasive practice can robustly predict positive attitudes.

Interpersonal level: the decisive influence of family networks

Family networks have a decisive influence at the interpersonal level, significantly shaping health personnel’s intentions to donate through familial interactions and attitudes toward donation [46]. Family consent operates as both a legal threshold and a practical veto [51]: where statutes mandate unanimity it blocks donation, while in jurisdictions without formal requirements relatives’ anticipated refusal still acts as a psychological brake on the attitude-to-behaviour transition. Empirical evidence confirms that personal donation commitment is enhanced by family discussions (particularly with partners) regarding donations, the awareness of familial willingness to donate, and perceived spousal support. Participation in relatives’ donation processes further reinforced this effect. This phenomenon occurs through dual sociocognitive mechanisms. Familial embeddedness in life-death decisions: Rooted in Confucian family values and communication dynamics, the family unit acts as the primary ethical arbiter for significant choices [52–54]. Resolving cognitive dissonance: Supportive family interactions act as pre-commitment signals, reducing psychological conflict [55]. Health personnel who anticipate spousal consensus proactively align their behaviors to prevent future discord, a behavioral adaptation known as ‘anticipated concordance.’ Clinicians who engage in explicit familial donation discussions demonstrate greater confidence during end-of-life conversations, as the ‘emotional safety net’ of partner support bolsters their self-efficacy. This security facilitates behavioral translation: when concerns about relational stability diminish, intentions crystallize into firm commitment [11]. Prior involvement in family consultations, NGO collaboration, or professional donation advice concurrently activates cognitive, emotional, and social pathways that significantly enhance acceptance. These experiences provide emotional rehearsal to alleviate bodily-integrity anxiety, and embed a “professional-hero” identity, thereby generating a public commitment effect. Future research should focus on developing customized family communication tools, such as dialogue guides and FAQs, to help individuals express their intentions regarding blood donation, understand their family members’ attitudes, address any resistance, and enhance the effectiveness of communication. Introducing joint participation in educational programs or awareness campaigns can help couples align their values better and work together toward shared goals.

Organizational level: occupational factors have a significant shaping effect

Four interrelated mechanisms significantly shape the attitudes of health personnel toward donation. Specialty-specific exposure differentials: Attitudinal variance across medical specialties reflects divergent clinical focus and donation awareness [13]. The attitudinal gap between physicians and nurses reflects an authority-knowledge-emotion asymmetry [56]: Physicians’ authority over brain-death decisions—boosted by heroic narratives—cuts uncertainty and boosts positive exposure; nurses’ caregiving role, fragmented information, and heavier emotional labour heighten risk perception and body-preservation concerns, yielding more cautious attitudes. Research should investigate the root causes of these differences in professional attitudes and address existing problems with the aim of fostering positive responses. Hierarchical knowledge capacity gradients: Junior practitioners with limited tenure often have negative perceptions, owing to knowledge deficits and limited response capacity [57]. The predominance of young and middle-aged staff in hospital workforces whose relative inexperience and cognitive uncertainty foster one-sided, limited perceptions of organ donation. Stratified precision interventions that target these gaps can enhance attitudinal support. Workplace exposure and systemic constraints: The routine management of brain-dead patients in tertiary hospitals with active transplant programs is associated with improved positive outcomes owing to increased procedural exposure among health personnel [57]. Healthcare workers in the ICU and neurology departments are key contributors to organ donation, and require specific support. Crucially, inadequate nurse-patient ratios objectively undermine the willingness to donate by exacerbating workload pressures. Optimal staffing levels and dedicated donor coordinators create a psychological safety zone that reduces burnout rates, secures time for family communication, and reinforces professional confidence [57]. Education-mediated knowledge reinforcement: Hospitals should implement tiered multidimensional educational initiatives on organ donation. Empirical evidence confirms that such programs effectively improve the receptiveness of health personnel to organ donation [58]. Crucially, advanced education and targeted training increase medical knowledge, thereby reducing fear-based clinical concerns and improving procedural literacy [13].

Community level: cultural, religious background and social participation at the community level significantly influence attitudes

Attitudes toward organ donation are profoundly influenced by community-level determinants, particularly cultural traditions, religious doctrines, and social participation [59, 60]. Religion-cultural embeddedness: Consistent with prior evidence, worldviews concerning life, death, and bodily integrity fundamentally shape decision making [59]. Those who are not affiliated with faith and those from faiths that support organ donation demonstrate heightened receptivity, reflecting the doctrinal compatibility of the principles of organ donation [61]. This requires a critical understanding of diverse religious frameworks and targeted engagement with religious institutions to develop effective cross-cultural educational interventions [62]. Social participation dynamics: Involvement in organizations such as the Red Cross or volunteering for donation-related activities are associated with positive attitudes. This occurs through dual pathways that involve altruistic reinforcement of ecosystems. Community networks cultivate prosocial norms and responsibilities among their members [63] and identity formation: Voluntary associations establish social reference groups that normalize donation behavior and strengthen collective identity. Voluntary organisations convert donation into self-realisation for health personnel. repeated collective activities and shared value narratives continuously reinforce this self-categorization“group identity”, while this role identification provides belonging and purpose, sustaining prosocial engagement [64]. Future research should prioritize interventions that strategically leverage community mechanisms to bridge the gap between organ supply and demand. This requires the development of culturally tailored educational modules co-created with religious authorities to ensure doctrinal alignment, the establishment of institution-community participation pipelines via hospital–volunteer partnerships, and harnessing reference group influence through peer-led ambassador programmes. Together, these evidence-based strategies address religion-cultural barriers while cultivating prosocial behavioral patterns through structured community engagement.

Public policy level: ensure institutional trust and system support, and establish a framework for trust and convenience

Policy frameworks and system support form the macro-environmental basis for attitude formation, and have dual effects. Concerns about regulations, particularly those relating to non-consensual organ retrieval under presumed consent legislation and illegal organ trade practices, erode the trust of health personnel, thereby negatively impacting their willingness to donate organs [65]. This highlights the importance of establishing transparent and equitable legal oversight systems to restore institutional postmortem. Conversely, a hierarchical hospital infrastructure is crucial for providing support: Tertiary institutions with coordinated mechanisms demonstrate an increased willingness to donate through integrated resources and specialized teams. Crucially, organ donation coordinators can significantly improve conversion rates by providing essential guidance to frontline clinicians. Empirical evidence shows that institutions that implement standardized protocols, including dedicated coordinators, validated brain death determination pathways, and 24/7 specialist availability, achieve 30–40% higher donation success rates than those that do not implement them [13]. The mandatory deployment of coordinators in tertiary hospitals increased consent rates by 25%, owing to optimized family communication and reinforced clinician confidence [66]. Consequently, disseminating knowledge about coordination systems and establishing professional teams constitutes high-yield interventions to foster positive attitudes. However, the effectiveness of coordinator deployment and presumed-consent laws is culture-contingent: in collectivist settings where family veto is normative, opt-out plus coordinator yields smaller gains than in individualist regions, suggesting that policy packages must be culturally tailored—combining legal reform with household-level dialogue tools and micro-ritual interventions to dismantle the “family veto culture” rather than merely adding.

Limitation

It is acknowledged that there are significant methodological limitations to this review. Firstly, the study’s reliance on observational designs alone prevents deeper mechanistic insights into attitude formation, which can be achieved through rich contextual exploration of qualitative methodologies. Moreover, the scope of the literature search was confined to Chinese and English publications, a decision that may have resulted in the exclusion of culturally distinct perspectives from nonindexed sources. Furthermore, due to the absence of an evaluation of the study’s quality, the quality of the reports exhibited variability, which may have led to the production of biased results. Consequently, future investigations should incorporate mixed-method approaches and expand their linguistic scope to enhance the theoretical saturation and cross-cultural validity of the findings.

Conclusion

This synthesis revealed that health professionals’ attitudes toward organ donation are complex and dynamic, characterized by the interplay of multiple determinants. To optimize donation outcomes, future initiatives must employ integrated frameworks that analyze the underlying causal mechanisms through an interdisciplinary lens and implement evidence-based, precision interventions that are dynamically calibrated across individual, organizational, and policy dimensions.
