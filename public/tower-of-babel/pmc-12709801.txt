Redefining cardiac risk in breast radiotherapy: a substructure-based dosimetric and biomarker correlation study
Authors: Rishi P. Nair, Depanshu Aggarwal, Atul Gupta, Bharti Devnani, Puneet Pareek, Akanksha Solanki, Parmod Kumar, Jeewan Ram Bishnoi, Dharma Ram Poonia, Nivedita Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the mean heart dose (MHD) as a predictor of radiation exposure to cardiac substructures and its association with markers of subclinical cardiotoxicity in breast cancer (BC) patients undergoing radiotherapy (RT). Although MHD provides an overall estimate of cardiac exposure, it does not capture the heterogeneous dose distribution across critical cardiac substructures.Materials and methodsIn an ambispective study design, dosimetric parameters, including MHD and dose–volume metrics for the left anterior descending artery (LAD), left circumflex artery (LCx), and left main coronary artery (LMCA), were analyzed in a cohort of BC patients receiving adjuvant RT. Early cardiotoxicity markers, such as N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), ejection fraction (EF), and the Tei index, were evaluated. The relationship between MHD and radiation doses to specific cardiac substructures, as well as their association with cardiac biomarker levels, was assessed.ResultsAmong 104 patients, the overall MHD was 6.4 ± 5.4 Gy. Left-sided BC patients received a significantly higher MHD of 10.5 ± 4.37 Gy. The LAD mean dose was 16.12 ± 15.19 Gy for the entire cohort, increasing to 30.17 ± 5.74 Gy in left-sided cases. Patients with higher MHDs were more likely to receive elevated doses to the LMCA and LCx; however, a notable discrepancy was observed in LAD dose correlation. Regression analysis showed that MHD explained only 9.4% of the variance in LAD mean dose compared to 65% for LMCA. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified heart Dmax (43.12 Gy), heart V25 (13%), and LAD mean dose (28.92 Gy) as strong predictors of subclinical cardiotoxicity, whereas MHD was not.ConclusionOur findings confirm that MHD is an inadequate surrogate for predicting the heterogeneous dose distribution among cardiac substructures. Elevated NT-proBNP levels and reduced EF were significantly associated with higher substructure doses, highlighting the need for individualized, substructure-specific dose constraints in RT planning. These findings support the use of advanced cardiac-sparing techniques such as deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and proton therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-025-00408-4.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide and in India, accounting for a substantial proportion of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in both developed and developing countries [1, 2]. Its management is inherently multidisciplinary, encompassing surgery, radiotherapy (RT), and systemic therapies such as chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, and targeted biological agents. With advancements in treatment modalities and early detection, survival outcomes for breast cancer patients have markedly improved over the past few decades. However, this improvement has also underscored the need to minimize long-term treatment-related toxicities, including radiation-induced cardiotoxicity.

Cardiac complications from RT, particularly in the therapeutic dose range, can include a wide spectrum of acute and late effects such as coronary artery disease (CAD), valvular dysfunction, pericarditis, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and congestive heart failure. Acute effects like pericarditis and pericardial effusion may emerge during or shortly after radiation treatment, while chronic complications, especially CAD and heart failure, may not manifest until years later [3]. Among various functional parameters, diastolic dysfunction has been identified as an early and more sensitive marker of radiation-induced cardiotoxicity compared to changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). This dysfunction has been shown to correlate with dose–volume parameters of cardiac irradiation in patients undergoing RT for left-sided breast cancer [4, 5].

Currently, the mean heart dose (MHD) remains the standard dosimetric surrogate for cardiac exposure in breast RT across most centers. Darby et al. demonstrated a linear increase of 7.4% in the risk of major coronary events per Gy of radiation received, even at low doses, suggesting no safe threshold for cardiac exposure [6]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the risk of adverse cardiac events may be more closely associated with the mean and maximum doses received by individual cardiac substructures, particularly the left anterior descending artery (LAD), rather than the overall MHD [7, 8]. Dosimetric analyses reveal that the LAD is often the most heavily irradiated substructure during left-sided breast RT, correlating with a heightened incidence of LAD stenosis [9, 10]. Moreover, studies on patients treated with three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT) have shown that left ventricular volumes receiving more than 5 Gy (LV-V5) are significantly associated with subsequent coronary events, suggesting the need for more granular substructure-level evaluation [11].

Subclinical cardiac damage may begin weeks to months after RT and precede symptomatic heart disease by years. Such damage can be identified through anatomical imaging or functional testing. Among biochemical markers, troponin I (TnI) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) have shown promise in detecting early radiation-related myocardial injury [12, 13]. Echocardiographic assessment of myocardial dysfunction, particularly through global longitudinal strain (GLS) analysis using automated two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE), offers a more sensitive and reproducible method compared to conventional LVEF measurements [14].

In this study, we aimed to quantify the radiation dose delivered to the whole heart (MHD) and key cardiac substructures—namely the LAD, left circumflex artery (LCx), and left main coronary artery (LMCA)—in patients undergoing adjuvant RT for breast cancer. We further evaluated whether these doses correlate with subclinical cardiac dysfunction as assessed by biochemical markers and echocardiographic parameters. Our goal was to assess the predictive value of MHD versus substructure-specific doses in estimating early radiation-induced cardiac injury, with the broader objective of informing more tailored and cardioprotective RT planning.

Aims and objectives

This study aimed to investigate whether the doses received by different cardiac substructures can serve as surrogate predictors for cardiotoxicity, as compared to the mean heart dose (MHD).

Study design and patient selection

This ambispective observational study was conducted in the Department of Radiation Oncology at AIIMS Jodhpur. Patients with biopsy-proven breast cancer who received adjuvant radiotherapy following surgery (modified radical mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery) between January 2019 and December 2021 were included. Data were collected retrospectively from treatment records, and patients presenting for routine follow-up were prospectively evaluated for subclinical cardiotoxicity using blood and imaging biomarkers.

Radiotherapy and dosimetric evaluation

All patients were planned with CT-based simulation. The LAD, LCx, and LMCA were contoured according to established guidelines (Fig. 1). Dose–volume histogram (DVH) parameters—including mean (Dmean), maximum (Dmax), and minimum (Dmin) doses—were extracted for each cardiac substructure and for the whole heart. Additionally, volumetric parameters such as V25 of the heart (volume receiving ≥ 25 Gy) and V15 of the LAD (volume receiving ≥ 15 Gy) were analyzed.

Cardiotoxicity markers

All patients presenting for routine follow-up were prospectively evaluated for subclinical cardiotoxicity. This assessment included serum biomarkers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HS-CRP) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), electrocardiographic findings including corrected QT interval (QTc) and T wave peak-to-end time, and echocardiographic indices such as myocardial performance index (MPI) and ejection fraction (EF).

Subclinical cardiotoxicity was defined based on NT-proBNP and LVEF values, wherein an NT-proBNP level above 300 pg/mL, and/or an LVEF below 50%, was classified as subclinical cardiotoxicity.

Sample size consideration

A total of 104 patients were included based on the availability of complete treatment and follow-up data during the study period. This pragmatic sample was considered sufficient for correlation and regression analysis based on previous studies (mainly BACCARAT Study [15, 16]) evaluating cardiac substructure dosimetry and early markers of cardiotoxicity in breast cancer radiotherapy.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the physical radiation doses, expressed in Gray (Gy), was performed. Continuous variables were presented as means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of distribution. The chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables, while the Wilcoxon Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-parametric comparisons of continuous variables. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between parametric and non-parametric variables, respectively. The relationship between MHD and mean doses to various cardiac substructures was further evaluated using linear regression analysis. The coefficient of determination (r²) was reported to indicate the proportion of variance in the substructure Dmean that could be explained by MHD.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to identify dosimetric predictors of subclinical cardiotoxicity, with NT-proBNP used as the reference biomarker. Cut-off values were determined using Youden’s index. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 104 patients were enrolled in our study, 59 (56.7%) of whom were more than 50 years of age. There were equal numbers of patients with left and right breast cancer. A majority (61.5%) of the patients were obese. Thirty-two (30.8%) patients had existing comorbidities (some with multiple comorbidities), including 1 patient with hyperthyroidism, 7 patients with hypothyroidism, 22 patients with hypertension, 16 patients with diabetes mellitus, and 1 patient with coronary artery disease (clinically stable, with well-controlled disease and no recent cardiac events, interventions, or symptoms relevant to the study period). Addictions were present in 4 patients (3 were tobacco chewers, and 1 was a smoker). Thirty-five (33.7%) females were premenopausal, whereas 69 (66.3%) were postmenopausal. Among the 104 patients, 5 (4.8%) were stage 1, 56 (53.8%) were stage 2, 22 (21.2%) were stage 3, and 2 (1.9%) were stage 4. Sixty-four (61.5%) and 40 (38.5%) patients had node-positive and node-negative disease, respectively. The tumor subtype was hormone positive in 76 (73.1%) patients, HER2 was enriched in 9 (8.7%) patients, and TNBC was enriched in 19 (18.3%) patients (Table 1).

Dose-volume histogram (DVH) parameters

The mean heart dose (MHD) in our study was 6.40 ± 5.41 Gy, ranging from a minimum of 0.79 ± 0.71 Gy to a maximum of 32.55 ± 16.00 Gy. The mean volumes of the hearts receiving 5 Gy, 10 Gy and 25 Gy were 27.98 ± 31.16%, 20.14 ± 23.49% and 7.71 ± 8.97% respectively.

The mean doses to the LAD, LMCA and LCx were 16.13 ± 15.19 Gy (range: 2.89–23.80 Gy), 4.38 ± 6.67 Gy (range: 2.98–5.71 Gy) and 2.87 ± 4.54 Gy (range: 1.78–4.44 Gy) respectively. The mean volume of the LAD artery receiving 15 Gy was 39.14 ± 39% (33%) (Table 3).

Association of mean heart dose with substructure radiation exposure

Table 5 shows distinct differences in the radiation doses delivered to cardiac substructures between patients with lower and higher overall mean heart doses. Each group comprised 26 patients. For the LAD, Dmax was identical between the two groups, with 46.2% in both the MHD < 9.7 Gy and MHD > 9.7 Gy groups having LAD Dmax < 45 Gy and 53.8% in each group exceeding this threshold. However, when Dmean was considered, a noticeable shift was observed. In the lower MHD group, 53.8% of patients had an LAD Dmean < 30 Gy compared with 38.5% (10/26) in the higher MHD group, with the proportion of patients receiving an LAD Dmean > 30 Gy increasing from 46.2% (12/26) to 61.5% (16/26) as MHD increased.

The dose–volume patterns of the LMCA and the LCx more or less mirrored those of the MHD. In patients with MHD < 9.7 Gy, the majority maintained low doses: 80.8% (21/26) had an LMCA Dmax < 2.5 Gy and 84.6% (22/26) had an LMCA Dmean < 2 Gy. In contrast, in the MHD > 9.7 Gy group, these figures reversed, with 80.8% (21/26) of patients receiving an LMCA Dmax > 2.5 Gy and 80.8% (21/26) receiving an LMCA Dmean > 2 Gy. A similar pattern was observed for the LCx: in the low MHD group, 88.5% (23/26) had an LCx Dmax < 2.8 Gy and 92.3% (24/26) had an LCx Dmean < 2 Gy, whereas in the high MHD group, the majority shifted to higher doses, with 84.6% (22/26) exceeding an LCx Dmax of 2.8 Gy and 73.1% (19/26) exceeding an LCx Dmean of 2 Gy.

While considering both left- and right-sided cancers, there appears to be a strong correlation between MHD and doses to cardiac substructures (Spearman’s correlation coefficient > 0.8 for all cases). However, when only left-sided cancers were considered, the LAD mean dose was not significantly correlated with MHD, and the other left-sided structures were less strongly correlated than the overall DVH parameters (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the mean heart dose (MHD) as a predictor of radiation exposure to specific cardiac substructures and its association with early markers of subclinical cardiotoxicity in breast cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy (RT). Consistent with previous findings, we observed that while MHD provides a general estimate of cardiac exposure, it does not fully account for the heterogeneous dose distribution across individual cardiac substructures, particularly the left anterior descending artery (LAD).

The mean heart dose (MHD) in our study was 6.4 ± 5.4 Gy for the overall cohort, whereas for left-sided breast cancer patients, it was significantly higher at 10.5 ± 4.37 Gy. Similarly, the mean dose to the left anterior descending artery (LAD) was 16.12 ± 15.19 Gy overall, but in left-sided breast cancer patients, it increased substantially to 30.17 ± 5.74 Gy. This difference can be attributed to LAD contours falling in the apex of the heart, through which the tangents pass, thereby receiving the brunt of the dose.

The biological plausibility of our findings is supported by the anatomical significance of the LAD, which supplies a large portion of the left ventricular myocardium. Radiation-induced injury to the LAD may lead to localized ischemia, microvascular damage, and eventual fibrosis—mechanisms that underpin the observed early rise in NT-proBNP and reduction in ejection fraction.

The results from Table 4 indicate that patients with higher overall heart doses (MHD >9.7 Gy) are significantly more likely to receive higher doses to critical cardiac substructures, particularly the LMCA and LCx; however, there was a marked discrepancy between the MHD and LAD. These data underscore that although MHD offers a general indication of cardiac exposure, it does not sufficiently capture the heterogeneous dose distribution among specific cardiac substructures. Our findings are consistent with the BACCARAT study [15, 16], which also demonstrated significant heterogeneity in dose distribution among cardiac substructures, reinforcing the limitations of MHD in predicting individual substructure exposure.

Although MHD remains widely used in clinical practice due to its simplicity and ease of extraction from treatment planning systems, our findings reinforce that it may inadequately reflect the true radiation burden on critical cardiac substructures, especially in left-sided breast cancer. This underscores the importance of integrating substructure-specific dose constraints into standard practice.

Our regression analysis demonstrated that MHD could explain only 9.4% of the variance in the LAD mean dose but accounted for 65% of the variance in the LMCA mean dose. This discrepancy underscores the heterogeneous dose distribution among cardiac substructures and suggests that relying solely on MHD may not be sufficient to predict individual substructure exposure [6, 17].

Furthermore, markers of subclinical cardiotoxicity, such as NT-proBNP and ejection fraction (EF), demonstrated moderate correlations with doses to specific cardiac regions. Elevated NT-proBNP levels were associated with increased LAD Dmax and mean dose, as well as increased heart V25, suggesting early myocardial strain and possible dysfunction [12]. Similarly, EF showed a significant inverse correlation with heart, LAD, and LCx volumes, with LAD volumes showing a stronger correlation than heart volumes, reinforcing prior evidence that exposure to these substructures can impact cardiac function and that the volumes of these structures exposed have vital clinical implications [9, 14, 15, 18].

Our receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further reinforces the importance of dosimetric parameters for individual cardiac substructures over the global mean heart dose. Specifically, we found that a heart Dmax of 43.12 Gy, a heart V25 of 13%, and a LAD mean dose of 28.92 Gy had excellent discriminatory ability (AUC >0.8) for detecting subclinical cardiotoxicity via NT-proBNP. Importantly, the MHD was not able to classify subclinical cardiotoxicity, whereas volumetric markers and point dose estimates such as Dmax and V25 were robust predictors. These findings suggest that high doses delivered to relatively small volumes of the heart—particularly to critical substructures such as the LAD—are more predictive of subclinical cardiotoxicity than the overall mean heart dose. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that dosimetric parameters of individual cardiac regions are superior predictors of cardiac events compared to global metrics [8, 11, 16].

Comparing our findings with historical data, Darby et al. [6] established a linear relationship between MHD and major coronary events, with a 7.4% increase in risk per additional Gy. Our study extends this understanding by demonstrating that while MHD correlates well with overall heart dose exposure, it fails to fully predict doses to the LAD in left-sided breast cancer, a key contributor to radiation-induced coronary artery disease. Additionally, previous research has suggested that left ventricular volume receiving >5 Gy (LV-V5) might be a stronger predictor of cardiac events than MHD [19], reinforcing the need for refined dose constraints targeting individual substructures. The BACCARAT study [15] also highlighted the importance of individual substructure dose assessment, particularly in evaluating the risk of radiation-induced coronary artery disease.

Despite our relatively modest sample size, the consistency of our findings with international cohorts, including the BACCARAT study, suggests a degree of external validity and reinforces their potential applicability across diverse practice settings and populations.

Our results support the ongoing global efforts to develop formal, evidence-based dose constraints for individual cardiac substructures in breast cancer radiotherapy planning. Incorporating such constraints into planning protocols may enhance cardiac safety, particularly for patients at higher baseline cardiovascular risk.

The ambispective nature of our study and the small sample size used to draw conclusions are limitations that need to be addressed in a prospective trial setting. Although all patients underwent CT-based simulation, this study did not include a comprehensive assessment of coronary calcification, due to technical limitations and the quality of the CT simulation scans. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study, as the inclusion of coronary calcification data could potentially provide additional insights into cardiac risk stratification. Future prospective studies incorporating dedicated coronary calcification assessment are warranted to further elucidate this aspect.

The clinical implications of these findings highlight the necessity of individualized treatment planning to reduce unnecessary radiation exposure to critical cardiac regions. Advanced techniques such as deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and proton therapy may provide better cardiac sparing, particularly for patients at higher risk of radiation-induced cardiotoxicity [20]. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up periods and larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and further refine cardiac dose constraints.

Finally, while our study focused on early subclinical cardiotoxicity markers, longitudinal follow-up will be essential to determine whether these early functional and biomarker changes translate into clinically overt cardiac morbidity over time.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the limitations of MHD as a sole predictor of radiation doses to cardiac substructures, particularly the LAD. While MHD remains a useful parameter, substructure-specific dose constraints should be incorporated into radiotherapy planning to minimize cardiotoxicity. The correlation of cardiac substructure doses with early markers of cardiotoxicity further emphasizes the need for tailored cardiac dose assessment in breast cancer radiotherapy to improve long-term cardiac outcomes.
