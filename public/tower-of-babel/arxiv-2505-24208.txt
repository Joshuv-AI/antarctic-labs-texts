Bootstrapping LLM Robustness for VLM Safety via Reducing the Pretraining Modality Gap
Wenhan Yang, Spencer Stice, Ali Payani, Baharan Mirzasoleiman

Abstract
Ensuring Vision-Language Models (VLMs) generate safe outputs is crucial for their reliable deployment. However, LVLMs suffer from drastic safety degradation compared to their LLM backbone. Even blank or irrelevant images can trigger LVLMs to generate harmful responses to prompts that would otherwise be refused in text-only contexts. The modality gap between image and text representations has been recently hypothesized to contribute to safety degradation of LVLMs. However, if and how the amount of modality gap affects LVLMs’ safety is not studied. In this work, we show that the amount of modality gap is highly inversely correlated with VLMs’ safety. Then, we show that this modality gap is introduced during pretraining LVLMs and persists through fine-tuning. Inspired by this observation, we propose a regularization to reduce the modality gap during pretraining. Our extensive experiments on LLaVA v1.5, ShareGPT4V, and MiniGPT-4 show that our method substantially improves safety alignment of LVLMs, reducing unsafe rate by up to 16.3 % 16.3\% without compromising performance, and can further boost existing defenses by up to 18.2 % 18.2\% .

Introduction

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) which combine language understanding capability of Large Language models (LLMs) with visual perceptions of vision encoders
have demonstrated impressive performance across diverse tasks, such as visual question answering, image captioning, and multimodal dialogues
(
Alayrac et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2023a
;
Chen et al., 2023b
)
.
To avoid generating harmful or toxic responses, LLMs go through a safety alignment process, where they are trained on human preference data
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
, and refined through red teaming, and safety-oriented pretraining and fine-tuning
(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Grattafiori et al., 2024
;
Wei et al., 2023
;
Kumar et al., 2023
)
.
However, when safety-aligned LLMs are paired with vision encoders, the resulting LVLMs suffer from drastic safety degradation, where even blank or irrelevant images can trigger harmful responses to prompts that would otherwise be refused in text-only contexts

(
Liu et al., 2024a
;
Li et al., 2024b
;
Gou et al., 2024
)
. This vulnerability presents a significant challenge for deploying LVLMs in real-world applications requiring trustworthy behavior.

A line of recent work has explored inference- and training-time mitigation strategies to improve the safety of LVLMs. Inference-time interventions identify a safe direction in the representation space and steer the LVLM’s output in that direction
(
Wang et al., 2024b
;
Liu et al., 2024a
;
Gao et al., 2024
)

or apply visual and text purifiers to the input
(
Pi et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024e
)
, or append empirically derived or learned safety-guiding prompts (e.g., “I’m sorry”) to reinforce harmless outputs
(
Wang et al., 2024c
;
Ding et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
)
.
Fine-tuning interventions
(
Zong et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
typically involve fine-tuning on curated safety datasets, or employ red teaming to identify safety vulnerabilities and use the resulting examples as supervision for further training
(
Liu et al., 2024c
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
. However, the effectiveness of inference-time methods varies significantly across different prompts and datasets, limiting their reliability in practice
(
Tan et al., 2024
)
. Meanwhile, training-time approaches require large volumes of safety data and costly retraining even when the base LLM has already undergone safety alignment, and suffer from over-refusal.

The modality gap between image and text representations is recently hypothesized to contribute to safety degradation of LVLMs
(
Liu et al., 2024a
;
Gao et al., 2024
)
. However, if and how the amount of modality gap affects LVLMs’ safety is not studied.
In this work, we investigate the effect of the modality gap of several open VLVMs and
make the following contributions:

1.

We show that the modality gap between image and text embeddings is highly inversely correlated with the safety of LVLMs, and models with larger modality gap are more likely to respond unsafely to harmful prompts.

2.

We find that the modality gap emerges during
pretraining
and persists through fine-tuning. Our study uncovers the pretraining modality gap as a key factor in safety degradation of LVLMs.

3.

Building on the above insights, we propose
ReGap
, which
Reg
ularizes the modality
Gap
, between image and text token embeddings during pretraining. In doing so, the safety-alignment of the LLMs remains effective in the LVLM structure,
without relying on safety data, changing the model structure, incurring additional overhead, or harming the model’s performance.

Our extensive experiments on LLaVA v1.5 demonstrate that
ReGap
improves the safety alignment of LVLMs against harmful prompts by up to
16.3\%
on various safety benchmarks, while preserving strong general capabilities in question answering, reasoning, and captioning compared to the base model. We further show that
ReGap
generalizes effectively across different pretraining datasets, pretraining methods, and model architectures, including ShareGPT4V
(
Chen et al., 2024a
)
and MiniGPT-4
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
, where it reduces the Unsafe Rate by up to
24.3\%
across harmful prompt categories. Moreover, we find that combining our approach with existing defense methods significantly enhances their effectiveness, improving some state-of-the-art safety alignment techniques by up to
18.2\%
, achieving safety performance on par with text-only LLMs across all evaluated benchmarks.

Related Work

Large Vision Language Model (LVLM).

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have sparked significant interest in extending them to the visual domain, leading to the development of powerful large vision-language models (LVLMs)
(
Chen et al., 2023a
;
Chen et al., 2023b
;
Hu et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Peng et al., 2023
;
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Alayrac et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Dai et al., 2023
;
Ye et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2024a
)
. By combining textual and visual inputs, LVLMs are capable of performing advanced tasks such as in-context learning
(
Mann et al., 2020
)
and chain-of-thought reasoning
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
in the visual domain. Most LVLMs consist of a pretrained LLM, a vision encoder, and an adapter that projects visual inputs into a format compatible with the LLM. For example, the popular open-source model, LLaVA
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
, employs a simple MLP to map visual outputs into the language model’s input space.

LLM and LVLM Safety.

To ensure LLMs’ generate responses are safe, honest, and helpful
(
Askell et al., 2021
)
,
safety alignment techniques such as instruction tuning
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
Azar et al., 2024
)
refine models to better reflect human values.
While LLMs go through extensive safety alignment

(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Grattafiori et al., 2024
;
Achiam et al., 2023
)
, the introduction of the visual modality in LVLMs disrupts the existing safety alignment of the LLM in the LVLM structure. Thus, LVLMs often produce harmful or biased responses to unsafe prompts or generate hallucinatory content that does not correspond to the provided images
(
Caffagni et al., 2024
)
.
This phenomenon, where safety-aligned LLMs generate harmful responses when prompted with harmful text alongside any images
(
Li et al., 2024b
)
, is referred to as safety degradation
(
Liu et al., 2024a
)
.
Modality gap between the image and text embeddings has been hypothesized to contribute to safety degradation
of LVLMs
Liu et al. (2023)
;
Gao et al. (2024)
. However, how this modality gap affects LVLMs’ safety is not well understood.

Improving LVLMs Safety Alignment.

Existing approaches to improving the safety alignment of LVLMs can be broadly classified into two categories: Training-time and inference time intervention.

Training-time intervention
(
Zong et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024c
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
usually involve training on curated safety datasets, or use red teaming to identify safety vulnerabilities and use them as supervision for further training
(
Liu et al., 2024c
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
. Although effective, these approaches are expensive, especially for large models, and the datasets may not capture all possible harmful images or prompts adequately.

Inference-time interventions often rely on purification or steering.
Purification methods
include sanitizing image inputs using diffusion models
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
, employing more robust CLIP encoders
(
Schlarmann et al., 2024
;
Hossain and Imteaj, 2024
)
, or converting images into text before inputting them into the LLM
(
Gou et al., 2024
)
. Other methods target text inputs or outputs, using auxiliary models such as LLM-based detectors or detoxifiers
(
Pi et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024e
)
, or appending empirically or learned safety-guiding prompts (e.g., “I’m sorry”) to the input prompts
(
Wang et al., 2024c
;
Ding et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
)
.
Purification approaches are often sensitive to detection errors, rely heavily on auxiliary models, and introduce additional inference-time latency.
Steering methods modify internal model representations to promote safer outputs.
Wang et al. (2024b)
compute activation differences between safe and harmful prompts in a safety-aligned LLM and apply these as guidance vectors in the target model. Similarly,
Liu et al. (2024a)
use the representation shift between text-only and multimodal inputs from a safety dataset to nudge the model’s internal state toward the text-only distribution. CoCA
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
adds the logit difference induced by a safety prompt at each decoding step. Steering techniques are typically dataset-specific, often fail to generalize across diverse harmful inputs, and can harm model utility
(
Tan et al., 2024
)
.

Our method is first to enhance LVLM safety alignment by reducing the modality gap during pretraining. It does not require curated safety data or auxiliary models, incurs minimal training overhead and no inference-time overhead. Despite its simplicity, it improves robustness against harmful prompts, generalizes across architectures, preserves model utility,
and easily stacks
with existing defenses.

3
Preliminary

3.1
LVLM Pretraining and Fine-tuning

Pretraining.
LVLMs consist of three key components: a vision encoder (often a CLIP-based Vision Transformer), a LLM, and an adapter module (projector) that bridges the two by projecting image embeddings from the vision encoder into the LLM’s input embedding space. Since LLMs are pretrained solely on textual data, a pretraining stage is necessary to align visual embeddings with the LLM’s input token space, enabling the model to interpret images and generate contextually relevant text. This alignment is achieved by pretraining the projector on large-scale datasets of image-caption pairs.

Formally, let the vision encoder be denoted as
f_{\text{ViT}}
, which produces image token embeddings
H_{v}=f_{\text{ViT}}(X_{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
. Let the LLM be denoted as
P_{\Theta}
, with a token embedding space
\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}
, and let the projection layer be
f_{W}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}
. During pretraining, given an image
X_{v}
and its corresponding caption
X_{a}
, the model minimizes the following objective with respect to the projection weights
W
, while keeping both the vision encoder
f_{\text{ViT}}
and the LLM
P_{\Theta}
frozen:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}=-\log P_{\Theta}(X_{a}\mid f_{W}(H_{v}))

(1)

This objective encourages the LLM to generate the correct caption
X_{a}
conditioned on the projected image embeddings, thereby aligning the visual and textual modalities.

Supervised Fine-Tuning.
After pretraining, the model acquires general multimodal understanding capabilities. However, downstream tasks such as visual question answering or dialog generation require additional task-specific supervision. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) adapts the model to these specialized formats and improves its ability to follow instructions. During this stage, all components of the model including the vision encoder, projection layer, and language model are trained jointly to optimize performance on the downstream objectives. Formally, given an image
X_{v}
, a task-specific instruction
X_{\text{instruct}}
, and a corresponding ground-truth response
X_{a}
, we minimize the following SFT loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\log P_{\Theta}\left(X_{a}\mid X_{\text{instruct}},f_{W}(H_{v})\right)

(2)

3.2
Modality Gap and Unsafe Rate

Modality Integration Rate (MIR).

MIR quantifies the representational gap between image and text tokens by measuring the distributional distance between their embeddings across all transformer layers
(
Huang et al., 2024
)
.
Formally, let
f_{k}^{v}
and
f_{k}^{t}
denote the vision and text token embeddings at the
k
-th transformer layer after projection into the LLM’s input space via
f_{W}(H_{v})
. MIR is defined as:

\text{MIR}=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{FID}\left(\omega\left(\alpha_{k}f_{k}^{v}\right),\omega\left(\alpha_{k}f_{k}^{t}\right)\right),

(3)

where
\alpha_{k}
is a text-centric scaling factor that normalizes the average
\ell_{2}
-norm of text tokens at each layer to
1
, and
\omega(\cdot)
denotes an outlier removal function. MIR captures both first-order (mean) and second-order (covariance) differences in the embedding distributions using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). A lower MIR indicates faster integration between image and text tokens.

Unsafe Rate.

We evaluate model safety using the Unsafe Rate
(
Liu et al., 2024a
)
:

\text{Unsafe Rate}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\mathcal{J}(y_{i})=\text{True}\right\},

(4)

where
y_{i}
is the model’s response to the
i
-th prompt,
\mathcal{J}
is the judge model that outputs True if the response is deemed harmful and False otherwise,
\mathbbm{1}\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function, and
N
is the total number of prompts. Following prior work
(
Li et al., 2024b
;
Wang et al., 2024a
;
Tekin et al., 2024
)
, we employ Beaver-dam-7B
(
Ji et al., 2023
)
as the judge model. This model is trained on high-quality human feedback covering various harmful categories.

To further reduce computational overhead, we randomly select 100 image-prompt pairs and compute the MIR based on their embeddings.

Observations.
We make the following two interesting observations:

(1) Modality Gap of Fine-tuned LVLMs are Highly Correlated with Their Unsafe Rate.

Fig.

shows the mode gap, measured by MIR, and the unsafe rate for different LVLM after instruction fine tuning. We see a strong correlation of 0.71 between MIR and unsafe rate for the fine-tuned models. Crucially, models with larger MIR consistently generate more unsafe outputs.

(2) Modality Gap Emerges During Pretraining and Persists During Fine-tuning.

Next, to understand when these gaps emerge, we further examined pretrained models prior to instruction tuning. Fig.
1
shows that the modality gap already presents during pretraining, and we also observe a strong correlation of 0.78 between pretrained MIR and unsafe rate. Fig.

These findings collectively demonstrate that reducing the modality gap, particularly during pretraining, is essential for improving the safety alignment of the LVLMs. This insight directly motivates our proposed method, which we detail in the following section.

Building on our finding that the modality gap during pretraining correlates with downstream safety degradation, we propose a pretraining regularization strategy that reduces this gap with only negligible additional training cost.

Reducing the Modality Gap via Regularization.

While we have shown that lower MIR values are strongly correlated with lower unsafe rates, MIR cannot be directly used as a regularization term, primarily because calculating the MIR involves computing the matrix root of the image and text covariance matrices, which is computationally expensive and numerically unstable.
To address this, we propose using the pairwise
L_{2}
distance between all image tokens and all text tokens. Specifically, for
m
image and
n
text tokens, we define:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{a=1}^{m}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left\|f_{a}^{v}-f_{b}^{t}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

where
\{f_{a}^{v}\}_{a=1}^{m}
and
\{f_{b}^{t}\}_{b=1}^{n}
denote the image and text token embeddings of the same input pair
X_{a}
. In Appendix
9.7
, we verify that similar to MIR, (i)
L_{2}
distance also correlates strongly with the unsafe rate, and (2) there is a strong correlation between
L_{2}
distances of the pretrained and fine-tuned models, further confirming the validity of our regularization loss.

Our regularizer enforces fine-grained matching between modalities. Importantly, the
L_{2}
loss ensures that all dimensions of the image embeddings are aligned with those of the text embeddings. In our ablation study, we show that a smaller randomly chosen subset of image and text tokens can also be used for regularization. However, using all tokens yields the best safety alignment.

Pretraining with Regularizer.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}
can be larger than
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}
and may dominate the optimization process, rendering
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}
ineffective. We illustrate the pretraining loss and regularization loss curves in Appendix Figure
9.7
.
To mitigate this, we introduce a scaling factor
\alpha
. During a warm-up phase (the first few epochs), we compute
\alpha
as the ratio
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}/\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}
, ensuring that the scaled
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}
matches the magnitude of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}
. This empirically determined
\alpha
, fixed after the warm-up phase, is used throughout the remainder of training. The values of
\alpha
used in all experiments are provided in Appendix
9.8
.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}={\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}}}{}+\alpha\cdot{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}}{}

(6)

This approach ensures stable updates and prevents either component from overwhelming the training process.

Reducing the Modality Gap in the First Layer.

Notably, we focus on reducing the modality gap at the input layer. This is because during pretraining only the lightweight projector (a two-layer MLP) is updated, while both the vision and language encoders remain frozen. Therefore, aligning representations in the input embedding space provides the most direct and tractable intervention. As shown in Fig.
2
, reducing the modality gap at the input layer yields a smaller gap in deeper layers.

Baselines.
We evaluate our approach against six baseline methods. First, we include the text-only Vicuna-7B, which serves as an upper bound, as our method aims to mitigate safety degradation and achieve safety alignment comparable to a text-only model. We also consider LLaVA-v1.5-7B-LoRA

ReGap

As shown in Tab.

More importantly,

can be seamlessly combined with existing inference-time defense methods to yield even stronger results. For instance, combining
ReGap
with SimCLIP or RobustCLIP reduces the average unsafe rate to 33.5% and 33.6%, respectively. In these cases, safety alignment improves by up to 23.3%, demonstrating the broad applicability and complementary nature of our approach.

ReGap
is effective across models and datasets.

substantially improves safety alignment across architectures and datasets, without relying on curated safety data or introducing additional training or inference costs. 7
Ablation Study

Different regularization Strategies.

ReGap
is applied during pretraining to reduce the modality gap of the model at its input layer.
Here,
we investigate applying
ReGap
to regularizing the first few layers, regularizing the final layer, and regularizing fine-tuning instead of pretraining. Results are shown in Tab.
4
. We observe that applying
ReGap
to deeper layers, either early or late, leads to increased unsafe rates.
Furthermore, we observed that regularizing the fine-tuning stage can cause the model to collapse and fail to produce any useful output. Even with a very small
\alpha=0.01
on the regularization term, it can still negatively impact the model’s safety alignment.

Different Sampled tokens.

Finally, we explore regularizing a smaller number
K
of randomly selected image tokens during training (the total number of tokens is 576). Fig.
3(a)
shows that sampling fewer image tokens negatively impacts safety alignment, leading to increased unsafe rates. Additionally, as shown,
ReGap
is lightweight, and using fewer tokens does not noticeably reduce training cost compared to using all tokens. Therefore, we recommend using the full set of image tokens during training.

8
Conclusion

We present
ReGap
, a simple yet effective regularization method that improves the safety alignment of LVLMs by reducing the modality gap during pretraining. Through extensive analysis, we show that the modality gap is strongly correlated with safety degradation in LVLMs. By applying a lightweight regularization to align image and text embeddings during pretraining,
ReGap
reduces unsafe rate across multiple benchmarks on different architectures and training dataset, and is highly compatible with existing defense methods and consistently enhances their performance.
ReGap
achieves up to 16.3% improvement in unsafe rate reduction when used alone and 18.2% when combined with other defense, and does not harm LVLMs’ performance.

9.1
Model Descriptions

LLaVA-v1.5 uses a simple MLP to map visual features from a CLIP encoder to the space of a language model. For our experiments, we primarily use LLaVA-v1.5-7b and LLava-v1.5-7b-LoRA with Vicuna-7b-v1.5 as the underlying language model and clip-vit-large-patch14-336 as the vision encoder.

ShareGPT4V shares the same architecture as LLaVA-v1.5 but is trained with different data. Namely, the authors describe the new data as capturing more diversity and utilizing higher quality captions than the LLaVA dataset. We apply our method to this model to evaluate its ability to generalize across datasets.

MiniGPT-4 shares a similar architecture as the previous models but with a critical difference: it employs a Querying Transformer (Q-Former) to bridge the image-text gap rather than just a simple MLP. In short, this module uses learned query vectors to extract the most semantically meaningful features from the image. It produces significantly fewer visual tokens than the previous architectures with the intuition that less tokens are necessary since the tokens themselves are higher quality. We apply our method to this model to evaluate its ability to generalize across model architecture designs.

9.2
Baseline Descriptions

SimCLIP and RobustCLIP are two methods designed to increase the robustness of the CLIP vision encoder against attacks. SimCLIP uses a Siamese architecture with cosine similarity loss while RobustCLIP trains in an unsupervised adversarial setup. We demonstrate that each of these modified CLIP models can be used in tandem with our method to further increase the robustness of the vision language model.

CMRM aims to close the modality gap during inference via steering vectors. In particular, safety directions are first extracted by comparing image and text inputs from a separate dataset, which are then added to other prompts during inference to encourage the model to produce safer responses. We demonstrate that our method can be used in tandem with this method as well.
