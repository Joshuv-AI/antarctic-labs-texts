Electrochemical hydrogenation of biomass substrates with cation shuttling in a solid electrolyte reactor
Authors: Yanjie Zhai, Shanhe Gong, Yunpeng Zuo, Libo Sun, Weisong Li, Yile Yang, Qing Xia, Lele Cui, Yingying Zhou, Jie Wu, Xiao Zhang, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical hydrogenation of biomass-derived substrates offers a sustainable route to valuable chemicals, yet conventional systems often struggle to simultaneously achieve high efficiency and high product purity. Here we report an electrochemical process based on a porous solid-electrolyte reactor that integrates efficient hydrogenation with intrinsic purification for biomass-derived substrates, thereby eliminating the need for supporting electrolytes. The system promotes the hydrogenation of maleic acid solution (a model unsaturated biomass substrate) into succinic acid (a model saturated biomass substrate), via a cation shielding effect while suppressing competing reactions such as hydrogen evolution. By continuously recycling the generated sodium succinate from the cathode to the middle layer, the reactor further converts it into pure succinic acid, establishing a closed-loop alkali-metal-cation shuttle that enhances selectivity and minimizes side reactions. This system delivers succinic acid Faradaic efficiencies ~ 85%, long-term operational stability (over 500 hours), and a final solution of > 98% purity, without downstream electrolyte recovery. These results highlight a scalable pathway toward cleaner, more efficient electrochemical upgrading of biomass.

Introduction

The sustainable conversion of biomass-derived molecules into valuable chemicals is increasingly vital for modern society, with broad implications for renewable materials, pharmaceuticals, food systems, and beyond1–6. Currently, the hydrogenation of organic compounds remains heavily dependent on fossil-based resources7,8, typically requiring high temperatures and pressures9,10, resulting in substantial energy consumption and CO2 emissions (Fig. 1a)11. These challenges highlight the urgent need for alternative, eco-friendly routes for biomass conversion12–15. In recent years, electrochemical biomass conversion has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional thermochemical biomass conversion routes (Fig. 1b). Electrochemistry16–20 offers numerous advantages, such as operation under ambient condition, scalability, and compatibility with decentralized renewable electricity sources21–24. Despite significant progress that has been made in traditional electrochemical hydrogenation of maleic acid to succinic acid with high synthesis efficiency, product purity, and low energy consumption25, most reported systems operate in batch or flow reactors that still rely on high concentrations of sulfuric acid as a recyclable supporting electrolyte (Fig. 1b). This system necessitates energy-intensive downstream separation to separate sulfuric acid. Furthermore, the use of lead-based cathodes raises concerns about heavy-metal toxicity, electrode sustainability, and environmental management. Electrolyte-free electrochemical systems have emerged as a potential alternative, which can operate without reliance on supporting electrolytes, addressing simplified product purification26–28. Among them, electrochemical palladium (Pd) membrane reactors could achieve hydrogenation by H* diffuses through a Pd membrane to hydrogenate substrates in a separate reaction chamber26 (Fig. 1c), enabling the production of pure products without the need for downstream separation. However, maintaining high selectivity and product purity at high current density remains a critical limitation for its practical application.

Here, we propose an electrochemical process based on a porous solid electrolyte (PSE) reactor by coupling efficient electrochemical hydrogenation with a purification process (Fig. 1d). The PSE layer functions both as an ion-conducting medium and as a process-integrating purification compartment. By enriching the local alkali-metal-cation concentration at the cathode interface, this architecture promotes selective hydrogenation at high current density while suppressing side reactions, and enables the continuous conversion of unsaturated biomass-derived substrates (taking maleic acid as a representative unsaturated biomass-derived substrate) without adding supporting electrolytes or downstream electrolyte-recovery steps (Fig. 1e). Consequently, a purified stream of saturated biomass derivative (succinic acid) solution can be directly obtained as the product. A techno-economic analysis (TEA) further confirms the feasibility of this electrochemical hydrogenation strategy.

Results

PSE reactor design with cation shielding effect

To address the low product purity of batch or flow reactors (Supplementary Fig. 1) and low selectivity of conventional membrane electrode assembly (MEA) reactors29–31 for hydrogenation of unsaturated biomass-derived substrates (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2), we propose a PSE reactor that enables efficient hydrogenation without the need for supporting electrolytes in unsaturated biomass-derived substrates solutions (Fig. 2a). Unlike previous solid-electrolyte reactors that relied on a forward-biased bipolar configuration for product separation32, our PSE reactor features a design that incorporates both a proton exchange membrane (PEM) and a cation exchange membrane (CEM), separated by an additional chamber between the cathode and anode. This chamber is filled with a thin, porous solid ion-conducting polymer layer composed of a styrene-divinylbenzene sulfonated co-polymer33. This extra layer minimizes the ohmic drop between the electrodes while retaining the advantages of the MEA design. Inspired by previous studies in electrochemical CO2 reduction35 and 2-electron oxygen reduction reaction36, where alkali cations suppress HER by substituting interfacial protons or generating a localized electric field, we explored the cation shielding effect in maleic acid hydrogenation by controlling the interfacial cation flux in the PSE reactor (Fig. 2b). Accordingly, a 1.0 M Na⁺ stream was introduced through the PSE layer, establishing a cation shielding effect and elevating the local interfacial pH, thereby suppressing undesired H⁺ crossover across the CEM. Na⁺ transport in the PSE reactor is primarily directed toward the cathode chamber under the present operating conditions, where Na⁺ compensates negatively charged maleate/succinate species and contributes to the cation-shuttling process. Nevertheless, minor Na⁺ migration toward the anode side may occur because of concentration gradients, local ion-exchange equilibrium, and non-ideal membrane selectivity. Inductively coupled plasma analysis (ICP) of the anode effluent after the stability test showed a Na⁺ concentration of only 96.32 mg L−1 (0.00419 mol L−1), confirming that Na⁺ transport toward the anode side is minor compared with the dominant cathode-directed crossover. Then, we used commercial Cu foam as electrocatalyst, confirming its structure using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping, and X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Figs. 3–5). A 0.5 M maleic acid solution was used as the biomass-derived feedstock, and Cu served as the cathodic electrocatalyst37. As a result, we observed a significant increase in succinic acid Faradaic efficiencies, accompanied by effective suppression of the HER.

Although the introduction of the additional layer in the PSE reactor could theoretically increase ohmic resistance (Supplementary Fig. 6), the use of a conductive solid electrolyte led to only a 22% increase in reactor voltage at 100 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 7). In contrast, the succinic acid Faradaic efficiency rose dramatically, from 21.9% to 83.4% at 100 mA cm−2 (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Figs. 2, 8), compared to the conventional MEA configuration. This strategy is not limited to maleic acid, but can also be extended to other unsaturated biomass-derived substrates, including fumaric acid, maleic anhydride, and acrylic acid, demonstrating the broader applicability of the cation-shuttling strategy to selected C=C-containing biomass-derived feedstocks. For example, fumaric acid is hydrogenated to succinic acid with a total Faradaic efficiency of 76.1% and a succinic acid production rate of 2.84 mmol cm−2 h−1 at 200 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Maleic anhydride and acrylic acid also undergo efficient hydrogenation, reaching total Faradaic efficiencies of 68.68% and 80.55%, respectively, at 200 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11). These results highlight the versatility of the PSE reactor for the electrochemical hydrogenation of unsaturated biomass-derived molecules. Notably, although NaOH serves as the cation source in the middle layer, the high pH was not the main factor contributing to the improved selectivity. When replacing OH⁻ with alternative anions that are either weakly basic or neutral, both with and without buffering capacity, resulted in similar succinic acid efficiencies, with HER effectively suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Process design for maleic acid hydrogenation in the PSE reactor

Based on the enhanced maleic acid hydrogenation enabled by cation shielding in the PSE reactor, we established a continuous reaction system within the PSE reactor that efficiently converts maleic acid to succinic acid, eliminating the need for supporting electrolytes (Fig. 2a). In this process, aqueous maleic acid serves as feedstock, while succinic acid (generation from cathodic maleic acid hydrogenation) and O2 (generation from anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER)) are the primary outputs. A stream of purified succinic acid is subsequently collected from the middle chamber as the treated cathodic solution. In an ideal scenario, no cations or electrolytes are consumed throughout the process.

The step-by-step operation of this process is as follows. First, a stream of maleic acid, 0.5 M unless otherwise noted, is fed into the cathode compartment of the PSE reactor. Next, the maleic acid hydrogenation occurs between the cathodic catalyst and the adjacent membrane surface, while cations from the middle PSE chamber cross over to the cathodic chamber. Assuming 100% efficiency for both succinic acid production and Na⁺ crossover, the maximum cation transfer equals two times the initial maleic acid amount (since the two-electron transfer involved requires two cations), meaning up to 1.0 M (0.5 M × 2) Na⁺ may be needed. PSE reactor optimization and its maleic acid hydrogenation performances

For the optimization of both the process and reactor architecture, a series of critical parameters were systematically explored. Initially, a single-pass flow of maleic acid solution through the cathode chamber was implemented to assess the intrinsic maleic acid hydrogenation performance of the PSE reactor under various conditions, including different catholyte flow rates and current densities. Unlike recirculation systems favored for maximal maleic acid conversion in practical applications, the single-pass mode maintains stable maleic acid influent concentration and more uniform conversion levels, allowing for a more accurate assessment of intrinsic catalytic activity, as reflected by the Faradaic efficiency (FE) for succinic acid. To ensure fair comparison across experimental setup, various flow rate optimizations were carried out to keep maleic acid conversion constant for a fair comparison (see Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figs. 14–17), with the optimal conditions yielding the highest succinic acid FE detailed in Supplementary Fig. 15.

Subsequently, we investigated the role of cations in the system from two key perspectives: (1) if interfacial cation shielding effect retains effectiveness without a middle chamber, and (2) if the beneficial effect of cations arises primarily from their localized presence at interface between the catalyst and membrane rather than in bulk electrolyte. We evaluated a conventional MEA configuration in which 1.0 M NaOH was introduced into the anode chamber to serve as a cation source. Despite this setup providing a potential Na⁺ supply across the PEM, the succinic acid FE remained significantly lower than that achieved in the PSE reactor under comparable high current density conditions (Fig. 2f). In fact, the performance with cation in the MEA was comparable to that in the absence of cation (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2), likely owing to the high local generation of H+ at the PEM–anode interface. This configuration further highlights the requirements for buffered compartments, such as the PSE compartment, to suppress H+ crossover and maximize cation shielding effect for enhanced performance (Fig. 2f).

To address the second question, we constructed a two-dimensional COMSOL model to visualize the spatial Na⁺ distribution near the catalyst surface (Fig. 2c–e). As expected, the Na+ concentration is highest at the left-hand boundary, where electrochemical reactions occur and Na⁺ flux is introduced. At a current density of 200 mA cm−2, the interfacial Na⁺ concentration exceeds 1.25 M, compared with an average channel concentration of around 0.106 M (theoretical calculations estimating the maximum possible Na⁺ crossover under 200 mA cm−2) (Fig. 2d, e). Similarly, the interfacial concentrations reach 0.94 M and 0.63 M at 150 and 100 mA cm−2, respectively, compared with average channel concentrations of around 0.079, 0.005 M (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). Thus, the interfacial concentration is more than 10-fold higher than the bulk channel concentration, highlighting the significance of interfacial enrichment in the PSE reactor. This simulation result suggests that, if we can confine the following hydrogenation reaction at the catalyst/electrolyte right interface, the overall efficiency of the reaction could be dramatically enhanced. We then employed the PSE reactor using 0.5 M maleic acid and 0.106 M NaOH as the catholyte, while the middle PSE layer contained only deionized water (i.e., no added cations). Although cations were fully introduced into the bulk catholyte in advance, the succinic acid Faradaic efficiency remained lower than that achieved in the PSE configuration with interfacial cation supply, particularly at higher current densities (Fig. 2f). Even under the assumption of complete transfer of 1.0 M Na⁺ to the cathode, the performance was inferior to that obtained with interfacial cation delivery (Supplementary Fig. 20). These results indicate that, despite sufficient cation concentration in the bulk electrolyte, the cathode–CEM interface remains locally acidic in the absence of cation crossover from the PSE layer. This strongly supports the conclusion that the critical role of interfacial cation shielding effect, with a conclusion that performance enhancement is mainly attributable to interfacial cation shielding effect, rather than simply pH changes or bulk cation concentration.

We then explored the suitable operating conditions for a range of initial maleic acid concentrations (from 0.5 M down to 0.01 M). As discussed earlier, comprehending the competition of HER and maleic acid hydrogenation, and their corresponding reaction kinetics, are essential for effective reactor design and process optimization. Thus, this investigation provides valuable insight for treating water sources with different concentrations of maleic acid but also serves as operation guidance at different conversion levels of maleic acid. The findings revealed that optimal current density decreases with the decrease of the influent maleic acid concentration, while maximum succinic acid FEs remain around 80% even at 0.05 M maleic acid (Fig. 2g). The requirement for lower operating current densities to maintain high succinic acid FEs at reduced concentrations is attributed to mass transfer limitations and the first-order kinetics characteristic of maleic acid hydrogenation, that is, the reaction rate is proportional to the maleic acid concentration38. Notably, the optimal flow rates were similarly predicted based on the results obtained for 0.5 M maleic acid (Supplementary Fig. 14) and further supported by first-order kinetics analyses (Supplementary Figs. 21–23). These operating conditions ensured that conversion levels were maintained at a consistent level (between 5% and 20%) across different concentrations, facilitating a fair comparison of the intrinsic succinic acid FEs under similar maleic acid hydrogenation kinetics.

Finally, insights from the single-pass system were applied to a batch conversion configuration to better reflect practical applications scenarios. In this configuration, both the cathode and middle layer streams were recirculated to maximize maleic acid conversion. The key parameter to optimize was the operating current density, as the optimal current density varies with both maleic acid concentration and conversion level, which are both functions of time in a batch reaction. Thus, guided by the intrinsic succinic acid FE data, a stepwise constant-current operation was manually established to adjust the current density at diverse time intervals consistent with various maleic acid conversion levels.

This step-current CP (chronopotentiometry) setup outperformed the one with constant-current CP setup (Supplementary Fig. 24), resulting in a maximum succinic acid FE of over 85%. Under these optimized conditions, 50 mL of 0.5 M maleic acid was reduced to levels over the basic industrial grade for biomass concentration (final succinic acid purity >98%, compared to basic industrial grade of purity >95%) (Supplementary Fig. 25). In this 50 mL of 0.5 M maleic acid, batch conversion was performed with the interfacial cation effect in the PSE reactor. Notably, it was achieved in the PSE reactor without the addition of 1.0 M NaOH supporting electrolyte.

Long-term stability test for direct maleic acid conversion

Following adjustment of various parameters and operational strategies, a durability test was performed to evaluate the practical durability and sustained batch conversion performance of maleic acid in the PSE reactor. In each cycle, 250 mL of fresh 0.5 M maleic acid was fed and then circulated as the influent in the cathodic chamber with the suitable flowrate of 1.0 mL min−1, while the catholyte effluent from the previous cycle (the produced succinic acid solution) was recirculated into the middle PSE layer (see Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 26, and Supplementary Note 2). After 10 consecutive batch cycles over 20 days (~580 hours) of continuous operation, the succinic acid FE in the final cycle remained above 85%, with an average succinic acid FE of 88% across all 10 cycles (Fig. 3b). The average conversion of maleic acid achieved exceeded 98%, reducing the residual maleic acid concentration to below the impurity threshold specified for industrial-grade succinic acid (Fig. 3b). The relatively low product purity observed in the first cycle is attributed to the fact that only sodium succinate was generated at this stage, prior to its subsequent purification to succinic acid in later cycles. Throughout the stability testing, cell voltage remained stable between ~3 and 4.5 V, varying only with applied current density and instantaneous conversion level (Fig. 3a). Additionally, cations continuously shuttled between the cathode and the PSE layer throughout the entire test. By the final cycle of long-term operation, the Na⁺ crossover ratio had reached 86% (Fig. 3b), which was sufficient to maintain the cation shuttling loop (Supplementary Fig. 27). Importantly, aside from the initial cycle, where a 1.0 M NaOH solution was introduced into the PSE compartment to initiate the process, almost no extra supporting-electrolyte was added throughout remaining operation. A stable oscillation profile of cation crossover was observed across the successive cycles (Supplementary Fig. 27), which can be explained by a self-correction process. Originally, the process began with an assumption of 100% succinic acid FE and complete cation crossover, corresponding to the initial 1.0 M Na⁺ concentration. However, as long as maleic acid was fully converted, the system naturally adjusted over time, with the Na⁺ crossover ratio and succinic acid FE gradually aligned as the process going on. If the FE for maleic acid hydrogenation exceeded that for Na⁺ crossover FE, the resulting low Na⁺ crossover to the cathode chamber would reduce the maleic acid hydrogenation FEs, thereby increasing the HER FEs. This imbalance would drive more Na⁺ migration toward the cathode interface in subsequent cycles, further boosting maleic acid hydrogenation FEs. This feedback mechanism forms the basis of a self-correcting cation shuttling loop, maintaining stable and controlled operation. In terms of product separation, the Na⁺ crossover strategy effectively separated purified succinic acid products, achieving separation efficiencies exceeding 98% (Fig. 3b). As a result, the PSE reactor system successfully converted ten batches of 0.5 M maleic acid water stream into a purified succinic acid stream in the middle chamber outlet. The majority of the purified succinic acid stream contained maleic acid levels below 0.01 M and Na⁺ concentrations below 0.01 M from the PSE layer outlet (Fig. 3a, b).

Beyond eliminating the need for supporting electrolytes, our PSE reactor design offers substantial advantages over conventional reactor configurations for maleic acid hydrogenation. In typical MEA systems equipped with CEMs, transition metal catalysts such as Cu are prone to degradation during long-term operation due to the formation of a strongly localized acidic environment at the cathode interface. This acid-induced degradation compromises catalytic activity and system durability. In contrast, after ~580 h of continuous operation in this PSE reactor, no significant morphological or performance degradation of the Cu catalyst was observed, as confirmed by SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 28). To further evaluate material stability, ICP analysis was performed on the cathode effluent collected after the long-term stability test. The concentration of dissolved Cu was only 0.0130 mg L−1, corresponding to 0.204 μmol L−1, indicating negligible catalyst leaching during extended operation. These results confirm the chemical stability of the electrode materials under the tested conditions. This high catalytic stability underscores the effectiveness of the interfacial cation shielding effect, which helps maintain a more alkaline interfacial pH environment. By mitigating proton accumulation at the catalyst–membrane interface, the PSE configuration protects the catalyst from acid-driven corrosion and prolongs its functional lifetime.

Techno-economic analysis (TEA)

With the promising performance of the PSE reactor configuration, we conducted a preliminary TEA to assess its feasibility and commercial potential (Supplementary Tables 1–3, Supplementary Note 3). Maleic acid-containing aqueous streams may originate from maleic anhydride hydrolysis, unsaturated polyester resin production, polymer and coating manufacturing, fine-chemical synthesis, pharmaceutical intermediate production, and biomass-upgrading processes. In such streams, maleic acid or maleate species may exist as residual reactants, hydrolysis products, intermediates, or by-products. In this work, maleic acid solution was used as a model of wastewater without cost and an unsaturated biomass-derived feedstock to evaluate the feasibility of coupling electrochemical hydrogenation with intrinsic product purification. As shown in the cost breakdown in Fig. 4a, entire production cost to produce succinic acid via the PSE reactor is US$0.2678 per kg of succinic acid produced, with both capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operating expenditure (OPEX). The OPEX was estimated at US$0.0992 per kg succinic acid produced. Among all cost components, electricity emerged as the dominant contributor, accounting for 28.2% of total cost, catalyst, membrane and solid electrolyte cost followed, which contributed 21.8%. Potential profits are based on succinic acid sales at the current market price39 subtract the expenses. When compared with the recent market price of succinic acid produced via traditional thermocatalytic hydrogenation (~US$1.61 per kg), our electrochemical route demonstrates strong economic competitiveness. To further investigate the cost-driving factors, a sensitivity analysis with a single variable was performed using baseline parameters derived from the performance results in this work (Supplementary Table 1). The findings indicated that the overall potential profit is largely affected by current density and stack cost (Fig. 4b). The strong sensitivity to these two factors is attributed to the use of expensive noble-metal catalysts in the PEM water electrolyzer model for the OER. Meanwhile, the significant impact of electricity cost reflects its dominant share in operating expenses (OPEX). These insights highlight key opportunities cost reduction through targeted improvements. A contour map illustrating the relationship between profit and cell performance reveals a distinct optimization goal (Fig. 4c). For instance, if technological advancements enable improved I–V performances, such as 450 mA cm−2 at a voltage of 2 V, or 300 mA cm−2 at a voltage of 1.7 V, the total production cost of succinic acid could potentially undercut the current market price of US$1.61 per kg succinic acid for traditionally produced succinic acid via thermocatalytic hydrogenation, even without factoring in the added value from wastewater treatment.

In this study, a maleic acid concentration of 0.5 M was chosen for experimental validation. However, this concentration is not a limitation of the electrochemical maleic acid hydrogenation technology. Integration of PSE reactor with electrodialysis (ED) process allows for the concentration of low maleic acid sources streams to the desired levels. When dealing with sources containing even lower concentrations, ED may become economically unfeasible due to its high operational cost, which cannot be offset by the value of the resulting product. In such cases, the development of cost-effective concentration techniques for dilute maleic acid sources becomes essential. Additionally, the sustainability and scalability of the present electrochemical process should also be evaluated in light of the critical materials used in MEA components. In particular, Ir-based anodic catalysts, which are widely employed for acidic OER in PEM-type electrolyzer configurations, pose an important challenge because iridium has a high environmental impact40, and faces severe supply criticality due to its limited production as a by-product of platinum-group-metal mining. Therefore, further development of PSE reactors with substantially lower noble-metal loading, or ideally noble/critical-metal-free OER catalysts, will be necessary to improve the long-term environmental and economic viability of this technology. Other challenges, including membrane and reactor durability, ion-balance control, operation with real wastewater matrices, energy efficiency at industrially relevant current densities, can be further optimized as future research directions to improve the hydrogenation energy efficiencies and costs for practical deployments.

Overall, this analysis highlights the potential of environmentally friendly electrochemical techniques as a viable and attractive substitute for conventional wastewater treatment systems and traditional thermocatalytic hydrogenation approach (Fig. 1a).

Discussion

This work presents a reactor design method aimed at overcoming the traditional challenge of requiring high concentrations of supporting electrolytes to achieve high FE for electrochemical maleic acid hydrogenation. Leveraging an interfacial cation shielding effect, our PSE system delivers succinic acid FEs ~ 85% with enhanced long-term operational stability (over 500 h) and produces a final succinic-acid solution with >98% purity. We also demonstrate the TEA of this method, which not only provides a more sustainable route for pure succinic acid production but also offers a practical solution for treating maleic acid-containing wastewater. This work highlights the importance of reactor engineering in advancing electrochemical hydrogenation with purification and reducing hydrogenation costs for practical deployment.

Methods

Chemicals

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%), Sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, ≥97%), Sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥99%), Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, ≥99%), Maleic acid (C4H4O4, ≥99%), Succinic acid (C4H6O4, ≥99%), Fumaric acid (C4H4O4, ≥99%), Maleic anhydride (C4H2O3, ≥99%), Succinic anhydride (C4H4O3, ≥99%), Acrylic acid (C3H4O2, ≥99%), Propionic acid (C3H6O2, ≥99.5%), Cu foam (1.6 mm thickness) were purchased from Aladdin. The deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was used throughout all experiments.

Preparation of electrode

In a typical procedure, the commercial Cu foams were first cut into suitable sizes and washed in ethanol and 1.0 M HCl for 30 min through ultrasonic method. This Cu foam catalyst was then directly used as the cathode electrode for later electrochemical measurements. The 0.1 mg cm−2 IrO2 electrode (Dioxide Materials) was used as the anode in the electrochemical experiments.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical experiments were performed at 25 °C in both MEA and PSE configurations41 using a BioLogic VSP-3e electrochemical workstation. The major electrocatalytic performance values were estimated from the intrinsic precision of test instruments. The cell performance was evaluated based on the measured full-cell voltage under a two-electrode configuration, without iR compensation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted under potentiostatic conditions over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 200 kHz to determine the cell resistance. The electrolyte flow rates were regulated using syringe pumps according to the requirements of each experiment. In reactor configuration, an IrO2 electrode (Dioxide Materials) was employed as the anode catalyst for the OER. Nafion-117 and Nafion-NX2100 membranes were used as cation exchange membranes and purchased from Fuel Cell Store. Prior to assembly, Nafion-117 was activated by sequential treatment with 5% (v/v) H2O2 and 10% (v/v) H2SO4 solutions at 80 °C for 1 h each.

The detailed configurations are described below. The MEA reactor was assembled using a Ti plate and a steel plate as the anodic and cathodic current collectors, respectively. Two polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) gaskets containing 1 cm2 openings were used to define the active reaction area, with a CEM positioned between the two electrodes. For the MEA-based control experiments, either deionized water or 1.0 M NaOH solution (pH = 14) was circulated through the anode chamber as the anolyte, while a 0.5 M maleic acid solution (pH = 1.28) served as the catholyte. The PSE reactor was constructed with a similar electrode configuration, consisting of Ti and steel current collector plates as the anode and cathode, respectively. In this configuration, two PTFE gaskets with a 1 cm2 window, two CEMs (Nafion-117 and Nafion-NX2100) were incorporated with a porous solid electrolyte layer placed between them, as depicted in Fig. 2a. The PSE compartment (2.0-mm-thick) filled with Dowex 50 W X8 hydrogen-form sulfonated styrene–divinylbenzene copolymer (Sigma-Aldrich) to provide continuous ionic transport. Prior to electrochemical operation, the PSE layer was conditioned by flowing the corresponding solution (deionized water or 1.0 M NaOH, depending on the experimental conditions) for 30 min to establish stable ion exchange across the membrane–electrolyte interfaces. Unless otherwise noted, a 0.5 M maleic acid solution prepared in deionized water was used as the catholyte, and deionized water was employed as the anolyte. NaOH solution was introduced into the middle PSE layer as the Na⁺ source. The concentrations of cations were determined using an Agilent ion chromatography system.

Interfacial Na+ concentration modeling

The local Na⁺ concentration profile at the cathode interface was examined through finite-element simulations. The model incorporated dilute species transport and single-phase fluid dynamics to describe Na⁺ migration under an operating current density of 200 mA cm−2. The cathode channel was supplied with DI water at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1, while Na⁺ ions were introduced through crossover from the middle chamber. The simulated concentration map revealed reduced Na⁺ accumulation near the inlet and progressive enrichment along the flow direction (Fig. 2c), which can be attributed to convection-driven ion transport under laminar flow conditions and the no-slip wall boundary. Material characterization

The morphology of Cu foam catalyst was examined using a Zeiss Sigma 300 scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Element composition was analyzed through energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. The crystal structure of Cu was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a D/max 2500PC system. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra was obtained on a Bruker 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. Inductively coupled plasma analysis (ICP) was obtained on an ICP-OES Optima 8000 spectrometer. The gaseous products generated during electrolysis were monitored by an online gas chromatograph (GC-2030, Shimadzu) equipped with both thermal conductivity (TCD) and flame ionization (FID) detectors for product identification and quantification.
