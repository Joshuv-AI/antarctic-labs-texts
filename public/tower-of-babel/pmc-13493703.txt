Focus on DnaJ protein research from Arabidopsis to crop improvement
Authors: Muhammad Arif, Hafiz Mamoon Rehman, Sehrish Bashir, Noman Mahmood, Atul Kumar Srivastava, Yakov Kuzyakov, Moh Sajid Ansari, Hon‐Ming Lam, Mingfang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDnaJ proteins (Hsp40s) are essential components of the cellular proteostasis network, functioning as molecular co‐chaperones that regulate protein folding, stability, and stress‐responsive homeostasis in plants. Beyond their classical role as Hsp70 partners, accumulating evidence demonstrates that DnaJ proteins participate in diverse biological processes by integrating proteostasis with hormonal, developmental, and defense signaling pathways. This review synthesizes recent advances in the structural diversity, evolutionary distribution, and functional specialization of DnaJ proteins across model plants and crops, including Arabidopsis, rice, maize, tomato, soybean, grapevine, citrus, cucumber, and other economically important species. We summarize mechanistic evidence demonstrating their involvement in abiotic stress tolerance, including heat, drought, salinity, osmotic, oxidative, and cold stress, through regulation of protein quality control, reactive oxygen species detoxification, ion homeostasis, abscisic acid and melatonin signaling, and transcriptional stress networks. Emerging studies further reveal their roles in biotic stress responses by modulating immune signaling, pathogen resistance, and host–microbe interactions, while additional evidence links DnaJ proteins to reproductive development, root architecture, chloroplast stability, fruit ripening, and postharvest quality maintenance. Advances in genome‐wide identification, transcriptomic profiling, gene editing, transgenic validation, and functional genomics have substantially expanded understanding of DnaJ‐mediated molecular networks and their contributions to climate resilience. By integrating mechanistic biology with translational breeding strategies, this review highlights DnaJ proteins as promising molecular targets for crop improvement and climate‐smart agriculture aimed at increasing productivity, stress tolerance, and postharvest performance under changing environmental conditions.

INTRODUCTION

In natural environments, plants are continuously exposed to adverse conditions, including multiple abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures (particularly heat stress), as well as biotic challenges from pathogens (Arif et al., 2025; Riaz et al., 2025). These stresses collectively induce morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular alterations that ultimately restrict plant growth and development (Dixit et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2018). At the cellular level, such conditions disrupt protein homeostasis and metabolic processes, leading to oxidative damage, protein misfolding, and aggregation, thereby causing proteotoxic stress that severely limits agricultural productivity (Trivedi et al., 2016). Maintaining protein homeostasis (proteostasis) under these fluctuating conditions is therefore essential for plant survival. Molecular chaperones, particularly heat shock proteins (Hsps), play a central role in this process by assisting protein folding, stabilization, and degradation (Kidwai et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2026). This protein family includes Hsp70 (DnaK), Hsp40 (DnaJ), Hsp90, Hsp100/Clp ATPases, and small Hsps (Hsp20), all of which function coordinately within the proteostasis network (PN) to maintain protein integrity (Huang & Xu, 2008; Yadav et al., 2020).

Among these, DnaJ proteins (Hsp40s) act as key co‐chaperones that regulate the activity of Hsp70s through a well‐defined mechanistic cycle (Qiu et al., 2006). The conserved J‐domain of DnaJ proteins stimulates the ATPase activity of Hsp70, promoting the transition from an ATP‐bound to an ADP‐bound state, which stabilizes the interaction between Hsp70 and its client proteins (Kampinga & Craig, 2010; Kityk et al., 2018). In this process, DnaJ proteins recognize and bind non‐native or unfolded polypeptides and deliver them to Hsp70, thereby facilitating efficient protein folding, refolding, or targeting for degradation. This ATP‐dependent chaperone cycle is essential for preventing protein aggregation and maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly under stress conditions (Adhikari, 2024; Pulido & Leister, 2018). In addition, DnaJ proteins cooperate with other components of the protein quality control (PQC) system, including proteases, to eliminate irreversibly damaged proteins.

Beyond their classical co‐chaperone roles, DnaJ proteins exhibit remarkable functional diversity. They are involved in multiple cellular processes, including protein translocation across membranes, organelle biogenesis, and regulation of developmental pathways such as embryogenesis, flowering, and seed setting (Roy et al., 2019). Experimental studies have further demonstrated their roles in pollen thermotolerance, ATPase regulation, cell proliferation, and inflorescence development, as well as in chloroplast development and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, particularly in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) (Bekh‐Ochir et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2015; Zhi et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2015).

Recent research has expanded the scope of DnaJ studies from the model plant Arabidopsis to a wide range of crop species, including cereals, horticultural crops, and oilseed plants. These crops frequently encounter environmental stresses that negatively impact yield, quality, and postharvest performance (Oyebamiji et al., 2024). Under such conditions, the demand for chaperone‐mediated protein folding increases significantly due to enhanced protein misfolding and cellular stress. DnaJ proteins modulate Hsp70 function by stimulating ATPase activity and stabilizing substrate interactions, forming a highly dynamic chaperone system that safeguards cellular proteostasis and stress resilience (Fatima et al., 2025; Pulido & Leister, 2018). In addition to PQC, certain J‐domain proteins (JDPs) have been implicated in non‐canonical functions, including gene silencing and mRNA processing, highlighting their broader regulatory roles (R. Zhang et al., 2023).

The structural diversity and functional conservation of DnaJ proteins underpin their involvement in a wide range of biological processes, including signal transduction, transcriptional and translational regulation, and post‐translational modifications (PTMs). Increasing evidence indicates their roles in plant growth and reproductive development, including flowering, fruit setting, and ripening, as well as in postharvest physiology and senescence (Cao et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2018). Genome‐wide identification studies across multiple plant species have revealed extensive expansion of the DnaJ gene family, suggesting lineage‐specific diversification and functional specialization (Razzaq et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2015). However, despite this progress, functional validation of many DnaJ genes remains limited, and comprehensive regulatory frameworks are still lacking in several crop systems (Gu et al., 2025).

Given these advances and existing knowledge gaps, this review focuses on DnaJ proteins across a broad spectrum of plant species, including horticultural, ornamental, oilseed, staple, and cereal crops. We synthesize current understanding of their classification, structural features, and functional roles, with particular emphasis on their mechanistic interaction with Hsp70 and their contribution to proteostasis and stress adaptation. Importantly, while DnaJ proteins have emerged as promising candidates for improving stress tolerance, most supporting evidence is derived from controlled experimental conditions, and field‐level validation remains limited. We therefore critically evaluate their translational potential and highlight future research priorities. Furthermore, we discuss emerging biotechnological approaches, including genome editing (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9) and transgenic strategies, as tools to dissect DnaJ functions and explore their application in crop improvement aimed at enhancing resilience, productivity, and postharvest quality under changing environmental conditions.

METHODOLOGY

This review was conducted through a comprehensive and structured survey of the scientific literature to synthesize current knowledge regarding the diversity, molecular mechanisms, biological functions, and biotechnological applications of DnaJ proteins in plants. Literature searches were performed using major scientific databases, including Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar, to ensure broad coverage of peer‐reviewed studies. The search strategy included combinations of keywords related to DnaJ proteins and plant stress biology, including “DnaJ proteins,” “Hsp40,” “JDPs,” “plant molecular chaperones,” “proteostasis,” “abiotic stress tolerance,” “biotic stress,” “Hsps,” “Hsp70 interaction,” “PTMs,” “CRISPR/Cas9,” “transgenic plants,” “crop improvement,” and “plant development.” Additional searches incorporated crop‐specific terms (e.g., rice, wheat, maize, soybean, tomato, citrus, grapevine, cucumber, and Arabidopsis) to capture species‐specific evidence.

The literature selection prioritized peer‐reviewed research articles, genome‐wide analyses, transcriptomics studies, functional characterization experiments, gene‐editing reports, and review articles relevant to DnaJ proteins in plants. Particular emphasis was placed on recent studies published within the last decade to capture advances in functional genomics, molecular mechanisms, and breeding technologies, while foundational earlier studies were included where necessary to explain classification systems, Hsp40‐Hsp70 interactions, and core mechanistic concepts. Studies were included when they provided experimental or computational evidence related to DnaJ protein classification, expression profiling, stress physiology, developmental regulation, PTM, protein‐protein interactions, or biotechnological applications. Reports lacking clear experimental support or unrelated to plant systems were excluded to maintain scientific rigor. To improve interpretative depth, findings were comparatively synthesized across monocot and dicot species to identify conserved mechanisms, evolutionary divergence, and functional specialization of DnaJ proteins. The collected literature was organized into thematic categories, including classification and diversity, comparative genomics, biological functions, molecular mechanisms, stress responses, post‐translational regulation, and biotechnological applications. Evidence from different plant systems was critically compared to highlight mechanistic insights, knowledge gaps, and future research priorities for advancing DnaJ‐based strategies in climate‐resilient crop improvement.

CLASSIFICATION, DIVERSITY, AND POTENTIAL APPLICATIONS OF DnaJ PROTEINS IN DIFFERENT CROPS

DnaJ proteins represent a structurally and functionally diverse family of co‐chaperones that regulate the activity of Hsp70 systems by controlling substrate recognition, delivery, and ATPase stimulation (Qiu et al., 2006). Rather than acting merely as auxiliary factors, DnaJ proteins determine client specificity and contribute to the functional versatility of the Hsp70 chaperone machinery. Their diversity in domain architecture, subcellular localization, and expression patterns underpins their involvement in a wide range of cellular processes, including stress adaptation, development, and metabolic regulation. Structurally, all DnaJ proteins are defined by the presence of a conserved J‐domain, typically comprising ∼70 amino acids and forming a characteristic four‐helix bundle (helices I–IV). This domain contains the highly conserved histidine‐proline‐aspartate (HPD) (His‐Pro‐Asp) motif, which is essential for stimulating the ATPase activity of Hsp70 and stabilizing substrate‐chaperone interactions (Musskopf et al., 2018). Based on domain organization, plant DnaJ proteins are traditionally classified into four major types, reflecting their structural complexity and functional specialization.

Type A (class I) DnaJ proteins contain the full complement of domains, including an N‐terminal J‐domain, a glycine/phenylalanine‐rich (G/F) region that contributes to substrate specificity, a cysteine‐rich zinc‐finger domain (CxxCxGxG motifs) involved in protein binding and stabilization, and a variable C‐terminal region responsible for client interaction. Type B (class II) proteins share the J‐domain and G/F‐rich region but lack the zinc‐finger domain, suggesting partial functional divergence. Type C (class III) proteins are the most structurally diverse, typically containing only the J‐domain with highly variable C‐terminal extensions, enabling specialized and often compartment‐specific functions (Pulido & Leister, 2018).

Type D (class IV) DnaJ‐related proteins possess a J‐like domain lacking the conserved HPD motif and are therefore considered functionally distinct from canonical Hsp70‐dependent co‐chaperones. These proteins may participate in regulatory pathways independent of direct Hsp70 interaction. For instance, members such as DnaJD9 (ARC6) are involved in chloroplast division, while DnaJD11 (CPP1) contributes to chlorophyll biosynthesis, indicating roles in organelle development and metabolic regulation. Some DnaJD proteins, including DnaJD2 (PAM16) and DnaJD15 (CRRL), may indirectly influence Hsp70 activity through interactions with other JDPs, highlighting the complexity of the chaperone network (Y. Luo et al., 2019; Verma et al., 2019; L. Zhao et al., 2022).

From an evolutionary perspective, the DnaJ gene family exhibits notable expansion and diversification across plant lineages. Comparative genomic analyses indicate that class A and B DnaJ proteins are relatively conserved across both monocots and dicots, reflecting their essential roles in core proteostasis functions. In contrast, class C members show substantial expansion, particularly in higher plants, likely driven by gene duplication events such as whole‐genome duplication and tandem duplication. This expansion is associated with functional diversification, allowing plants to adapt to species‐specific environmental conditions and developmental requirements (Qin et al., 2024). Differences in gene family size and composition between monocot and dicot species further suggest lineage‐specific evolutionary trajectories, with polyploid crops often harboring a greater number of DnaJ genes due to genome complexity. Mechanistically, the interaction between DnaJ proteins and Hsp70 is central to their function; however, emerging evidence indicates that certain JDPs may also perform Hsp70‐independent roles. Experimental approaches using HPD motif mutants have been employed to dissect these functions, although such strategies may lead to ambiguous interpretations due to the continued presence of endogenous JDPs and potential formation of non‐functional dimers or multimers (Kampinga et al., 2019). This highlights the need for careful experimental design when studying DnaJ protein function.

Further subclassification of DnaJ‐related proteins has identified additional groups (e.g., DnaJD, DnaJE, and DnaJF), which share partial structural similarity with canonical DnaJ proteins but lack complete co‐chaperone functionality. These proteins may represent evolutionary intermediates or functionally specialized variants that have diverged from classical Hsp70‐dependent roles (Pulido & Leister, 2018). Despite these advances, the precise molecular mechanisms and evolutionary origins of J‐like domain proteins remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation. Collectively, the structural diversity and evolutionary expansion of DnaJ proteins provide a foundation for their functional versatility in plants. While increasing genomic evidence highlights their potential roles in stress adaptation and development, translating these findings into practical applications, particularly in crop improvement, requires more robust functional validation under field conditions. Future research integrating comparative genomics, molecular characterization, and phenotypic validation will be essential to fully exploit the potential of DnaJ proteins in agricultural systems (Figure 1).

COMPARATIVE GENOME‐WIDE STUDIES

Genome‐wide identification and transcriptomic profiling have substantially advanced our understanding of DnaJ proteins across diverse plant species. While early studies primarily focused on cataloguing gene numbers, recent comparative analyses provide deeper insights into the evolutionary expansion, functional diversification, and stress‐responsive roles of DnaJ gene families. These studies integrate in silico predictions, phylogenetic analyses, and expression profiling to uncover both conserved functions and lineage‐specific adaptations (Safder et al., 2022). A key emerging pattern is the variation in DnaJ gene family size across plant species, which reflects differences in genome complexity, polyploidy, and evolutionary history. For example, the model plant Arabidopsis contains approximately 97 JDPs, many of which have been functionally linked to development and stress responses (Miernyk, 2001; Pulido & Leister, 2018; Chen et al., 2025). Comparable numbers have been reported in diploid cereal crops such as rice (Oryza sativa) (∼115 genes) and maize (Zea mays) (∼91 genes), whereas polyploid species such as wheat (Triticum aestivum) exhibit a marked expansion (∼236 genes), likely driven by whole‐genome duplication events and subgenome retention (Luo ying et al., 2019; G. Li et al., 2024; T.‐T. Liu et al., 2022). Similar expansion patterns are observed in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), which harbors over 224 DnaJ genes (Chen et al., 2025), further supporting the role of genome duplication in shaping this gene family (Figure 2).

Beyond gene counts, phylogenetic and domain‐based classifications reveal important evolutionary trends. Class A and B DnaJ proteins are generally conserved across monocots and dicots, indicating their essential roles in core proteostasis functions. In contrast, class C members show extensive expansion and diversification, often associated with subcellular specialization and stress‐responsive functions. This suggests that class C DnaJs have undergone functional divergence, enabling plants to adapt to species‐specific environmental pressures. Comparative collinearity and phylogenetic analyses further support the idea that gene duplication events, followed by neofunctionalization or subfunctionalization, have contributed to the diversification of DnaJ functions across plant lineages.

Expression profiling studies provide additional functional insights by linking DnaJ genes to specific biological processes. In cereals such as rice and maize, subsets of DnaJ genes exhibit tissue‐specific and stress‐inducible expression patterns, particularly under heat, drought, and salinity stresses, indicating their roles in stress adaptation and developmental regulation (Y. Luo et al., 2019; H. Wang et al., 2024). Similarly, in sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), DnaJ genes are implicated in coordinating stress responses with hormone signaling pathways, highlighting their integration into broader regulatory networks (Nagaraju et al., 2020). In wheat, selected TaDnaJ genes have been associated with both biotic stress tolerance and physiological regulation (T‐T. Liu et al., 2022), although comprehensive functional validation remains limited (Figure 3).

Comparative analyses across dicot crops further underscore the functional diversity of DnaJ proteins. In Solanaceae species, including potato (Solanum tuberosum), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), and pepper (Capsicum annuum), DnaJ genes exhibit diverse expression profiles linked to stress responses, development, and secondary metabolism, such as capsaicinoid biosynthesis in pepper (F. F. Fan et al., 2020; K. Li et al., 2022; Q. Wang et al., 2025) (Figure 2). In oilseed crops like soybean (Glycine max), DnaJ genes are associated with growth regulation, yield‐related traits, and stress responses, including alkaline stress tolerance (Razzaq et al., 2023; B. Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, sunflower (Helianthus annuus) shows a large repertoire of DnaJ genes responsive to heat stress, reinforcing their conserved role in thermotolerance. In horticultural crops, DnaJ proteins also contribute to stress adaptation and quality‐related traits. For instance, citrus species possess 86 DnaJ genes, some of which have been functionally validated in biotic stress responses (Tian et al., 2024). In grapevine (Vitis vinifera), 78 DnaJ genes have been identified, with roles predicted in stress tolerance and fruit development, suggesting potential links to postharvest quality and storage physiology (T. Chen et al., 2021). Similarly, cucumber (Cucumis sativus) and cabbage (Brassica oleracea) exhibit DnaJ gene families responsive to temperature and other stress conditions, highlighting their conserved stress‐related functions across horticultural systems (M.‐f. Song et al., 2021; B. Zhang et al., 2018) (Figure 2).

Despite these advances, a critical limitation across most genome‐wide studies is the gap between gene identification and functional validation. While expression profiling provides valuable clues, only a small fraction of DnaJ genes have been experimentally characterized at the molecular or physiological level, and even fewer have been evaluated under field conditions. This limits our ability to directly translate genomic insights into practical crop improvement strategies. Overall, comparative genome‐wide analyses reveal that DnaJ proteins are evolutionarily conserved yet functionally diversified regulators of plant proteostasis and stress responses. Future research should prioritize integrative approaches combining phylogenomics, gene editing, and field‐based validation to bridge the gap between genomic discovery and agricultural application. FUNCTIONAL VALIDATION AND MECHANISTIC INSIGHTS OF DNAJ PROTEINS

Genome‐wide surveys have revealed large and diverse DnaJ gene families across monocots, dicots, and horticultural, oilseed, and staple crops. While gene identification provides a foundational understanding, recent functional studies have moved beyond theoretical annotation to reveal that DnaJ proteins execute specific mechanistic roles in stress adaptation, development, organellar proteostasis, and hormone‐mediated signaling (Y. Jin et al., 2024; Y. Luo et al., 2019; Nagaraju et al., 2020). Integration of transgenic overexpression, gene knockouts, RNAi silencing, and expression profiling has enabled researchers to dissect how these co‐chaperones coordinate PQC, developmental programs, and stress‐responsive networks across tissues and species (Kong et al., 2014; Nagaraju et al., 2020).

In Arabidopsis, mechanistic studies have demonstrated diverse DnaJ functions. The TMS1 gene (AtERdJ3A) enhances pollen tube thermotolerance by stimulating BiP ATPase activity, facilitating proper folding of heat‐sensitive proteins, and mitigating proteotoxic aggregation (Ma et al., 2015; Tak et al., 2023) (Figure 4). Similarly, AtDjA3 regulates abscisic acid (ABA)‐dependent stress responses, functioning as both a molecular chaperone and a transcriptional regulator during germination and early seedling growth (Salas‐Muñoz et al., 2016). AtDjC17, a type III J‐protein in Arabidopsis thaliana, is required for position‐dependent root hair development. T‐DNA knockout mutants exhibited aberrant cortical cell divisions, leading to ectopic root hair formation, highlighting the importance of DnaJ co‐chaperone activity in root cell fate determination (Petti et al., 2014). AtDjA2 and AtDjA3 genes modulate seedling thermotolerance, with double knockout lines showing impaired heat tolerance and overexpression enhancing stress resilience (G. L. Li et al., 2007). The type‐I DnaJ protein NtDnaJ1 from Nicotiana tabacum enhances drought and osmotic stress tolerance in Arabidopsis when overexpressed. Transgenic plants show reduced H2O2 accumulation and increased expression of ABA‐dependent and antioxidant genes, indicating that NtDnaJ1 mediates abiotic stress responses through regulation of stress‐responsive pathways (Xia et al., 2014). Together, these studies highlight the diverse gene‐ and protein‐level roles of DnaJs in plant stress adaptation.

In cereal crops, mechanistic validation highlights DnaJ roles in ion homeostasis and organellar proteostasis. In rice, OsDNAJ15, a plasma membrane‐localized DnaJ protein in rice, is essential for salt stress tolerance. Loss‐of‐function mutants exhibit growth defects and salt sensitivity, whereas overexpression enhances tolerance. Under salt stress, OsDNAJ15 relocates to the nucleus and interacts with OsBAG4, and together they activate OsMYB106 to regulate OsHKT1;5 expression, highlighting a novel chaperone‐mediated transcriptional mechanism (Y. Liu et al., 2023). OsDjA7 and OsDjA8 are critical for chloroplast differentiation, linking DnaJ‐mediated PQC to photosynthetic competency and stress adaptation (X. Zhu et al., 2015) (Figure 4). In maize, chloroplast‐localized ZmDnaJs (e.g., ZmNCED6) modulate ROS accumulation and drought‐responsive gene networks, highlighting the context‐dependent and isoform‐specific activity of DnaJ proteins in stress regulation (Dong et al., 2025; G. Li et al., 2024).

Oilseed crops provide complementary mechanistic insights. Soybean GmDnaJA6 enhances saline and alkaline stress tolerance by interacting with Hsp70 partners and coordinating hormone signaling pathways (B. Zhang et al., 2023). Nuclear‐localized type III DnaJs, such as GmHSP40.1, trigger hypersensitive response‐like programmed cell death, conferring resistance to viral pathogens and demonstrating that DnaJ‐mediated proteostasis intersects with plant immunity (Liu & Whitham, 2013). Wild soybean GsJ11 modulates root‐specific gene expression, improving alkaline tolerance and revealing tissue‐specific mechanistic action (X. Song et al., 2017).

In horticultural species, DnaJ proteins also regulate fruit development, ripening, and postharvest quality. In grapevine, VvDnaJs coordinate developmental and stress signaling, while pepper CaDnaJs exhibit stage‐specific upregulation during fruit maturation, linking DnaJ activity mechanistically to secondary metabolism and hormone regulation (T. Chen et al., 2021; F. Fan et al., 2017). In potato, StDnaJs enhance salinity tolerance via protein folding and ROS mitigation, highlighting their direct involvement in stress adaptation pathways (Fu et al., 2025).

Interestingly, functional conservation extends to plant‐associated microbes. In Pseudomonas cichorii, DnaJ is essential for virulence, stress tolerance, and evasion of host defenses, illustrating the broad evolutionary conservation of DnaJ‐mediated PQC in both plants and associated pathogens (Tran Thi Ngoc et al., 2023). DNAJ genes in Myzus persicae play crucial roles in UV‐B stress adaptation. Six MpDNAJ genes showed stage‐ and tissue‐specific expression, with all genes upregulated under UV‐B exposure. RNA interference‐mediated knockouts reduced survival, growth, and development, demonstrating that DNAJ proteins are essential for insect stress resilience (He et al., 2024). The rice DnaJ protein OsDjA6 negatively regulates basal resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae. Silencing OsDjA6 enhances disease resistance, increases ROS production, and upregulates defense genes (WRKY45, NPR1, and PR5), likely via the salicylic acid (SA) pathway. OsDjA6 also interacts with the E3 ligase OsZFP1, suggesting a role in ubiquitin‐mediated regulation of innate immunity (Zhong et al., 2018) (Figure 4).

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that DnaJ proteins operate through specific protein–protein interactions, co‐chaperone partnerships with Hsp70/Hsp90, organellar targeting, and modulation of hormonal and stress signaling networks. Their functions are context‐ and tissue‐specific, moving beyond theoretical predictions to experimentally validated mechanistic roles. By integrating functional genomics, molecular biology, and physiological analyses, these findings position DnaJ proteins as precision targets for genetic and biotechnological strategies aimed at improving stress resilience, productivity, and crop quality (Y. Jin et al., 2024; Kong et al., 2014; Y. Luo et al., 2019).

STRESS RESPONSES AND POSTHARVEST FUNCTIONS MEDIATED BY DNAJ PROTEINS

DnaJ proteins are central regulators of cellular proteostasis, functioning as co‐chaperones of Hsp70 to stabilize non‐native polypeptides, prevent aggregation, and ensure correct protein folding under normal and stress conditions. Beyond this canonical role, accumulating evidence demonstrates that they are deeply integrated into stress signaling networks and developmental regulation, thereby influencing plant adaptation to environmental constraints, pathogen defense, and postharvest physiology.

Abiotic stress tolerance

Abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, heat, cold, osmotic imbalance, and UV‐B exposure severely disrupt cellular homeostasis by inducing protein misfolding and oxidative damage. DnaJ proteins mitigate these effects by maintaining proteostasis and linking chaperone activity with hormone‐ and redox‐based signaling pathways, including ABA, melatonin, brassinosteroids, and ROS detoxification systems. In rice, OsDNAJ15 mediates salinity tolerance through nuclear relocation and interaction with OsBAG4 and OsMYB106, leading to activation of OsHKT1;5 and improved sodium homeostasis (Y. Liu et al., 2023). In maize, chloroplast and nuclear DnaJ members such as ZmNCED6 regulate drought‐responsive ABA signaling networks involving ZmPYL10, ZmPP2C44, ZmEREB65, and ZmABI5, highlighting their role in transcriptional reprogramming under water deficit (Dong et al., 2025; G. Li et al., 2024).

Tomato DnaJ proteins further expand functional diversity in abiotic stress tolerance. SlCDJ2 maintains Rubisco activity and CO2 assimilation under heat stress, preserving photosynthetic efficiency. In contrast, DnaJ1–F3HL enhances antioxidant enzyme activity and ROS scavenging under drought conditions, improving cellular protection against oxidative injury. Additionally, Hsp40 interacts with SNAT enzymes to regulate melatonin biosynthesis, forming a novel DnaJ‐melatonin axis that contributes to heat stress tolerance (C. Chen, Wang, et al., 2025; G. Wang et al., 2015; X. Wang et al., 2020). In tobacco, NtDnaJ1 enhances drought and osmotic stress tolerance by suppressing oxidative damage and activating ABA‐dependent antioxidant gene expression when overexpressed in heterologous systems (Xia et al., 2014). In soybean, GmDnaJC7, GmDNJ1, and GsJ11 regulate drought and alkaline tolerance through protein refolding, root‐specific transcriptional regulation, and Hsp70‐assisted folding mechanisms (T. Jin et al., 2022; K. P. Li et al., 2021; X. Song et al., 2017). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DnaJ proteins function not only as chaperones but also as key modulators of stress‐responsive transcriptional and hormonal networks.

Biotic stress and immunity

DnaJ proteins play essential roles in plant immune responses by ensuring proper folding and stability of immune receptors, signaling proteins, and transcription factors involved in defense activation. They regulate both basal and inducible immunity through SA, jasmonic acid (JA), and ROS‐mediated pathways. In rice, OsDjA6 acts as a negative regulator of resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae; its silencing enhances ROS accumulation and upregulates defense‐related genes such as WRKY45, NPR1, and PR5, indicating involvement in SA‐dependent immunity (X., Zhong et al., 2018). In soybean, nuclear‐localized GmHSP40.1 triggers hypersensitive response‐like programmed cell death via MAPK signaling, enhancing viral resistance (Liu & Whitham, 2013).

In wheat, multiple TaDnaJ genes are strongly induced during wheat yellow mosaic virus infection, and viral proteins interact with TaDJC, altering host transcriptional responses (T.‐T. Liu et al., 2022). In tomato, LeCDJ2 contributes to resistance against bacterial pathogens such as Pseudomonas solanacearum, while SlDnaJ participates in SA/JA‐mediated defense against tomato spotted wilt virus (Qi et al., 2025; G. Wang et al., 2014). In cassava and citrus, MeHAM1–MeDnaJA2 links histone acetylation with disease resistance, while ClDJC24 is associated with HLB ‐resistance, indicating epigenetic and transcriptional regulation roles of DnaJ proteins in immunity (Y. Tian et al., 2024; H. Zhao et al., 2023). In cotton, coordinated Hsp40/Hsp70 networks contribute to Verticillium wilt resistance and broader stress tolerance responses (X. Zhou et al., 2023). Importantly, microbial DnaJ proteins also contribute to pathogenic fitness. In Pseudomonas cichorii, DnaJ enhances bacterial survival, colonization, and virulence by improving tolerance to host‐derived stresses, highlighting the evolutionary conservation and dual functionality of this chaperone system in host‐pathogen interactions (Tran Thi Ngoc et al., 2023).

Postharvest fruit quality and stress protection

DnaJ proteins also play emerging roles in postharvest physiology by maintaining protein stability, preserving photosynthetic and metabolic function, and mitigating oxidative stress during storage and ripening. In tomato, chloroplast‐targeted DnaJs stabilize photosystem II and reduce oxidative damage, thereby maintaining fruit quality during postharvest conditions. Grapevine VvDnaJs and pepper CaDnaJs are involved in fruit development and ripening processes, suggesting roles in developmental regulation of fruit quality traits (T. Chen et al., 2021; F. F. Fan et al., 2020). Hormonal regulation further strengthens postharvest performance. Ethylene controls fruit ripening and senescence, while methyl jasmonate enhances fruit quality, antioxidant capacity, and stress tolerance during pre‐ and postharvest stages. These hormone pathways likely interact with DnaJ‐mediated PN, although direct mechanistic evidence remains limited (Alonso‐Salinas et al., 2024; Hasan et al., 2025).

Collectively, DnaJ proteins function as multifunctional regulators that integrate proteostasis, stress signaling, and hormonal regulation across abiotic stress adaptation, immune responses, and postharvest fruit biology. Their highly context‐dependent and tissue‐specific activities highlight their central role in plant survival strategies and position them as promising molecular targets for improving crop resilience, productivity, and postharvest quality under changing environmental conditions.

MOLECULAR MECHANISMS AND INTERACTIONS

Stimulation of Hsp70 ATPase activity and protein folding cycle

DnaJ (Hsp40) proteins function as central regulators of the Hsp70 chaperone system and are indispensable for maintaining cellular proteostasis during normal growth and stress adaptation. Mechanistically, DnaJ proteins do not merely assist Hsp70 but actively control substrate recognition, ATPase activation, protein folding efficiency, and substrate specificity. Under environmental stress conditions, such as heat, salinity, drought, and oxidative stress, cellular proteins frequently undergo unfolding, denaturation, or aggregation. DnaJ proteins act as first‐line molecular sensors that recognize exposed hydrophobic regions of unstable or misfolded polypeptides and subsequently recruit Hsp70 to initiate PQC (R. Zhang et al., 2023).

Mechanistically, Hsp70 contains two major functional domains: an N‐terminal nucleotide‐binding domain (NBD), which possesses ATPase activity, and a C‐terminal substrate‐binding domain (SBD), responsible for client protein interaction. In its ATP‐bound conformation, Hsp70 exists in an open state with relatively low substrate affinity and rapid substrate exchange. The mechanistic contribution of DnaJ proteins begins when their conserved J‐domain, particularly the HPD motif, physically interacts with the ATPase domain of Hsp70. This interaction stimulates ATP hydrolysis and triggers a conformational transition from the ATP‐bound open state to the ADP‐bound closed state, thereby increasing substrate affinity and stabilizing binding of partially unfolded proteins (Cyr & Ramos, 2022) (Figure 5).

The mechanistic sequence of the Hsp40‐Hsp70 cycle therefore proceeds through multiple coordinated stages: (i) substrate recognition by DnaJ proteins, (ii) delivery of unfolded proteins to Hsp70, (iii) stimulation of Hsp70 ATP hydrolysis via the conserved HPD motif, (iv) stabilization of client proteins during folding in the ADP‐bound state, and (v) nucleotide exchange‐mediated release of folded substrates. Nucleotide exchange factors subsequently catalyze ADP dissociation and ATP rebinding, restoring Hsp70 to its open conformation and enabling substrate release or another folding cycle (Kampinga et al., 2019). Repeated cycling enhances folding accuracy and minimizes irreversible aggregation, particularly during environmental stress.

Importantly, DnaJ proteins contribute substantial functional specificity to this system. Different DnaJ subclasses exhibit distinct organelle localization patterns and client preferences, thereby directing Hsp70 activity toward compartment‐specific proteostasis. For example, chloroplast‐localized DnaJs participate in photosystem protection and redox balance, and mitochondrial DnaJs facilitate protein import and respiratory homeostasis, whereas endoplasmic reticulum‐localized DnaJs regulate protein maturation and stress signaling. This spatial specialization enables PQC to be dynamically coordinated within the cytosol, chloroplasts, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and nucleus, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis under both optimal and stress conditions (C.‐Y. Fan et al., 2003) (Figure 5).

Multi‐chaperone complexes and client protein maturation

The functional mechanism of DnaJ proteins extends beyond a simple binary interaction with Hsp70 and operates within an integrated multi‐chaperone network involving Hsp90 and several regulatory co‐chaperones. Once Hsp40‐Hsp70 complexes stabilize partially folded substrates, selected client proteins are transferred to Hsp90 for final maturation, conformational refinement, or signaling‐related activation. This substrate transfer is coordinated by the co‐chaperone Hop (Hsp70‐Hsp90 organizing protein), which simultaneously binds Hsp70 and Hsp90 through tetratricopeptide repeat domains, thereby facilitating directional client trafficking between chaperone systems (Cintron & Toft, 2006).

Mechanistically, co‐chaperones fine‐tune distinct stages of PQC. Hip stabilizes the ADP‐bound form of Hsp70 to prolong substrate retention and improve folding efficiency, whereas Hop promotes substrate transfer to Hsp90. In contrast, CHIP (C‐terminus of Hsp70‐interacting protein) functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that redirects terminally misfolded or irreparable proteins toward ubiquitin‐mediated proteasomal degradation. Additional regulators, including p23 and Aha1, modulate Hsp90 ATPase activity and client stabilization, thereby coordinating final protein maturation (Genest et al., 2019) (Figure 5).

This mechanistic integration determines whether proteins undergo productive refolding, signaling activation, stabilization, or degradation. Consequently, DnaJ proteins function as molecular decision‐making components within the broader chaperone network by influencing substrate prioritization and cellular proteostasis outcomes under stress.

Hormonal crosstalk and stress signaling networks

Beyond PQC, DnaJ proteins mechanistically participate in hormonal signaling pathways that coordinate stress adaptation, growth, and developmental plasticity. Under abiotic stress, protein stability of signaling components becomes highly vulnerable; therefore, DnaJ proteins and Hsp70–Hsp90 chaperone complexes maintain signaling fidelity through stabilization of key regulatory proteins.

In ABA‐mediated stress responses, the Hsp40‐Hsp70‐Hsp90 complex contributes to stabilization of ABA receptors (PYR/PYL), SNF1‐related protein kinases (SnRK2s), and ABA‐responsive transcription factors (ABI proteins), thereby regulating drought and salinity adaptation. Similarly, in JA signaling, DnaJ‐mediated folding assists components such as CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1), JAZ repressors, and MYC transcription factors, ensuring appropriate activation of defense‐associated transcriptional responses during stress exposure (di Donato & Geisler, 2019).

Emerging evidence from rice, sorghum, soybean, and peanut suggests that DnaJ proteins influence hormonal balance through tissue‐specific expression, stress‐responsive regulation, and interaction with signaling‐associated proteins. These observations indicate that DnaJ proteins function not solely as proteostasis regulators but also as molecular integrators connecting protein folding machinery with hormone‐dependent stress signaling and developmental regulation (Kumari et al., 2012; Y. Luo et al., 2019; Nagaraju et al., 2020).

POST‐TRANSLATIONAL MODIFICATIONS

PTMs constitute an important regulatory layer controlling the activity, specificity, localization, and stability of molecular chaperones. Hsps, including Hsp70 and their DnaJ co‐chaperones, undergo diverse PTMs such as phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, AMPylation, methylation, and lipid‐associated modifications, which collectively function as molecular switches to fine‐tune proteostasis under both normal and stress conditions (Griffith & Holmes, 2019). Rather than acting as static protein‐folding machinery, DnaJ‐Hsp70 systems are dynamically regulated through PTM‐dependent changes that alter substrate recognition, ATPase activity, protein‐protein interactions, and intracellular localization.

Mechanistically, PTMs influence the efficiency of the Hsp40‐Hsp70 chaperone cycle at multiple levels. In Hsp70 proteins, phosphorylation can regulate ATPase activity, subcellular trafficking, and stress‐responsive signaling. For example, phosphorylation at specific residues may promote nuclear translocation of Hsp70 during environmental stress, thereby facilitating the stabilization of stress‐associated transcriptional regulators. Likewise, modifications within the N‐terminal NBD can alter ATP consumption and chaperone cycling efficiency, whereas modifications in the C‐terminal SBD influence client protein affinity, substrate release, and interactions with co‐chaperones (Porter et al., 2020). Such PTM‐mediated regulation determines whether damaged proteins undergo refolding, stabilization, or degradation under fluctuating cellular environments.

Emerging evidence further suggests that DnaJ proteins themselves are subject to regulatory PTMs that influence their mechanistic contribution to proteostasis. Co‐chaperones such as Ydj1/DnaJA1 undergo farnesylation, which promotes membrane association and facilitates spatial targeting of protein‐folding activity to specific cellular compartments. Acetylation can modify substrate‐binding behavior and alter DnaJ interactions with Hsp70, thereby influencing chaperone efficiency and substrate specificity. Moreover, acetylation‐dependent regulation of Hsp40 proteins has been implicated in ribosome stability and translational fidelity, suggesting broader functions in maintaining cellular protein homeostasis beyond classical protein refolding (Mitchem et al., 2024).

Ubiquitination also contributes to chaperone regulation by coordinating the degradation of irreversibly damaged proteins through interaction with quality‐control systems, including ubiquitin–proteasome pathways. Through these mechanisms, PTMs function collectively as a “chaperone regulatory code,” dynamically controlling the timing, localization, and selectivity of DnaJ‐Hsp70 activity in response to developmental and environmental cues (Backe et al., 2022). Nevertheless, mechanistic understanding of PTMs in plant DnaJ proteins remains comparatively limited. Most available evidence originates from non‐plant systems or broader Hsp studies, whereas plant‐specific experimental validation of phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and redox‐associated modifications of DnaJ proteins remains scarce.

Future investigations integrating proteomics, phosphoproteomics, structural biology, and functional genomics will be necessary to identify PTM‐sensitive residues, tissue‐specific modification patterns, and stress‐responsive regulatory networks governing DnaJ proteins in crops. Such mechanistic insights may reveal new opportunities to manipulate co‐chaperone activity for enhancing stress tolerance, proteostasis maintenance, and climate resilience in plants.

BIOTECHNOLOGICAL AND BREEDING PERSPECTIVES

CRISPR/Cas9 and functional validation of DnaJ genes

Recent advances in genome‐editing technologies, particularly CRISPR/Cas9, have accelerated functional characterization of DnaJ genes in plants by enabling precise mutagenesis, targeted gene disruption, and regulatory modification of stress‐associated pathways. Rather than directly serving as breeding tools, these approaches primarily provide mechanistic validation of DnaJ functions in proteostasis, stress signaling, hormonal regulation, and cellular homeostasis (Kumar et al., 2023). Through targeted manipulation, CRISPR/Cas9 enables identification of DnaJ family members acting either as positive regulators of stress tolerance or susceptibility‐associated components.

In rice, CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated disruption of susceptibility‐associated genes such as OsDjA2, OsDjA6, and the transcription factor OsERF104 enhanced resistance to rice blast disease caused by Magnaporthe oryzae, partly through activation of immune signaling and suppression of pathogen‐supportive pathways (Távora et al., 2022; X. Zhong et al., 2018). Similarly, targeted disruption of AtDjC5 in Arabidopsis improved thermotolerance by alleviating negative regulation of heat‐shock responses, highlighting how DnaJ‐associated genes can modulate stress responsiveness through protein quality‐control pathways (So et al., 2014).

In horticultural crops, functional silencing of the citrus DnaJ gene ClDJC24 demonstrated its negative regulatory role in immunity against citrus HLB, supporting its relevance as a candidate target for future genome‐editing strategies (Y. Tian et al., 2024). Likewise, gene knockout studies in the plant pathogen Pseudomonas cichorii JBC1 revealed that DnaJ contributes to bacterial survival, colonization, and virulence under stress, suggesting that manipulation of DnaJ‐associated mechanisms may also inform crop disease management strategies (Tran Thi Ngoc et al., 2023).

Collectively, genome‐editing studies provide a robust experimental framework for dissecting DnaJ‐mediated regulatory mechanisms and validating candidate genes for stress adaptation. Nevertheless, evidence supporting their direct deployment in breeding programs remains limited because most studies are restricted to laboratory, greenhouse, or proof‐of‐concept systems. Large‐scale field validation, yield stability assessment, and genotype‐by‐environment analyses remain necessary before DnaJ‐targeted editing can be translated into practical crop improvement strategies.

Transgenic approaches and proof‐of‐concept for stress adaptation

Transgenic overexpression and functional suppression studies have substantially improved understanding of the biological functions of DnaJ proteins and their involvement in stress adaptation. In most cases, DnaJ overexpression improves tolerance through enhanced proteostasis, antioxidant defense, hormonal regulation, stabilization of photosynthetic machinery, and reduction of oxidative damage. However, these studies should be interpreted primarily as mechanistic proof‐of‐concept rather than direct evidence for breeding utility.

In tomato, chloroplast‐localized LeCDJ1 and LeCDJ2 proteins improve thermotolerance through maintenance of redox homeostasis, enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and APX), reduced membrane damage, and protection of photosystem II photochemical efficiency under heat stress (Kong et al., 2014). LeCDJ2 overexpression in tobacco further enhanced drought tolerance and resistance to Pseudomonas solanacearum, suggesting cross‐talk between abiotic and pathogen‐responsive signaling pathways mediated partly through SA signaling (G. Wang et al., 2014).

Similarly, heterologous expression of VaDJI from moth bean (Vigna aconitifolia) in tobacco enhanced tolerance to salinity, drought, and oxidative stress through reduced ROS accumulation and increased antioxidant capacity, whereas expression of MsDnaJ and MsDJLP from alfalfa improved resistance to drought, chilling, and heat stress through protection against photoinhibition, enhancement of proline metabolism, and stabilization of photosynthetic machinery (Gautam et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2018, 2019).

Mechanistic evidence from rice further supports the regulatory role of DnaJ proteins in cellular homeostasis. For example, the fungal effector MoCDIP4 disrupts mitochondrial dynamics by targeting the OsDjA9‐OsDRP1E module, whereas overexpression of OsDjA9 enhances resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae through maintenance of mitochondrial integrity (Xu et al., 2020). In soybean, GmDnaJA6 and GsJ11 improved tolerance to saline‐alkaline stress through modulation of Hsp70‐associated pathways, hormonal signaling, and root‐specific stress adaptation (X. Song et al., 2017; B. Zhang et al., 2023).

Additional evidence from maize, tomato, and Arabidopsis indicates that DnaJ overexpression enhances stress tolerance through antioxidant activation, ROS scavenging, ABA‐mediated stomatal regulation, stabilization of photosystem components, and maintenance of chlorophyll content under environmental stress (Cai et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025). Despite these encouraging findings, most functional evidence remains limited to transgenic model systems, controlled environments, or early developmental stages. Consequently, whether DnaJ‐mediated stress tolerance translates into stable yield improvement under fluctuating field environments remains unresolved. Taken together, transgenic studies strongly support the mechanistic importance of DnaJ proteins in plant stress adaptation; however, their utility for breeding climate‐resilient crops requires further validation under realistic agricultural conditions.

DnaJ proteins as potential biomarkers for stress detection and breeding

DnaJ proteins are stress‐responsive molecular chaperones whose inducible expression patterns suggest potential utility as biomarkers for early stress detection and candidate indicators for molecular breeding. Several DnaJ genes exhibit tissue‐specific and environmentally responsive transcriptional regulation during abiotic stress, indicating their possible application in identifying early physiological perturbations before visible phenotypic damage occurs.

For example, AtDjA3 expression varies substantially during exposure to ABA, osmotic stress, and salinity during seed germination and post‐germination development in Arabidopsis, suggesting responsiveness to stress‐associated hormonal signaling pathways (Salas‐Muñoz et al., 2016). Similar stress‐responsive expression profiles reported in rice, soybean, maize, and horticultural crops further support their potential utility in stress monitoring and functional marker development.

Nevertheless, the use of DnaJ proteins as reliable biomarkers for marker‐assisted breeding remains preliminary. Expression‐based associations alone are insufficient to establish predictive breeding value, particularly because DnaJ responses are highly tissue‐specific, environmentally dependent, and influenced by developmental stage. Future studies integrating transcriptomics, phenomics, quantitative genetics, and multi‐environment validation will be necessary to determine whether DnaJ‐related markers can consistently predict stress resilience and agronomic performance across diverse crop systems. FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Despite substantial advances in understanding the structure, classification, and biological roles of DnaJ proteins, several important knowledge gaps remain. Future research should move beyond descriptive genome‐wide studies and prioritize mechanistic, evolutionary, and translational investigations to better define the practical significance of DnaJ proteins in crop improvement.

Limited exploration of wild species and evolutionary diversity

Current DnaJ research is largely concentrated in model systems and major crops, whereas neglected, underutilized species and crop wild relatives remain poorly explored. These species often thrive in harsh ecological niches and may harbor unique DnaJ alleles, lineage‐specific expansions, or adaptive regulatory mechanisms associated with resilience to drought, salinity, heat, or nutrient stress. Comparative studies across monocots and dicots further suggest that DnaJ family evolution is shaped by gene duplication, domain diversification, and functional specialization, yet these evolutionary trajectories remain insufficiently characterized in many plant lineages.

Future investigations should therefore integrate phylogenomics, synteny analysis, and functional characterization to determine how DnaJ proteins evolved across diverse taxa and how adaptive divergence contributed to species‐specific stress responses. For example, genome‐wide analysis in yerba mate (Ilex paraguariensis) identified a stress‐responsive DnaJ family with substantial diversity, highlighting the untapped potential of underrepresented species for discovering novel stress‐adaptive traits (Aguilera et al., 2023). Nevertheless, experimental validation of these candidate genes remains scarce, emphasizing the need to move beyond genome annotation toward functional and physiological studies.

Mechanistic insights into DnaJ function

Although increasing evidence supports the involvement of DnaJ proteins in stress tolerance and developmental regulation, mechanistic understanding remains incomplete in most plant species. Current evidence indicates that DnaJ proteins do not merely function as general stress markers but act through specific molecular mechanisms involving substrate recognition, stimulation of Hsp70 ATPase activity, protein refolding, organelle‐specific proteostasis, and modulation of signaling pathways. However, the identity of many client proteins, co‐chaperone networks, and downstream signaling interactions remains unresolved, particularly outside model species.

Future studies should prioritize mechanistic dissection of DnaJ‐Hsp70‐Hsp90 complexes, client specificity, subcellular localization, and interaction with hormone‐responsive regulators involved in ABA, JA, SA, and brassinosteroid signaling. Emerging evidence further suggests that PTMs and redox‐mediated changes influence DnaJ stability, localization, and functional activity under stress conditions (Porter et al., 2020). Integrating protein interaction assays, mutant analysis, structural biology, and gene‐editing approaches will be essential to establish causal mechanisms and distinguish direct DnaJ‐mediated effects from indirect stress responses (Y. Jin et al., 2024; Marszalek et al., 2024).

Multi‐omics integration and translational research

Multi‐omics approaches provide promising opportunities to uncover the regulatory complexity of DnaJ proteins in plant adaptation. Transcriptomic studies have revealed tissue‐specific and stress‐responsive expression profiles, whereas proteomics can identify interacting partners, PTMs, and subcellular protein dynamics involved in proteostasis. Likewise, metabolomics can help explain how DnaJ‐mediated protein stabilization influences osmotic adjustment, antioxidant metabolism, and energy homeostasis during environmental stress.

Future research should integrate transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and comparative genomics with functional validation to establish predictive regulatory networks associated with DnaJ‐mediated stress adaptation (Qin et al., 2024). Importantly, although DnaJ proteins are increasingly proposed as candidates for climate‐resilient crop breeding, robust field‐level validation remains limited. Therefore, future translational studies should focus on validating promising DnaJ genes under realistic agricultural environments, including multi‐location trials and combined stress scenarios, before their broad deployment in crop breeding programs. Such integrative approaches will help bridge the gap between genome‐wide discovery and practical agricultural applications (Tian et al., 2024; Q. Wang et al., 2025).

CONCLUSION

This review highlights the diverse and evolutionarily conserved functions of DnaJ proteins in plant growth, development, proteostasis, and stress adaptation. Across monocots and dicots, comparative genomic studies reveal substantial diversification of DnaJ gene families through expansion, domain specialization, and subfunctionalization, while preserving the conserved J‐domain‐mediated interaction with Hsp70 chaperones. Mechanistically, DnaJ proteins regulate PQC by stimulating Hsp70 ATPase activity, facilitating substrate recognition and refolding, maintaining organelle‐specific proteostasis, and interacting with signaling pathways associated with abiotic and biotic stress responses.

Functional evidence further demonstrates that DnaJ proteins participate in developmental regulation, chloroplast maintenance, reproductive success, hormone signaling, and tolerance to environmental stresses through highly context‐dependent mechanisms. However, despite extensive genome‐wide identification and transcriptomic profiling, functional characterization and mechanistic validation remain limited in many crops. Likewise, although DnaJ proteins have emerged as promising targets for biotechnological applications, including gene editing, transgenic manipulation, and biomarker‐assisted breeding, direct field‐based evidence supporting their contribution to climate‐smart crop improvement is still insufficient. Collectively, future progress in DnaJ research will depend on integrating evolutionary analyses, mechanistic studies, multi‐omics approaches, and rigorous functional validation. A deeper understanding of DnaJ‐mediated molecular networks may ultimately support the development of resilient crop systems capable of maintaining productivity and quality under increasingly variable environmental conditions while contributing to sustainable agricultural production and food security.
