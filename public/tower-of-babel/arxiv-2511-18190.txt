Local polynomial convexity at hyperbolic CR-singularities in M ⊂ ℂ n M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
Harshith Alagandala

Abstract




1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a real
k
-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The presence of CR-singularities is often governed by the topology of M:
not every compact surface has a totally real embedding in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, e.g.,
a sphere embedded in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
will have CR-singularities (
[
1
]
).
The structure of CR-singularities depends on Euler characteristic and Pontryagin numbers
for a generically embedded manifold
M
(
[
11
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
3
]
).
When
n<\lfloor\frac{3k}{2}\rfloor
, there are
k
-dimensional manifolds which do not have totally real embedding in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(
[
9
]
).
Such a manifold will have CR-singularities where it fails to be totally real.

It is of interest to study polynomially convex embeddings of a manifold in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
There are global results for certain dimensions:
a non-maximal (
k<n
) totally real embedding of
M
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
can be deformed via a small perturbation into a polynomially convex one. When
\lfloor\frac{3k}{2}\rfloor\leq n
, any
k
-dimensional compact real manifold admits a totally real embedding into
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Stronger bounds exist (
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
).
We are interested in the local polynomial convexity of
M
.
For a generic embedding
M^{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
where
k<n
,
we will show in Section
3
that

M
is locally polynomially convex at a generic CR-singular point
p\in M
of order 1 (Definition
2.10
).
For
k>n
, if
M
does not contain any proper submanifolds of the same CR dimension,
then a family of analytic discs can be attached to
M
which obstructs polynomial convexity (
[
15
]
).
This paper will focus at the maximal case when
k=n
.
Then
M
is locally polynomially convex at
p
when the point is not a CR-singularity (as
M
is totally real at
p
).
Can we asses the local polynomial convexity at CR-singularities?

When
k=n
and
n\leq 7
, only order 1 CR-singularities may occur for a generic embedding (
[
16
, Proposition 2.1]
,
[
11
]
).
When
n\geq 8
, higher order CR-singularities may exist, but order 1 CR-singularities will still be present.
Further, given an order 1 CR-singularity in
M
, there exists a neighbourhood of that point where the CR-singularities of
M
are of order 1.

In this paper, we assess the local polynomial convexity of
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
at order 1 CR-singularities.
For this, we use the classification given by
[
1
]
:
let
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth manifold with an order 1 CR-singularity at
0\in M
.
Then after a biholomorphic change of coordinates,
M
can be parametrized near
0
as

(1)

\begin{split}z_{1}&=t_{1}+if_{1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2},w,\bar{w}),\\
&\vdots\\
z_{n-2}&=t_{n-2}+if_{n-2}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2},w,\bar{w}),\\
z_{n-1}&=w=u+iv,\\
z_{n}&=w\bar{w}+\gamma(w^{2}+\bar{w}^{2})+F(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2},w,\bar{w}),\end{split}

where
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2})\in[-T,T]^{n-2}
for some
T>0
,

w\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}\subset\mathbb{C}
for some
r>0
,

f_{j}:[-T,T]^{n-2}\times\overline{B_{r}(0)}\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions that vanish to order two at zero for
1\leq j\leq n-2
,

F:[-T,T]^{n-2}\times\overline{B_{r}(0)}\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function that vanishes to order three at zero,
and
\gamma\geq 0
.

Note that
\gamma
is independent of the biholomorphic coordinate change chosen.
We will refer to equation (
1
) as the normal form of an order 1 CR-singularity.
An order 1 CR-singularity
p\in M
can be classified as elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic point.
We can bring
M
to the normal form (
1
) centered at
p
.
If
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
, then
p
is elliptic.
If
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, then it is parabolic.
And if
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, then it is hyperbolic.
This classification can also be obtained without the use of a normal form (
[
16
]
).

[
1
]
has shown that
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is not locally polynomially convex at an elliptic point
by attaching a family of analytic discs around the point.
For a generic embedding, the set of parabolic points is nowhere dense in the set of CR-singularities.
Further, existence of elliptic points near parabolic points will prevent local polynomial convexity of
M
at parabolic points.

[
5
]
have shown that for a two dimensional submanifold
M^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
,

M
is polynomially convex at hyperbolic complex points.
In this paper, we will look at local polynomial convexity at hyperbolic points of
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
when
n\geq 3
.

The first result will impose a condition on
F
in equation (
1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p
be a hyperbolic point of
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Let equation (
1
) be the normal form of
M
centered at
p
(
p=0
).
If
F(t,w,\bar{w})
vanishes to order two in
w
(Definition
4.1
), then
M
is locally polynomial convex at
p
.
Further,
\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathscr{C}(K)
is satisfied on some compact neighbourhood
K\subset M
of
p
.

The second result will impose a condition on
f_{j}
.
A hyperbolic point
p\in M
will be called
flat
if
f_{j}
in the normal form (
1
)
do not depend on
t_{j+1},\ldots,t_{n-2},w,\bar{w}
.
Equivalently
M
can be parametrized centered at
p
as

(2)

\begin{split}z_{1}&=t_{1}+if_{1}(t_{1}),\\
z_{2}&=t_{2}+if_{1}(t_{1},t_{2}),\\
&\vdots\\
z_{n-2}&=t_{n-2}+if_{n-2}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2}),\\
z_{n-1}&=w=u+iv,\\
z_{n}&=w\bar{w}+\gamma(w^{2}+\bar{w}^{2})+F(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2},w,\bar{w}),\end{split}

where

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-2})\in[-T,T]^{n-2}
,

w=u+iv\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}
,

f_{j}:[-T,T]^{j}\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions that vanish to order two at zero for
j=1,\ldots,n-2
,

F:[-T,T]^{n-2}\times\overline{B_{r}(0)}\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function that vanishes to order three at zero,
and
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(Flat hyperbolic point)

Let
p
be a hyperbolic point of
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
which is flat, i.e.,
M
can be represented
locally at
p
by the above equation (
2
).
Then
M
is locally polynomially convex at
p
.
Further,
\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathscr{C}(K)
is satisfied on some compact neighbourhood
K\subset M
of
p
.

For the proof of the above theorem, we will prove a result of independent interest.

[
5
]
assert the local polynomial convexity of
M^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
at hyperbolic points.
We will give a quantitative size for a polynomially convex neighbourhood of a hyperbolic point in
M^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}

that depends on the normal form (
3
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a manifold given by

(3)

z_{2}=z_{1}\bar{z}_{1}+\gamma(z_{1}^{2}+\bar{z}_{1}^{2})+F(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}),

where
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
,

z_{1}\in\overline{D_{R}(0)}\subset\mathbb{C}
,
and a smooth function
F:\overline{D_{R}(0)}\to\mathbb{C}

that vanishes to order three at
0\in M
.

Choose
r\in(0,R]
such that

(4)

\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}(\overline{B_{r}(0)})}\leq\frac{(2\gamma-1)^{3}}{2^{14}\gamma^{3}}.

Define
M_{r}=M\cap(\overline{B_{r}(0)}\times\mathbb{C})
.
Then
M_{r}
is polynomially convex and satisfies

\mathscr{P}(M_{r})=\mathscr{C}(M_{r})
.

It is evident that the size of the neighbourhood decreases as
\gamma
approaches
\frac{1}{2}
.
Conversely, if
F\equiv 0
, then the size of the neighbourhood can be arbitrarily large.

Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

do not cover all possible hyperbolic points.
The following is an example of a hyperbolic point that does not satisfy the condition for both theorems:
set
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
and
consider the manifold
N^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
given by the parametric equation:

\begin{split}z_{1}=&t+i(w^{2}+\bar{w}^{2}),\\
z_{2}=&w,\\
z_{3}=&w\bar{w}+\gamma(w^{2}+\bar{w}^{2})+t^{2}(\bar{w}+w),\\
\end{split}

where
t\in[-T,T]
and
w\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}
where
T,r>0
.
This manifold can also be represented as the solution to equations:

(5)

\begin{split}y_{1}=&z_{2}^{2}+\bar{z}_{2}^{2},\\
z_{3}=&z_{2}\bar{z}_{2}+\gamma(z_{2}^{2}+\bar{z}_{2}^{2})+x_{1}^{2}(\bar{z}_{2}+z_{2}),\\
y_{3}=&0.\\
\end{split}

The point

is a hyperbolic point. We do not know whether

is locally polynomially convex at
0
.

2.
Background

In this section, we gather background material on polynomial convexity, CR-singularities, and prove some technical inequalities.

2.1.
Polynomial Convexity

Definition 2.1

(Polynomial Convexity)
.

A compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
polynomially convex
if
for any point
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus K
, there exists a (holomorphic)
polynomial
P:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

|P(w)|>\sup_{z\in K}|P(z)|.

Let
\mathscr{C}(K)
denote the set of continuous functions on a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
This set forms a Banach algebra when equipped with the norm
\left\|P\right\|_{K}:=\sup_{z\in K}|P(z)|
.
Define
\mathscr{P}(K)
as the uniform closure in
\mathscr{C}(K)
of the set of polynomials restricted to
K
.
The following theorem relates function algebras to polynomial convexity.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
14
, Theorem 1.2.10]
)
.

A compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is polynomially convex if
\mathscr{C}(K)=\mathscr{P}(K)
.

Definition 2.3

(Local Polynomial Convexity)
.

A compact subset
K
is
locally polynomially convex
at
p
if there exists a compact polynomially convex neighbourhood
U
of
p
in
K
.

Lemma 2.4
.

A compact set
K
is locally polynomially convex at
p
, if and only if, there exists a polynomially convex neighbourhood basis of
p
in
K
.

Proof.

(\implies)

Take the intersection of
K
with sufficiently small closed balls.

(\impliedby)
Follows by definition of local polynomial convexity.
∎

Remark
.

If there exists a compact neighbourhood
K
of
p
in
M
such that
\mathscr{C}(K)=\mathscr{P}(K)
,
then
M
is locally polynomially convex at
p
.

The polynomial convexity of a set can be determined by the polynomial convexity of
slices formed by taking fibers over a real-value function:

Theorem 2.5

(
[
14
, Theorem 1.2.16]
)
.

If
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is compact and if
\mathscr{P}(X)
contains a real-valued function
f
,
then
X
is polynomially convex if and only if each fiber
f^{-1}(t),\;t\in\mathbb{R}
, is polynomially convex.
If
X
is polynomially convex, then
\mathscr{P}(X)=\mathscr{C}(X)
if and only if

\mathscr{P}(f^{-1}(t))=\mathscr{C}(f^{-1}(t))
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark
.

The set
\mathbb{R}
can be replaced by any arc in
\mathbb{C}
.

We know that a compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is polynomially convex
and satisfies
\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathscr{C}(K)
. The same holds if
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is replaced by a totally real plane.
The following theorem determinates the polynomial convexity of perturbations of a totally real plane:

Theorem 2.6

(
[
14
, Corollary 1.6.12]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
R=(R_{1},...,R_{n}):X\to\mathbb{C}^{n}

be a map that satisfies the Lipschitz condition
|R(z)-R(z^{\prime})|<c|z-z^{\prime}|

for all
z,z^{\prime}\in X
and some fixed
c\in[0,1)
.
Consider the set
X_{R}
defined as

X_{R}=\{(z_{1},...,z_{n},\bar{z}_{1}+R_{1}(z),...,\bar{z}_{n}+R_{n}(z)):z\in X\}.

Then
\mathscr{P}(X_{R})=\mathscr{C}(X_{R})
, and
X_{R}
is polynomially convex in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.

Example 2.6.1

(Totally real manifold)
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth
k
-dimensional manifold for
k<n
.
If
M
is totally real at
p
, then
M
is locally polynomially convex at
p

[
14
, Corollary 1.6.15]
.
There exists a compact neighbourhood
K\subset M
of
p
which satisfies
\mathscr{C}(K)=\mathscr{P}(K)
.

Arbitrary intersection of polynomially convex sets is polynomially convex.
On the other hand, the polynomial convexity of the union of polynomial convex sets is a difficult question.
We will use the following version of Kallin’s lemma
[
10
]
,
[
14
, Theorem 1.6.19]
:

Theorem 2.7

(Kallin’s lemma)
.

Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be compact polynomially convex sets in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Let
P:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be a polynomial such that

P(K_{1})\subset\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:y>0\}\cup\{(0,0)\},

P(K_{2})\subset\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:y<0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}.

If
P^{-1}(0)\cap(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
is polynomially convex in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
then
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
is polynomially convex.
Further, if
\mathscr{P}(K_{1})=\mathscr{C}(K_{1})
and
\mathscr{P}(K_{2})=\mathscr{C}(K_{2})
,
then
\mathscr{P}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\mathscr{C}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
.

Under a holomorphic map
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
the pre-image of a polynomially convex
set is polynomially convex if the pre-image is compact:
let
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be polynomially convex.
If
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus F^{-1}(K)
, then
F(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus K
, and
we have a polynomial
P
such that
P(F(x))=1
and
\left\|P\right\|_{K}<1
.
The function
P\circ F
is entire and
P\circ F(x)=1
with
\left\|P\circ F\right\|_{F^{-1}(K)}<1
.
Hence,
F^{-1}(K)
is polynomially convex.
In addition, if the map is proper, we have
[
14
, Theorem 1.6.24]
:

Theorem 2.8

(Proper holomorphic mapping)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a holomorphic map that is proper. Then
K
is polynomially convex
if and only if
F^{-1}(K)
is polynomially convex,
and
\mathscr{P}(K)=\mathscr{C}(K)
if and only if

\mathscr{P}(F^{-1}(K))=\mathscr{C}(F^{-1}(K))
.

A map is called proper if the pre-image of a compact set is compact.
In particular, polynomial convexity is preserved under
biholomorphic maps from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

2.2.
CR-singularities

Let
M
be a real
k
-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
At a point
p\in M
, we study the structure of the tangent space
T_{p}M\subset T_{p}\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Let
J
be the almost complex structure on
T_{p}\mathbb{C}^{n}
and define the vector space

H_{p}M:=\{X\in T_{p}M:J(X)\in T_{p}M\}=T_{p}M\cap JT_{p}M.

The almost complex structure
J
turns
H_{p}M
into a complex vector space.
Then
H_{p}M
is the maximal complex vector space that is a subset of
T_{p}M
.
We have
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{p}M=k
and
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}JT_{p}M=k
.
By the pigeon-hole principle,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H_{p}M\geq\max\{k-n,0\}
.

Definition 2.9
.

The
CR-dimension
of
M
at
p
is defined as
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H_{p}M
.
The manifold
M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called a CR-submanifold
if
p\mapsto\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H_{p}M
is constant on
M
.
The manifold
M
is said to be
totally real at a point

p
if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H_{p}M=0
.
The manifold
M
is called
totally real
if
M
is totally real at
p
for all
p\in M
.

For a generic embedding
M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}

and a generic point
p\in M
, the CR-dimension is equal to
\max\{k-n,0\}
.
When
n=k
, the CR-dimension of
M
is zero at a generic point.

Definition 2.10
.

A point
p\in M
is a CR-singularity of order
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
if

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H_{p}M=\max\{k-n,0\}+\mu.

For a generically embedded
M
and
k\leq n
, the set of CR-singularities of order
\mu
given by
\{p\in M:\text{CR-dim}_{p}M=\mu\}

forms a (non-closed) submanifold of codimension
2\mu(n-k+\mu)
in
M

[
3
]
.

2.3.
Preliminary inequalities

Similar to the Bernoulli’s inequality for real numbers, we require the following for complex numbers:

Lemma 2.11
.

For the principle branch of square root,

\left|1-\sqrt{1+z}\right|\leq\frac{10}{11}|z|

when
|z|<\frac{1}{4}
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|<\frac{1}{4}
,
and

q(z)=1-\sqrt{1+z}
.
Then
z=q(z)(q(z)-2)
.

Let
z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta))
where
0\leq r\leq 1/4
and
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]
.
Then
1+z=(1+r\cos(\theta))+i(r\sin(\theta))
.
Write
1+z
in polar coordinates as
1+z=|1+z|e^{i\phi}=|1+z|(\cos(\phi)+i\sin(\phi))
where
\phi\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

We can compute
|1+z|^{2}=|(1+r\cos(\theta))+i(r\sin(\theta))|^{2}=\sqrt{1+r^{2}+2r\cos(\theta)}
.
This yields the inequality

(6)

3/4<(1-r)\leq|1+z|\leq(1+r)\leq 5/4.

We get

\phi=\arctan\left(\frac{r\sin(\theta)}{1+r\cos(\theta)}\right)=\arccos\left(\frac{1+r\cos(\theta)}{\sqrt{1+r^{2}+2r\cos(\theta)}}\right),

and
\sqrt{1+z}=\sqrt{|1+z|}(\cos(\phi/2)+i\sin(\phi/2))
.
As
r\leq 1/4
, we can show

(7)

\cos(\phi/2)\geq\sqrt{\frac{4}{5}}.

We simplify
\left|1-\sqrt{1+z}\right|^{2}=1+|1+z|-2\sqrt{|1+z|}\cos(\phi/2)
.

From equation (
6
) and (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\left|1-\sqrt{1+z}\right|^{2}<\frac{4}{5}.

Hence,

|q(z)|<9/10
.

Using this inequality with
z=q(z)(q(z)-2)
:

|q(z)|\leq\frac{10}{11}|z|

when
|z|\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

∎

In then following lemmas,
F_{w}
and
F_{\bar{w}}
denote the partial derivatives of
F
with respect to
w
and
\bar{w}
respectively.

Lemma 2.12
.

If
F:D_{r}(0)\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function, then

\left|\frac{F_{w}(w^{\prime})}{w^{\prime}}\right|\leq 4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}};\quad\left|\frac{F_{\bar{w}}(w^{\prime})}{w^{\prime}}\right|\leq 4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}};\quad\left|\frac{F(w^{\prime})}{(w^{\prime})^{2}}\right|\leq 8\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}

for
w^{\prime}\in D_{r}(0)
.

Proof.

By the Mean Value Theorem for real-valued function
\text{Re}F_{x}
, we have a point
w_{o}
on the line segment
joining
w^{\prime}
and
0
such that

\text{Re}F_{x}(w^{\prime})-0=\text{Re}F_{xx}(w_{o})\text{Re}(w^{\prime}-0)+\text{Re}F_{xy}(w_{o})\text{Im}(w^{\prime}-0),

|\text{Re}F_{x}(w^{\prime})|\leq|F_{xx}(w_{o})||w^{\prime}|+|F_{xy}(w_{o})||w^{\prime}|\leq 2\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|w^{\prime}|.

Similarly, we get

|\text{Im}F_{x}(w^{\prime})|\leq 2\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|w^{\prime}|.

Hence,

|F_{x}(w^{\prime})|=|\text{Im}F_{x}(w^{\prime})|+|\text{Re}F_{x}(w^{\prime})|\leq 4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|w^{\prime}|

and

|F_{w}(w^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{2}|F_{x}(w^{\prime})|+\frac{1}{2}|F_{y}(w^{\prime})|\leq 4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|w^{\prime}|.

Similarly,

|F_{\bar{w}}(w^{\prime})|\leq 4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|w^{\prime}|.

For the final inequality, apply then Mean Value Theorem on the real function
\text{Re}F
:
we have a point
v
on the line segment joining
w^{\prime}
and
0
such that

\text{Re}F(w^{\prime})-0=\text{Re}F_{x}(v)\text{Re}(w^{\prime}-0)+\text{Re}F_{y}(v)\text{Im}(w^{\prime}-0).

By taking the absolute value, we get

|\text{Re}F(w^{\prime})|\leq|\text{Re}F_{x}(v)||w^{\prime}|+|\text{Re}F_{y}(v)||w^{\prime}|.

As shown before,
|\text{Re}F_{x}(v)|\leq 2\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}|v|
.
Since
v
is in the line segment joining
w^{\prime}
and

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
f:D_{r}(0)\to\mathbb{C}
be a
\mathscr{C}^{1}
-smooth function and
r,k>0
.
If
|f_{w}(w_{o})|\leq k
and
|f_{\bar{w}}(w_{o})|\leq k
for all
w_{o}\in D_{r}(0)
,
then

|f(w^{\prime})-f(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq 8k|w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime}|

for all
w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\in D_{r}(0)
.

Proof.

We have

|f_{x}(w_{o})|=|f_{w}(w_{o})+f_{\bar{w}}(w_{o})|\leq|f_{w}(w_{o})|+|f_{\bar{w}}(w_{o})|\leq 2k.

Similarly,
|f_{y}(w_{o})|\leq 2k
.
By the Mean Value Theorem for real valued function, we have a point
w_{o}
on the line segment
joining
w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime}
such that

\text{Re}f(w^{\prime})-\text{Re}f(w^{\prime\prime})=\text{Re}f_{x}(w_{o})\text{Re}(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime})+\text{Re}f_{y}(w_{o})\text{Im}(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime}).

Taking the absolute value gives

|\text{Re}f(w^{\prime})-\text{Re}f(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq|\text{Re}f_{x}(w_{o})\text{Re}(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime})|+|\text{Re}f_{y}(w_{o})\text{Im}(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime})|

Similarly,

Now we split the following into real and imaginary parts

|f(w^{\prime})-f(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq|\text{Re}f(w^{\prime})-\text{Re}f(w^{\prime\prime})|+|\text{Im}f(w^{\prime})-\text{Im}f(w^{\prime\prime})|\leq 8k|w^{\prime}-w^{\prime\prime}|.

0=\gamma f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})^{2}+(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})-F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}).

Solving for
f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})
yields:

f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left((\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})-\sqrt{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}+4\gamma F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}\right).

Here we choose the root that ensures
df(0)=0
.

To make sense of the square root, consider the following inequality:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{4\gamma F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4\gamma\left|\frac{F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{4\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\left|\frac{F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{4\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}8\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}8\left(\frac{(2\gamma-1)^{3}}{2^{14}\gamma^{3}}\right)\leq\left(\frac{(2\gamma-1)}{2^{9}\gamma^{2}}\right)<\frac{1}{4}.

Here, we have used Lemma
2.12
, equation (
4
),
and the assumption
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
.

This function is well defined as we take
the square root over the principal branch in the inequality above.

\displaystyle f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})}{2\gamma}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{4\gamma F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}}}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left|1-\sqrt{1+\frac{4\gamma F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}}}\right|

By Lemma
2.11
,

\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\frac{10}{11}\left|\frac{4\gamma F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}.

In particular, using equation (
4
):

\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{8\gamma},

and

\left|\frac{F_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(2\gamma-1)}\left|\frac{F_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(2\gamma-1)}4\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}.

Applying
\partial/\partial z
to equation (
17
) and solving for
f_{z}
gives

f_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=\frac{F_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})-f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{2\gamma f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})+(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})}=\frac{\frac{F_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}-\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}}{2\gamma\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}+1}.

|f_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{\left|\frac{F_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|+\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|}{1-2\gamma\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta}}\right|}.

Using the above inequalities, we obtain:

|f_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{\frac{4}{(2\gamma-1)}\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}+\frac{8}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}}{1-2\gamma\frac{1}{8\gamma}}\leq\frac{16}{3}\left(\frac{2\gamma+1}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\right)\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}

\leq\frac{2^{5}\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}\leq\frac{2^{5}\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\frac{(2\gamma-1)^{3}}{2^{14}\gamma^{3}}\leq\frac{(2\gamma-1)}{2^{9}\gamma^{2}}.

Similarly, solving for
f_{\bar{z}}
gives

f_{\bar{z}}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=\frac{F_{\bar{z}}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})-2\gamma f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{2\gamma f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})+(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})}.

We obtain

|f_{\bar{z}}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{16}{3}\left(\frac{6\gamma-1}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\right)\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}\leq\frac{2^{5}\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\left\|F\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}}\leq\frac{2^{5}\gamma}{(2\gamma-1)^{2}}\frac{(2\gamma-1)^{3}}{2^{14}\gamma^{3}}\leq\frac{(2\gamma-1)}{2^{9}\gamma^{2}}.

Hence,
|f_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{4}}\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)

and
|f_{\bar{z}}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{4}}\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)

for
\zeta\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}
.

As
P(S_{2})\subset M
,

P(\zeta,-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))\in M

for
\zeta\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}
.
So
P(\zeta,-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))
must satisfy
the defining equation (
16
) of
M
:

\zeta\bar{\zeta}+\gamma(\zeta^{2}+\bar{\zeta}^{2})+F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=\zeta(-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))+\gamma(\zeta^{2}+(-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))^{2}),

which is a quadratic equation in

:

(19)

0=\gamma g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})^{2}-(\zeta+2\gamma\bar{\zeta})g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})-F(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}).

Doing a similar analysis as above:

|g_{z}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{4}}\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)

and

|g_{\bar{z}}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{4}}\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)

for
\zeta\in\overline{B_{r}(0)}
.

By Lemma (
2.13
),

(20)

\begin{split}|f(\zeta_{1},\bar{\zeta}_{1})-f(\zeta_{2},\bar{\zeta}_{2})|\leq\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)|\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}|,\\
|g(\zeta_{1},\bar{\zeta}_{1})-g(\zeta_{2},\bar{\zeta}_{2})|\leq\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)|\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}|.\end{split}

We can check that
\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
for

.
By Proposition
2.6
,
S_{1}
and

are polynomially convex:
for

before applying the proposition.

5.2.
Polynomial convexity of
S_{1}\cup S_{2}

We will use Kallin’s lemma (Theorem
2.7
)
to obtain the polynomial convexity of the union.

Consider the polynomial
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

\psi(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{4}(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})+\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{16\gamma^{2}}\right)z_{1}z_{2}.

We will show
\text{Re}\psi(S_{1})
is to the right of the y-axis and only touches the y-axis at zero
and
\text{Re}\psi(S_{2})
is to the left of the y-axis and only touches the y-axis at zero.

From the Lipschitz inequalities in (
20
), we have

|f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)|\zeta|
and

|g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|\leq\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)|\zeta|
.
Denote
\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{32\gamma^{2}}\right)
by
\alpha
, then
\left(\frac{2\gamma-1}{16\gamma^{2}}\right)=2\alpha
.

•

Compute
\text{Re}\psi(S_{1})
:
let
(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}+f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))\in S_{1}
.

\displaystyle\psi(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}+f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\zeta^{2}-(\bar{\zeta}+f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))^{2})+2\alpha(|\zeta|^{2}+\zeta f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\zeta^{2}-\bar{\zeta}^{2}-f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})^{2}-2\bar{\zeta}f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))+2\alpha|\zeta|^{2}+2\alpha\zeta f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}).

Taking the real part and applying triangle inequality, gives

\displaystyle\text{Re}\psi(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}+f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))

\displaystyle\geq 2\alpha|\zeta|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\text{Re}(\zeta^{2}-\bar{\zeta}^{2})-\left|\frac{1}{4}(-f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})^{2}-2\bar{\zeta}f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))+2\alpha\zeta f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\right|

\displaystyle\geq 2\alpha|\zeta|^{2}+0-\left|\frac{f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})^{2}}{4}\right|-\left|\frac{\bar{\zeta}f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{2}\right|-2\alpha\left|\zeta f(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\right|

\displaystyle\geq 2\alpha|\zeta|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}|\zeta|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha|\zeta|^{2}-2\alpha^{2}|\zeta|^{2}\geq\frac{3}{8}\alpha|\zeta|^{2}.

Hence,
\text{Re}\psi(S_{1})
is to the right of the y-axis and touches the y-axis only at zero.

•

Similarly, a computation for
\text{Re}\psi(S_{2})
shows that

\displaystyle\psi(\zeta,-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}-\left(\zeta^{2}/\gamma^{2}+\bar{\zeta}^{2}+g^{2}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})+2\zeta\bar{\zeta}/\gamma-2\bar{\zeta}g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})-2\zeta g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})/\gamma\right)\right)

\displaystyle+2\alpha\left(-\zeta^{2}/2-\zeta\bar{\zeta}+\zeta g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\right).

Taking the real part, and applying the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\text{Re}\psi(\zeta,-\zeta/\gamma-\bar{\zeta}+g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left|\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right|+\frac{1}{4}\left|g^{2}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|\bar{\zeta}g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{\zeta g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{\gamma}\right|+\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma}|\zeta^{2}|+2\alpha|\zeta g(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})|-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}+2\alpha\right)|\zeta|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta|^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{8}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\alpha+4\alpha+\alpha-2\alpha\right)+|\zeta|^{2}\left(\frac{1-2\gamma}{4\gamma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta|^{2}\left(5\alpha+\frac{1-2\gamma}{4\gamma^{2}}\right)=|\zeta|^{2}\left(5\alpha-8\alpha\right)=-|\zeta|^{2}\left(3\alpha\right).

Hence,
\text{Re}\psi(S_{2})
is to the left of the y-axis and touches the y-axis only at zero.

Finally,
\psi^{-1}(0)\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})=\{(0,0)\}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
: if
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in S_{1}
and
\psi(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=0

then
\text{Re}\psi(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=0
and hence,
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(0,0)
. Similarly,
\psi|_{S_{2}}^{-1}(0)=\{(0,0)\}
.
Hence, by Kallin’s lemma,
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is polynomially convex and satisfies
\mathscr{P}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})=\mathscr{C}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
.

As
P:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
was a proper map, we have
M
is polynomially convex and satisfies

\mathscr{P}(M)=\mathscr{C}(M)
.

∎

6.1.
