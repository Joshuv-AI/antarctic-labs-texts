Diagnostic Value of Inflammatory Biomarkers in Bacterial Meningitis: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Valentine Cheba Koyiri, Abass Abdul Karim, Samuel Addo Akwetey, Williams Walana, Ezekiel Kofi Vicar, Enoch Weyori, Stebleson Azure, Alentis Abeifaa Dombadoh, Akosua Bonsu Karikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBacterial meningitis (BM) is a potentially life‐threatening condition that could rapidly progress to permanent brain damage, neurologic complications, or even death. Diagnostic tools to distinguish between BM and aseptic meningitis (AM) are essential to target the appropriate treatment to patients at great risk and concurrently spare patients with AM from wrongful prescription. The aim was to evaluate the diagnostic utility of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)‐malondialdehyde (MDA), C‐reactive protein (CRP) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels for differentiating BM and AM in all age groups.MethodsThis was a 2‐year retrospective study involving suspected meningitis cases from the five regions of Northern Ghana. We employed a Real‐Time PCR multiplex assay to directly detect Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae, simultaneously from CSF samples. Analysis of CRP, LDH, and MDA, respectively, was run on the Mindray BS240 automated chemistry analyzer (Mindray Inc., Nanshan, Shenzhen, China) and by the TBARS ASSAY (Solarbio Inc., China).ResultsOnly 90 S. pneumoniae were detected in the 210 CSF samples screened. Though a greater proportion of patients with BM and AM had elevated levels of MDA and CRP, a significant difference (p < 0.001) was only established between CRP levels of BM and AM cases. Measured LDH levels were rather low in the majority of both BM and AM cases. Patients' clinical outcomes for the biomarkers revealed; MDA levels of patients who died had no significant difference in odds (OR = 1.169, 95% CI: 0.114–12.372, p > 0.05) compared to those alive; but the CRP levels of dead patients had a higher odds (OR = 13.058, 95% CI: 2.864–59.526, p < 0.05) of having high abnormal CRP compared to those alive. The LDH levels of dead patients had a decreased risk (OR = 0.121 with a 95% CI: 0.020, 0.812, p > 0.05) of experiencing low abnormal levels of LDH compared to patients alive.ConclusionThe MDA and LDH levels were not reliable for distinguishing BM status in this study population; rather, CRP was a stronger biomarker for differentiating BM from AM. Larger prospective studies are required to investigate the role of these biomarkers in BM cases.Keywords: bacterial meningitis, biomarker, CRP, Ghana, LDH, MDA

Introduction

Bacterial meningitis (BM) is an acute disease expressly considered a medical emergency due to its high morbidity and mortality globally. Disease fatality remains high across the general population, with documented rates between 2% and 30%, but rises to at least 40% in people older than 60 years [1]. In Africa, the incidence is significantly higher, usually 10–40 per 100,000 persons per year [2]. The rapid progression of symptoms and potentially debilitating effects of the disease necessitate early recognition and prompt treatment. Coagulation disorders, shock, endocarditis, pyogenic arthritis, and prolonged fever are some commonly observed complications [3].

BM is a potentially life‐threatening condition that could rapidly progress to permanent brain damage, neurologic complications, or even death. In severe cases, infection can lead to acute neurological complications such as confusion, seizures, and coma. Furthermore, the disease poses a significant health burden due to its rapid progression and the potential for severe outcomes, including death and long‐term neurological damage. Indeed, approximately 20% of individuals who survive cerebrospinal meningitis experience long‐term consequences. Etiological data for community‐acquired acute BM points to almost 50% of Streptococcus pneumoniae, 25% Neisseria meningitidis, 13% Streptococcus agalactiae, 8% Listeria monocytogenes, and 7% of Haemophilus influenzae, in contrast to nosocomial meningitis that is associated with enteric gram‐negative bacilli in up to 33% of cases [4].

Diagnosing BM and aseptic meningitis (AM) using routine laboratory tests could be problematic [5], as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings may be inconclusive and indistinguishable, due to similar determinations in BM and AM [6, 7]. Rather, early detectable biomarkers in CSF from meningitis could overcome diagnostic challenges. CSF biomarkers such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), C‐reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, and cytokines are useful in diagnosing bacterial infections [5]. CRP is an acute‐phase protein synthesized in the liver in response to interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and IL‐8 and is elevated during infectious and inflammatory conditions [8, 9, 10]. A few studies have reported CRP elevation in bacterial infections, including BM [11]. Similarly, defensin and lactoferrin are secreted mainly by neutrophils and are elevated in CSF during the inflammatory response to bacterial infection [12]. As a catalyst of lactate metabolism, LDH is elevated in various CNS pathologies, including BM [13], and the increase is attributable to anaerobic metabolic conditions induced by BM [14]. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a measure of oxidative stress, is also an essential inflammatory response in BM [15]. Significant oxidative damage, which exacerbates neuronal injury, is indicated by a rise in MDA [16].

Biomarkers thus play a significant role in early diagnosis and early antimicrobial treatment, both of which have a crucial influence on the survival of BM patients. Furthermore, CSF biomarkers could support the prediction of disease severity, treatment response, and clinical outcome, since increased, decreased, or stable biomarker values could reflect disease progression and the response to treatment [10].

Studies on diagnostic outcomes of BM in Africa are few but critical in the disease prognosis [17]; highlighting a need for sustained surveillance on early clinical and laboratory diagnosis which is essential in disease management to reduce complications and fatalities particularly in a continent where prevalence is high. This study evaluated the levels of Inflammation (CRP and LDH) and Oxidative stress (MDA) markers in CSF of pneumococcal meningitis cases from the five regions of northern Ghana, which fall within the meningitis belt and experience seasonal BM outbreaks.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Site

This was a retrospective cross‐sectional study of archived CSF samples from 210 reported cases of meningitis across five regions of northern Ghana: Northern, Upper East, North East, Savannah, and Upper West. Samples were collected during the 2022–2023 meningitis season and analyzed at the Zonal Public Health Reference Laboratory in Tamale from August 2023 to March 2024.

Patient and Data Selection

The study population comprised individuals from whom BM surveillance data were recorded in health facilities across Ghana's five northern regions. A comprehensive logbook of recorded suspected BM cases, detailed by period and geographical area, served as the sampling frame. Data were systematically collected from patient and laboratory records at peripheral health facilities across 55 districts within these regions, including those in the Northeast [6], Northern [16], Savannah [7], Upper East [15], and Upper West [11] areas. This approach aimed to ensure the study sample accurately represented the suspected BM cases within the defined surveillance population.

Sample Collection

CSF samples were obtained aseptically from suspected meningitis patients via lumbar puncture (LP) conducted by a qualified Physician. Immediately after collection, samples were stored in cryotubes and transported from peripheral sites to the reference laboratory in cold‐chain boxes maintained at 2°C–8°C. Upon arrival at the reference laboratory, samples were stored at −20°C until further processing for Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐PCR) detection of BM pathogens. Additionally, levels of inflammation (CRP, LDH, and MDA) were estimated from the CSF samples. CSF samples were collected at the time of initial clinical presentation, during diagnostic LP performed upon clinical suspicion of meningitis, and prior to definitive laboratory confirmation. Samples represented the earliest available CSF obtained for each patient as part of routine meningitis surveillance and clinical care.

Laboratory Analysis

RT‐PCR Detection of BM

We employed RT‐PCR multiplex assay to screen 210 CSF samples to directly detect S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, and H. influenzae, simultaneously targeting the lytA, sodC, and hpd genes without prior nucleic acid extraction (Table 1). Sterile nuclease‐free water was used to prepare the PCR reaction mix, which included 1X PCR buffer with MgCl₂, 200 µM of each dNTP, 0.5 µM of each primer specific for the target genes, and 0.5 units of Taq DNA polymerase. A 2 µL aliquot of the clinical specimen, that is, CSF, was directly added to the reaction mixture to achieve a total volume of 25 µL per reaction. The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C–60°C for 30 s (annealing temperature optimized based on the primers used), and 72°C for 1 min. The final extension was carried out at 72°C for 5 min, and the reaction was then held at 4°C. Post‐PCR positive controls for each gene and a negative control without a template were included in each run to validate the results [18].

Measurement of Biomarkers in CSF Samples

CRP

All analyses were done on the Mindray BS240 automated chemistry analyzer (Mindray Inc., Nanshan, Shenzhen, China) using reagents from Kamiya Biomedicals Company (K‐ASSAY, Seattle, USA). The Analyzer was programmed to aspirate 12 µL of CSF into 280 µL of reagent 1(buffer reagent) and 70 µL of reagent 2 (antiserum reagent) in a multi‐point endpoint, 340 and 670 nm primary and secondary wavelength reaction according to the manufacturer's specifications. The CRP calibrators, ranging from concentrations 0.0, 1.0, 4.0, 7.7, 11.8, and 24.7 mg/dL, and controls (level 1 and level 2) were programmed into the first eight positions of the analyzer. Quality assurance requirements for controls and calibrators were met before analysis.

LDH

Analysis was done on the Mindray BS240 automated chemistry analyzer (Mindray Inc., Nanshan, Shenzhen, China) with reagents from Lab Care Diagnostics, India. The Analyzer was programmed to aspirate 25 µL of CSF into 1000 µL of working reagent. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 340 nm based on the manufacturer's specification.

MDA

The TBARS ASSAY (Solarbio Inc., China) was prepared by pipetting 100 μL of CSF into 300 mL of MDA working diluent and Thio barbituric acid (TBA) lyophilized powder, and 100 μL of 3,5,5‐three methyl sulfamethoxazole‐2,4‐two ketone. The mixture was incubated at 100°C for 60 min, tightly closed to prevent moisture loss, cooled on ice, and centrifuged at 10,000 for 10 min at room temperature to remove insoluble materials. Two hundred microliters of supernatant was taken into a 96‐well flat‐bottom plate and measured at an absorbance of 450, 532, and 600 nm with an Apollo 11 LB 13 microplate reader (Montreal Biotech Inc., USA). MDA and TBA condensed to generate brown‐red 3,5,5‐three methyl sulfamethoxazole‐2,4‐two ketones with the highest absorption wavelength of 532 nm at an acidic pH and high temperature. The concentration of MDA was determined by subtracting the change in concentration of blank absorbance from sample absorbance at 532, 450, and 600 nm and multiplying by the manufacturer‐provided factor.

Statistical Analysis

Data collected from laboratory analysis was exported to Microsoft Suite 365 (Excel) for cleaning. Statistical Package for Social Science version 26 (SPSS, v26) was used for analysis. In all cases, p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. Mean values between or among groups were compared with an independent Student t‐test or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) where appropriate. The chi‐square regression and correlational tests were applied to test the association between variables. The results were analyzed using the graph pad (bar charts) to show various percentages.

Some outliers were present in both groups, indicating a few cases with unusually high MDA levels. The p‐value (0.572) suggests the difference in MDA levels between the two groups was not statistically significant.

Similarly, among the normal MDA group, the distribution was fairly even between AM (54.1%) and BM (45.9%) cases. Discussion

Diagnosis of meningitis in infants and adults is of critical importance and is considered to be one of the most urgent microbiological medical emergencies. We evaluated the concentration levels of CSF biomarkers to predict their usefulness in the differentiation of BM and AM after screening for organisms prevalent in the study setting (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and N. meningitidis). Our BM consisted of only pneumococcal meningitis after screening 210 CSF samples across all age groups. This finding was consistent with the literature, which lists S. pneumoniae as a primary pathogen for community‐acquired acute BM [4, 19, 20]. Analysis of MDA biomarker revealed the majority of BM and AM cases had high abnormal concentrations, but no significant difference (p‐value, 0.572) was established, suggesting that MDA levels alone may not be a distinguishing factor for BM status in our study population. Nonetheless, MDA, which is a measure of oxidative stress and an important part of the inflammatory response in BM, has been reported to be elevated in the CSF of patients with pneumococcal meningitis [21, 22, 23, 24], which was observed in our study. This elevation is believed to be associated with significant oxidative damage, which exacerbates neuronal injury. Given the association between high MDA levels and the severity of the condition, treatments targeted at lowering oxidative stress may be helpful in the treatment of BM. Our study focused on CRP, MDA, and LDH due to their well‐established roles as indicators of inflammation and cellular damage, key processes in BM pathophysiology. These biomarkers were chosen for their potential to aid in differentiating bacterial from AM. Looking forward, we intend to explore a broader panel of biomarkers in these valuable samples to further enhance diagnostic and prognostic capabilities for meningitis.

CRP has previously been considered a useful marker of acute BM due to the significant increase in levels during the infection. Elevated CRP is highly suggestive of BM as demonstrated by earlier studies [25]. Our findings revealed that the majority of BM cases had elevated CRP (84.3%), contrasting a smaller proportion of AM cases with elevated CRP (15.7%); this difference was statistically significant p‐value (< 0.001), confirming CRP as a distinguishing factor between BM and AM. This observation is quite in accordance with the findings of [26, 27, 28, 29]. On the contrary, some authors found no significant difference in CRP between BM and AM patients [30, 31]. Higher CRP levels in CSF have been linked to worse outcomes and more severe disease in individuals with BM [32]. Our multinomial logistic regression analysis indicated that being dead (OR = 0.589, 95% CI: 0.012–0.945, p < 0.05) as a result of BM had reduced odds of experiencing low abnormal CRP levels compared to being alive with BM, corroborating earlier reports connecting high CRP levels with adverse outcomes. Though measurement of CRP in CSF is reliable, sensitive, and a simple test for rapid diagnosis of meningitis [27, 33, 34], it should not be a replacement for RT‐PCR, CSF biochemistry, cytology, and culture [27]. CSF CRP offers rapid, cost‐effective insights into CNS inflammation, useful for initial triage, especially where RT‐PCR is limited. However, it cannot replace RT‐PCR as it lacks specificity for bacterial etiology and cannot identify specific pathogens. Therefore, CSF CRP serves best as a complementary biomarker, aiding early clinical decision‐making alongside other diagnostic parameters.

The enzyme LDH is released during tissue damage because of its involvement in energy synthesis [35], and its elevations in CSF indicate increased cellular damage and inflammation. This study found that most cases had decreased LDH levels, with a relatively even distribution of cases within this category. Even so, a few cases with unusually high LDH levels were recorded in both BM and AM. No significant difference (p = 0.5412) in LDH levels between BM and AM was established. Our results, however, were inconsistent with previous studies that reported CSF LDH levels to be substantially greater in BM than in viral meningitis [33, 34]. Increased disease severity and unfavorable outcomes have been linked to elevated CSF LDH levels. The outcomes of dead patients in this study had a decreased risk of experiencing high abnormal levels of LDH compared to outcome of patients alive. Elevated LDH levels in CSF usually predict increased cellular damage and inflammation [35]; thus, LDH levels may aid in determining treatment success and disease progression.

The study limitation includes our inability to compare our results to conventional methods such as culture, biochemistry, and cytology. Notwithstanding, valuable data on biomarkers have been generated to augment the limited available data. The absence of comprehensive clinical details for all AM cases, including information on prior medical therapy, co‐infections, or pre‐existing comorbidities. This lack of detailed clinical history hinders a more nuanced assessment of potential confounding factors and the precise evaluation of misclassification between BM and AM beyond our RT‐PCR‐based definitions. A limitation of our study is the specific targeting of only three common BM pathogens by our RT‐PCR panel. This implies that some cases classified as AM might be caused by other less prevalent bacterial species not included in our screening, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true BM burden. Because of the retrospective design, information on antibiotic administration prior to LP was incomplete. Empirical antibiotic use before CSF sampling may have reduced bacterial load and modulated inflammatory biomarker levels, potentially contributing to misclassification and attenuation of differences between BM and AM groups. Although RT‐PCR is less affected by prior antibiotic exposure than culture, this potential confounder cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Due to the serious nature of BM, using a biomarker alone to establish or exclude the diagnosis is not recommended. Our study did not find MDA and LDH as strong biomarkers for distinguishing BM from AM. CRP, however, showed stronger potential to distinguish BM from AM, but its validation, as well as that of other biomarkers, requires larger prospective studies involving other bacterial aetiologies of meningitis.
