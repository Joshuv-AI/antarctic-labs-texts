Oral care interventions in adult intensive care unit patients: A systematic review of oral and respiratory outcomes
Authors: Vincenzo Ronsivalle, Paola Lo Giudice, Francesco Leonforte, Simona Santonocito, Giulia Tropea, Marco Cicciù
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult intensive care unit (ICU) patients, especially those receiving mechanical ventilation, are prone to rapid oral and mucosal deterioration, which may contribute to respiratory infection. Oral care protocols vary widely and their effects on clinically important outcomes remain uncertain.ObjectivesTo synthesize evidence regarding oral care interventions in adult intensive care patients across oral, microbiological, respiratory, and major clinical outcomes, including feasibility and safety when reported.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and LILACS were searched from inception to February 1, 2025. Full-text English-language primary studies that evaluated oral care interventions in adult ICU patients were included, whereas non-ICU, pediatric, non-interventional, non-primary, and non-English studies were excluded. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I. Due to heterogeneous designs, interventions, and outcomes, findings were narratively synthesized by outcome domain.ResultsTwenty-three studies were included. Most protocolized interventions, which usually combined mechanical plaque removal, antiseptic cleansing, suction, and moisturization, improved oral and mucosal outcomes, with benefits most evident after 48–72 h. Microbiological effects were generally transient, with rapid recolonization. Respiratory findings were inconsistent, where some studies reported lower incidences of ventilator-associated pneumonia or diagnostic scores with alternative mouthwashes or higher-concentration chlorhexidine, whereas others found no benefit for mortality or major respiratory outcomes, including a large bundle-based trial. Certainty was limited by heterogeneity, short follow-up, variable study quality, and lack of quantitative pooling.ConclusionsProtocolized multimodal oral care appears to improve oral and mucosal health in adult ICU patients, but consistent effects on ventilator-associated pneumonia and major clinical outcomes still need to be demonstrated. Standardized bundles and higher-quality pragmatic trials are required.

Introduction

Adult patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) are highly vulnerable to rapid oral deterioration. Critical illness, mechanical ventilation, impaired consciousness, and reduced self-care promote early biofilm accumulation, xerostomia, and mucosal compromise.1,2 In mechanically ventilated adults, substantial dental plaque deposition occurs within the first days of ICU stay, and pathogens isolated from dental plaque have been shown to be genetically related to those later recovered from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid in patients with suspected ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), supporting the oral cavity as a clinically relevant reservoir of infection.1,3 Accordingly, oral care in ICUs should be considered a core component of supportive critical care rather than a comfort-only intervention.4,5

Critical illness and airway devices disrupt oral homeostasis through reduced salivary flow, mouth breathing, sedation, and polypharmacy, promoting plaque accumulation, mucosal injury, and a microbial shift toward potential VAP pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.1,2,6,7 Oropharyngeal colonization is a key upstream step in VAP pathogenesis because contaminated secretions that pool above the endotracheal cuff may leak and microaspirate into the lower airways in patients with impaired defenses.8 This provides a biologically plausible rationale for protocolized oral care strategies that aim to reduce the plaque burden, preserve the mucosal integrity and moisture, and modulate colonization within broader VAP-prevention bundles, although effects on patient-important outcomes remain variable among trials and reviews.9

In contemporary ICU practice, oral care is not a single intervention but instead a set of protocolized measures are applied, which may include chlorhexidine mouth rinse or gel, toothbrushing, swab-based cleansing, secretion removal, oral assessment, and moisturization [9,10]. These strategies vary considerably in terms of their composition, timing, frequency, and intensity, making direct comparisons difficult and partly explaining the inconsistency of pooled estimates.9, 10, 11, 12 Randomized trials have compared chlorhexidine alone with toothbrushing plus 0.12% chlorhexidine,13 as well as different chlorhexidine concentrations, including 0.2% and 2%.14 More recent bundle-based approaches have evaluated the de-adoption of chlorhexidine in favor of standardized protocols focused on oral assessment, toothbrushing, moisturization, and secretion removal.15 Thus, the evidence base is shaped by differences in agents and techniques, but also by variability in terms of care delivery and protocol adherence.9, 10, 11, 12

One of the most debated issues in ICU oral care is the role of routine chlorhexidine use in mechanically ventilated patients. Earlier syntheses suggested that chlorhexidine-based oral hygiene probably reduces the incidence of VAP, although without clear beneficial effects on mortality, duration of mechanical ventilation, or ICU length of stay.9 More recent evidence has been less consistent. One meta-analysis reported a lower incidence of VAP with chlorhexidine, but the certainty of evidence regarding mortality was very low.16 By contrast, a network meta-analysis found no significant benefit of chlorhexidine at any concentration for preventing VAP or improving major clinical outcomes, and did not support its routine use.12 Similarly, the CHORAL stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial showed that the de-adoption of chlorhexidine combined with a standardized oral care bundle did not improve ICU mortality, infection-related ventilator-associated complications (IVACs), or time to extubation, although oral health dysfunction scores improved.15 Overall, the key question is no longer whether oral care matters, but what are the beneficial components for specific ICU populations, and whether antiseptic-centered approaches should be replaced by broader, assessment-driven bundles.11,12,15,16

Despite longstanding interest in oral care for mechanically ventilated patients, the available evidence remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous. Previous systematic reviews focused mainly on VAP, whereas oral health status, secretion management, comfort-related outcomes, and broader clinical trajectories have been examined less consistently.9,11,12 Moreover, substantial variations in the intervention content, antiseptic concentration, frequency of administration, comparator strategies, and ICU case-mix limit the comparability of studies, and weaken the certainty of pooled conclusions.9,11,12 Earlier syntheses suggested a probable reduction in VAP with chlorhexidine-based oral hygiene but the certainty was also low or very low for major outcomes such as mortality, duration of mechanical ventilation, and ICU length of stay.9,17 More recent studies and reviews have further challenged the assumption that antiseptic-centered oral care is uniformly effective across ICU populations, highlighting the need to interpret oral care as a complex, multi-component intervention rather than a single standardized procedure.11,12,15 Therefore, an updated systematic review is warranted to integrate evidence across oral, respiratory, and clinically relevant outcomes while also accounting for differences in protocol design, outcome selection, and contemporary bundle-based approaches.9,11,12,15,17

The present systematic review was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.18 The primary objective was to synthesize and critically appraise evidence regarding oral care interventions in adult ICU patients, including mechanically ventilated populations, by comparing protocolized oral hygiene strategies, such as antiseptic-based regimens, mechanical plaque removal, suction-assisted cleansing, moisturization, and bundled approaches, with routine care or alternative protocols. We aimed to evaluate oral and mucosal, microbiological, respiratory, and major clinical outcomes, as well as summarizing feasibility and safety signals, and to interpret findings considering heterogeneity in terms of intervention components, delivery, adherence, and study design.

For consistency, the term “protocolized oral care” is used throughout this review as an umbrella term to describe structured, standardized, or multicomponent oral care interventions, whereas “oral care bundle” is applied to studies that explicitly evaluated bundle-based protocols.

Materials and Methods

Eligibility criteria

All studies were screened according to the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design).

Non-comparative feasibility, usability, or descriptive studies were considered eligible only when they provided relevant data on predefined secondary outcomes, such as feasibility, safety, usability, symptom relief, or implementation aspects of ICU oral care. These studies were included to provide complementary information on bedside applicability and tolerability, but they were not treated as comparative evidence when interpreting the effectiveness of interventions.

P (Population): Adult patients (≥18 years) admitted to ICUs, mainly invasively mechanically ventilated patients. Mixed ICU populations were also eligible when oral care outcomes were assessed in the ICU setting.

I (Intervention): Any protocolized oral care intervention delivered during ICU stay, including toothbrushing, suction-assisted oral hygiene, irrigation, chlorhexidine-based protocols (any concentration/formulation), alternative antiseptic/herbal mouthwashes, probiotics, hydration-based strategies, oral care bundles, nurse-led protocols, professional oral hygiene, and oral care devices/systems.

C (Comparison): Usual or routine oral care, alternative oral care protocols, different antiseptic agents or concentrations, different delivery methods or frequencies, pre-versus post-implementation periods, or crossover comparisons. For example, alternative oral care protocols included comparisons between chlorhexidine and saline, chlorhexidine and alternative mouthwashes, different chlorhexidine concentrations, professional oral hygiene versus standard nursing oral care, or bundle-based protocols versus previous standard care. Studies without a formal comparator were included only for predefined secondary outcomes, including feasibility, safety, usability, symptom relief, or implementation-related findings, and were not used to infer comparative effectiveness.

O (Outcomes): The primary outcome domains were oral/mucosal outcomes and respiratory outcomes. Oral/mucosal outcomes included validated or structured oral assessment tools, such as the Beck Oral Assessment Scale (BOAS), Oral Assessment Guide (OAG), Oral Mucositis Assessment Scale (OMAS), modified Revised Oral Assessment Guide, mucosal condition, plaque burden, and dryness-related oral findings. Respiratory outcomes included confirmed VAP, nosocomial pneumonia, IVACs, and pulmonary infection-related endpoints. Secondary outcome domains included: microbiological and colonization outcomes; oral comfort/physiological outcomes such as thirst, dry mouth, and salivary pH; major clinical outcomes including mortality, ICU length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, and time to extubation; as well as feasibility, implementation, adherence, usability, and safety outcomes.19, 20, 21 The Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS) and modified Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (MCPIS) were treated as surrogate or composite respiratory indicators, and interpreted separately from confirmed VAP.

S (Study design): Randomized controlled trials (RCTs; including cluster and crossover designs), quasi-experimental studies, observational studies (prospective or retrospective), pre–post implementation studies, and feasibility/pilot studies conducted in ICU settings.

Only full-text primary studies that reported analyzable data for at least one predefined outcome were included.

Exclusion criteria: (1) non-ICU studies; (2) pediatric-only populations; (3) studies that did not evaluate ICU oral care interventions/protocols; (4) studies without relevant outcomes; (5) reviews, editorials, letters, and expert opinions; (6) conference abstracts/posters without full text; (7) animal, in vitro, or simulation studies; (8) case reports/small case series without a predefined protocol; and (9) non-English publications.

Search strategy

A structured literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and LILACS to identify studies published from database inception to February 1, 2025. The search combined terms related to oral care/oral hygiene with terms related to intensive care and critical care settings. Controlled vocabulary terms, including MeSH terms in PubMed, were combined with free-text terms where applicable. No publication-year restriction was applied. Only full-text articles published in English were considered eligible. The complete electronic search strategies for each database, including Boolean operators and database-specific syntax, are presented in Table 1. In addition, the reference lists of relevant articles and previously published systematic reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible studies. This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Data extraction

Data were independently extracted from the included studies by two reviewers (XX and XX) using a standardized, custom-designed Microsoft Excel data extraction form. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (XX) was consulted when necessary to reach consensus. The following information was extracted from each study: (1) first author; (2) year of publication; (3) study design; (4) ICU setting and patient population (including sample size and ventilation status, when available); (5) intervention and comparator characteristics; (6) outcomes assessed; (7) follow-up duration; and (8) main findings/clinical relevance.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was independently assessed for the included studies by two reviewers (XX and XX) using a design-specific approach. RCTs were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Non-randomized comparative studies of interventions were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.

For randomized studies, RoB 2 domains were assessed according to the tool guidance, including: (D1) bias arising from the randomization process, (D2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (D3) bias due to missing outcome data, (D4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (D5) bias in selection of the reported result. For non-randomized comparative studies, the ROBINS-I assessment was conducted across the following domains: (D1) bias due to confounding, (D2) bias in the selection of participants, (D3) bias in the classification of interventions, (D4) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (D5) bias due to missing data, (D6) bias in the measurement of outcomes, and (D7) bias in selection of the reported result.

Studies without a formal comparator were not assigned a formal comparative risk-of-bias judgment and were considered only in the descriptive synthesis of symptom-related, feasibility, usability, and safety outcomes.

Certainty of evidence

Due to substantial heterogeneity in terms of the study designs, intervention components, comparator protocols, follow-up times, and outcome definitions, a quantitative meta-analysis and formal summary of findings table were not considered appropriate. However, the certainty of evidence was interpreted narratively using the main GRADE domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and potential publication bias. Certainty was considered separately for the main outcome domains: oral and mucosal outcomes, oral comfort and physiological outcomes, microbiological and colonization outcomes, respiratory outcomes, and major clinical outcomes. These judgments were used to guide the strengths of the interpretations presented in the Results, Discussion, and Conclusions sections.

Data synthesis

Due to substantial clinical, methodological, and outcome heterogeneity, meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Clinical heterogeneity was related to differences in ICU populations, ventilation status, illness severity, baseline oral conditions, and co-interventions delivered as part of routine ICU care. Methodological heterogeneity included differences in study design, ranging from RCTs and crossover studies to quasi-experimental, observational, pre–post, and feasibility studies, as well as variability in terms of sample size, follow-up duration, blinding, and risk of bias. In addition, intervention protocols differed markedly in composition, frequency, duration, and intensity, including chlorhexidine-based regimens, alternative mouthwashes, toothbrushing, suction-assisted oral care, hydration strategies, professional oral hygiene, and bundled approaches.

Outcome variability further limited quantitative pooling because studies assessed different oral, microbiological, respiratory, comfort-related, clinical, feasibility, and safety endpoints using non-uniform definitions and measurement tools. Respiratory outcomes were particularly heterogeneous, including confirmed VAP, CPIS/MCPIS scores, IVAC, nosocomial pneumonia, and microaspiration-related surrogate markers. Effect estimates were also inconsistently reported, where several studies only provided within-group changes, descriptive findings, or non-comparable summary measures. Consequently, overall meta-analysis and subgroup quantitative synthesis were not considered feasible.

Therefore, findings were synthesized narratively and grouped by predefined outcome domains: (1) oral health and mucosal outcomes; (2) oral comfort/physiological outcomes; (3) microbiological and colonization outcomes; (4) respiratory outcomes, including VAP incidence and CPIS/MCPIS/IVAC-related endpoints; and (5) major clinical outcomes, feasibility, implementation, and safety. Differences in study design and risk of bias were considered when interpreting the consistency and strength of findings, including the distinction between randomized and non-randomized evidence. Non-comparative feasibility or descriptive studies were used to support the synthesis of implementation, usability, safety, and symptom-related outcomes, but were not considered as comparative evidence when interpreting the effectiveness of interventions.

Oral health and mucosal outcomes

Studies that assessed oral health status generally showed that structured or intensified oral care protocols improved oral and mucosal conditions, particularly after the first 48–72 h.5,14,23,24,28,31,33,34,36 A BOAS-guided systematic oral care protocol improved BOAS and MPS scores from day 3 onward compared with routine care.5 Similarly, professional oral hygiene delivered by dental hygienists in trauma ICU patients produced progressive improvements in oral status (modified Bedside oral exam (BOE)), with significant between-group differences emerging at 72 h and later.23

Combinations of protocols that integrated toothbrushing, chlorhexidine, and frequent hydration also improved BOAS domains, salivary pH, and oral dryness.24 In a large multicenter stepped-wedge trial,15 chlorhexidine de-adoption combined with an oral care bundle produced a modest but statistically significant improvement in BOAS, although the magnitude of change remained below the prespecified threshold for clear clinical relevance.15 Similarly, a multicenter pre–post study found better OAG scores with a toothbrush-based protocol than a foam-swab protocol, particularly from day 3 onward.28

Mucosal-targeted nurse-led interventions also obtained benefits. A protocol that combined toothbrushing, mouthwash, and Tegaderm-based protection yielded more favorable OMAS trajectories and improved mucosal healing over time.31 Supportive approaches such as using artificial saliva spray during non-invasive ventilation were associated with more favorable BOAS trends by day 3 and slower progression of oral dysfunction.34 Trials that compared alternative mouthwashes (e.g., licorice, Blue®m, or Zataria multiflora combinations) generally found oral health improvement in both groups, with either comparable efficacy or small advantages in selected domains.14,33,36,37

Dry mouth, thirst, and salivary outcomes

Evidence for dry mouth and thirst was more nuanced. In an ICU observational study, oral care significantly reduced subjective thirst immediately after the procedure, but the effect was transient and largely disappeared after 1 h.38 Objective oral moisture remained relatively stable during the same short post-care observation period, including the 1-h assessment, and was not correlated with thirst intensity, suggesting that thirst perception and measurable oral dryness may represent distinct dimensions.38

By contrast, interventions specifically aimed at supporting oral moisture, such as frequent hydration and artificial saliva spray, obtained clearer benefits in terms of dryness-related outcomes, including improved BOAS trajectories and reduced dry mouth severity over time.24,34 In the licorice versus chlorhexidine equivalence trial, both regimens improved the total BOAS scores, but chlorhexidine had a stronger effect on the saliva-related BOAS subdomain.33

Microbiological and colonization outcomes

Several studies documented rapid oral colonization after ICU admission or intubation, including potentially pathogenic and multidrug-resistant organisms.14,22,26,32,35,37 Interventions frequently reduced the bacterial burden, but the effects were often transient or dependent on the protocol's intensity.14,22,23,26,32,35,37

A single-center RCT that compared chlorhexidine with saline in elderly ventilated patients found only limited microbiome shifts, where chlorhexidine transiently reduced the bacterial diversity, but robust differences were not observed in major alpha-diversity indices, dominant genera, or clinical outcomes.22 By contrast, professional oral hygiene in trauma ICU patients reduced the abundances of selected respiratory pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae, as measured by qPCR.23

Experimental and crossover studies have highlighted the importance of the mechanical component of oral care. Hayashida et al. showed that intubation was associated with a marked increase in the oropharyngeal bacterial load, irrigation temporarily reduced bacterial counts, and rebound occurred rapidly within hours, where topical tetracycline produced a stronger and more sustained short-term reduction than povidone–iodine.26 Similarly, Muramatsu et al. found that the bacterial load was higher during intubation than after extubation, and showed that the critical reduction occurred after finally removing dislodged contaminants, where the efficacy was similar using wiping and rinsing techniques.32

Among alternative antiseptic or biological strategies, probiotics (Lp299) obtained microbiological effects broadly comparable to chlorhexidine, with a lower rate of fungal emergence in the oropharynx.30 Blue®m and chlorhexidine both reduced the oral microbial burden transiently, with no significant between-group difference and evidence of recolonization before the next care session.37 In a small double-blind trial, 2% chlorhexidine reduced total multidrug-resistant bacterial colonization and Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA) compared with placebo, although no clear advantage for VAP was demonstrated.35 A direct concentration-comparison trial also showed that 2% chlorhexidine was more effective than 0.2% chlorhexidine in reducing oropharyngeal colonization.14

VAP, pulmonary infection, and CPIS/MCPIS-related outcomes

Respiratory outcomes were heterogeneous and included direct VAP incidence, pulmonary infection diagnoses, and surrogate/composite scores (CPIS, MCPIS, and IVAC), which were synthesized separately where possible.

Findings were mixed for VAP and pulmonary infection outcomes, although several studies reported reductions with specific regimens.5,14,22,23,25,27, 28, 29, 30, 31,35,36,39 No significant between-group difference in VAP incidence was observed in the BOAS-guided protocol trial5 or the chlorhexidine-versus-saline microbiome trial.22 Similarly, the trauma ICU professional hygiene trial found no statistically significant difference in CPIS despite favorable oral and microbiological results, although no VAP cases occurred in the intervention arm during the observation window.23

By contrast, several antimicrobial or alternative mouthwash interventions were associated with lower VAP incidence. Propolis mouthwash reduced the incidence of VAP at days 3, 5, and 7 compared with chlorhexidine, with parallel reductions in MCPIS at early follow-up.25 Clove mouthwash also significantly reduced MCPIS-defined VAP incidence compared with chlorhexidine.27 Nanosil mouthwash was associated with lower MCPIS scores and markedly lower VAP incidence than chlorhexidine.29 In a multicenter pre–post cohort, implementation of a toothbrush-based oral care protocol was associated with reduced VAP among patients ventilated for more than 48 h.28 A large observational comparison further suggested lower rates of nosocomial pneumonia/VAP during chlorhexidine periods than topical metronidazole use, although the non-randomized design limits causal inference.39 Finally, 2% chlorhexidine significantly reduced both oropharyngeal colonization and VAP incidence compared with 0.2% chlorhexidine.14

Some studies reported favorable changes in pulmonary surrogate scores rather than direct VAP incidence. In the Zataria multiflora plus chlorhexidine trial, CPIS worsening was significantly attenuated compared with chlorhexidine alone, and short-term mortality (5-day) was also lower in the combination arm.36 However, not all improvements in oral health translated into statistically significant VAP reductions, particularly in smaller or shorter studies.5,23,31,32,35

Major clinical outcomes, microaspiration, feasibility, and safety

Most studies found no consistent differences in ICU mortality, length of stay, or duration of mechanical ventilation.14,15,22,29,30,39 Despite being the largest study included, the CHORAL trial found no significant reduction in ICU mortality, IVAC, or time to extubation following chlorhexidine de-adoption and oral care bundle implementation.15 Similarly, the chlorhexidine-versus-saline RCT in elderly ventilated patients found no difference in 28-day mortality or ICU-related outcomes,22 and other trials using probiotics or 2% vs 0.2% chlorhexidine detected either no mortality benefits or only non-significant trends.14,29,30

Microaspiration was evaluated in a randomized crossover study that compared two suction protocols during oral care. The optimized suction strategy increased the duration of the procedure but did not improve the primary surrogate endpoint (tracheal/oral amylase ratio), suggesting no measurable reduction in microaspiration risk under the test conditions.40

Feasibility and usability outcomes were assessed in the Swiftsure complete care system study, which found high procedural completion rates, favorable nurse-reported usability, and no device-related adverse events, although effects on VAP or major clinical outcomes were not assessed.41 When reported, adverse events were generally infrequent and mild (e.g., minor mucosal irritation, mild gingival bleeding, or tooth discoloration), supporting the overall tolerability of the evaluated oral care protocols.14,24,36,37,41

These findings should be interpreted cautiously because many studies were small, with short follow-ups, and were not powered to detect differences in major clinical endpoints.

Most randomized studies were assessed as having some concerns, mainly due to incomplete reporting of the randomization process and allocation concealment, limited feasibility of blinding because of the nature of oral care interventions, and incomplete outcome reporting in small-sample studies with short follow-ups. One randomized study was assessed as at high risk of bias.

Among the non-randomized comparative studies, the overall ROBINS-I assessments were consistently serious or critical, mainly due to confounding, participant selection, heterogeneity in co-interventions, and limitations in terms of outcome measurement and reporting. Bias due to confounding was one of the main concerns across these studies, whereas bias in the classification of interventions was generally low.

Two non-comparative studies (by Doi et al. and Almeida et al.)38,41 were not assigned a formal comparative risk of bias assessment because they lacked a formal comparator, and they were retained only for the descriptive synthesis of symptom-related and feasibility/usability outcomes.

Overall, the methodological quality of the evidence was heterogeneous, and thus the findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly those related to respiratory and major clinical outcomes.

Certainty of evidence

Using a GRADE-informed narrative approach, the certainty of evidence was considered low for oral and mucosal outcomes, mainly because the findings were relatively consistent but derived using heterogeneous protocols and short follow-up periods, and there were concerns about risk of bias for some studies. Certainty was considered low to very low for microbiological and colonization outcomes because effects were frequently transient and the outcome methods varied substantially across studies. Certainty was considered very low to low for respiratory outcomes, including VAP and CPIS/MCPIS-related endpoints, due to inconsistency, imprecision, heterogeneous diagnostic criteria, and the contributions of small or non-randomized studies at serious or critical risk of bias. For major clinical outcomes, including mortality, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation, certainty was considered very low because few studies were adequately powered and the findings were inconsistent. Therefore, the conclusions were intentionally worded cautiously, with stronger confidence for short-term oral and mucosal improvement than respiratory or patient-important clinical benefits.

Discussion

The findings obtained in the present systematic review suggest that protocolized oral care in ICU patients is associated with improved oral and mucosal conditions, whereas its effects on respiratory and major clinical outcomes remain more heterogeneous. Among the studies included, protocolized oral care interventions, particularly those that combined mechanical plaque removal, antiseptic application, suction, and hydration, tended to improve oral assessment scores such as BOAS, OAG, OMAS, and related mucosal indices. By contrast, the translation of these local oral benefits into reductions in VAP, mortality, or ICU length of stay was less consistent, suggesting that improved oral status may be necessary, but not sufficient, for measurable downstream clinical benefit.5,15,23,24,28,31

A key finding is the relative consistency of the oral-health outcomes. Studies that used systematic or intensified protocols generally showed that benefits became evident after the first 48–72 h, which is clinically plausible given the rapid deterioration in oral conditions during critical illness and mechanical ventilation. This pattern was observed in trials using BOAS-guided nursing care, combination protocols including toothbrushing and hydration, nurse-led mucosal protection strategies, and professional oral hygiene delivered by dental hygienists. Overall, these findings demonstrate that oral care in ICU settings should not be considered a single, brief task, but instead it is a repeated, multimodal intervention requiring adequate frequency, mechanical biofilm disruption, and ongoing reassessment. The positive effects observed with protocolized oral care bundles also suggest that oral deterioration in ICU patients is modifiable when care is standardized and consistently implemented.5,15,23,24,28,31,33,34

The relationship between improved oral care and VAP prevention was more complex. Some studies reported significant reductions in VAP incidence or surrogate pulmonary infection scores, particularly with specific antimicrobial or alternative mouthwash protocols such as propolis, clove, Nanosil, and higher-concentration chlorhexidine, as well as in observational studies that evaluated toothbrush-based care bundles. However, other trials found no significant differences in VAP despite clear oral health improvements and bacterial burden reductions. This inconsistency probably reflects several factors because VAP is a multifactorial outcome influenced by sedation, aspiration risk, cuff management, antibiotic exposure, ventilator duration, and baseline illness severity, which may all dilute the isolated impact of oral care. In addition, several studies were short, underpowered, or relied on CPIS/MCPIS rather than microbiologically confirmed VAP, which may have led to misclassification of outcomes and limited comparability across studies.5,14,23,25,27, 28, 29,39

The hierarchy of outcomes is important for interpreting these findings. Oral and mucosal improvement was considered a primary outcome domain because preserving oral health is clinically relevant in itself for ICU supportive care, comfort, mucosal integrity, and reducing local oral deterioration. However, oral health improvement alone should not be interpreted as sufficient evidence of respiratory or patient-important clinical benefit. Consequently, respiratory outcomes were evaluated as a separate primary domain, with confirmed VAP and nosocomial pneumonia considered more clinically relevant than surrogate or composite indicators such as CPIS and MCPIS. Importantly, several findings that suggested reductions in VAP or pulmonary infection were obtained in small trials, short follow-up studies, or non-randomized observational and pre–post designs, which were more susceptible to confounding, selection bias, co-intervention imbalance, and outcome misclassification. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of effectiveness. Accordingly, the strength of the conclusions is greater for short-term oral and mucosal improvement than respiratory or major clinical outcomes.

Another important point that emerged from the evidence is that chlorhexidine should no longer be viewed as an unquestioned default standard. Historically, chlorhexidine-based regimens have been central to ICU oral care protocols, but our evidence suggests a more nuanced picture. In particular, higher chlorhexidine concentrations appeared more effective than lower concentrations in reducing oropharyngeal colonization, and VAP incidence in some studies. Moreover, lower chlorhexidine concentrations did not consistently outperform saline or other alternatives in major outcomes, and the large trial15 showed that chlorhexidine de-adoption combined with an oral care bundle did not worsen ICU mortality or other major endpoints while still maintaining modest oral-health benefits. Overall, these findings support a shift away from a “chlorhexidine-centered” approach toward a broader, bundle-based strategy, where mechanical cleaning, moisture control, suction, and protocol fidelity may be at least as important as the antiseptic agent itself.14,15,22,30,37

The microbiological findings further strengthen this interpretation. Multiple studies showed that the oral cavity is rapidly colonized after ICU admission or intubation, including by multidrug-resistant organisms, and that the oral bacterial load can change within hours. Mechanical interventions such as irrigation, wiping, rinsing, and professional oral hygiene can reduce the bacterial burden, but recolonization often occurs quickly, indicating that oral biofilm control in critically ill patients is dynamic and requires repeated intervention rather than sporadic cleaning. This rapid rebound helps explain why single-component or low-frequency strategies may have limited clinical effects, as well as supporting the concept that ICU oral care is less about one specific product and more about maintaining continuous biofilm disruption and reducing the oral reservoir available for microaspiration and lower-airway seeding.23,26,30,32,37,40

In addition to infection-related outcomes, the findings obtained in this review suggest that oral care has value in terms of patient comfort and bedside feasibility. Oral care produced an immediate, although short-lived, reduction in thirst perception, while moisture-focused interventions such as frequent hydration and artificial saliva were more effective for persistent dry mouth and BOAS-related dryness domains. These findings are clinically relevant because discomfort, thirst, and oral dryness are highly prevalent but often under-recognized ICU symptoms. Moreover, feasibility studies and caregiver-reported outcomes showed that simpler and more protocolized approaches may improve acceptability and perceived quality of care, even when they do not reduce the procedure time. This is particularly important in the ICU where intervention uptake depends on efficacy but also on the staff workload, usability, and integration into routine nursing workflows.24,34,38,41

A particularly meaningful implication highlighted in this review is the importance of the organizational dimension of oral care. The studies with the most favorable and reproducible results were generally those where oral care was embedded in a defined protocol delivered at prespecified intervals, and sometimes supported by trained dental personnel or nurse-led structured assessment tools. This suggests that the effectiveness of oral care depends on the selected rinse or device, but also on who delivers the intervention, how consistently it is applied, and whether the ICU team has the training and resources to maintain protocol fidelity. Thus, the available evidence supports a multidisciplinary model where nurses remain central to daily implementation, and dental professionals may contribute to the protocol design, staff education, and management of more complex oral conditions.5,15,23,24,28,31,41

Overall, the available evidence supports protocolized oral care as a key component of supportive ICU practice. However, the strength of inference varied substantially across the outcome domains and study designs.

Limitations

This review had several limitations. The evidence included was highly heterogeneous in terms of study design, ICU case-mix, ventilation status, intervention components and intensity, comparators, outcome definitions, and follow-up duration; therefore, meta-analysis was not considered appropriate, and a narrative synthesis was conducted. In addition, studies with different levels of evidence were included, ranging from randomized trials and non-randomized comparative studies to pre–post designs, observational studies, and feasibility studies, which may have limited the direct comparability of findings. To reduce this concern, evidence from different study designs was interpreted separately where appropriate, and non-comparative studies were used only for descriptive synthesis of feasibility, usability, safety, and symptom-related outcomes, rather than for reaching conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Respiratory endpoints were inconsistently defined and often based on surrogate measures, such as CPIS/MCPIS or IVAC, which may have led to the misclassification of outcomes. Risk-of-bias assessments indicated that most randomized studies had some concerns, and non-randomized comparative studies were consistently assessed as at serious or critical risk of bias, limiting confidence in causal inference, particularly for respiratory and major clinical outcomes. Finally, the search strategy relied on broad oral care and ICU-related terms, which together with the English-language restriction may have led to studies being missed. Publication bias and selective reporting should also be considered. Formal funnel-plot analysis was not feasible because studies were highly heterogeneous in terms of design, interventions, comparators, and outcome definitions, and only a limited number of studies were available within comparable outcome domains. Nevertheless, positive ICU oral-care trials, particularly those reporting reductions in VAP or pulmonary infection, may be more likely to be published than neutral or negative studies, potentially overestimating the apparent benefit of some interventions, especially for respiratory outcomes.

Conclusion

The available evidence suggests that protocolized oral care in adult ICU patients may be associated with improved oral and mucosal health, particularly when delivered as a multicomponent protocol that combines mechanical plaque disruption, secretion removal, and moisturization. However, the effects on VAP and other major clinical outcomes were inconsistent among studies. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because much of the evidence for respiratory benefit came from small studies or non-randomized comparative designs with serious or critical risk of bias, whereas larger and more robust studies did not consistently confirm clinically important benefits. Therefore, confidence in causal inference remains limited, especially for VAP reduction, mortality, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation. From a clinical perspective, ICU oral care should be considered an essential component of supportive critical care and implemented through standardized protocols that emphasize regular oral assessment, staff training, and adherence monitoring rather than reliance on a single antiseptic agent. Future pragmatic multicenter trials using harmonized outcome definitions and longer follow-ups are needed to clarify the protocol components that are most effective and to determine whether oral-health improvements translate into meaningful respiratory and patient-important benefits.
