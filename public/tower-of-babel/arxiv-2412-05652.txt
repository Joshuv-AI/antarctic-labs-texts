Ali Hasan Ali, Zsolt Páles

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to establish various factorization results and then to derive estimates for linear functionals through the use of a generalized Taylor theorem. Additionally, several error bounds are established including applications to the trapezoidal rule as well as to a Simpson formula-type rule.

1.
Introduction

Let
X
,
Y
and
Z
be normed spaces over the field
\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathbb{K}
stands either for the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
or for the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
A:X\to Y
and
B:X\to Z
are given linear maps. To describe the properties of
A
and to connect it to that of
B
, it could be useful if
A
admits a factorization
A=C\circ B
, where
C:Z\to Y
is also linear map. (For instance, if
B
and
C
are bounded linear operators, then
\|A\|\leq\|B\|\|C\|
.)

An obvious necessary condition for a decomposition
A=C\circ B
is that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits B\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits A
(where
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits(\cdot)
denotes the null space of the corresponding operator). On the other hand, this is also sufficient.
Indeed, suppose that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits B\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits A
. We define
C
on
B(X)
first. For each element
z\in B(X)
, there exists
x\in X
such that
z=B(x)
and then we define
C(z):=A(x)
. This definition is correct since if
x^{\prime}\in X
also satisfies
z=B(x^{\prime})
, then
x-x^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits B
and hence, by the assumption,
x-x^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits A
, which implies that
A(x^{\prime})=A(x)
. It is easy to see that the map
C:B(X)\to Y
so defined is linear. If
B(X)=Z
, then it is obvious that
C
is uniquely determined. If
B(X)
is a proper subspace, then
C
can be extended to a linear map
C:Z\to Y
(using Hamel bases) arbitrarily. However, it is a more important problem to obtain a factorization
A=C\circ B
in terms of a bounded linear map
C
provided that
A
and
B
are bounded. The next result establishes a sufficient condition for this factorizability.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
and
Z
be Banach spaces and
Y
be a normed space over
\mathbb{K}
. Assume that
A:X\to Y
and
B:X\to Z
are bounded linear maps such that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits B\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits A
and
B(X)=Z
. Then there exists a unique bounded linear map
C:Z\to Y
such that
A=C\circ B
.

Proof.

In view of the argument above, there exists a uniquely determined linear map
C:Z\to Y
such
that
A=C\circ B
holds. We only have to prove that
C
is bounded using that
A
and
B
are bounded linear maps.

By Banach’s Open Mapping Theorem,
B
is an open map. Therefore, there exists
r>0
such that
\mathscr{B}_{Z}\subseteq B(r\mathscr{B}_{X})
. (Here
\mathscr{B}_{X}
and
\mathscr{B}_{Z}
denote the closed unit balls of the spaces
X
and
Z
, respectively.)

Let
z\in Z
be a nonzero vector. Then
z/\|z\|\in\mathscr{B}_{Z}
and hence there exists
u\in r\mathscr{B}_{X}
such that
z/\|z\|=B(u)
.
Therefore,
z=\|z\|B(u)=B(\|z\|u)
, where
\|u\|\leq r
. This implies that
C(z)=A(\|z\|u)
, thus

\displaystyle\|C(z)\|=\|A(\|z\|u)\|\leq(r\|A\|)\|z\|.

This inequality is obviously true also for
z=0
and yields that
\|C\|\leq r\|A\|
and proves the boundedness of
C
.
∎

The main purpose of this paper is to investigate the problem of factorization in the following setting. Let
I
denote the compact interval
[a,b]
and, for
n\geq 0
, let
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}(I)
denote the space of
n
times continuously differentiable
\mathbb{K}
-valued functions (equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\mathscr{C}^{n}}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\dots+\|f^{(n)}\|_{\infty}
). (The space
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{0}(I)
will simply be denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)
.) Furthermore, let
\mu
be a nonzero
\mathbb{K}
-valued and bounded Borel measure on
[a,b]
throughout this paper. The main goal is to obtain various estimates for the linear functional
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)\to\mathbb{K}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f):=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)d\mu(x).

In order to construct
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a linear map
B:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}(I)\to\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)
such that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits B\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
, we search for exponential polynomials in the kernel of
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
. For this aim, let us define the function
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda):=\int_{[a,b]}e^{\lambda x}d\mu(x).

The function
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
will be termed the
spectral function related to the measure
\mu
. (In fact, the Laplace transformation of the measure
\mu
is strongly connected to this function.) Clearly, (due to the boundedness of
\mu
),
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
is an entire function.

The set of zeros of
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
will be denoted by
\Lambda_{\mu}
and will be called the
spectral set related to
\mu
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mu}
, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be the
multiplicity of the root of
\lambda
, i.e., the largest number such that

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}^{(j)}(\lambda)=\int_{[a,b]}x^{j}e^{\lambda x}d\mu(x)=0\qquad(j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}).

Since
\mu\neq 0
, it follows that, for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}^{(\ell)}(\lambda)\neq 0
, and hence the multiplicity of an element
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mu}
is well-defined and will be denoted by
m({\mathscr{S}}_{\mu},\lambda)
. The above equality shows that, for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mu}
, the exponential polynomials

\displaystyle x\mapsto x^{j}e^{\lambda x}\qquad(j\in\{0,\dots,m({\mathscr{S}}_{\mu},\lambda)-1\})

belong to the kernel of
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
.

For fixed elements
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\Lambda_{\mu}
, we consider the polynomial
P:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

(1)

\displaystyle P(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_{1})^{m_{1}}\cdots(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{m_{k}}=c_{n}\lambda^{n}+\dots+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0},

where
1\leq m_{i}\leq m({\mathscr{S}}_{\mu},\lambda_{i})
for all
i
and
n:=m_{1}+\dots+m_{k}
. Clearly,
c_{n}=1
. Then we define the linear differential operator
D_{c}:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}(I)\to\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)
by the formula

(2)

\displaystyle D_{c}(f):=c_{n}f^{(n)}+\dots+c_{1}f^{\prime}+c_{0}f\qquad(f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)).

It follows from the theory of ordinary differential equations (see
[
9
]
) that
D_{c}
is a bounded and surjective linear operator and the exponential polynomials

(3)

\displaystyle x\mapsto x^{j}e^{\lambda_{i}x}\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,k\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,m_{i}-1\})

form a fundamental system of solutions of the differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
. In other words, the above exponential polynomials span the kernel of
D_{c}
and hence
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits D_{c}\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
. In view of Theorem
1.1
, there exists a unique bounded linear map
C:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)\to\mathbb{K}
such that
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}|_{\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}(I)}=C\circ D_{c}
. In what follows, we explicitly construct
C
and then we present several applications to obtain sharp upper bounds for the error terms of quadrature rules. For standard references about error bounds for quadrature rules, we refer to the monographs
[
2
]
by Atkinson and
[
5
]
by Faires and Burden and to the recent papers
[
3
]
by Barnett
et al.
,
[
4
]
by Cruz-Uribe and Neugebauer,
[
6
]
by Masjed-Jamei
et al.
,
[
7
]
by Talman and
[
8
]
by Ujević.

3.
Estimating linear functionals

Our basic factorization theorem is stated as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu
be a nonzero bounded
\mathbb{C}
-valued Borel measure on
[a,b]
, let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\Lambda_{\mu}
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with
m_{i}\leq m({\mathscr{S}}_{\mu},\lambda_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Define
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
by
(
1
)
(where
n:=m_{1}+\dots+m_{k}
) and the differential operator
D_{c}:\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}([a,b])\to\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{C}}([a,b])
by
(
2
)
. Let
\omega_{c}\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the characteristic solution of
D_{c}(\omega)=0
.
Finally, define
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}
by

(14)

\displaystyle g(t):=\int_{[t,b]}\omega_{c}(x-t)d\mu(x).

Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}([a,b])
,

(15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f):=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{[a,b]}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt.

In other words,
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}|_{\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}(I)}=C_{g}\circ D_{c}
, where
C_{g}:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{C}}(I)\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\displaystyle C_{g}(h)=\int_{[a,b]}h(t)g(t)dt.

Proof.

Due to the assumptions
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\Lambda_{\mu}
and
m_{i}\leq m({\mathscr{S}}_{\mu},\lambda_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, it follows that the exponential polynomials given in (
3
) are in the kernel of
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
. On the other hand, these functions form a fundamental system of solutions for the differential equation
D_{c}(\omega)=0
, which implies that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits D_{c}\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
. Since the differential operator has constant coefficients, it follows that, for all
i\geq 0
, the function

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\ni x\mapsto\omega_{c}^{(i)}(x-a)

is also in the kernel of
D_{c}
and hence in the kernel of
\mathscr{A}_{\mu}
. Therefore, for all
i\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\omega_{c}^{(i)}(x-a)d\mu(x)=0.

In what follows, let
\chi_{S}
denote the characteristic function of any subset
S
of
[a,b]
. Applying Theorem
2.2
, the above equalities and finally Fubini’s Theorem, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}([a,b])
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{[a,b]}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\bigg(f^{(j)}(a)\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-j}c_{i+j+1}\omega_{c}^{(i)}(x-a)\bigg)+\int_{a}^{x}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}(x-t)dt\bigg)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(a)\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-j}c_{i+j+1}\int_{[a,b]}\omega_{c}^{(i)}(x-a)d\mu(x)+\int_{[a,b]}\bigg(\int_{a}^{x}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}(x-t)dt\bigg)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{[a,b]}\bigg(\int_{a}^{x}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}(x-t)dt\bigg)d\mu(x)=\int_{[a,b]}\int_{[a,b]}\chi_{[a,x]}(t)\cdot D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}(x-t)dtd\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{[a,b]}\int_{[a,b]}\chi_{[a,x]}(t)\cdot D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}(x-t)d\mu(x)dt=\int_{[a,b]}D_{c}(f)(t)\int_{[t,b]}\omega_{c}(x-t)d\mu(x)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{[a,b]}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt.

This proves (
15
).
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu
be a nonzero bounded
\mathbb{C}
-valued Borel measure on
[a,b]
, let
0\leq k\leq n
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
. Assume that

(16)

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}x^{i}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad(i\in\{0,\dots,k-1\},

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\exp\big(\sqrt[n-k]{\gamma}\exp\big(\tfrac{2j\pi}{n-k}\boldsymbol{i}\big)x\big)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad(j\in\{0,\dots,n-k-1\},

where
\sqrt[n-k]{\gamma}
denotes the root of order
(n-k)
of
\gamma
with the smallest nonnegative argument in the interval
[0,2\pi)
.
Define
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}
by

(17)

\displaystyle g(t):=\int_{[t,b]}\zeta^{\prime}_{n,k,\gamma}(x-t)d\mu(x).

Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}([a,b])
,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)=\int_{[a,b]}(f^{(n)}(t)-\gamma f^{(k)}(t))\cdot g(t)dt.

Proof.

Consider the differential operator
D_{c}:\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}(I)\to\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{C}}(I)
given by
D_{c}(f):=f^{(n)}-\gamma f^{(k)}
. Its characteristic polynomial
P_{c}
is given by
P_{c}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n}-\gamma\lambda^{k}=\lambda^{k}(\lambda^{n-k}-\gamma)
. The roots of this polynomial are
\lambda=0
with multiplicity
k
and the roots of order
(n-k)
of
\gamma
(with multiplicities equal to
1
). The second group of roots can be written in the form

\displaystyle\sqrt[n-k]{\gamma}\exp\big(\tfrac{2j\pi}{n-k}\boldsymbol{i}\big)\qquad(j\in\{0,\dots,n-k-1\}).

Therefore, a fundamental solution system of the differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\big\{x^{i}\mid i\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}\big\}\cup\big\{\exp\big(\sqrt[n-k]{\gamma}\exp\big(\tfrac{2j\pi}{n-k}\boldsymbol{i}\big)x\big)\mid j\in\{0,\dots,n-k-1\}\big\}.

Thus, the condition (
16
) ensures that
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits(D_{c})\subseteq\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits(\mathscr{A}_{\mu})
. Therefore, the polynomial

\displaystyle x\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}f^{(j)}(a)\frac{(x-a)^{j}}{j!}

(whose degree is at most
k-1
) is in the kernel of
D_{c}
and hence, it belongs to
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits(\mathscr{A}_{\mu})
. On the other hand, by equality (
8
) of Lemma
2.3
,
\zeta_{n,k,\gamma}
solves the differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
and hence, the mapping

\displaystyle x\mapsto\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(a)\zeta_{n,k,\gamma}^{(n-j)}(x-a)

belongs to the kernel of
D_{c}
. Consequently, it is also in
\mathop{\mbox{\rm ker}}\nolimits(\mathscr{A}_{\mu})
. Combining these inclusions, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}f^{(j)}(a)\frac{(x-a)^{j}}{j!}+\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(a)\zeta_{n,k,\gamma}^{(n-j)}(x-a)\bigg)d\mu(x)=0.

Now applying Theorem
2.4
and following a similar reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we can conclude that (
18
) holds, where the function
g
is defined by (
17
).
∎

Before formulating the next result, we recall the definition of the
p
th norm of a Lebesgue measurable function
f:I\to\mathbb{C}
and

:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\Big(\int_{[a,b]}|f(t)|^{p}dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\mbox{if }p\in[1,\infty),\\[5.69054pt]
\inf\{s\colon|f(t)|\leq s\mbox{ for a.e.\ }t\in I\}&\mbox{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Corollary 3.3
.

Under the notations and assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}(I)
and for all
p,q\in[1,\infty]
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
,

(19)

\displaystyle|\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)|\leq\|D_{c}(f)\|_{p}\cdot\|g\|_{q}.

If, in addition,
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\mu
is a real-valued measure,
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}(I)
, and both
g
and
D_{c}(f)
are nonnegative (or nonpositive) on
[a,b]
, then

(20)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)\geq 0.

If
g
and
D_{c}(f)
have opposite signs over
[a,b]
, then this inequality reverses.

Proof.

Applying the identity (
15
) and Hölder’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle|\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)|=\bigg|\int_{[a,b]}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt\bigg|\leq\big\|D_{c}(f)\big\|_{p}\cdot\|g\|_{q},

which proves (
19
). In the real-valued setting, if
D_{c}(f)\cdot g
is nonnegative on
[a,b]
, then we can see that (
20
) holds. If
D_{c}(f)\cdot g
is nonpositive, then (
20
) is valid with reversed inequality sign.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\mu
be a nonzero bounded
\mathbb{C}
-valued Borel measure on
[a,b]
, let
0\leq k\leq n
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
. Assume that the equalities in
(
16
)
hold. Define
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}
by
(
17
)
.
Then, for all

and

with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
,

(21)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{[a,b]}f(x)d\mu(x)\bigg|\leq\|f^{(n)}-\gamma f^{(k)}\|_{p}\cdot\|g\|_{q}.

If, in addition,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu
is a real-valued measure and both

and

are nonnegative (or nonpositive) on

, then the inequality
(

)
holds. If

and

Proof.

The statement of this corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem

and Hölder’s inequality.
∎

4.
Application to the trapezoidal rule

The following lemma is very likely well-known, however, for the sake of completeness, we provide its very simple proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{t}{\sinh(t)}<1<t\coth(t)

and, for all
t\in(0,\pi)
,

(23)

\displaystyle t\cot(t)<1<\frac{t}{\sin(t)}.

Proof.

To prove (
22
), let
t
be a positive number. By the Taylor series expansions of
\sinh
and
\cosh
, we get that

\displaystyle\sinh(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}>\frac{t}{1!}=t,

and

\displaystyle\sinh(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k)!}=t\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}=t\cosh(t).

These two inequalities directly imply the left and the right hand side inequality in (
22
), respectively.

To verify (
23
), let
t\in(0,\pi)
. Applying the Taylor mean value theorem to the
\sin
function at
0
, and then using that
\sin
is positive over
(0,\pi)
, we can conclude that there exists
s\in(0,t)
such that

\displaystyle\sin(t)=\sin(0)+\sin^{\prime}(0)t+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{\prime\prime}(s)t^{2}=t-\frac{1}{2}\sin(s)t^{2}<t,

which proves the second inequality in (
23
).

If
t\in[\frac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
, then
\cot(t)\leq 0
, and hence the first inequality in (
23
) is obvious. In the case when
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\pi)
, we use the Taylor mean value theorem to the
\tan
function at
0
, which asserts the existence of
s\in(0,t)
such that

\displaystyle\tan(t)=\tan(0)+\tan^{\prime}(0)t+\frac{1}{2}\tan^{\prime\prime}(s)t^{2}=t+\frac{\sin(s)}{\cos^{3}(s)}t^{2}>t,

where we used that the functions
\sin
and
\cos
are positive over
(0,\frac{1}{2}\pi)
. This inequality implies the first inequality in (
23
) also in the case when
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\pi)
.
∎

Given a compact interval
[a,b]
, the classical trapezoidal rule asserts that, for a twice differentiable function
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f=\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-R_{T}(f),

where the remainder term
R_{T}(f)
has various estimates in terms of the norms of the second derivative of
f
and the length of the interval
[a,b]
. For instance (see
[
2
, pp. 252–253]
),

\displaystyle|R_{T}(f)|\leq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{12}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}.

The aim of this section is to establish various further estimates for
R_{T}(f)
, which is defined by

\displaystyle R_{T}(f):=\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f.

Observe that, with
\mu:=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{a}+\delta_{b})-\nu
(where
\delta_{t}
denotes the Dirac measure concentrated at
t
and
\nu
stands for the normalized Lebesgue measure on
[a,b]
), we can obtain that
R_{T}(f)=\mathscr{A}_{\mu}(f)
. The corresponding spectral function is given by

(24)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\lambda a}+e^{\lambda b}}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}e^{\lambda x}dx\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
\lambda
is a root of the spectral function
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
given by
(
24
)
if and only if
u:=\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}
is a fixed point of the tangent hyperbolic function. The multiplicity of
\lambda
equals
1
if
\lambda\neq 0
and equals
2
of
\lambda=0
.

Proof.

We can see that
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda)=0
holds trivially if
\lambda=0
, and then
u=0
is indeed a fixed point of the tangent hyperbolic function.

An easy computation yields that, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\lambda b}+e^{\lambda a}}{2}-\frac{e^{\lambda b}-e^{\lambda a}}{\lambda(b-a)}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(\frac{e^{\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}}+e^{\lambda\frac{a-b}{2}}}{2}-\frac{e^{\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}}-e^{\lambda\frac{a-b}{2}}}{\lambda(b-a)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})-\frac{2\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda(b-a)}\bigg).

Now, assume that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
is a solution of the equation
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda)=0
. Then it satisfies

\displaystyle\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})=\frac{2\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda(b-a)}.

In this case,
\cosh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})
cannot be zero, because then
\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})
would also be zero, which is impossible. Therefore,
with the notation
u:=\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}
, the equation
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}(\lambda)=0
is equivalent to
u=\tanh(u)
, which shows that
u
must be a fixed point of the tangent hyperbolic function.
To check the multiplicities of the roots of
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
, let
u=\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}
be a fixed point of the tangent hyperbolic function. Then
\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})\neq 0
and, for
\lambda\neq 0
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}_{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{a+b}{2}e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})-\frac{2\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda(b-a)}\bigg)+e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})-\frac{2\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda(b-a)}\bigg)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\sinh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})+\frac{2\sinh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda^{2}(b-a)}-\frac{\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda}\bigg)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})\bigg(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\tanh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})+\frac{2\tanh(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2})}{\lambda^{2}(b-a)}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})\bigg(\lambda\big(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg)=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\cosh(\lambda\tfrac{b-a}{2})\lambda\big(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\big)^{2}\neq 0,

which proves that the multiplicity of
\lambda\neq 0
equals
1
.

In the case when
\lambda=0
, from the defining formula of
{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu}
, we get

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{\mu}(\lambda)=\frac{ae^{\lambda a}+be^{\lambda b}}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}xe^{\lambda x}dx,\qquad S^{\prime}_{\mu}(0)=\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}xdx=0,

and

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(\lambda)=\frac{a^{2}e^{\lambda a}+b^{2}e^{\lambda b}}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}e^{\lambda x}dx.

Thus,

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(0)=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}dx=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}-\frac{a^{2}+ab+b^{2}}{3}=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{6}\neq 0,

which proves that the multiplicity of
\lambda=0
equals
2
.
∎

In order to apply our main theorems to the trapezoidal rule, we shall need to describe the fixed points of the tangent hyperbolic function.

Lemma 4.3
.

A number
u\in\mathbb{C}
is a fixed point of the tangent hyperbolic function, i.e.,

(25)

\displaystyle\tanh(u)=u

holds if and only if
u=v\boldsymbol{i}
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed point of the tangent function. Furthermore, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the open interval
\big((k-\frac{1}{2})\pi,(k+\frac{1}{2})\pi\big)
contains exactly one fixed point of the tangent function.

Proof.

Assume that
u=v\boldsymbol{i}
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tan(v)=v
. Then

\displaystyle\tanh(u)=\tanh(v\boldsymbol{i})=\frac{\sinh(v\boldsymbol{i})}{\cosh(v\boldsymbol{i})}=\frac{\sin(v)}{\cos(v)}\boldsymbol{i}=\tan(v)\boldsymbol{i}=v\boldsymbol{i}=u,

which shows that
u
is a fixed point of the function
\tanh
.

Assume now that (
25
) holds for
u=w+v\boldsymbol{i}
, where
w,v\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\tanh(u)

\displaystyle=\tanh(w+v\boldsymbol{i})=\frac{\tanh(w)+\tanh(v\boldsymbol{i})}{1+\tanh(w)\tanh(v\boldsymbol{i})}=\frac{\tanh(w)+\tan(v)\boldsymbol{i}}{1+\tanh(w)\tan(v)\boldsymbol{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tanh(w)+\tan(v)\boldsymbol{i})(1-\tanh(w)\tan(v)\boldsymbol{i})}{1+\tanh(w)^{2}\tan(v)^{2}}.

Thus, (
25
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\tanh(w)(1+\tan(v)^{2})}{1+\tanh(w)^{2}\tan(v)^{2}}=w,\qquad\frac{\tan(v)(1-\tanh(w)^{2})}{1+\tanh(w)^{2}\tan(v)^{2}}=v.

We show that these two equalities imply that
w=0
(which then easily implies that
\tan(v)=v
). To the contrary, assume that
w\neq 0
. Then
w\neq\tanh(w)
, and hence the first equation implies that
\tan(v)\neq 0
implying that
v\neq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{w}{v}=\frac{\tanh(w)(1+\tan(v)^{2})}{\tan(v)(1-\tanh(w)^{2})}=\frac{\sinh(2w)}{\sin(2v)},

equivalently

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(2v)}{2v}=\frac{\sinh(2w)}{2w}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
4.1
, for every nonzero real numbers
x
and
y
,
the following inequalities hold

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(x)}{x}<1<\frac{\sinh(y)}{y}.

Therefore, these inequalities with
x=2v
and
y=2w
yield an obvious contradiction. This contradiction shows that
w=0
must hold and hence
\tan(v)=v
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The function
h
defined by
h(x):=\tan(x)-x
is differentiable on the open interval
I_{k}:=\big((k-\frac{1}{2})\pi,(k+\frac{1}{2})\pi\big)
with a derivative
h^{\prime}(x)=\tan^{2}(x)>0
for all
x\in I_{k}\setminus\{k\pi\}
. This shows that
h
is strictly increasing and hence it can have at most one zero in
I_{k}
. On the other hand, we have that
\lim_{x\to(k\pm\frac{1}{2})\pi}h(x)=\pm\infty
, which shows that
h
changes sign over
I_{k}
and therefore it has a unique zero in
I_{k}
.
∎

The unique fixed point of the tangent function in the open interval
\big((k-\frac{1}{2})\pi,(k+\frac{1}{2})\pi\big)
will be denoted by
\tau_{k}
in the sequel.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq n_{1}<\dots<n_{k}
be integers, let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
and let

\lambda_{j}:=\frac{2}{(b-a)}\tau_{n_{j}}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.
Define
(c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{2k})\in\mathbb{R}^{2k+1}
by the equality

\displaystyle\big(z^{2}+\lambda_{1}^{2}\big)\cdots\big(z^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}\big)=c_{2k}z^{2k}+\dots+c_{1}z^{1}+c_{0}=:P_{c}(z)\qquad(z\in\mathbb{C}).

Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{2k}([a,b])
,

\displaystyle\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f=\int_{a}^{b}D_{c}(f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt,

where

(26)

\displaystyle g(t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{j}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2)}}&\mbox{if }n_{1}>0,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{(b-t)(t-a)}{2Q_{1}(0)(b-a)}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{j}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2)}}&\mbox{if }n_{1}=0,\end{cases}

and
Q_{j}(z):=\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{j\}}(\lambda_{\ell}^{2}-z^{2})
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.

Proof.

Due to the evenness of the characteristic polynomial
P_{c}
, we have that
c_{1}=c_{3}=\dots=c_{2k-1}=0
and
c_{2k}=1
. The roots of
P_{c}
are
\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i}
and
(-\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i})
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
with multiplicities equal to
1
except if
n_{1}=0
, then
\lambda_{1}=0
and its multiplicity is equal to
2
.

First, consider the case when
0<n_{1}
. Then all roots of the characteristic polynomial have multiplicity
1
. To apply Lemma
2.1
, observe that the polynomial connected to the root
\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i}
is given as

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{j\}}(z-\lambda_{\ell}\boldsymbol{i})\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}}(z+\lambda_{\ell}\boldsymbol{i})=(z+\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i})\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{j\}}(z^{2}+\lambda_{\ell}^{2})=(z+\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i})Q_{j}(z\boldsymbol{i}).

Similarly, the polynomial connected to the root
(-\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i})
is given as
(z-\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i})Q_{j}(z\boldsymbol{i})
. Therefore, in view of Lemma
2.1
, we can obtain the characteristic solution
\omega_{c}
of
D_{c}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bigg(\frac{\exp(\lambda_{j}t\boldsymbol{i})}{2\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}-\frac{\exp(-\lambda_{j}t\boldsymbol{i})}{2\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{i}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}\bigg)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}t)}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}.

In the case when
n_{1}=0
. Then the first root (
\lambda_{1}=0
) of the characteristic polynomial has multiplicity
2
and the other roots have multiplicity
1
. Applying Lemma
2.1
in a similar manner, we can see that the characteristic solution
\omega_{c}
of
D_{c}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(t)=\frac{t}{Q_{1}(0)}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}t)}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}.

By Theorem
3.1
,
g:[a,b]\to\mathscr{C}
is defined by formula (
14
). Therefore, for
t\in(a,b]
, we have

\displaystyle g(t)=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{c}(b-t)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{t}^{b}\omega_{c}(x-t)dx.

If
n_{1}>0
, then

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-\int_{t}^{b}\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}(x-t))}{b-a}dx\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-\frac{1-\cos(\lambda_{j}(b-t))}{\lambda_{j}(b-a)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2\cos(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-2+\lambda_{j}(b-a)\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))}{2\lambda_{j}^{2}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})(b-a)}.

Then, for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, we have that
\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2=\tau_{n_{j}}
, which is a fixed point of the tangent function, therefore,

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(b-a)=2\tan(\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2).

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-2+\lambda_{j}(b-a)\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))}{2\lambda_{j}^{2}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})(b-a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-1+\tan(\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2)\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))}{\lambda_{j}^{2}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})(b-a)}.

To simplify the numerator of this fraction, we use the identity,

\displaystyle\cos(2u)-1+\tan(v)\sin(2u)

\displaystyle=-2\sin(u)^{2}+2\tan(v)\sin(u)\cos(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sin(u)}{\cos(v)}(\cos(u)\sin(v)-\sin(u)\cos(v))=\frac{2\sin(u)\sin(v-u)}{\cos(v)}

with
u:=\lambda_{j}(b-t)/2
and
v:=\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos(\lambda_{j}(b-t))-2+\lambda_{j}(b-a)\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t))}{2\lambda_{j}^{2}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})(b-a)}=\frac{\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{j}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{j}Q_{j}(\lambda_{j})\sin(\lambda_{j}(b-a)/2)},

which proves the equality (
26
) when
n_{1}>0
.

If
n_{1}=0
, then (
26
) can be obtained analogously.
∎

In the particular case when
k=1
, the above theorem simplifies to the following result.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
and let

\lambda_{n}:=\frac{2\tau_{n}}{b-a}
. Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{2}([a,b])
,

\displaystyle\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f=\int_{a}^{b}(f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt,

where

(28)

\displaystyle g(t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{n}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{n}\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-a)/2)}}&\mbox{if }n>0,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{(b-t)(t-a)}{2(b-a)}}&\mbox{if }n=0.\end{cases}.

Our final statement is a new error estimate for the trapezoidal rule.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
and let

\lambda_{n}:=\frac{2\tau_{n}}{b-a}
. Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}^{2}([a,b])
,

(29)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f\bigg|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{12}(b-a)^{2}\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}&\mbox{if }n=0,\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{(n+1)n\pi}{2\tau_{n}^{3}}(b-a)^{2}\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f\|_{\infty}&\mbox{if }n>0,\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{1}{8}(b-a)\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}&\mbox{if }n=0,\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{1+|\cos(\tau_{n})|}{4\tau_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}(b-a)\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f\|_{1}&\mbox{if }n>0.\end{cases}

Proof.

According to Corollary
4.5
, we have that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f\bigg|=\bigg|\int_{a}^{b}(f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f)(t)\cdot g(t)dt\bigg|\leq\begin{cases}\|g\|_{1}\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f\|_{\infty},\\[5.69054pt]
\|g\|_{\infty}\cdot\|f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{n}^{2}f\|_{1},\end{cases}

where
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is given by (
28
). Therefore, to verify the first inequality in (
29
), we have to compute
\|g\|_{1}
.

If
n=0
, then
\tau_{n}=0
,
\lambda_{n}=0
and, with the substitution
t=a+s(b-a)
, we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{1}=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{(b-t)(t-a)}{2(b-a)}dt=\frac{1}{2}(b-a)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)sds=\frac{1}{12}(b-a)^{2}.

If
n>0
, then, with the substitution
t=a+s(b-a)
, we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\bigg|\frac{\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{n}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{n}\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-a)/2)}\bigg|dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\tau_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}\int_{0}^{1}\big|\sin((1-s)\tau_{n})\sin(s\tau_{n})\big|ds.

For this computation, we need to find the zeros of
s\mapsto\sin((1-s)\tau_{n})\sin(s\tau_{n})
in
[0,1]
:

\displaystyle s=\frac{k}{\tau_{n}}\pi,\qquad s=1+\frac{k}{\tau_{n}}\pi\qquad(k\in\mathbb{Z}).

The
n
th positive fixed point
\tau_{n}
of the tangent function is in
[n\pi,(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi)
. Then
\frac{k}{\tau_{n}}\pi\in[0,1]
if
k\in[0,\frac{\tau_{n}}{\pi}]
, which holds if and only if
0\leq k\leq n
.
Analogously,
1+\frac{k}{\tau_{n}}\pi\in[0,1]
if
k\in[-\frac{\tau_{n}}{\pi},0]
, which holds if and only if
-n\leq k\leq 0
. Therefore, the roots of the map
s\mapsto\sin((1-s)v)\sin(sv)
in
[0,1]
in increasing order are

\displaystyle s_{0}:=0<s_{1}:=1-\frac{n\pi}{\tau_{n}}<s_{2}:=\frac{\pi}{\tau_{n}}<s_{3}:=1-\frac{(n-1)\pi}{\tau_{n}}<\dots<s_{2n}:=\frac{n\pi}{\tau_{n}}<s_{2n+1}:=1.

We have that

\displaystyle\int\sin((1-s)\tau_{n})\sin(s\tau_{n})ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int(\cos((1-2s)\tau_{n})-\cos(\tau_{n}))ds

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\tau_{n}}\big(\sin((1-2s)\tau_{n})+2s\tau_{n}\cos(\tau_{n})\big)=:F(s).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big|\sin((1-s)v)\sin(sv)\big|ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}\int_{s_{j-1}}^{s_{j}}\big|\sin((1-s)v)\sin(sv)\big|ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}|F(s_{j})-F(s_{j-1})|=\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}(-1)^{j}(F(s_{j})-F(s_{j-1}))\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|2\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j})-F(s_{0})-2\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j+1})+F(s_{2n+1})\bigg|.

Using that

\displaystyle F(s_{0})=F(0)=-\frac{\sin(\tau_{n})}{4\tau_{n}}=-\frac{\cos(\tau_{n})}{4},\qquad F(s_{2n+1})=F(1)=\frac{\sin(\tau_{n})}{4\tau_{n}}-\frac{\cos(\tau_{n})}{2}=-\frac{\cos(\tau_{n})}{4},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}F\big(\tfrac{j\pi}{\tau_{n}}\big)=-\frac{1}{4\tau_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\Big(\sin(\tau_{n}-2j\pi)+2j\pi\cos(\tau_{n})\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n+1}{4\tau_{n}}\big(\sin(\tau_{n})+n\pi\cos(\tau_{n})\big),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}F\big(1-\tfrac{(n-j)\pi}{\tau_{n}}\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}F\big(1-\tfrac{j\pi}{\tau_{n}}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\tau_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\Big(\sin(2j\pi-\tau_{n})+2(\tau_{n}-j\pi)\cos(\tau_{n})\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n+1}{4\tau_{n}}\big(-\sin(\tau_{n})+(2\tau_{n}-n\pi)\cos(\tau_{n})\big).

Therefore,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j})-F(s_{0})

\displaystyle-2\sum_{j=0}^{n}F(s_{2j+1})+F(s_{2n+1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n+1}{\tau_{n}}\sin(\tau_{n})+\frac{n+1}{\tau_{n}}(\tau_{n}-n\pi)\cos(\tau_{n})

\displaystyle=(n+1)\cos(\tau_{n})\Big(-1+\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}(\tau_{n}-n\pi)\Big)=-(n+1)n\frac{\cos(\tau_{n})}{\tau_{n}}\pi.

Putting together the pieces, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\|g\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\tau_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}\int_{0}^{1}\big|\sin((1-s)\tau_{n})\sin(s\tau_{n})\big|ds

\displaystyle=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\tau_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}(n+1)n\frac{|\cos(\tau_{n})|}{\tau_{n}}\pi=\frac{(n+1)n\pi}{2\tau_{n}^{3}}(b-a)^{2}.

Thus, the second inequality in (
29
) is proved.

To verify the third and fourth inequalities in (
29
), we need to compute
\|g\|_{\infty}
.

If
n=0
, then
\lambda_{n}=0
and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\frac{(b-t)(t-a)}{2(b-a)}=\frac{b-a}{8}.

If
n>1
, then
\lambda_{n}>0
and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\bigg|\frac{\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{n}(t-a)/2)}{\lambda_{n}\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-a)/2)}\bigg|.

The supremum is attained at a point
t
belonging to the interior of
[a,b]
, therefore,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sin(\tau_{n})}\big(-\cos(\lambda_{n}(b-t)/2)\sin(\lambda_{n}(t-a)/2)+\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-t)/2)\cos(\lambda_{n}(t-a)/2)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\lambda_{n}(a+b-2t)/2)}{2\sin(\tau_{n})}.

It follows from here that there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle t=\sigma_{k}:=\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{k\pi}{\lambda_{n}}.

Clearly,
\sigma_{k}\in(a,b)
if and only if

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{k\pi}{\lambda_{n}}\bigg|<\frac{b-a}{2},

i.e., if and only if

\displaystyle|k|\pi<\tau_{n}<(n+\frac{1}{2})\pi,

which is equivalent to the inequality
|k|\leq n
.
For the value of
g
at
\sigma_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle g(\sigma_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-\sigma_{k})/2)\sin(\lambda_{n}(\sigma_{k}-a)/2)}{\lambda_{n}\sin(\lambda_{n}(b-a)/2)}=\frac{\sin(\frac{1}{2}\tau_{n}+k\frac{\pi}{2})\sin(\frac{1}{2}\tau_{n}-k\frac{\pi}{2})}{\lambda_{n}\sin(\tau_{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(k\pi)-\cos(\tau_{n})}{2\lambda_{n}\sin(\tau_{n})}=\frac{(-1)^{k}-\cos(\tau_{n})}{2\lambda_{n}\sin(\tau_{n})}.

This shows that the sequence
\big(|g(\sigma_{k})|\big)_{k=-n}^{n}
takes only the following two values:

\displaystyle\frac{1-\cos(\tau_{n})}{2\lambda_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|},\qquad\frac{1+\cos(\tau_{n})}{2\lambda_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}

and hence

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\infty}=\frac{1+|\cos(\tau_{n})|}{2\lambda_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}=\frac{1+|\cos(\tau_{n})|}{4\tau_{n}|\sin(\tau_{n})|}(b-a).

Thus, the proof of the theorem is completed.

5.
An extension of the Simpson formula

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
,
u=w+\boldsymbol{i}v
, where
w\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
v\in(0,\pi)
and define
\alpha_{u},\beta_{u}
by

\displaystyle\alpha_{u}:=\frac{\overline{u}\sinh(u)-u\sinh(\overline{u})}{2u\overline{u}(\cosh(u)-\cosh(\overline{u}))},\qquad\beta_{u}:=\frac{u\cosh(u)\sinh(\overline{u})-\overline{u}\cosh(\overline{u})\sinh(u)}{u\overline{u}(\cosh(u)-\cosh(\overline{u}))}.

Then, for all

,

\displaystyle\bigg|

\displaystyle\alpha_{u}f(a)+\beta_{u}f\Big(\frac{a+b}{2}\Big)+\alpha_{u}f(b)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{(b-a)^{3}\big(v\sinh(w)-w\sin(v)\big)^{2}}{32(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}wv\sinh(w)\sin(v)}\cdot\bigg\|f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+\dfrac{8(v^{2}-w^{2})}{(b-a)^{2}}f^{\prime\prime}+\dfrac{16(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}}{(b-a)^{4}}f\bigg\|_{1},\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{(b-a)^{4}(2\alpha_{u}+\beta_{u}-1)}{16(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}}\cdot\bigg\|f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+\dfrac{8(v^{2}-w^{2})}{(b-a)^{2}}f^{\prime\prime}+\dfrac{16(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}}{(b-a)^{4}}f\bigg\|_{\infty}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We have that

(30)

\displaystyle\cosh(u)

\displaystyle-\cosh(\overline{u})=\cosh(w+\boldsymbol{i}v)-\cosh(w-\boldsymbol{i}v)=2\sinh(w)\sinh(\boldsymbol{i}v)=2\boldsymbol{i}\sinh(w)\sin(v),

which is different from zero due to the assumptions
0<w
and
0<v<\pi
and hence
\alpha_{u},\beta_{u}
are well-defined. Observe that
\alpha_{u},\beta_{u}
are invariant with respect to conjugation, therefore, they are real numbers,
and one can also see that
\alpha_{u}
and
\beta_{u}
are the unique solution of the following system of equations:

(31)

\displaystyle 2\alpha_{u}\cosh(u)+\beta_{u}=\frac{\sinh(u)}{u},\qquad 2\alpha_{u}\cosh(\overline{u})+\beta_{u}=\frac{\sinh(\overline{u})}{\overline{u}}.

Therefore, each element
z
of the set
\{u,-u,\overline{u},-\overline{u}\}
is a root of the function
\varphi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{u}(z):=2\alpha_{u}\cosh(z)+\beta_{u}-\frac{\sinh(z)}{z}.

Now, we construct the measure
\mu_{u}
on the Borel subsets of
[a,b]
by

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(A):=\alpha_{u}\delta_{a}(A)+\beta_{u}\delta_{\frac{a+b}{2}}(A)+\alpha_{u}\delta_{b}(A)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{A}1\qquad(A\subseteq[a,b]),

where
\delta_{t}
denotes the Dirac measure concentrated at
t\in[a,b]
, and we consider the linear function
\mathscr{A}_{\mu_{u}}:\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{K}}\to\mathbb{K}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{\mu_{u}}(f):=\int_{[a,b]}fd\mu_{u}=\alpha_{u}f(a)+\beta_{u}f\Big(\frac{a+b}{2}\Big)+\alpha_{u}f(b)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f.

It is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle|\mathscr{A}_{\mu_{u}}(f)|\leq(2|\alpha_{u}|+|\beta_{u}|+1)\|f\|_{\infty},

and the constant
2|\alpha_{u}|+|\beta_{u}|+1
is the sharpest possible one. Furthermore, if
p\in[1,\infty)
and
2|\alpha_{u}|+|\beta_{u}|>0
, then
\mathscr{A}_{\mu_{u}}
is not bounded with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.

The spectral function of
\mathscr{A}_{\mu_{u}}
is now of the form

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{\mu_{u}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{u}e^{\lambda a}+\beta_{u}e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}+\alpha_{u}e^{\lambda b}-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}e^{\lambda x}dx

\displaystyle=\alpha_{u}e^{\lambda a}+\beta_{u}e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}+\alpha_{u}e^{\lambda b}-\frac{e^{\lambda b}-e^{\lambda a}}{\lambda(b-a)}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\frac{a+b}{2}}\bigg(2\alpha_{u}\cosh\Big(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}\Big)+\beta_{u}-\frac{2}{\lambda(b-a)}\sinh\Big(\lambda\frac{b-a}{2}\Big)\bigg).

Therefore, the equation

holds if and only if
\varphi_{u}\big(\frac{1}{2}\lambda(b-a)\big)=0
is valid. Consequently, each element of the set
\{\lambda_{u},-\lambda_{u},\overline{\lambda_{u}},-\overline{\lambda_{u}}\}
, where

, is a root of the spectral function

. The polynomial whose roots are these numbers is equal to

\displaystyle P_{u}(z)=\big(z^{2}-\lambda_{u}^{2}\big)\big(z^{2}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}\big)=z^{4}-(\lambda_{u}^{2}+\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2})z^{2}+\lambda_{u}^{2}\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}.

The differential operator whose characteristic polynomial is

is given by

\displaystyle D_{u}(f)=f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-(\lambda_{u}^{2}+\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2})f^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{u}^{2}\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}f.

The characteristic solution of the differential equation

(32)

\displaystyle\omega_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh(\lambda_{u}t)}{\lambda_{u}(\lambda_{u}^{2}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2})}+\frac{\sinh(\overline{\lambda_{u}}t)}{\overline{\lambda_{u}}(\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}-\lambda_{u}^{2})}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{u}^{2k}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2k}}{\lambda_{u}^{2}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.

Finally, we compute

\displaystyle g_{u}(t)

\alpha_{u}\omega_{u}(b-t)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{0}^{b-t}\omega_{u}(x)dx&\mbox{if }\frac{a+b}{2}<t\leq b,\end{cases}

and, if
a\leq t\leq\frac{a+b}{2}
, then, according to (
32

\displaystyle g_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=\beta_{u}\bigg(\frac{\sinh(\lambda_{u}\big(\frac{a+b}{2}-t\big))}{\lambda_{u}(\lambda_{u}^{2}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2})}+\frac{\sinh(\overline{\lambda_{u}}\big(\frac{a+b}{2}-t\big))}{\overline{\lambda_{u}}(\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}-\lambda_{u}^{2})}\bigg)+\alpha_{u}\bigg(\frac{\sinh(\lambda_{u}(b-t))}{\lambda_{u}(\lambda_{u}^{2}-\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2})}+\frac{\sinh(\overline{\lambda_{u}}(b-t))}{\overline{\lambda_{u}}(\overline{\lambda_{u}}^{2}-\lambda_{u}^{2})}\bigg)

If

In what follows, we shall prove the following properties of

(1)

\displaystyle\beta_{u}\bigg(\frac{\sinh\big(\lambda_{u}\big(t-\frac{a+b}{2}\big)\big)}{\lambda_{u}}-\frac{\sinh\big(\overline{\lambda_{u}}\big(t-\frac{a+b}{2}\big)\big)}{\overline{\lambda_{u}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\beta_{u}\bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh\big(\lambda_{u}\big(t-\frac{a+b}{2}\big)\big)}{\lambda_{u}}-\frac{\sinh\big(\overline{\lambda_{u}}\big(t-\frac{a+b}{2}\big)\big)}{\overline{\lambda_{u}}}\bigg)

With the substitution

\displaystyle h_{u}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle v\sinh(w)\sinh(sw)\sin((1-s)v)>w\sin(v)\sin(sv)\sinh((1-s)w).

\displaystyle v(\cot(sv)-\cot(v))>w(\coth(sw)-\coth(w)).

,

\displaystyle v(\cot(sv)-\cot(v))>\frac{1-s}{s}>w(\coth(sw)-\coth(w)).

follows.

Then, with

In order to determine

, we need the following computation.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}h_{u}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(-(\overline{u}\sinh(u)-u\sinh(\overline{u}))(\overline{u}\sinh(us)-u\sinh(\overline{u}s))

\displaystyle+(\cosh(\overline{u})-\cosh(u))(u^{2}\cosh(\overline{u}s)-\overline{u}^{2}\cosh(us)+\overline{u}^{2}-u^{2})\bigg)ds

\displaystyle+(\cosh(\overline{u})-\cosh(u))\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{\overline{u}}\sinh(\overline{u}s)-\frac{\overline{u}}{u}^{2}\sinh(us)+(\overline{u}^{2}-u^{2})s\Big)\bigg]_{s=0}^{s=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(u^{2}-\bar{u}^{2}\right)(u\sinh(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\sinh(u)+\bar{u}(\sinh(u)-u)\cosh(\bar{u})+u\cosh(u)(\bar{u}-\sinh(\bar{u})))}{u\bar{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8vw\big((w\sin(v)+v\sinh(w))(\cosh(w)-\cos(v))-(v^{2}+w^{2})\sinh(w)\sin(v)\big)}{v^{2}+w^{2}}.

We have

\displaystyle(v^{2}+w^{2})(2\alpha_{u}+\beta_{u})\sinh(w)\sin(v)=(v\sinh(w)+w\sin(v))(\cosh(w)-\cos(v)),

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}h_{u}(s)ds

\displaystyle=8vw(2\alpha_{u}+\beta_{u}-1)\sinh(w)\sin(v)

and hence

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}g_{u}(x)dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{\frac{a+b}{2}}^{b}g_{u}(x)dx=(b-a)\int_{0}^{1}g_{u}\Big(s\frac{a+b}{2}+(1-s)b\Big)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{(b-a)^{4}}{128(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}wv\sinh(w)\sin(v)}\int_{0}^{1}h_{u}(s)ds=\frac{(b-a)^{4}(2\alpha_{u}+\beta_{u}-1)}{16(w^{2}+v^{2})^{2}}.

Using now Corollary

with
