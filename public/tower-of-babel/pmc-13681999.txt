High Prevalence and Limited Perinatal Prediction of Kidney Dysfunction in Adolescents Born Extremely Preterm
Authors: Keia R. Sanderson, Jesus E. Vazquez, Jennifer E. Flythe, David Askenazi, Michael R. Kosorok, Minxin Lu, Kartikeya Makker, Matthew Laughon, Christine E. Kistler, Rebecca C. Fry, Thomas Michael O’Shea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExtremely preterm birth (< 28 weeks’ gestation) increases chronic kidney disease (CKD) risk. However, among those born extremely preterm, the prevalence of abnormal kidney health during adolescence and its perinatal predictors remain poorly defined.MethodsAdolescents from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborn -Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ELGAN-ECHO) cohort (14 US hospitals) were assessed for abnormal kidney health defined as ≥ 1 indicator—chronic kidney disease in children (CKiD) U25 estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, blood pressure (BP) ≥ 120/80 mm Hg, or microalbuminuria > 30 mg/g.Five feature selection methods screened perinatal covariate predictors using a 60 to 40 training-test split. Multiple imputation addressed missing data in sensitivity analyses. Area under the curve (AUC) was the primary performance metric.ResultsOf the 675 adolescents (mean age 18 years), 354 (52%) had a classifiable kidney health status, as follows: 188 with all 3 measurements and 166 with partial measurements showing ≥ 1 abnormality. The prevalence of abnormal kidney health was 66% among complete cases (125/188) and 82% among all determinate cases (291/354). In the full cohort, prevalence ranged from 43% to 91% (bounding estimates accounting for missing data). Individual measurements revealed 30.5% with reduced eGFR, 42% with elevated BP, and 9% with microalbuminuria. Across all methods, perinatal covariates achieved random test-set discrimination (AUC: 0.48–0.55) for abnormal kidney health prediction, compared with training set AUCs of 0.66–0.72.ConclusionAbnormal kidney health is highly prevalent after extremely preterm birth, underscoring the importance of protocolized longitudinal kidney health surveillance throughout childhood to enable early identification of CKD and elevated BP in children born extremely preterm. The perinatal factors we examined did not meaningfully predict kidney health abnormalities and variable importance did not translate to individual-level prediction in this cohort.

In the US, 1 in 10 births are premature (before 37 weeks of gestation).1 Although life expectancy of individuals born preterm is only slightly reduced compared with term-born peers, preterm birth is associated with a substantially elevated lifetime risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with hazard ratios 2-fold higher than term born individuals.2,3

Identifying individuals born preterm at the highest risk for abnormal kidney health will support proportional intensity of surveillance, with more intensive monitoring for those at greatest risk. Such stratification could facilitate early CKD detection and interventions to slow CKD progression.4,5 Large-scale population-based studies from Europe and Japan have documented kidney disease risk across the lifespan after preterm birth; however, few studies focus specifically on kidney health during adolescence, a critical period of rapid physiologic change and potential CKD progression.6

Previous research from our group shows that 50% of adolescents born extremely preterm have at least 1 biomarker of abnormal kidney health, including microalbuminuria, reduced renal mass, and/or elevated blood pressure (BP).7 Potential mechanisms include disrupted nephrogenesis associated with preterm birth and contributions from neonatal acute kidney injury (AKI), maternal smoking, preeclampsia, maternal low socioeconomic status, and nephrotoxic neonatal antibiotic exposures.2,8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Despite the higher prevalence of CKD after preterm birth and individually recognized risk factors, whether combinations of perinatal risk factors can better predict abnormal kidney health in adolescence remains understudied.17 Consequently, no CKD prediction models currently exist for identifying at-risk individuals born preterm from neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) discharge to late adolescence.

The primary objective of this study was to characterize the prevalence of abnormal kidney health in a large, multicenter cohort of adolescents born extremely preterm. The secondary objective was to determine whether perinatal risk factors, modeled with complementary machine learning approaches that accommodate high-dimensional data are sufficient to identify which individuals develop abnormal kidney health.

Methods

This cohort study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (IRB# 16-2535).

ELGAN-ECHO Study Participants

Study participants were a subset of the ELGAN-ECHO cohort of infants born < 28 weeks’ gestation between 2002 and 2004 at 14 US centers. Cohort details and recruitment procedures appear in previous publications.18, 19, 20 Research assistants collected perinatal covariates from medical records during NICU hospitalization. Of the 1198 survivors in the ELGAN study cohort, 675 (56%) completed at least 1 adolescent kidney health assessment (Figure 1). Perinatal and Adolescent Assessments

A total of 473 perinatal covariates were evaluated, including maternal sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, detailed neonatal data, and placental pathology. Maternal BMI was categorized per World Health Organization definitions. Maternal covariates (age, race/ethnicity, education, Medicaid status, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension) were obtained from interview or chart review; neonatal covariates (birthweight, gestational age, biological sex assigned at birth, and clinical course) were abstracted from medical records.

Kidney health was assessed once between 15 and 19 years of age and included the following 3 indicators: serum creatinine CKiD Under 25 estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), BP (median of 1–3 seated oscillometric readings), and microalbuminuria (random urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio).5 Standardized Isotope Dilution Mass Spectrometry-traceable enzymatic assays measured serum creatinine, and eGFR was calculated using the CKiD U25 equation.21,22 Since the mean age at BP assessment was 18 years, the ≥ 120/80 mm Hg threshold was used to classify elevated BP according to American Academy of Pediatrics clinical practice guideline and the 2025 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guideline.23,24

Kidney Health Indicator Composite Outcome Definition

We defined the primary outcome as the presence of ≥ 1 indicator of abnormal kidney health, as follows: serum creatinine eGFR < 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, elevated BP ≥ 120 mm Hg (systolic) or ≥ 80 mm Hg (diastolic), and/or microalbuminuria >30 mg albumin/g creatinine.5,21, 22, 23, 24

Outcome Classification and Handling of Missing Data

Of the 675 participants, 354 (52%) had a determinate kidney health status; 188 with all 3 measurements and 166 with partial measurements showed ≥ 1 abnormality. We classified the remaining 321 (48%) as indeterminate, meaning that these participants had partial kidney health measurements and all other measured indicators were normal, precluding complete outcome classification.

The prevalence of abnormal kidney health was estimated by the following 3 ways: (1) determinate-case estimation using classifiable participants only, (2) bounding estimation, using the full cohort, classifying all indeterminate participants as normal (lower bound) or abnormal (upper bound), and (3) multiple imputation estimation of the full cohort in a sensitivity analysis under missing-at-random assumption.

Statistical Analysis

Covariate Selection

We described baseline characteristics as counts and proportions for categorical covariates and as means with SD or medians with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous covariates. We retained 138 of 473 available perinatal covariates, after excluding those with substantial collinearity (r > 0.7) or more than 20% missingness, ensuring retained covariates reflected clinically observed measures and improved signal-to-noise ratio.

Sample Splitting and Missing Data

The dataset was randomly split into training (60%) and test (40%) sets with stratification by outcome. We addressed missing predictor values with multiple imputation by chained equations (100 imputations) performed separately within training and test sets to prevent information leakage across sets. Within each imputed dataset, the chained-equations procedure conditions variables on each other, consistent with standard practice.

Feature Selection and Model Assessment

We used a 2-stage analytical approach. First, 5 feature-selection methods screened 138 candidate covariates, then backward-selection models assessed final discrimination.

The 5 methods were as follows: (i) full backward model selection (P< 0.05), (ii) individual backward model selection (≥ 50% selection across 100 imputed data sets), (iii) Lasso regression (10 most frequently selected via 300 bootstrap resamples, 5-fold cross-validation and 100 equally spaced λ values (0.0001–0.5), (iv) Random Forest (Gini-ranked), and (v) XGBoost (Gain-ranked).25, 26, 27 We fit ensemble methods separately to each imputed data set and aggregated importance scores across imputations.28,29 Full hyperparameter specifications appear in the Supplementary Table S1.

Final Model Assessment

We entered each feature-selection output into backward-selection models to determine final covariates and assess discrimination. The principal assessment used observed complete-case test-set outcomes (no imputation); imputed outcomes were evaluated in a sensitivity analysis only to test for inflation. This 2-stage design allows feature screening methods to identify potentially informative variables while using backward-selection models for the final discrimination. AUC served as the primary performance metric, categorized as random (<0.60), poor (0.60–0.69), fair (0.70–0.79), good (0.80–0.89), or excellent (≥0.90).30

Statistical Software and Reporting

Descriptive statistics and tabulations were performed in SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Manuscript reporting followed the STROBE statement (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter cohort of US adolescents born extremely preterm, abnormal kidney health was common, with a prevalence of 66% among those with complete assessments (125/188 participants) and 82% among all classifiable cases (291/354 participants). In the full cohort, prevalence estimates ranged from 43% (lower bound, assuming all 321 indeterminate cases were normal) and 91% (upper bound, assuming all were abnormal), with 70% under missing-at-random assumptions. Perinatal factors, examined with 5 feature-selection methods to interrogate 138 perinatal covariates, did not identify which individuals had abnormal kidney health. Test-set discrimination for the composite outcome was random, providing no clinically meaningful individual-level predictive performance.

These findings demonstrate that perinatal factors captured in this dataset were insufficient to achieve clinical discrimination for individual kidney risk stratification in later childhood. This does not preclude the existence of other meaningful perinatal predictors; rather, it suggests that identifying at-risk infants at NICU discharge may require additional variables not captured in this cohort, alongside longitudinal approaches that incorporate childhood exposures.

The prevalence of abnormal kidney health in the present sample (66% among complete cases) substantially exceeds estimates from population-based studies of preterm birth, though few large-scale cohort studies report the prevalence of kidney abnormalities during adolescence in extremely preterm survivors. Crump and colleagues reported clinical CKD in only 0.19% of extremely preterm children ages 10 to 19 (incidence 24.13 per 100,000 person-years).2 Gjerde et al.31,32 found that small for gestational age and low birth weight carried a cumulative incidence of end-stage kidney disease of 0.014%, by age 20 years. Hirano et al.33 reported a CKD prevalence of 0.006% (5.97 per 100,000) among children ages 3 months to 15 years who were born low birth weight. The higher prevalence in our study reflects several methodological distinctions, as follows: (i) our use of a broader outcome definition, encompassing elevated BP and microalbuminuria in addition to reduced eGFR, (ii) restriction of our sample to extremely preterm births (< 28 weeks), the highest-risk preterm subset; and (iii) our use of standardized laboratory assessments and oscillometric BP measurements to identify subclinical kidney dysfunction, rather than relying solely on clinical diagnoses. Both perspectives are clinically relevant. Registry data establish that preterm birth increases lifetime CKD risk, whereas our findings emphasize the high frequency of subclinical kidney abnormalities as early as adolescence among individuals born extremely preterm.

Notably, only 52% of this high-risk cohort had a classifiable kidney health status (354 of 675); 48% had indeterminate status because of missing measurements. The 354 determinate cases may differ systematically from the 321 indeterminate participants, introducing potential selection bias. The bounding estimate (43%–91%) is valid under any missingness mechanism, including missing not at random, and does not assume homogeneity between groups. To address this potential bias, we used the following 3 complementary prevalence approaches with different assumptions: complete-case (66%), bounding estimates (43%–91%), and imputation (70%). This range allows evaluation of the prevalence across plausible assumptions regarding missing data. Regardless of the inability of perinatal factors to predict individual risk, the high prevalence of abnormal kidney health in adolescents born extremely preterm serves as a call to action for protocolized longitudinal surveillance extending well beyond current consensus recommendations (at the age of 2 years) to enable early CKD detection and intervention.34 It also highlights an opportunity to raise community awareness of the importance of kidney health testing in high-risk children in the same way that efforts have been made to encourage kidney health testing in adults.

This study represents the first application of 5 analytically distinct methods to screen 138 perinatal covariates for CKD prediction among children born extremely prematurely. The same 5 perinatal factors were consistently identified—maternal prepregnancy obesity, multiple gestation, lower gestational age at birth, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and neonatal hypercarbia. However, discrimination collapsed from training (AUC: 0.66–0.72) to test-set performance (AUC: 0.52–0.53), indicating that, although these perinatal variables may be indepdently associated with kidney health abnormalities, they were insufficient for predicting individual kidney health status in the validation sample. Complete-case test-set results (observed outcomes, no imputation) were the principal assessment; multiple-imputation results were comparable (AUC: 0.52–0.53), confirming that imputation did not artificially inflate discrimination. In secondary analyses of individual kidney health indicators, training-set AUCs exceeded 0.70 for elevated BP and microalbuminuria; however, test-set discrimination remained poor for all indicators, with only Random Forest achieving AUC > 0.60 for elevated BP.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, cross-sectional assessment of kidney health at a single time point prevents evaluation of disease trajectory or determination of when abnormalities emerge. In addition, glomerular hyperfiltration may have masked early kidney disease in some adolescents.35,36 Second, single-visit BP measurement using oscillometric readings may not capture usual BP, introducing misclassification from white-coat or masked hypertension. Third, random microalbuminuria samples, rather than first morning urine samples may overestimate the prevalence because of orthostatic proteinuria, particularly in adolescents. Fourth, 48% of participants had indeterminate kidney health data. This introduces potential selection bias, as those with complete data may differ systematically from those with missing indicators. Fifth, the cohort is restricted to extremely preterm births (< 28 weeks), limiting generalizability to the broader preterm population. Sixth, we evaluated only exposures during NICU hospitalization (averaging 13 postnatal weeks), yet the gap between discharge and adolescent assessment spans 15 to 19 years during which unmeasured “second hits” likely occur. Post-NICU exposures including childhood obesity, nephrotoxic medications, dietary sodium intake, and social determinants of health may exert effects equal to or greater than perinatal factors, introducing variance that birth hospitalization data alone cannot capture. Seventh, congenital kidney anomalies and acute kidney injury were not rigorously assessed, so we cannot be certain that the study sample was free of such abnormalities.

Conclusion

The high prevalence of abnormal kidney health in this cohort of extremely preterm adolescents underscores the need for protocolized longitudinal kidney health surveillance throughout childhood to enable early identification of CKD. The perinatal factors we analyzed did not identify which adolescents were affected, suggesting that kidney health risk stratification at NICU discharge will require additional data sources. Future research should identify additional data sources and childhood factors that improve kidney health risk prediction after preterm birth and develop protocolized surveillance pathways to enable early identification and intervention in children born extremely preterm.
