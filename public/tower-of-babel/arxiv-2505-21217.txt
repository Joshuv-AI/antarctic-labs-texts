Frostman and Fourier characterisations of fractal dimensions
Kenneth J. Falconer, Shuqin Zhang

Abstract
We examine Frostman-type characterisations and other extremal measure criteria for a range of fractal dimensions of sets. In particular we derive properties of the less familiar modified lower box dimension and upper correlation dimension. We also express a number of fractal dimensions in terms of Fourier properties of measures.



1
Introduction

1.1
Overview

Starting with the introduction of Hausdorff measure and dimension in 1919, many ways of assigning a dimension to fractal sets have been introduced. Almost all of these fractal dimensions are intimately related to measures, in particular the dimension of a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be characterised as the extreme value of some expression taken over probability measures supported by
E
.

The prototype for this goes back to Frostman’s work in the 1930s
[
10
]
. He showed that there exists a Borel probability measure
\mu
supported by a closed (or more generally Borel) set
E
such that

\mu(B(x,r))\leq cr^{s}\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ and }r>0

(1.1)

for some constant
c>0
if and only if

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)>0
, where
\mathcal{H}^{s}
is
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
In particular this leads easily to a characterisation of Hausdorff dimension
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}
:

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)}\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r},

(1.2)

where
{\mathcal{P}}(E)
denotes the set of Borel probability measures supported by
E
.

The Frostman inequality (
1.1
) is closely related to an integral form:

\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq cr^{s}\text{ for all }r>0.

(1.3)

(Note that the integral here equals
(\mu\times\mu)\{(x,y)\in E\times E:|x-y|\leq r\}
.)
Clearly (
1.1
) immediately implies (
1.3
) for all
r
, but also it is easily shown that (
1.3
) implies a Frostman inequality for a restriction of the measure
\mu
.

There are other well-known characterisations of Hausdorff dimension by integral forms, see Section
2.1.1
, as well as Fourier transformed versions of some of these criteria in Section
4
.

A benefit of having a range of measure characterisations of the dimension of a set is that different forms can be appropriate for different applications. In this paper we present measure representations for a number of fractal dimensions, some are known and are included for context and overview. Indeed, Cutler
[
1
]
gives a thorough treatment for Hausdorff dimension
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}
and packing dimension
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}
. Here we are specially interested in the rather less studied modified lower box dimension
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
and upper correlation dimension
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}
. In particular we obtain a Frostman-type expression for
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
and we show that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
,
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}
and
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}
are essentially different, providing a counter-example to a conjecture of Fraser. In the final section we establish an equivalence that enables many of the criteria to be expressed in terms of Fourier transforms.

1.2
Notation and conventions

Throughout this paper we will work with subsets of
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, though much extends to more general metric spaces. We write
B(x,r)
for the closed ball of centre
x
and radius
r
. We will assume that all sets and measures mentioned are Borel though this will not always be stated explicitly. For a set
E
an overbar
\overline{E}
denotes the closure,
{\rm int}E
the interior, and
\partial E
the boundary. We write
f\asymp g
to mean that there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

c_{1}f(r)\leq g(r)\leq c_{2}f(r)
over an appropriate range of
r
.

We define a dyadic cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with side
2^{-n}(n\geq 0)
as the
d
-fold product of half-open intervals
(j_{1}2^{-n},(j_{1}+1)2^{-n}]\times\cdots\times(j_{d}2^{-n},(j_{d}+1)2^{-n}]
, where
j_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
k=1,\cdots,d
.
We will frequently refer to a dyadic cube of side
2^{-n}
as an
n
th-level cube and denote such a cube by
C_{n}
. For every
n\geq 0
, each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is contained in exactly one
n
th cube, which we denote by
C_{n}(x)
. In much of what follows, results involving expressions such as
{\log\mu(B(x,r))}/{\log r}
are equally valid where this is replaced by the dyadic form
{\log\mu(C_{n})}/{-n\log 2}
, using that balls are contained in cubes of comparable radii and vice-versa. However, working with dyadic versions is often easier because of the ‘nested’ property of dyadic cubes, that any two cubes are either disjoint or one contains the other.

2
Frostman-type conditions for dimensions

In this section we consider how various definitions of fractal dimensions of a set
E
may be characterised as maxima of expressions involving measures supported by
E
.

2.1
Correlation dimensions

We first consider correlation dimensions of measures before transferring the definition to sets.
Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
lower correlation dimension
of
\mu
is defined by

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu=\liminf_{r\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\log\int\mu(B(x,r))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{\log r},

(2.1)

and similarly the
upper correlation dimension
of
\mu
is given by

\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\log\int\mu(B(x,r))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{\log r}.

(2.2)

If the limit exists, that is
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu=\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu
, we term the common value the
correlation dimension
of
\mu
; this is the case for many common measures including self-similar and self-conformal measures. Note that correlation dimension has many alternative names across the literature including information dimension,
L^{2}
-dimension and energy dimension.
Correlation dimensions of measures have been intensively studied, not least as a special case of
L^{q}
-dimensions in multifractal analysis and also in information theory. The paper by Mattila, Moran and Rey
[
15
]
presents a number of properties of correlation dimensions of measures that show they are not particularly well-behaved. Projection theorems for lower correlation dimensions of measures are given in
[
6
]
and
[
11
]
and for upper correlation dimensions in
[
6
]
, along with results for the more general
L^{q}
-dimensions. See also
[
1
,
5
]
.

The correlation dimensions of sets are now defined in terms of those of measures in the natural way.
For a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
lower correlation dimension
is given by

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu,

(2.3)

and the
upper correlation dimension
by

\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu,

(2.4)

and again we refer to the
correlation dimension
of
E
if these are equal.

2.1.1
Lower correlation dimension – Hausdorff dimension

The lower correlation dimension of a set equals its Hausdorff dimension.
For the definition of Hausdorff dimension first recall that a
\delta
-
cover
of
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite or countable collection of sets
\{U_{i}\}_{i}
, each with diameter at most
\delta
, that covers
E
. That is,
E\subset\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
and
0<|U_{i}|\leq\delta
, where
|\ |
denotes diameter. For each
s\geq 0
and
\delta>0
, let

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(E)=\inf\Big\{\sum_{i}|U_{i}|^{s}:\{U_{i}\}_{i}\text{ is a }\delta\text{-cover of }E\Big\}.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(E)
increases as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, the limit

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(E)

exists, possibly infinite. We term
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of
E
. Then
\mathcal{H}^{s}
is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
Hausdorff dimension
of
E
is

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0\right\}=\sup\left\{s:\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\right\}.

We have already noted Frostman’s characterisation of Hausdorff dimension in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). To see that Hausdorff dimension equals lower correlation dimension,
first note that if
s
is such that (
1.1
) is satisfied then integrating gives
\int\mu(B(x,r))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq cr^{s}
, from which if follows that
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E\leq\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E
. On the other hand,
if there is a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
such that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu>s
then there exists
c>0
such that
\int\mu(B(x,r))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq cr^{s}
for
0<r\leq 1
, so for all
\epsilon>0

\int\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k(\epsilon-s)}\mu\big(B(x,2^{-k})\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq c\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k\epsilon}<\infty.

(2.5)

It follows that there is a set
E^{\prime}\subset E
with
\mu(E^{\prime})>0
such that
\mu(B(x,2^{-k}))\leq c^{\prime}2^{-k(s-\epsilon)}
for all
x\in E^{\prime}
.

This inequality extends from dyadic to continuous values of
r
to give a Frostman inequality, so (
1.2
) and (
2.3
) imply that
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E\geq s-\epsilon
and

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E=\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E.

(2.6)

Closely related is the energy characterisation of Hausdorff dimension by which the bounds at all scales can be combined into a single expression.
The
s
-energy
of a measure
\mu
is given by

\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mu)=\iint\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}

(2.7)

Then

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E=\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=\sup\Big\{s\geq 0:\exists\ \mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mu)<\infty\Big\},

see, for example,
[
2
,
13
]
.
In fact, if
\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mu)<\infty
for some
s>0
, it follows from

\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mu)\geq\iint_{|x-y|\leq r}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{s}}\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\geq\frac{\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}{r^{s}}

that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\geq s
. On the other hand, if
t
is such that (
1.1
) is satisfied, then for any
0<s<t
,

\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mu)=\int\int_{0}^{\infty}sr^{-s-1}\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq cs\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{t-s-1}\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

2.1.2
Upper correlation dimension

The upper correlation dimensions of measures (
2.2
) and sets (
2.4
) are less studied than their lower counterparts. There are various useful equivalent definitions of upper correlation dimension.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. Then

\displaystyle\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{s\geq 0:\forall\ \mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E),\text{ for all sufficiently small }r,\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\geq r^{s}\Big\}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{s\geq 0:\exists\ \mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)\text{ and $\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0$ s.t. }\int\mu(B(x,r_{k}))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq r_{k}^{s}\Big\}

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{s\geq 0:\exists\ \mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)\text{ and $\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0$ s.t. }\mu(B(x,r_{k}))\leq r_{k}^{s}\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\Big\}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{s\geq 0:\exists\ \mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)\text{ and $\{n_{k}\}_{k}\nearrow\infty$ s.t. }\mu(C_{n_{k}})\leq 2^{-n_{k}s}\text{ for all }C_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{k}}\Big\}.

(2.11)

Proof.

Identity (
2.8
) follows directly from the definitions (
2.2
) and (
2.4
). The equivalence between (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), as well as that between (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), is clear, and (
2.10
) clearly implies (
2.9
). To see that (
2.9
) implies (
2.10
), assume without loss of generality that
r_{k}
decreases at least geometrically,
and integrate the sum of terms
r_{k}^{\epsilon-s}\mu(B(x,r_{k}))
as was done for (
2.5
).
∎

We remark that, according to (
2.10
), it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle{\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}}E=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)}\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}.

Moreover, it is evident that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}\mu\geq\dim_{\rm F}\mu
, where the
Frostman dimension
of
\mu
is defined as

\displaystyle\dim_{F}\mu=\sup

\displaystyle\big\{s\geq 0:\text{{\rm there exists a constant $c>0$ such that }}\mu(B(x,r))\leq cr^{s}\text{\rm\ for all}

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0<r<1
}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}.

(2.12)

There is no general relationship between the upper correlation dimension
\displaystyle{\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}}\mu
of
\mu
and the lower Minkowski dimension
\underline{\dim}_{\rm M}\mu
of
\mu
defined in
[
5
]
as

\underline{\dim}_{\rm M}\mu:=\liminf_{r\to 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}.

In Section
3
we consider the relationship between
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E
and other dimensions.

2.2
Box-counting dimensions

For
r>0
and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
bounded and non-empty we write
N_{r}(E)
for the least number of sets of diameter
r
that can cover
E
.
The
lower
and
upper box-counting dimensions
or
Minkowski dimensions
of
E
are given by

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E\ =\ \liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(E)}{-\log r}\ \text{ and }\ \overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E\ =\ \limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(E)}{-\log r}.

(2.13)

Note that there are various ways of defining
N_{r}
that give equivalent values, so
N_{r}(E)\asymp N^{\prime}_{r}(E)
, with the implied constants depending only on
d
, leading to the same values of the dimensions. In particular, we can take
N_{r}(E)
to be the greatest number of points in an
r
-separated subset of
E
, or alternatively the number of cubes in the lattice of half-open cubes of side length
r/\sqrt{d}
(so diameter
r
) which intersect
E
, see
[
2
]
.

In this section, we will review several ways of representing the box dimensions of a set
E
in terms of measures supported by
E
. The following simple lemma is key in relating covering numbers to integrals.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and non-empty. Then

N_{r}(E)^{-1}\asymp\inf_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)}\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\inf_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)}(\mu\times\mu)\{(x,y)\in E\times E:|x-y|\leq r\},

(2.14)

where the implied constants depend only on
d
and the particular definition of
N_{r}
in use.

Proof.

The infimum is attained by putting point masses of weight
1/N_{r}(E)
on each of the points of an
r
-separated subset of
E
containing
N_{r}(E)
points.

On the other hand, taking
N_{r}(E)
to be the number of half-open cubes
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}
in the lattice of cubes of diameter
r
which intersect
E
, for all
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)
,

1=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}\mu(B_{i})\bigg)^{2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}1^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}\mu(B_{i})^{2}\bigg)\leq N_{r}(E)\int\mu\big(B(x,r)\big)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded. Then

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\ \liminf_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big)}{\log r}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\ \limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big)}{\log r}.

(2.16)

Proof.

These identities follow immediately from (
2.13
) and (
2.14
).
∎

It is not difficult to see that
N_{r}(E)^{-1}\asymp\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\sup_{x\in E}\mu(B(x,r))
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\ \liminf_{r\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\ \limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}.

(2.18)

The drawback of (
2.15
)-(
2.18
) is that the measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
that minimise (or nearly minimise) the integrals depend on the scale
r
.
However, there is an anti-Frostman result that avoids this.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Borel. Then

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\bigg\{\liminf_{r\to 0}\sup_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}\bigg\}

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\bigg\{\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}\bigg\}.

(2.20)

Proof.

This is essentially
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
. For (
2.20
), take
N_{r}(E)
to be the largest number of disjoint balls of radii
r
with centres in
E
, say
\{B(x_{i},r)\}_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}
. If
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)
and
\mu(B(x,r))\geq r^{s}
for all
x\in E
and
0<r<1
, then

1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r}(E)}\mu(B(x,r))\geq N_{r}(E)r^{s},

giving
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E\leq s
.

Now take
N_{2^{-k}}(E)
to be the maximal number of points of
E
in an
2^{-k}
-separated set, say
\{x_{k,i}\}_{i}
, which implies that for all
x\in E
the ball
B(x,2^{-k+1})
contains at least one of the
x_{k,i}
. Define
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)
by

\mu=c\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2^{-k}}(E)}\frac{1}{N_{2^{-k}}(E)}\delta_{x_{k,i}},

where
\delta_{x_{k,i}}
is a point mass at
x_{k,i}
and
c
is a normalising constant.
It is easy to see that
\mu
is supported on
E
.
Let
t>s>\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E
.
It is easily seen that
\mu(B(x,2.2^{-k}))\geq\frac{1}{k^{2}N_{2^{-k}}(E)}\geq 2^{k(s-t)}2^{-ks}
for all
x\in E
and
k
large enough,
which gives
\mu(B(x,r))\geq r^{t}

for all sufficiently small
r
. In particular this measure attains the minimum in (
2.20
).

Identity (
2.19
) may be verified in a similar way by summing over
k
in a suitable subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
∎

It is possible to express the box-counting definitions (
2.13
) in terms of energy integrals with respect to certain kernels, see
[
3
]
. As well as giving alternative forms of (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) this turns out to be useful in studying the box dimensions of projections and other images of sets, but we do not discuss this further here.

2.3
Modified box dimensions

Whilst box dimensions have some advantages, such as often being easy to calculate or estimate, mathematically they have several shortcomings. In particular, lower box dimension is not finitely stable (that is the dimension of union of two sets may differ from the maximum dimension of the individual sets), and neither lower nor upper box dimension is stable for a countable family of sets. This can be remedied by ‘modifying’ the definitions by considering countable covers
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
of a set
E
and taking the infimum value of
\max_{i}\{\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm B}E_{i}\}
over all such covers for the modified box dimension of
E
.

For upper box dimension, this procedure leads to an alternative characterisation of packing dimension. On the other hand, modified lower box dimension has been little studied.

2.3.1
Modified upper box dimension – packing dimension

We recall the definition of packing dimension via packing measures. A
\delta
-packing
of
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite or countable family of disjoint closed balls of radii at most
\delta
with centres in
E
. For
s\geq 0
, the
s
-dimensional packing premeasure
is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{0}^{s}(E)=\inf_{\delta>0}{\mathcal{P}}_{\delta}^{s}(E),

where

\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{s}(E)=\sup\Big\{\sum_{i}|B_{i}|^{s}:\{B_{i}\}_{i}\textit{ is a $\delta$-packing of $E$}\Big\}.

The
s
-dimensional packing measure
is then

\mathcal{P}^{s}(E)=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{0}^{s}(E_{i}):E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}\bigg\}.

Then
\mathcal{P}^{s}
is a Borel measure. The
packing dimension
of
E
is defined analogously to Hausdorff dimension as

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=\inf\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{P}^{s}(E)=0\}=\sup\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{P}^{s}(E)=\infty\}.

Packing dimension can be characterised in terms of upper box dimension, that is as
modified upper box dimension

\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
:

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E:=\inf\bigg\{{\sup_{i}}\ \overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{i}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i},\text{ where the }E_{i}\text{ are compact}\bigg\},

(2.21)

where the infimum is over all countable covers of
E
.
See, for example,
[
2
,
20
]
, for details of this equivalence. By virtue of the countable unions in the definition, it is immediate that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
is countably stable. Moreover, in many applications of packing dimension, the form (
2.21
) is used rather than the measure definition.

Cutler
[
1
]
gives a detailed treatment of Frostman and anti-Frostman-type results for packing dimensions, see also
[
4
, Chapter 2]
. In particular:

Lemma 2.5
.

[
1
, Lemma 3.4]

Let
E
be a non-empty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
0\leq s<\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E
, there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there are arbitrarily small positive
r
such that
\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{s}
.

Due to the analytic approximation lemma (see
[
1
]
, Lemma 2.5), the result in Lemma
2.5
remains valid for all analytic sets.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
1
,
4
]

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set and let
\mu
be a Borel measure with

\mu(E)>0
. If for all
x\in E

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}\geq s.

then
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\geq s
.

Let

be a Borel set.
From Lemmas

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\inf_{x\in E}\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}\inf_{x\in E}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(C_{n}(x))}{-n\log 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{s\geq 0:\text{there exists }\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)\text{ and }c>0\text{ such that for each }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\text{there is }

\displaystyle=

2.4
Modified lower box dimension

Analogously to (
2.21
) we write
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}(E)
for the
modified lower box dimension
of

, defined by

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E:=\inf\bigg\{{\sup_{i}}\ \underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{i}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i},\text{ where the }E_{i}\text{ are compact}\bigg\}.

It follows directly from the definitions that

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E\leq\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\leq\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E
.

Modified lower box dimension seems to have been neglected compared to its upper counterpart and we now obtain a Frostman-type characterisation involving balls with radii of subsequences of given sequences. Parts of the proof follow the lines of
[
1

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact. Then

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{s\geq 0:\exists F\subset E\text{{\rm\ s.t. for any sequence $\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0$ there exists $\{r_{k_{i}}\}_{i}\subset\{r_{k}\}_{k}$} and}

a Borel measure
\mu
with
\spt\mu=F
s.t.
\mu(B(x,r_{k_{i}}))\leq r_{k_{i}}^{s}
for all

and

}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{F\subset E}\inf_{\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0}\sup_{\mu:\spt\mu=F}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r_{k}))}{\log r_{k}}.

Proof.

We prove the first equality from which the second is immediate.
Let
s
be such that there exists
F\subset E
such that for every sequence
\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0
there exists a subsequence
\{r_{k_{i}}\}
and a Borel measure
\mu
with
\spt\mu=F
such that for all

,

(2.23)

Let
\{E_{j}\}_{j}
be an arbitrary countable family of closed subsets of
E
satisfying
E=\cup_{j}E_{j}
.
Then
F=\cup_{j}(E_{j}\cap F)
.
Since

such that

has non-empty interior relative to
F
.
We claim that

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{j_{0}}\geq s.

(2.24)

Otherwise, there exists

such that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{j_{0}}<s-\epsilon
. By (
2.17
), there exists a decreasing sequence

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E_{j_{0}})}\inf_{x\in E_{j_{0}}}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r_{k}))}{\log r_{k}}<s-\epsilon.

(2.25)

For any Borel measure
\mu

\spt\mu=F
, the non-empty interior of

. Thus,

\widetilde{\mu}=\dfrac{\mu|_{E_{j_{0}}}}{\mu(E_{j_{0}})}\in\mathcal{P}(E_{j_{0}}).

According to (

Thus, for sufficiently large

\sup_{x\in E_{j_{0}}}{\mu}(B(x,r_{k}))>\mu(E_{j_{0}})\cdot r_{k}^{s-\epsilon}>r_{k}^{s},

which contradicts (
2.23
). Therefore, we have proved that (
2.24
) holds. Consequently,

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\geq s.

For the opposite inequality, assume that
E
is contained in the interior of the unit cube

. Let

. Define
\mathcal{D}_{n}

n

that are contained in
C_{0}
.
Set

(2.26)

Since
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}
is finitely stable, for

so

.
Define

F_{n}=\bigcup_{C_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{s}}C_{n}\quad\textit{ and }\quad F=\bigcap_{n}F_{n}.

If

, then
C_{n}(x)\cap F_{n}=\emptyset

sufficiently large (where

is the
n
th-level dyadic cube containing

. Then

.
It is clear that

2.26

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}\big(\bigcup_{m}\bigcup_{C_{m}\in\mathcal{D}_{m}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{m}^{s}}C_{n}\cap E\cap C_{m}\big)\leq s.

Then for
C_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{s}
,

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}\big(C_{n}\cap F\big)\geq\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}\big(C_{n}\cap F\big)>s,

(2.27)

a property that we use repeatedly. Let
\{r_{k}\}_{k}
be an arbitrary sequence decreasing to zero.
For each

(2.28)

We will define inductively an increasing sequence of integers

, set collections

(2.29)

To begin we choose

to be the least integer in the sequence

(2.30)

where

We index the cubes in

, probability measures

3
Relationships between dimensions

The main aim of this section is to demonstrate that modified lower box dimension, upper correlation dimension and packing dimension are all distinct, disproving a conjecture in
[
9
]
.

3.1
Inequalities

Apart from the inclusion of
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E
in the diagram below, the other relationships are well-known and there are examples that show that sets
E
exist with all possible values of these dimensions attained simultaneously, see for example,
[
16
,
19
]
. Here
\dim_{\rm A}
denotes Assouad dimension, and further relationships between Assouad type dimensions and lower dimensions may be found in
[
8
]
.

\begin{array}[]{clccll}&\rotatebox[origin={c}]{20.0}{$\leq$}&\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E&\rotatebox[origin={c}]{340.0}{$\leq$}&\\
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E\equiv\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\leq\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E&&&&\!\!\!\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E\leq\dim_{\rm A}E.\\
&\rotatebox[origin={c}]{340.0}{$\leq$}&\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\leq\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E\equiv\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E&\rotatebox[origin={c}]{20.0}{$\leq$}&\\

\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E
and

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

are not comparable, see for example
[

]
.

The following proposition allows us to include upper correlation dimension in this picture.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all Borel sets

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\leq\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\leq\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E,

Proof.

According to Theorem
2.7
, it is clear that

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\leq\inf_{\{r_{k}\}_{k}\searrow 0}\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(E)}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\inf_{x\in E}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r_{k}))}{\log r_{k}}\leq\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E.

2.10
) and (

∎

\displaystyle\geq N_{2^{-m}}(E\cap C_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\#\mathcal{C}_{m}}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n}}\begin{cases}\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k}},&\text{if }n_{2k-1}<m\leq n_{2k}\\
\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k}}\cdot 2^{m-n_{2k}},&\text{if }n_{2k}<m\leq n_{2k+1}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n}}\begin{cases}2^{n_{2k}(t-\epsilon_{k})-1},&\text{if }n_{2k-1}<m\leq n_{2k}\\
2^{n_{2k}(t-\epsilon_{k})-1+(m-n_{2k})},&\text{if }n_{2k}<m\leq n_{2k+1}\end{cases}\quad\big(\text{by }\eqref{2k}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\#\mathcal{C}_{n}}2^{m(t-\epsilon)},

where the last inequality is valid if
m
is large enough so that the corresponding
k
satisfies
\epsilon_{k}<\epsilon
.
Thus,
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{i}\geq t-\epsilon
. According to the definition of the modified lower box dimension and the arbitrariness of
\epsilon
, we conclude
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E\geq t

and thus

\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E=\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E=t.

We next verify that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}(E)=\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=s
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define a measure

\mu_{m}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{2m+1}}}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2m+1}}}\frac{\mathcal{L}|_{C}}{\mathcal{L}(C)},

where
\mathcal{L}
is Lebesgue measure.
For
C_{n_{2k+1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k+1}}
and
k\leq m
,

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(C_{n_{2k+1}})=

\displaystyle\ \#(\mathcal{C}_{n_{2m+1}}\cap C_{n_{2k+1}})\cdot\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2m+1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k+1}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2^{-n_{2k+1}s+1}\quad\big(\text{by }\eqref{2k+1}\big).

(3.6)

Let
\{\mu_{m_{j}}\}_{j}
be a weakly convergent subsequence of
\{\mu_{m}\}_{m}
, and let
\mu
be the limit of this subsequence.
It is easy to see that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
.
For each
x\in E
,
x
lies in the interior of the union of two
{n_{2k+1}}
th adjacent dyadic intervals, denoted as
U_{k}(x)
.
Thus

\displaystyle\mu(\{x\})\leq

\displaystyle\ \mu(U_{k}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \liminf_{m}\mu_{m}(U_{k}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2^{-n_{2k+1}s+2}\quad\big(\text{by }\eqref{mumc}\big).

Letting
k
tend to infinity implies that
\mu
has no atoms.
We denote the interior of
{C}_{n_{2k+1}}
by
{\rm int}\,{C}_{n_{2k+1}}
and its boundary by
\partial C_{n_{2k+1}}
. Since
\mu(\partial C_{n_{2k+1}})=0
,

\displaystyle\mu(C_{n_{2k+1}})\leq

\displaystyle\ \mu(\partial C_{n_{2k+1}})+\mu({\rm int}\,{C}_{n_{2k+1}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \liminf_{m}\mu_{m}({\rm int}\,{C}_{n_{2k+1}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2^{-n_{2k+1}s+1}\quad\big(\text{by }\eqref{mumc}\big).

By Proposition
2.1
,
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\geq s
.

If
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E>s
, then there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}(E)\geq\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E>s+\epsilon_{0}
.
Then there exists a sequence of integers
\{m_{k}\}_{k}
such that

\#\mathcal{C}_{m_{k}}\geq 2^{m_{k}(s+\epsilon_{0})}.

(3.7)

However, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}_{m}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k-1}},&\text{if }n_{2k-1}<m\leq n_{2k}\\
\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{2k+1}}\cdot 2^{m-n_{2k+1}},&\text{if }n_{2k}<m\leq n_{2k+1}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}2^{n_{2k-1}s},&\text{if }n_{2k-1}<m\leq n_{2k}\\
2^{n_{2k+1}s+m-n_{2k+1}},&\text{if }n_{2k}<m\leq n_{2k+1}\end{cases}\quad\big(\text{by }\eqref{2k+1}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{ms},

which contradicts (
3.7
).
Thus

\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=s.

∎

The next example distinguishes upper correlation dimension and packing dimension.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given
s,t
with
0<t<s<1
, there exists a compact set
E
such that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=t
and
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=s
.

Proof.

Set
N_{0}=1
, and for
k\geq 1
, define

n_{k}=\max\Big\{\Big\lfloor\frac{N_{k-1}}{1-s}\Big\rfloor,\ \Big\lceil\frac{t}{s-t}\Big\rceil\Big\}\text{ and }N_{k}=\max\Big\{\Big\lfloor\frac{sn_{k}}{t}\Big\rfloor,\ \Big\lceil\frac{1-s}{s}\Big\rceil\Big\},

so
n_{k}<N_{k}<n_{k+1}
. Then for all
k\geq 2
,

sn_{k}-(1-s)<n_{k}-N_{k-1}\leq sn_{k}

(3.8)

and

sn_{k}-t<N_{k}t\leq sn_{k}.

(3.9)

Let
C_{0}=[0,1]
.
We let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
denote the family of half-open
n
th-level dyadic intervals of length
2^{-n}
that are contained in
C_{0}
.
We select
n
th-level dyadic intervals from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
in a specific way and denote the set of chosen intervals by
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. We also
designate
certain intervals, which we denote by a tilde.
For
n=1
, we select all intervals in
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{1}=\big\{(0,\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}],(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}},1]\big\}.

Designate the interval
\widetilde{C}_{1}=\big(\mathcal{C}_{1}\big)_{L}=(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, where
\big(\mathcal{C}\big)_{L}
denotes
the left-most interval in the interval collection
\mathcal{C}
.
For
1<n\leq n_{1}
, select all
n
th intervals from
\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap\widetilde{C}_{1}
.
Simultaneously, from
\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap(\frac{1}{2},1]
, we select only the left-most interval
\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap(\frac{1}{2},1]\big)_{L}
.
Thus

\mathcal{C}_{n_{1}}=\big(\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{1}\big)\cup\big(\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}}\cap(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}},1]\big)_{L};

For
n_{1}<n\leq N_{1}
and each
C_{n_{1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{1}}
, from
\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap C_{n_{1}}
we select only the left-most interval
\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap C_{n_{1}}\big)_{L}
.
Then

\mathcal{C}_{N_{1}}=\Big\{\big(\mathcal{D}_{N_{1}}\cap{C}_{n_{1}}\big)_{L}:{C}_{n_{1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{1}}\Big\}.

Designate the interval
\widetilde{C}_{N_{1}}=\big(\mathcal{C}_{N_{1}}\big)_{L}^{\widetilde{C}_{1}}=\big(\mathcal{D}_{N_{1}}\cap(\frac{1}{2},1]\big)_{L}
,
where
\big(\mathcal{C}\big)_{L}^{C}
denotes the left-most interval in the interval collection
\mathcal{C}
to the right of the interval
C
.
For
N_{1}<n\leq n_{2}
, we select all
n
th-level intervals from
\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{1}}
, and for each
C_{N_{1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{N_{1}}\setminus\{\widetilde{C}_{N_{1}}\}
, we only select
\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{N_{1}}\big)_{L}
. Then we define

\mathcal{C}_{n_{2}}=\big(\mathcal{D}_{n_{2}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{1}}\big)\cup\Big\{\big(\mathcal{D}_{n_{2}}\cap{C}_{N_{1}}\big)_{L}:{C}_{N_{1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{N_{1}}\setminus\{\widetilde{C}_{N_{1}}\}\Big\};

For
n_{2}<n\leq N_{2}
and each
C_{n_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{2}}
, we select only
\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{n_{2}}\big)_{L}
.
Consequently,

\mathcal{C}_{N_{2}}=\Big\{\big(\mathcal{D}_{N_{2}}\cap{C}_{n_{2}}\big)_{L}:{C}_{n_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{2}}\Big\}.

For
m\geq 2
, if
\widetilde{C}_{N_{m-1}}
is the right-most interval in
\mathcal{C}_{N_{m-1}}
we define
\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}=\big(\mathcal{C}_{N_{m}}\big)_{L}
, otherwise

\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}=\big(\mathcal{C}_{N_{m}}\big)_{L}^{\widetilde{C}_{N_{m-1}}}
. For
N_{m}<n\leq n_{m+1}
, we form
\mathcal{C}_{n}
by selecting all
n
th-level intervals from
\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}
, but for each
C_{N_{m}}\in\mathcal{C}_{N_{m}}\setminus\{\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}\}
we only select
\Big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{N_{m}}\Big)_{L}
.
That is,

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\Big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}\Big)\cup\Big\{\Big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{N_{m}}\Big)_{L}:{C}_{N_{m}}\in\mathcal{C}_{N_{m}}\setminus\{\widetilde{C}_{N_{m}}\}\Big\}.

For
n_{m+1}<n\leq N_{m+1}
, we select only
\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{n_{m+1}}\big)_{L}
for each
C_{n_{m+1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{m+1}}
.
That is,

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\Big\{\big(\mathcal{D}_{n}\cap{C}_{n_{m+1}}\big)_{L}:{C}_{n_{m+1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n_{m+1}}\Big\}.

By iterating this process, we generate a sequence of families of sets
\{\mathcal{C}_{n}\}_{n}
. Define

E=\bigcap_{n}\bigcup_{C_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\overline{C_{n}}.

It is clear that
\#\mathcal{C}_{N_{m}}=\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{m}}
and
\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{m}}-\#\mathcal{C}_{N_{m-1}}=2^{{n_{m}}-N_{m-1}}-1
. It follows that for
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{k}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{k}\big(\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{m}}-\#\mathcal{C}_{N_{m-1}}\big)+\#\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{k}\big(2^{{n_{m}}-N_{m-1}}-1\big)+2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle k2^{n_{k}s}\quad\text{\big(by \eqref{nka}\big)}.

(3.10)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\mathcal{J}_{k}=\big\{j:\widetilde{C}_{N_{j}}\subset\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}\big\}.

By virtue of the construction of these sets,
\mathcal{J}_{k}
is formed of countably many disjoint segments of consecutive integers, which we can write as

\mathcal{J}_{k}=\{{j_{1}},j_{1}+1,\cdots,j_{1}+l_{1},j_{2},j_{2}+1,\cdots,j_{2}+l_{2},\cdots\},

where
j_{i}
and
j_{i}+l_{i}
are the end points of these segments. For
k\geq 2
, we see that
j_{i+1}-(j_{i}+l_{i})>2
.
If
N_{j}\leq m\leq n_{j+1}
with
j\in\mathcal{J}_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\#\big(\mathcal{C}_{m}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\#\mathcal{C}_{N_{j}}+2^{m-N_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq j2^{n_{j}s}+2^{m-N_{j}}\quad(\text{by \eqref{cnk}})

\displaystyle\leq j2^{ms}+2^{ms}\quad(\text{by \eqref{nka}})

\displaystyle\leq m2^{ms}.

(3.11)

If
n_{j+1}\leq m\leq N_{j+1}
, then

\displaystyle\#\big(\mathcal{C}_{m}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}\big)\leq\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{j+1}}\leq(j+1)2^{n_{j+1}s}\leq m2^{ms}.

(3.12)

In particular, when
N_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1}\leq m\leq N_{j_{i+1}}
,

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{C}_{m}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\leq

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}_{n_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(j_{i}+l_{i}+1)2^{n_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1}s}\quad\text{\big(by \eqref{cnk}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(j_{i}+l_{i}+1)2^{N_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1}t+1}\quad\text{\big(by \eqref{nkb}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m2^{mt+1}.

(3.13)

We call
[N_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1},N_{j_{i+1}}]
a
lower-count range
of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
.

We can now show by contradiction that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E\leq t
. For if
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E>t+\epsilon_{0}
for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
, (
2.11
) implies that there exist a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
and a sequence
\{m_{l}\}_{l}
such that for every designated interval
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
with positive measure,

\mu(\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\leq\#(\mathcal{C}_{m_{l}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\cdot 2^{-m_{l}(t+\epsilon_{0})}.

(3.14)

We note that if
m
is not in the lower-count range of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
and
m>N_{k}
, then
N_{j_{i}}<m<N_{j_{i}+l_{i}+1}
for some
j_{i}\in\mathcal{J}_{k}
.
Suppose that
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}=\emptyset
and
m>N_{k^{\prime}}
. As
\mathcal{J}_{k^{\prime}}\cap\mathcal{J}_{k}=\emptyset
, it is easy to check that
m
lies in a lower-count range of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}}
. Since
\mu
is not a Dirac measure, we can find two disjoint designated intervals
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
and
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}}
with positive measure.
If
m_{l}
lies in the lower-count range of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
for at most finitely many
l
, then there are infinitely many
l
such that
m_{l}
are in the lower-count range of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}}
.
Combining (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), there are infinitely many
m_{l}
such that

\mu(\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}})\leq m_{l}2^{m_{l}t+1}\cdot 2^{-m_{l}(t+\epsilon_{0})}=m_{l}2^{-m_{l}\epsilon_{0}+1},

contradicting that
\mu(\widetilde{C}_{N_{k^{\prime}}})>0
. If otherwise,
m_{l}
lies in the lower-count range of
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
for infinitely many
l
which also leads to a contradiction.

To show that
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=s
, first consider a partition
E=\bigcup_{i}E_{i}
with each
E_{i}
closed. Since
E
is compact, Baire’s category theorem implies that there exists an index
i
such that
E_{i}
has non-empty relative interior, that is there exists an open set
V
such that
E\cap V\subset E_{i}
.
There are infinitely many designated intervals
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
contained in
V
and by the construction of
E
, each
n_{k+1}
th-level interval in
\mathcal{C}_{n_{k+1}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
intersects
E
. Then

\displaystyle N_{2^{-n_{k+1}}}(E\cap V)\geq

\displaystyle\ \#(\mathcal{C}_{n_{k+1}}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2^{n_{k+1}-N_{k}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\ 2^{n_{k+1}s-1}

by (
3.8
). Hence,
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E_{i}\geq s
and so
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E\geq s
by (
2.21
).

By (
3.11
)-(
3.13
), for any designated interval
\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}
and
m>N_{k}
,

N_{2^{-m}}(E\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})=\#\big(\mathcal{C}_{m}\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}}\big)\leq m2^{ms},

implying that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}(E\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\leq s
.
Then

\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=\sup_{k}\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}(E\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\leq\sup_{k}\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}(E\cap\widetilde{C}_{N_{k}})\leq s,

so
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=s
.

Let
E_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be any compact set with
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E_{0}=\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E_{0}=t
. By (
3.1
)

t\leq\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm H}E_{0}\leq\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E_{0}\leq\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E_{0}\leq t
.
Taking
E
to be
E\cup E_{0}
with
E
as above in the statement of the proposition gives a set with the desired dimensions.
∎

The following proposition combines the previous two results.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given numbers
0<a<b<c<1
, there exists a compact set
E
such that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E=a
,
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E=b
and
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E=c
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
we can find a compact set
E_{1}
such that
\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm MB}E_{1}=a
and
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E_{1}=\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E_{1}=b
.
By Proposition
3.3
, there exists a compact set
E_{2}
such that
\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}E_{2}=a
and
\mbox{\rm dim}_{\rm P}E_{2}=c
. The set

has the required properties.
∎

4
Fourier characterisations of dimensions

Characterisation of a fractal dimension in terms of Fourier transforms goes back at least to Kaufman’s
[
12
]
proof of Marstrand’s projection theorem relating to Hausdorff dimension, see also Mattila’s books
[
13
,
14
]
. However, other dimensions of a set
E
may be expressed in terms of the Fourier transform of measures on
E
. We define the Fourier transform of a measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}(E)
by

\widehat{\mu}(z)=\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot z}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\quad(z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proposition
4.1
relates the behaviour of
\int\mu(B(x,r))d\mu(x)
of a measure
\mu
for small
r
with the mean square of its Fourier transform
\widehat{\mu}
over large balls. The underlying idea is that
1_{B(0,r)}(x)\approx\exp(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2r^{2}})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that the Fourier transform of the Gaussian
\exp(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2r^{2}})
is
(2\pi)^{d/2}r^{d}\exp(-\frac{|z|^{2}r^{2}}{2})
with both of these Gaussians strictly positive. The uniformity of the constants for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(B(0,\rho))
is important for applications.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
and
\rho>0
. There are constants
b_{1},b_{2}>0
and
0<r_{0}<1
, depending only on
d,\epsilon
and
\rho
, such that for all probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with support in
B(0,\rho)
and all
r\leq r_{0}
,

b_{1}r^{d(1+\epsilon)}\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\leq\int\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq b_{2}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z.

(4.1)

Proof.

We note that
1_{B(0,r^{1-\epsilon})}(x)\leq\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\exp(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2r^{2(1-\epsilon)}})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for
0<r<1
,

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,r))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\int\mu(B(x,r^{1-\epsilon}))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\int 1_{B(0,r^{1-\epsilon})}(x-y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\int\!\!\int\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}\Big)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\int\bigg(\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{2r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}\Big)*\mu\bigg)(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\hbox{\hskip 51.1643pt\hskip-51.1643pt\hbox{$\bigg(\exp\!\Big(\displaystyle{-\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{2r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}}\Big)*\mu\bigg)$}\hskip-51.1643pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{8.85pt}{\hbox{$\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern 0.1pt\mathchar 866\kern 0.1pt}}}}$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 51.1643pt}(z)\overline{\widehat{\mu}(z)}\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{(Plancheral)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int(2\pi)^{d/2}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|z|^{2}r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}{2}\Big){\widehat{\mu}(z)}\overline{\widehat{\mu}(z)}\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{(Parseval + convolution)}

\displaystyle=c_{1}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\int\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|z|^{2}r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}{2}\Big)|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\qquad

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\bigg[\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z+\int_{|z|>r^{-1}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|z|^{2}r^{2(1-\epsilon)}}{2}\Big)\mathrm{d}z\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\bigg[\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z+c_{2}\bigg]

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}r^{d(1-\epsilon)}\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z.

(4.3)

For (
4.2
) we note that the second integral above is bounded in
r
. For (
4.3
) we note that
{\widehat{\mu}(0)}=1
and

|\nabla\widehat{\mu}(z)|\leq\sqrt{d}\int_{|x|\leq\rho}|x|\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\sqrt{d}\rho
.
Then
|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|\geq\frac{1}{2}

if
|z|\leq\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{d}\rho)^{-1}=:r_{0}^{-1}
,
so

\int_{|z|\leq r^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\geq\int_{|z|\leq r_{0}^{-1}}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\geq\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}v_{d}r_{0}^{-d}

if
r\leq r_{0}
, where
v_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball.

The left hand inequality of (
4.1
) follows in a similar way, working with
\exp(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2r^{2(1+\epsilon)}})
and its Fourier transform.
∎

This proposition allows us to read off Fourier characterisations of various dimensions discussed in Section
2
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Borel set. Then

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm B}E

There are simple Fourier expressions for the correlation dimensions of measures.

Corollary 4.4

For

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }\quad\displaystyle{\overline{\mbox{\rm dim}}_{\rm C}}\mu

\displaystyle=\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\Big(R^{-d}\int_{|z|\leq R}|{\widehat{\mu}(z)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\Big)}{-\log R}.

(4.5)

Proof.

Corollary 4.5

Let
