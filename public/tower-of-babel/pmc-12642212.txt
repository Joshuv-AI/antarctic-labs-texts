Identifying sleep patterns as a synthetic signature in metabolic comorbidities using a stratified medicine approach
Authors: Shizhen Zhang, Qian Qin, Zhuoqing Wu, Lin Wang, Suying Ding, Jingfeng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbundant evidence suggests that sleep is closely associated with metabolic diseases. However, studies focusing on sleep patterns in relation to metabolic comorbidities defined as the coexistence of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperuricemia, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) remain limited. This study aimed to explore the association between sleep patterns and both metabolic comorbidities and individual metabolic diseases using novel analytical methods.MethodsA total of 4,970 participants was included. Four sleep-related factors and six metabolic diseases were examined. Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), K-means clustering, logistic regression, and subgroup analysis were applied to assess associations between sleep patterns and metabolic conditions.ResultsMCA and clustering of sleep factors identified three categories of sleep patterns: good sleep pattern (GSP), intermediate sleep pattern (ISP), and poor sleep pattern (PSP). Within the PSP group, 77.9% had a late bedtime, 83.7% reported poor or very poor sleep quality, 90.4% slept fewer than 7 h per night, and 29.1% snored. After adjusting for sex, age, and other confounders, PSP was associated with a 34.5% higher odds of metabolic comorbidities compared with GSP, with an even stronger association observed in men (51.9%). ISP was not associated with metabolic comorbidities overall, but was associated with increased odds of T2DM (19.4%) and NAFLD (23.4%). Specific sleep factors, including consistently having a late bedtime and snoring, were associated with higher odds of metabolic comorbidities by 67.7% [OR (95%CI): 1.677 (1.059–2.658)] and 46.4% [OR (95%CI): 1.464 (1.179–1.819)], respectively. Mediation analysis indicated that body mass index (BMI) explained only 31.58% of the total effect of sleep patterns on comorbidity risk in men.ConclusionSleep factors aggregated into three prevalent sleep patterns, with strong internal correlations observed within each pattern. PSP was associated with increased odds of metabolic comorbidities, with this association being pronounced in all participants and men. Notably, BMI served as a partial mediator of this relationship in the male subgroup. ISP showed significant associations with T2DM and NAFLD, highlighting their clinical relevance. These findings emphasize the role of sleep in metabolic health and support the need for longitudinal sleep interventions and strategies to improve sleep conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25351-y.Keywords: Sleep patterns, Multiple correspondence analysis, Clustering algorithm, Stratified medicine, Metabolic comorbidities

Background

With population aging and lifestyle transitions, the global prevalence of metabolic diseases has increased markedly [1]. Metabolic diseases are characterized by insulin resistance, impaired glycolipid metabolism, and obesity-associated pro-inflammatory reactions. These include obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [2]. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults has gradually risen to 26.4% [3]. This increase has been accompanied by steadily rising rates of diabetes [4], hypertension [5], and NAFLD [6, 7]. The Lancet Commission has highlighted the importance of the “Global Syndemic” framework, which views metabolic diseases as synergistic epidemics with shared pathogenic pathways. Together, these conditions contribute to cardiovascular disease, stroke, disability, and premature death [8]. Data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) show that the prevalence of multimorbidity among the elderly population is 65.6% [9]. Positioning metabolic comorbidities within a syndemic framework is therefore critical, as it enables a foucus on upstream interventions aimed at improving the overall metabolic environment of individuals. Global estimates also suggest that obesity is a major contributor to metabolic diseases burden [2, 10].

Recent studies further indicate that poor sleep health is associated with increased all-cause mortality and higher incidence of metabolic diseases [11, 12]. However, most existing research has examined the effects of single sleep behaviors on metabolic diseases and comorbidities. Short sleep duration and poor sleep quality have been identified as independent risk factors for comorbidities [9, 13]. Conversely, a sleep duration of 7 h has been associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer and all-cause mortality [14]. Findings from the UK Biobank confirm that irregular sleep patterns and sleeping more than 7 h per night not only increase the risk of T2DM [15], but also predict higher mortality [16]. Evidence also shows that late bedtime is strongly associated with newly diagnosed NAFLD and increased diabetes risk [17, 18]. In contrast, early bedtime (before 10 pm or between 10 and 11 pm) has been associated with a lower risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease [19]. Snoring has similarly been associated with metabolic disorders, including hypertension, impaired glucose metabolism, and dyslipidemia [19].

Beyond individual sleep factors, some studies have adopted a “one-size-fits-all” approach by summing the scores of multiple sleep parameters into a composite sleep score to evaluate associations with metabolic diseases [20]. This method, however, does not account for the heterogeneity of sleep factors across individuals and diseases. Stratified medicine, grounded in the principles of precision medicine, emphasizes individual differences and integrates all sleep factors for each person. Such an approach enables more accurate evaluation of associations between sleep variability and metabolic diseases across groups, thereby providing a theoretical basis for targeted interventions [21].

In this context, the present study applies multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) and clustering methods to categorize population sleep patterns based on multiple sleep factors. It investigates the direct associations between sleep factors, sleep patterns, and metabolic diseases and comorbidities. In addition, it evaluates the mediating role of body mass index (BMI) in the prevalence of metabolic comorbidities across different sleep pattern groups. The aim is to describe the current burden of metabolic diseases in the population and to elucidate associations between sleep, metabolic diseases, and comorbidities, thereby informing strategies for disease prevention.

Methods

Data collection and processing procedures

Data were obtained from health check-up participants at a tertiary hospital in Zhengzhou, Henan Province between 2021 and 2024. A total of 11,812 individuals aged 18 years and above were initially enrolled. Individuals with severe liver or kidney diseases, tumors or suspected tumors, or incomplete information were excluded. After exclusions, 4,970 individuals remained eligible for analysis. A detailed flowchart of the study population is provided in the supplementary material (Fig. S1). All subjects provided written informed consent (Ethics Approval No.2018-KY-56). Each participant under a standardized health examination, which included a questionnaire, biochemical tests, physical examination, and abdominal color ultrasound. Questionnaires were administered through one-to-one interviews to ensure accuracy. Reliability and validity testing of the sleep section showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.799, item-level content validity index (I-CVI/Ave) scores ranging from 0.8 to 1.0, and a scale-level content validity index/average (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.95. Factor analysis identified two common factors from the eight sleep-related variables, with a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of 0.741 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity χ² = 156.561, P < 0.001.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe liver or kidney diseases, (2) suspected tumors or confirmed tumors, (3) metabolic diseases caused by medications such as hormones, and (4) pregnancy or lactation. Until 2024, participants were classified as having metabolic comorbidities according to standard diagnostic criteria.

Based on the collected data, we have developed a research and analysis process framework to identify sleep patterns and explore the association between sleep and metabolic comorbidities or diseases, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Regarding the collection and analysis of each indicator, we conducted standardized measurements. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the umbilical level with a measuring tape. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were recorded using an Omron sphygmomanometer (HBP 9021, OMRON, Dalian, China). Biochemical indices were assessed in fasting samples using an automatic biochemical immune analyzer (cobas 8000, Roche, Switzerland). Measurements included serum uric acid (SUA), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c).

Overall research process

Classification of indicators and disease definition criteria

(1) Smoking and drinking were classified as Yes/No. (2) Regular exercise was defined as ≥ 150 min/week of moderate physical or ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous physical activity [22]. (3) Dietary preference was categorized as vegetarian-based diet, meat-based diet, or meat-vegetarian balance. (4) Four sleep-related factors were assessed as bedtime routine, sleep quality, sleep duration, and snoring. (1) Bedtime routine: early bedtime (10–11 pm) [19], occasional late bedtime (≥ 1 day/week after 11 pm), frequent late bedtime (≥ 3 days/week after 11 pm), consistent late bedtime (4–5 days/week after 11 pm). (2) Sleep quality (self-reported): very good, good, normal, poor, very poor. (3) Sleep duration: < 7 h, 7–8 h, >8 h. (4) Snoring: Yes/No. 5. T2DM was defined as meeting any of the following: (1) documented history of T2DM, (2) use of hypoglycemic agents, (3) FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [23]. 6. Central obesity was defined as WC ≥ 90 cm in males or WC ≥ 85 cm in females [24]. 7. Hypertension was defined as: (1) documented history of hypertension, (2) use of antihypertensive medications, or (3) SBP ≥ 140mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. 8. Hyperuricemia was defined as SUA >420 µmol/L in males or SUA >360 µmol/L in females [25]. 9. Dyslipidemia was defined as meeting any of the following: (1) TC >240 mg/dL, (2) TG >200 mg/dL, (3) LDL >160 mg/dL, (4) HDL < 40 mg/dL, or (5) use of lipid-lowering drugs [26]. 10. NAFLD was defined by ultrasound findings. 11. Metabolic comorbidities were defined as the coexistence of two or more conditions, including central obesity, hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, hyperuricemia, and NAFLD.

Statistical analysis

MCA and K-means clustering were performed in R version 4.4.1, using the FactoMineR package for MCA. These methods were applied to cluster sleep-related variables and derive sleep pattern subgroups. Categorical variables were presented as n (%). Normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro - Wilk and Kolmogorov - Smirnov tests. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (), whereas non-normal distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range). The Chi-squared (χ²) test was used for categorical variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, one-way ANOVA was applied. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal - Wallis test was used. A two-sided α level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For overall tests with P < 0.05, pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations between sleep factors or sleep pattens and metabolic comorbidities or individual metabolic diseases. Sensitivity analyses additionally considered sleep-inducing medications, psychological stress, and mental state. Mediation analysis was performed using the bruceR package to assess the extent to which BMI explained associations between sleep factors and the odds of metabolic diseases or comorbidities. Because BMI was not normal distribution, it was standardized before inclusion in logistic analysis and mediation analyses.

Results

Sleep pattern recognition

MCA and K-means clustering classified all sleep factors into three patterns (Fig. 2A). Youth and men tended to occasionally or frequently have late bedtimes, normal sleep quality and snoring. In contrast, older adults and women tended to have early bedtimes, very good sleep quality, and no snoring. Consistently having a late bedtime was often associated with very poor sleep quality. Using the silhouette coefficient method and the elbow method (Fig. S2, Fig. S3), K-means clustering divided the population into three categories: good sleep pattern (GSP), intermediate sleep pattern (ISP), and poor sleep pattern (PSP) (Fig. 2B and C).

Sleep patterns recognition results

In GSP, 66.8% of participants had early bedtimes, 48.8% reported very good sleep quality, 51.8% slept for 7–8 h per night, and 91.3% did not snore. In ISP, 57.5% occasionally had late bedtimes, 67.4% reported normal sleep quality, 87.9% slept less than 7 h, and 74.1% did not snore. In PSP, 46.0% frequently had late bedtimes, 28.3% consistently had late bedtimes, 83.7% rated their sleep quality as poor or very poor, 90.4% slept less than 7 h, and 29% snored. The distribution and correlation of sleep pattern factors are shown in Fig. 2(C, D1, D2, D3). In GSP, the largest subgroup (60%) consisted of participants who had early bedtimes without snoring, followed by those who slept 7–8 h without snoring (46%) and those with very good sleep quality without snoring (42%). In ISP, the largest subgroup (66%) consisted of participants who slept less than 7 h without snoring. Additionally, 58% slept less than 7 h with normal sleep quality, whereas 49% slept less than 7 h and occasionally had late bedtimes. In PSP, the largest subgroup (65%) consisted of participants who slept less than 7 h and snored, followed by those who slept less than 7 h with poor sleep quality (64%). Meanwhile, 42% of this group slept less than 7 h at night and frequently had late bedtimes.

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 shows that participants in the PSP group had significantly higher proportions of unfavorable sleep factors compared with those in the GSP and ISP groups (P < 0.05). In the PSP group, 77.9% had late bedtimes, 83.7% reported poor or very poor sleep quality, 90.4% slept less than 7 h, and 29.1% snored. The prevalence of T2DM, dyslipidemia, and NAFLD was significantly higher in the PSP group than in the GSP group (P < 0.05). Specifically, 19.3% had dyslipidemia, 64.9% had T2DM, and 58.7% had NAFLD. Meanwhile, the prevalence of hypertension, hyperuricemia, and NAFLD was higher in the ISP group than in the GSP group. There were statistically significant differences in SBP, TG, HDL-C, FPG, and HbA1c across sleep patterns (P < 0.05). SBP in the PSP group was significantly lower than in the GSP group, with median values of 126 (24) mmHg. TG levels were significantly higher in the PSP group than in both the GSP and ISP groups, at 1.51 (1.22) mmol/L. After removing outliers, BMI was significantly higher in the PSP compared with the GSP and ISP groups (Fig. 3). The GSP group had significantly higher SBP and HDL-C levels than the ISP and PSP groups, higher FPG and HbA1c levels than the ISP group, and significantly lower TG levels than the ISP and PSP groups.

Differential analysis of biochemical indicator distributions among sleep patterns

Univariate logistic regression analysis of the risk of metabolic comorbidities

Figure 4 shows that male sex [OR (95% CI): 3.521 (3.115, 3.981)], smoking [OR (95% CI): 2.553 (2.203, 2.959)], drinking [OR (95% CI): 1.985 (1.759, 2.239)], and meat-vegetarian balance preference [OR (95% CI): 2.607 (1.996, 3.404)] significantly increased the odds of comorbidities. By contrast, having a university education or above and engaging in regular exercise reduced the odds of comorbidities by 24.2% [OR (95% CI): 0.758 (0.674, 0.852)] and 12.1% [OR (95% CI): 0.879 (0.782, 0.988)], respectively. No association was observed between income and metabolic comorbidities. For each standard deviation (SD) increase in BMI, the odds of comorbidities increased by 626.6% [OR (95% CI): 7.266 (6.456, 8.179)] in the total population and by 24.6% [OR (95% CI): 1.246 (1.026, 1.514)] in the PSP group.

Forest plot of univariate logistic regression for metabolic comorbidities

Sex-subgroup analyses revealed that for each SD increase in BMI, the odds of comorbidities increased by 429.8% [OR (95% CI): 5.298 (4.551, 6.168)] in men and 604.0% [OR (95% CI): 7.040 (5.926, 8.363)] in women. Detailed results for the total population and sex-subgroups are presented in Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3.

Multiple logistic regression analysis of metabolic comorbidities

Table 2 presents the results of multivariate logistic regression. After adjusting for all confounders in Model 4, compared with the GSP group, the odds of comorbidities increased by 34.5% [OR (95%CI): 1.345(1.043, 1.735)] in the PSP group. Subgroup analyses by sex and age confirmed these results. Compared with the GSP group, the odds of comorbidities increased by 51.9% [OR (95% CI): 1.519 (1.035, 2.230)] in men and by 84.4% [OR (95% CI): 1.844 (1.112, 3.055)] in youth, whereas no significant association was found in women [OR (95% CI): 1.109 (0.777, 1.583)]. Age-specific subgroup results are presented in Table S4. A dynamic nomogram was developed for the total population (Fig. S5), illustrating the relative contribution of each adjusted factor in the models. The cumulative score of all factors was converted into the probability of comorbidity occurrence. For example, in Fig. S5, a total score of 329 corresponds to a predicted probability of 0.162 for the occurrence of metabolic comorbidities.

In a sensitivity analysis incorporating sleep-related medications, psychological stress, and states of mind, PSP remained associated with increased odds of comorbidities both in the total population [32.4%; OR (95% CI): 1.324 (1.013, 1.731)] and in men [51.5%; OR (95% CI): 1.515 (1.014, 2.262)], consistent with the results of the unadjusted model (Table S5).

To further evaluate associations between sleep patterns and specific metabolic diseases, we performed disease-specific logistic regression analyses (Table S6 - S11). Figure 5 shows that compared with the GSP group, the odds of T2DM increased by 19.4% [OR (95%CI): 1.194(1.045, 1.365)] in the ISP group and 39.5% [OR (95%CI): 1.395(1.144, 1.702)] in the PSP group. Both ISP and PSP groups also had higher odds of NAFLD: 23.7% [OR (95%CI): 1.237(1.061, 1.443)] in the ISP group and 27.4% [OR (95%CI): 1.274(1.015, 1.600)] in the PSP group. Sex-stratified analyses indicated that compared with the GSP group, the PSP group had higher odds of T2DM [47.6%; OR (95%CI): 1.476(1.115, 1.953)] and NAFLD [48.7%; OR (95%CI): 1.487(1.081, 2.046)] in men, whereas the ISP group showed increased odds of NAFLD [29.6%; OR (95%CI): 1.296(1.056, 1.591)] in men.

Sleep patterns and comorbidities as well as metabolic diseases

In addition, we further analyzed the associations between four sleep factors and comorbidity risk. After adjustment for all confounders in Model 4, consistently having a late bedtime [OR (95%CI): 1.677(1.059, 2.658)] and snoring [OR (95%CI): 1.464(1.179, 1.819)] were significantly associated with increased odds of comorbidities (Table 3). No significant association was found between sleep factors and comorbidity risk in men, whereas in the female subgroup, only snoring was significantly associated with an increased comorbidity risk by 61.8% [OR (95%CI): 1.618(1.121,2.335)] (Table S12 – Table S13). Consistently having a late bedtime had a stronger effect than snoring, resulting in a 67.7% increase in comorbidities risk. No significant associations were observed between sleep quality or sleep duration and comorbidity risk.

As shown in Fig. 4, each SD increase in BMI was associated with a 626.6% increase in the odds of metabolic comorbidities [OR (95% CI): 7.266 (6.456, 8.179)]. In sex-stratified analyses, after full adjustment, only PSP was significantly associated with higher comorbidity risk in men [OR (95% CI): 1.519 (1.035, 2.230)], prompting mediation analysis to evaluate the role of BMI. BMI significantly mediated the association between sleep patterns and comorbidities, with an indirect effect of [OR (95%CI): 0.012(0.001, 0.024)], accounting for 31.58% of the total effect in men (Table 4). At the sleep factor level, bedtime routine and snoring both exerted significant direct effects, whereas BMI mediated their indirect effects, explaining 41.38% and 76.52% of the total effects, respectively. For individual metabolic diseases, BMI partially mediated the effects of sleep patterns on NAFLD and T2DM, accounting for 24% and 14.89% of the total effects, respectively (Table S14 - S19). These results indicate that BMI only partially mediates the influence of sleep patterns and factors on metabolic comorbidities in men.

Discussion

MCA and the K-means algorithm were used to categorize four sleep factors into different sleep patterns, revealing that PSP was significantly associated with higher odds of metabolic comorbidities in all participants and in males. In addition, consistently having a late bedtime and snoring were associated with increased odds of metabolic comorbidities all participants. Mediation analysis further showed that sleep patterns had a direct effect on metabolic comorbidities, and BMI partially mediated this association in men.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that sleep behaviors are synergistically correlated and collectively influence metabolic health [27]. Early research often examined single sleep factors, but in practice, several sleep behaviors may exert counteracting or synergistic effects. Therefore, a single sleep factor is insufficient for evaluating the association between overall sleep and metabolic diseases. MCA is useful in identifying individuals with similar characteristics [28]. In this study, MCA and the K-means algorithm clustered bedtime routine, sleep duration, sleep quality, and snoring into three groups: GSP, ISP, and PSP. The GSP group typically had earlier bedtime and better sleep quality, with fewer snorers and healthier sleep duration. The ISP group had moderately early bedtime and good sleep quality, but shorter sleep duration and a higher proportion of snorers. The PSP group consistently had late bedtime, poor sleep quality, shorter sleep duration and more frequent snoring. These findings suggest that sleep factors interact and collectively shape overall sleep health.

Further analysis indicated that PSP was strongly associated with increased odds of metabolic comorbidities. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that poor sleep quality is associated with diabetes [27], metabolic-associated fatty liver disease [29, 30], hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases [31, 32]. Both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies consistently support the link between poor sleep quality and metabolic comorbidities [9, 33, 34]. Subgroup analyses by sex and age validated the robustness of these findings. Among males, PSP significantly increased the odds of metabolic comorbidities. Comparisons of biochemical indicators revealed that, except for LDL-C and HbA1c, all measures differed by sex. Women had higher HDL-C and TC levels, whereas men had a higher-risk profile for other indicators (Table S24). These differences are consistent with studies reporting that men bear a heavier metabolic risk burden compared with younger women (< 60 years) [35]. The stronger association of PSP with metabolic comorbidities in men may therefore reflect higher baseline metabolic risk. We also examined individual sleep factors by sex. Late bedtime and snoring were more prevalent in men, although only a small proportion reported poor sleep quality. This may suggest limited awareness of healthy sleep among men (Table S25). These sex differences may also be explained by lifestyle factors. Baseline data showed that men were overrepresented in the low healthy lifestyle group compared with the high healthy lifestyle group [36]. Women generally adopt healthier lifestyles, whereas men are more likely to smoke and drink [37]. Men also showed higher prevalence of metabolic diseases, in agreement with evidence that men have greater risk of T2DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia across all age groups in China [38].

Among the four sleep factors, having a late bedtime was associated with increased odds of metabolic comorbidities, central obesity, dyslipidemia, NAFLD, and T2DM, regardless of sleep duration. This finding is consistent with studies linking late bedtime to higher risk of NAFLD [17]. On the one hand, circadian rhythm disruption is associated with imbalanced sympathetic-parasympathetic nerve activity, which in turn leads to fat accumulation in the liver. On the other hand, an experimental study had shown that circadian misalignment disrupts liver clocks and induces global hepatic metabolic dysfunction [17]. Accumulating evidence supports the role of late bedtime in circadian rhythm disruption [39, 40]. Dysregulation of diurnal rhythms, whether from shift work or other factors, can decouple central and peripheral circadian cycles, resulting in metabolic disturbances [41]. For example, shift workers often exhibit higher rates of dyslipidemia, potentially due to impaired melatonin production and elevated nighttime salivary cortisol levels [42]. Cortisol rhythms play a key role in synchronizing central and peripheral clocks [41]. Disruption of cortisol circadian rhythms is associated with diabetes, as cortisol impairs insulin signaling in skeletal muscle, elevates free fatty acids, and promotes insulin resistance. It also increases gluconeogenesis in the liver and suppresses insulin secretion in rodent beta cells [43]. These mechanisms collectively heighten the risks of obesity, insulin resistance, NAFLD, and cardiovascular diseases [44].

In addition to consistently having a late bedtime, snoring also increased the odds of metabolic comorbidities. Previous studies have shown that snoring increases the risk of diabetes [19], possibly because it activates the sympathetic nervous system, elevates catecholamine levels, and stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which raises cortisol levels and triggers oxidative stress responses that alter glucose metabolism [45]. Snoring has also been independently associated with higher odds of NAFLD, as confirmed in a prospective study [46].

Obesity remains the largest contributor to metabolic comorbidities worldwide [2, 10]. The interaction between obesity, sleep disorders, and metabolic comorbidities creates a vicious cycle [47], making causal pathways difficult to disentangle. Recent work by Mark T.U. Barone suggested that sleep factors may exert direct effects. As stressors, insufficient sleep and sleep disorders modulate obesity and glucose metabolism by altering hormone balance and levels including leptin, ghrelin, insulin, and cortisol thereby activating the nervous system, increasing stress hormone secretion, and triggering inflammatory responses [48]. This aligns with our mediation analysis in men, which showed that BMI only partially mediated the effect of sleep patterns on comorbidities, T2DM, and NAFLD. The direct effects of sleep patterns on these conditions further support the association between sleep and metabolic comorbidities in men.

The strengths of this study lie in its novel integration of four sleep-related factors using MCA and K-means algorithms to classify sleep patterns. This approach complements existing research by revealing associations between sleep patterns and metabolic comorbidities, thereby advancing prevention and management strategies from a sleep perspective. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal associations between sleep patterns and metabolic comorbidities cannot be inferred. These associations should be validated with external datasets, particularly in community populations. Second, sleep data relied on self-reports, which may not fully reflect actual sleep habits, and only a subset of sleep factors was included. Variables such as napping and insomnia were not assessed. Third, because of the challenges of community-based research, health check-up participants were used as study subjects, and their results may not represent the general population. Finally, not all confounding factors were accounted for, particularly detailed lifestyle information.

Future work will include prospective cohort studies to clarify causal relationships between sleep patterns and metabolic diseases, incorporating additional sleep-related factors for a more comprehensive analysis. We also plan to investigate genetic associations between sleep and metabolic comorbidities through Mendelian randomization, to refine and strengthen the current findings.

Conclusion

Unlike a “one-size-fits-all” approach, our findings demonstrated a strong agglomeration effect among sleep factors, with significant correlations within each sleep patterns. Using MCA and K-means clustering, we identified that PSP was associated with increased odds of metabolic comorbidities, with this association being pronounced in all participants and men. BMI partially mediated this association in the male subgroup. ISP showed significant associations with T2DM and NAFLD. These results highlight the importance of overall sleep patterns in relation to metabolic comorbidities compared with overweight and obesity alone and provide a theoretical basis for strategies aimed at preventing and managing metabolic diseases and comorbidities.
