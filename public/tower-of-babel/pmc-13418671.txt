DFT insight into tuning electron–phonon transmission and thermoelectric transport in nanoscale GeTe devices: effects of Sb substitutional doping
Authors: A. Sakthi Balaji, Akash Ramasamy, C. Preferencial Kala, K. Janani Sivasankar, D. John Thiruvadigal, Hariharan Rajalakshmi Mohanraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermoelectric materials have received much attention for their ability to directly convert heat into electricity for energy-conversion applications. This work presents a complete study of the effect of antimony (Sb) substitutional doping on the thermoelectric transport properties of a GeTe system based on the DFT-NEGF framework. In this study, we investigated three different doping concentrations by substituting Ge atoms with Sb atoms, namely, 1Sb-doped, 2Sb-doped, and 3Sb-doped GeTe systems. The incorporation of Sb atoms dramatically changes the local atomic structure and electronic environment of the GeTe monolayer, which leads to remarkable changes in charge distribution, electronic structure and thermoelectric transport behaviour. To evaluate the thermoelectric response, two-probe device models are constructed for pristine and doped GeTe monolayers, and transport parameters such as electrical conductance, Seebeck coefficient, thermal conductivity, and figure of merit (ZT) are calculated. The results show that Sb substitution enhances the thermoelectric performance of GeTe, with the 2Sb-doped configuration exhibiting the highest ZT value of about 7.2 at 700 K. These findings demonstrate that Sb substitutional doping is an effective approach for improving the thermoelectric efficiency of two-dimensional GeTe-based devices.

Introduction

At present, most of the world's energy needs are fulfilled by fossil fuel-based non-renewable resources. The heavy use of fossil fuels is a major reason for global warming and contributes more than 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions.1 Their excessive use also raises concerns about future resource depletion and causes serious environmental problems such as climate change and air pollution. Because of these issues, governments, industries, and researchers are actively searching for renewable, sustainable, and eco-friendly energy sources such as solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, and thermoelectric (TE) energy.2,3 However, renewable energy sources like wind and tidal energy have limitations due to their intermittent nature and location-dependent applications. At the same time, a large amount of energy produced worldwide is lost as waste heat. Reports show that more than 60% of generated energy is wasted as heat in household and industrial activities.3 Recovering this waste heat is very important for improving energy efficiency and reducing environmental impact. Waste heat to useful energy conversion can be done using traditional waste heat recovery methods such as the Rankine cycle and Organic Rankine cycle, but these methods often require complex systems with moving parts and working fluids.4,5 By contrast, thermoelectric (TE) technology converts heat to electricity directly with solid-state devices.6,7 TE systems have the advantages of no moving parts, long operating life, and zero emissions. Thermoelectric technology is a promising and environmentally friendly approach for the recovery of waste heat, but the widespread application of this technology is hindered by the drawbacks of existing thermoelectric materials, such as high cost, scarcity, and poor stability at high temperatures. Therefore, to advance this technology, it is imperative to develop efficient, stable and low-cost thermoelectric materials. The search for 2D materials with outstanding transport properties is crucial for the realisation of next-generation thermo-electric devices.

Since the discovery of graphene,8 many other 2D materials, such as phosphorene, germanene, silicene, antimonene, and transition metal dichalcogenides, have attracted considerable research interest due to their wide range of applications.9–12 Among these, group IV–VI chalcogenides have received much attention due to their stable 2D structures, tunable electronic properties, narrow band gaps and excellent thermoelectric performance.13 Compared to group IV–VI materials, GeTe has recently emerged as a robust monolayer form.14 Monolayer GeTe shows unique properties such as buckled atomic structures, higher crystallization temperature, lower melting point, indirect bandgap, and high carrier mobility compared to bulk GeTe.15–17 The atomic planes of GeTe have a buckling form where the Ge and Te atoms are interconnected through weak vdW forces.16 Also, monolayer GeTe exhibits high carrier mobility with size dependency and has been utilised in many applications, including thermoelectric systems,18 nanoelectonics,19 and non-volatile memory storage.20 Panpan Zhang et al. successfully synthesised GeTe in monolayer form and achieved up to four layers by a liquid-phase exfoliation method.21 Man Qiao et al. conducted a study to examine photocatalytic water splitting through experimentation. This experimental synthesis22 underscores the utility of a two-dimensional chalcogenide GeTe monolayer.22

From a thermoelectric point of view, Fouad N. Ajeel's research examined the improvement of thermoelectric performance in single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) by the NEGF method with doping of Al, Si, P, and S. The research demonstrated that Al- and S-doped SWCNT systems have enhanced thermoelectric performance, attaining a ZT value of around 2.23 Similarly, Fouad N. Ajeel and Ali Ben Ahmed explored the thermoelectric behaviour of graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) with BN dimer doping. Their study demonstrated that the BN (1–4) doped configuration significantly improves thermoelectric efficiency, reaching a maximum ZT value of 2.6 at 500 K.24 For GeTe monolayers, theoretical studies have reported very high thermoelectric performance, with ZT values exceeding 1 at room temperature and reaching above 7 at higher temperatures.25 Aihemaitijiang Sidike et al. investigated the thermoelectric properties of two-probe van der Waals heterojunction devices consisting of black phosphorus (BP) and black arsenic (BAs). Their investigation showed a maximum thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) of 3.5 at 350 K for the vdW heterostructure.26 Similarly, Wu et al. also showed significant enhancement in thermoelectric efficiency for the MoS2/h-BN heterostructure compared with the individual monolayers, demonstrating that heterostructure engineering is a promising strategy to improve the energy conversion performance of thermoelectric devices.27 In another work, Yaohong Zhou et al. investigated the thermoelectric performance enhancement in black phosphorene/Ti2C vdW heterolayers using the ATK + NEGF approach.28 L. J. Gong et al. theoretically studied high thermoelectric properties of monolayer GeTe allotropes via first-principles + NEGF, and they report high ZT values (∼4.5 at 300 K for γ-GeTe, 3.5 for ε-GeTe, 5.91 at 700 K).29

The various strategies such as strain engineering, alloying and defect engineering have been explored to enhance the thermoelectric properties of GeTe-based materials, the concentration-dependent effect of substitutional Sb doping on the coupled electron and phonon transport properties of monolayer GeTe is still unexplored. Motivated by the above research gap, we investigate the antimony (Sb) substitutional doping effects on the structural, electronic, and thermoelectric transport performance of GeTe monolayers using the DFT + NEGF approach. We considered three doping concentrations in the GeTe system by substituting Ge atoms with Sb atoms. The structural stability of the investigated systems was evaluated with the formation energy, phonon dispersion and molecular dynamics calculations, and their energetic and thermal stability were confirmed. To further analyse the thermoelectric performance, two-probe devices were fabricated for the pristine and Sb-doped GeTe systems. The important transport parameters, such as Seebeck coefficient, thermal & electrical conductance, and thermoelectric figure of merit, are calculated systematically. The following subsequent part comprehensively addresses computational methodologies, structural, electrical, and thermoelectric transport studies as a result of Sb doping engineering.

Computational details

The transport properties of Sb-doped 2D GeTe systems were studied with the Quantum ATK package based on Density Functional Theory (DFT).30 Firstly, structural optimisation of the GeTe monolayer and Sb-doped configurations was performed using the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) method.31 The exchange–correlation interactions were described by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional in the framework of the Generalised Gradient Approximation (GGA).32 The Grimme's DFT-D3 correction method was used for the van der Waals interactions.32 The ion-core-valence interaction was described by means of the Troullier–Martin pseudo-Dojo pseudopotentials. Accurate calculations were performed with a mesh cutoff energy of 100 hartree and an energy convergence criterion of 10−5 eV. The atomic positions were relaxed until the Hellmann–Feynman force on each atom was less than 0.05 eV Å−1. Brillouin zone integration was performed using a 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid. A supercell of 4 × 4 with 32 atoms was constructed, and a vacuum distance of 20 Å was added in the c-direction to avoid interaction between the layers.

Structural and stability studies of Sb-substituted GeTe monolayer

Geometric structure analysis

The structural, electronic, and optical properties of the pristine GeTe monolayer were systematically investigated in our previous studies35,36 and are presented in Fig. S1. Sb is chemically compatible with GeTe due to similar atomic size and valence characteristics, making substitutional incorporation a realistic modification strategy. The optimized atomic structures of Sb-doped GeTe monolayers at different dopant concentrations are illustrated through top and side views in Fig. 1. To introduce Sb doping, Ge atoms in the pristine GeTe lattice were selectively substituted with Sb atoms, resulting in three distinct doped configurations, namely 1Sb-GeTe, 2Sb-GeTe, and 3Sb-GeTe, as shown in Fig. 1(a–c). Large local distortions were found around the Sb substitution sites upon structural relaxation due to the difference in atomic radius and electronegativity between Ge and Sb atoms. These structural alterations alter the bond lengths and bond angles of the GeTe lattice. The computed bond lengths for the 1Sb-GeTe and 2Sb-GeTe systems were around 2.92 Å, whereas the 3Sb-GeTe configuration had a reduced length of 2.79 Å. Similarly, the bond angles of 1Sb-GeTe, 2Sb-GeTe, and 3Sb-GeTe were adjusted to 87.3°, 87.31°, and 90.01°, respectively. The computed geometric structural parameters are summarized in Table 1. There are small distortions around the dopant sites, but the overall lattice parameter remains nearly constant, indicating that the GeTe monolayer keeps its structural frame at greater Sb concentrations. The results reveal that Sb substitution can effectively alter the local atomic environment and adjust the geometrical properties of the GeTe monolayer without generating lattice instability.

Structural stability investigation

The structural stability of both pristine and Sb-doped GeTe monolayers was investigated through cohesive energy calculations, which provide important information about the energetic feasibility of the doped systems. The cohesive energy was calculated using the following relation:37,388ECoh = 1/N (Esystem − nGeEGe − nTeETe − nSbESb)Esystem represents the total energy of the Sb-doped GeTe system, nSb,nGe, and nTe denote the number of Sb, Ge, and Te atoms present in the supercell, respectively. ESb,EGe, and ETe represent the total energies of isolated Sb, Ge, and Te atoms. N represents the total number of atoms present in the system.

Cohesive energy analysis is an effective way to analyse the energetic stability of doped materials. In general, a negative cohesive energy means that the system's creation is energetically favourable, and that the doped structure may exist in a stable configuration without additional energy input. Eqn. (8) yields the predicted cohesive energies for the 1Sb-GeTe, 2Sb-GeTe, and 3Sb-GeTe systems as −5.74 eV, −7.12 eV, and −6.30 eV, respectively. Among the three configurations, the 2Sb-GeTe system has the lowest cohesive energy, indicating strong thermodynamic stability. The negative cohesive energies obtained for all doped configurations demonstrate that Sb atoms can be successfully integrated into the GeTe monolayer lattice without destabilising the system. These results clearly demonstrate that Sb substitution is energetically favourable and suited for tuning the characteristics of GeTe monolayers. Dynamic stability-phonon band structure analysis

Phonon dispersion calculations are useful for determining the vibrational or dynamical stability of low-dimensional materials since the existence of imaginary phonon frequencies suggests structural instability.39 In this study, from Fig. 2, all Sb-doped GeTe configurations have totally positive phonon dispersion spectra across the entire Brillouin zone. This confirms the dynamic stability of the Sb-doped systems.

Phonon band structure of (a) 1Sb-GeTe, (b) 2Sb-GeTe, and (c) 3Sb-GeTe monolayer.

The number of atoms in the supercell of each Sb-doped GeTe configuration results in 96 phonon branches. Three of these branches are acoustic phonon modes (shown in red), whereas the remaining 93 are optical phonon modes (shown in blue). Acoustic modes dominate the low-frequency domain and are primarily responsible for heat transport, but optical modes play a crucial role in phonon scattering and lattice anharmonicity.40 As illustrated in Fig. 2, the substitution of Sb causes significant modifications in the phonon spectra, particularly in the optical phonon region. Doping with Sb results in optical phonon modes in the energy range of 0.014–0.0148 eV. The presence of an optical phonon gap indicates a modulation of vibrational coupling due to dopant atoms. The absence of a wide acoustic-optical gap suggests a stronger phonon interaction between the low and high frequency modes.

Furthermore, the introduction of doping modifies the phonon band structure by flattening phonon branches, inducing mass fluctuation, local lattice distortion, creating localized vibrational modes, and breaking the lattice periodicity.41,42 These effects suppress the number and magnitude of available phonon transmission channels, leading to lower phonon transmission. This suppression of lattice transmission lowers the phonon thermal conductance, which is highly favourable for thermoelectric applications.

Thermal stability-molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The thermal stability of the Sb-doped GeTe monolayer systems was studied by using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations.43,44 AIMD analysis is a powerful tool to study the behaviour of materials under finite temperature conditions, as it provides information on atomic motion, lattice stability and structural durability under thermal excitation. In this work, the simulations were performed in the microcanonical ensemble (NVE).45 NVE means that the total number of atoms (N), system volume (V) and total energy (E) are constant during the simulation process. The atomic-scale dynamics were simulated with a time step of 1 fs for a total simulation time of 50 ps at 300 K, 500 K, and 700 K, as shown in Fig. S2. The results show that for all the systems, the simulation exhibits only small fluctuations in temperature and potential energy. No abrupt energy changes, abnormal oscillations or lattice reconstruction were observed even at the highest temperature of 700 K. The lack of any major structural changes confirms that the Sb-doped GeTe monolayers retain their structural integrity under thermal perturbation.

The observed thermal robustness is attributed to the strong bonding interactions between Ge, Te and substituted Sb atoms, which provide effective stabilization of the lattice framework. Therefore, the extended 50 ps AIMD simulations provide further evidence that the studied Sb-doped GeTe monolayers remain dynamically and thermally stable from room temperature up to 700 K, supporting their potential suitability for high-temperature thermoelectric applications.

From overall stability analysis, the calculated cohesive energies indicate that the Sb substitution is energetically favourable, the phonon band structure shows dynamical stability and the ab initio molecular dynamics simulations exhibit structural stability over the range of temperatures studied. These results indicate that the proposed Sb-doped structures are not just theoretical proposals, but are expected to be realized experimentally.

Electronic properties of Sb-substituted GeTe systems

Electronic band structure analysis

The electronic band structures of the Sb-doped GeTe monolayer systems are shown in Fig. 3(a–c). Substitutional incorporation of Sb atoms into the GeTe lattice produces significant modifications in the electronic properties by disturbing the pristine crystal symmetry and redistributing the electronic states around the Fermi level. Fig. 3(a) shows the 1Sb-doped GeTe structure, which is still a semiconductor with an indirect band gap of about 1.49 eV. The presence of Sb-related impurity states at the valence band maximum (VBM) increases the band gap when compared to pure GeTe. When Ge is replaced by Sb, the orbital hybridisation between Ge, Te, and Sb atoms changes, causing the electronic states to shift towards the Fermi level. The smaller band gap also promotes carrier excitation, which improves the system's electrical transport properties. Fig. 3(b) shows that the electronic band gap is further lowered to roughly 0.84 eV for the 2Sb-doped GeTe structure, but the indirect semiconducting nature remains. Higher Sb concentrations cause stronger disturbances of the crystal potential, resulting in a greater overlap of neighbouring electronic orbitals. This improved orbital contact increases electrical conductivity, which is highly useful for thermoelectric applications. The reduction of the band gap also means that less excitation energy is required for carrier movement in the system, resulting in improved electronic response. As illustrated in Fig. 3(c), the change is more pronounced in the 3Sb-doped GeTe system. In this situation, the band gap fully disappears, resulting in a transition from semiconducting to metallic behaviour.46 Heavy Sb doping causes a substantial reconstruction of electronic bands with continuous energy levels that cross the Fermi level. Strong Sb-induced hybridisation processes and significant charge density redistribution in the lattice drive this semiconductor-to-metal transition. Free carriers can travel throughout the system because there exist continuous states near the Fermi level, resulting in enhanced electrical conduction. A significant change in the Fermi level is detected for all Sb-doped configurations, confirming the successful modification of the GeTe monolayer's electrical structure by substitutional Sb doping. The continuous shift from a broad band-gap semiconductor to a metallic state demonstrates GeTe's remarkable sensitivity to dopant concentrations. Hence, controllable electronic tunability is critical for thermoelectric applications because it enables optimisation of electrical conductivity and Seebeck response.

Band structure and PDOS of (a and d) 1Sb-GeTe, (b and e) 2Sb-GeTe, and (c and f) 3Sb-GeTe systems.

Electronic states and orbital distribution analysis

The total and projected density of states (TDOS and PDOS) of Sb-doped GeTe monolayers with different Sb concentrations, i.e., 1Sb-GeTe, 2Sb-GeTe, and 3Sb-GeTe, are shown in Fig. 3(d–f). Density of states analysis provides detailed insights into the distribution of electronic states on different energy levels, and PDOS analysis helps to identify the individual atomic orbital contribution to the electronic structure.47 In the 1Sb-doped GeTe system, sharp and localised peaks are observed near the Fermi level, which are related to the impurity-related electronic states introduced into the GeTe lattice by Sb substitution. The conduction band region (from about 0.5 to 2 eV) is mainly formed by Ge-4p and Te-5p orbitals, shown in Fig. 3(d). Sb-5p orbitals weakly contribute around the Fermi level. This relatively small contribution from Sb states implies that low concentration Sb doping induces a minor perturbation to the original electronic structure of pristine GeTe. For the 2Sb-doped GeTe case, the DOS exhibits broader and stronger peaks in the valence and conduction band regions, particularly in the valence band region between −2 and −0.5 eV. These enhanced states hint at a stronger orbital hybridisation among Sb-5p, Ge-4p and Te-5p orbitals. The higher orbital interaction increases the overlap between electronic states, which reduces the band gap and makes it easier to excite carriers across the Fermi level. Strong hybridisation improves electrical conductance, which is very useful for thermoelectric transport. For the 3Sb-doped (Fig. 3f) GeTe monolayer, a large increase in DOS intensity near the Fermi level is found, which can be attributed to the creation of shallow impurity states and a transition to metallic behaviour. The strong contribution of Sb-5p orbitals and Te-5p orbitals mainly compose the valence band region, while the conduction band has a significant contribution of Ge-4p states. The strong coupling between Sb-p orbitals and Te-p orbitals leads to strong electronic delocalisation and continuous energy states crossing the Fermi level. This is consistent with the semiconductor-to-metal transition found in the electronic band structure calculations. The DOS analysis generally shows that the electronic properties of GeTe can be systematically controlled by Sb substitution, and the dominant p-orbital hybridisation is of great importance for the control of electronic transport behaviour. These tunable electronic properties are highly desirable for optimising thermoelectric performance and designing advanced GeTe-based nanoelectronic and energy conversion devices.

The work function for the pristine GeTe monolayer is determined to be 3.93 eV (Fig. S1). In the 1Sb-doped GeTe system, the work function is significantly lowered to 3.35 eV. The reduction in work function can be attributed to the upward shift in the Fermi level generated by the impurity states provided by Sb. The work function of the 2Sb-doped GeTe configuration improves to 3.64 eV, whereas the 3Sb-doped system increases even more to 3.76 eV. The work function steadily increases with Sb concentration due to increased orbital hybridisation and redistribution of electronic states toward the Fermi level. These changes affect the electrostatic potential profile and electron escape barrier in the system. Despite the increase, the work function values of the doped systems are lower than those of pure GeTe, indicating that Sb substitution effectively modifies the material's surface electrical properties. The tunability of the work function in the 3.35–3.93 eV range reported in this study suggests that Sb doping is a viable method for altering the transport properties of GeTe monolayers.

Analysis of electron localization function

The electron localisation function (ELF)12,49 was calculated to investigate the nature of the bonding and charge distribution in the Sb doped GeTe monolayer, and the corresponding results are presented in Fig. S3. The ELF is an important quantum mechanical descriptor providing comprehensive information about the spatial distribution and localization behaviour of electrons in a material system. It is commonly used to classify the different types of chemical bonding such as covalent, ionic and metallic interactions, by analysing the probability of finding localized pairs of electrons in a given region of space. The ELF contour plots represent the images with red-coloured regions, corresponding to strong electron localization, indicating high probability of electron pairing and formation of localized chemical bonds. Instead, the blue regions represent electron delocalization, where the electrons are more evenly distributed throughout the lattice. Intermediate colours suggest a partial localization behaviour between these two extremes.

As can be seen in Fig. S3, the local electronic environment around the dopant sites is strongly changed after the introduction of the Sb atom in the GeTe monolayer. A strong localization of electrons is found around the Sb atoms, which indicates a strong hybridization of orbitals between Sb and surrounding Ge/Te atoms. This better localization shows the presence of directional covalent bonding character in the doped systems. Moreover, the progressive variation of ELF distribution with increasing Sb doping concentration reveals that the dopant atoms have a significant influence on the local charge density and bonding strength of the host lattice.

Electron difference density (EDD) mapping

The electron difference density (EDD) of Sb-doped GeTe monolayers is shown in Fig. 5 to demonstrate the charge redistribution.50 The electron transport of the Sb doping is well elucidated by the EDD analysis. The green areas in the EDD maps are the sites of electron accumulation, whereas the yellow areas are electron depletion regions. In Fig. 5, a large yellow cloud is observed around the Sb atoms, showing the fact that the electrons are pushed away from the dopant sites. This confirms the role of Sb atoms as charge donors in the lattice of GeTe. The electrons that are transferred are redistributed into neighbouring Ge and Te atoms, which changes the local bonding environment and electronic structure. The redistribution of charge can increase the orbital hybridization and change the carrier transport properties of the system. The charge transfer was calculated by ref. 51 and 52. The charge transfer values are estimated as ∼0.08e for the 1Sb-doped system, 0.21e for the 2Sb-doped system and 0.258e for the 3Sb-doped system. The gradual increase of charge transfer with increasing Sb concentration indicates a stronger electronic coupling between dopant and host lattice. The results show that Sb doping can effectively tune the charge distribution and electrical properties of GeTe monolayers, which will be conducive to the optimization of thermoelectric performance.

Thus, the electronic analysis, band structure, PDOS, work function, ELF and electron density difference analyses consistently suggested that the substitution of Sb tunes the electronic environment of GeTe by narrowing the band gap, increasing the orbital hybridization, shifting the Fermi level and redistributing the charge. These electronic-structure changes alter the availability and energy distribution of charge carriers near the Fermi energy, which are key factors for the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductance and electronic thermal conductance. The electronic analyses thus serve as a basis for the understanding of the evolution of the transport properties. From a thermoelectric perspective, the improved performance of Sb-doped GeTe originates from the coupling between these electronic modifications and the corresponding changes in electron and phonon transport, ultimately leading to the observed enhancement in the thermoelectric transport properties.

Study of thermoelectric transport in Sb-doped GeTe devices

Construction of two-probe device model

The thermoelectric properties of Sb-doped GeTe systems are investigated by using the two-probe model of transport,53 which is a well-accepted model to study electrical and thermal transport properties at the nanoscale. In this model, the means of transport are divided into three main regions, namely, the left electrode (LE), the central scattering region, and the right electrode (RE), as shown in Fig. 6. The electrodes are semi-infinite contacts injecting and collecting carriers, and the central scattering region is where interactions such as doping, defect scattering and charge redistribution occur. For the present study, the length of the central transport zone is 27.36 Å, and each electrode length is 6.84 Å. With this set-up, transmission spectra and thermoelectric coefficients such as Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductance and thermal conductance can be directly evaluated.

Electron and phonon transmission characteristics

The electronic transmission spectrum contains information on the probability of carriers with a certain energy (E) to pass through the device region.26,36 The electronic part of the spectra of the pristine and Sb-doped GeTe systems is presented in Fig. 7(a). In the pristine system, the spectrum displays a clear band gap, which restricts electron flow across the device. With Sb substitutional doping, the transmission gap is progressively reduced, reflecting the narrowing of the electronic band gap observed in the band structure analysis. Distinct peaks in the spectra correspond to preferred transport pathways, where carrier transmission is most efficient. Among the doped systems, the 1Sb-doped GeTe shows enhanced and well-defined transmission features, indicating stronger electronic transport channels, while the 3Sb-doped system exhibits a nearly continuous transmission spectrum due to its metallic nature.

(a) Electronic part of transmission (Te), and (b) phonon (or) lattice transmission spectra (Tph) of pristine and Sb-doped systems.

Fig. 7(b) illustrates the lattice transmission spectra plotted as a function of phonon frequency. Peaks in the spectra correspond to frequencies at which phonons can effectively propagate, while troughs indicate suppressed phonon transmission. The NEGF-based lattice transmission calculations account for elastic phonon scattering but exclude phonon–phonon interactions because of the ballistic transport phenomenon.28,33,54,55 Compared to pristine GeTe, Sb-doped systems generally show suppressed phonon transmission, particularly at higher Sb concentrations, due to enhanced phonon scattering introduced by the dopant atoms.

Electrical conductance analysis (Ge)

Fig. 8 shows the variation of the electronic conductance (Ge) of pristine and Sb-doped GeTe devices as a function of chemical potential (µ) at different temperatures. According to eqn (1), the electronic conductance is directly correlated with the electron transmission spectrum, and thus any change in the transmission channels is reflected in the Ge values. The maximum overall conductance across all systems is recorded for pristine GeTe, with 635 µS observed at µ = −1.7 eV. With an increase in operating temperature, the conductance of the pristine system decreases, a behaviour attributed to enhanced electron–phonon scattering that suppresses carrier mobility. In comparison, the 1Sb-doped system shows a reduced maximum conductance of about 295 µS at µ = −1.8 eV, indicating that the introduction of Sb dopants alters the transport channels and suppresses carrier mobility. For 2Sb and 3Sb doping, the conductance values further shift, with 2Sb showing moderate suppression compared to pristine, while 3Sb-doping reflects metallic-like behaviour but with reduced peak sharpness due to strong scattering effects. In general, as the temperature increases, the Ge valleys of all systems gradually shrink, consistent with the narrowing of the energy gap and the increased scattering at elevated temperatures. Overall, Sb-doped systems exhibit lower conductance compared to pristine GeTe, which can be attributed to stronger dopant-induced scattering of charge carriers.26,28 However, this suppression in electrical conductance is partially compensated in thermoelectric applications by reduced lattice thermal conductance, leading to an overall improvement in ZT for optimally doped systems, particularly in the case of 2Sb-doping.

Seebeck coefficient (S)

Fig. 9 shows the variation of the Seebeck coefficient (S) with the chemical potential (µ) for pristine and Sb-doped GeTe devices in the temperature range of 300–700 K. In all systems, the Seebeck coefficient shows the characteristic inverse dependence on the electrical conductance (Ge) predicted by the thermoelectric transport theory. (Positive Seebeck values (S > 0) relate to hole-dominated transport (p-type conduction) and negative values (S < 0) to electron-dominated transport (n-type conduction).56 The simultaneous presence of positive and negative Seebeck peaks throughout the entire explored chemical potential range suggests the ability to continuously tune the transport behaviour from p-to n-type regimes by shifting the Fermi level.

Seebeck coefficient (S) at the temperatures of (a) 300 K, (b) 500 K, and (c) 700 K of pristine and Sb-doped systems.

All systems show peaks in the Seebeck coefficient at 300 K centred at the Fermi level (µ = 0 eV) indicating the strong energy dependence of the electronic transmission at the band edges. The pristine GeTe monolayer has a maximum positive Seebeck coefficient of 2500 µV K−1, and the corresponding negative peak value is −2900 µV K−1. The positive and negative extrema are observed more pronounced upon Sb substitution, especially for the 2Sb- and 3Sb-doped systems. The 3Sb-doped device shows the largest Seebeck response (−3127 µV K−1 at µ = 0.0009 eV), indicating very efficient electron-dominated transport. The sharp variation of S near the Fermi level is due to the enhanced asymmetry in the electronic density of states and transmission function caused by Sb incorporation.

The overall magnitude of the Seebeck coefficient is gradually decreasing for all configurations with the increase of temperature to 500 K and 700 K. The maximum positive peak reduces from about 2500–2700 µV K−1 at 300 K to 1500 µV K−1 at 500 K and to 1100 µV K−1 at 700 K. Similarly, the negative Seebeck extrema decrease in magnitude from −3200 µV K−1 at 300 K to −2000 µV K−1 at 500 K and −1450 µV K−1 at 700 K. The reduction is attributed to the thermal broadening of the Fermi–Dirac distribution, which decreases the energy selectivity of the carrier transport, and to the asymmetry of the electron and hole contributions. The enhanced thermal excitation also favours bipolar transport of carriers that suppresses the overall Seebeck response. The most striking feature in all the curves is the sharp sign reversal of S near the Fermi level. This change results from the transition from hole-dominated to electron-dominated transport with a crossing of the chemical potential at the band edges. The steeper slope of the Sb-doped systems compared to pristine GeTe suggests a stronger modulation of the electronic structure around the Fermi level. This behaviour is in accordance with the band-gap narrowing in the electronic band structures, where the Sb substitution refers to more electronic states near the conduction and valence band edges. These states enhance the sensitivity of the Seebeck coefficient to changes in the chemical potential by increasing the carrier concentration. We find that the 2Sb and 3Sb doped structures in the studied systems show the largest Seebeck coefficients in a wide range of chemical potentials, indicating that moderate-to-high Sb concentrations are favourable for enhancing thermopower. The enhanced Seebeck response is attributed to doping-induced changes in the electronic band dispersion, such as increased band convergence, increased orbital hybridisation between Ge, Te and Sb atoms, and increased energy dependence of the electronic transmission close to the Fermi level. Such effects raise the entropy of the charge carriers and thus the thermoelectric voltage generated by a temperature gradient. Moreover, the asymmetry between the positive and negative Seebeck peaks reveals a more pronounced effect of Sb doping on the electron transport as opposed to the hole transport. The larger negative peaks in the doped systems suggest that the conduction-band states are more modified than the valence-band states, which is positive for n-type thermoelectric performance. This is in agreement with the reduced band gap and the improved electronic transmission near the conduction-band edge reported for Sb-doped GeTe.

Hence, the Seebeck coefficient analysis shows that Sb substitution can efficiently control the magnitude and sign of thermoelectric transport in GeTe monolayers. Thermal broadening effects result in a reduction of the absolute value of S with increasing temperature, while Sb-doping gives a strong enhancement of the Seebeck response close to the Fermi level and greater control of the carrier type. The very high Seebeck coefficients obtained for the 2Sb and 3Sb doped systems are a key factor for the enhanced thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) of these structures.

Electron (κe), phonon (κph), and total thermal conductance (κT) characteristics

Within the NEGF formalism, the electronic thermal conductance (κe) and phonon thermal conductance (κph) are obtained directly from the electronic and phonon transmission functions using eqn (1) and (6). Since the transport properties are evaluated directly from transmission spectra, a constant Lorenz number is not assumed, unlike in conventional Boltzmann transport approaches.37Fig. 10(a–c) illustrates the variation of electronic thermal conductance (κe) with chemical potential (µ) for pristine GeTe and Sb-doped systems (1Sb, 2Sb, and 3Sb) at different temperatures. The trends clearly show that κe follows the electrical conductance behaviour as explained by the Wiedemann–Franz law (κe = LσT).57 At 300 K, pristine GeTe exhibits the highest κe peaks above 3.8 nW K−1, while Sb doping suppresses the thermal conductance, with the 3Sb-doped system showing the lowest values. As the temperature rises to 500 K, the κe values increase across all systems due to enhanced carrier excitation, with pristine GeTe reaching peaks above 6 nW K−1, whereas doped systems remain significantly reduced in the 2 to 3 nW K−1 range. For all systems, κe reaches the maximum value at 700 K, in which pristine GeTe has a strong peak above 8.0 nW K−1, while Sb-doped systems have a lower conductance.58

Fig. 4.10(d–f) shows that the Sb doping in GeTe significantly reduces the phonon thermal conductance (κph) compared to the pure GeTe in all considered temperatures (300 K, 500 K, and 700 K) and the range of chemical potential (µ). The pristine system exhibits the highest value of κph around 0.15 nW K−1, while the 1Sb, 2Sb and 3Sb doped systems have conductance values of about 0.09 nW K−1, 0.07 nW K−1 and 0.06 nW K−1, respectively. The persistent reduction is attributed to the increased phonon scattering and lattice disorder due to the Sb atoms, which limit the phonon mean free path and inhibit the heat conduction.59 In particular, the introduction of doping modifies the phonon band structure by flattening phonon branches, lowering phonon group velocities, creating localized vibrational modes, and breaking the lattice periodicity. These effects suppress the number and magnitude of available phonon transmission channels, leading to lower phonon transmission (Fig. 7b). This suppression of lattice transmission lowers the phonon thermal conductance, which is highly favourable for thermoelectric applications. For all the systems, κph is independent of the chemical potential. These results are in agreement with theoretical models such as BAs,60 BN dimer doped GNR24 and Al/γ-Al2O3,61 confirming that the strong defect-induced phonon scattering is the dominant reason for the large reduction in the lattice thermal conductance, leading to the increase of the thermoelectric efficiency of doped GeTe.

Fig. 10(g–i) shows the total thermal conductance (κT) of pristine and Sb-doped GeTe (1Sb, 2Sb, and 3Sb doping levels) as a function of chemical potential µ at temperatures of 300, 500, and 700 K. In all three temperatures shown in each panel the pristine GeTe system (white curve) always presents the highest conductance throughout almost the entire range of µ values with peaks of about 4 nW K−1 at 300 K, 6 nW K−1 at 500 K, and about 8 nW K−1 at 700 K. For the doped systems, doping with one, two, and three Sb atoms leads to an obvious decrease in total thermal conductance. 1Sb-doped GeTe typically reaches peak values of approximately 2–3 nW K−1, 2Sb-doped remains around 1.5–2.5 nW K−1, and 3Sb-doped remains the lowest at approximately 1–2 nW K−1 across all temperatures. Within the whole range of chemical potential, all systems show a strong decrease of κT close to µ = 0 eV, followed by large peaks at more negative and more positive values. The thermal suppression effect of Sb inclusion improves conductance values and separation between pristine and doped systems as temperature rises. This suggests that Sb doping reduces GeTe's overall thermal conductance, especially at high temperatures (Table 2).

Evaluation of the thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT)

The thermoelectric efficiency of a system is determined through a figure of merit (ZT), which is a fundamental parameter26,27. The ZT of Sb-doped GeTe systems is very sensitive to doping levels, as shown in Fig. 11. The system doped with 2Sb is better than the pristine and other doped systems, achieving a high ZT value of 7.2 at 700 K (µ = 0.85 eV) due to low lattice thermal conductance (κph) and a high Seebeck coefficient. As from the band structure report, Sb doping progressively reduces the band gap of GeTe from 1.49 eV to 0.84 eV and eventually induces metallic behaviour at higher doping concentrations. However, a smaller band gap does not always mean a higher ZT. Instead, the thermoelectric performance is the balance of carrier concentration, electrical conductance and the Seebeck coefficient. The 2Sb-GeTe system shows a large band-gap narrowing but keeps its semiconducting nature, leading to an enhanced electrical conductance without significantly decreasing the Seebeck coefficient. According to the Mott relation,62,63 the Seebeck coefficient depends on the energy dependence of the electronic conductivity near the Fermi level. Further, the highest ZT obtained for the 2Sb-GeTe configuration arises from an optimal trade-off between the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductance. Although further Sb incorporation (3Sb-GeTe) increases carrier concentration and induces metallic behaviour, the accompanying reduction in the Seebeck coefficient outweighs the conductivity enhancement, resulting in a lower overall thermoelectric performance. The calculated ZT order is 2Sb (7.2) > 3Sb (6.7) > 1Sb (6.2) > pristine GeTe, which indicates that substitutional Sb doping is a viable route to optimise thermoelectric properties. The observed thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT = 7.2 at 700 K) is higher than most theoretical and experimentally reported GeTe-based materials. Table 3 presents a comparison of the computed thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) values obtained in this study with existing experimental and theoretical reports available in the literature. The state-of-the-art GeTe-based thermoelectric materials exhibit a maximum ZT value of 0.9–2.5 at 600–800 K experimentally. For instance, Y. Jin et al. reported a maximum ZT of 2.4 at 740 K by applying band-convergence engineering in GeTe,64 and Wu et al. achieved a peak ZT of around 2.3 near 773 K using defect engineering combined with multiscale phonon-scattering strategies.65 Through alloy engineering of PbTe-based systems, Perumal et al. found exceptional thermoelectric performance in GeTe-based alloys, reaching peak ZT values of roughly 2.5 at 800 K.66 However, direct comparison between experimental results and DFT-NEGF calculations should be made with caution. The NEGF approach assumes ballistic electron and phonon transport, which generally leads to higher theoretical ZT values than those observed experimentally. Therefore, the present results are more appropriately compared with previous DFT-NEGF studies. Earlier NEGF investigations reported ZT values of 2.03–2.7 for an InTe and strain-induced InTe monolayer67 and 6.2 for a GeTe/antimonene heterostructure.53 Furthermore, L. Gong et al. reported a ZT value of 7.15 at 700 K for γ-GeTe using the same DFT-NEGF methodology.25 Thus, the ZT value of ∼7.2 obtained for the 2Sb-doped GeTe monolayer should be regarded as a ballistic upper-limit estimate and suggests that Sb-doped systems are very promising for high-temperature thermoelectric applications.

Conclusion

In this work, we systematically investigate structural, electronic, and thermoelectric transport properties of the pristine and Sb-doped GeTe monolayers based on first-principles calculations combined with the DFT + NEGF approach. The electronic band structure and transport properties of the GeTe monolayer were significantly changed by different doping concentrations of Sb atoms, which led to a significant improvement in its thermoelectric performance. Our study shows that all the systems are energetically, dynamically and thermally stable, as confirmed by the formation energy analysis, phonon dispersion calculations and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. To study further its transport behaviour, two-probe device configurations were fabricated for both pristine and Sb-doped GeTe systems. The Sb-doped GeTe monolayer has low lattice thermal conductance due to the enhanced phonon scattering, lattice softening and localized vibrational modes. In particular, the 2Sb-doped GeTe system achieved the ultra-low lattice thermal conductance of 0.06 nW K−1 in combination with the very high Seebeck coefficient of −3167 µV K−1. As a result, the maximum thermoelectric figure of merit obtained was ZT = 7.2 at 700 K. Among all investigated systems, the 2Sb-doped GeTe monolayer demonstrated the best thermoelectric performance, indicating its strong potential for high-efficiency thermoelectric device applications. Overall, the present study demonstrates the effectiveness of Sb substitutional doping as a strategy to tune the transport properties and enhance the thermoelectric efficiency of two-dimensional GeTe-based materials.
