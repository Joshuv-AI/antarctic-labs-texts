Bedside transcranial Doppler as a diagnostic bridge for suspected intracranial hypertension after traumatic brain injury surgery: A case report
Authors: Muhammad Yogi Prandani, Iwan Fuadi, Radian A. Halimi, Erlangga Prasamya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Early recognition of postoperative neurologic deterioration in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) is essential but remains challenging, as clinical assessment may lack sensitivity and computed tomography (CT) imaging is not always immediately available. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) provides a noninvasive bedside method to detect early hemodynamic abnormalities, including impaired perfusion and increased vascular resistance. This case report highlights the role of TCD in recognizing early indicators of intracranial hypertension and prompting expedited CT evaluation.Case Description: A 20-year-old male presented with an acute decline in consciousness following a traffic accident. Initial CT imaging revealed an epidural hematoma, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, cerebral edema, and a temporoparietal depressed skull fracture. The patient underwent decompressive craniectomy with hematoma evacuation. On postoperative day 1, neurologic status failed to improve, accompanied by anemia and elevated lactate. Bedside TCD demonstrated an increased pulsatility index (PI 1.49) and hypoechoic–hyperechoic changes in the lateral ventricle suggestive of new hemorrhage or cerebrospinal fluid accumulation. These findings prompted an urgent repeat CT scan, which confirmed new intracranial bleeding and the need for reoperation. Following re-craniotomy and further stabilization, repeat TCD showed a reduced PI (1.09) consistent with improved hemodynamics.Conclusion: When clinical assessment is limited and CT imaging is delayed, TCD serves as a valuable bedside tool for early recognition of rising intracranial pressure or new hemorrhage. Although not a substitute for CT, TCD functions as a diagnostic bridge that accelerates clinical decision-making in critical postoperative TBI care.

INTRODUCTION

Acute neurologic deterioration in postoperative patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a life-threatening condition that requires prompt identification of the underlying cause. Such deterioration may arise from multiple etiologies, including cerebral edema, new intracranial hemorrhage, and medication-related effects resulting in increased intracranial pressure (ICP), with subsequent impairment of cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) and cerebral blood flow.[4,10]Clinical examination alone is often insufficiently sensitive, while access to neuroimaging, such as computed tomography (CT), may be delayed due to logistical constraints or patient instability. In this context, bedside monitoring modalities capable of providing real-time assessment of cerebral hemodynamics become critically important.

Transcranial Doppler (TCD) is a noninvasive, real-time, bedside, and cost-effective modality that enables evaluation of cerebral hemodynamics, monitors the development of arterial vasospasm, intracerebral hematomas, and indirect markers of increasing ICP. Parameters such as the peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, mean flow velocity, pulsatility index (PI), Lindegaard Index, and Doppler waveform patterns can provide early insight into cerebral perfusion disturbances or evolving intracranial pathology, thereby serving as a potential diagnostic bridge when radiologic imaging is not immediately available.[2] Accordingly, integrating TCD into the management of critically ill TBI patients may facilitate earlier clinical decision-making and guide anesthetic, ventilatory, and hemodynamic management, particularly in cases of unexplained postoperative neurologic deterioration.

This case report highlights the clinical value of bedside TCD for identifying early cerebral hemodynamic changes in a postoperative TBI patient, facilitating in the suspicion of intracranial hypertension and informing the decision to obtain further evaluation with CT imaging.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 20-year-old male was admitted with acute neurologic deterioration following a traffic accident approximately 6 h before presentation. There was no initial history of loss of consciousness, posttraumatic amnesia, nausea, or vomiting. However, during interfacility transfer for CT, the patient developed vomiting accompanied by a decline in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score to E4M5V4. Initial CT demonstrated a left-sided epidural hematoma, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, cerebral edema, a temporoparietal depressed skull fracture, and fracture of the left zygomaticotemporal [Figure 1]. On admission, the patient experienced a deterioration in the level of consciousness, progressing from agitation to decreased consciousness (E3M4Vx). Rapid sequence intubation was therefore performed in the resuscitation room, and the patient was subsequently mechanically ventilated. At that time, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Neurologic examination revealed anisocoria (2 mm/4 mm) with preserved bilateral pupillary light reflexes without clinical evidence of raccoon eyes or Battle’s sign. Arterial blood gas analysis showed compensated metabolic acidosis with marked hyperlactatemia (pH 7.36, HCO3 16.1, base excess −7, lactate 7.7). Chest and cervical radiographs were unremarkable. Due to progressive neurologic decline, the patient underwent emergent decompressive craniectomy with hematoma evacuation.

Initial noncontrast computed tomography imaging.

On postoperative day 1, the patient failed to show neurologic improvement, with a GCS score of E2M3Vt under sedation. Pupillary examination revealed anisocoria (2/5 mm) with sluggish bilateral light reflexes. Laboratory evaluation showed postoperative anemia (hemoglobin 9.1 g/dL) and persistent hyperlactatemia (4.8 mmol/L), whereas systemic hemodynamics and oxygenation remained stable. Given the unexplained postoperative neurologic status and the absence of immediate access to repeat neuroimaging, bedside TCD was performed to assess cerebral hemodynamics. TCD examination demonstrated an elevated PI of 1.49 in the middle cerebral artery, along with hypoechoic and hyperechoic changes in the region of the lateral ventricle, raising suspicion of increased ICP or new intracranial hemorrhage [Figure 2]. Based on these findings, an urgent cranial CT scan was obtained. CT scan demonstrated bilateral frontotemporoparietal subgaleal hematomas with pneumocephalus, along with multiple left frontotemporal intracerebral hemorrhages associated with mass effect and a midline shift of 3.1 mm [Figure 3].

(b) PI measurement at the distal segment of the MCA. (c) Hypoechoic and hyperechoic findings in the region of the left lateral ventricle on TCD examination.

Follow-up cranial CT scan. (a–b) Axial images at different levels demonstrating progressive intracerebral hemorrhage with mass effect and midline shift.

The patient subsequently underwent re-craniotomy for hematoma evacuation. Following the second surgical intervention, neurologic status improved, with a GCS score of E3M5Vt under sedation and bilaterally equal pupils (2 mm) with preserved light reflexes. Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters remained stable. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated improvement in metabolic markers, including normalization of serum lactate levels (1.3 mmol/L). Follow-up TCD examination revealed a reduction in PI to 1.09, along with decreased hypoechoic changes surrounding the lateral ventricle, consistent with improved cerebral hemodynamics [Figure 4].

Follow-up cranial CT scan. (a–b) Axial images at different levels demonstrating progressive intracerebral hemorrhage with mass effect and midline shift.

Following clinical stabilization, ventilator weaning was successfully initiated, and the patient was extubated. On subsequent evaluation, the patient remained hemodynamically stable with improved consciousness (GCS score of E4M5Vaphasia), although residual neurologic deficits, including aphasia and right-sided hemiparesis, were noted. The patient was subsequently transferred to a high-dependency unit for further care.

DISCUSSION

Postoperative neurologic deterioration in patients with TBI often progresses rapidly and may arise from multiple underlying etiologies. This case highlights how TCD can bridge the diagnostic gap when clinical assessment lacks sufficient sensitivity and access to a CT scan is limited, and how early hemodynamic findings can meaningfully influence subsequent clinical management.

Neurologic deterioration in postoperative TBI is often rapid and multifactorial, and the absence of classic clinical signs does not reliably exclude evolving intracranial pathology.[5] In sedated patients, reliance on neurologic examination alone may delay recognition of secondary brain injury, underscoring the need for adjunctive bedside tools to assess cerebral physiology. Importantly, the absence of classic clinical signs such as pupillary abnormalities or Cushing’s triad does not reliably exclude elevated ICP, particularly in sedated postoperative patients, highlighting the limitations of neurologic examination alone.[17]

Cerebrovascular ultrasonography, including TCD and transcranial color-coded duplex, provides a noninvasive bedside assessment of cerebral hemodynamics and is particularly valuable for serial monitoring in critically ill neurologic patients.[2] This modality allows differentiation between cerebral hyperemia and vasospasm through evaluation of middle cerebral artery flow velocity and the Lindegaard index.[15] Changes in PI may reflect increased distal cerebrovascular resistance related to distal vasospasm, or other conditions such as elevated cardiac output, increased intracranial arterial resistance, or increasing ICP.[11] In addition, TCD waveform morphology represents a continuum of cerebrovascular resistance, ranging from normal low-resistance patterns to high-resistance waveforms characterized by elevated PI.[6] Recognition of these waveform alterations may provide an early hemodynamic warning of secondary brain injury before overt clinical deterioration becomes apparent. In selected settings, cerebrovascular ultrasonography may demonstrate echogenic changes suggestive of intracerebral hemorrhage or newly evolving intracranial lesions and may assist in identifying midline shift as an indicator of mass effect.[16] A midline shift >5 mm is generally considered clinically significant and indicative of substantial mass effect.[13]

Increases in PI have been associated with impaired cerebral perfusion and may serve as an indirect indicator of evolving intracranial hypertension, although PI does not provide a direct measurement of ICP. Accordingly, PI should be interpreted as a dynamic trend rather than an absolute threshold, as normal values do not reliably exclude elevated ICP. Clinically, a PI >1.2 is commonly regarded as a warning sign of increased ICP or reduced CPP that warrants further evaluation, rather than as a definitive diagnostic marker, given the weak correlation between PI and ICP reported in previous studies.[7,8] In the present case, an elevated PI of 1.49 prompted expedited neuroimaging, which confirmed progressive intracranial pathology requiring surgical intervention. Following hematoma evacuation, a subsequent reduction in PI to 1.09 was consistent with radiologic and clinical improvement, underscoring the role of TCD as an early hemodynamic warning tool rather than a definitive diagnostic modality. In patients who have undergone hemicraniectomy, PI interpretation requires caution, as focal injury-related hemodynamic asymmetry, absence of the bone window, and the effects of decompression may render side-to-side measurements noninterchangeable.[8] In the present case, an increased PI triggered an expedited CT scan, underscoring the role of TCD as a diagnostic bridge rather than a replacement for definitive imaging. Subsequent urgent CT scan confirmed bilateral frontotemporoparietal subgaleal hematomas with pneumocephalus and multiple left frontotemporal intracerebral hemorrhages causing mass effect and a 3.1-mm midline shift.

TCD offers several practical advantages in the neurocritical care setting, including bedside applicability, noninvasiveness, portability, and the ability to provide real-time and repeatable assessment of cerebral hemodynamics without the risks associated with patient transport or radiation exposure.[1] These features make TCD particularly valuable when neurologic deterioration occurs, and access to a CT scan is delayed or limited. In the present case, abnormal TCD findings prompted early diagnostic escalation, leading to timely neuroimaging and surgical reintervention.

Despite these advantages, important limitations must be recognized. TCD is operator dependent, requires adequate acoustic windows, and provides limited anatomic information compared with a CT scan.[3,9,14] Furthermore, PI values may be influenced by factors unrelated to intracranial pathology and should therefore be interpreted within the broader clinical context.[12,18] Accordingly, TCD should not be considered a substitute for definitive neuroimaging but rather an adjunctive bedside tool that complements clinical assessment and structural imaging in the management of critically ill neurologic patients.

This case demonstrates that the combination of postoperative neurologic deterioration and limited access to immediate neuroimaging can render bedside ultrasonography a particularly valuable adjunct in neurocritical care. Secondary brain injury is highly time-sensitive, and delays in recognizing evolving intracranial pathology may significantly worsen neurologic outcomes. When conventional imaging, such as a CT scan, cannot be promptly obtained, TCD may provide early hemodynamic and echogenic information that supports timely diagnostic escalation. In the present case, ultrasonographic findings preceded a confirmatory CT scan and contributed to earlier recognition of evolving intracranial pathology, thereby accelerating clinical decision-making and reintervention. Importantly, TCD should not be viewed as a substitute for structural imaging but rather as a diagnostic bridge between bedside clinical assessment and definitive neuroimaging, offering dynamic cerebral perfusion information that complements other monitoring modalities in the management of critically ill postoperative TBI patients. This approach may be particularly relevant in resource-limited settings, where immediate access to repeat neuroimaging is not always feasible.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the clinical value of bedside TCD as an adjunctive tool in the evaluation of postoperative neurologic deterioration following TBI. Although it does not replace definitive imaging, TCD can function as a diagnostic bridge between bedside examination and CT scan, facilitating earlier recognition of evolving intracranial pathology and supporting timely clinical decision-making in neurocritical care settings.
