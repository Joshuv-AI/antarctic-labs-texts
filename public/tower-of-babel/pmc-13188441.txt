Effectiveness of an education intervention in reducing self- and other-directed ageism: a pre-/post- pilot study
Authors: L M O’Brien, M Mundy, N Cook, S Sabatini, R C Ambagtsheer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgeism is a serious and growing social issue experienced throughout the world. While education interventions have been shown to effectively address ageism, accessible education interventions targeted to the general public are few. Consequently, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of a freely available, short, self-directed online ageism course (‘Challenging Ageism’) in reducing self- and other-directed ageism amongst the general public. Utilising a pre- and post- intervention design, the study measured change in (1) self-directed ageism through the Brief Aging Perception Questionnaire (BAP-Q) and (2) other-directed ageism via the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (FSA). Self-selecting participants aged 18 years and over (n = 39, F = 92.3%, 51.3% aged 50 + years) were a subset of those enrolled in the course over a six-month period from July to December of 2023. Change in the outcome measures pre- and immediately post-course were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test for related samples. Significant reductions were obtained between the pre- and post-course test results for self-directed (p = .003) and other-directed (p < .001) ageism, along with several ageism sub-scale measures. While further research with long follow-ups is needed, these findings show promise for addressing ageism with scalable, broad-based solutions that are accessible to all.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04487-5.Keywords: Adult Education, Ageism, Ageing Perceptions, Short Course, Intervention

Introduction

Ageism can be defined as the pervasive and largely hidden stereotypes (i.e., thoughts), prejudice (i.e., feelings), and discrimination (i.e., actions) directed towards oneself (i.e., self-directed ageism) or others (i.e., other directed ageism) based on age [1, 2]. The consequences of ageism are wide-ranging and include exacerbation of chronic illness, depression, and anxiety [3, 4]. Although ageism can be experienced by individuals of any age [5], older people tend to be disproportionately targeted, with a survey conducted with 83,000 participants from 57 countries finding that negative stereotypes and prejudice towards older people were present in 55.5% of the gloal population and 44.2% of the European population [5]. Ageism can result in adverse health consequences for older individuals at an individual level, such as reduced healthcare access, decreased confidence in their abilities, impaired decision-making skills, and low compliance with medical interventions [6]. Furthermore, ageism can detrimentally affect the mental well-being of older individuals by causing heightened stress levels, increased anxiety, and depression which can worsen existing health issues and diminish overall life satisfaction [7]. On a societal scale, ageism may lead to the disempowerment of older adults and perpetuate disparities in the availability and standard of healthcare [6, 8]. A growing body of literature on ageism in later life underscores the importance of attending to both self-directed and other-directed forms of ageism, alongside emerging efforts to reduce their effects among older people [9].

Several theories have been advanced to explain how and why ageism develops, and how it might best be addressed within modern society. These are too numerous to cover in depth so only the most relevant will be highlighted here. Amongst the earliest of these theories is the contact hypothesis [10], which posits that prejudice and discrimination (e.g. ageism) towards a specified group stem from a lack of familiarity and knowledge, so that increased contact with the group will reduce prejudice. Much of the intergenerational work on ageism is grounded in the contact hypothesis. Later theories, such as the Stereotype Embodiment Theory (SET) and Stereotype Threat Theory (STT), seek to explain how ageism develops and comes to be internalised within individuals. SET argues that we are first exposed to negative images of older people as very young children; these images are then internalised over time, to the point that they eventually become activated (‘embodied’) within ourselves as we approach older age [11]. A complementary theory, STT, predicates the existence of age-based stereotype threats to individuals (for example, an assumption that an individual might encounter in the workplace that ageing inevitably brings cognitive decline) that once triggered, may impair subsequent cognitive or physical performance on a range of tasks [12]. Lastly, sociologists point to the role of medicalisation in creating the conditions for societal and institutional ageism. In this context, medicalisation refers to the increasing tendency to equate the concept of ageing with declining mental and physical health, positioning ageing as a medical ‘problem’ to be solved, rather than a natural and universal process [13]. While no one theory will be sufficient to explain such a complex and multifaceted problem as ageism, each has its place in tracing the potential mechanisms that lead to entrenched ageism within individuals and societies, and in illuminating potential pathways for intervention [9].

While ageism interventions are still not commonplace [14], several prior reviews have indicated that it is possible to reduce ageism [15, 16]. To date, two broad approaches to intervention have been found to be effective with respect to reducing ageism against older people specifically, these being (1) inter-generational interventions and (2) education interventions, and less commonly, a mix of the two [15]. Intergenerational programs, intentionally designed to enhance positive contact between generations (and often targeted at changing attitudes within the younger group), generally take place face-to-face and involve a structured session or sessions in which two or more generations are brought together to engage in a collaborative activity [17]. For example, a recent study by Guardabassi provides the exemplar of a summer camp intervention that significantly reduced ageist attitudes amongst primary-school-aged children, who were provided regular exposure to local older volunteers via structured activities designed to encourage intergenerational dialogue and engagement [18]. Other common intergenerational interventions include daycare programs, educational interventions, volunteer programs and recreational events [17]. By their nature, these types of interventions commonly take place face-to-face.

In contrast, educational interventions can take place either virtually or in person and usually consist of instructional experiences intended to either reduce ageist attitudes and/or enhance knowledge about ageing [15]. To date, the typical target population for education interventions has been undergraduate university students, commonly undertaking medicine or nursing courses [16], due in part to the historically high prevalence of ageism within health care settings [8]. An indicative exemplar includes a brief video intervention that reduced ageism among undergraduate students [19]. Other online interventions targeting the general public have included brief video interventions [20] and online controlled experiments testing the impact of reading explanatory texts about older adults’ relative health [21] or stereotype threat theory [22]. Educational interventions to reduce ageism have also been implemented among school students, underscoring the value of fostering awareness and challenging age-based stereotypes from a young age [23]. In comparison, there have been relatively few education interventions structured as a course, delivered wholly online and targeted to the public rather than to health care providers or students. One educational intervention implemented by Lytle and colleagues provides a notable exception [14], being targeted towards the general public and demonstrating significant reductions in ageist attitudes directed towards others pre- and post-intervention as measured by the Fraboni Ageism Scale. However, this study did not explore the impact of the intervention on the various sub-scales of the ageism outcome measure to identify which aspects of ageism are most amenable to modification.

In short, despite a growing emphasis on the imperative of addressing ageism from respected institutions such as the World Health Organization and the demonstrated modifiability of ageist attitudes through an increasing body of intergenerational and/or educational interventions, there remains a dearth of wholly online education interventions targeted to the general public. Consequently, this study aims (1) to determine the effectiveness of an accessible, short, online pilot course targeted to the general public and aimed at reducing both self- and other-directed ageism, and (2) to assess the impact on sub-scales of the ageism outcome measures.

Methods

Course design

The objectives of the Challenging Ageism short course were for participants to understand the concept of ageism, gain knowledge of the consequences of ageism for older people, and to develop a capacity to challenge ageism through a range of strategies. The course was designed at Torrens University Australia (TUA) by a gerontology researcher (RA) in consultation with Health Faculty executives (MM, NC) and the product development team (AL). The course was designed to engage participants in learning and reflection, allowing for assumptions and attitudes to ageing to be challenged, while framing the course material without bias (e.g., suggestive of right or wrong ways to age) [24, 25]. It was launched in July of 2023 and made available free of charge to the public.

The course content was structured into four modules of approximately equal length (What is Ageism?, Causes and Consequences of Ageism, Recognising Ageism in Different Settings and Challenging Ageism), as shown in Table 1. Course materials featured subject matter content drawn from the literature, pre-recorded video testimonies from older people with lived experience and a health service provider, URL links to additional relevant content, readings and one short multiple-choice style quiz. The aim was for each section to be able to be completed in approximately 15 min, although as the course was self-paced, participants wanting to extend their knowledge by viewing the additional links and information sources provided might be anticipated to take longer to complete each module. Overall, the total course was designed to be completed in approximately one to two hours. The course was not formally assessed, although an additional ‘test your knowledge’ style multiple choice quiz was provided at the end. On completion, participants were issued a completion certificate generated by the system.

Participants and setting

We aimed to recruit a convenience sample of diverse participants from amongst members of the general public aged 18 years or more who registered to undertake the Challenging Ageism course over the fifteen months from July 2023 to October 2024 (total enrollees over this period = 485). As is consistent with many courses of this type (Massive Open Online Courses or MOOCs offering a good comparison) [26], the course had a relatively low completion rate of 28.9%.

An a priori power analysis was conducted for paired pre–post comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. In the absence of prior data specific to this intervention, a moderate standardised effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) was assumed. With α = 0.05 (two-sided) and 80% power, the required sample size was estimated at 34 participants. Consistent with this estimate [27], a minimum recruitment target of 35 participants was set.

Participants self-selected to complete a pre- and post-course online survey via a link to the survey platform Qualtrics [28]. The pre-course survey and study information and consent form (PICF) were presented to potential participants on a landing page. At the end of the pre-course survey, participants were issued a unique code as proof of completion and asked to enter the code at the start of the post-course survey, which appeared as a link at the end of the course (Appendix A). Participants who completed both the pre- and post-course surveys were included in the study. All questions were made mandatory to ensure completeness of data. A prize draw was offered for a $50 shopping voucher for participants who completed both surveys and who opted in by email. Researchers selected the winner randomly and they were notified by email. Ethics approval was granted by the TUA Human Research Ethics Committee (#0259) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research, with participants giving informed consent to participate via an online form.

Ageism outcome measures

Two survey tools were selected to measure ageism levels pre‑ and post‑course: the Brief Ageing Perceptions Questionnaire (B‑APQ) and the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (FSA) [29, 30]. No additional psychological or survey measures were administered. These two instruments capture standardised ageism‑related constructs such as attitudes toward ageing, knowledge about ageing, comfort with older adults, and anxiety about one’s own ageing [31]. The primary outcomes of this study are change in self-directed ageism, as measured by the Brief Ageing Perceptions Questionnaire (B-APQ) [30] and change in other-directed ageism, as measured by the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (FSA) [29]. The B-APQ was recently found to have the strongest psychometric properties reported in a systematic review of ageing stereotype measures, with particular identified strengths that it is grounded in theory and designed to be purpose-built to measure self-perceptions of ageing, which largely overlap with self-directed ageism [32].

The B-APQ is widely applied within the field and its brevity provides ease of use for adult participants [30]. B-APQ is a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher ageism. The B-APQ includes 17 items with five sub-dimensions (i.e., Timeline Chronic, Consequences Positive, Emotional Representations, Consequences and Control Negative, Control Positive) which collect participant perceptions on coping and resilience through the ageing process [30] (Table 2). The sequence and framing of the B-APQ questions mitigates bias, an important consideration in the complex area of ageism where both perceived gains and losses relating to ageing are likely to occur [25]. Due to the nature of the questions, the six items in the Consequences-Positive and Control-Positive sub-dimensions were reverse scored [3, 33].

FSA was used to measure affective ageism towards others [34] and applied as a complement to the B-APQ. The FSA was designed for use as a complementary measure of cognitive ageist attitudes, particularly relevant in educational studies [35–37]. FSA is a 29 item, four-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher ageism [33]. The FSA items are categorised into three factors (i.e., Antilocution, Discrimination, Avoidance) [38] (Table 2). Due to the nature of the survey questions, FSA items 8, 12, 14, 21, 22, 23 and 24 were reverse scored [29].

Data collection and analysis

Participants accessed the online surveys and entered data via the Qualtrics [28] survey platform. Data were extracted into SPSS [39] once the sample quota was reached. Participant demographics collected at the baseline pre-course survey were analysed and tabled through descriptive statistics. The Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test and median quartiles were used to compare pre- and post-test data [40, 41]. Given the pre–post within-subjects design and ordinal Likert-scale outcomes, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used as the primary inferential analysis. Study findings have been presented in accordance with the Quality Assessment Tool for Pre-Post No Control Group studies (S3). Although this is not a standardised tool, it was selected for internal validity due to its specific alignment with the study’s pre-/ post-test design, including the sample group [42].

Participants

A total of 39 participants (approximately 8.0% of total enrolees in the Challenging Ageism course over the study period and 27.9% of completers) elected to complete both the pre-and post-course survey. Of the sample, the majority (92.3%) were female, with approximately half (51.3%) aged 50 years or older (Table 3). Most participants were currently employed in the workforce (85.3%) and had completed a tertiary level of education (82.1%). Six participants (15.4%) identified that they were currently working as health service providers in the health and aged care sector.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a short, online self-guided course (Challenging Ageism) on self- and other-directed ageism outcome measures, and their sub-scales, amongst a sub-set of the general public enrolled over a six-month period. Our study found significant reductions in both self- and other-directed ageism amongst adult participants as measured pre- and post-course completion. In terms of the overall reduction in ageism, as measured by the BAP-Q and FSA measures, these findings are consistent with previous research on this topic. For example, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis (n = 74 studies and 6,271 participants) confirmed that educational interventions of within-subject designs do significantly reduce ageism [15], although noted that most had been conducted with undergraduate students rather than the general public. Amongst the few studies targeted to the general public, one online education intervention employing the same ageism outcome measure (FSA) similarly found a reduction in ageist attitudes immediately post-course [13]. In another study, a one-hour course targeted to older adults within the general public and including information and video content about ageism reported reductions in ageism at one week and one month post-course [41], albeit using an alternative ageism outcome measure.

With respect to changes within the individual sub-scales of the ageism outcome measures, we found mixed results. Only certain sub-scales within the outcome measures changed significantly pre- and post-course, inclusive of the Time-line Chronic, Consequences Positive, and Emotional Representation sub-scales of the BAP-Q and the Antilocution sub-scale of the FSA. One potential reason for the observed changes in some sub-scales, but not others, is the degree of alignment with the course content. With respect to self-directed ageism (BAP-Q), certain aspects of the subject matter of the course, inclusive of the lived experience video content from older adults, emphasised positive aspects of ageing, which would potentially align with the Consequences Positive and Emotional Representation sub-scales of the BAP-Q in particular. However, the two sub-scales of the BAP-Q relating to Control remained unchanged pre- and post- intervention. It may be that while the course did not increase participants’ sense of control over the ageing process, it did positively influence how they felt about the ageing process itself, thus reducing self-directed ageism overall. Further measurement of these factors over time may be required, along with potential revision of the course content to better reflect all aspects of ageism.

Regarding other-directed ageism (FSA), much of the course content focused on being able to recognise the more subtle forms of ageism, inclusive of ageism occurring within different contexts (work, home etc.). It is possible that this focus on more ‘subtle’ forms of ageism, such as language and terminology used when talking to and about older people, had the effect of impacting Anti-locution, the FSA sub-scale most closely related to misconceptions and myths about older people [43], while arguably more overt forms of ageism (Avoidance, Discrimination) remained unchanged, due to greater public awareness about these. This is an important contribution because addressing the more subtle forms of ageism has great potential for addressing institutional and systemic ageism, e.g. for example amongst health and aged care workers. To our knowledge, most studies analysing FSA by sub-scale are cross-sectional, and so it is unclear how our finding relates to previous research on this topic. Future research should seek to analyse ageism outcome measures by sub-scale longitudinally, so that the aspects of ageism most amenable to modification can be readily identified and targeted.

Strengths and limitations

Our study had several strengths. Although this was a small pilot study and further research is needed to replicate our results, our findings provide support for the idea that it is possible to reduce ageism among adults aged 18 years and over through participation in a short, self-directed online course. Further, the online, self-directed format is highly scalable, potentially providing an efficient, accessible and effective means for reducing ageism and raising public awareness on a large scale as well as potentially forming the basis for education and training modules for health and aged care professionals. Other strengths of our study included the use of multiple standardised and validated instruments for measuring ageism, thus increasing rigour, analysis of the outcome measures by sub-scale, and the analysis of both self- and other-directed ageism. However, our study was also subject to a number of limitations. While appropriate to a course in ‘pilot’ stage, chief among these were the relatively small sample size and pre-post design. Notwithstanding the nascent stage of our course development, replication of this study, conducted with a larger sample and using a control group, is needed. In addition, evaluating the course over a longer period of follow-up would also be desirable. We acknowledge that some aspects of the study design may have inadvertently introduced elements of bias into the findings, e.g. self-selection of participants into the course, prior knowledge that the course focused on ageism, monetary incentive to participate. Further, the sample was predominantly women, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to men and to more gender-balanced adult populations. Lastly, while our ageism intervention was intended to raise awareness of ageism, only level of ageism was directly measured, rather than comprehension of ageism. Future research could explore this distinction more explicitly.

Conclusion

This study has shown that a short, online self-directed education intervention can have a significant impact on reducing self- and other-directed ageism among participants, as measured pre- and post-course completion. Further studies in a range of other contexts are needed to provide a robust evaluation of the proposed approach. However, the readily scalable nature of this intervention suggests that it, and others like it, could provide the foundations for addressing one of the key social problems of our time. The universal experience of ageing, and near-universal prevalence of ageism, appear inextricably entwined, but this association is not inevitable. It is essential to educate the public about how ageism manifests, along with its harmful consequences. By empowering individuals to recognise and confront age-based discrimination whenever it occurs, we can tackle this often unacknowledged form of prejudice and discrimination.
