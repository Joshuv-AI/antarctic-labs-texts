Timeliness of switching to second-line antiretroviral therapy following virologic failure in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania
Authors: Majid Hussein Thadeo, George Msema Bwire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTimely transition from first-line to second-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) is critical to prevent treatment failure and minimize resistance. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends switching within 90 days of confirmed virologic failure (VF). This study assessed the timeliness of switching to second-line ART in three HIV care and treatment clinics in Ilala District, Dar es Salaam.ResultsA retrospective cross-sectional study reviewed patient records from 2019 onward. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire in Kobo Toolbox, verified against patient files, and analyzed in SPSS version 29. Of 5456 patients on ART, 213 were on second-line therapy; 121 patients with complete records were included in the analysis. Most participants were female (69.4%), aged 25–34 years (31.4%), married (49.6%), self-employed (38.8%), and living with family (90.9%). Before switching, most patients had at least two viral load tests (94.3%). The most common second-line regimen was Tenofovir + Emtricitabine + Atazanavir/ritonavir (38.8%). Overall, 72.7% of patients were switched within 90 days of confirmed VF, while 27.3% experienced delays. No statistically significant associations were found between timely switching and sociodemographic or clinical factors. Conclusively, more than 25% of patients experienced delayed switching, indicating suboptimal adherence to WHO-recommended timelines in the studied clinics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07848-w.

Introduction

In Tanzania, an estimated 1.7 million people are living with HIV, and more than 1.5 million are receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1]. According to the Tanzania HIV Impact Survey (THIS 2022–2023), 97.9% of adults living with HIV who knew their status were on ART, and among those on treatment, 94.3% had achieved viral load suppression [2]. The current national first-line regimen for all age groups is Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Dolutegravir (TLD) [3]. When ART failure occurs, switching to a second-line regimen is recommended to prevent drug resistance, advanced immunosuppression, increased morbidity and mortality, and onward transmission of HIV. Second-line therapy in Tanzania typically consists of protease inhibitors [3], and the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that switching should occur within 90 days of confirmed treatment failure [4, 5].

According to the WHO [4] and Tanzania HIV treatment guidelines [3], ART failure may be classified into three categories: virological failure, defined as a viral load above 1000 copies/ml on two consecutive measurements after at least 6 months of therapy; immunological failure, defined as a CD4+ count persistently below 200 cells/mm³ despite ongoing ART; and clinical failure, defined as the recurrence of WHO stage IV conditions while on therapy. Among these, virological failure is considered the most reliable marker, as it directly reflects ongoing viral replication and the potential for developing drug resistance. In contrast, immunological and clinical criteria are less sensitive and may identify failure at later stages, often after substantial immune deterioration has already occurred [6].

Despite the clear advantages of viral load monitoring, its routine implementation in many low-resource settings, including Tanzania, remains challenging [7]. Health system constraints such as limited laboratory capacity, delays in sample transport, and shortages of reagents frequently compromise the availability and reliability of viral load testing [8]. Consequently, many patients continue on failing regimens for prolonged periods, exposing them to risks of accumulating drug resistance mutations, poor immune recovery, increased morbidity, and higher mortality [9]. These delays also undermine broader public health efforts, as patients with unsuppressed viral loads remain at higher risk of transmitting HIV to others.

Tanzania has made substantial progress in scaling up ART [1], with high treatment coverage and viral suppression rates reported at the national level. However, programmatic indicators do not reveal whether switching practices are timely and appropriate once treatment failure occurs. This issue has gained greater importance following the nationwide rollout of Dolutegravir (DTG)-based regimens in 2019. DTG offers a higher genetic barrier to resistance and improved tolerability compared to previous first-line regimens. Nevertheless, questions remain regarding how treatment failure is identified and managed in the DTG era, and whether switching to second-line therapy is implemented in accordance with WHO and national guidelines.

Currently, there is limited evidence in Tanzania on the appropriateness of switching to second-line ART, especially in the context of DTG-based first-line regimens. Without such data, it is difficult to evaluate the quality of clinical decision-making and the effectiveness of national treatment protocols in ensuring optimal patient outcomes. Addressing this evidence gap is essential, as inappropriate or delayed switching undermines the clinical benefits of ART, contributes to the development of drug resistance, and compromises progress toward epidemic control.

This study was conducted to assess the appropriateness of switching to second-line antiretroviral therapy following first-line treatment failure in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. By examining switching practices in this setting, the study aims to generate evidence that can guide improvements in clinical management, strengthen adherence to treatment guidelines, and ultimately enhance health outcomes for people living with HIV.

Methods

Study design and settings

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using patient records collected between 20th April and 20th July 2025 from three HIV Care and Treatment Clinics (CTCs) located in Ilala District, specifically within Ukonga and Gongolamboto administrative wards. Although data collection took place during this period, the study involved a retrospective review of patient records spanning a broader treatment timeline, including individuals who were enrolled in care prior to the widespread rollout of DTG-based regimens. This approach explains the predominance of efavirenz- and nevirapine-based regimens observed within the study cohort. The study was designed to reflect real-world programmatic conditions, where patients may remain on legacy regimens due to phased national transitions to DTG-based therapy, clinical considerations, or individual treatment histories.

Tanzania adopted TLD as the recommended first-line regimen in 2019, which provided the clinical framework for this investigation [3]. The study population was drawn from the Dar es Salaam Urban Cohort Study (DUCS), a well-established longitudinal cohort that provides care and treatment to over 7000 individuals living with HIV [10]. DUCS operates six CTCs across Ilala District, with facilities in Ukonga and Gongolamboto wards serving as key points of access for HIV management.

Study population

The study population consisted of individuals living with HIV who were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). This group was selected to provide insight into treatment outcomes among patients managed with first-line regimens in routine programmatic settings. Participants included in the study were HIV-infected patients with a confirmed first-line ART failure. Additionally, only patients residing in Dar es Salaam and actively attending HIV clinics in the study area were considered eligible. Patients were excluded if they were below the age of 18 years. Furthermore, individuals who had been on ART for less than 1 year at the time of data collection were not considered eligible for inclusion. Given the limited number of eligible patients, a total population sampling approach was employed. The study considered all HIV-infected individuals who had been switched to second-line antiretroviral therapy within the selected clinics. Data collection process

A structured data extraction questionnaire was utilized to systematically obtain information from patient medical records. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study, drawing on established data elements routinely captured in HIV care and treatment services in Tanzania. Its development was informed by researcher experience in HIV program monitoring, national HIV treatment guidelines [3], and standardized facility reporting forms, ensuring that all variables relevant to the assessment of switching timelines and clinical management were included. Data were collected using Kobo Toolbox (Harvard Humanitarian Initiative, Cambridge, MA, USA), which served as the primary data collection tool. The questionnaire was designed to capture both background and clinical information, and data were obtained through a review of patient records from both electronic databases and physical files. Each patient’s information was extracted and entered into the questionnaire until data collection was completed.

The questionnaire was divided into four parts. The first part gathered background and sociodemographic information, including patient identification, age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, district of residence, and living situation. The second part focused on adherence and treatment monitoring, capturing the date of HIV diagnosis, date of initiation of first-line ART, type and duration of first-line therapy, number of viral load tests prior to treatment switch, and whether the switch to second-line ART occurred within 90 days (Supplementary File 1). After data collection, the completed questionnaires were exported from Kobo Toolbox into Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) for preliminary organization. The dataset was then imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for cleaning, management, and statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, and measures of central tendency, were used to summarize the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Logistic regression analysis was employed to determine factors associated with timely switching to second-line antiretroviral therapy (defined as within 90 days of confirmed first-line ART failure). Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All independent variables were included in the multivariate regression model regardless of their statistical significance in univariate testing, in order to control for potential confounding. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Patient recruitment and sample derivation

A total of 5456 HIV patient records from the three selected care and treatment clinics were reviewed. Of these, 1523 files were excluded due to ineligibility: 198 patients were under 18 years of age, 1298 had been on ART for less than 1 year, and 27 had died. This left 3933 eligible patient files, of which 3720 were not studied as they remained on first-line ART. A total of 213 files belonged to patients on second-line ART regimens and were therefore considered for review. Among these, 35 were excluded because the patients had been referred to the clinics already on second-line ART without sufficient prior data, and 57 were excluded due to incomplete sociodemographic or clinical information. The final analytical sample comprised 121 patients. Of these, 88 had been appropriately switched to second-line therapy, while 33 had not (Fig. 1).

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 121 patients on second-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) were included in the study. The majority were female (69.4%, n = 84), while males accounted for 30.6% (n = 37). The predominant age groups were 25–34 years (31.4%, n = 38) and 35–44 years (32.2%, n = 39), representing 63.6% of the study population. Only 3.3% (n = 4) of patients were aged 18–24 years. Most participants resided in Ilala District (70.2%, n = 85), and the remainder were from Temeke (29.8%, n = 36). With respect to living arrangements, the majority (90.9%, n = 110) lived with family, whereas 9.1% (n = 11) lived alone. Marital status distribution showed that 49.6% (n = 60) were married, 24.8% (n = 30) were single, and 14.9% (n = 18) were divorced, while the remainder were separated (5.8%, n = 7) or widowed (5.0%, n = 6). Employment status indicated that 38.8% (n = 47) were self-employed, 33.1% (n = 40) were formally employed, 25.6% (n = 31) were unemployed, and 2.5% (n = 3) were students (Table 1).

In terms of treatment monitoring, 57.9% (n = 70) of patients had undergone three viral load (VL) tests prior to switching, 36.4% (n = 44) had two VL tests, and a small proportion had either one or unspecified tests (5.8%). For second-line regimens, the most frequently used combinations were Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate + Emtricitabine + Atazanavir/ritonavir (TDF + FTC + ATV/r, 38.8%) and Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate + Emtricitabine + Lopinavir/ritonavir (TDF + FTC + LPV/r, 19.8%). Additional regimens included Abacavir + Emtricitabine + Atazanavir/ritonavir (ABC + FTC + ATV/r, 14%), Abacavir + Didanosine + Lopinavir/ritonavir (ABC +3TC + LPV/r, 10.7%), Abacavir + Emtricitabine + Lopinavir/ritonavir (ABC + FTC + LPV/r, 8.3%), and other second-line adult regimens (8.3%).

Factors associated with appropriate switching

Multivariate regression analysis did not reveal any statistically significant associations between sociodemographic or clinical characteristics and switching to second-line ART within 90 days (all p-values > 0.05). However, notable trends were observed. Younger patients (18–34 years) and those aged 45–54 years exhibited higher adjusted odds ratios (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] range: 2.1–6.5) compared with those older than 54 years, though the associations were not significant due to wide confidence intervals. Marital status analysis suggested that separated patients were less likely to be switched within 90 days (aOR 0.148, 95% CI 0.011–1.955), but again the result was not significant. Employment status, residence, living arrangement, number of viral load tests, and specific first- or second-line ART regimens did not show significant associations with timely switching.

Discussion

This study assessed the appropriateness of initiating second-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) following confirmed first-line treatment failure in three HIV care centers in Ilala District, Dar es Salaam. The primary focus was adherence to the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline recommending a switch within 90 days of confirmed virologic failure. Our findings showed that while the majority of patients (72.7%) were transitioned to second-line therapy within the recommended timeframe, more than one-quarter (27.3%) experienced delays, indicating incomplete adherence to national and international protocols [4, 5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) early warning indicators (EWIs), timely switching defined as initiation of a second-line regimen within 90 days of confirmed virologic failure is essential for maintaining treatment efficacy and preventing the emergence of drug resistance [4]. Performance below 80% is classified in the red category, indicating poor adherence to this quality standard [5, 11–13].

This study assessed the appropriateness of initiating second-line ART following confirmed first-line treatment failure in three HIV care centers in Ilala District, Dar es Salaam, with a focus on adherence to WHO guidelines recommending a switch within 90 days of virologic failure [4]. The findings showed that although the majority of patients (72.7%) were transitioned to second-line ART within the recommended timeframe, a considerable proportion (27.3%) experienced delays beyond 90 days. These results highlight incomplete adherence to national and international treatment protocols and underscore persistent gaps in programmatic implementation.

These findings are consistent with evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the incidence of switching to second-line ART was very low, at 2.65 per 100 person-years, with programmatic barriers rather than individual patient-level factors being the primary drivers of delayed switching [14]. Similarly, a Ugandan cohort study demonstrated substantial delays, with only 30.2%, 44.6%, and 65.0% of patients transitioned to second-line therapy at 6, 12, and 24 months following virologic failure, respectively. These delays were associated with increased morbidity and mortality [9].

Despite patients in this study undergoing enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) and multiple viral load assessments, delays in transitioning to second-line therapy persisted. Similar barriers have been described in Tanzania and other Sub-Saharan African settings. Provider hesitancy and inconsistent adherence to national guidelines have been identified as key obstacles to timely switching [15]. In addition, health system constraints, including documentation inefficiencies, limited ART stock, and prolonged turnaround times for viral load results, further contribute to delays. A study among Tanzanian clinicians also highlighted inadequate drug availability, limited laboratory support, and uncertainty in clinical decision-making as major barriers to switching patients to second-line regimens [16]. Collectively, these findings indicate that systemic and programmatic factors, rather than patient characteristics, remain central to explaining suboptimal adherence to WHO-recommended switching protocols.

The analysis also explored whether patient sociodemographic factors influenced the likelihood of timely switching. Logistic regression demonstrated that none of the variables, including age, sex, employment status, marital status, residence, or living arrangement were statistically significant predictors of switching within 90 days. Although older patients (≥ 45 years) appeared more likely to switch on time and males slightly less so, these trends did not achieve statistical significance.

These findings emphasize that timely ART switching is not primarily determined by patient demographics but is more likely shaped by systemic and facility-level factors, such as provider practices, decision-making variability, availability of second-line regimens, and adherence to clinical protocols. The wide confidence intervals and implausible odds ratios observed for some variables (e.g., employment status) may reflect limited subgroup sizes and potential model instability, highlighting the need for larger sample sizes or stratified analyses in future research.

The delayed switching observed in over one-quarter of patients raises critical concerns, as prolonged exposure to failing regimens allows for ongoing viral replication, increased risk of disease progression, and accumulation of drug resistance. Improving adherence to WHO-recommended switching timelines requires a multifaceted approach, including strengthening provider training, improving real-time clinical decision support, and reinforcing monitoring systems during and after EAC.

Virologic failure on DTG-based regimens is relatively uncommon and is more often associated with adherence challenges rather than the emergence of resistance [17–19]. Emerging evidence, including findings from the NADIA trial, suggests that treatment strategies following virologic failure are evolving, particularly in settings where resistance testing is limited [20]. In this context, our findings primarily reflect programmatic challenges related to delayed switching in resource-limited settings, including constraints in viral load monitoring and clinical decision-making [10]. These challenges were most pronounced during the period when NNRTI-based regimens were the standard of care [21]. The clinical implications of delayed switching may differ in the DTG era, given the higher genetic barrier to resistance and improved regimen durability [22, 23]. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as providing operational and historical insights during the transition to DTG-based treatment, and their applicability to current dolutegravir-based treatment paradigms should be interpreted with caution.

Future interventions and research should also incorporate provider- and system-level data, including ART stock monitoring, turnaround time for viral load results, health information systems, and clearer accountability for switching decisions. Addressing these operational bottlenecks is essential for optimizing HIV treatment outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

This study relied on routinely collected health-facility records, which may contain incomplete or inaccurate information. Such data quality issues could have affected correct classification of switching timelines. The analysis included only adults aged ≥ 18 years who had been on ART for more than 1 year. This excluded pediatric patients and individuals recently initiated on ART, who may exhibit different patterns and determinants of switching. The retrospective nature of the study limited the ability to assess important individual-level factors, such as adherence behaviors, psychosocial barriers, and provider–patient decision-making processes, all of which may influence switching to second-line ART.

The relatively small sample size (N = 121) reduced statistical power and contributed to wide confidence intervals for some predictors, limiting precision in estimating associations. The study was conducted in only three high-volume clinics within Ilala District. This restricted geographic scope may limit generalizability of the findings to other districts, regions, or health system contexts in Tanzania. Logistic regression was applied using odds ratios as the primary measure of association. Given the relatively common outcome in this cohort (72.7% switched within 90 days), odds ratios may overestimate the magnitude of associations compared with risk ratios. Moreover, switching decisions may have been influenced by factors other than virologic failure, such as drug toxicity, regimen transitions, or other clinical considerations. This may limit the ability to attribute regimen changes solely to treatment failure and could introduce potential misclassification bias.

Conclusions

Most patients with confirmed first-line ART failure were switched to second-line regimens within 90 days; however, more than one-quarter remained on failing regimens beyond this period, placing performance in the WHO red (poor) category for early warning indicators. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were not associated with timely switching, suggesting that delays could be linked to health system and programmatic factors such as laboratory result turnaround, documentation processes, and drug availability.
