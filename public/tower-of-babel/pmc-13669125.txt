Stroke burden and associated predictors: A methodological reflection
Authors: Aria Hosseini, Rayeheh Bozorgi, Amir Molavi zad, Kosim Rakhmanov, Guloyim Avezova, Shukhrat Teshayev
Published: 2026

To the Editor

We read with interest the article by Iddrisu and Mohammed on stroke burden in Ghana [1]. While the study addresses an important public health issue, several methodological considerations warrant attention: the justification for the Bayesian approach, reliance on self-reported stroke, interpretation of education as a risk factor, absence of prior sensitivity analysis, and potential selection bias in the physical activity finding. We offer these reflections constructively to strengthen future research.

Bayesian model for a non-existent clustering problem

The authors employed a Bayesian multilevel logistic regression to address potential cluster-level heterogeneity. However, their own estimated ICC was essentially zero (0.0001; 95% CrI: 0.0000–0.0005), indicating that virtually all variability in stroke risk resides at the individual level.

This raises a fundamental question: if the ICC is practically zero, what justifies the analytical complexity of a Bayesian multilevel model? Simulation studies have shown that the potential for error from ignoring clustering is greatest when the ICC exceeds 0.01 [2]. With an ICC of 0.0001, 100 times smaller than this threshold, there is little evidence that failing to account for clustering would meaningfully distort inferences. While Bayesian multilevel models can improve predictive ability and provide more precise probability intervals when genuine clustering exists, their advantages depend on the presence of meaningful group-level variation [3]. In the absence of such variation, the added complexity may be unnecessary. The authors themselves acknowledge that both models produced largely consistent findings, raising the question of whether the Bayesian model offers truly more stable and precise estimates or is simply a computationally expensive confirmation of what a simpler, more transparent model could have shown.

Self-reported stroke

Another concern relates to the reliance on self-reported stroke status as the primary outcome. Participants were simply asked whether they had ever been told they had a stroke. This approach carries a well-documented risk of misclassification, particularly in settings where diagnostic confirmation is limited. A systematic review examining the accuracy of patient self-report for stroke found that in population-based studies with low stroke prevalence, such as the 2.37% observed here, a substantial portion of self-reported cases may be false positives [4]. This raises the possibility that some of the 120 reported strokes in this sample could be inaccurate, potentially biasing the estimates of prevalence and the associations with risk factors.

The concern is further supported by a validation study conducted within the Korean Health Examinees Study, which evaluated self-reported stroke against medical records [5]. The positive predictive value (PPV) of self-report was only 51.4%, meaning that nearly half of the participants who reported a stroke could not be confirmed as definite cases upon record review. This substantial rate of false-positive reporting weakens the reliability of the outcome measure and, consequently, the validity of the predictors identified. Without clinical validation, the findings may represent associations with the report of a stroke rather than with a biologically confirmed stroke event [4, 5]. Future studies would benefit from supplementing self-reports with medical record verification or diagnostic data to improve outcome accuracy.

Education as a risk factor

The positive association between education and stroke (aOR = 1.48) is unexpected, as higher education is typically protective in high-income settings [6]. The authors attribute this to better health awareness and diagnostic access among educated individuals. However, this interpretation presents a conceptual tension. A more plausible explanation is diagnostic bias: educated individuals may have greater opportunities to seek care, receive a formal diagnosis, and recall it when surveyed. In resource-limited settings, stroke often remains undiagnosed among those with limited healthcare access [6]. The positive education–stroke association may therefore reflect differential case capture across socioeconomic groups rather than a causal effect. Distinguishing true risk from diagnostic bias is essential for both epidemiological inference and health policy.

The missing sensitivity analysis

The authors employed weakly informative Gaussian priors (mean = 0, variance = 1000) for fixed effects and log-gamma hyperpriors for the random intercept variance, yet provided no assessment of how these choices influenced their results. This omission matters because prior specification can substantially affect Bayesian estimates, particularly in models with rare outcomes or limited cluster-level information.

A systematic survey found that sensitivity analyses for priors are frequently underused, with only 27.0% of Bayesian trials conducting them [7]. Furthermore, methodological research has shown that interval estimates can be more sensitive to prior choice than point estimates [8]. This challenges the authors’ claim of more precise estimates from the Bayesian model. Without sensitivity analyses, the precision of the estimates cannot be reliably attributed to the data rather than analytical choices. We suggest that future Bayesian studies routinely include sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of findings to prior specifications.

Physical activity

The observed protective effect of vigorous physical activity against stroke is consistent with findings from a large US population-based study, which reported a similar 40% reduction in stroke odds (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.5–0.8) [9]. However, a key question remains: does this association reflect a genuine biological benefit, or is it partly driven by selection bias?

The healthy worker survivor effect describes how individuals who remain in physically demanding jobs or activities tend to be healthier than those who leave them due to illness [10]. This selection process can attenuate or even reverse estimated effects in occupational and lifestyle studies when not properly addressed. Although cross-sectional analyses cannot fully resolve this issue, the consistency of the protective association across multiple studies, including the present one, suggests that physical activity confers a genuine benefit. Nevertheless, we suggest that future research employing objective physical activity measures or longitudinal designs could help distinguish the biological protective effect from selection bias.

Conclusion

While this study provides valuable insights into stroke burden in Ghana, several methodological considerations warrant attention. The near-zero ICC questions the necessity of the Bayesian multilevel approach. Reliance on self-reported stroke introduces potential misclassification bias. The education-stroke association may reflect diagnostic bias rather than true risk. The absence of prior sensitivity analysis undermines claims of model precision. Finally, the protective effect of physical activity cannot be fully disentangled from selection bias without objective measures. Addressing these considerations would strengthen future stroke research in resource-limited settings.
