Layered automata: A canonical model for automata over infinite words
Antonio Casares, Christof Löding, Igor Walukiewicz

Abstract
We introduce layered automata, a subclass of alternating parity automata that generalises deterministic automata. Assuming a consistency property, these automata are history deterministic and 0-1 probabilistic. We show that every omega-regular language is recognised by a unique minimal consistent layered automaton, and that this canonical form can be computed in polynomial time from every layered or deterministic automaton. We further establish that for layered automata both consistency checking and inclusion testing can be performed in polynomial time. Much like deterministic finite automata, minimal consistent layered automata admit a characterisation based on congruences.

Introduction

Automata over infinite words (
\omega
-automata in the following) were first introduced by Büchi to show the decidability of monadic second-order logic over
(\mathbb{N},<)

[
11
]
.
Since then, they have found numerous applications in the verification and synthesis of reactive systems
[
17
,
43
,
36
]
.

A key limitation of current
\omega
-automata theory is the lack of minimal canonical
models. Minimisation is instrumental for practical applications, particularly
when automata are derived from specifications, like LTL formulas, through
hierarchical translations.
In this process, each constructor in the
specification corresponds to an operation on the automaton — some of which
necessitate a powerset construction.
We know from the case of finite words and trees that minimisation after each operation
is key to efficient translations
[
32
]
.
Minimisation is also important for theory.
One immediate example is learning algorithms for
\omega
-regular languages, but
more broadly, it indicates that we have gained insights into the structural
properties of acceptors.
Such insights may eventually allow us to
revisit long-standing open problems, such as the index problem for tree automata
[
18
,
31
]
.

Unlike to the case of automata over finite words, deterministic

\omega
-automata do not have minimal canonical models, as a language may admit several
non-isomorphic deterministic automata with a minimal number of states. Moreover,
the minimisation problem is NP-hard for all kinds of deterministic

\omega
-automata expressive enough to recognise all
\omega
-regular
languages
[
44
,
15
,
1
]
.
While there are some canonical ways to represent
\omega
-regular languages,
such as
\omega
-semigroups or congruences, these representations can be potentially exponentially
larger than
deterministic parity automata
, and furthermore, they cannot be used directly in applications from verification and synthesis.

History deterministic
(HD) automata are a subclass of non-deterministic automata for which non-determinism can be resolved on the fly. They were originally introduced under the name of
good for games

[
28
]
1
1

1

The related notion of
good for trees automata
was previously introduced in
[
35
]
.
, as they are exactly those automata that can be composed with any game while preserving the winner. This property allows their use in applications in verification and synthesis instead of deterministic automata. Every
deterministic automaton
is
history deterministic
, but a
history deterministic
automaton can be exponentially smaller than the smallest deterministic one
[
34
]
.
History determinism
has been generalised to
alternating
\omega
-automata

[
19
,
10
]
. History deterministic alternating automata have the same good compositionality properties, making them suitable for applications in verification and synthesis, while being even smaller than non-deterministic HD automata
[
9
, Lemma 6]
.

Other types of automata that have been actively studied recently are those in which the non-determinism can be resolved randomly
[
6
,
29
,
41
]
, or automata that compose faithfully with MDPs
[
27
]
. In particular,
0-1 probabilistic automata
are automata in which a random walk over an infinite word is accepting with probability
1
if and only if the word belongs to the semantics of the automaton; otherwise, it is rejecting with probability
1
.
Such automata are in particular suitable for the study of MDPs
[
29
]
.

In this work, we propose a new model of acceptors for
\omega
-regular languages
that we call
layered automata
.
Our starting point for this model is the result of Abu Radi and
Kupferman
[
2
]
stating that (transition-based)
history deterministic

coBüchi automata
can be minimised in polynomial time and admit a canonical minimal representation. In
[
37
]
it has furthermore been shown that this canonical automaton can be described in a natural way using a congruence over pairs of finite words.
However,
HD

coBüchi automata
can only recognise a fragment of
\omega
-regular languages
: those that are in the
\Sigma_{2}^{0}
level in the Borel hierarchy, or equivalently, persistent properties in the Manna-Pnueli hierarchy
[
39
]
.

The model of
layered automata
that we propose in this work can be seen as an extension of the minimisation result of Abu Radi and
Kupferman to all
\omega
-regular languages
.
This is in line with some recent works such as
chains of coBüchi
automata

[
23
]
and
rerailing
automata

[
24
]
to which we compare in Section
3.6
.
Orthogonally,
layered automata
can also be seen as a generalisation of the Zielonka tree of a Muller language
[
47
]
and of signature automata for positional languages
[
14
]
.
We provide ample evidence that layered automata have good structural and
algorithmic properties.

Contributions

We introduce
layered automata
, a formalism to represent all

\omega
-regular languages.
One can think of a
layered automaton
as a representation of an
alternating
parity automaton
with some good structural properties.
If a layered automaton satisfies an additional property of
consistency
,
then the associated alternating automaton is
history deterministic
.

Consistent

layered automata
combine the structure of both minimal
history deterministic

coBüchi automata
(Proposition
11
) and Zielonka trees. They also encompass
deterministic
parity automata (Proposition
10
).
They enjoy remarkably good properties. Most notably, they admit a canonical minimal form that is computable in polynomial time.

This canonicity result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Every
\omega
-regular language

L
can be recognised by a
consistent

layered automaton

\mathcal{A}_{L}
such that every equivalent
consistent

layered automaton
contains a
subautomaton
admitting a surjective
morphism
to
\mathcal{A}_{L}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{A}_{L}
can be computed in polynomial time from any given
consistent

layered automaton
recognising
L
.

We detail further properties of
layered automata
.

Applicability.

Consistent

layered automata
are representations of alternating parity
automata that are guaranteed to be both
history deterministic
and
0-1 probabilistic
(Propositions
21
and
22
).
Therefore,
consistent

layered automata
have the key properties that make them suitable for applications in verification and synthesis.
Moreover, they provide an easy-to-check sufficient condition for
HDness
and being
0-1 probabilistic
, as
layered automata
are defined syntactically and checking
consistency
can be done in polynomial time (Proposition
28
).

As
layered automata
generalise minimal coBüchi automata, they can be exponentially smaller than minimal
deterministic parity automata

[
34
]
.

We also show that emptiness and inclusion of
consistent

layered automata
can be done in polynomial time (Propositions
29
and
30
).

Canonicity, morphisms, and congruences.

We show that every
\omega
-regular language
can be recognised by a unique minimal
consistent

layered automaton
.
More precisely, we show that for every
\omega
-regular language

L
there is a
consistent

layered automaton

\mathcal{A}_{L}
that is
normal
,
centralised
and
safe minimal
and any
layered automaton
with these three properties is
isomorphic
to
\mathcal{A}_{L}
(Theorem
33
).
Moreover, every
consistent

layered automaton

recognising

L
contains a
subautomaton
that admits a surjective
morphism
to
\mathcal{A}_{L}
(Corollary
43
). Furthermore, we provide a characterisation in terms of congruences for the minimal
layered automaton

\mathcal{A}_{L}
(Theorem
72
).
This characterisation is purely based on the language
L
, and does not depend on a given representation as an automaton.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results of this type for an
automaton model capable of recognising all
\omega
-regular languages and having the
minimal form never bigger than
deterministic parity automata
.

Minimisation in PTIME.

We show that given a
consistent

layered automaton

\mathcal{A}
, we can construct in polynomial time the minimal
layered automaton
for
L(\mathcal{A})
(Theorem
42
). Since every
deterministic parity automaton
is trivially a
consistent

layered automaton
, this provides an efficient minimisation procedure for
deterministic parity automata
into
layered automata
. This does not contradict the above-mentioned lower bounds on minimisation, as the result of the procedure is an alternating automaton.

Relation to other models.

We provide examples and results comparing and relating
layered automata
to
other models, such as chains of coBüchi automata and Zielonka trees. We also
discuss how
layered automata
can be used to characterise important properties
of
\omega
-regular languages, such as positionality,
following
[
14
]
. While we leave a more detailed study of
all these relations as future work, our discussion shows that
layered automata

have many interesting connections to various other well-established and more
recent formalisms from the theory of
\omega
-regular languages.

Related work

The starting point of our work is the minimisation procedure for
HD

coBüchi automata
from Abu
Radi and Kupferman
[
2
]
. By duality, this entails a minimisation procedure for universal
HD
Büchi automata, and the notion of
layered automata
can be seen as
a natural extension to arbitrary parity indices that mixes nondeterministic and universal branching.

Layered automata
are also inspired by
signature and
\varepsilon
-complete automata
from
[
14
, Sect 3.3]
, as well as the minimisation
procedure for them.
The proof that all
layered automata
are
history deterministic
generalises ideas from
[
34
, Lemma 3]
.
The congruence-based characterisation is an extension of the congruences for
coBüchi automata
from
[
37
, Sect 3]
, using conditions closely related to the priority mapping on finite words defined in
[
8
, Def 3.1,3.5]
.

Various representations of
\omega
-regular languages
admitting–in some sense–canonical models exist in the literature.
These include some algebraic approaches based on congruences, such as Arnold’s
congruence
[
5
]
, Wilke-algebras and

\omega
-semigroups
[
46
,
42
]
,
families of right congruences
[
38
, Def. 5]
and families
of DFAs
[
33
,
4
]
. These objects have the
disadvantage that they can be exponentially larger than deterministic parity
automata (it is folklore that already for regular languages of finite words, the
syntactic monoid can be of size
n^{n}
for the minimal DFA being of size
n
, see

[
30
, Thm. 3]
for a concrete example; this exponential gap transfers
to all the aforementioned formalisms).
While their size is incomparable to the size of a smallest
DPA
for a language,
they cannot directly be used in applications like synthesis.

Another representation, taking the form of a specific
deterministic parity automaton
(DPA), is proposed in
[
8
, Sect. 3]
.
There, the “precise DPA” of a language is defined as a product of deterministic Mealy
machines that are extracted directly from the language in terms of a coloring
function. However, the precise DPA can be exponentially larger than a smallest
DPA
.

More recently, some canonical representations based on
HD
automata have been introduced.
In 2022, Ehlers and Schewe proposed to represent an
\omega
-regular language
as a decreasing chain of coBüchi-recognisable languages
[
23
]
.
Each of these languages can be given by the minimal
HD

coBüchi automaton
from Abu Radi and Kupferman, leading to a canonical representation. This formalism has subsequently been studied under the name
COCOA

[
22
,
21
]
.
Minimal
layered automata
are clearly related to COCOA, but there are some important differences.
A key difference is that
layered automata
bring a direct connection with
alternating

history deterministic
automata–which are widely accepted as the class of
\omega
-automata suitable for verification and synthesis.
Notably, we conjecture that canonical
layered automata
are minimal amongst all
history deterministic

alternating parity automata
(Conjecture
78
). The size comparison between both models is a bit subtle.
While COCOA can be smaller than
layered automata
, in order to obtain an
HD
automaton from a COCOA, one needs to perform a product of all its components
[
22
]
.
We give a more detailed comparison with COCOA in Section
3.6
.

In 2025, Ehlers introduced
rerailing automata

[
24
, Def. 1]
and showed that they can be minimised efficiently.
These are automata where each run is associated with a
priority
(the minimal appearing infinitely often), and a word is accepted if the maximal priority of its runs is even. Moreover, they must satisfy a semantic “rerailing property”, inspired by the properties of minimal
HD

coBüchi automata
.
In
[
24
, Sect. VI]
, rerailing automata are shown to be usable in reactive synthesis, but their exact relation with
HD
automata is not yet established.

Preliminaries

We let
[d]=\{1,\dots,d\}
, and we call the elements of
[d]

priorities
. We use the variables
x,y
to denote
priorities
.
The
parity condition
is the language over
[d]^{\omega}
given by:

\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.6484,0.1094,0.1211}\mathsf{Parity}}=\{x_{1}x_{2}\dots\in[d]^{\omega}\mid\liminf x_{i}\text{ is even}\}.\;\text{ (Note the use of min-parity in this paper.)}

Transition systems.

A
transition system
over a set
\Sigma
is an edge-labelled directed
graph
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Delta)
, where
\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
.
If
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
, we also write
q\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
.
It is
complete
if for all
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
, there is some
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
.
It is
deterministic
if for every
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
there is at
most one
q^{\prime}
such that
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
.
We prefer to represent the transitions of deterministic
transition systems

by a function
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to Q\cup\{\bot\}
, where

\delta(q,a)=q^{\prime}
if
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
, and
\delta(q,a)=\bot
if no such
transition exists.

For a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we write
q\xrightarrow{w}q^{\prime}
if there is a
path from
q
to
q^{\prime}
whose label is
w
, which we call a
run
.
We sometimes omit
q^{\prime}
, writing just
q\xrightarrow{w}
, if
q^{\prime}
is irrelevant.
We write
q\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}}\bot
it there is no path with label
w

from
q
.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a
transition system
over a product alphabet
\Sigma\times[d]
, by an abuse of notation we call a
run over
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
any path having
w
as labels in the
\Sigma
-component.
In this case, we also say that a run
is accepting

if its sequence of
[d]
-labels satisfies the
parity condition
.
We write
q\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
if
(q,(a,x),q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
, and
q\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:\geq x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
if
(q,(a,y),q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
for some
y\geq x
, similarly for
>x
, etc.
We write
q\xrightarrow{w:\geq x}q^{\prime}
if the minimal priority over the path is
\geq x
, similarly for
>x
, etc.

A
morphism
between two
transition systems

\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Delta)
,

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
over the same set
\Sigma
is a function
\mu\colon Q\to Q^{\prime}

such that if
(p,a,q)\in\Delta
then
(\mu(p),a,\mu(q))\in\Delta^{\prime}
.
Note that any
run
over
w
in
\mathcal{T}
from
q
maps to a
run
over
w
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
from
\mu(q)
.

Deterministic parity automata.

A
deterministic parity automaton
is a
complete

deterministic

transition system

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Delta)
over the
set
\Sigma\times[d]
with a designated initial state
q_{\mathsf{init}}\in Q
.

A word
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
is accepted by the automaton if the unique run over
w
satisfies the
parity condition
.
The language of the automaton is the set of words it accepts.
A language is
\omega
-regular
if it can be recognised by a
deterministic parity automaton
.
The class of
\omega
-regular
languages admits many equivalent definitions,
for instance, through definability in
\mathrm{MSO}
logic, or recognition by
non-deterministic Büchi automata, see for instance
[
26
]
.

Alternating parity automata.

In full generality, an
alternating parity automaton
is given by a set of states together with a transition function of the form
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to\mathsf{Bool}^{+}(Q\times[d])
.
In this work, all alternating automata are of a simpler form, which we call
simple by priorities
alternating automata
. These correspond to automata where all transitions are either

\delta(q,a)=\bigwedge_{p\in Q^{\prime}}(p,x)\;\text{ for some even }x,\;\;\text{ or }\;\;\delta(q,a)=\bigvee_{p\in Q^{\prime}}(p,x)\;\text{ for some odd }x.

We refer to
[
10
]
for definitions in the general case.
We give next a formal, alternative presentation of the subclass of
simple by priorities
alternating automata.

A
simple by priorities
automaton is a
complete

transition system

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Delta)

over the set
\Sigma\times[d]
with a designated
initial state

q_{\mathsf{init}}\in Q
,
satisfying that for every
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma

all
a
-transitions from
q
have the same
priority
.
So there is
x
a
priority of action
a
in
q
, meaning that for all

(q,(a,y),q^{\prime})\in\Delta
we have
y=x
.

The semantics of a
simple by priorities
automaton is defined via game where
the
priority of an action
determines who makes the move. Given
an input word
w=a_{1}a_{2}\dots\in\Sigma^{\omega}
, we define the game
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A},w)

where two players, called Eve and Adam, build a
run
over
w
in
\mathcal{A}
as follows:

•

The initial position is the state
q_{1}=q_{\mathsf{init}}
.

•

Suppose the current run constructed in a play ends in a state
q_{i}
,
and
x_{i}
is the priority of
a_{i}
in
q_{i}
.
If
x_{i}
is odd then Eve picks a transition
q_{i}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i}:x_{i}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q_{i+1}
,
otherwise Adam makes the choice.

•

Eve wins if the constructed path is
accepting
.

We say that
w
is
accepted
by
\mathcal{A}
if Eve has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A},w)
.
Note that, by determinacy of parity games
[
40
]
, on the contrary Adam has a winning strategy.
The
language recognised
by
\mathcal{A}
, written
\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.6484,0.1094,0.1211}L}(\mathcal{A})
, is the set of words it
accepts
.
The
language of a state

q\in Q
is the language recognised by the
automaton obtained by fixing
q
as the
initial state
. We denote it

L(\mathcal{A},q)
, or just
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L}(q)
if
\mathcal{A}
is clear from the context.

Semantic determinism.

An automaton
\mathcal{A}
is
semantically deterministic
if whenever we have

p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q_{1}
and
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q_{2}
then
L(\mathcal{A},q_{1})=L(\mathcal{A},q_{2})
. Equivalently, if

p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
then
L(\mathcal{A},q)=a^{-1}L(\mathcal{A},p)
.

Remark 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
semantically deterministic
automaton
recognising
a language
L
. Let
u
be a word labelling a path from the initial state to
q
. Then, the
language
of
q
is:

u^{-1}L=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid uw\in L\}.

Resolvers and history determinism.

Consider a
simple by priorities automaton

\mathcal{A}
.
A
resolver
for Eve in
\mathcal{A}
is a function
\sigma\colon(\{q_{\mathsf{init}}\}\cup\Delta^{+})\times\Sigma\to Q
, satisfying that whenever a run
\rho\in(\{q_{\mathsf{init}}\}\cup\Delta^{+})
ends in a state

q
, and the
priority of

a
in
q
is odd then
q^{\prime}=\sigma(\rho,a)
is an

a
-successor of
q
, namely
q\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
.
A
run
is
consistent with

\sigma
(or a
\sigma
-run
)
if for every prefix
\rho\cdot(q,(a,x),q^{\prime})
with
x
odd, we have

\sigma(\rho,a)=q^{\prime}
.
The
language accepted by an Eve-resolver

\sigma
is

L(\mathcal{A},\sigma)=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\text{ every {\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}$\sigma$-run} over $w$ is accepting}\}.

Note that we always have
L(\mathcal{A},\sigma)\subseteq L(\mathcal{A})
.
We say that the
Eve-resolver

\sigma
is
valid
if
L(\mathcal{A},\sigma)=L(\mathcal{A})
.
We define symmetrically
resolvers for Adam
and their languages (note that if
\tau
is an
Adam-resolver
, then
L(\mathcal{A},\tau)\supseteq L(\mathcal{A})
).
For two fixed resolvers
\sigma
and
\tau
for Eve and Adam, respectively, every word admits a unique run that is
consistent with
both
\sigma
and
\tau
. We write
L(\mathcal{A},\sigma,\tau)
for the set of words for which this run is accepting.

An
alternating automaton
is
history deterministic
(HD) if both Eve and Adam have
valid

resolvers
. That is, if there are
resolvers

\sigma
and
\tau
for Eve and Adam such that

L(\mathcal{A})=L(\mathcal{A},\sigma)=L(\mathcal{A},\tau)=L(\mathcal{A},\sigma,\tau).

3
Layered automata

In this section we introduce
layered automata
, the central notion of this paper.
We develop their definition in two stages: the syntactic structure of
layered
automata
(Section
3.1
), and the language they recognise via an associated
alternating
parity automaton
(Section
3.2
).
Thanks to the structure of a
layered automaton
the associated automaton is
guaranteed to be
simple by priorities
.

Next, we introduce
uniform semantic determinism
, a slightly stronger notion
than
semantic determinism
.

Semantic determinism
is a necessary condition for an
automaton to be HD.
For
layered automata
,
uniform semantic determinism
yields two significant
consequences: the associated alternating automaton is both
history
deterministic
and
0-1 probabilistic
(Propositions
21

and
22
).

Our main technical tool, presented in Section
3.3
, is a
characterisation of acceptance by
layered automata
that does not rely on
alternation.
For this we introduce a notion of
consistency
, which characterises
uniform semantic determinism
. For
layered automata
, we can test
consistency
in polynomial time (Proposition
28
).
Furthermore, for
consistent

layered automata
, emptiness and language inclusion are decidable in polynomial time (Propositions
29
and
30
).

3.1
Syntactic definition of layered automata

A
layered automaton

\mathcal{A}
is a tuple of
deterministic
transition systems

\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{d}
together with

3.2.3
From alternating parity automata to layered automata

We have introduced layered automata as a tree shaped structure that is
interpreted as an alternating parity automaton.
Here we offer another perspective on this class.
We characterise directly alternating parity automata coming from
layered
automata
in terms of equivalence relations on states that should
be well-behaved.

Consider a
simple by priorities
alternating parity automaton together with a family of
d
nested equivalence relations on its states, that is:

Q\times Q\supseteq\;\mathrel{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.6484,0.1094,0.1211}\sim}}_{1}\;\supseteq\;\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\sim}}_{2}\;\supseteq\dots\supseteq\;\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\sim}}_{d}.

We consider the following properties. For all
a\in\Sigma
,
x\in[d]
and

:

•

(
x
-determinism
)
If
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
and
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
, then
q^{\prime}\sim_{x}q
.

•

(
x
-coherence
)
If
p\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\sim}}_{x}p^{\prime}
and
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:\geq x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
then
p^{\prime}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:\geq x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
for some
q^{\prime}\sim_{x}q
.

•

(
x
-saturation
)
If
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
then for all
q^{\prime}\sim_{x}q
there is a transition
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a:x}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q^{\prime}
.

.

\mathcal{B}
be a

Then, there is a

\mathcal{A}
such that

can be equipped with

Assume that

.

By definition of

Assume that
\mathcal{B}
can be equipped with

\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\sim}}_{1},\dots,\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\sim}}_{d}

This section offers an alternative characterization of layered automaton

We introduce a restriction called

3.6
Connections and comparison with other models

In this section, we discuss how

Sometimes we refer to the minimal layered automaton for a language that

We leave for future work a thorough analysis on the size of

Deterministic and alternating automata.

First, we notice that

layered automata

automata, as this is already the case for
HD

coBüchi automata

On the other hand, they can be doubly exponentially larger than alternating
parity automata. The double exponential gap already occurs for automata over
finite words

consistent

layered automata
because on the first layer they need to have at least as

Minimal

As discussed in Proposition

coBüchi

.
The properties characterising minimal

In 2024, Casares and Ohlmann characterised which

Rerailing automata.

In 2025, Ehlers introduced

[
24
]
, a kind of automata with specific semantics:
a word is accepted if the maximal priority of its runs is even.

We believe that a

. However, it may be the case that the minimal rerailing automaton of a language and its minimal

6
Congruence-based characterisation

In this section, we provide an alternative characterization of minimal
layered
automata
based on congruences over tuples of finite words. This approach yields
a direct, algebraic construction of the canonical minimal
layered automaton

for any
\omega
-regular language. The congruence-based perspective complements
the syntactic minimisation procedure presented in Section
5
and offers
additional insight into the structure of
layered automata
through the lens of
equivalences on sequences of finite words.
For example, the maximal length of such sequences reflects a parity index of the
language.

6.1
Definitions and basic properties of congruences

Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{\omega}
(the definitions can be applied to every language of infinite words, but the results concerning
layered automata
only work for regular
\omega
-languages). In our congruence-based description of the minimal
layered automaton
for
L
, the states on layer
x
are classes of an equivalence relation
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
over
x
-tuples
\overline{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{x})\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
of finite words. The relation
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
can be seen as one equivalence relation over the set of all finite tuples (of length at least one) of finite words over
\Sigma
, in which equivalent tuples must have the same length.
For defining the transition relations on the individual layers and the morphisms between the layers, we consider two operations on such finite tuples:

Concatenation
:

We write
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}
for concatenation with a word
u_{x+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
in the last component, that is
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{x-1},u_{x}u_{x+1})
is the tuple that has the same first
x-1
components as
\overline{u}
, and
u_{x}u_{x+1}
as last component (if
x=1
, then the resulting tuple is just
(u_{1}u_{2})
). We use this operation for defining the transition relation on the individual layers.

Merging
:

We write
(\overline{u},u_{x+1})
for the tuple
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{x},u_{x+1})
obtained by adding
u_{x+1}
as additional component. Merging the last two components of the tuple
(\overline{u},u_{x+1})
of length at least
2
, is the operation of removing the last component and
concatenating
it to the second last component, resulting in the tuple
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}
. We use this operation for the morphism from layer
x+1
to layer
x
.

Before going into the formal definitions, let us look an example to get an intuition for the notions that we are going to define.

Regularity and Finiteness.

We show that for
\omega
-regular languages, the number of
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
-classes is finite. The proof consists of two parts, the first part (Lemma
66
) shows that on each layer (for each fixed length of the tuples), there are finitely many
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
-classes. The second part (Lemma
67
) shows that there are only finitely many layers that contain classes that are different from
\bot
.

Lemma 66
.

If
L
is
\omega
-regular, then
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
has finite index over
(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
for all
x\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
L=L(\mathcal{A})
for a nondeterministic Büchi automaton (NBA)
\mathcal{A}
. For a finite word
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, let the transition profile of
u
contain for each pair
p,q
of states of
\mathcal{A}
the information, whether there is a run from
p
to
q
on
u
, and whether there is such a run passing through an accepting state. It is not difficult to show by induction on
x
that if
\overline{u},\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
are such that
u_{i},v_{i}
have the same transition profile for all
i
, then
\overline{u}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}
.
Since there are only finitely many possible transition profiles, the claim follows. So let
\overline{u},\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
be such that
u_{i},v_{i}
have the same transition profile for each
i
. If
u_{1},v_{1}
have the same transition profile, then they also are in the same residual class, because the same states are reachable from the initial state of
\mathcal{A}
via
u_{i}
and via
v_{i}
. So the claim follows for
x=1
. For the step, consider

with the same transition profile, and show that
(\overline{u},u_{x+1})\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}(\overline{v},v_{x+1})
. Since
u_{x},v_{x}
and

have, respectively, the same transition profile, also

have the same transition profile, so
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}
by induction. This implies that
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}w^{\prime}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}w^{\prime}
for all

by Lemma
63
(

). Thus,
(\overline{u},u_{x+1}w^{\prime})\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
iff

because also
\overline{u}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}
by induction.
Further, since all pairs

have the same transition profiles, we get that

. This means that for every

.
∎

&=&\delta^{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}_{x}(\mu^{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}_{x}({\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[(\overline{u},u_{x+1})]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}},a))\end{array}

So
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is a
layered automaton
.

6.3
Correctness of
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}

We show that
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is a
consistent

layered automaton
that accepts
L
(Theorem

). We first need some auxiliary definitions and lemmas.

Our first goal is to show that the
safe language
s of tuples are covered by the
pointed
tuples. This is expressed for finite and infinite words respectively in Lemmas
69
and
70
. For constructing corresponding tuples, we start with the auxiliary Lemma
68
that is applied in the proof of Lemma
69
(so the reader may skip Lemma
68
now and come back to it when reading the proof of Lemma
69
).

Lemma 68
.

Let
\overline{u},\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
and
u_{x+1},v_{x+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
. If
\overline{u}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{u},u_{x+1})\supseteq{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})
.

Proof.

Let
w\in{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})
, that is,
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1}w)\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
. We show that
w\in{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{u},u_{x+1})
. It is sufficient to show this for finite words
w
because the infinite words in the
safe language
s are determined by the finite ones.

If
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1}w)\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
, then there is
w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{+}
with

\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}u_{x+1}ww^{\prime}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}
and
v_{1}\cdots v_{x}(v_{x+1}u_{x+1}ww^{\prime})^{\omega}\in L
iff
x+1
is even.
Observe that
\overline{u}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}
in
particular implies
u_{1}\cdots u_{x}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}v_{1}\cdots v_{x}v_{x+1}
.
Since
v_{1}\cdots v_{x}(v_{x+1}u_{x+1}ww^{\prime})^{\omega}=v_{1}\cdots v_{x}v_{x+1}(u_{x+1}ww^{\prime}v_{x+1})^{\omega}
, we get

u_{1}\cdots u_{x}(u_{x+1}ww^{\prime}v_{x+1})^{\omega}\in L
iff
x+1
is even. Further

\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}ww^{\prime}v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\underbrace{\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}u_{x+1}ww^{\prime}}_{{}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}}v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}

So the suffix
w^{\prime}v_{x+1}
witnesses that
(\overline{u},u_{x+1}w)\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
and hence
w\in{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{u},u_{x+1})
.
∎

The following lemma states that if we start from a
pointed
tuple
\overline{u}
of
length
x
, then the
safe language
of the
(x+1)
-length tuple
(\overline{u},\varepsilon)
with
empty
(x+1)
-st component is covered by the
pointed
children
(\overline{v},v_{x+1})
of
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
, that is, those
pointed

(\overline{v},v_{x+1})
of
length
x+1
with
\mu^{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}_{x}({\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[(\overline{v},v_{x+1})]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}})={\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
.
Recall that
\mu^{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}_{x}({\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[(\overline{v},v_{x+1})]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}})={\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is equivalent to

\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
.

Lemma 69
.

Consider
(\overline{u},u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
such that
\overline{u}
is
pointed
.
Then there is
pointed

(\overline{v},v_{x+1})
such that
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}=\overline{u}
and
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
.

Proof.

Assume that we can show that there is are
\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,
v_{x+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
and
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})
is
pointed
(note that the lemma claims that the tuple without
u_{x+1}
at the end has to be
pointed
). From that we can construct the desired tuple as follows. Since
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
(because it is
pointed
), there is
w\in\Sigma^{+}
such that
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}u_{x+1}w\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}
and
v_{1}\cdots v_{x}(v_{x+1}u_{x+1}w)^{\omega}\in L
iff
x+1
is even. Then also
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1}wv_{x+1}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
. Further
\underbrace{\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}u_{x+1}w}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}}v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
by Lemma
63
and
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1}wv_{x+1})
is
pointed
as an extension of a
pointed
element by Lemma
65
. So with
v_{x+1}^{\prime}=v_{x+1}u_{x+1}wv_{x+1}
we obtain a tuple
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}^{\prime})
as claimed in the lemma.

It remains to show that we can find
\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,
v_{x+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
and
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}u_{x+1})

pointed
. The idea is quite simple. We start with
(\overline{u},u_{x+1})
as current tuple. As long as the current tuple is not
pointed
, there is a witness for that: a new tuple whose last component ends in
u_{x+1}
, and whose
safe language
is different from the
safe language
of the current tuple. By Lemma
68
, the
safe language
of the new tuple must be strictly included in the
safe language
of the previous tuple. This can happen only finitely often, since the
safe language
of a tuple only depends on its
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
-class, and there are only finitely many
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
-classes. We formalise this idea below.

Starting with
i=0
we define a sequence
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)})
of tuples with
\overline{v}^{(i)}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
and
v_{x+1}^{(i)}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that

(1)

\overline{v}^{(i)}
is
pointed
,

(2)

\overline{v}^{(i)}\cdot v_{x+1}^{(i)}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
,

(3)

(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
, and

(4)

if
i>0
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\subsetneq{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{(i-1)},v_{x+1}^{(i-1)}u_{x+1})
.

We show that we can extend the sequence by one more tuple if
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
is not
pointed
.
Because of the last property, each such sequence must be finite because the
safe language
of a tuple only depends on its
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
-class, and by Lemma
66
there are only finitely many
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}
classes for each tuple length (we are assuming that
L
is regular).

We start with
\overline{v}^{(0)}=\overline{u}
and
v_{x+1}^{(0)}=\varepsilon
, which is easily seen to have all the properties. If
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
is
pointed
, then we have found the desired tuple with
\overline{v}:=\overline{v}^{(i)}
and
v_{x+1}:=v_{x+1}^{(i)}
. Otherwise, we show how to obtain the next tuple in the sequence.

So assume that
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
is not
pointed
. Since
\overline{v}^{(i)}
is
pointed
, there must be
\overline{v}^{\prime}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
and
v_{x+1}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that

(i)

\overline{v}^{\prime}
is
pointed
,

(ii)

\overline{v}^{\prime}\cdot v_{x+1}^{\prime}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}^{(i)}
,

(iii)

(\overline{v}^{\prime},v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
, and

(iv)

(\overline{v}^{\prime},v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
.

If we define
\overline{v}^{(i+1)}=\overline{v}^{\prime}
and
v_{x+1}^{(i+1)}:=v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}
, then properties (1) and (3) for the new tuple are immediate from (i) and (iii). Property (2) follows from (ii) by
\overline{v}^{(i+1)}\cdot v_{x+1}^{(i+1)}=\overline{v}^{\prime}\cdot v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{v}^{(i)}v_{x+1}^{(i)}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
, where the second last equivalence is from (ii), and the last equivalence from (2) for
i
. Property (4) follows from (iv) and Lemma
68
because
(\overline{v}^{\prime},v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
is the same as
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{\prime},v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\not={\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
, and thus by Lemma
68

{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{\prime},v_{x+1}^{\prime}v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})\subsetneq{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}L^{s}}(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u_{x+1})
. That we can apply Lemma
68
is guaranteed by (ii).
∎

The following is an analogue of Lemma
69
for infinite words.

Lemma 70
.

Let
\overline{u}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
be
pointed
and
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
such that
(\overline{u},w)\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
. Then there are
\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,
v_{x+1}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
,
(\overline{v},v_{x+1})
is
pointed
and
(\overline{v},v_{x+1}w)\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
.

Proof.

We can apply Lemma
69
to each finite prefix

u^{(i)}=u_{x+1}
of
w
, obtaining
\overline{v}^{(i)}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,
v_{x+1}^{(i)}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\overline{v}^{(i)}\cdot v_{x+1}^{(i)}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}
,

(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)})
is
pointed
and
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)}u^{(i)})\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot
. We can assume that each
(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)})
is the
shortest

tuple from its
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}

\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}

(\overline{v},v_{x+1})

(\overline{v}^{(i)},v_{x+1}^{(i)})

∎

The next lemma is used in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 71
.

Let
x
be even and
\overline{u}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
be

such that
u_{x+1}^{\omega}
is
strongly accepted
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}

{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
. Then

. The corresponding statement holds for

.

Proof.

We show the case

even. Since we never refer to layer
x-1

x
odd is symmetric.

Since

is
strongly accepted
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
, we have
\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}^{\omega}\not\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\bot

\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}^{k}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}^{k+\ell}
. Assume that

. Then, by Lemma
70
, there would be
\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,

with
\overline{v}\cdot v_{x+1}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}\overline{u}\cdot u_{x+1}^{k}
,
(\overline{v},v_{x+1})

and

from

Theorem 72
.

Let
L
be an
\omega
-regular language. Then
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is a
consistent

layered automaton
with
L({\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}})=L
.

Proof.

Assume that
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is not
uniformly semantically deterministic
and thus not
consistent
by Corollary
19
. Then there is an even
x
and an odd
y
and
pointed
tuples
\overline{u}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{x}
,
\overline{v}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{y}
with
u_{1}\cdots u_{x}\sim_{L}v_{1}\cdots v_{y}
and such that some
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
is
strongly accepted
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
and
strongly rejected
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{v}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
. Clearly, the set of all these
w
is
\omega
-regular, so there is also an ultimately periodic
w=uv^{\omega}
with this property. Then
v^{\omega}
is
strongly accepted
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}\cdot u]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
and
strongly rejected
from
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{v}\cdot u]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
. By Lemma
71
we get that
u_{1}\cdots u_{x}uv^{\omega}\in L
and
v_{1}\cdots v_{y}uv^{\omega}\notin L
, which contradicts
u_{1}\cdots u_{x}\sim_{L}v_{1}\cdots v_{y}
. We conclude that
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is
consistent
.

For showing that
L({\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}})=L
, let
uv^{\omega}
be an ultimately periodic word. By Corollary
20
,
uv^{\omega}
contains a suffix that is
strongly accepted
or
strongly rejected
by some state in
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
, and not both because we have already shown that
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
is
consistent
. Then
v^{\omega}
is
strongly accepted
or
strongly rejected
from some
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}[\overline{u}]_{\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}\equiv_{L}}}}}
for a
pointed
tuple
\overline{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{x})\in\Sigma^{x}
such that
(u_{1}\cdots u_{x})
is reachable on layer 1 from
(\varepsilon)
by a prefix
uv^{k}
of
uv^{\omega}

7
Conclusions

We have introduced
layered automata
, a model merging and generalising minimal

HD
coBüchi automata and Zielonka trees. This model provides canonical
representations for
\omega
-regular languages
. We have characterised these
representations via machine-independent congruences over tuples of finite words.
Beyond this foundational characterisation,
consistent

layered automata

exhibit strong algorithmic properties.
Emptiness and inclusion testing are in PTIME for
consistent

layered automata
.
Furthermore,
consistent

layered automata
can be minimised in PTIME, and the
result is never bigger than the smallest equivalent deterministic automaton.
These properties make
consistent

layered automata
an attractive model of
acceptors for
\omega
-regular languages as they have good
algorithmic properties and at the same time are both
history deterministic
and
0-1
probabilistic
.

While we establish that canonical
layered
automata
are minimal within the class of
consistent

layered automata

(Corollary
43
), we conjecture they are also minimal among
all
alternating

history deterministic
automata.

Conjecture 78
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a
history deterministic

alternating parity automaton
.
Then, there is an equivalent
layered automaton

\mathcal{A}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0.0547,0.375,0.4453}{[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]}}
has no more states than
\mathcal{B}
.

In particular, the minimal
layered automaton
of a language is minimal among all
history deterministic

alternating parity automata
.

Naturally, there are many aspects of the theory of
layered automata
itself
that remain to be developed. We believe

layered automata
offer a promising framework for broader practical and
