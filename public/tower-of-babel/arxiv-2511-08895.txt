Probing Intrinsic Ellipticity in Neutron Star Binaries
Zhiqiang Miao, Huan Yang

Abstract
We present a novel resonance mechanism that can naturally occur in neutron star binaries: a spin-orbit resonance. This resonance locks the binary into a unique state where the neutron star spin evolves alongside the orbit. The resonance requires a finite neutron star ellipticity ϵ \epsilon possibly sourced by strong internal magnetic fields in magnetars, which are motivated by population study of eccentric neutron star-black hole binary GW200105. We find that the locking probability is proportional to ϵ \sqrt{\epsilon} . We derive the phase correction in the gravitational waveform due to this resonance locking effect, and have conducted a search in all neutron star binaries up to the O4a gravitational-wave catalog, with no positive event found so far. Observations by next-generation detectors such as Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer, or even the upcoming upgrade of Advanced LIGO, have the potential to detect such locking signals, enabling precise measurements of both neutron star’s ellipticity and moment of inertia. Future searches should be performed to discover this resonance, or, if undetected, to place constraints on the magnetar fraction in neutron star binaries.

For simplicity, we merge the axis of principal inertia and the spin axis, and assume the spin is perpendicular to the orbital plane.
We further restrict our discussion to circular orbits.
(see Supplementary Material

The equations of motion for the orbit and the star’s rotation can be written as

\displaystyle\ddot{f}+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon\frac{GM^{\prime}}{a^{3}}\frac{I}{I_{\rm orb}}\sin\left[2(f-\theta)\right]={\dot{\Omega}_{\rm orb}}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\ddot{\theta}-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon\frac{GM^{\prime}}{a^{3}}\sin\left[2(f-\theta)\right]=\dot{\Omega}_{\rm spin}\,,

(3)

where
M^{\prime}
is the black hole mass,
I
and
I_{\rm orb}
are the moments of inertia of the star and the orbit, respectively.
f
is the true anomaly and
\theta
is the rotation angle of the neutron star, as shown in Fig.
1
.

\dot{\Omega}_{\rm orb}
is the orbital decay rate and
\dot{\Omega}_{\rm spin}
is the neutron star spin down rate due to electromagnetic radiation, gravitational-wave radiation and the tidal dissipation. We set
\dot{\Omega}_{\rm spin}
to zero, since it is negligible compared to
\dot{\Omega}_{\rm orb}
, within the regime of our interest. We also neglect the
I/I_{\rm orb}
term in Eq. (
2
) since
I\ll I_{\rm orb}
.
By defining the relative phase
\gamma=2\theta-2f
, and combining Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) we obtain the following equation

\ddot{\gamma}+\mathcal{B}\sin\gamma+\mathcal{C}=0\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{B}=3\epsilon GM^{\prime}/a^{3}
and
\mathcal{C}=2\dot{\Omega}_{\rm orb}
.

Equation (
4
) resembles the equation of motion of a rigid pendulum that is subject to a periodic torque and a (nearly) constant external torque.
Such systems generally exhibit two types of motion: circulation and libration. When
\mathcal{B}>\mathcal{C}
, resonance locking becomes possible, driving the system into a libration state in which the stellar spin and orbital frequency evolve synchronously.
The general theory of resonance capture predicts that the probability of entering resonance is approximately
102
;
44
;
79

\mathcal{P}_{\rm lock}=\frac{2}{1+\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\mathcal{B}^{1/2}\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{\dot{B}}}}\approx\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\mathcal{\dot{B}}}{\mathcal{B}^{1/2}\mathcal{C}}=\frac{4\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}\,,

(5)

where we have used the relation
\dot{\Omega}_{\rm orb}=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\Omega_{\rm orb}
and
q=M^{\prime}/M
is the mass ratio. This is also confirmed by our numerical investigations.

On the other hand, when
\mathcal{B}<\mathcal{C}
, the resonance cannot be sustained and eventually breaks down. This occurs when the orbital frequency reaches a critical value, which we denote as
\Omega_{\rm br}
. At this point, the corresponding gravitational wave frequency is given by

\begin{split}f_{\rm br}&=\frac{\Omega_{\rm br}}{\pi}=\frac{1}{\pi(M+M^{\prime})}\left(\frac{5}{64}\frac{M+M^{\prime}}{M}\right)^{3/5}\epsilon^{3/5}\\
&\approx 10\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\right)^{2/5}\left(\frac{1.4\,M_{\odot}}{M}\right)\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10^{-5}}\right)^{3/5}\,{\rm Hz}\,.\end{split}

(6)

In Fig.
2
, we present an illustrative example of locking by numerically evolving Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), where
M=1.4\,M_{\odot}
,
q=3
and
\epsilon=5\times 10^{-5}
.
The evolution starts with an initial orbital frequency of
f_{\rm orb}=0.4\,{\rm Hz}
and an initial neutron star spin frequency of
f_{\rm spin}=0.5\,{\rm Hz}
.
As
f_{\rm orb}
increases and passes through
0.5\,{\rm Hz}
, resonance occurs and

begins to evolve synchronously with
f_{\rm orb}
. This locking continues until
f_{\rm orb}
reaches about
7.1\,{\rm Hz}
(corresponding to
f_{\rm br}=14.2\,{\rm Hz}
), at which point the locking breaks down.

Resonance locking will induce the angular momentum exchange between the orbit and the neutron star, which should affect the orbital decay and cause a phase shift in the gravitational waveform.
Using the formula
d^{2}\psi/d\omega^{2}=2(dE/d\omega)/\dot{E}
for the phase
\psi(f)
of the frequency domain waveform
\tilde{h}(f)=\mathcal{A}e^{i\psi(f)}

95
, we derive the phase correction due to resonance locking as

\delta\psi_{\rm lock}(f)=\left\{\!\begin{aligned} &-\frac{45}{64}\frac{I}{\mu^{2}M_{t}}(\frac{1}{v}-\frac{4}{3v_{i}}+\frac{v^{3}}{3v_{i}^{4}})\,,&f\leq f_{br}\,,\\
&-\frac{45}{64}\frac{I}{\mu^{2}M_{t}}(\frac{1}{v_{\rm br}}-\frac{4}{3v_{i}}+\frac{v_{\rm br}^{3}}{3v_{i}^{4}})\,,&f>f_{br}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

where
M_{t}=M+M^{\prime}
and
\mu=MM^{\prime}/M_{t}
are the total mass and the reduced mass, respectively.
Here
v=(\pi M_{t}f)^{1/3}
,
v_{i}<v_{\rm br}
is an arbitrary constant related to the initial time and phase of the waveform. The correction clearly enters at 2PN (Post-Newtonian) order, with a large prefactor
\sim I/(\mu^{2}M_{t})\sim(R/M)^{2}
. For a NSBH system with
M=1.4\,M_{\odot}
and
M^{\prime}\in[3\,M_{\odot},10\,M_{\odot}]
, the accumulated phase correction reaches
\mathcal{O}(1)
radians when integrated from a start frequency of
5\,{\rm Hz}
up to a breaking frequency
f_{\rm br}=10\,{\rm Hz}
.

VI
Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we present a novel resonance locking mechanism–the sustained spin-orbit resonance –that may occur in neutron-star binaries.
Locking occurs when the orbital frequency sweeps through the neutron star spin frequency, typically occurring in the range of
0.1-1\,{\rm Hz}
for magnetars, which lies within the frequency band of space-based detectors like DECIGO
53
.
However, because of the relatively large ellipticity of magnetars, the locking can persist up to
\mathcal{O}(10){\rm Hz}
or higher, making it detectable by ground-based detectors such as A+ and CE+ET.
Although we have not yet observed a positive event, further searches should be conducted to facilitate discovery or place meaningful constraints.

The expected merger rate for a resonance-locking NSBH system is approximately

\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}_{\rm lock}=f_{\rm mag}\mathcal{P}_{\rm lock}\mathcal{R}_{\rm NSBH}\\
&\approx 0.18\,f_{\rm mag}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10^{-5}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\rm NSBH}}{30\,{\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}}}\right){\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}}\,,\end{split}

(10)

where
f_{\rm mag}
is the fraction of neutron stars that are magnetars in NSBH events.
If we take a volumetric merger rate (number of mergers per comoving volume per cosmic time at redshift
z
) as
\mathcal{R}(z)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm lock}\times(1+z)^{3.2}e^{-z^{2}/3}
for
z<6
, then the total merger rate in the observer frame is written as

\begin{split}\dot{N}&=\int\frac{dV_{c}(z)}{dz}\frac{\mathcal{R}(z)}{1+z}dz\\
&=603\,f_{\rm mag}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10^{-5}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\rm NSBH}}{30\,{\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}}}\right)\,{\rm yr^{-1}}\end{split}

(11)

where
V_{c}(z)
is the comoving volume up to redshift
z
.
However, not all resonance-locking signals in these merger events can be individually resolved by CE+ET.
To quantify resolvable events, we generate a synthetic NSBH population of
10^{5}
mergers (see Supplemental Material
1

We then place this population at different redshifts to evaluate the resolvable fraction,
f_{\rm res}(z)

For each choice of

f_{\rm br}
for the population and identify mergers as resolvable if the accumulated SNR in the frequency band
[1\,{\rm Hz},f_{\rm br}]
exceeds the threshold
\rho_{\rm thr}=10
.
This allows us to estimate the resolvable merger events as

\dot{N}_{\rm res}=\int\frac{dV_{c}(z)}{dz}\frac{\mathcal{R}(z)}{1+z}f_{\rm res}(z)dz\,,

(12)

and hence the expected observing time for CE+ET to detect one positive event,

T_{\rm obs}=\frac{1}{\dot{N}_{\rm res}}\,.

However, successful detection of a resonance locking signal will bring in profound opportunities for studying neutron stars in terms of formation mechanism and fundamental physics tests.
First, the measurement of
f_{\rm br}
can be translated into neutron star ellipticity, which may provide direct evidence of a magnetar in the binary system. The measured ellipticity can further be used to infer the internal magnetic field strength.
Second, such a detection enables the most precise measurement of the neutron star moment of inertia, with an accuracy that reaches 1% or even 0.1%, which offers powerful constraints on the dense matter EOS
63
;
74
.
Note that currently the best method for measuring the neutron star moment of inertia is in the double pulsar system PSR J0737-3039
28
, where long-term pulsar timing is used to determine the periastron advance induced by the Lense-Thirring effect. This approach is expected to produce a measurement with
\sim 10\%
precision by around 2030
48
.
Moreover, spin-orbit resonance locking systems allow simultaneous measurement of the moment of inertia and the tidal love number, providing a unique way to test gravity through the EOS-independent I-Love relation
100
.

While our previous discussion has primarily focused on magnetars, which is the most astrophysically compelling explanation for neutron star ellipticity, the spin-orbit resonance locking mechanism is a general framework that can be applied to a wider range of scenarios.
For example, systems involving elastic materials, such as neutron star crusts, solid-quark cores
22
, or even solid-quark stars
99
;
75
, can sustain relatively large ellipticities. Theoretical estimates suggest that a solid crust can support a maximum ellipticity on the order of
\epsilon_{\rm max}\sim 10^{-6}(\sigma_{\rm br}/0.1)

96
, with the breaking strain
\sigma_{\rm br}\sim 0.1
from the molecular dynamics simulations of crustal fracture
47
, while internal stress within a solid quark core or a solid quark star may allow even larger deformations.
Moreover, exotic compact objects (ECO) have been proposed to deviate from Kerr black holes by supporting finite asymmetric deformations
86
;
26
;
45
.
The resonance locking framework can be applied to test these black hole mimickers and to probe their deviations from Kerr geometry.

VIII
Eccenetric orbit

In the main text, we discuss spin-orbit resonance locking for circular orbits.
In this section, we extend the analysis to eccentric orbits.
For simplicity, we still consider the case where the long axis of the neutron star lies within the orbital plane. In the next section, we will briefly discuss the more general scenario, where the long axis is oriented off the orbital plane.

VIII.1
Equation of Motion and the Probability of Capturing Resonance

VIII.2
Gravitational waveform correction

In the main text, we derive the gravitational-wave phase correction due to resonance locking for circular orbits, which is not applicable to eccentric orbits.
Here, we provide an alternative correction for eccentric orbits, based on the post-Newtonian inspiral-only waveform model (
pyEFPE
) developed in Ref.
77
.
The equations of motion for
y
[
(1-e^{2})y^{2}=(M_{t}\omega)^{2/3}
with
M_{t}=M+M^{\prime}
the total mass and
\omega
the mean orbital angular velocity] and
e^{2}
in Ref.
77
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}y

\displaystyle=\nu y^{9}\left(a_{0}+\sum_{n=2}^{6}a_{n}y^{n}\right)\,,

(S33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}e^{2}

\displaystyle=-\nu y^{8}\left(b_{0}+\sum_{n=2}^{6}b_{n}y^{n}\right)\,,

(S34)

where
\nu=\mu/M_{t}
is the symmetric mass ratio with the reduced mass
\mu=MM^{\prime}/M_{t}
.

a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are PN coefficients with

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{32}{5}+\frac{28}{5}e^{2}\,,

(S35)

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{608}{15}e^{2}+\frac{242}{15}e^{4}\,,

(S36)

and
\mathcal{D}
is defined by

\mathcal{D}=\frac{M_{t}}{(1-e^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{d}{dt}\,.

(S37)

Adding resonance locking effect, these equations should be modified as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{0}y+\mathcal{D}^{1}y\,,

(S38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}e^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{0}e^{2}+\mathcal{D}^{1}e^{2}\,,

(S39)

where
\mathcal{D}^{0}

S33
) and (

Since the locking occurs at very low

At Newtonian order, the energy and angular momentum of a spin-orbit locked system are given by

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu}{2}y^{2}(1-e^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{I}{M_{t}^{2}}y^{6}(1-e^{2})^{3}\,,

(S40)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mu M_{t}y^{-1}+\frac{I}{M_{t}}y^{3}(1-e^{2})^{3/2}\,,

(S41)

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{32}{5}\frac{\mu^{2}}{M_{t}^{2}}y^{10}(1-e^{2})^{3/2}\left(1+\frac{73}{24}e^{2}+\frac{37}{96}e^{4}\right)\,,

(S42)

\displaystyle\frac{dL}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{32}{5}\frac{\mu^{2}}{M_{t}}y^{7}(1-e^{2})^{3/2}\left(1+\frac{7}{8}e^{2}\right)\,.

(S43)

\displaystyle D^{1}y

\displaystyle=\nu y^{13}c_{0}\frac{3(1-e^{2})^{1/2}I}{\mu M_{t}^{2}-3(1-e^{2})^{2}Iy^{4}}\,,

(S44)

\displaystyle D^{1}e^{2}

\displaystyle=-\nu y^{12}c_{0}\frac{6(1-e^{2})(1-e^{2}-\sqrt{1-e^{2}})I}{\mu M_{t}^{2}-3(1-e^{2})^{2}Iy^{4}}\,,

(S45)

where

IX
General Case for Spin-Orbit Resonance

For a deformed neutron star, we denote the three unit eigenvectors along the principal axes as
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3}
, with corresponding eigenvalues
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
.
The angular velocity is
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\omega_{1}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1}+\omega_{2}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2}+\omega_{3}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3}
.
We illustrate the geometry and the motion of the neutron star in Fig.
S8
.
In general, the spin vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
does not coincide with the principal axes. Additionally,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
also does not need to align with the orbital normal
\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}
. These features result in more complex dynamics of the neutron star, including two types of precession: one is the precession of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
about the normal to the orbital plane with rate
\Omega_{p,1}\sim\epsilon n^{2}/\omega
, and the other is the precession of
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3}
about
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with rate
\Omega_{p,2}\sim\epsilon\omega
.
Near the resonance, we have
\omega\simeq n
, therefore both two precessions have a timescale of roughly
1/(\epsilon n)
, which is much longer than the resonance timescale
\sqrt{1/\dot{n}}
for
\epsilon\lesssim 10^{-4}
.
In the following discussion, we will neglect the precessions due to their relatively long timescales. A detailed study of the precessions may be pursued in future work.

We work in the body frame
(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3})
.
The Euler’s equations of motion are

\displaystyle I_{1}\dot{\omega}_{1}-(I_{2}-I_{3})\omega_{2}\omega_{3}=N_{1}\,,

(S47)

\displaystyle I_{2}\dot{\omega}_{2}-(I_{3}-I_{1})\omega_{3}\omega_{1}=N_{2}\,,

(S48)

\displaystyle I_{3}\dot{\omega}_{3}-(I_{1}-I_{2})\omega_{1}\omega_{2}=N_{3}\,,

(S49)

where the gravitational torques received by the companion are
79

\displaystyle N_{1}=\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{r^{3}}(I_{3}-I_{2})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3})\,,

(S50)

\displaystyle N_{2}=\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{r^{3}}(I_{1}-I_{3})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1})\,,

(S51)

\displaystyle N_{3}=\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{r^{3}}(I_{2}-I_{1})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1})(\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2})\,.

(S52)

Note we have

\left\{\!\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{1}=\cos w\cos\theta+\sin w\sin\theta\cos\varepsilon\,,\\
&\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{2}=-\cos w\sin\theta+\sin w\cos\theta\cos\varepsilon\,,\\
&\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3}=-\sin w\sin\varepsilon\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(S53)

where
w=\varpi+f
.
Plugging Eq. (
S53
) into Eqs. (
S50
-
S52
) we get

\displaystyle N_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{2r^{3}}(I_{3}-I_{2})\Big[\sin(\theta-2w)\cos^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\varepsilon

\displaystyle-\sin(\theta+2w)\sin^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\sin\theta\sin 2\varepsilon\Big]\,.

(S54)

\displaystyle N_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{2r^{3}}(I_{1}-I_{3})\Big[\cos(\theta-2w)\cos^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\varepsilon

\displaystyle+\cos(\theta+2w)\sin^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\cos\theta\sin 2\varepsilon\Big]\,.

(S55)

\displaystyle N_{3}=

\displaystyle-\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{2r^{3}}(I_{2}-I_{1})\Big[\sin(2\theta-2w)\cos^{4}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle+\sin(2\theta+2w)\sin^{4}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta\sin^{2}\varepsilon\Big]\,.

(S56)

We now consider that the rotation frequency
\omega_{3}=\dot{\theta}
and the mean motion
n=\dot{\ell}
are close to resonance (
\omega_{3}\simeq pn
).
By doing average over one orbital period and retaining all the terms with argument
2(\theta-p\ell)
, we then obtain the average torque
33

\begin{split}\langle N_{3}\rangle=&-\frac{3GM^{\prime}}{8a^{3}}(I_{2}-I_{1})\Big[(x+1)^{2}H(p,e)\sin 2(\theta-p\ell-\varpi)\\
&+(x-1)^{2}H(-p,e)\sin 2(\theta-p\ell+\varpi)\\
&+2(1-x^{2})G(p,e)\sin 2(\theta-p\ell)\Big]\,,\end{split}

(S57)

where
x=\cos\varepsilon
and
G(p,e)
is a power series in
e
. For
p=1
and
p=3/2
, we have

G(1,e)=\frac{9}{4}e^{2}+\frac{7}{4}e^{4}\quad{\rm and}\quad G(\frac{3}{2},e)=\frac{53}{16}e^{3}\,.

(S58)

Substituting Eq. (
S57
) into Eq. (
S49
), we get

\dot{\omega}_{3}-\epsilon\omega_{1}\omega_{2}=-\frac{\mathcal{B}_{x}}{2}\sin 2(\theta-p\ell-\varpi_{x})\,,

(S59)

where

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathcal{B}_{x}}{\mathcal{B}}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle 4\big[(g+h^{+}+h^{-})^{2}-4g(h^{+}+h^{-})\sin^{2}\varpi

\displaystyle-4h^{+}h^{-}\sin^{2}2\varpi\big]\,,

(S60)

\displaystyle\tan 2\varpi_{x}=

\displaystyle\frac{(h^{+}-h^{-})\sin 2\varpi}{g+(h^{+}+h^{-})\cos 2\varpi}\,,

(S61)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=

\displaystyle 3\epsilon n^{2}\frac{q}{1+q}\,,

(S62)

\displaystyle g=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(1-x^{2})G(p,e)\,,

(S63)

\displaystyle h^{\pm}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}(x\pm 1)^{2}H(\pm p,e)\,.

(S64)

Let
\gamma=2(\theta-p\ell-\varpi_{x})
, then
\ddot{\gamma}=2(\dot{\omega}_{3}-p\dot{n})
, where we have neglect the slow variation of
\varpi_{x}
caused by the precessions. Thus, Eq. (
S59
) can be rewritten as

\ddot{\gamma}+\mathcal{B}_{x}\sin\gamma=-2p\dot{n}+2\epsilon\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\,,

(S65)

which is the same as Eq. (
4
) in the main text or Eq. (
S25
) in the Supplemental Material. We may further use Eq. (
5
) to evaluate the probability of capturing into resonance.
Note here
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
can be considered as constants due to the long period of precession.
For example, for a circular orbit with
e=0
, we have
g=h^{-}=0
and thus

\mathcal{B}_{x}=\frac{3}{4}\epsilon n^{2}\frac{q}{1+q}(1+x)^{2}.

(S66)

If we further assume
\epsilon\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ll\dot{n}
, we can get the probability as

\mathcal{P}(1,0)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}\frac{1+x}{2}\,,

(S67)

which is reduced by a factor of
(1+x)/2
compared to Eq. (
5
).

XII
NSBH population

In order to calculate how many NSBH merger events with locking can be resolved by CE+ET, we performed a population analysis. In this section, we provide a detailed description of our population model. For the black hole mass, we take a power-law distribution with a spectral index of
2.7
, featuring a sharp cutoff at the lower end of
4\,M_{\odot}
and an upper bound of
40\,M_{\odot}
:

p(m_{\rm BH})\propto\left\{\!\begin{aligned} &m_{\rm BH}^{-2.7}\,,&4\,M_{\odot}<m_{\rm BH}<40\,M_{\odot}\,,\\
&0\,,&{\rm otherwise}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(S69)

For the neutron star mass, we assume a uniform mass distribution between
1.1\,M_{\odot}
and
2.1\,M_{\odot}
, i.e.,
p(m_{\rm NS})\sim{\rm U}(1.1\,M_{\odot},2.1\,M_{\odot})
.
For simplicity, we do not consider black hole spin in this analysis.
We also take a volumetric NSBH merger rate with locking signal as

\mathcal{R}(z)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm lock}\times(1+z)^{3.2}e^{-z^{2}/3}\,,

(S70)

where the exponent
3.2
is taken from the redshift dependence for the BBH merger rate inferred in GWTC-4
5
, and the factor
e^{-z^{2}/3}
accounts for the suppression at high redshifts. We assume a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
H_{0}=67.4\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
and
\Omega_{m}=0.315

19
.

For each choice of
\epsilon
, we evaluate the corresponding breaking frequency
f_{\rm br}
for the population, and assume locking signal can be resolved by CE+ET if the accumulated SNR in the frequency band
[1\,{\rm Hz},f_{\rm br}]
exceeds the threshold
\rho_{\rm thr}=10
.
This allows us to calculate the fraction of resolvable events as a function of redshift, i.e.,
f_{\rm res}(z)
. Then we get the resolvable merger events

\dot{N}_{\rm res}=\int\frac{dV_{c}(z)}{dz}\frac{\mathcal{R}(z)}{1+z}f_{\rm res}(z)dz\,.

(S71)

In Fig.
S11
, we show the distribution of
{d{\dot{N}_{\rm res}}}/{dz}
as a function of redshift
z
for different values of
\epsilon
.
