Altered neural response to social awkwardness in schizophrenia spectrum disorders
Authors: Emily Przysinda, Bridget Shovestul, Abhishek Saxena, Xiaoyu Dong, Stephanie Reda, Emily Dudek, J. Steven Lamberti, Edmund Lalor, David Dodell-Feder
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with a schizophrenia spectrum disorder (SSD) have difficulties with social information processing, including mental state attribution, or “theory of mind” (ToM). Prior work has shown that these difficulties are related to disruption to the neural network subserving ToM. However, few such studies utilize naturalistic stimuli that are more representative of daily social interaction. Here, SSD and non-SSD individuals underwent fMRI while watching The Office to better understand how the ToM network responds to dynamic and complex social information, such as socially awkward moments. We find that medial prefrontal cortex tracks less with moment-to-moment awkwardness in SSD individuals. We also find a broad decrease in functional connectivity in the ToM network in SSD. Furthermore, neural response during awkward moments and functional connectivity was associated with psychotic experiences and social functioning. These results suggest that during naturalistic, socially awkward moments where mental state attribution is critical, individuals with SSD fail to recruit key regions of the ToM network, possibly contributing to decreased social understanding and impaired functioning.

Introduction

The ability to create and maintain social connections is vital to health and well-being (Horan et al. 2007, Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010, Perkins et al. 2015). Individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) have well-documented disruptions to social connection (Green et al. 2018), and this makes them more susceptible to the damaging consequences of social disconnection. Increasing research shows these disruptions are partly due to impairments in social information processing (Fett et al. 2011, Green et al. 2019). Most notably, SSD individuals have deficits in theory of mind (ToM), which involves deducing what others are thinking or feeling (Biedermann et al. 2012, Bora and Pantelis 2013, Thibaudeau et al. 2020). Impairment of ToM in SSD has been associated with difficulties in many aspects of daily functioning (Fett et al. 2011, Halverson et al. 2019, Thibaudeau et al. 2020, 2021).

ToM is supported by a network of brain regions (often called the “ToM network”) including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), temporal parietal junction (TPJ), right superior temporal sulcus (rSTS), and precuneus (PC) (Schurz et al. 2014). These brain regions are reliably recruited across a range of ToM tasks, although each region has been shown to have distinct functional profiles in the context of mental state attribution (Schurz et al. 2014, 2021). The mPFC is thought to contribute to ToM via integration of stable social traits and higher order processes (Koster-Hale and Saxe 2013, Schurz et al. 2014, Molenberghs et al. 2016). The right TPJ (rTPJ) has been found to be involved in developing short-term expectations in social situations and belief attribution (Koster-Hale and Saxe 2013, Schurz et al. 2014, Filmer et al. 2019), and it is thought to be the most selective region for mental state information (Saxe and Kanwisher 2003, Saxe and Powell 2006). Studies suggest that left TPJ (lTPJ) has a relatively similar functional profile to rTPJ but may play a more general role in meta-representation (Perner et al. 2006). The rSTS tracks moment-to-moment changes in social states and is thought to facilitate the application of social scripts to a specific context, such as how to order dinner at a restaurant (den Ouden et al. 2005, Koster-Hale and Saxe 2013, Lee et al. 2014, Redcay et al. 2016). Finally, the PC has been shown to be involved in mentalizing about another’s spatial perspective, such as “walking in another’s shoes” (Schurz et al. 2014). All these regions work together to help an individual understand the intentions of others (Atique et al. 2011, Marchetti et al. 2015, Tettamanti et al. 2017).

Increasing research shows that the ToM network is disrupted in SSD (Benedetti et al. 2009, Brüne et al. 2011, Lee et al. 2011, Dodell-Feder et al. 2014, 2021, Kronbichler et al. 2017, Vucurovic et al. 2020). For example, a recent meta-analysis of fMRI studies of ToM in SSD demonstrated hypoactivation in mPFC during most ToM tasks and hyperactivation in rTPJ, which was task-specific (Vucurovic et al. 2020). Although this prior work strongly suggests disruption to the ToM network in SSD, most of the ToM tasks used in these studies are nonnaturalistic and tend to evaluate singular components of ToM, despite it being a multifaceted construct (Schurz et al. 2014). Current task-based paradigms designed to measure ToM do not closely resemble how ToM may be implemented in the context of real-life social information processing.

Increasingly, video stimuli have been used to test the response of neural networks in a naturalistic manner that better captures the complexity of the real-world (Sonkusare et al. 2019, Vanderwal et al. 2019, Fan et al. 2021). Naturalistic video stimuli are on a continuum of “life-like” paradigms that range from static images to real-world interactions, in which experimental control is often traded-off with external validity (Fan et al. 2021). For this study, “naturalistic video” will be defined as the dynamic portrayal of scenes that one might experience in everyday life that is passively viewed by participants (Sonkusare et al. 2019). During a naturalistic video, the task is implicit to the stimuli, and all the participant needs to do is pay attention. This allows for the researcher to include specific media elements that engage viewers and evoke specific cognitive processes, and it reduces cognitive demands that can be especially taxing for clinical populations (Sonkusare et al. 2019, Vanderwal et al. 2019, Eickhoff et al. 2020, Grall and Finn 2021). There are a variety of analysis approaches that can be applied to neural data during video watching, but of particular interest for this study is examining how brain regions track with specific social features of the stimulus.

A series of recent fMRI studies demonstrated the utility of this approach to analyzing naturalistic video in individuals with autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) by using an episode of the television show, The Office, to probe the ToM network (Byrge et al. 2015, Pantelis et al. 2015, Gedek et al. 2018). The Office depicts complex social interactions involving characters in socially awkward situations where someone, either intentionally or unintentionally, commits a social “faux pas” that is unexpected or violates social norms in a way that may cause others to feel uncomfortable (Baron-Cohen et al. 1999, Berthoz et al. 2002). The feeling of awkwardness has been postulated to arisen from our social need to be accepted by the group and thus the uncomfortable feeling during awkward situations can trigger a feedback loop for individuals to adjust their behaviors so they feel more accepted in groups (Clegg 2012). These awkward moments require viewers to simultaneously process and integrate multiple characters’ mental states and emotions with the social context, which nicely approximates the types of inferences that may be made in real-world social interaction. These moments are ubiquitous in The Office, making it an ideal, naturalistic stimulus to study social information processing.

In a study of ASD individuals, Pantelis et al. (2015) had participants view an episode of The Office while undergoing fMRI. They found that moment-to-moment perceived awkwardness during the episode (i.e. moments in which a participant deemed that what was happening was awkward) was reliably associated with activity in the ToM network, suggesting the importance of mental state attribution when processing naturalistic, complex, social scenes. Furthermore, a cluster in rTPJ/rSTS tracked less with awkwardness ratings in the ASD group, suggesting that the rTPJ/rSTS might contribute to social information processing and real-world social functioning difficulties in ASD (Koster-Hale and Saxe 2013, Schurz et al. 2014).

Here, we aimed to probe the neural bases of naturalistic social information processing in SSD by using the same stimulus and analysis procedure as Pantelis et al. (2015). This provides an informal comparison of how the ToM network responds to naturalistic social stimuli in SSD and ASD. This neural comparison may be particularly useful as individuals with SSD and ASD show similar levels of behavioral impairment on a variety of ToM tasks (Chung et al. 2014, Gilleen et al. 2017, Fernandes et al. 2018, Pinkham et al. 2020, Oliver et al. 2021). However, these behavioral measures do not necessarily speak to the nature or mechanism of ToM impairment, which may differ between patient groups (Dodell-Feder and Germine 2018). This is particularly important as different mechanisms of impairment might suggest different interventions. Neuroimaging studies may provide important complementary information, which together with behavioral findings, can help to better discern the nature of impairment (Gilleen et al. 2017, Fernandes et al. 2018, Pinkham et al. 2020, Hudson et al. 2023). We first aimed to replicate Pantelis et al.’s findings demonstrating that neural activity in the ToM network tracked with moment-by-moment awkwardness during the episode. Second, we evaluated group differences in how the ToM network tracked with awkwardness. Third, we evaluated functional connectivity between regions of the ToM network across the entire episode, irrespective of awkwardness. Finally, we evaluated brain–behavior associations to assess whether brain responses in the ToM network were associated with symptoms and social functioning.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 29 individuals with a SSD and 31 non-SSD individuals recruited from the greater Rochester area through Craigslist, ResearchMatch, the University of Rochester Clinical & Translational Science Institute listserv, and the University of Rochester Medical Center’s Strong Ties Community Clinic. Diagnosis of an SSD (i.e. schizophrenia [n = 19], schizoaffective disorder [n = 10]) was confirmed with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5) (First et al. 2015) administered by a clinical psychologist, clinical psychology students, or trained lab staff. Diagnostic consensus was obtained during regular meetings. Eligibility criteria included a passing score on the UCSD Brief Assessment of Capacity to Consent (Jeste et al. 2007), no cognitive impairment [IQ > 70 assessed with the Wide Range Achievement Test (WRAT) (Wilkinson and Robertson 2017) or the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI) (Wechsler 1999)], no history of pervasive developmental disorder or major neurological disorder/impairment, English fluency, no history of violence/extreme agitation, no history of substance/alcohol use disorder in prior 6 months, and no claustrophobia. Non-SSD participants were excluded if they had met criteria for current psychiatric illness, a prior psychiatric hospitalization, or a first-degree relative with psychotic disorder or mood disorder with psychotic features (based on self-report). Participants in the SSD group were on a stable medication regimen for at least 1 month. This study was approved by the University of Rochester Research Subjects Review Board. There were no statistically significant group differences in age, IQ, or parental education (see Table 1 for stats).

Measures

Behavioral testing was completed in 1–2 sessions that began before the fMRI session, and for some participants, depending on time, continued after fMRI data was collected.

fMRI task

Participants watched an episode of the TV show, The Office (Season 1 Episode 6, ‘Hot Girl’; production code 1003; copyright NBC Universal), that was not edited except for the opening title sequence and the credits. The episode was 20:52 min long and presented in three runs that were approximately 7 min long. During the episode, a purse saleswoman, Katie, visits the office, and the males in the office compete for her attention (Pantelis et al. 2015). We included a 30-s fixation period on a central cross at the beginning and end of each run. Continuous ratings of social awkwardness were provided by Pantelis et al. (2015). These ratings were collected from an independent sample of subjects (N = 46) who watched the episode of The Office for the first time outside of the scanner while continuously rating awkwardness with a joystick. Specifically, participants were instructed to “push a spring-loaded joystick … forward to the extent that they deemed what was happening within the scene to be awkward” (Pantelis, et al., 2015, p. 1349). Thus, these ratings may be interpreted as the participant’s perception of awkwardness regarding the events in the episode.

Behavioral tasks

Post fMRI questionnaire

After the fMRI scan, participants answered a set of 10 true/false questions assessing episode attention [e.g. “Pam (the secretary) is engaged.”] and questions about if they had seen the TV show and specific episode before. For one of the attention questions, the proportion of all participants that answered correctly was below chance (50%), so we excluded that question. We calculated the percentage of the nine questions correct for each participant. Two non-SSD participants did not complete this survey due to a technical error. Another non-SSD participant did not answer one of the questions, so we used the percentage correct out of eight questions for that participant.

Social Functioning Scale

The Social Functioning Scale (SFS) is a 79-item self-report questionnaire asking about functioning in seven domains (social engagement/withdrawal, interpersonal behavior, pro social activities, recreational activities, independence–competence, independence–performance, and employment/occupation) (Birchwood et al. 1990). Scores are summed for each domain with point values assigned in the scoring manual and domain scores are summed to obtain a total score (Erdeniz et al. 2017, Oliver et al. 2021). Two non-SSD participants withdrew from the study before completing this measure.

Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief

The Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief (PQ-B) is a 21-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the occurrence and distress of psychotic-like experiences (e.g. “Do familiar surroundings sometimes seem strange, confusing, threatening or unreal to you?”) (Loewy et al. 2011). We calculated the total score as the sum of endorsed symptoms. Two SSD participants withdrew from the study before completing this measure.

Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) is a 30-item, clinician-rated instrument that assesses five categories of symptoms experienced over the prior 2 weeks: positive, negative, cognitive, excitement, emotional. Each question within each symptom domain is scored with a 7-point rating (1 = “absent,” 7 = “extreme”) and then summed for a symptom domain score (Kay et al. 1987). This measure was administered only to SSD participants.

fMRI procedure and analysis

Data collection and preprocessing

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were obtained using a Siemens Magnetom Prisma 3 T MRI scanner at the University of Rochester Center for Advanced Brain Imaging & Neurophysiology. The anatomical images were acquired using a 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared, rapid acquisition, gradient echo sequence (TR = 2532 ms, TE = 2.34 ms, flip angle = 7 degrees, 192 slices, FOV = 256 mm, acquisition voxel size = 1 × 1× 1 mm3). FMRI data were acquired with an echo-planar imaging sequence (TR = 2000s, TE = 32 ms, flip angle = 70 degrees, 87 slices, FOV = 256 mm, acquisition voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3).

Preprocessing was performed using the CONN Toolbox (Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon 2012) in MATLAB in the following steps: realignment to the first image, outlier detection using the Artifact Detection Toolbox for motion correction (outliers were defined as scans exceeding 3 SDs of global signal or >1 mm in composite motion from the prior scan), direct functional segmentation and normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template, and smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Temporal preprocessing was conducted using the aCompCor strategy, which takes a principal component analysis approach to identify components reflecting noise in the white matter, regions of noninterest, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon 2012). Signal from these components, along with estimated motion, and outliers in global signal and movement, are subsequently regressed from the BOLD signal. Data were band pass filtered using 0.01–0.5 Hz.

Whole brain correlations with awkwardness

Following Pantelis et al. (2015), we conducted first-level general linear models in the whole brain using the continuous awkwardness ratings convolved with the hemodynamic response function (HRF) as the main variable. Movement parameters and outlier scans identified with the Artifact Detection Tools were included as covariates. These maps were submitted to second-level analyses in which we evaluated voxels positively correlated with the awkwardness ratings within each group, and then group differences in voxels that demonstrated a correlation with the awkwardness ratings. Like Pantelis et al. (2015), we used a cluster-wise false discovery rate (FDR) corrected threshold of P < .05 with a voxel-defining threshold of P < .01, uncorrected.

ROI analysis

Following Pantelis et al. (2015), we conducted hypothesis-driven region-of-interest (ROI) analysis in regions of the ToM network defined from a large-scale study evaluating neural correlates of verbal false-belief task performance in a healthy community sample (Dufour et al. 2013): dorsal/middle/ventral medial prefrontal cortex (d/m/vmPFC), left/right temporal parietal junction (l/rTPJ), right superior temporal sulcus (rSTS), and precuneus (PC). We extracted subject-level Fisher r-to-z values representing the mean correlation between awkwardness and neural activity in all voxels in each ROI and compared these values between groups with Welch’s two-sample t-tests (two-tailed). Effect sizes are provided as Cohen’s d and are accompanied by 95% bias-corrected-and-accelerated (BCa) CI derived from 10 000 bootstrap samples. These analyses were performed in R Statistical Software (R Core Team 2021) and RStudio (RStudio Team 2020).

Connectivity in the ToM network

Although our primary aim was to evaluate SSD-related alterations in the brain-basis of social awkwardness, one limitation of using just this approach is that we might miss important SSD-related alterations in social processing that are not directly related to awkwardness. Analyzing functional connectivity across the entire episode allows us to test for potential SSD-related alterations in social information processing beyond awkwardness specifically. To evaluate overall connectivity in the ToM network while participants viewed The Office, irrespective of awkwardness, we calculated the average connectivity between all 21, ROI-to-ROI connections in the ToM network. As we found groups differences in functional connectivity, we followed-up this analysis by evaluating group differences in individual ROI-to-ROI connectivity. These analyses were conducted using Welch’s two-sample t-tests (two-tailed) in R with an FDR-corrected P value for the 21 tests.

Behavioral correlations with neural data

We evaluated the association between our main neural measures (awkwardness correlation with ToM ROIs and ToM average functional connectivity) and behavioral measures, including SFS, PQ-B, and PANSS, using Pearson’s correlations (two-tailed) in R. These values are accompanied by 95% BCa CI derived from 10 000 bootstrap samples. As we expected these associations to exist regardless of diagnostic status, we conducted them across all participants and evaluated diagnostic status as a moderator by conducting follow-up regressions that include a predictor by group interaction term.

Results

Group characteristics

There was a greater proportion of males versus females in the SSD versus non-SSD group and a significant difference in race between the two groups (Table 1). However, analyses including sex and race as a covariate did not change the main findings (see supplementary materials).

Episode attention check scores were high, indicating that participants generally paid close attention, and scores did not significantly differ between groups (Table 1). Familiarity with the show and episode did not differ significantly between groups. Given that these variables could influence how one processes the show, we performed follow-up analyses that controlled for show familiarity and found that the results did not change (see Supplementary Table 2).

Neural correlates of awkwardness

Whole brain analyses

We first searched the whole brain for voxels that were associated with the awkwardness ratings, separately within each group. Across all non-SSD participants, clusters of voxels within regions of the ToM network, including r/lTPJ, rSTS, PC, and d/mmPFC, were correlated with the degree of moment-to-moment awkwardness (Fig. 1a). The SSD group showed similar results, although the degree of correlation was less widespread in these regions, and there were no significant clusters in PC (Fig. 1b).

Next, we evaluated whether the association between neural activity and awkwardness differed between groups in the whole brain (Fig. 2). We found two clusters in the ToM network where the non-SSD group demonstrated an increased association between neural activity and awkwardness compared to the SSD group: dmPFC and mmPFC (k = 513, peak voxel MNI coordinate [−14, 50, 32]). ROI analysis

Using the a priori ROIs from Dufour et al. (2013), we conducted hypothesis-driven ROI analysis to evaluate group differences in the ToM regions’ associations with awkwardness. Consistent with the whole-brain analysis, we found that neural activity in dmPFC (t(56) = 3.96, FDR-p = 0.002, d = 1.02, 95%CI [0.49, 1.53]) (Fig. 3a) and mmPFC (Fig. 3b) (t(58) = 2.62, FDR-p = 0.039, d = 0.68, 95%CI [0.15, 1.14]) was less strongly associated with the awkwardness ratings in SSD compared to non-SSD group.

Between group differences in ToM network connectivity

It is possible that individuals with SSD’s ToM-related brain regions may show more global alterations across the entire episode, which involves social dynamics beyond those observed in just socially awkward situations. To evaluate this possibility, we tested group differences in functional connectivity within the ToM network (i.e. averaging across all possible ROI-to-ROI connections). We found that there was an overall decrease in functional connectivity in the SSD compared to non-SSD group (Fig. 4a) (t(46) = 2.88 P = .008, d = 0.72, 95% CI [0.15, 1.28]).

The following regions had a significantly lower functional connectivity in SSD versus non-SSD group (Fig. 4b-e): dmPFC-PC (t(47) = 2.8, FDR-p = 0.032, d = 0.72, 95% CI [0.17, 1.48]), mmPFC-PC (t(48) = 2.8, q < 0.001, d = 1.03, 95% CI [0.47, 1.51]), PC-vmPFC (t(50) = 2.81, FDR-p = 0.007, d = 0.73, 95% CI [0.22, 1.19]), mmPFC-vmPFC (t(55) = 3.4, FDR-p = .001, d = 0.87, 95% CI [0.25, 1.46), mmPFC-rTPJ (t(47) 3.0, FDR-p = 0.005, d = 0.78, 95% CI [0.24,1.29]).

Brain–behavior correlations

We examined how our primary neural measures of interest (association with awkwardness and overall ToM connectivity) were associated with symptoms and social functioning across all participants. None of the neural measures in any ToM ROI was associated with PANSS scores in the SSD group (see supplementary material for statistics). However, across all participants, psychotic-like experiences as measured with the PQ-B were negatively correlated with the extent to which the dmPFC tracked with awkwardness (Fig. 5a) (r(56) = −0.32, P = .015, 95% CI [−.51, −0.08]) and average connectivity of the ToM network (Fig. 5b) (r(56) = −0.39, P = .002, 95% CI [−.57, −0.14]). These findings were unchanged when excluding one non-SSD outlier in the PQB (dmPFC-awkwardness: r(55) = −0.31, P = .021, ToM network connectivity: r(55) = −0.40, P = .002). In other words, the more dmPFC tracked with awkwardness, and the more ToM brain regions were functionally connected during the episode, the fewer the psychotic-like experiences. Follow-up linear regression models showed that group did not moderate either association (awkwardness association: interaction b =−0.001, SE = 0.004, P = .849; functional connectivity: interaction b =−0.007, SE = 0.007, P = .338). These findings were also unchanged when excluding the one non-SSD outlier on the PQB (Ps > .366).

We found a positive correlation between the dmPFC-awkwardness association and social functioning, meaning the more the dmPFC tracked with awkwardness, the better the social functioning across all participants (Fig. 5c) (r(56) = 0.31, P = .018, 95% CI [0.03, 0.50]). Discussion

Summary

Here, following Pantelis et al. (2015), we used a naturalistic social stimulus to assess the neural bases of social information processing in SSD. We replicated findings from Pantelis et al. (2015) demonstrating that ToM brain regions track with the degree of moment-to-moment awkwardness in non-SSD individuals. This reflects the importance of mental state attribution in processing a realistic, dynamic, and complex social stimulus. In SSD individuals, neural activity in dmPFC and mmPFC tracked less with moment-by-moment awkwardness compared to non-SSD. When looking at the connectivity of the ToM network across the entire episode, independent of awkwardness, we found reduced overall connectivity within the ToM network for SSD versus non-SSD group, suggesting that disrupted neural activity in SSD is not simply restricted to processing awkwardness. Brain–behavior correlations showed the magnitude of association between awkwardness and neural activity in the dmPFC and ToM network connectivity was associated with psychotic experiences and social functioning across all participants. This provides evidence of real-world clinical and social consequences for how the brain processes social information.

mPFC exhibits reduced association with awkwardness in SSD

The reduced correlation between mPFC activity and awkwardness in SSD could speak to a variety of social information processes that may go awry when viewing a naturalistic social stimulus. However, much of what we know about the function of the mPFC has come from more traditional fMRI paradigms utilizing static stimuli, such as vignettes describing social interactions, and requiring explicit judgement of the stimuli presented. Our analyses based on naturalistic video provide converging evidence of differences in mPFC in SSD using a different, more externally-valid experimental paradigm.

Research using more traditional fMRI techniques has shown that the mPFC has been shown to process stable and enduring intrapersonal states, such as personality characteristics, attitudes, and preferences, as well as social scripts, like how someone may be expected to act based on social norms (Van Overwalle 2009). Therefore, reduced mPFC activity during awkward scenarios in SSD may reflect SSD individuals missing subtle context- or interpersonal-related social cues (e.g. how someone should behave based on the social scenario or their personality/preferences) that hamper the accuracy of mental state attribution. Other work has implicated mPFC in representing abstracted emotion information (Skerry and Saxe 2014, 2015), tracking the consistency of others’ mental states, and predicting others’ mental states (Dungan et al. 2016, Thornton et al. 2019, Kim 2020, McManus et al. 2023). These processes are ostensibly critical while viewing a dynamic, awkward, norm-violating, social stimulus. Additionally, these awkward moments of interest may involve inferring mental states of multiple individuals simultaneously, which could place increased demands on the mPFC (Lewis et al. 2017). Less modulation of mPFC activity in SSD participants as a function of awkwardness could suggest that SSD individuals are less able to integrate the characters’ emotional responses to the violations of social norms. This could lead to differences in generating prediction about the characters’ behaviors or intentions. This interpretation is consistent with evidence of SSD behavioral impairments in processing emotions, predicting social information, and making intrapersonal attributions (Halverson et al. 2019, Thibaudeau, et al., 2023).

The mPFC has also been implicated in playing a domain-general role in cognition associated with reducing uncertainty or ambiguity (Jenkins and Mitchell 2010, Berkay and Jenkins 2023). This may be at play in the context of particularly awkward scenarios where the action or words violate social norms, so it might not be clear how the characters will react to these unexpected violations. People with SSD consistently demonstrate a “jumping to conclusions” bias that is characterized by rapid and unjustified certainty in interpretations and decisions (Dudley et al. 2016, So et al. 2016, McLean et al. 2017). Related to our findings, less mPFC tracking of awkwardness could reflect individuals with SSD forming judgments about others’ mental states more confidently and more quickly with less evidence. In other words, patients with SSD may not be treating inherently ambiguous stimuli as ambiguous. This could carry substantial negative implications for real-world social interaction as it might contribute to inappropriately confident social inferences and fewer attempts at perspective-taking (Eyal et al. 2018). This could leave the individual unable to meet their social partner’s needs and social partners feeling misunderstood.

Other work has demonstrated associations between ToM, symptoms, and social cognition (Fett et al. 2011, Halverson et al. 2019, Thibaudeau et al. 2023). Consistent with these behavioral findings, we found a positive correlation between dmPFC tracking with awkwardness and the SFS total score. We also found a negative correlation between the number of psychotic experiences and dmPFC neural tracking of awkwardness suggesting a continuum between psychosis experiences, disorders, and associated social phenomena. The fact that we did not observe similar associations just within the SSD group may reflect reduced power. Alternatively, both set of associations may be inflated given that we found between-group differences in all variables tested. Thus, these particular associations should be interpreted with care and be treated as preliminary additional evidence that, along with other work, suggests the behavioral and clinical importance of ToM and its neural bases.

We found an unexpected group difference in voxels in the rSTG, where the SSD group showed a smaller association between neural activity and awkwardness, compared to non-SSD group. This finding could point toward well-documented language deficits in SSD that come to bear during socially critical moments (Shaw et al. 2013, Jiang et al. 2016, Koelsch et al. 2018). Given that this area also processes more basic acoustic features, this finding could also point to differences in understanding social meaning conveyed by language acoustic features, such as the characters talking louder or softer during these awkward moments. Future studies could parse out the role of language and acoustic processing in SSD by looking at how well the brain tracks language content during The Office.

SSD versus ASD

Using a similar study design, Pantelis et al. (2015) found that voxels in the rTPJ/rSTS tracked with moment-by-moment awkwardness less in the ASD group. Given that patients with ASD and SSD have similar behavioral deficits in ToM, as shown by individual studies and meta-analyses (Fernandes et al. 2018, Pinkham et al. 2020, Oliver et al. 2021), one possibility is that we would see similar group differences in neural disruption. Alternatively, while behavioral deficits are similar, they may manifest in different nodes of the ToM network, suggesting that the underlying causes of those behavioral impairments may differ (Dodell-Feder and Germine 2018, Crespi 2020, Pinkham et al. 2020, Hudson et al. 2023). Indeed, other work evaluating the brain-basis of biological motion have uncovered divergent neural mechanisms associated with schizotypal and autistic traits, despite similar behavioral phenotypes (Hudson et al. 2023). While our study did not include ASD individuals, we were able to make an informal comparison to the Pantelis et al. study because we used the exact same stimuli and similar analysis methods. We found evidence for the latter scenario: both of our whole brain and ROI associations with awkwardness differences converge on the dmPFC and mmPFC, with these regions having decreased tracking with awkwardness in the SSD group, with no differences in rTPJ/rSTS, as was found in previous studies with ASD. While these findings are consistent with some other work in SSD and ASD, they should be interpreted with caution, as we did not directly compare the two clinical populations within our study. In addition to Pantelis et al.’s finding of reduced neural activity in rTPJ/rSTS, others have found that in ASD, rTPJ demonstrates reduced selectivity for mental state information, which was associated with the degree of social impairment, but no group differences in mPFC (Lombardo et al. 2015). Conversely, other work has shown that SSD individuals demonstrate reduced selectivity for mental state information in the mPFC, which was similarly associated with social impairment, but no group differences in TPJ (Dodell-Feder et al. 2014). In another study that directly compared ASD to SSD individuals on a ToM task, SSD individuals showed reduced neural activity in the dMPFC when making inferences regarding communicative intention (Ciaramidaro et al. 2015). As rTPJ has been shown to be most selective for mental state information in the ToM network (Saxe and Wexler 2005, Perner et al. 2006, Saxe and Powell 2006) and activated most consistently when inferring transient mental states, beliefs, and intentions (Van Overwalle 2009), collectively, the brain imaging evidence suggests that behavioral differences in ToM may have unique etiologies in ASD and SSD. If confirmed, these findings could generate new work designed to improve the efficacy of existing interventions or designing new ones better tailored to a specific group.

ToM network connectivity is reduced in SSD

Using functional connectivity, we find that SSD individuals have lower overall connectivity within the ToM network. This suggests that the social brain in SSD is largely disconnected while processing naturalistic social stimuli, irrespective of the degree of awkwardness. Follow-up analyses showed that the PC is less connected to all three subdivisions of the mPFC. This mPFC to PC connection is thought to be involved in constructing personal meaning from stimuli, suggesting that social partners or events may be experienced as less personally meaningful or relatable (Holt et al. 2011, Andrews-Hanna et al. 2014). Our finding of less connectivity between the mmPFC and rTPJ might mean that information from the rTPJ about monitoring current moment to moment mental states of others (Koster-Hale and Saxe 2013, Filmer et al. 2019) might not be integrated with individual social preconceptions and trait judgements of others via the mmPFC. Importantly, these interpretations, and those above, are based on reverse inference from primarily non-naturalistic tasks, so future research examining these connections during specific scenes of the video evoking specific social processes would be helpful for better understanding these functional connectivity differences. Further, because these connectivity findings were not anchored to a specific feature, like awkwardness, it is possible that group differences in connectivity could reflect a number of different processes. That said, because we evaluated connectivity in regions that were functionally defined as being selective for mental state information, it is reasonable to assume that these differences reflect, at least in part, alterations in social processes.

Regardless of what the connectivity findings mean in terms of social information processing, dysconnectivity of the ToM network converges with previous studies showing decreased functional connectivity in SSD (Mothersill et al. 2017, Park et al. 2020), and is generally consistent with other findings showing network dysconnectivity in SSD (Uhlhaas 2013, Friston et al. 2016, Brandl et al. 2019). This view is further supported by our finding of an association between ToM network connectivity and the number of psychotic-like experiences. It would be interesting for future work to determine if these connectivity differences are due to just an overall decrease in functional connectivity or, alternatively, a specific decrease in connectivity of the ToM network in SSD.

While the use of naturalistic video paradigms in SSD is still evolving, other studies have applied analytic techniques that may shed insight into the nature of social alterations beyond the conventional functional connectivity analysis performed here, such as intersubject correlation (ISC). For example, one such study used a silent film paradigm to find a decreased ISC in SSD in part of the TPJ that was correlated with the specific ToM process of integrating lower-level facial expressions with higher level social processing (Patel et al. 2021). Other studies suggest that patients with schizophrenia have particular problems recruiting the mPFC during social content conveyed by gestures (He et al. 2021). Given the difference between these visual-only stimuli and our audio-visual stimuli, it is difficult to directly compare these paradigms, as it is possible that participants pay closer attention to ToM resulting from verbal cues when language is present. Other studies using ISC confirm that there are decreases in ISC in various ToM regions during video stimuli, including the mPFC, for both patients with first-episode psychosis (Mäntylä et al. 2018) and controls with high schizotypy traits (Finn et al. 2018). These results indicate that there may be widespread differences in activation patterns in the ToM network for patients with SSD, and illustrate how naturalistic stimuli along with different analytic techniques can shed light on this important issue.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations are notable. First, our sample size was modest meaning that we may be overestimating effects. Second, we largely focused on how neural activity in the ToM network is associated with a single social dimension of the episode, that being awkwardness. Although evaluating the association between awkwardness and ToM activity using an approach as similar as possible to Pantelis et al. (2015) was our goal, there are likely other important social dimensions that may be explaining neural differences in SSD, such as the number of faces onscreen, degree of surprise, or valence/intensity of emotion, among others. Furthermore, these findings, especially the FC analyses that are not tied to a behavioral construct, could be partially explained by differences in processing nonsocial aspects of the stimuli, such as perceptual processing. It is also important to note that the awkwardness ratings we use are based on a normative sample, so it is possible that the patient group could differ in which moments they find awkward. Third, given that we were trying to match our analysis to Pantelis et al., we restricted the ROIs to those evaluated in Patenlis, and did not include subdivisions of regions that are known to have distinct roles, such as the temporal pole (Saxe and Powell 2006). Additionally, although we modeled our analyses after Pantelis et al., there exist a host of analytic techniques that could usefully be applied to the data collected here, which may also help to uncover important insights regarding the nature of social information processing alterations in SSD. Fourth, the magnitude of our correlations may be inflated due to the clustering of data points since the groups differed on all the dimensions evaluated (i.e. neural connectivity, psychotic experiences, social functioning). Fifth, as discussed, while we used the same stimuli and a similar analysis to Pantelis et al., this study can only intimate, and not directly test differing etiologies for similar social cognitive phenotypes in SSD and ASD. Finally, as mentioned, much of our interpretations of group differences rely on reverse inference. It would be important for future work to test some of the ideas we speculate about here with paradigms that manipulate those processes we suspect might be contributing to the alterations observed in the SSD group.

Conclusion

Here we find evidence that the mPFC is not recruited as much during awkward moments in SSD, which suggests difficulties with ToM in the context of a dynamic and realistic social stimulus. In contrast to some of our findings, Pantelis et al. (2015) found that ASD individuals show differences in the rTPJ/rSTS using the same paradigm. This could implicate a different neural mechanism for similar behavioral ToM deficits between ASD and SSD. We also find a widespread decrease in functional connectivity in the ToM network for individuals with SSD, providing converging evidence for ToM network functioning impairment in SSD. Importantly, neural response to the stimulus was associated with both psychotic experiences and social functioning. Together our findings have implications for better understanding ToM deficits in SSD that could inform interventions tailored to specific brain differences in SSD.
