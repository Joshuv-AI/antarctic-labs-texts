Incognito Standardized Patients to Assess Physician Awareness and Capacity to Recognize Primary Syphilis Presentation in Chinese STI Clinics
Authors: M. Kumi Smith, Ligang Yang, Hui Xie, Joseph Tucker, Danyang Luo, Siyan Meng, Yunqing Fei, Liuyuan Wang, Xiaohui Zhang, Cheng Wang, Bin Yang, Sean Y. Sylvia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Primary syphilis is frequently undertested in clinical settings due to its protean presentation. China is experiencing a mass syphilis resurgence, but medical community knowledge of syphilis remains low. We assessed clinical performance of Chinese sexually transmitted infection (STI) physicians using data collected by “incognito” or standardized patients (SPs).Methods:SPs presented cases of postprimary syphilis to physicians who consented to visits in advance but were not told the timing. SPs reported details on diagnostics and examinations performed by the physician. Logistic regression models identified predictors of performance metrics, adjusting for HIV status and sexual orientation of presented cases.Results:One hundred twenty-three visits were conducted with 41 physicians. Physicians recommended syphilis testing in 84.5% of visits and performed at least 2 of 5 recommended clinical indicators in 31.7% of visits. Physicians were less likely to offer syphilis testing to patients with HIV, whether presenting as men who have sex with men (odds ratio [OR], 0.06; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.01–0.57) or as straight men (OR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.03–0.61). Those working >40 h a week were less likely to offer testing (OR, 0.3; 95% CI, 0.09–1.01), and those older than the median age were less likely to perform a thorough exam (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.14–0.83).Conclusions:Chinese STI physicians frequently test patients for syphilis but are less likely to conduct thorough physical examinations. The undertesting of patients with HIV exposes missed opportunities to address the HIV/syphilis syndemic. Future trainings must emphasize the public health importance of dual screening and address physician concerns about occupational HIV exposure.

In the absence of an effective vaccine, testing and treatment remain the cornerstones of population-level syphilis control. A global resurgence of syphilis, particularly in gay and bisexual men who have sex with men (MSM), has sounded calls for more aggressive testing and treatment.1,2 Syphilis testing is particularly important for people living with HIV who are more susceptible to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and in whom active STIs can increase the risk of onward HIV transmission.3 Testing-based solutions to arrest the spread of syphilis rely greatly, however, on the ability of outpatient providers to discern and diagnose the condition across diverse patient types. Its notoriously protean clinical manifestations have earned syphilis the title, the “great imitator,” and syphilis diagnostic are often difficult to interpret.4 Success of comprehensive syphilis control policies will therefore require providers to implement testing and behavioral strategies for success.

Our team assessed syphilis-related clinical performance among physicians in STI clinics in a high-transmission setting in southern China. These assessments were conducted to measure stigma in clinical settings as part of a larger trial to design and evaluate a stigma reduction training for providers.5 Assessments were accomplished by training incognito standardized patients (SPs) to present as real patients in participating clinics. SPs presented identical cases of primary syphilis, and their observations were documented immediately after each visit using a standardized checklist. Physicians consented to visits in advance but were not told when they would happen, allowing direct observation of clinical behaviors and limiting the Hawthorne effect (in which providers alter behaviors when under observation).6 Stigma was quantified by varying the HIV status and sexual orientation across SP visits and calculating the difference in care quality received across presented scenarios. Results of our findings on baseline stigma are in preparation, and the preliminary impact of our intervention is in preprint at the time of writing.5 Here we analyze data collected by SPs to share insights on the state of physicians’ real-time capacity to recognize, diagnose, and manage syphilis in a high-transmission setting.

Direct knowledge of the syphilis-related skills in their healthcare workforce can provide policymakers with valuable insights into key training gaps and preparedness of health systems for rising syphilis rates. Our study site of China is particularly instructive, where rapid economic development in the past few decades has created conditions—increased migration, growing income inequality, and changing norms around sex—that have fueled a large syphilis resurgence.7 And as in many other rapidly developing settings, providers in China are thought to be less informed about the clinical presentation of syphilis, as many received training when low prevalence made clinical encounters a rare occurence.8 In these ways, both our methodological approach and choice of study site can provide valuable insights into public health challenges faced globally. In addition, this assessment provides a useful baseline to which future assessments can be compared or to evaluate the systems-level impact of clinical interventions.

METHODS

Data used for this analysis come from the baseline round of SP visits conducted as part of a pilot cluster randomized control trial to preliminarily evaluate the impact of a stigma reduction training for STI providers in Guangzhou, China. Guangzhou is the capital of the most populous province in China, Guangdong, and its syphilis epidemic in MSM is representative of regional trends. A nationwide survey in 2013 reported that 13.8% of Guangdong MSM were currently infected with syphilis, the sixth highest prevalence rate in China’s 31 provinces.9 More recent data suggest that syphilis incidence is still high in Guangdong MSM, although it may be slowing.10 Research activities took place in close partnership with the Dermatology Hospital of the Southern Medical University (SMU), which oversees STI control policy, disease surveillance, and clinical standards of care throughout the province. Engagement with STI clinics and their providers was facilitated by SMU’s provincial network of over 400 practices with whom it maintains regular communications. Key aspects of our study design and preliminary interpretation of baseline results were also informed by iterative consultations with 2 community advisory boards (CAB), one made up of local STI providers and a second made up of MSM.

Sampling

We restricted SMU’s roster of STI clinics to those located in Guangzhou as our clinic sampling frame. We used a 2-step recruitment process, consisting first of direct contact with clinic or ward directors to brief them on study design and to obtain a roster of eligible providers at their clinic. Eligibility criteria for practices included: (1) has formal government medical accreditation and (2) has the capacity to provide enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay testing for HIV, treponemal (e.g., Treponema pallidum particle agglutination), and nontreponemal tests (e.g., rapid plasma reagin) for syphilis. Our second stage of recruitment involved individual-level engagement with interested physicians at each practice in which we explained study goals, answered questions, and obtained consent. Eligibility criteria for providers included (1) at least 18 y of age; (2) certified to provide STI-related care in Guangdong Province, and (3) planning to remain at the practice clinic for at least 1 y. Consent procedures included instructions to ensure that physicians do not confront suspected SPs and to instead document visit details that could then be later verified with the study team to confirm their suspicions. This information also provides an estimated rate of SP detection for the team. Upon enrollment, physicians responded to a brief survey to provide information on demographics and professional background. At each clinic, we also completed a facility-level survey to collect relevant information about clinics, including staff size and patient load.

SP Training and Clinic Visits

The simulated case involved a young male between the ages of 20 and 40 y presenting with symptoms consistent with primary syphilis (i.e., a recently healed ulcer on the penis). Primary syphilis was selected for its protean features because it allowed for healthy volunteers to plausibly present a realistic case. In addition, by randomly varying the HIV status and sexual orientation presented in each visit, our strategy allowed us to generate 4 case scenarios: a straight man not living with HIV, an MSM not living with HIV, a straight man living with HIV, and an MSM living with HIV. Although these scenarios were originally devised as a means of measuring stigma for the parent study, for the purposes of this analysis, the case diversity served as a useful test of provider performance when presented with patients from the highest burden populations: MSM, people living with HIV, and those with both identities.

Recruitment and training of SPs were oriented around the 2 goals of achieving realistic, standardized case presentations and of harmonizing SP assessment of provider performance metrics. We recruited and trained SPs in close partnership with the Zhitong LGBT Center, a Guangzhou-based community-based organization specializing in LGBT+ advocacy and health promotion. The SP training took place over the course of 2 d and involved didactic sessions on syphilis pathology and epidemiology, role plays, discussion of checklist items, and field testing to refine the case script and build SP confidence.

Each provider received 3 visits by a randomly sampled set of SPs who each presented 1 of the 4 case scenarios selected at random. Visits were conducted in a specialized order that randomized the specific SP, the presented scenario, and the order of scenarios across enrolled physicians. Since each physician received 3 visits, repeat sessions were spaced out by at least 2 wk to reduce the risk of detection. Immediately after each visit, enumerators who accompanied SPs on visits administered the healthcare quality checklist with the SP to record details of the visit, as well as to conduct an emotional debrief to ensure SPs’ mental well-being in the event of a challenging encounter. The SP team met routinely as a group throughout the study to discuss visit logistics and technique and to take part in drills to maintain high interrated reliability on provider performance assessments.

Measures

Data collection was conducted using a healthcare quality checklist whose design was informed both by China’s national syphilis clinical guidelines8 and input from our separate CAB of providers and MSM. Items capture both clinical aspects of care—for example, testing and physical examinations—and provision of relevant sexual health counseling. Checklist items were then operationalized into 3 primary outcomes as follows. Syphilis testing indicated whether the physician ordered a syphilis test and was measured as a binary indicator (yes/no). Thoroughness of patient exam indicated the extent to which providers completed the 5 most critical examination tasks as indicated by our physician CAB members: examination of chancre site, checking of lymph nodes, sexual history taking around partner numbers, sexual history taking around same sex behaviors, and appropriate patient privacy measures (e.g., closing the exam room door). Finally, sexual health counseling indicated the extent to which providers offered relevant sexual health counseling and included whether they offered advice on sexual behaviors (e.g., abstaining until diagnosis or cure), condom use, or partner-related counseling such as disclosure of diagnosis or partner testing.

For coding, thoroughness of patient exam was dichotomized into 2 categories: more thorough (completion of 2 or more items) and less thorough (completion of only 1 item) patient exams. Sexual health counseling was dichotomized into 2 categories: at least one form of counseling versus none. Although both variables were informed by instruments that captured multiple dimensions of each domain (e.g., 5 exam-related tasks, 3 counseling elements), our decision to use dichotomous coding was justified by the need for results to be communicable to stakeholders involved in our provider training design. Our limited sample size (41 physicians, 123 visits) also meant that more granular scoring would have led to small cell sizes and unstable estimates in adjusted models. To ensure clinical relevance, thresholds for dichotomization of these variables were selected with the input of clinicians in our study team and the provider CAB.

In addition, brief qualitative interviews were conducted with each SP after each visit to capture richer data on more complex events such as provider-initiated referrals to other healthcare facilities or adverse events.

Statistical Analysis

We first explored provider and clinic characteristics and performance metrics using frequencies and chi-square tests for categorical variables and t tests for continuous variables. Logistic regression models were then used to identify provider and clinic characteristics associated with provider performance. Models were adjusted for the presented case scenario; that is, the HIV status and sexual orientation that each SP presented. Finally, an exchangeable covariance matrix accounted for intraphysician correlations.

Ethical Approvals

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of Minnesota, the University of North Carolina, and the Dermatology Hospital of SMU. All study participants provided written informed consent in Chinese.

RESULTS

A total of 41 physicians working across 17 STI facilities took part in study visits between March and July of 2021. Physician characteristics are presented in Table 1. Physicians were on average 42 y of age (standard deviation, 9), mostly male (62%), of the assistant- or intermediate-level rank (63%, vs associate- or senior-level clinicians), and worked out of STI wards within larger health entities (93%, vs standalone outpatient clinics). The minority worked more than 40 h a week (29%), half had 45 or fewer patients a week (average 9 daily; 51%), and in regards to fairness of their salary, most (71%) answered “fair” or “neutral.”

In the 123 visits conducted, no adverse events were reported. Physicians offered syphilis testing 84.5% of the time. Critically, syphilis testing was offered least often in encounters where SPs presented case scenarios of MSM living with HIV (64.5%). Testing was offered most frequently in case scenarios of straight men not living with HIV (96.7%), followed by MSM not living with HIV (93.5%), and finally, straight men living with HIV (83.9%). Exam thoroughness—that is, completion of at least 2 of 5 key examination tasks—was evident in 31.7% of SP visits. Although not shown in the tables, the least performed of the 5 examination tasks (i.e., examination of chancre site, checking of lymph nodes, sexual history taking around partner numbers, sexual history taking around same sex behaviors, and appropriate patient privacy measures) were the checking of lymph nodes (0.8%) and sexual history taking about partner volume (9.8%) or same sex behaviors (9.8%). Finally, sexual health counseling of any kind was offered in 19.5% of encounters. Of the 3 indicators that make up the sexual counseling indicator, advice on sexual behaviors was offered the least often (3.3%), followed by partner-related counseling (7.3%), then advice on condom use (11.4%).

Results of case scenario–adjusted models are presented in Table 2. Results show that providers who worked more than 40 h a week were less likely to offer syphilis tests (odds ratio [OR], 0.3; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.09–1.01), although this estimate lacked statistical significance. In addition, those who were female, more senior ranking, or who worked out of outpatient clinics were more likely to offer SPs a syphilis test (OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 0.45–4.63; OR: 2.34, 95% CI: 0.7–7.79; OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 0.17–10.75, respectively); these estimates also lacked statistical precision. For thoroughness, providers older than the median age of 42 were less likely to conduct a thorough clinical exam (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.14–0.83), whereas more senior-ranking providers were more likely to do so (OR, 1.36, 95% CI, 0.57–3.23), although the latter estimate lacked precision. Finally, sexual health counseling was more likely to be offered by female providers (OR, 2.5, 95% CI, 0.98–6.35) but less so by providers with higher patient loads (i.e., weekly averages of more than 45 a week; OR, 0.37, 95% CI, 0.16–0.87), although again, the latter estimate lacked precision.

Regarding associations between clinical performance and the case scenario presented by the SP (i.e., the sexual orientation and HIV status), we found that when SPs presented as straight HIV positive men or as HIV positive gay men, they were far less likely to be offered syphilis testing (OR, 0.12, 95% CI, 0.02-0.61). But for physical exams and counseling, measures seriously lacked precision, not surprising since we were not powered to look at this.

Finally, we documented 7 instances in which providers advised the SP to seek care at other healthcare facilities (Table 3). In the 2 such referral events in which SPs were presenting as patients not living with HIV, providers justified the referral based on the supposed expediency and superior accuracy of the STI testing service of their recommended facility. But in 4 of the 5 instances when SPs presenting as patients living with HIV were referred elsewhere, providers explicitly recommended they visit Guangzhou’s main HIV treatment center.

DISCUSSION

Our study documented high rates of clinically appropriate diagnostic screening (84.6%) among Chinese STI providers presented with a classical case or penile ulcer. However, this testing was offered unevenly across tested case scenarios, in which SPs presenting as MSM living with HIV were 94% less likely to be offered a test compared with a straight man not living with HIV. This highlights a critical service gap for 2 populations—MSM and people living with HIV—who merit the most careful screening on account of their elevated STI risk and numerous healthcare access barriers.11–14 Another key finding was the infrequency with which study physicians provided thorough clinical assessments or sexual health counseling across all case scenarios. Although ease and accuracy of syphilis serodiagnosis have improved in recent years, the process can still be complicated by the stage of infection15 or phenomena such as the prozone effect.16 Taking accurate sexual histories is therefore important for correct diagnosis and management, as is safe sex counseling for prevention of onward transmission during the treatment process. Members of our provider CAB hypothesized that providers’ reluctance to examine or counsel may stem from a lack of training in talking to marginalized patients about sex. Although such theories have yet to be empirically tested, our findings highlight important service gaps in health system preparedness to face already increasing caseloads of syphilis.

Structural factors may also have played a role in physicians’ greater reluctance to offer syphilis testing to patients living with HIV, particularly those also reporting same sex behaviors. Lessons from China’s recent history of HIV control and input from both our CABs are instructive on this front. In short, China’s national system of subsidized HIV specialty care, while successful at reducing HIV mortality, has also resulted in segregated systems of care for patients with and without HIV. As a result, non-HIV specialists are far less likely to encounter or have any training in the management of HIV, leading to the well-documented phenomenon of providers needlessly referring HIV patients to infectious disease clinics for care.17,18 Indeed, our data showed that needless referrals to other healthcare facilities were 2.5 times more common when SPs presented as living with HIV (5 vs 2 instances) and 1.3 times more likely when presenting as MSM (4 vs 3 instances; Table 3). Whether such referrals are motivated by fear, aversion, provider’s earnest beliefs about patients’ best interests, or some combination of all these factors is difficult to ascertain empirically, and possible goal of future applications of the SP audit method. Regardless, our findings suggest that future training will benefit from the inclusion of practical skills in safe and respectful care of patients living with HIV.

Emergent in the profile of providers who provided more optimal care was that of the younger, female, and more senior-ranking providers. By contrast, providers less able to provide optimal care were older and bore heavier work burdens (i.e., larger patient loads, longer work weeks). These profiles prompt a rethinking of the conventional notion that higher caseloads or more work days drive positive patient outcomes.19 The fact that younger, more senior women outperformed their peers is also noteworthy, given how representative they are of the newer generation of providers in China and other middle-income countries.20,21 In China specifically, younger providers likely entered the workforce after the 2003 SARS outbreak, which heightened public awareness of infectious disease control and which spurred stronger policy responses to the emerging domestic HIV epidemic.22 The subsequent nationwide information campaigns about HIV may have also shaped how younger Chinese providers approach their work, not only regarding individual medicine but also about public health.

Finally, our finding that higher patient loads and longer work hours predict less ideal performance echoes findings from occupational studies in medicine, which report that burnout can impair patient outcomes.23 Burnout among healthcare workers is thought to undermine clinical performance by eroding provider job satisfaction and weakening their interest in relationships or the work environment.24,25 Providers in many settings, including China, are also spending larger portions of their workdays on clerical duties rather than patient care, likely contributing to role stress (i.e., the discrepancy between job expectations and personal values).26,27 The additional responsibilities of disease reporting and infection control imposed on Chinese healthcare workers during the COVID-19 epidemic likely exacerbated burnout among our participants, suggesting that our findings on clinician performance may be underestimates of prepandemic levels. Strategies to manage burnout have included programs that target either the individual (e.g., strategies to improve mindfulness, communication, or coping) or the organization (e.g., reductions in workload, increased pay, and improved control over work schedules). While most research on the topic promotes a 2-pronged approach, China-based scholarship warns that Western strategies for managing burnout will likely be underutilized by Chinese doctors unless efforts are made to integrate cultural perspectives into interventions.28

Findings presented here should be interpreted in light of several key study limitations. First, although several participating clinics expressed interest in reviewing SP-collected data on their own clinical performance, we did not share these results with participants. This decision was made as a precaution, given the absence of robust safeguards to prevent potential misuse of the data by employers for staff evaluation or disciplinary purposes. Second, as a pilot randomized controlled trial, this study was not powered to detect care quality differences across the demographic and professional characteristics considered. This is a likely explanation for the lack of precision in most estimates, although the magnitude and direction of effect are valuable for hypothesis generation.

Nevertheless, this study provides much-needed insights into the abilities of healthcare providers in a high-transmission setting to detect and manage syphilis in frontline clinics. Our findings show that the Chinese medical workforce is equipped to recognize and administer appropriate diagnostic tests. But we also identified gaps in their willingness or capacity to engage in the more time-consuming and emotionally involved process of examining patients’ sexual histories or discussing tailored risk reduction strategies. The short-term gains for providers who invest the extra time and effort in these activities may be minimal, but their potential impact on longer-term public health goals can be substantial. This underscores the need for closer collaboration between clinical practitioners and public health authorities to align their priorities for individual and population-level health.
