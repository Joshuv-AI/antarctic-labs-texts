Understanding the lived experiences of individuals with post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) in Rwanda: a phenomenological qualitative study
Authors: Iliza Grace Ndatinya, Simal Thind, Fernand Rwamwejo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo qualitatively explore the lived experiences of individuals with post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) in Rwanda, with the aim of informing context-appropriate interventions to improve patients’ quality of life.DesignQualitative, phenomenological study using in-depth interviews and thematic analysis.SettingAll provinces of Rwanda, including the City of Kigali.ParticipantsAdults who tested positive for PASC during a nationwide prevalence study were purposively sampled across all study sites. A total of 30 people were interviewed before saturation was reached.ResultsParticipants’ lived experiences with PASC were characterised by numerous themes. Despite describing significant life changes since their COVID-19 infection, they remained uncertain about the relationship between their ongoing symptoms and their previous COVID-19 infection. This uncertainty was worsened by healthcare providers who did not ask about COVID-19 history during consultations. Participants also described feelings of frustration and sadness caused by their inability to perform physically demanding jobs such as farming and manual labour, which had previously been their sources of income. Symptoms such as body weakness, persistent fatigue, breathlessness and memory loss contributed to financial hardship and difficulty meeting basic family needs, such as school fees and food, because participants were unable to work. For many participants, this financial pressure translated into constant fear, isolation and depression. Participants called for greater public awareness about PASC, more research into its symptoms and treatment and for healthcare providers to routinely ask patients about their previous COVID-19 infection. The results of this study also highlight the need for comprehensive approaches to address the physical, emotional and socioeconomic burden of PASC in Rwanda.ConclusionsIndividuals living with PASC in Rwanda face a compounded burden shaped by both the symptoms themselves and a broader environment of diagnostic uncertainty, limited specialist care and social stigma. These findings point to an urgent need for PASC-specific education for healthcare providers, public awareness initiatives and integrated psychosocial support within Rwanda’s existing community health infrastructure. Future research should explore feasible approaches to address the burden of PASC in low-resource settings.Keywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, PUBLIC HEALTH, SARS-CoV-2 Infection

Background

The WHO defines post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) as the continuation or emergence of new symptoms 3 months after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection.1 These symptoms must persist for at least 2 months and cannot be explained by any other diagnosis. PASC can affect anyone regardless of age, gender or the severity of their initial illness.2

Since the identification of the first COVID-19 case in 2019, extensive research has been conducted to understand the virus which quickly escalated into a global pandemic. Scientific efforts have focused on strategies to prevent infection, manage symptoms and ultimately develop effective treatments. As the pandemic progressed, healthcare professionals and researchers began to observe that many patients continued to experience lingering symptoms well beyond the acute phase of the disease. This led to the recognition of COVID-19 not only as an acute illness but, for many, as a long-term condition now referred to as PASC.3 This was especially noted by frontline healthcare professionals who were in direct contact with COVID-19 patients.4

Clinical manifestations of PASC are diverse and commonly include fatigue, breathlessness and musculoskeletal pain among other symptoms. Recent research suggests that between 10% and 50% of patients recovering from COVID-19 may go on to experience PASC symptoms.5 The duration and severity of these symptoms can vary considerably and are often influenced by factors such as age, gender and pre-existing conditions.6

The complex and persistent nature of PASC presents a considerable burden not only for patients but also for healthcare systems worldwide. As such, it calls for targeted research and the implementation of evidence-based policies to ensure comprehensive long-term care for affected individuals. Importantly, PASC symptoms such as chronic fatigue have not only physical implications but also profoundly impact psychological and social well-being.

Globally, qualitative studies have sought to explore the lived experiences of individuals suffering from PASC. A common finding is that many people feel deeply uncertain about their condition as they move from one consultation to the next, receive inconclusive test results and struggle to get a clear explanation for their symptoms. Recurring themes such as isolation, depression and emotional distress have emerged as central to patients’ experiences.7 For example, a study in the Netherlands highlighted how individuals with PASC experienced both internalised and societal stigma due to the invisible yet debilitating nature of their illness.8

This invisibility has wider consequences. Research has shown that people with PASC often face stigma both from within, where they begin to doubt whether their illness is real and from those around them, including employers, family members and communities who may not believe or understand what they are going through.7 Many patients also describe experiences of being dismissed by healthcare providers, with their symptoms being minimised or attributed to anxiety, which has led to delays in diagnosis and a loss of trust in the health system.6 Together, these findings show that the psychological and social burden of PASC is not just a result of the physical symptoms; it is also shaped by how healthcare systems and society respond to an illness that resists simple biomedical classification.

Importantly, almost all of this evidence comes from high-income western countries, where healthcare systems, levels of health literacy, cultural understandings of illness and social support structures are very different from those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Rwanda. It is not yet known whether people in these settings have similar experiences or whether there are specific challenges associated with living in a low-resource setting. This matters because care models and interventions developed in high-income countries may not work well if applied without adaptation to settings such as Rwanda.

In LMICs, including Rwanda, the experience of COVID-19 and its long-term effects are shaped by unique socioeconomic and cultural factors. These contexts can influence disease outcomes, access to care and coping mechanisms. Yet, despite the global nature of the pandemic, the experiences of individuals in these settings, particularly in relation to PASC, remain significantly underexplored.

Rwanda recorded its first COVID-19 case in March 2020 and had documented around 133 000 confirmed cases and 1468 deaths by March 2023.9 The pandemic followed a trajectory similar to global patterns, with waves linked to the Alpha, Delta and Omicron variants.10 Rwanda’s response was widely recognised for being fast and well-coordinated, and by September 2022, approximately 79% of the population had been fully vaccinated making Rwanda the only low-income country to exceed the WHO’s 70% vaccination target at that time.11 Despite this, very little is known about the long-term health consequences of COVID-19 for Rwandan survivors, or about how those consequences are experienced and navigated within Rwanda’s specific social and health system context.

Earlier studies in Rwanda during the pandemic looked at barriers to healthcare and how patients coped with disruptions to services, finding that many people found ways to manage despite significant challenges.9 More recently, a nationwide study found that around 34% of Rwandans with a history of symptomatic COVID-19 had persistent symptoms consistent with PASC, including fatigue, headache, dizziness, back pain and reduced sexual desire.12 While this tells us how common PASC is in Rwanda, it does not tell us what it is like to live with it or how people understand their symptoms.

This study therefore set out to explore and interpret the lived experiences of people with PASC in Rwanda, looking at how personal, social, cultural and health system factors shape what it means to live with a post-viral illness in a low-resource setting. The findings are intended to contribute to a better understanding of PASC beyond the high-income country context, provide evidence to inform care in Rwanda and similar settings and to interrogate whether existing models for understanding post-viral illness are transferable to low-income African contexts or require adaptation.

This study was guided by a phenomenological approach, which focuses on understanding how people make sense of and give meaning to their lived experiences. This approach was considered suitable for studying PASC, given that much of what makes the condition complex is the challenge of understanding what is happening to one’s body in the absence of a clear diagnosis. Rather than simply describing what participants reported, the study aimed to understand what these experiences meant to them and how they made sense of their situation. Thematic analysis was used to identify patterns of meaning across participants’ accounts, keeping the analysis grounded in participants’ own words.13

Research aim

To qualitatively explore and interpret the lived experiences of individuals with PASC in Rwanda examining how personal, social, cultural and health system factors influence the experience of living with a post-viral illness in a low-resource setting to inform context-specific care strategies for Rwanda and similar settings.

To address this aim, the study sought to describe the physical, psychological, social and economic dimensions of living with PASC in Rwanda and to interpret how these dimensions shape overall quality of life and daily functioning. It also critically examined individuals’ experiences of seeking care for PASC and how these experiences shaped their illness experience and psychosocial burden. Together, these strands of inquiry were intended to generate contextually relevant evidence to inform equitable care strategies and regional and global responses to post-viral illnesses.

Methodology

Setting

This study was conducted in Rwanda, a densely populated low-income country in East-Central Africa. Rwanda shares its borders with Uganda, Tanzania, Burundi and the Democratic Republic of Congo. Known for its mountainous terrain, the country has a predominantly rural population.

The country is administratively divided into five provinces: Northern, Southern, Eastern, Western and Kigali City, each with unique demographic, cultural and socioeconomic characteristics. Rwanda’s healthcare system is structured around community health workers, health posts, sector health centres, district hospitals and referral hospitals at the national level. The system has near-universal health insurance coverage through the Community-Based Health Insurance scheme.14

The country’s specific sociocultural environment, including cultural norms around illness disclosure, mental health stigma and community-based coping, shapes how individuals experience and make sense of long-term illness.2 Recruitment across all five provinces was intended to ensure diversity of geographic, socioeconomic and cultural perspectives, thereby enhancing the transferability of findings within Rwanda and to comparable LMIC settings.

Design

This study employed a constructivist/interpretivist paradigm, which holds that individuals actively construct meaning from their experiences and that there is no single objective reality independent of the knower.15 Specifically, this study draws on a descriptive phenomenological approach, which seeks to understand and characterise the experience as described by those who have lived it. This approach is preferable to grounded theory, which focuses on theory generation,16 or narrative inquiry, which centres on storytelling over time, given that our primary aim was to explore and interpret the experiential meaning of PASC symptoms for participants.1718 In-depth interviews (IDIs) were used as the primary data collection method because they allow for detailed exploration of individual experiences and are particularly appropriate for sensitive health topics. The number of IDIs was not predetermined but was determined based on theoretical saturation.19

Sample

This qualitative study drew participants from a nationwide prevalence study on PASC in Rwanda.12 Those who were identified as having persistent PASC symptoms and were categorised as PASC positive were contacted for the qualitative study.

In the parent study, the total number of individuals identified as PASC positive was 1076, drawn from all five provinces in the country, including Kigali City. Contact details collected during this study were used to reach prospective participants for the qualitative research. During the participant identification process, we considered factors such as gender, age and the severity of long-term COVID-19 symptoms (mild, moderate and severe) to ensure diverse representation.

Of the 50 individuals contacted from the eligible sampling frame, five declined to participate and five could not be reached due to logistical constraints, leaving 40 eligible and available participants. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was reached at 30 interviews, at which point recruitment was discontinued, and the remaining 10 eligible participants were therefore not interviewed. Inclusion criteria were as follows: a confirmed history of COVID-19 infection, classification as PASC-positive in the parent prevalence study, experience of persistent symptoms at the time of recruitment and willingness to participate. No upper or lower age limit was applied beyond the legal capacity to consent.

Sample size was not predetermined. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes or meaningful variations in experience emerged across successive interviews.20 Saturation was assessed through ongoing team debriefs after each set of interviews, during which emerging codes and themes were reviewed. Data collection procedures

This was a national study of Rwandans who tested positive for COVID-19, identified in the Rwanda Biomedical Centre registries during the study time period. IDIs were conducted in the location of the respondent’s preference. Prospective participants (individuals identified as having PASC symptoms) were contacted by telephone and requested to participate in the study. Data were collected through in-person interviews. The interviews were 30–45 min long.

To maintain anonymity, unique identifiers were assigned to the transcripts, and the audio recordings were securely stored on password-protected devices. Access to the transcripts and audio recordings was limited to the research team, and strict confidentiality measures were followed to protect the participants’ privacy throughout the study.

Data collectors

The interviewers were trained by the research team, including two experienced qualitative researchers. The training focused on conducting interviews using semistructured interview guides, ensuring accurate recording of participants’ responses.

The IDIs took place in a private setting, and participants were informed about the audio recording of their responses before providing consent. The audio recordings were first transcribed in Kinyarwanda, the language of the interviews, and subsequently translated into English for analysis by a professional bilingual translator.

Data collection tool

IDIs were conducted in Kinyarwanda using semistructured interview guides designed to capture the lived experiences and perspectives of participants who were identified as having persistent symptoms consistent with PASC. The interview questions were developed in English and then translated into Kinyarwanda by professional translators following standard translation procedures.

The interview guide was reviewed after the first five interviews, and minor clarifications were made based on participant responses and data collector debriefs, with no major structural changes being required. The interview guide (online supplemental file 1) covered the following domains: lived experiences with prolonged COVID-19 symptoms, family and community support, fear and stigma, help-seeking and medical care for prolonged COVID-19 symptoms. Each domain had its own set of open-ended and probing questions to explore the participants’ experiences in-depth.

Measures

The study explored participants’ lived experiences with PASC symptoms, their healthcare seeking experiences and their recommendations for improving care.

Data collection

All interviews were audio recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim in Kinyarwanda by trained transcribers with experience in qualitative research. Transcripts were reviewed against audio recordings by a second team member to verify accuracy, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Audio recordings were securely stored on password-protected devices.

Participant confidentiality

To protect participant confidentiality, no names or identifying information were recorded during interviews. Each participant was assigned a unique numeric identifier, which was used across all transcripts, audio files and analysis outputs. Any potentially identifying details mentioned by participants during interviews, including locations or names of healthcare facilities, were removed or generalised during transcription. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored on password-protected, encrypted devices accessible only to the core research team. Data will be retained for 10 years in accordance with institutional data management policy.

Data analysis

Data collection and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, using inductive reflexive thematic analysis, which was selected for its flexibility and compatibility with the interpretivist phenomenological orientation guiding this study. Emerging themes were discussed among team members after each set of interviews to assess whether any gaps in the interview guide needed to be addressed. A preliminary codebook was developed by the study team, applied to three transcripts by all team members, then modified to produce the final version. All transcripts were coded and analysed through Dedoose software using the final codebook to generate themes.21 Final themes were reviewed against the raw data to ensure they authentically represented participants’ experiences.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team comprised a medical doctor with a background in clinical medicine and direct involvement in the parent prevalence study and two public health professionals, with backgrounds in public health and qualitative research. The team’s clinical and public health perspectives may have shaped the framing of interview questions and the interpretation of findings. To address this, data collectors had no prior clinical relationship with participants and were not involved in their care, reducing the likelihood that participants moderated their disclosures based on perceived clinical authority. Reflexivity was further addressed through collaborative codebook development and team discussions of emerging interpretations.

Patient and public involvement statement

Our participants were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of the study.

The study team plans to disseminate the findings to the public, including to study participants.

Emerging themes

Theme 1: ambiguity about the relationship between symptoms and COVID-19

Study participants consistently expressed ongoing uncertainty regarding the connection between their symptoms and COVID-19, frequently attributing them to undiagnosed conditions or pre-existing health conditions. This theme focuses on participants’ own interpretations and understanding of their ongoing symptoms, independent of what healthcare providers told them. Participants’ experiences of seeking clarity from the health system, and their reactions to how providers responded (or did not respond), are addressed separately in Theme 4.

Subtheme 1.1: chronic symptoms remain undiagnosed despite seeking medical care

Most participants reported experiencing long-lasting symptoms that had persisted despite repeated medical consultations and the use of prescribed treatments. This contributed to uncertainty about their illness and suspicions about a possible relationship with COVID-19, as these symptoms began around the time of their infection and were noticeably different from their pre-COVID-19 health status. One participant said: ‘I usually buy medicine from the pharmacy to try and feel better. Recently, I even went to the health centre and a private hospital, but they confirmed I didn’t have malaria. At one point, they tested me for typhoid, but it turned out to be a false diagnosis. Still, I’m unwell and not getting better. I’m starting to suspect it could be a result of COVID-19’.

Subtheme 1.2: perceived worsening of pre-existing conditions, for example, HIV and pregnancy

Participants perceived that COVID-19 may have worsened pre-existing conditions, making it difficult for many of them to distinguish ongoing symptoms from their prior condition and those they perceived as caused or intensified by COVID-19. One of our participants said: ‘It’s hard to say for sure whether it’s because of COVID, pregnancy, or maybe the long-term medications I’ve been taking. I’ve been on painkillers and other treatments since around 2013, and I suspect they might be contributing too’. Similarly, another participant said: ‘But now I get sick often, and I don’t even know where these illnesses come from. HIV didn’t make me feel this way…now I sneeze a lot, my teeth haven’t healed, and I’m facing new problems I never had before’.

Subtheme 1.3: memory loss

Multiple participants reported experiencing memory impairment, which they perceived as significantly diminishing their quality of life and daily functioning. According to participants, this cognitive difficulty emerged following their recovery from COVID-19, which they interpreted as suggesting a potential link between the illness and subsequent memory difficulties. ‘One thing that really affects me in life is forgetfulness. I forget things a lot… we might be in the middle of a conversation, and suddenly I forget what we were talking about. It wasn’t like this. But now, forgetfulness is something that seriously affects me, and it started after I had COVID-19’, said one participant.

Theme 2: negative impact of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection on patients’ quality of life

Participants consistently described the profound and lasting impact of PASC on their daily lives, particularly in terms of physical capacity, productivity and household finances. This theme focuses on these functional and material consequences of PASC; while these impacts gave rise to emotional distress and worry, the associated psychological responses are addressed separately in Theme 3.

Subtheme 2.1: reduced physical capacity leading to a decrease in productivity

After recovering from COVID-19, most participants were unable to regain their physical strength due to the persistence of prolonged symptoms. This led to reduced productivity, which in turn affected their ability to maintain a good quality of life. One participant shared the following experience: ‘Before COVID, I used to work a lot. Now when I farm, I get headaches. If I dig in hard soil, my head hurts badly. My fingers go stiff and I struggle to breathe. I also get chest congestion’.

Subtheme 2.2: substantial financial hardship caused by prolonged COVID-19 symptoms

The majority of participants reported that ongoing symptoms had severely impacted their ability to provide for basic necessities and ensure their families’ well-being. One participant shared: ‘My life has been disrupted. Before, I could work and earn something, but now I can't do anything, and that has consequences. Even though I wasn’t selling things, at least I could provide for myself. Now I can’t. There’s also the burden of caring for my children, like paying school fees, it’s a challenge because I can no longer work’.

Theme 3: in addition to prolonged physical symptoms, participants reportedly experienced significant psychological effects of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection

Participants consistently described significant psychological and emotional consequences of living with PASC, including depression, anxiety, fear and social isolation. This theme focuses specifically on these emotional responses, arising from the symptoms themselves and their social visibility, rather than from the financial consequences addressed in Theme 2, and it captures the distress that was compounded by the diagnostic uncertainty described in Theme 1.

Subtheme 3.1: experiences of depression, anxiety and social isolation

Several participants reported experiencing significant psychological and emotional distress as a result of prolonged COVID-19 symptoms. The persistent nature of these symptoms contributed to feelings of anxiety and a tendency to self-isolate, often driven by a desire to avoid drawing attention to their ongoing health concerns. One participant recounted: ‘Also, when you have a persistent cough, it can be really embarrassing depending on where you are. Sometimes, you even feel like the cough is coming even when it’s not—just from fear or anxiety. It happens unexpectedly, and it’s hard to manage’.

Subtheme 3.2: fear, concern and apprehension of potential illness

As a result of PASC, participants struggled to support their families, reporting persistent fear about their ability to meet household needs and anticipating future health crises. One participant said: ‘I worry about whether my children will not have what to eat. It’s like I’m always anticipating the next wave of pain or difficulty breathing. That constant fear—it makes it hard to feel truly well’.

Theme 4: unsatisfactory experiences seeking healthcare among individuals with prolonged COVID-19 symptoms

When reflecting on their healthcare experiences, participants voiced frustration with the limited scope of medical evaluations they received, particularly the failure of providers to investigate a potential link to their prior COVID-19 infection. While Theme 1 addresses participants’ own interpretations of their symptoms, this theme focuses specifically on what occurred or failed to occur during healthcare encounters, including whether providers explored a COVID-19 history and whether a diagnosis or treatment plan was offered.

Subtheme 4.1: unexplored relationship of presenting symptoms with COVID-19

As reported by our participants, healthcare providers did not explore a potential relationship between participants’ presenting symptoms and COVID-19. Directly addressing the gap identified in subtheme 1.1, one participant noted: ‘… They only ask about my current symptoms. No one asks if I ever had COVID-19’, reflecting a missed opportunity to consider post-viral illness as a potential explanation for their ongoing symptoms.

Subtheme 4.2: patients not provided with definitive diagnosis for their symptoms

Most participants expressed frustration after visiting healthcare facilities multiple times with the same symptoms but never receiving a definitive diagnosis or treatment plan. Instead, they were only given pain relief medication, which failed to address their recurring symptoms or provide lasting relief. One participant shared: ‘I went to the hospital. They did two scans and gave me medicine that didn’t help. One doctor even said I might never recover, which made me very sad… I tried two different hospitals, but the medicine didn't work. Now I’ve stopped going to hospitals, only nearby health centres, but they also haven’t helped’.

Theme 5: participant proposed areas for improvement

Participants articulated a clear set of priorities for improving the response to PASC in Rwanda. Building on the gaps identified in Themes 1 and 4, particularly the absence of COVID-19 history-taking and the resulting diagnostic uncertainty, these recommendations position participants as active advocates for systemic change rather than passive recipients of care.

Subtheme 5.1: increased awareness about prolonged COVID-19 symptoms

According to participants, there was a significant lack of public awareness about PASC (post-acute sequelae of COVID-19) or prolonged COVID-19 symptoms. While this limited awareness may unintentionally reduce stigma toward affected individuals, it can also hinder support for those experiencing these symptoms and for those trying to assist them. One participant highlighted this, saying: ‘There should be more awareness campaigns about persistent COVID symptoms. They should organise village meetings to educate people about it’.

Subtheme 5.2: research on prolonged COVID-19 symptoms and treatments

Participants emphasised the importance of conducting further comprehensive research to gain a deeper understanding of the symptoms associated with PASC. They also highlighted the need to develop evidence-based treatments that could improve the quality of life for individuals affected by this condition. One participant said: ‘What I would ask for is help with treatment and continued research, so that those affected by COVID get proper care. I’d also advise expanding the research to reach as many people as possible’.

Subtheme 5.3: further investigations for individuals with a history of COVID-19

Most participants reported that they were not asked about their medical history, especially regarding COVID-19. Directly addressing the gap identified in subtheme 4, they felt that this information should be routinely collected and that individuals who have recovered from COVID-19 should undergo further evaluation to improve the diagnosis of PASC. One participant shared: ‘I’d say that during consultations, if a patient describes symptoms that sound like they could be related to COVID, the nurse should ask if the person has ever had COVID-19’.

Discussion and recommendations

This study revealed that individuals experiencing PASC often grappled with confusion and uncertainty regarding the origins of their symptoms. Many participants reported suspecting that their symptoms were related to a previous COVID-19 infection; however, they were unsure whether these symptoms were new or linked to pre-existing health conditions. This echoes similar uncertainty documented in a US-based study, where participants described feelings of confusion and frustration due to the persistence of symptoms despite ongoing treatment efforts.22 In Rwanda, however, this uncertainty had an added layer of complexity. Some participants were living with pre-existing conditions such as HIV or were pregnant, which made it even harder for them to understand whether their symptoms stemmed from their existing condition, their long-term medications or their COVID-19 infection. This complexity is not well captured by existing clinical frameworks, which were mostly developed in high-income countries where the health profiles of patients are markedly different. This suggests that the diagnostic uncertainty experienced by participants in this study was not simply about a lack of awareness of PASC but also about the limitations of available clinical frameworks when applied to patients with different health histories and limited access to specialist care.

Participants reported experiencing memory loss yet remained uncertain about whether it was directly related to COVID-19. This aligns with findings from a study investigating the neuropsychiatric consequences of COVID-19, which found that PASC symptoms can significantly impair memory, attention and mood.23 It is important to note that this study captures participants’ subjective perceptions and interpretations of memory changes rather than clinically verified cognitive impairment. The significance of this finding, therefore, lies not in establishing a causal relationship but in understanding the meaning participants attributed to these experiences.

Memory loss was often perceived as a marker of a fundamentally altered sense of self, a clear before-and-after boundary that reinforced participants’ conviction that COVID-19 had changed the course of their health and well-being. In this context, cognitive symptoms functioned as tangible evidence of illness, lending legitimacy to participants’ experiences despite the absence of formal diagnostic confirmation. This interpretive role of cognitive symptoms represents an important analytical finding that calls for further exploration in both clinical and qualitative research.

A reduction in physical capacity and productivity also emerged as a major impact of PASC. Participants reported that these impairments significantly diminished their quality of life, often rendering them unable to carry out daily activities, resulting in financial strain. This is supported by findings from a study exploring the role of physical activity in managing PASC symptoms among White, Asian and Black populations, in which participants reported experiencing severe fatigue and a dramatic decline in physical functionality.24 This finding carries particular significance in the Rwandan context because most participants were engaged in physically demanding occupations such as farming, woodwork and activities requiring long-distance travel. This resulted in loss of income, food insecurity and the inability for parents to provide school fees for their children. This is specific to the Rwandan context given that the majority of its population depends on daily wages, which is different from high-income settings where people may benefit from social protection systems. This finding therefore suggests that the economic burden of PASC is structurally amplified in LMICs not only because individuals have fewer financial resources but also because the absence of social safety nets leaves little margin for managing a chronic illness. Addressing the physical symptoms, the socioeconomic consequences and the psychological burden on affected individuals should therefore be central to any intervention designed for this context.

The psychological toll of PASC symptoms was another prominent theme. Participants commonly reported experiencing depression, anxiety and social isolation. Many expressed a persistent fear of becoming ill again or infecting others, which contributed to heightened stress and self-imposed isolation. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted in Italy where participants conveyed fears of reinfection and the risk of transmitting the virus to others.7 This study reveals a distinct form of fear. For participants in this study, fear was not primarily about reinfection but about the collapse of social and economic roles including fear of being unable to feed their children, fear of becoming a burden and fear of a persistent cough causing public embarrassment. This suggests that the psychological burden of PASC in this context appears to be inseparable from its socioeconomic dimensions. The mental health consequences of the condition are at least in part materially produced rather than purely symptomatic. This has significant implications for intervention design which would need to address social role disruption, community-level understanding of chronic illness and mental health support.

Another concern highlighted by participants was dissatisfaction with healthcare services when seeking help for their PASC symptoms. Many felt that healthcare providers failed to consider a potential connection between their symptoms and a previous COVID-19 infection, leading to incomplete assessments and a lack of clear diagnoses. Similar concerns were raised in a study conducted by Johns Hopkins University where participants expressed frustration at having their symptoms dismissed or minimised with some providers even denying the existence of PASC.25 In the Rwandan context, these healthcare interactions carried a deeper meaning given that trust in healthcare institutions in Rwanda is generally high and healthcare providers hold significant social authority, a negative experience may carry particular weight potentially leading to discontinued healthcare seeking as was mentioned by some of our participants. This calls for targeted clinical education and protocol development around PASC recognition and management in Rwanda’s healthcare system.

Participants recommended that healthcare providers routinely collect information about individuals’ prior COVID-19 infection. They believed this would facilitate timely identification and management of PASC symptoms. Such systematic documentation could serve as a valuable diagnostic aid allowing for earlier intervention and improved outcomes for those affected. These recommendations are notable not only for their content but also because they were shared by patients themselves. Rather than positioning themselves as passive recipients of care, participants actively identified structural failures and proposed concrete solutions suggesting that including affected individuals as active partners in designing interventions for PASC in low-income settings is both ethically appropriate and likely to produce more contextually relevant responses.

Conclusion

This study contributes contextually grounded qualitative evidence on the lived experience of PASC in Rwanda, a setting largely absent from the existing literature. The findings reveal that diagnostic uncertainty, psychosocial burden and health system unpreparedness are not simply individual challenges but are structurally produced and mutually reinforcing, compounding the suffering of those affected and limiting their ability to seek and receive appropriate care. These insights extend beyond symptom documentation to highlight a fundamental inadequacy in how post-viral illness is currently recognised and managed in low-resource settings. Addressing this requires not only further research into the causes and treatment of PASC but also investment in clinical education, public awareness and psychosocially integrated models of care that are responsive to the social and economic realities of affected communities in Rwanda and comparable settings.

Study limitations

These findings should be considered in light of the following limitations:

First, some time elapsed between participants’ identification as PASC-positive in the parent study and the qualitative interviews conducted for this study. It is possible that some participants had partially or fully recovered by the time of the interviews, potentially affecting the currency of their accounts. However, even participants who had improved were able to provide authentic accounts of their experience of living with PASC, consistent with the retrospective phenomenological approach of the study.

Furthermore, recall bias is an inherent limitation of retrospective qualitative interviews. Participants were asked to reflect on experiences that may have begun months prior to the interview and the precise onset and trajectory of symptoms may have been difficult to reconstruct accurately. Given the phenomenological aims of the study which focus on the meaning participants attributed to their experiences rather than precise factual recall, this limitation is unlikely to have substantially affected the core findings.

Additionally, formal reflexive practices such as structured journaling were not part of the original study protocol. Reflexivity was instead addressed through collaborative codebook development and team discussion of emerging interpretations, and we acknowledge that the influence of researcher assumptions on data collection and analysis cannot be fully accounted for.

Finally, factors such as the duration of symptoms, severity of acute infection and vaccination status were not linked to individual qualitative participants, limiting our ability to examine how these factors may have shaped participants’ experiences. Future qualitative studies should aim to link such variables at the individual level where possible.
