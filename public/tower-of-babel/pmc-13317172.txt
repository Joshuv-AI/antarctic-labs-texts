Risk factors for drug-related gestational diabetes mellitus: a real-world pharmacovigilance study based on the FAERS database
Authors: Hong Jing, Xiaohu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common pregnancy complication linked to adverse outcomes for both mother and infant. Research on drug-related GDM remains limited.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the risk factors and potential mechanisms of drug-related GDM using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS).MethodsWe analyzed FAERS data from the past, focusing on drugs potentially associated with GDM. Significant drug signals were identified using statistical reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM), bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and univariate logistic regression analysis. We used multivariate logistic regression to analyze independent risk factors, built a multivariate logistic regression model to predict GDM, and used the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) to evaluate the multicollinearity of the predictive factors in the model. Furthermore, we performed gene target prediction, functional enrichment analysis, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis to explore the mechanisms involved, and assessed drug-genes interactions through molecular docking simulations.ResultsBy analyzing 1137 cases of potentially drug-related GDM, we identified 12 drugs as potential independent risk factors for GDM. We constructed a multivariate logistic regression model incorporating drug and patient characteristics to predict GDM, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.847. GVIF analysis confirmed that there was no multicollinearity among the predictors. Potential target genes of antipsychotic drugs are mainly enriched in cellular response to dopamine, phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, and blood circulation. After integrating the target gene group and the insulin receptor signaling pathway gene set, the potential target genes have extensive connections with the insulin receptor signaling pathway, with DRD2 and KCNH2 being hub genes in the PPI network.ConclusionThis study identified 12 drugs that may be independent risk factors for GDM, including quetiapine, aripiprazole, olanzapine, prednisolone, venlafaxine, risperidone, escitalopram, clozapine, mirtazapine, ziprasidone, rosuvastatin, and trazodone. The established predictive model has certain clinical value, and the potential mechanisms by which antipsychotic drugs may lead to GDM were further analyzed. These results provide potential clues for the early identification of GDM and early warning of suspected drugs, and offer a reference for further exploration of its pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09200-3.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common pregnancy-related condition characterized by the onset of high blood glucose levels during pregnancy, typically diagnosed in the second or third trimester [1, 2]. The World Health Organization defines GDM as hyperglycemia caused by carbohydrate intolerance that is first diagnosed during pregnancy, but does not include patients who meet the diagnostic criteria for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3]. The pathophysiology of GDM is complex, involving insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion, and changes in hormonal regulation during pregnancy [4, 5]. GDM not only poses significant risks to maternal health, including an increased likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes later in life, but also contributes to complications for the baby, such as macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and an elevated risk of obesity and metabolic disorders in childhood [6–9]. Recent studies have increasingly pointed to the role of medications in influencing the risk of gestational diabetes, for example, glucocorticoid use during pregnancy may increase the risk of gestational diabetes [10]. However, despite increasing awareness of the risks of medication-related GDM, research in this area remains limited and has not addressed the broader effects of various medications and their underlying mechanisms.

Given the clinical significance of GDM and its potential long-term consequences for both mothers and infants, understanding the drug-related risk factors is essential for effective prevention and management. Investigating the molecular mechanisms and drug-gene interactions involved in GDM could uncover novel therapeutic targets and enhance our understanding of the condition’s pathogenesis. This study aims to comprehensively evaluate the risk factors associated with drug-related GDM and explore the potential molecular mechanisms involved. By utilizing the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), the world’s largest database for adverse drug event reports, this study will conduct an in-depth analysis of drug-related GDM, identifying potential drug risk factors and their underlying mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Data source and collection

Data from Q1 2004 to Q2 2025 were all sourced from the FAERS database. Data from the past decade (Q2 2015 to Q2 2025) was used as the primary analytical data for this study, for constructing the predictive model. The FAERS database adheres to the International Safety Reporting Guidelines (ICH E2B) issued by the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), with adverse events coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) [11, 12]. In this study, the exposure window period refers to any timeframe during which an individual is exposed to the relevant drug prior to reporting gestational diabetes. Based on FDA guidance, duplicate entries were removed, and only the most recent report was retained. All weight data will be converted to kilograms (1 pound = 0.4536 kg) and rounded to two decimal places. Additionally, cases with implausible values, such as age less than 10 or more than 150 years, and weight less than 10 or more than 500 kg. Only data with complete information on age and weight were included in the subsequent analysis. Age was categorized as: <20, 20–30, 30–40, 40–50, and > 50 years. Weight was classified as: <50, 50–75, 75–100, 100–125, 125–150, and > 150 kg. The article specifies age and weight ranges, with values ​​greater than a certain threshold representing an open interval and values ​​less than or equal to a certain threshold representing a closed interval. The process of data cleaning and analysis is shown in Fig. 1.

Aevents events were collected using the term “gestational diabetes” according to MedDRA. Only drugs identified as “primary suspects” for GDM included in the analysis.

We first constructed a 2 × 2 contingency table as the basis for subsequent statistical analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Four key statistical indicators were used in the analysis: ROR, PRR, EBGM and BCPNN (Supplementary Table 2). The specific formulas and thresholds of these indicators are listed in Appendix S2. The positive signal between the drug and gestational diabetes was defined as: all four indicators (ROR, PRR, EBGM, BCPNN) were positive. To ensure the accuracy of the p-values ​​for each signal, we performed Fisher’s exact test and then adjusted the p-values ​​using the Bonferroni correction method, which controls the risk of Type I errors (false positives) by reducing the significance level. Ultimately, drugs with a corrected p-value less than 0.05 were considered positive signals. By combining multiple statistical indicators, the reliability and robustness of signal detection were improved.

Logistic regression analysis

We included complete reports of clinical information (age and weight) for analysis and selected suspected drugs with adjusted p-values < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression analysis for further evaluation. Subsequently, we used the selected drugs and patients’ basic information (age, weight) as independent variables to conduct multivariate logistic regression analysis, where the “non-event” group is defined as all other reports in FAERS other than those of pregnant women who developed GDM from taking the target medication, construct a risk prediction model, and evaluate its predictive efficacy. Finally, to ensure model stability against multicollinearity, we applied GVIF(1/(2×Df)) (Df: variables per categorical predictor), with values exceeding 5 indicating collinearity.

Functional enrichment analysis

We obtained the SMILES ID of drug active ingredients through PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and then use swisstargetprediction platform (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) predict possible gene targets [13, 14]. The results of the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis were analyzed and visualized using Metascape ([https://metascape.org/], version 3.5.20250701) with default parameters [15].

Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network construction and analysis

STRING version 12 (https://string-db.org/) was employed to construct the PPI network of the drug-related genes using prior knowledge, which was then analyzed with Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3). Download the insulin receptor signaling gene set from the Reactome Pathway Database (Pathway browser version 3.7; Reactome database version: 94; http://reactome.org/), integrate it with drug-related genes, and Hub genes were identified and screened using Cytoscape MCODE.

Molecular docking

We retrieved the 3D structures of active compounds from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and minimized their energy using Chem3D software (version 14.0.0.117). Core target protein crystal structures were obtained from the PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/), and processes like dewatering, ligand removal, hydrogen addition, and charge calculation were conducted using PyMOL (version 3.1.6.1) and autodocktools (version 1.5.6). Autodock Vina (version 1.1.2) facilitated the molecular docking, and PyMOL was employed to visualize it.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarize the clinical characteristics of patients with drug-related GDM. The ROR, PRR, EBGM, and BCPNN algorithm are used for preliminary screening of positive signals, while performing chi square test and Bonferroni correction. Subsequently, univariate logistic regression was used to further identify potential drugs, and multiple regression was applied to determine independent risk factors for drug-related GDM and establish a model. ROC was then used to evaluate the model. The glm function was used for univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The pROC package was used for ROC analysis. The forestplot package was used to draw forest plots, and other visualizations were performed using the ggplot2 package. All data analysis was performed in R software version 4.4.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of GDM

From the second quarter of 2015 to the second quarter of 2025, a total of 52,509,909 adverse events (AEs) were reported, of which 1137 cases were linked to GDM (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of potential drug-related GDM are summarized in Table 1. The majority of affected patients were aged between 30 and 40 years (68.6%). 521 (45.8%) from those weighing between 50 and 75 kg, and 426 (37.5%) from those weighing between 75 and 100 kg. The majority of reports (59.5%, 676 cases) were filed by professional sources (MD, HP, OT, and PH), while 34.4% (392 cases) were reported by healthcare non-professionals (CN, and LW). The Germany, United States, and France contributed the highest number of GDM reports, with 658, 188, and 81 cases, respectively. The hospitalization rate due to GDM is 11.3%, and in rare cases it can lead to death. Figure 2A shows the distribution of GDM reported cases in the past 10 years. Bipolar disorder was the most common underlying condition associated with GDM (Fig. 2B). The highest number of other serious adverse reaction cases reflects the diversity and complexity of adverse reaction outcomes (Fig. 2C).

Drugs associated with GDM

Drugs positioned in the upper-right quadrant of the plot exhibit both high signal strength and statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 1). We screened 13 signals using ROR, PRR, MGPS, and BCPNN algorithms (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3), and based on the adjusted p-value < 0.05 after Fisher’s exact test and Bonferroni correction, we identified 12 drugs as positive signals (Supplementary Table 3). The top drug category was antipsychotic (6/12).

Risk factors for drug-related GDM

Using univariate logistic regression analysis (adjusted p-value < 0.05), a total of 12 potential drugs affecting the induction of GDM were screened (Supplementary Table 4). The results of the univariate logistic regression analysis were calculated based on Supplementary Table 5. To assess the risk factors associated with drug-related GDM, multi-factor logistic regression and constructed a predictive model was performed using the 12 drugs, in combination with patient demographic data. The analysis revealed that 12 drugs, including Rosuvastatin (OR 135.585; 95% CI: 28.947-701.997), Olanzapine (OR 95.591; 95% CI: 62.926-147.779), and Ziprasidone (OR 89.326; 95% CI: 27.646-339.384), were identified as independent risk factors for drug-related GDM (Fig. 4). The multivariate logistic regression analysis was based on Supplementary Table 6. We selected data from the past decade for the multivariate logistic regression model. The predictive accuracy of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.847(Fig. 5A). All retained variables exhibited GVIF below 2, demonstrating negligible multicollinearity and supporting model stability (Fig. 5B).

Time interval between drug use and the onset of GDM

More than three-fifths of drug-related GDM cases occur more than 180 days after drug use (Fig. 6A). The average onset time is about 275 days (Fig. 6B). It is worth noting that the cumulative incidence curve shows that the incidence of GDM gradually increased from 0% to nearly 50% from the start of medication to 250 days, and the incidence increased by more than 50% from 250 days to 365 days after medication (Fig. 6C).

Analysis of drug-gene related functional enrichment and PPI network

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, most of the drugs were antipsychotic with high OR. Therefore, further analysis was conducted on all drugs in this class (Quetiapine, Aripiprazole, Olanzapine, Risperidone, Clozapine, Ziprasidone). Their common potential target genes were DRD2, ADRA1A, HTR1A, HTR6, KCNH2, DRD4, HTR2B, HTR2C, HTR2A, HRH1, DRD1, DRD3, HTR7, and H1F0. To further understand the potential roles of these potential target genes in the pathogenesis of GDM and their involvement in biological signaling pathways, GO enrichment analysis was performed. These potential target genes were mainly enriched in cellular response to dopamine, phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, and blood circulation (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, we constructed a protein-protein interaction network (PPI) of the potential target genes using the STRING database, with DRD2 and KCNH2 being hub genes (Supplementary Fig. 2). We further integrated the potential target genes with the insulin receptor signaling pathway gene set and found that the potential target genes DRD2 and KCNH2 remain hub genes in the integrated PPI network (Fig. 7B, C). These two hub genes, along with their respective modules, exhibit extensive interactions with the insulin receptor signaling pathway gene set.

The results of molecular docking

Most of these six antipsychotic drugs were able to bind well to the active sites of the core proteins (DRD2 and KCNH2), with absolute binding energies of less than − 7 kcal/mol (Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests that the high affinity of these drugs for core proteins may influence the occurrence and development of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) by acting on these core targets.

Discussion

Medications may affect GDM through potential mechanisms, for example, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis, the use of antipsychotics during pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of GDM, which suggests that drugs may increase the risk of diabetes by affecting insulin sensitivity or other metabolic pathways [16]. Currently, there is a need to effectively manage complications during pregnancy while minimizing the use of related drugs that lead to gestational diabetes, and to optimize prevention and treatment strategies based on the potential mechanisms of drug exposure-related gestational diabetes. This is crucial for developing safer clinical treatment guidelines.

Data from the FAERS indicate a growing trend of drug-related GDM, highlighting the need for improved monitoring and management of high-risk medications. We found that Germany had a higher reporting rate, which may be due to stricter policies in Germany for monitoring adverse events related to psychotropic drugs. German medical professionals may also be more vigilant or more incentivized to report adverse events, resulting in more comprehensive data collection compared to other countries. Of the more than 1000 reports of drug-related GDM, the majority involved patients aged 30 to 40 years, and most patients weigh between 50 and 100 kg. Studies have found that higher maternal age is associated with an increased risk of early and late GDM [17]. Studies have shown that age is an independent risk factor for GDM, with the risk of GDM increasing by 12.5% ​​for every additional year of age [18]. Older pregnant women are more likely to experience abnormal blood sugar regulation, which increases the risk of GDM [19]. This study found that an age range of 30–40 years is an independent risk factor for GDM, whereas advanced maternal age (over 40 years) does not independently increase the risk of GDM. Some of the observed results may reflect reporting biases or characteristics specific to certain populations, rather than representing true epidemiological trends. Given that FAERS was not originally designed for rigorous epidemiological studies, the findings should be interpreted with caution, taking into account the research context. This study also found that pregnant women weighing less than 100 kg had a lower risk of GDM than those weighing more than 100 kg. Studies have found that a higher BMI is an independent predictor of GDM [20]. However, the lack of height data in the FAERS database directly prevents us from calculating BMI. Obesity is closely associated with metabolic abnormalities related to GDM. Studies have shown that obese women are more likely to experience lipid metabolism disorders during pregnancy, which are closely related to the development of GDM [21]. In addition, obese women have higher triglyceride levels during pregnancy, and high triglyceride levels are an important risk factor for GDM [22]. This study failed to find a significant association between weighing more than 100 kg and the occurrence of GDM. However, the finding that weighing less than 100 kg was a protective factor is consistent with previous findings and further supports the potential importance of weight management in the prevention of GDM.

The main indications for patients most susceptible to drug-related GDM are those with bipolar disorder, depression, and schizophrenia. These diseases typically require the use of psychotropic drugs, which may have metabolic side effects, including the possibility of inducing GDM. Antipsychotic drugs can cross the placental barrier and may lead to gestational diabetes. Therefore, when using antipsychotic drugs during pregnancy, the risks and benefits need to be carefully weighed [23]. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that a history of depression is significantly associated with an increased risk of GDM, with an odds ratio of 1.29, indicating that pregnant women with a history of depression have a 29% increased risk of developing GDM [24]. In addition, mental illness often accompanies anxiety, and a study found that anxiety is an independent predictor of GDM, suggesting that psychological factors may play a role in the pathogenesis of GDM [25]. A case report emphasizes the risk of olanzapine related hyperglycemia in a patient with a history of GDM and bipolar disorder, and highlights the necessity of close blood glucose monitoring in psychiatric patients taking such medications [26]. This case suggests that psychotropic drugs may exacerbate metabolic disorders, thereby increasing the risk of GDM in susceptible populations. These drugs may affect GDM independently, therefore their use may require more rigorous monitoring and evaluation. Genetic susceptibility, such as variations in drug metabolic pathways, may further increase the susceptibility of these vulnerable populations to GDM. Future studies should explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these drug-related metabolic disturbances and explore potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of psychotropic drugs during pregnancy.

A detailed analysis of the FAERS database identified several drug classes strongly associated with GDM, particularly antipsychotic and antidepressants. This analysis of the FAERS database highlights the significant role of certain drug classes in the development of GDM. Our study identified 12 medications as independent risk factors for GDM. Studies have shown that certain antipsychotic drugs may increase the risk of gestational diabetes [27]. For example, a study showed that some patients who were treated with quetiapine developed new-onset diabetes at a median time of 1.6 years. After stopping quetiapine, some patients’ diabetes symptoms were relieved and their blood sugar levels were even controlled without the use of hypoglycemic drugs [28]. One study reported that a male patient developed symptoms of diabetes, including polyuria and thirst, and eventually developed diabetic ketoacidosis after receiving olanzapine treatment [29]. This suggests that olanzapine may accelerate the onset of diabetes by inducing significant hyperglycemia, and that the metabolic changes induced by this drug may play a similar role in the development of gestational diabetes. It is important to note that the OR value only reflects the association between variables, not the absolute risk. Therefore, although the OR value can reveal the degree of association between a certain drug or clinical feature and the occurrence of gestational diabetes, it cannot directly indicate the actual risk of the drug or feature causing the disease. Furthermore, the confidence intervals for many drugs are too wide, suggesting that the results may be unstable. For example, the odds ratio (OR) for rosuvastatin is 135.585. Therefore, future studies should use larger sample sizes and more rigorous statistical methods to verify the true relationship between these drugs and gestational diabetes. The data shows that most drug-related GDM cases require a longer accumulation time, indicating that drug-related GDM is usually subacute. Therefore, it is necessary to develop new active monitoring and prevention strategies for these drugs. The AUC value of the GDM prediction model built based on medication and clinical characteristics is 0.847, and the GVIF of its predictor was also low, indicating that the model has high accuracy in predicting the risk of GDM, and can effectively identify high-risk groups. Further research should focus on its universality and stability across different populations to ensure its widespread application and reliability in clinical practice. It is important to note that these observed associations may be influenced by concomitant medication use. Patients may have multiple diseases or be taking multiple medications simultaneously, and the interactions between these medications may promote the development of gestational diabetes, further complicating the mechanisms by which drug-related gestational diabetes occurs.

The results from the GO enrichment analysis offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying antipsychotic-related GDM. The significant enrichment of antipsychotic-genes in processes such as cellular response to dopamine, phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, and blood circulation. These findings suggest that antipsychotic-related GDM may arise from the disruption of multiple interconnected molecular pathways. Specifically, the involvement of dopamine signaling in glucose homeostasis is of particular interest. Dopamine is known to modulate insulin secretion and the response of peripheral tissues to insulin [30]. Dysregulation of this signaling pathway may impair glucose uptake and promote insulin resistance, a hallmark of GDM [31–33]. Furthermore, the phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway plays a crucial role in cellular functions such as proliferation, differentiation, and metabolic regulation [34, 35]. Aberrations in this pathway could exacerbate the metabolic dysfunctions typically observed in GDM, potentially through altered lipid metabolism and cellular responses to hormonal signals like insulin. Additionally, the significant enrichment of genes related to blood circulation suggests a potential link between vascular dysfunction and GDM pathogenesis. Impaired blood flow and endothelial dysfunction may further exacerbate insulin resistance by affecting nutrient and oxygen delivery to key tissues involved in glucose metabolism [36–38]. The interactions between these signaling pathways underscore the complex interplay of factors that contribute to the development of GDM in individuals treated with antipsychotic medications. These molecular insights provide a foundation for future studies to investigate the precise mechanisms by which antipsychotics influence GDM development. Understanding these mechanisms could inform therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing the risk of GDM in pregnant patients using antipsychotic drugs.

To explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying antipsychotic-related GDM, we constructed a PPI network using molecules linked to antipsychotic. The PPI network found that DRD2 and KCNH2 were the central nodes, indicating that they played an important role in mediating the relationship between drugs and GDM. After the potential target genes of antipsychotic drugs were integrated with the insulin receptor signaling pathway, DRD2 and KCNH2 were found to remain key hub genes in the integrated network. This finding suggests that DRD2 and KCNH2 may indirectly influence the pathogenesis of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) by regulating the insulin receptor signaling pathway. Their interaction suggests that DRD2 and KCNH2 may play a crucial bridging role in antipsychotic drug-related metabolic disorders. Future research should further explore the specific mechanisms by which these two genes contribute to insulin resistance, aiming to provide novel molecular targets for the prevention and treatment of antipsychotic drug-related metabolic disorders. DRD2 is the dopamine receptor D2, which is involved in regulating insulin resistance and is a key pathway protein in regulating glucose homeostasis [39]. Alterations in the DRD2 signaling pathway may impair insulin sensitivity, thereby promoting the occurrence of insulin resistance. The KCNH2 gene encodes a potassium channel belonging to the voltage-gated potassium channel family [40]. Dysfunction of potassium channels can lead to decreased cellular responsiveness to insulin, thereby promoting insulin resistance. However, further research is needed to elucidate the potential mechanisms between hub genes and GDM. Statistical and bioinformatics analyses in this study suggest that some antipsychotic drugs may induce biological effects associated with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) by acting on untargeted genes. Molecular docking results further support the binding potential of these drugs to proteins encoded by DRD2 and KCNH2, suggesting that they may participate in the pathogenesis of GDM through unexpected pharmacological mechanisms. These findings provide a new perspective for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which antipsychotic induces GDM. Future research should delve deeper into the mechanisms of action of antipsychotic and other drugs, revealing their potential roles in insulin resistance and gestational diabetes mellitus, and providing new targeted treatment strategies for clinical intervention in GDM.

The FAERS plays a crucial role in pharmacovigilance, however, it also has limitations. First, FAERS data may be affected by differences in the reporter’s identity and reporting methods. Data submitted by non-professionals may be incomplete or misclassified, leading to reporting bias. Second, the relationship between a drug and gestational diabetes may not be causal. Furthermore, this study relied on a single database, and the model stability was not fully assessed, which may have led to data bias. Confounding indications and prodromal symptom bias may affect the reliability of the results. Future studies need to validate the data using multiple data sources. Therefore, while FAERS data provides important insights for pharmacovigilance, its inherent limitations require careful interpretation.

Conclusion

This study identified 12 medications that may be independent risk factors for GDM, including quetiapine, aripiprazole, olanzapine, prednisolone, venlafaxine, risperidone, escitalopram, clozapine, mirtazapine, ziprasidone, rosuvastatin, and trazodone. The constructed predictive model has clinical predictive value, and the potential mechanisms by which antipsychotic drugs lead to GDM were further analyzed. These results provide potential clues for GDM early warning and identification of suspected medications, and offer a reference for further exploration of its pathogenesis.
