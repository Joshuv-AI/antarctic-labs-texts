An international randomised controlled trial of a novel antimicrobial dressing for peripheral intravenous catheters (ProP trial)
Authors: Bertrand Drugeon, Daner L Ball, Tricia M Kleidon, Catherine O’Brien, Gabor Mihala, Nicole Marsh, Amanda Ullman, Amanda Corley, Jessica A Schults, Jérémy Guenezan, Kate McCarthy, Sabrina Seguin, Guillaume Batiot, Joshua Byrnes, Thiago Lopes Silva, Olivier Mimoz, Claire M Rickard

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) are extensively used in hospitals worldwide and frequently associated with complications. Phlebitis is a common complication and may indicate infection, while infectious events, although uncommon, contribute to antimicrobial use and healthcare-associated infection. Chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) impregnated dressings effectively prevent infections in central venous catheters but evidence for PIVC use is lacking. We assessed the feasibility and preliminary clinical outcomes of CHG-impregnated dressings for PIVCs.MethodsThe ProP trial was a multi-centre, open-label, two-arm, parallel adaptive randomised controlled trial (RCT) in Australia and France. Adult and paediatric patients in emergency and acute settings who were expected to require a PIVC for ≥48 hours were eligible. Randomisation was centralised (1:1 ratio; allocation concealed; stratified by site) to either a CHG-impregnated dressing or a standard polyurethane dressing. In Phase I, feasibility outcomes included eligibility, recruitment, retention, protocol fidelity, missing data and satisfaction, with exploratory analysis of clinical outcomes. Phase II was planned to definitively compare clinical outcomes (composite infectious complications of local infection, catheter tip colonisation, PIVC-related bloodstream infection (BSI), and/or phlebitis), however progression to Phase II depended on pre-defined Phase I feasibility criteria.ResultsBetween May 2023 and March 2024, 300 patients were enrolled at three hospitals. Most feasibility criteria achieved pre-specified targets including 92% recruitment, 99% retention, 99% protocol fidelity, 0% missing data, and >98% satisfaction for both participant and staff. However, eligibility was 76%, (<80% feasibility threshold), triggering trial cessation. Composite infectious complications and/or phlebitis occurred in 19% of control participants and 20% of the intervention participants. This included local infection (1% control; 0% intervention), PIVC-tip colonisation (3% control; 1% intervention), phlebitis (14% control; 18% intervention), and PIVC-BSI (none in either group). All adverse events were minor, and none required dressing removal.ConclusionThis study can inform the design of a definitive trial to assess the clinical and economic effectiveness of CHG-impregnated dressings for PIVCs, including refinement of the eligibility criteria and primary outcome.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT05741866Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01767-y.Keywords: Antimicrobial dressing, Peripheral Venous Catheter, Antiseptics, Healthcare-associated infection, Phlebitis, Complication, Costs, Safety

Background

Peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) are indispensable medical devices, with over 2 billion units sold annually worldwide, enabling intravenous therapy in up to 90% of hospitalised patients [1]. Despite their ubiquity, PIVC-related complications remain a significant and often-overlooked challenge with complications occurring in up to 50% of patients, and although mostly non-infectious, they disrupt treatment continuity and cause patient discomfort, anxiety and pain [2, 3]. Although most PIVC complications are non-infectious, infectious events remain clinically significant because of their contribution to healthcare-associated infection and downstream antimicrobial use.

Phlebitis (vein inflammation) at the PIVC insertion site is common and could indicate an infection, including bloodstream infection (BSI) or localised infection (which is more prevalent) [4]. BSI is the most severe PIVC adverse event, with potential for death, metastatic infection, and secondary organ dysfunction which can prolong hospitalisation, require extended antimicrobial therapy and potentially contribute to antimicrobial resistance [5]. Although individual BSI risk is estimated at just 0.03–0.2 cases per 100 PIVCs, their widespread use likely incurs a similar, or greater cumulative number of related BSIs [PIVC-BSIs] as for with central venous catheters (CVCs), which have historically received more prevention focus [3, 4, 6]. PIVC-BSIs are associated with 25% mortality and an estimated additional cost of €6,400 per episode (median 2024 cost) [4, 6, 7]. PIVC infections may require antibiotic therapy, including last-resort antibiotics if multi-resistant organisms are involved, contributing to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance and limiting treatment options [7]. The proportion of resistant organisms associated with PIVC infections has been reported as to % [8, 9]. Preventing PIVC infections is therefore a critical health system priority for risk mitigation.

Clinical practice guidelines for PIVC infection prevention are typically less robust and consistent compared to those for CVCs, due to insufficient high-quality evidence available in this catheter population. However, given the widespread use of PIVCs, this difference in guidance highlights the need for comparable attention to evidence-based management and infection prevention strategies for PIVCs. Chlorhexidine (CHG) impregnated dressings have been shown to reduce infectious complications in multiple randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in CVCs [10, 11] and are widely recommended for these devices by international guidelines [12, 13]. However, the potential benefit of CHG dressings to prevent infectious complications in PIVCs has not yet been explored, with no RCTs published to date. We designed an adaptive RCT (the ProP Trial) whereby in Phase I we aimed to assess the feasibility and describe preliminary clinical outcomes of CHG dressings across diverse healthcare settings, to inform future research (including Phase II of ProP) and clinical guidelines.

Methods

Trial design

The Protect PIVCs (ProP) trial was designed as an international, multi-centre, two-arm, parallel, two phase adaptive (feasibility, then superiority), RCT, conducted in two centres in Brisbane, Queensland, Australia (Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital [RBWH] and Queensland Children’s Hospital [QCH]) and in one centre in France (Poitiers University Hospital [PUH]). Phase I (feasibility) was assessed according to pre-defined criteria. We used an internal pilot design, whereby the feasibility of continuing to Phase II was re-assessed after the completion of Phase I, with clinical outcomes only reviewed after Phase I if the trial was stopped at this point. This paper reports the outcomes of Phase I at which time the trial was halted in line with the protocol. The study was prospectively registered (https://clinicaltrials.gov/NCT05741866) and the full trial methodology is published [14]. The results are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standard of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Statement [15].

Participants

Although a common protocol and essential eligibility criteria were applied across all locations, site-specific differences (e.g., age limits) reflected local service models or national legislation. As the first RCT of this intervention, we prioritised testing of the intervention across the varied clinical and demographic groups that use PIVCs to better inform generalisability. The study settings were medical and surgical wards at RBWH, predominantly surgical wards at QCH, and the emergency department (ED) at PUH. The patient inclusion criteria were: (a) aged ≥ 6 years (due to dressing size) in Australia, and ≥ 18 years in France (due to national legislation), (b) expected to require a PIVC for ≥ 48 h (based on prescribed therapy or medical care), and (c) written informed consent from the participant/parent/legal representative. Patients were excluded if: (a) the PIVC was placed under non-aseptic conditions, (b) the skin at the PIVC insertion site was burned, non-intact or scarred, (c) there was a known allergy to CHG or to transparent dressing adhesives, (d) patient receiving palliative care/end-of-life pathway, or (e) already participating in the ProP trial. Due to inter-country variation in local or national research requirements, additional exclusion criteria in Australia were: (a) non-English-speaking (without an interpreter), or (b) under the care of Child and Family Services and unable to gain consent from case worker, while in France were: (a) predictably difficult vascular access (e.g., IV drug addiction or obesity), and (b) criteria specific to French legislation (persons not benefiting from a Social Security scheme, persons benefiting from enhanced protection, namely minors, pregnant or breastfeeding women, persons deprived of their liberty by a judicial or administrative decision, adults under legal protection).

Randomisation and blinding

Participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to antimicrobial (intervention) or standard dressing (control) using a centralised, web-based randomisation, stratified by centre, using randomly-permuted block sizes of 4 and 6 (REDCap, Vanderbilt University, Nashville) [16]. The randomisation schedule was prepared and uploaded by an independent statistician. Group allocation was not revealed until written participant consent was obtained. Due to the nature of the intervention, masking/blinding of participants and clinicians to the intervention was not possible. However, statisticians were blinded as well as the laboratory staff and medical assessors determining infection outcomes.

Interventions

Insertion, maintenance and removal of PIVCs was standardised as per local practices at the participating centres. Before the study, all caregivers were trained in the intervention dressing application, follow-up and removal. The PIVCs used were Insyte Autoguard BC with/without wings (BD, Franklin Lakes), or Introcan (BBraun, Melsungen) with separate short extension tubing in adults, and integrated catheters (Nexiva, BD) for children. All PIVCs were inserted by experienced caregivers, adhering to local policy including hand hygiene, ANTT® approach, and skin site disinfection with 2% CHG in 70% alcohol. Ultrasound insertion was optional and used only in Australia. Intravenous treatments were administered through a three-way stopcock and/or a needleless connector, after disinfecting the connector hub with commercial 70% isopropyl alcohol wipes (in Australia), or sterile gauze soaked with 70% ethanol (in France).

Research and local nurses applied the dressings. PIVCs were dressed and secured with a bordered polyurethane dressing in the control group (Tegaderm™ IV Advanced 1683, 3 M, St Paul), and with an antimicrobial (CHG incorporated into the adhesive) polyurethane dressing in the intervention group (Tegaderm™ Antimicrobial I.V. Advanced Securement 9132, 3 M, St Paul). Adult participants additionally received non-sterile tape strips external to the dressing over the extension or infusion tubing for stabilisation. In children, dressings could be supplemented with a tubular bandage, arm board, or non-sterile stretchy tape (if PIVC over a joint). Tissue adhesive (SecurePort IV™, Adhezion Biomedical, Hudson) could be applied under the dressing at the insertion point, as per clinician assessment.

A Research Nurse (ReN) (RBWH; QCH), or the investigators or ReN (PUH) visited participants daily to reinforce the protocol and collect study data. Participants were visited if inpatients, or contacted by phone if discharged, to assess complications for 48 h post PIVC-removal. In person follow up was possible 7 days a week (excluding weekends in Australia). We accounted for this by prioritising Australian recruitment early in the week, with the average dwell time meaning most PIVCs were removed by the following weekend (if not, data was obtained from medical records or clinical staff). All ongoing PIVC maintenance, care and removal were performed by clinical staff, not study investigators, and in accordance with international and local policy [12, 17]. PIVCs were reviewed by clinical staff for complications at least once in each nursing shift. As this was a pragmatic trial, some participant- or clinician-directed variation in care (consistent with the spectrum of usual clinical practice) was acceptable. Use of additional products, e.g., subsequent bandages, tissue adhesive, additional tapes or bandages, were recorded.

The decision to remove the PIVC was determined by the treating clinical team according to standard practice, and independent of the research team. At catheter removal (unless accidentally removed), the catheter tip was aseptically cut, placed in a sterile container, and sent to the local hospital laboratory for culture using standard institutional.

Outcomes

Phase I was assessed in terms of six feasibility criteria (Table 1) based on published recommendations and prior trials [18–21]. Participant and staff satisfaction were measured using separate, 3-level categorical (ordinal) variables, with survey responses coded as low, medium, or high level of satisfaction. We chose to trial this approach as in our previous PIVC trials 11-point satisfaction scales had limited utility to detect discrimination between different PIVC approaches, and to date there is no validated patient reported experience measure for PIVCs [22].

Phase I feasibility criteria

The primary outcome of Phase II was a composite of catheter-related infectious complications (tip colonisation, local infection, PIVC-BSI and phlebitis [23]). Tip colonisation was defined as ≥ 15 colony-forming units (cfu) of growth on semi-quantitative roll plate culture (Australia) or ≥ 1,000 cfu/mL using quantitative broth dilution (France), in accordance with local laboratory protocols [24]. Local infection was defined as per the NSHN Cardiovascular System—arterial or venous infection (CVS-VASC; adult criteria) [25]. PIVC-BSI was defined as per NHSN as a primary BSI (laboratory confirmed BSI with no other source identified) [25] in a patient with a PIVC within 48 h. Phlebitis was pain or tenderness (≥ 2 out of 10), or at least two of erythema, swelling, purulence and palpable cord [18]. Phase II secondary endpoints were individual components of the primary clinical endpoint, and PIVC failure (composite of infiltration/extravasation, blockage/occlusion with/without leakage, phlebitis, thrombosis, complete/partial dislodgement or infection), dressing durability (defined as dressing adhered to skin on all four sides and no accidental dislodgement), skin colonisation (if performed for clinical diagnosis), skin adverse event (complication) at insertion site, serious adverse event (CHG anaphylactic reaction or PIVC infection-associated death), and participant/staff satisfaction with the dressing. Direct product costs were collated in Phase I, with cost-effectiveness assessments planned for Phase II. The protocol allowed the per-protocol assessment of Phase II outcomes after the completion of Phase I if Phase I was unsuccessful and the trial was therefore stopped.

Sample size

In Phase I, we recruited 300 participants (200 in Australia and 100 in France) to test protocol feasibility and gain clinical experience, rather than for statistical power [26]. Progression to Phase II depended on meeting the pre-established feasibility criteria as determined by the Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in Australia after preparing a data-sharing agreement compliant with European and Australian regulations. The two databases were merged, and analyses were performed using Stata (v19, StataCorp, TX). Phase I feasibility outcomes, including eligibility, recruitment, retention, protocol fidelity, missing data and satisfaction were calculated as proportions and were compared to pre-defined targets.

The unit of analysis was the participant; one PIVC per participant was included. To assess the clinical outcomes, all randomised participants were analysed by intention-to-treat, regardless of dressing received, except those who withdrew their consent to participate. Participants wrongly included or lost to follow-up were described.

The analysis deviated from the protocol in two points. Firstly, analyses were stratified by site due to the baseline site differences that we felt could impact clinical outcomes and make interpretation of results unnecessarily complicated, e.g., the proportion of patients with immune deficiencies (Supplementary Table 1). Secondly, regression analyses were changed to Cox regression, due to the availability of dwell time data at all sites. Results were reported as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Multivariable regression models included potential covariates specified in the protocol, such as gender [27–29], catheter gauge [27, 29], cancer diagnosis [30, 31], baseline infection [27], and insertion site [27, 32]. Correlations between covariates were tested. The covariates for the final multivariable models were retained as guided by the Bayesian Information Criteria score. The proportional hazard assumptions were tested. Microorganisms were identified by species and counts, and missing data were described; no imputation was applied. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

Safety analyses included participants exposed to at least one dressing. The proportion of adverse events was compared by group using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Research ethics approval

In Australia, The Children’s Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee approved the multi-site protocol (22/QCHQ/85411), with local governance approvals at all sites in addition to ethics committee approval from The University of Queensland (Sponsor). The intervention product is registered with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and a clinical trial notification was submitted to the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). In France, the Ethics Committee, the National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety, and the Data Protection Authority approved the trial in compliance with French regulations. Trial registration is with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05741866).

Between May 3rd, 2023, and March 27th, 2024, 300 participants were recruited (two-thirds in Australia; one-third in France) for phase 1, with 150 randomised to each study group (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

Feasibility

All feasibility criteria were met except for eligibility. Of 432 patients screened (143 in France; 289 in Australia), 327 met the inclusion criteria (76%, lower than the pre-defined threshold of ≥ 80%). Eight eligible patients declined participation, and 319 (94%) were recruited (Fig. 1). Reasons for non-eligibility were typically (61 of 105; 58%) patients not meeting one or more inclusion criteria, which anecdotally was most often not “expected to require a PIVC for ≥ 48 hours”. Recruitment, retention, protocol fidelity, data completeness, and participant and staff satisfaction all exceeded predefined targets. Of the 319 recruited, 19 were excluded (pre-randomisation) due to a change from IV to oral medication (n = 3), the presence of a PIVC already in situ (n = 5), the absence of intravenous treatment orders (n = 1), or other reasons (n = 10). A total of 300 participants were randomised, 297 were included in analysis of Phase I results (control n = 150; intervention n = 147; Fig. 1). No patients were lost to follow up, and retention was 99% (one French and one Australian participant withdrew their consent, and another French participant was found to be under legal protection and therefore ineligible). In these cases, only baseline data could be included for the French participants, and no data could be included for the Australian participant. Protocol fidelity was 99%, with 296 of 300 randomised participants (146 intervention participants and 150 control participants) receiving the allocated dressing. Consistent with the pragmatic design, supplementary dressings and securements were used at the discretion of clinicians based on individual patient needs, with comparable proportions across groups (Supplementary Table 2). There were no missing data for the primary clinical endpoint (100% follow-up).

Staff satisfaction with the ease of dressing application was ‘high’ for both groups but somewhat higher in the intervention group (121/136, 89% vs. 115/144, 80%; Table 4). Staff satisfaction with the dressing was most commonly ‘moderate’ and comparable by study group (intervention 52%; control 51%). Overall patient/parent satisfaction was predominantly ‘high’ in both groups although more respondents had ‘high’ satisfaction in the intervention group (65% intervention; 54% controls). We ceased recruitment after Phase I, due to the pre-defined feasibility criteria.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were assessed as secondary endpoint and are therefore considered preliminary. The primary clinical outcome (infectious complications and/or phlebitis) occurred in 20% (30/147) of participants in the intervention group and 19% (28/150) of participants in the control group (HR: 1.27, 95% CI: 0.76–2.13; Table 3). There were no significant between-group differences in the variables comprising the composite endpoint, and no PIVC-BSI in either group. Site-specific regression analyses did not identify factors associated with the primary clinical outcome (Supplementary Table 3).

Overall, all-cause device failure occurred in one in four (78/297, 26%) PIVCs, with incidence comparable between groups. Device failure types were also comparable between groups (Tables 2 and 4). The most common causes of PIVC failure were phlebitis (47/297, 16%) and infiltration (28/297, 9%). Twenty-eight per cent (83/297) of catheter tips, and eight of 297 (3%) skin sites were sent for culture by clinicians, with none of these returning a positive result.

Dressing durability did not differ between study groups. The initial dressing remained completely adherent on all four sides and without accidental dislodgement in 224/297 (75%) participants (Table 4). For dressings that became partially non-adherent, the average time to lifting was similar at 3.0 days (standard deviation [SD] 2.1) for control and 2.9 days (SD 3.3) for intervention. Bedside nurses assessed that (18/297) 6% of all patients required additional dressings at the same time as the study dressing, and (18/297) 6% required the trial dressing replaced during the study (Supp Table 3). Most participants had extra securement products used as determined by the bedside nurses; this was slightly lower in the intervention group (101/144, 70% vs. 112/150, 75%) and was most commonly non-sterile tape used external to the dressing.

Mechanical skin complications occurred in 11/297 (4%) patients, and inflammatory complications occurred in 24/297 (8%) patients overall (Table 5). There were no deaths related to infectious complications and no anaphylactic episodes in the study. No dressing was changed because of skin adverse reactions (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

We report the first ever RCT of CHG-impregnated dressings compared with standard polyurethane dressings for the prevention of PIVC infectious complications, with a primary focus on feasibility outcomes. While this new PIVC technology, traditionally only available for CVCs, and the trial protocol, was generally observed to be feasible, safe, and well accepted by patients/parents and nurses, one pre-defined feasibility threshold (eligibility) was not met, therefore as per our protocol, feasibility was considered insufficient to continue recruitment. Future trials can be informed by our comprehensive feasibility data including acceptable recruitment and retention, and high protocol fidelity indicating strong engagement.

The proportion of eligible screened patients was 76%, which did not meet, but was close to, the 80% benchmark commonly used to define feasibility [33, 34]. This could be achieved with minor adjustments to the inclusion criteria, e.g., ‘Expected need for PIVC for ≥ 48 hours’ was often difficult to meet, especially in the ED, due to uncertain treatment plans at the time of screening. Future trials could focus on populations commonly in need of prolonged therapy such as cancer or high dependency unit patients. Feasibility studies are vital to tease out challenges regarding participant eligibility, highlighting patient and procedural factors that can inform future trials’ recruitment and protocol adherence [33–35]. Although Phase I was not designed to evaluate clinical effectiveness, the collection of descriptive outcome data provides useful context for planning future studies [19, 36]. Device failure, phlebitis, dislodgement, and infiltration all occurred at rates consistent with prior literature supporting the generalisability of our results [28]. No serious adverse events or positive skin cultures were observed, suggesting that the intervention is safe and well-tolerated. While lacking statistical power to compare groups, these observations indicate that clinical outcomes can be reliably captured, supporting the feasibility of evaluating infection prevention and PIVC outcomes in a larger RCT.

Local infection incidence was low in both groups (control 0.7%; intervention 0%) and catheter tip colonisation (control 2.7%; intervention 0.7%). Although from a small sample, the direction of effect is consistent with a large meta-analysis of CHG impregnated dressings for CVCs (12 RCTs; nearly 6000 participants) which found significantly reduced CVC colonisation (odds ratio [OR]: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.36–0.58) and CRBSI risk (OR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.42–0.85), compared to plain dressings [37]. The mechanism of microbial entry into the blood through the intravascular device is comparable for peripheral and central catheters. Thus, a chemical barrier that reduces local PIVC insertion site skin colonisation, could be an effective infection prevention strategy.

The effect of the multitude of CHG-containing medical products in hospitals on the development of CHG-related bacterial resistance remains controversial—due to variability between species, and lack of robust clinical evidence [38] – it is possible that repeated exposure could theoretically promote cross-resistance with antibiotics. Efflux pump activation is a known mechanism for bacterial adaptation and resistance to biocides, and some efflux pumps impact both specific antibiotic classes and CHG [39, 40]. To our knowledge, no studies have demonstrated that CHG use directly leads to antibiotic resistance in clinical practice [41]. However, these concerns underscore the importance of careful CHG use in infection prevention protocols. The bactericidal activity of CHG-impregnated gel dressings lasts up to 7 days, suggesting that sub-inhibitory CHG concentrations, conditions that could potentially foster resistance, are not reached during this period [42]. This prolonged activity likely limits the emergence of resistance or reduced CHG susceptibility [43].

Undertaking clinical trials of PIVC-BSI prevention products is challenging, due to low incidence and diagnostic complexity. Hence, we adopted an outcome strategy designed to maximise event detection, which included phlebitis as a clinically relevant indicator that may signal infection in addition to microbiologically confirmed outcomes [44]. No PIVC-BSIs occurred in either trial group, consistent with the typically low infection rate for this device [3]. We observed pooled phlebitis at 16%, somewhat lower than a meta-analysis with a pooled phlebitis of 19% (95% CI 15.9–22.8%), also using a definition, but higher than the reported 4.5% (95% CI 2.5–7.0%) when no clear definition was used [45]. Phlebitis in our trial (14% control; 18% intervention) could reflect early infection, minor irritation from CHG dressings or skin antiseptic, or non-infective inflammation from irritant infusates [46]. Some cases we classified as infectious could have been chemically induced, especially given that most presented only a single criterion, such as pain or erythema. Since the direction of effect for phlebitis was different to that of the microbiologically-based infection endpoints, we do not consider that it was a valuable addition to the composite endpoint. A better approach may be to increase the number of PIVC skin site and/or tips cultured. Although routine tip culture is not recommended for clinical diagnosis, and practically it is not always possible (e.g. if the patient dislodges the PIVC), in a previous RCT where 86% of PIVC tips were cultured, significant differences were able to be detected between groups for various skin preparation antisepsis [47].

No serious subcutaneous or systemic reactions occurred, and no catheter was removed due to skin injury. These findings are consistent with the literature, which reports similar skin tolerance with CHG-impregnated gel dressings, even with concurrent CHG-alcohol skin disinfection [39, 40]. However, to minimise irritation, it is essential to allow the antiseptic to dry completely before applying the dressing [12]. It was reassuring that dressing durability was comparable with the tested dressings, since fewer dressing disruptions have been linked to fewer infectious complications in other vascular catheter types [48]. Minimising dressing changes also is important for catheter stability, and patient comfort by maintaining a consistent protective barrier at the insertion site.

As Phase I of the study did not meet one of the feasibility criteria, Phase II was not conducted, and therefore there is insufficient evidence from this study to support a full economic evaluation. Although only simple cost data were analysed, CHG-impregnated dressings were more expensive per unit price than standard ones, but there was no substantial difference between the overall material costs per patient (AUD$2.17 for CHG-impregnated dressing patients and AUD$0.63 for controls). These preliminary findings suggest that a comprehensive health-economic analysis will help assess whether the potential reduction in infectious complications justifies the additional cost of these dressings. However, the additional cost should be viewed in the context of the average PIVC treatment per patient which was €102–144 in a recent study [49], and the potential to avoid the substantial costs in treating PIVC associated BSI. Some hospitals may consider evaluating such dressing further, particularly in high-risk population or outbreak settings.

This trial had several limitations including the primary focus on feasibility which precludes definitive conclusions about effectiveness. Stratified randomisation by centre allowed for inclusion of different practices and criteria, but there was excessive heterogeneity and so we could not pool the data for multivariable analyses. Our heterogenous sample brought varied baseline risk, although this reflects the diverse patient and clinical groups for whom PIVC guidelines are applied. Differences in catheter culture methods between Australia (semi-quantitative) and France (quantitative) were unavoidable but would ideally be standardised to enhance comparability. The open-label design introduced potential bias in the assessment of the primary outcome although we avoided this with predefined assessment criteria and a blinded statistician and microbiologists. Including phlebitis in the primary endpoint may either have overestimated infectious events, or increased sensitivity given PIVC tips are rarely sent for culture, and even blood cultures may not be taken if the PIVC is removed and the patient is not unduly unwell. In addition, many paediatric participants were surgical cases (PIVC dwell often < 24 h), and ED participants had shorter average dwell than admitted settings (46 vs. 72 h). Although this reduced exposure to infection risk, surgical and emergency groups represent a large proportion of hospital inserted PIVCs and data in these settings is needed. Results were not adjusted for difficult IV access (DIVA) status. These patients were excluded in France due to limited ultrasound access but included in Australia, where there was some access to ultrasound. Future studies are needed in DIVA and other patients who likely have longer-dwell PIVCs and baseline infection risk.

Despite these limitations, the study had a rigorous feasibility RCT design with daily monitoring by research staff to promote protocol adherence, data accuracy and patient safety. Recruitment across medical and surgical wards in two hospitals in Australia and ED in France enhanced representativeness, as did the pragmatic design. As a result, our findings are broadly generalisable to diverse clinical environments and patient populations requiring PIVCs.

Clinical impact and future research

Our findings provide preliminary data regarding the potential role of CHG dressing for PIVCs. Future studies are needed to comprehensively assess the impact of these dressings on PIVC outcomes, particularly focussing on high-risk groups and economic evaluation.

Conclusion

This rigorous international multi-centre feasibility RCT provides comprehensive early assessment of CHG antimicrobial dressings to prevent PIVC infectious complications. Most aspects of feasibility were met, and a larger clinical trial is now needed to definitively assess clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. Protocol refinements should include a focus on high-risk populations, a primary outcome excluding phlebitis, and expanded microbiological techniques.
