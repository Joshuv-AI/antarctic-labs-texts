Bistability in type I toxin-antitoxin systems may lead to stress-induced persister formation
Authors: Sofija Markovic, Magdalena Djordjevic, Hong-Yu OU, Marko Djordjevic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotic persistence, characterized by a dormant subpopulation of bacterial cells that causes chronic and recurrent infections, remains poorly understood despite being recognized nearly a century ago. Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems, which include a toxin and an antitoxin, are promising candidates for elucidating persister formation. We present the first theoretical model of persister formation driven by type I TA systems, in which the antitoxin is a small RNA molecule. Our analyses and simulations reveal two steady states—low toxin (normal growth) and high toxin (persistence)—with stochastic switching between them. Bistability requires both positive and negative feedback mediated by inhibition of antitoxin degradation. We derive stability diagrams that map mechanistic properties to system dynamics. The model suggests that while type I TA systems may not produce persisters under normal conditions, they can enter a bistable regime under stress, such as antibiotic exposure or nutrient limitation, leading to increased toxin expression or slower growth. Moreover, transiently slow-growing cells can be stabilized as long-living persisters through bistable TA dynamics. Using a cusp catastrophe surface, we identify distinct roles for two toxin inhibition mechanisms in modulating steady states and hysteresis. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for experimental observations and a framework for future studies.Subject terms: Computer modelling, Differential equations, Multistability, Nonlinear dynamics, Stochastic modelling

Introduction

Bacterial persisters are a dormant subpopulation of bacteria that survives antimicrobial agents despite being genetically identical to the susceptible population. Persisters do not proliferate but can revert to a susceptible phenotype, and once the antibiotic is discontinued, repopulate the environment1. Bacterial persistence leads to chronic and recurrent infections2 and is a key factor in biofilm tolerance to antibiotics3. First described by Joseph Bigger in 1944 during his study of penicillin’s effects on staphylococcal infections4, the exact mechanism of persister formation remains unknown despite several considered scenarios1,5,6.

The phenotypic variability underlying persistence serves as a valuable bet-hedging strategy under challenging environmental conditions7 and benefits normally growing cells by reducing competition for limited resources8. Spontaneous persister formation, where cells switch to dormancy under normal growth conditions (in the absence of stress), ensures a constant presence of persisters in a bacterial population, as evidenced by the biphasic kill curve following antibiotic introduction2,3,9,10. In contrast, antibiotic-induced persistence requires the application of antibiotics at low levels for persister formation, with no persisters present in the absence of stress2. Under increasing stress levels, both the number of persisters and the duration of their dormancy increase11,12.

Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems are genetic elements widespread in prokaryotes that belong to bacterial defense systems13,14. They consist of a small toxin protein and an antitoxin that inhibits the activity of the toxin, preventing toxin protein expression under normal growth conditions15–18. Initially described as plasmid-stabilizing factors19, TA loci are now known to be found on both plasmids and bacterial chromosomes20. They are involved in several processes in bacterial cells, which are related to the protection of the bacterial population through programmed cell death21, or survival under environmental stress10,15. Eight types of TA systems have been described22,23 two of which are associated with the phenomenon of persistence—type I and type II24. While the antitoxin in type II systems is a protein, in type I systems, the antitoxin is an RNA molecule that usually interferes with toxin translation20,25. In type I systems, the toxin is a small protein that can disrupt membrane potential and ATP levels, leading to cell death at high concentrations and quiescence at lower concentrations6,26,27, making type I systems good candidates for persistence-forming factors5.

The first type I TA system described, the hok/sok system28, is associated with bacterial persistence when located on the chromosome29,30. In this system, the hok (host killing) toxin is translated as part of the upstream mok (modulation of killing) reading frame. Toxin pre-mRNA is translationally inactive as its translation initiation region is sequestered within a secondary RNA structure at the 5’ end of the pre-mRNA29,31. The primary transcript is slowly processed into the translationally active form by cell ribonuclease, abolishing this first inhibition mechanism. In the second inhibition mechanism, translation of the mRNA is inhibited by the unstable Sok RNA antitoxin (suppressor of killing), which binds to the translation initiation region of processed toxin mRNA, forming an RNA heteroduplex that is subsequently cleaved by RNase III20,29.

In addition to hok/sok, tisB/istR-1 is prominent in persister formation32,33. Both systems share a similar two-step toxin inhibition mechanism (Fig. 1), which is also conserved in many other type I TA systems20,27. In this study, we focus on the tisB/istR-1 system, involved in persister formation during the SOS response in bacteria12,29,32,34, where the stress response activates the system at the transcription level. tisB (toxin) and istR-1 (antitoxin) genes are transcribed divergently; tisB transcription is regulated by the stress response pathway involving LexA (transcription regulator), while IstR-1 is constitutively expressed35. For TisB toxin to be expressed, it has to overcome the two toxin inhibitory steps, which happens through LexA activation of tisB gene transcription (an increase of φh in Fig. 1) as a response to stress29,36. High enough TisB causes membrane depolarization and ATP depletion, presumably leading to growth arrest and subsequent persistence12,35.

Under normal growth conditions, toxin expression is suppressed both by slow conversion of toxin pre-mRNA (h) into active mRNA (h′) and by binding of constitutively expressed antitoxin (s) to the ribosome standby site (RSS) of active toxin mRNA (inhibiting its translation). Stress (symbolized by the bolt) increases toxin gene transcription, which tends to remove its inhibition. By causing growth arrest, the toxin reduces the dilution rate (λd(H)), making positive feedback. Experimental deletion of the tisB gene in the presence of DNA-damaging antibiotics leads to a tenfold decrease, while deletion of the istR-1 causes a 10- to 100-fold increase in persister formation12. These observations were made under stress conditions, and while they provide a convincing argument for tisB/istR-1 involvement in antibiotic-induced persister formation, it remains unclear whether this system could be responsible for spontaneous persister formation under normal conditions. It was also proposed that the tisB/istR-1 system may serve as a persister-stabilizing factor, maintaining cells in the persister state for extended periods32; however, how this stabilization is achieved remains unclear. Additional support for antibiotic-induced persistence (as opposed to the spontaneous persistence commonly assumed for type II systems) comes from studies35 showing that the coexistence of persistent and non-persistent phenotypes depends on TisB induction via the SOS response to DNA damage. tisB/istR-1 system, however, does not contribute to the stochastic switch between the two phenotypes under normal growth condition35.

Starting from the qualitative model30 proposed to explain hok/sok regulation (which has an equivalent mechanism to tisB/istR-1), and advancing previous quantitative modeling approaches from type II TA systems37–39, we model (to our knowledge for the first time) persister formation involving type I TA systems. While one should distinguish between the term persister in the functional sense (survival during antibiotic exposure and regrowth thereafter), and dormancy as a physiological state of slow or arrested growth, within our modeling framework, the high-toxin, low-growth (dormant-like) state is used as a proxy for persistence. The qualitative hypothesis suggests that an RNase E-dependent pathway is a vital regulatory mechanism in type I TA-driven persister formation. As shown in Fig. 1, membrane RNase E regulates antitoxin levels by degrading antitoxin RNA. Under stress, increased toxin pre-mRNA expression leads to an overall toxin increase, causing membrane depolarization and growth arrest. Depolarization disrupts RNase E’s association with the membrane, reducing antitoxin degradation and providing homeostatic control (negative feedback on toxin expression) to prevent cell death. Since RNase E cleaves single-stranded sRNAs non-selectively40, this hypothesis likely applies to other type I TA modules targeting antitoxins. Potential reduction of the translation rate due to toxin activity also limits toxin production (additional negative feedback), while slowing growth increases overall toxin production due to reduced protein dilution rate (positive feedback41).

The multiple nonlinear feedback loops make understanding the system dynamics a highly nontrivial problem that requires a quantitative model. Moreover, the toxin’s effect on cell growth is undisputed, while the effect on translation rate and antitoxin degradation remains to be experimentally validated. Thus, our model investigates both the individual and the combined effects of toxins on the antitoxin degradation and the translation rate.

In this manuscript, we develop a quantitative model that clarifies the mechanisms of persister formation mediated by type I toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems. While several models of type I TA-regulated plasmid maintenance have been proposed so far42,43, to our knowledge, this represents the first biophysical model for type I TA system-induced persister formation. In contrast, type II TA systems, characterized by protein antitoxins, have been extensively modeled in previous studies, see e.g., refs. 37–39. These studies consistently linked persister formation in type II systems to bistability in bacterial growth rates, resulting in subpopulations with two distinct growth rates within genetically identical bacterial populations. The subpopulation with high toxin levels grows slowly and is insensitive to antibiotics, corresponding to persisters. In contrast, most cells (having low toxin levels) grow normally and are sensitive to antibiotics. Therefore, persister formation emerges as a quantitative phenomenon governed by nonlinear dynamics that promote bistability. Stochastic effects trigger switching between the low and high toxin stable states, corresponding to non-persister and persister cells, respectively.

The above highlights the importance of combined biophysical44–48 and nonlinear dynamics49–51 modeling for understanding type I systems. We here build on our previous work on bacterial type II restriction-modification (R-M) systems regulated by C (control) protein, where we used combined biophysical and dynamical modeling to: (i) explain intuitively surprising in-vitro transcription control measurements on restriction-modification (R-M) systems52,53; (ii) understand in-vivo dynamics of these systems54,55; (iii) computationally perturb key features of these systems to elucidate how they affect system dynamics56,57; and (iv) show that C protein-mediated regulation can generate bistability and substantially amplify cell-to-cell variability in the M-to-R ratio58.

R-M systems, along with TA systems, form an extended toxin–antitoxin group59. Similar to TA systems, the regulation of type II R-M systems by C protein involves multiple nonlinear feedback loops52,53. Recently, we employed a similar modeling approach to explain unexpected experimental findings on the regulation of type II TA systems under antibiotic stress60,61. In ref. 58, we demonstrated that the regulation of C-controlled R-M systems also fosters bistability and bacterial population heterogeneity. This suggests a broader evolutionary strategy, where system regulation enhances bistability across mechanistically distinct yet functionally similar systems within the extended toxin–antitoxin group59. Here, we mathematically investigate this possibility for type I TA systems, a hypothesis that is further supported by experimental evidence of antibiotic persistence in this group6,12,26,62.

Specific questions we aim to address in this study are: (i) Could type I systems be responsible for spontaneous persister formation under normal conditions? While this has not been experimentally observed for type I systems, type II systems are commonly associated with spontaneous persister formation9,38,63–65. (ii) Can the regulatory mechanism in Fig. 1 lead to a subpopulation of dormant cells, i.e., bistability in toxin levels in the presence of antibiotics? Confirmation of this would provide a mechanistic explanation behind the experimentally observed involvement of type I systems in antibiotic-induced persistence. (iii) Can distinct roles be assigned to the two seemingly redundant toxin inhibition mechanisms? (iv) Can a quantitative explanation be provided for the qualitative notion of type I systems stabilizing persister formation35.

The outline of the manuscript is as follows: The stability and bifurcation analyses are presented in the Results section to demonstrate bistability through the system’s regulatory mechanisms, where negative feedback is (unexpectedly) crucial for bistability in addition to (as expected) positive feedback66. The system stability diagram are derived analytically, streamlining the subsequent analysis. This section also details our deterministic and stochastic numerical analyses that explore non-persister and persister states and transitions between them. In the Discussion section, we present the biological implications of our findings and place them into a framework for a more comprehensive understanding of how different effects, which we also assess through the cusp catastrophe surface, influence type I TA system-induced persister formation. Finally, the Methods section describes the derivation of the biophysical and nonlinear dynamics model.

Results

Stability and bifurcation analysis

To determine whether our models generate bistability and to identify the parameter values r and S at which the system is bistable, we analytically derived an r−S stability diagram for both models (see Methods section). Figure 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1 show that the system is bistable across a wide range of r−S combinations, with two stable states corresponding to low (normal growth) and high (persister) toxin levels. In Model 1, which includes negative feedback on dilution and antitoxin degradation but excludes translation effects, the bistable range is r = 0−1.6 and S = 0−0.35. In Model 2, which incorporates feedback on translation, the bistable range corresponds to r = 0−2.7 and S = 0−0.3. Consistent with this mapping, the bistable region is confined to moderate antitoxin strength (S ≲ 0.3–0.35), while the saturating limit s/KD ≫ 1 (i.e., large S) is monostable at low toxin. This further supports the validity of the unsaturated-binding approximation (s/KD ≪ 1) employed in deriving the reduced forms of the model, demonstrating that bistability indeed arises in the biologically relevant regime.

We first focused on Model 1 to investigate the individual influence of parameters r and S on steady state toxin concentration. We fixed one parameter at points representative of qualitative differences in system behavior (as indicated by the vertical and horizontal lines in Fig. 2A) and varied the other parameter in the bifurcation diagrams. Fixing r at different values (Fig. 2B–D) and S at different values (Fig. 2E–G) allowed us to assess the existence of two apparently redundant toxin inhibition mechanisms: slow pre-mRNA processing (reflected through r) and negative antitoxin regulation (reflected through S).

Note that r is directly proportional to both φh (which increases with stress, e.g., antibiotic exposure) and the pre-mRNA processing step k′. Therefore, the first toxin checkpoint (slow pre-mRNA processing) ensures that the system remains in the low toxin state in the absence of stress (i.e., when φh and consequently r are low), avoiding cell dormancy regardless of S (antitoxin expression), as shown in Fig. 2B where the blue line (corresponding to low toxin state) starts from S=0, that is, even in the absence of antitoxin low toxin state is possible, solely due to the slow preprocessing step. As r increases (e.g., due to higher stress), the toxin level becomes bistable, with low and high toxin states achieved at high and low S, respectively (Fig. 2C). At this point, antitoxin expression becomes necessary for the existence of low toxin state, that is, for low S values, system is in high toxin state, while by increasing S, the system undergoes transition from high to low toxin state and finally, for very high S, only low toxin state is possible, as the antitoxin amount significantly surpasses that of processed toxin mRNA. Further increase in r, such as through toxin gene overexpression, makes the system monostable with high toxin values that decrease monotonically with increasing S (Fig. 2D). This situation may correspond to experiments where toxin overexpression leads to cell growth arrest.

To elucidate the effect of antitoxin as the second inhibitory mechanism, we start from Fig. 2F, which exhibits the standard hysteresis effect characteristic of bistable systems. At low r (low φh and fast-growing cells with high λd), the system remains monostable with low toxin levels and no persisters. As r increases and the system enters the bistable region, it deterministically stays on the low toxin branch. However, sufficiently large stochastic fluctuations can cause transitions to the high toxin state, and further increases in r will enhance such switching. For sufficiently high r, a discontinuous jump to the high toxin state occurs (upper dashed arrow), as previously noted (Fig. 2D). When r decreases (e.g., due to stress removal), the hysteresis effect occurs, and the toxin remains in the high steady state until a significant reduction causes a jump back to the low state (downward dashed arrow). For low S values (Fig. 2G), the system remains bistable with an unusual hysteresis, staying in the high toxin state even as r reverts. Thus, repression by antitoxin appears necessary for expected bistable/hysteresis behavior, allowing transitions between low and high toxin states with sufficiently large r changes.

The saddle-node bifurcation from a monostable to a bistable state and back can be analyzed using the phase plane analysis, which is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Eqs. (29) and (30) are rescaled in this figure so that the nullcline for h~t′ corresponds to r (straightforward derivation not shown). The system is in a monostable (low toxin) state for small r (Supplementary Fig. 2A), in the bistable state for intermediate r (Supplementary Fig. 2B), and again in a monostable state for high r values (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The bifurcation, i.e., the transition from monostable to bistable (and back from bistable to monostable), occurs when the h~t′ nullcline touches the H~ nullcline at its local minimum or maximum, which leads to the bifurcations shown in Fig. 2.

In Supplementary Fig. 1, we repeat the analysis for Model 2, which includes effects on dilution, degradation, and translation. The results are qualitatively similar to those for Model 1, with only minor differences in the parameter values at which bifurcation occurs. This suggests that the addition of negative feedback on translation does not alter the model’s qualitative behavior but slightly modifies the conditions for transitioning between low and high toxin states. As our results remain robust across model variations, we present further analysis based on Model 1.

Stochastic simulations

Next, we performed stochastic simulations to investigate noise-induced transitions between low and high toxin states. Figure 3A shows two steady states and stochastic transitions between them. The two plateaus in the stochastic simulation align well with the deterministic steady state solutions, demonstrating feasible stochastic switching. The switching time scales are similar to those previously obtained in the simulation of type II systems39. The system’s bistability is further evident from the bimodal distribution of steady state toxin concentrations obtained from 1000 stochastic simulations (Fig. 3B). The higher peak corresponds to the low toxin state, while the lower peak (centered around 200 molecules) corresponds to the persister state. Different parameter combinations result in different stationary molecule distributions. For higher r values within the bistable range, the system makes a more frequent transition to the high toxin state and remains longer in that state, resulting in a more pronounced peak at high toxin concentrations Fig. 3D, E, which is a qualitative agreement with the previous bifurcation diagram analysis (Fig. 2F). The bimodal distributions of log cell division periods, calculated from the simulations and represented in units of toxin-free cell division period (Fig. 3C, F) show the clear link between toxin levels and growth heterogeneity in stress conditions.

Discussion

Effective treatment of chronic and recurrent infections requires identifying and characterizing the key mechanisms leading to persister formation10,67,68. Several mechanisms have been proposed6,10,69, but the key factors for the phenotypic switch to persister cells and the stability of the persister state remain unclear. Type I and II TA systems have been considered key factors in persister cell formation24,70, but recent evidence suggests these claims might hold primarily in the presence of DNA-damaging agents32. There is limited direct evidence for their ability to cause a stochastic switch under normal growth conditions in the absence of antibiotics71,72. Additionally, for type II systems, deleting a single TA module has little effect on persister formation frequency73, likely due to the presence of multiple TA systems within the cell6. Understanding if and how TA systems promote persistence is critical due to their presence in many pathogens15.

In this study, we aimed to quantitatively describe a potential mechanism of type I TA-driven persister formation, which, though less studied than type II TA systems, mainly due to their small size and hydrophobicity18,74, represents a strong candidate for promoting persistence5. We created a simple and explainable representation of a complex system by dividing the model parameters into two dimensionless groups, r and S, with distinct physiological interpretations. Parameter r is influenced by stress through the toxin transcription rate φh, which increases with stress signals like antibiotic-induced DNA damage; toxin pre-mRNA processing rate k′ (the first inhibition mechanism); and the cell division rate, corresponding to protein dilution λd under the common assumption that proteins are stable in the cell. The cell division rate is affected by type I toxins that cause membrane depolarization and ATP depletion6, leading to growth arrest. This effect leads to overall positive feedback on the system, as increasing the toxin amount slows cell growth, decreasing the dilution of toxin molecules and further elevating toxin concentration.

Positive feedback is, as expected, crucial for achieving bistability75–77, as demonstrated by the λd feedback. More surprisingly, a negative feedback loop through the antitoxin turnover λs(H) is mathematically required for bistability in our framework: removing the toxin dependence of λs(H) necessarily yields a monostable system (see Sec. 2.4). Negative feedback is classically known to stabilize steady states or, when coupled with delays, generate oscillations66,78,79. Our model is robust to the presence of the second negative feedback via a reduced translation rate, producing bistability in both cases. This suggests that such a mechanism, if present, alters the parameter values required for bistability but not the system’s qualitative behavior, at least under the assumption that toxin-induced translation inhibition is non-cooperative. This robustness also demonstrates that the bistability obtained and the analysis of the associated regulatory mechanisms do not depend on model details.

To better understand the influence of system parameters on bistable and hysteresis behavior and, consequently, on persister formation, we generated the cusp catastrophe surface plot77 shown in Fig. 4. Similar to Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1, this plot visually represents how the system transitions between low and high toxin states under varying conditions, but this time, when r and S are simultaneously changed.

The cusp catastrophe surface plot.

The surface plot is generated for Model 1, where the three axes correspond to S, r (forming a horizontal plane), and H~ on the vertical axis. The color code for each surface is the same as in Fig. 2. The upper (red) surface corresponds to the high H~ stationary state, and the lower (blue) surface corresponds to the low H~ stationary state. The middle (green) surface that connects the lower (blue) and the upper (red) surface represents an unstable steady state. The green surface is inaccessible due to hysteresis behavior (see the text).

The cusp catastrophe surface plot is calculated for the tisB/istR-1 system using Model 1. The three axes correspond to S and r (forming a horizontal plane), and H~ (toxin concentration) on the vertical axis. In the bistable region, the surface—shown above the S-r plane—folds over itself. The bistable dynamics are shown on the plot so that the projection of the folds on the S-r plane leads to the stability diagram in Fig. 2. The upper and lower surfaces correspond to high and low toxin steady states. The middle surface connecting the lower and upper surfaces corresponds to the unstable steady state. This middle surface is inaccessible due to hysteresis behavior, i.e., the system will reside on either the lower or the upper surface until it reaches the surface edge (fold), at which point it will make an abrupt transition to the other surface (a “catastrophe”), so the “intermediate” (unstable) steady state is never reached.

If the system is initially in the low steady state, a decrease in S (e.g., due to lower antitoxin expression) will cause the system to travel along the lower surface until it reaches the fold. At this point, it will jump to the high toxin state on the upper surface. Conversely, if the system starts in the high toxin state, an increase in S will keep it on the upper surface until it reaches the fold and drops to the lower surface. An equivalent discussion applies to changes in r due to, for example, changes in stress levels. Thus, hysteresis provides robustness to small perturbations, ensuring that the system remains stable in either the high or low toxin state until a significant change in conditions occurs.

The “decision” to switch from one surface to the other significantly affects the system’s fitness and survivability in the presence of stress. While on the low surface (low toxin), the system will divide rapidly and have high fitness but will be susceptible to antibiotics. Conversely, on the upper surface (high toxin), the high toxin amount induces a dormant state, with slow division and low fitness but resistance to antibiotics. Thus, only significant changes in external conditions should induce drastic switching.

Stochastic effects allow for a more gradual transition between these two extreme states (normally growing and dormant cells), resulting in a small subpopulation of persisters within a larger population of dividing cells. This bet-hedging strategy ensures that most cells grow normally with high fitness while a smaller subpopulation of low-fitness cells survives sudden stress, such as antibiotic application. Interestingly, our model shows that this behavior is induced by initial stress, with the system being monostable at low r values. As r increases (e.g., with higher antibiotic levels), the proportion of stochastically induced persisters increases.

Our model thus indicates that antibiotic-induced persistence rather than spontaneous persister formation occurs in type I systems, aligning with key experimental observations5,12,80,81. For instance, in ref. 12 the authors showed that inducing the SOS response (and thus tisB expression) increases persister frequency, whereas deleting tisB abolishes this increase and deleting istR-1 (antitoxin) dramatically elevates persistence. Consistently, the model produces no persisters under non-stress conditions but yields a bistable persister fraction under stress, and model perturbations analogous to tisB or istR-1 knockouts qualitatively reproduce the experimentally observed changes in persister levels. The observed bet-hedging is “activated” by initial low stress, allowing the population to survive potential subsequent high stress. Although the evolutionary implications are yet to be determined, this strategy may allow high fitness (absence of dormant cells) for the population without prior stress signals, and increased survival once the stress signals occur. The effect of stress may be even more pronounced by the hysteresis: once a sufficiently high stress is applied so that the cells transition to the high toxin state, a memory of this stress will be retained so that even if the stress signals are subsequently lowered, the cells may remain in the dormant state.

We specifically focused on the seemingly redundant RNA-driven two-step inhibition of toxin synthesis. Our findings reveal that this mechanism is not redundant; each step plays a distinct role in regulating toxin expression and switching between physiological states. Intermediate values of S (antitoxin expression) allow for a “toggle” switch, with the system transitioning between low and high toxin states for significant perturbations (hysteresis). Changing S minimally influences the low steady state but significantly affects the high steady state, likely keeping high toxin levels at dormancy-inducing but non-lethal levels. This can be seen from both Figs. 2C, 4 (note the steep change of the upper surface in S direction).

On the other hand, r (overall toxin expression rate) significantly influences the low toxin steady state but has a smaller impact on the high toxin steady state (Fig. 2F). Our modeling highlights the distinct roles of the two inhibition mechanisms, aligning with findings that TisB overexpression after regulatory element deletion significantly increases cell death80. Specifically, in our model, this deletion corresponds to high r (due to allowing pre-mRNA processing) and low S (due to abolishing the antitoxin repression) for which from Fig. 2D we see that the toxin amount steeply diverges to very high levels, which may lead to cell death.

Biological systems rarely behave deterministically due to random biochemical fluctuations. These fluctuations, coupled with bistability, generate phenotypic heterogeneity in bacterial populations7. Stochastic simulations are crucial for understanding transitions between two steady states of a bistable system, since they depend entirely on noise82,83. We performed stochastic simulations to support the discussion on stochastic transitions from low to high toxin states, i.e., between normally growing and persister cells.

Our model demonstrated switching behavior in the stochastic regime, leading to increased stability of high toxin states and supporting our discussion of bifurcation diagrams. Different r and S combinations within the bistable region resulted in long-term bimodal distributions, with peaks at low and high toxin states. As r increased, the frequency and stability of the high toxin state also increased, directly supporting the notion that the model leads to a subpopulation of high-toxin (persister) cells. Previous studies have indicated that regulation by small RNAs can enhance the resilience of target proteins, such as toxins, to transcriptional noise bursts84. Such noise has been shown to facilitate switching between high and low protein expression states85–87. The specific regulatory mechanism of type I toxin–antitoxin systems, which involves repression by small RNAs, may thus contribute to further stabilization of the high toxin state.

The stabilization of high-toxin states, combined with bifurcation diagrams, allows us to frame the hypothesis of persister stabilization32 quantitatively. Persisters can be transiently generated spontaneously, even without stress, through stochastic fluctuations independent of TA activity2. These short-lived persisters rapidly revert to a normal state, making them susceptible to stressors like antibiotics. However, it has been proposed that type I TA systems could stabilize these short-lived persisters, inducing them into a long-lived persister state32.

Previously, the specific mechanism for this stabilization was undefined. Based on our results, we propose the framework illustrated in Fig. 5. Normally growing cells reside in the monostable (normal growth) region (beginning of the dashed arrow) and can be transiently induced into a slow growth state, increasing the bifurcation parameter r (which is inversely proportional to the cell growth rate). This transient induction is independent of TA systems. However, type I TA activity may induce a switch to a high-toxin state, as indicated by the vertical dashed red line in Fig. 5. Such a switch was obtained by our stochastic simulations, which also show that once short-lived persisters transition to the high-toxin state, they become stabilized, i.e., transition to another stable steady state prevents their reversal. Our results, therefore, provide a specific mechanism for stabilizing short-lived persisters.

A scheme of the mechanism of persister stabilization by type I systems.

The bifurcation diagram corresponds to Fig. 2F. The blue dashed arrows represent transient persister formation by non-TA mechanisms. The red arrow indicates a stochastic switch of these transient persisters to the high toxin state, resulting in stable persister cells.

Thus, to effectively combat persistence, it is crucial to consider the mechanisms that spontaneously generate persisters and the stabilization mechanisms proposed here. Resuscitating persister cells is essential for developing anti-persister therapies68. Some viable but non-culturable bacterial strains can be revived by removing stress factors or introducing nutrients or growth-promoting factors69,88,89. In our model, a significant decrease in stress factors (related to a sufficient decrease in the bifurcation parameter r) is necessary for such a switch, i.e., to exit the hysteresis region and switch back to normal growth. Similarly, a sufficiently significant increase in cell growth rate due to, e.g., certain growth-promoting factors could achieve the same, as it would have the same effect on r. However, our model predicts that resuscitating persisters may be challenging due to the same hysteresis effect that provides robustness to moderate fluctuations in external conditions.

In future work, an immediate priority is to test in vivo the model-predicted negative feedback from toxin activity to antitoxin RNA degradation; within our framework, this feedback is required for bistability and therefore for robust persistence under stress. By contrast, an additional toxin-mediated reduction in translation appears dispensable for bistability, indicating that the antitoxin-degradation loop is the dominant control element. A second priority is quantitative parameterization—measuring antitoxin turnover under stress, binding affinities, RNA-processing rates, and growth-dependent dilution—to enable calibration and placement of natural systems in the two-parameter r–S plane and to support calibrated stochastic predictions of switching frequencies and persistence lifetimes. More broadly, the framework can be extended to incorporate additional regulatory inputs, cell-to-cell growth heterogeneity, and explicit RNA-processing noise while preserving analytical tractability.

The discussion above underscores a need to understand the physiological processes and diversity of persister cells and the different mechanisms leading to persister formation. TA systems are likely only a critical piece of the puzzle, where several persister-forming mechanisms may act synergistically71,73.

In this study, we developed a theoretical model to elucidate the mechanisms of persister formation mediated by type I toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems, focusing on the tisB/istR-1 system. Our model integrates biophysical and nonlinear dynamics approaches, demonstrating that bistability, characterized by low toxin (normal growth) and high toxin (persister formation) states, requires both negative and positive feedback mechanisms. Specifically, negative feedback through antitoxin degradation inhibition is crucial, contradicting the common notion that cooperative positive feedback alone drives bistable dynamics. Thus, it is clear that experimental efforts should focus on investigating the influence of toxins on RNase E activity, particularly with respect to antitoxin degradation.

Our findings show that type I TA systems may not generate persisters under standard conditions but can shift into the bistable regime under stress conditions, such as antibiotic application or slow cell division, promoting persister formation. Stochastic simulations revealed that the transition between low and high toxin states can occur due to random fluctuations, supporting the hypothesis that phenotypic heterogeneity arises from stochastic processes. The time scales of these transitions are comparable to those previously observed in type II systems, while regulation of the toxin by small RNAs may confer additional robustness against transcriptional burstiness.

Furthermore, the analytical derivation of the stability diagrams, as applied both in our recent work58 and here, has proven extremely valuable for method-oriented research. In contrast to scanning parameter grids with purely numerical simulations, this approach simplifies the bifurcation analysis and provides clear reference points that guide subsequent stochastic simulations. More broadly, a combined methodology—encompassing biophysical modeling of regulatory processes, nonlinear dynamics (bifurcation analysis), and stochastic simulations—has proven to be particularly successful in unraveling the mechanisms generating phenotypic heterogeneity in the broader toxin–antitoxin family.

The critical role of both antitoxin RNA and slow pre-mRNA processing in regulating toxin levels and ensuring cell survival during stress conditions aligns with previous studies and underscores the importance of RNA regulatory mechanisms in the system dynamics. We moreover showed that both toxin inhibition steps have a distinct role and related these roles to the modulation of low and high toxin steady states and the hysteresis behavior. These are nontrivial findings from the model, as it is intuitively hard to understand the exact roles of the apparently redundant mechanisms, particularly in the context of the system’s complex (bistable) dynamics. Our predictions on the roles of the two inhibitory mechanisms should be further experimentally tested in the future e.g., by quantifying the effects of abolishing these mechanisms (individually or in combination) on persister formation in the presence of stress.

Our model results have not only proposed a significant hypothesis on persister stabilization by type I TA systems within a more quantitative and mechanistic framework but also highlighted the likely complex interactions between type I TA systems and other cellular processes potentially involved in persister formation. These synergies, along with the potential to exploit their understanding for therapeutic purposes, should inspire further experimental and theoretical efforts. In summary, this study advances our understanding of type I TA system dynamics and their role in bacterial persistence, offering a robust framework for interpreting experimental measurements and developing strategies to combat persistent infections. Furthermore, a better quantitative grasp of bacterial persistence may aid in addressing other pressing biomedical problems, such as cancer cell persistence90, where parallels with bacterial persistence have been drawn91.
