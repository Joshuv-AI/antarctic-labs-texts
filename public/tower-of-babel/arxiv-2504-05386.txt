Supercooled Audible Axions
Christopher Gerlach, Daniel Schmitt, Pedro Schwaller

Abstract
In the audible axion mechanism, axion-like particles source primordial gravitational waves via their coupling to a dark Abelian gauge field. The original setup, however, relies on a large axion decay constant and coupling to produce sizable signals. In this article, we show that delaying the onset of axion oscillations opens up the testable parameter space and reduces the required coupling to α ≳ 1 \alpha\gtrsim 1 . Furthermore, we investigate the emission of gravitational waves via the axion coupling to the Standard Model photon in the presence of Schwinger pair production, generating a strong signal in the μ \mu Hz or ultra-high frequency range. Cosmological constraints and gravitational wave projections are provided for both scenarios.

I.1  The standard audible axion

The basic setup consists of an axion-like particle (ALP)
\phi
interacting with a
U(1)
gauge field
X
in an expanding spacetime
[
81
,
84
]
:

\displaystyle{\cal S}=\int

\displaystyle d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-V(\phi)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{4}X_{\mu\nu}X^{\mu\nu}-\frac{\alpha}{4f_{\phi}}\phi X_{\mu\nu}\tilde{X}^{\mu\nu}\right],

(1)

with ALP decay constant
f_{\phi}
, dimensionless coupling
\alpha
, potential
V(\phi)
and
the field strength and dual field strength tensors
X_{\mu\nu}
and
\tilde{X}_{\mu\nu}
, respectively.

Using conformal time
dt=a(\tau)d\tau
, the line element can be written as

ds^{2}=a(\tau)^{2}(d\tau^{2}-\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j})
, where
a(\tau)
is the scale factor. The Hubble rate in conformal coordinates reads
H=a^{\prime}/a^{2}
, where the prime denotes derivatives with respect to conformal time
\tau
. Accordingly, we express all momenta in comoving coordinates in the following.

The original audible axion scenario
[
81
,
82
]
considers a potential

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\phi}{f_{\phi}}\right)\right)\,,

(2)

with minimum at
\phi=0
where
\phi
has the mass
m_{\phi}
. The field is initially displaced from the origin at
\phi_{i}=\theta f_{\phi}
, with an equation of motion

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}+2aH\phi^{\prime}+a^{2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{f_{\phi}}a^{2}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{B}\,,

(3)

where
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}
are the electric and magnetic fields associated with
X_{\mu\nu}
. Hubble friction keeps the field at
\phi_{i}
until
H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc,aa}}\sim m_{\phi}
, when it starts to oscillate around the origin. During radiation domination (RD), this happens at a temperature

\displaystyle T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc,aa}}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{90}{\pi^{2}g^{\mathrm{osc,aa}}_{\epsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{m_{\phi}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,,

(4)

where
M_{\mathrm{Pl}}=2.4\times 10^{18}~{\rm GeV}
is the reduced Planck scale and
g^{\mathrm{osc,aa}}_{\epsilon}
denotes the number of relativistic degrees of freedom in the thermal bath.
The initial relative energy density of the ALP then reads

\Omega^{\mathrm{aa}}_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}=\frac{V(\phi_{i})}{3H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc,aa}}^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\approx\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{\theta f_{\phi}}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\right)^{2}\,,

(5)

where we have expanded for small misalignment angles,
\rho_{\phi,{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}^{{\mbox{\scriptsize aa}}}\approx\theta^{2}m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}/2
.

Once the ALP starts to oscillate, it modifies the dispersion relation of the gauge field. The latter is usually
[
81
,
84
]
decomposed into modes of definite spatial momentum
\vec{k}
, whose occupation numbers are given by mode functions
v_{\pm}(k,\tau)
for helicities
\pm
, which only depend on
k=|\vec{k}|
due to spatial homogeneity. They satisfy an equation of motion
[
81
]

\displaystyle v_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}(k,\tau)+\omega^{2}_{\pm}(k,\tau)v_{\pm}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6)

with a frequency that is affected by the evolution of
\phi
:

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}^{2}(k,\tau)=k^{2}\mp k\frac{\alpha}{f_{\phi}}\phi^{\prime}(\tau)\,.

(7)

As explained in detail in
[
81
]
, the frequencies can now become imaginary for a range of momenta when
\phi^{\prime}\neq 0
, causing exponential growth of the occupation numbers of the corresponding modes. The result is a rapid transfer of energy from the ALP to the dark photon (see also
[
8
]
), and the amplification of small quantum fluctuations into macroscopic spatial anisotropies, which source gravitational waves.

As discussed here, the scenario is rather minimal, depending mainly on the parameters
m_{\phi}
and
f_{\phi}
, which control the frequency and amplitude of the GW signal, and the coupling
\alpha
, which is required to be rather large,
\alpha\gtrsim 20
, for GW production to be efficient. The GW amplitude is ultimately proportional to the square of the fraction of the total energy density that is carried by the ALPs at
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
, which leads to a
(f_{\phi}/M_{\mathrm{Pl}})^{4}
scaling of the signal. Thus only values of
f_{\phi}
close to the Planck scale yield observable GWs. Furthermore it is worth noting that the SM photon would not experience a tachyonic instability in the above setup, since its dispersion relation is affected by the presence of a plasma of charged particles, prohibiting efficient tachyonic growth.

2

2

The SM photon always exhibits a tachyonic band, however, the growth rate is suppressed by its Debye mass
\sim eT
. We return to this issue in sec.
III
.

In this work, we explore the possibility of delaying the onset of ALP evolution to temperatures below
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
, in order to expand the parameter space of the audible axion scenario. This will allow us to

•

obtain observable GWs for smaller decay constants
f_{\phi}
,

•

reduce the required magnitude of
\alpha
to
\alpha\gtrsim 1
,

•

consider the scenario where the SM photon plays the role of the gauge field
X_{\mu}
.

I.2  Effect of supercooling

To parametrize the delay of oscillations in a model independent way, we introduce the temperature ratio

r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}\equiv\frac{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc,aa}}}\,,

(8)

where the subscript
“aa”
denotes quantities in the original setup discussed above, and
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
is the temperature where the ALP actually starts to evolve. While we will mostly remain agnostic regarding the specific mechanism that realizes this delay, we demonstrate in appendix
A
that huge temperature ratios can be easily realized in models of trapped misalignment
[
64
,
70
,
91
,
43
,
44
,
73
,
45
]
.

Eq. (
8
) quantifies the amount of supercooling undergone by the axion.
We directly note the enhancement of the relative axion energy density at the onset of oscillation,

\Omega_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}=r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}^{-4}\,\Omega^{\mathrm{aa}}_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}\,\frac{g^{\mathrm{osc,aa}}_{\epsilon}}{g^{\mathrm{osc}}_{\epsilon}}\,,

(9)

where
\Omega^{\mathrm{aa}}_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}
is given by eq. (
5
) and
\rho_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}=\rho_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize aa}}=V(\phi_{i})
.
By solving
\rho_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}=\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}(T_{i})
, we find the temperature ratio where the energy density of the ALP starts to exceed the one in the thermal bath,

r_{{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}},i}=\left(\frac{g^{\mathrm{osc,aa}}_{\epsilon}}{g^{\mathrm{osc}}_{\epsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\theta f_{\phi}}{\sqrt{6}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx\left(\frac{f_{\phi}}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(10)

For lower
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}\leq T_{i}
, the axion dominates the energy density of the Universe,
\Omega_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}\to 1
, inducing a period of thermal inflation which ends with the onset of axion oscillations.
Hence, the Hubble parameter at the start of oscillations reads

H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}=\max\left\{m_{\phi}r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}^{2}\left(\frac{g^{\mathrm{osc}}_{\epsilon}}{g^{\mathrm{osc,aa}}_{\epsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\frac{\theta m_{\phi}f_{\phi}}{\sqrt{6}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\right\}\,,

(11)

where the second expression applies if the axion is the dominant energy component in the Universe.

In the following, we first apply this setup to ALPs coupled to a dark photon, where delayed oscillations open up the detectable parameter space towards smaller decay constants
f_{\phi}
and couplings
\alpha
.
We then extend our analysis to ALP-SM photon systems, where a
large amount of supercooling suppresses the Debye mass of the photon to allow for efficient GW production.

II  Dark photon case

Let us start by studying ALPs coupled to a dark photon, which we assume to be secluded from the SM.
3
3

3

In the absence of couplings to the SM the dark sector does not thermalize, i.e., we assume the initial dark photon distribution to be given by the Bunch-Davies vacuum.
All temperature scales are hence given with respect to the SM thermal bath.
If the dark sector had a finite temperature, the discussion from sec.
III
would apply.

Furthermore, we assume the dark photon to remain massless.
At the temperature
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
, the ALP starts to oscillate. At
T_{\star}
, most of its energy is transferred to the dark photon via the tachyonic instability, producing GWs in the process. The dark photons then behave as dark radiation, contributing to the effective relativistic degrees of freedom
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
The ALP scales like matter, therefore contributes to the DM abundance if its decay rate to dark photons is sufficiently small.
If the decay rate is large, ALPs decay into dark photons before big bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), sourcing an additional contribution to
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

The dark photon dispersion relation (
7
) dictates the range of unstable modes

0<k<\frac{\alpha}{f_{\phi}}|\phi^{\prime}|\,,

(12)

which is unaltered compared to the original setup without supercooling.
The fastest growing dark photon mode carries momentum

\tilde{k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}=\frac{\alpha}{2f_{\phi}}|\phi^{\prime}|=\frac{\alpha}{2}\theta m_{\phi}a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}=\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}\,,

(13)

at the onset of ALP oscillations
[
81
]
.
The dark photon energy density grows with the square of the mode functions, hence
\rho_{X}\propto\exp(2|\omega|\tau)
.
Tachyonic amplification becomes inefficient once all unstable modes have growth rates less than half of the oscillation frequency
|\omega|<am_{\phi}/2
. This yields

\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize close}}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}=(\alpha\theta)^{\frac{2}{3}}\,.

(14)

For details, see appendix
B
.

In the following we first compute an upper bound on the amount of supercooling, before deriving cosmological constraints to ensure a consistent cosmic evolution.
Finally, we compute the GW signal in the presence of supercooling and identify the parameter space observable by future GW experiments.

II.1  Amount of supercooling

In the case of an axion-dark photon system, eq. (
10

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
.
If
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}<r_{{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}},i}
, the Universe enters a phase of axion-driven thermal inflation.
In the absence of decay channels to the SM, the total energy density then would be dominated by either ALPs or dark photons from the time of production until today.

A stronger limit can be derived by considering the experimental bounds on the effective relativistic degrees of freedom
N_{\mathrm{eff}}

Dark photons free-stream from the time of production, scaling as radiation.

In addition, we have set

This gives a conservative upper bound on the amount of supercooling.
Since the axion follows a matter-like scaling from the onset of oscillations, its abundance is enhanced relative to the radiation-dominated background.
Then, the axion energy density at the time of dark photon production reads

II.2  Cosmological constraints

Having imposed the amount of supercooling, let us now derive constraints on the model parameters to ensure a consistent cosmological evolution.
To this end, we divide the parameter space into two regimes: light, cosmologically stable axions that may constitute DM and heavy axions, decaying into dark photons before BBN and thus contributing to the effective relativistic degrees of freedom.
For an axion to decay before BBN, its decay rate must exceed the Hubble parameter,

\Gamma_{\phi\to XX}=\frac{\alpha^{2}m_{\phi}^{3}}{64\pi f_{\phi}^{2}}>H_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{BBN}}}\,.

(19)

The axion energy density after dark photon production reads

\Omega_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\Omega_{\phi,\star}\,,

(20)

where
\Omega_{\phi,\star}
is given by eq. (
16
) and
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
parametrizes the energy transfer to dark photons through the tachyonic resonance.
As we restrict ourselves to linearized simulations (cf. appendix
D
), i.e., backreaction effects are not fully included
[
101
]
, we cannot reliably extract
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
.
Therefore we treat
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
as a free parameter, leaving a lattice analysis of this quantity for the future.

To obtain a limit on the light axion parameter space, we demand that the axion energy density, redshifted to today, does not exceed the observed DM abundance.
In terms of the model parameters, this requires a suppression of the axion abundance of at least

\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{DM}}}\lesssim 2.89\,\times\,10^{-9}\left(\frac{g_{\epsilon}^{\star}}{g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{eV}}{m_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{10^{10}\,\mathrm{GeV}}{\theta f_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(21)

We refer to appendix
C
for the derivation.

Regarding heavy axions, we demand that the contribution to the effective degrees of freedom through perturbative decays into dark photons is
\Delta N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize decay}}<0.3
.
This yields a limit on the required suppression of the abundance,

\begin{split}\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}^{{\mbox{\scriptsize decay}}}\lesssim 6.&64\times 10^{-8}(g_{\epsilon}^{\star})^{-\frac{1}{4}}(g_{s}^{\star})^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
\times&\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\frac{\alpha}{\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\frac{m_{\phi}}{\mathrm{GeV}}\left(\frac{10^{10}\,\mathrm{GeV}}{f_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\,,\end{split}

(22)

where the derivation is again relegated to appendix
C
.
The size of the available parameter space is determined by the competition between the suppression of the relative axion abundance through the tachyonic instability, and the subsequent enhancement until decays into dark photons become efficient.

III  Standard Model photon case

We now replace the dark gauge field with the SM photon which implies additional complications.
Due to interactions with the thermal bath, the photon dispersion relation is modified, suppressing the growth rate of tachyonic modes. Even if this suppression is overcome, the generation of large electromagnetic fields inevitably leads to Schwinger pair production of light SM fermions, reducing the efficiency of the tachyonic resonance.
In the following, we demonstrate how supercooled ALP oscillations lift these restrictions, and identify the parameter space where ALP-SM photon systems produce sizable GWs.

III.2  Timescale of emission

In contrast to the dark photon, the SM photon is thermalized initially.
As for the dark photon, we parametrize the growth time in terms of the scale factor ratio between oscillation of the ALP
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
and the GW emission
a_{\star}
. The detailed derivation is left for appendix
B
. We identify two scenarios: For large ALP masses, less supercooling is required (cf. eq. (
32
)) and the ALP does not necessarily dominate the energy density. The scaling changes in the regime of lower ALP masses, where the Universe needs to be matter-dominated at the time of oscillation, which is possible in the absence of
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
bounds.
The resulting scale factor ratio therefore depends on whether we have RD,

\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}=1+\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}m_{\phi}}{\langle\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}\rangle}r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\theta^{2}m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}}{2\rho_{\gamma}(\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}})}\right)\,

(36)

or matter domination (MD),

\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}=\left[1+\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}{\theta m_{\phi}f_{\phi}}}{2{\sqrt{6}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\langle\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}\rangle}\ln\left(\frac{\theta^{2}m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}}{2\rho_{\gamma}(\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}})}\right)\right]^{2}\,.

(37)

Here,
\rho_{\gamma}(\tau_{\mathrm{osc}})
is the initial photon energy density in the instability band and
\langle\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}\rangle=4/(3\pi)\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}
is the average peak growth rate at finite temperature.
Note that due to the large amount of supercooling we have
\langle\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}\rangle/H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}\gg 1
in most of the parameter space. Then tachyonic growth happens faster compared to the dark photon case, and
a_{\star}/a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}\sim 1
.

To determine the minimal
\alpha
we again demand
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize close}}~>~a_{\star}
, where the time of band closure is determined by eq. (
14
).
This is justified, since the cutoff wavenumber is the same as in the dark photon case. Furthermore, most of the parameter space requires significant supercooling, rendering the finite-
T
photon dispersion relation essentially identical to its zero-

First, to parametrize the efficiency of photon production, we introduce the efficiency factor

\xi=\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}}{aH}\,,

(38)

where
\tilde{\omega}_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}

As a consequence of the large amount of supercooling, hence the decreased Hubble parameter, we have

From the gauge field equation of motion one can derive the energy conservation equation

\dot{\rho}_{\gamma}=-4H\rho_{\gamma}+2\xi HEB-eQEJ_{\mathrm{ind}}\,.

Here,
E=|\vec{E}|

, and

is the energy of the gauge field.
The first term of the equation corresponds to Hubble friction. The second term describes photon production from ALP oscillations
and the last term stems from fermion production, where

is the charge factor.

J_{\mathrm{ind}}
is the induced current, given by
[
47
]

eQJ_{\mathrm{ind}}=\frac{(e|Q|)^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{EB}{H}\coth\left(\frac{\pi B}{E}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\pi m_{e}^{2}}{e|Q|E}\right)\,,

with electron mass
m_{e}\approx 511\,\mathrm{keV}
.

7

We set

if the oscillation temperature
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
is above the electroweak scale,
T_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{ew}}}\approx 150\,\mathrm{GeV}
.
This result assumes that the electron as lightest fermion dominates the Schwinger mechanism.

Interactions with the thermal bath modify the electron dispersion relation.
In principle, a rigorous analysis therefore necessitates a rederivation of eq. (
40

This is approximated by replacing

m_{e,\mathrm{th}}^{2}=(eT)^{2}/8
, which is relevant for larger
m_{\phi}
where less supercooling is required.
While our treatment is simplified, it allows us to identify the parameter space where sizable GW production is possible.

To analyze the amplification of photon modes in the presence of Schwinger pair production, we assume a dynamical equilibrium between the axion, the photon, and the fermions.
Since the growth is sufficiently fast, the emission happens well below one Hubble time and we can set
\dot{\rho}_{\gamma}
in eq. (
39

E^{2}+B^{2}-\xi EB+\frac{eQ}{2}\frac{E}{H}J_{\mathrm{ind}}=0\,.

(41)

For a given efficiency parameter, eq. (

Following the strategy provided in
[

]
, we maximize the photon energy density numerically to find its upper bound for given model parameters.
This will eventually provide an upper limit on the GW amplitude from axion-SM photon systems.

In fig.

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}=\left(\frac{90}{\pi^{2}g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,,

where the Hubble parameter at the onset of oscillations,

Two regions of interest can be identified. For reheating temperatures sufficiently close to BBN, Schwinger pair production is suppressed by the vacuum mass of the electron, and the energy can be transferred to the photon efficiently. The maximal photon energy density then drops rapidly due to the blocking from Schwinger production.
This changes when thermal effects start increasing the effective electron mass, suppressing the Schwinger production. This leads to a range of ALP masses for which the energy can be efficiently deposited in the photon sector again.
For even higher reheating temperatures, the Universe remains radiation-dominated during the tachyonic resonance (cf. eq. (
33

\Omega_{\gamma,{\mbox{\scriptsize max}}}

In addition, note that the efficiency of photon production depends strongly on

Smaller

Therefore, the cosmologically viable axion mass range is tightly constrained for large decay constants (see sec.

Furthermore, fermion production, as can be seen from eq. (

E_{s}=\frac{\pi m_{e}^{2}}{e|Q|}\,.

for our parameter range.

III.4  Cosmological constraints

In the absence of dark radiation, the
\Delta N_{\mathrm{eff}}
constraints from sec.
II

In addition to the requirement

To this end, let us again divide the parameter space into cosmologically stable ALPs and ALPs that decay before BBN,
\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}~>~H_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{BBN}}}

We first consider light ALPs which contribute to the DM density.
If the Schwinger effect is negligible, i.e., energy transfer to the photon is efficient, the ALP abundance after GW emission is given by eq. (

Expressing

\begin{split}\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{SM,DM}}}\lesssim\,&1.66\times 10^{-10}\;g_{s}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}})^{-\frac{3}{4}}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\\

(44)

We refer to appendix
C
for the computation.
Here,
a_{\star}/a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
is the growth time estimate from sec.
III.2
.
This defines the viable parameter space in the small-
m_{\phi}

Let us stress again that lattice studies of the original setup imply
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\sim 10^{-2}
, which would rule out the light ALP parameter space.
As argued in sec.
II.2
, the reduced axion-photon coupling in the presence of supercooling and further model building can decrease the suppression of the axion abundance.
Furthermore, in contrast to the dark photon case studied in
[

]
, the SM photon has large couplings to SM fermions, which could additionally reduce the backscattering into ALPs.
In addition, the presence of the thermal bath affects the gauge field backreaction, which is, however, difficult to estimate analytically.
In the absence of lattice studies of the axion-SM photon system, we therefore treat
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
as a free parameter.

In parameter regions with efficient Schwinger production, photon production is ineffective and
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\sim~1

As only a negligible fraction of the axion energy density is transferred during the tachyonic resonance, we have

\Omega_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\ll 1

Without additional decay channels, the ALP therefore continues to dominate the energy density of the Universe after the onset of oscillations until today.
Thus, this parameter space is excluded.
This mainly affects large decay constants, where the efficiency parameter (
38

In the large-
m_{\phi}

Since less supercooling is required for efficient photon production (cf. eq. (
32

This leads to different thermal histories depending on the efficiency of Schwinger production, the subsequent suppression of the axion abundance through the tachyonic resonance, and the axion decay rate.
Ultimately, this will only affect our results via the redshift factors of the GW amplitude and frequency.
These are determined numerically; see appendix
C
for details.

IV  Conclusion

We have studied the audible axion mechanism in the case of supercooled axion oscillations, which is realized if the pseudoscalar is trapped in a local minimum initially.
Our main results are presented in figs.
2
and
5
, which show the GW predictions for ALP-dark photon and ALP-SM photon systems, respectively.

In the case of ALPs coupled to dark photons, a period of supercooling significantly enhances the resulting stochastic GW background.
This allows for GW probes of a large portion of the axion parameter space towards smaller axion decay constants
f_{\phi}
and axion-photon couplings
\alpha
.
Specifically,
f_{\phi}\sim 10^{12}\,\mathrm{GeV}
and
\alpha\sim 1
are sufficient to produce GW signals in the reach of future observatories, while the original setup requires
\alpha\gtrsim 20
and
f_{\phi}\gtrsim 10^{16}\,\mathrm{GeV}
.

Furthermore, if the onset of oscillations is sufficiently delayed, GW production becomes possible via the axion coupling to the SM photon.
The emergence of a large electromagnetic field leads to Schwinger pair production of electron-positron pairs, which restricts the available parameter space.
We identify a region for small ALP masses and
f_{\phi}\lesssim 5\times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{GeV}
that will be tested by
\mu
ARES
[
107
]
and BBO
[
37
]
.
In addition, heavy ALPs produce sizable GWs at ultra-high frequencies
f_{0}=\mathcal{O}(10^{8}-10^{10})\,\mathrm{Hz}
.

Finally, let us comment on possible future directions. In both scenarios, the light ALP parameter range requires a large suppression of the relic abundance
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}=\mathcal{O}(10^{-4}-10^{-6})
through tachyonic (dark) photon production to not overclose the Universe.
Previous lattice results
[
101
]
indicate
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\sim 10^{-2}
for large
\alpha\gtrsim 20
, which may however change for small couplings
\alpha\sim 1
.
In addition, couplings to the SM are expected to modify the backreaction dynamics compared to the dark photon case, further affecting the relic abundance.
To this end, a lattice study of the modified setup is required, which we relegate to the future.
On top, it would be interesting to explore whether the bound on the relic abundance could be alleviated by further model building efforts, such as a time-varying axion mass.

Also, note that we have merely focused on observational signatures in the form of GWs.
In the case of the SM, large helical magnetic fields are produced which can survive until today as intergalactic magnetic fields
[
12
,
114
,
93
,
53
,
34
,
3
]
. This may provide a complementary probe of the audible axion model, which we aim to investigate in the future.

Appendix A
Trapped misalignment toy model

There exist some models which motivate a delay of ALP oscillations, providing the supercooling we consider in this investigation. Here, we briefly discuss one such model: trapped misalignment
[
64
,
70
,
91
,
43
,
44
,
73
,
45
]
, where additional
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{PQ}}}
-breaking operators trap the ALP in a false minimum.

Originally introduced for the QCD axion, the constraints for the trapped misalignment mechanism are much simpler for a general ALP. Yet, in contrast to the QCD axion potential, the original ALP potential does not change with the temperature. Therefore, we add an additional temperature-dependent part to the ALP potential,

\begin{split}V(\phi,T)=&m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}\left(1-\cos\frac{\phi}{f_{\phi}}\right)\\
+&\Lambda^{4-q}\,T^{q}\,\left(1-\cos(n\frac{\phi}{\fphi}+\delta)\right)\,,\end{split}

(48)

where
\Lambda
is a newly introduced scale that controls the temperature at which the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{PQ}}}
breaking effects become relevant.
The additional term includes a displacement from the initial potential by
\delta
, while
n
is an integer and determines the number of false minima in the potential. For now, we keep the temperature-scaling general, parametrized by the
T
-exponent
q
. Depending on
n
and the initial misalignment, the ALP can be trapped in a false minimum until a certain release temperature
T_{\mathrm{rel}}
is reached at which the ALP becomes free to oscillate around the true minimum.
At the moment of release, two conditions have to be met
[
45
]
,

\frac{\partial V(\phi,T)}{\partial\phi}=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}V(\phi,T)}{\partial\phi^{2}}=0\,.

(49)

Figure 6
:
Scale of the necessary trapping potential as a function of the supercooling ratio generated by trapped misalignment.

These equations can be solved for the scale
\Lambda
, which, in turn, can then be written as a function of the supercooling ratio
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}
, if we identify the release temperature with the oscillation temperature in the supercooled scenario,

T_{\mathrm{rel}}=T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}\approx r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}\sqrt{m_{\phi}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,.

(50)

The resulting
\Lambda
scales as

\Lambda\sim\left(r_{sc}^{-q}m_{\phi}^{2-q/2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{-q/2}f_{\phi}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4-q}}\,.

(51)

In fig.
6
we present
\Lambda(r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}})
for different model parameters.
Clearly, there is a large parameter space where we obtain sufficiently large supercooling ratios
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}
.
Only for too much supercooling (small
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize sc}}
) combined with larger ALP masses and a steeper temperature scaling, the scale would approach the Planck scale.
Note that the ALP acquires a thermal contribution to its effective mass from the
T
-dependent potential. By construction (
49
), thermal and vacuum contribution are of the same order, if
n
is not too large. After the release, the thermal mass becomes suppressed.

Growth time and minimal
\alpha

In this section, we determine the minimal ALP-photon coupling
\alpha
in the case of delayed oscillations.
8
8

8

Note that the possibility of imposing a small

to have efficient dark photon production in the case of trapped misalignment has already been pointed out in

.

To this end, we derive an expression for the scale factor ratio

that parametrizes the (dark) photon growth time. We first give a general expression, before specializing to the dark and SM photon, respectively.

We estimate the time when photon production is completed by noting that the (dark) photon energy density grows as

\rho_{X,\gamma}(\tau)\approx\rho_{X,\gamma}(\tau_{\mathrm{osc}})\exp(2 \Tilde{\omega} \tau)\,,

(52)

where

The dominant photon helicity only grows when the sign of

takes its initial value, i.e., half of the time. Then the conformal growth time reads

\frac{\delta\tau}{\tau_{\mathrm{osc}}}=\frac{\tau_{\star}-\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}=\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}H_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{\tilde{\omega}}\ln\left(\frac{\rho_{X,\gamma}(\tau_{\star})}{\rho_{X,\gamma}(\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}})}\right)\,,

(53)

where we neglect the redshift of all quantities between the start of oscillation and dark photon production.
Furthermore, we need to take into account that the peak mode is amplified with the averaged peak growth rate

Hence, we replace
\tilde{\omega}\to\langle\tilde{\omega}\rangle

Since we are interested in the time when the (dark) photon energy density becomes comparable to the initial axion energy density, we further set

\rho_{X,\gamma}(\tau_{\star})=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}\,.

(54)

Then, the conformal growth time is translated to a scale factor ratio by solving

(55)

C.2  SM photon

Since no dark radiation is involved in the ALP-SM photon system, constraints from

9

Therefore, an axion-driven inflationary period before GW production is not only allowed, but also necessary to sufficiently suppress the Debye mass of the photon in most of the parameter space.
That is, the Universe is matter-dominated when ALP oscillations start.
It is, however, crucial to ensure that the MD period terminates before BBN, either through the tachyonic resonance or perturbative axion decays into photons.
To this end we again split the parameter space into several regimes, depending on their lifetime and the efficiency of Schwinger pair production.

Let us start with light axions, where

\Omega_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}<1

If, on the other hand, the electron mass suppresses Schwinger pair production, hence
\Omega_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=1

(72)

where
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}/a_{\star}
is our growth time estimate from appendix
B
.
Demanding
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}>T_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{BBN}}}
then gives the first bound on the model parameters.
The second constraint is derived from the present-day ALP abundance (
62
).
In terms of the model parameters, we find

\begin{split}h^{2}\Omega_{\phi,0}=\,&7.24\times 10^{8}\;\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}(g_{s}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}})^{-1}(g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}})^{\frac{3}{4}}\left(\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{a_{\star}}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\\
&\times\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{\mathrm{eV}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{f_{\phi}}{10^{10}\,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,\end{split}

(73)

where we have used that for light axions,
\Omega_{\phi,\star}=1
, hence
\Omega_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
(cf. eq. (
20
)).
Conversely,
h^{2}\Omega_{\phi,0}\leq 0.12
yields the required suppression through the tachyonic instability

\begin{split}\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{SM,DM}}}\lesssim\,&1.66\times 10^{-10}\;g_{s}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}})^{-\frac{3}{4}}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\\
&\times\left(\frac{\mathrm{eV}}{m_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{10^{10}\,\mathrm{GeV}}{\theta f_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.\end{split}

(74)

If this bound is fulfilled, no MD period occurs and the GW spectrum receives the standard redshift factors (
26
).

The heavy axion regime is defined by the condition
\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}\geq H_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{BBN}}}
.
This corresponds to the large-
m_{\phi}
region, where less supercooling is required (cf. eq. (
32
)).
Then the ALP does not necessarily dominate the energy density of the Universe at the time of production and the Hubble parameter at production reads

H_{\star}=\left[\frac{1}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\left(\rho_{\phi,\star}+\rho_{{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}},\star}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(75)

with

\begin{split}\rho_{\phi,\star}&=\rho_{\phi,\mathrm{osc}}\left(\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{a_{\star}}\right)^{3}\,,\\
\rho_{{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}},\star}&=\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}(T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}})\left(\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}}{a_{\star}}\right)^{4}\,,\end{split}

(76)

where
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
is computed as outlined in sec.
III.1
.

We can distinguish several cases.
If
\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}\geq H_{\star}
, the ALP decays right after photon production.
In this case the Universe remains radiation-dominated after production, and the GW abundance redshifts in the usual way.

If
\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}<H_{\star}
, the emergence of a MD period driven by ALP oscillations depends on the efficiency of Schwinger pair production and the resulting suppression of the axion abundance.
If fermion production is efficient, only a fraction of the axion energy density is transferred to photons, i.e.,
\Omega_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}<1
.
As a consequence, the suppression of the axion abundance is fixed.
If, however, the entire energy density is transferred to the photons,
\Omega_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=1
.
Then the axion suppression is dictated by the efficiency of the tachyonic instability, parametrized by
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}
.
Hence, we have

\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\rho_{\phi,\star}-\rho_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\,,\quad&\rho_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}<\rho_{\phi,\star}\,,\\
\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\,\rho_{\phi,\star}\,,\quad&\rho_{\gamma}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=\rho_{\phi,\star}\,.\end{cases}

(77)

The entire radiation energy density is then composed of the thermal bath along with the photons produced through the resonance,

\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}=3H_{\star}^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}-\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\,.

(78)

Since the axion energy density compared to the background is enhanced
\propto a
, MD sets in when

\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}}{\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}}\right\}\,.

(79)

The scale factor at the time of axion decay is obtained by solving

H_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}=\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}\,,

(80)

where

\begin{split}H_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}=\Biggr[\frac{1}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\biggr(\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}&\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{3}\\
&+\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{4}\biggr)\Biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.\end{split}

(81)

If
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}\geq a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}
, MD is avoided, and the redshift factors of the GW signal reduce to the standard expressions (
26
).

If
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}<a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}
, the ALP starts to dominate the expansion rate of the Universe before decays become efficient. In that case we compute the length of the MD period by equating the Hubble parameter and the axion decay rate, where

\begin{split}H_{\mbox{\scriptsize decay}}=\Biggr[\frac{1}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\biggr(\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{md}}}&\left(\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{3}\\
&+\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{md}}}\left(\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{4}\biggr)\Biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.\end{split}

(82)

Here,

\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{md}}}=\rho_{\phi}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}\right)^{3}=\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{after}}}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}\right)^{4}=\rho_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{rad}}}^{\mbox{\tiny\rm{md}}}\,.

(83)

We solve for
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}/a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}
and modify the redshift factors for the GW spectrum accordingly,

\begin{gathered}h^{2}\tilde{\Omega}_{{\mbox{\tiny\rm{GW}}},0}=1.67\times 10^{-5}\left(\frac{100}{g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\tilde{\Omega}_{{\mbox{\tiny\rm{GW}}},\star}\frac{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize md}}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\,,\\
\tilde{f}_{0}=1.65\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Hz}\,\frac{2\tilde{k}_{\star}}{a_{\star}H_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\frac{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}{\mathrm{GeV}}\left(\frac{g_{\epsilon}^{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}{100}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}}\,,\end{gathered}

(84)

where
H_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}=\Gamma_{\phi\to\gamma\gamma}
and
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}
is given by eq. (
70
).
Hence, the amplitude is only affected by the additional MD period.
The peak frequency, however, receives an additional redshift during a potential RD period between GW production and MD.

We conclude that a suppression of the Schwinger effect enhances the GW amplitude by increasing the energy budget available for GW emission and decreasing the length of the MD epoch.
In the case where Schwinger production is negligible, a large suppression of the axion abundance delays the onset of MD, i.e., further boosts the amplitude.

In this section, we discuss the numerical simulations of the supercooled ALP-dark photon system.
We use these results to verify our growth time estimate and extract the best-fit parameters for the GW template (
23

To this end, we solve the coupled linearized equations of motion (
3

6

27

Once
\rho_{X}\sim\rho_{\phi}

Let us note that fig.
8
implies a suppression of the axion abundance by many orders of magnitude.
This is an artifact of the linearized approach.

Appendix E
Varying
\theta

Throughout the main part of this work we have employed an initial misalignment angle
\theta=1
.
In this section, we impose
\theta=10^{-2}
,
\alpha=2\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize min}}
and repeat our analysis for the ALP-dark photon system; see fig.
10
.
The colored curves again display the sensitivity regions of several future observatories in the
m_{\phi}-f_{\phi}^{-1}
plane, and the solid lines indicate the parameter space consistent with ALP DM assuming
\epsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize sup}}\in\{10^{-2},10^{-4}\}
.

Lowering
\theta
increases the mass range where the axion can constitute DM.
This is due to the fact that the supercooling ratio (
18
), hence
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize osc}}
, decreases with
\sqrt{\theta}
.
Then, the enhancement of the axion abundance relative to the background after tachyonic photon production is diminished (cf. eq. (
62
)).

From the considerations in appendix
B
, in particular eqs. (
57
) and (
59
), we see that when lowering
\theta
,
\alpha
has to be increased by the same factor in order to maintain the efficiency of dark photon production.
As a consequence, the GW amplitude decreases (cf. eq. (
24
)), and the observable parameter space shrinks.
As a consequence, the size of the region that is both observable and realizes ALP DM effectively remains unchanged.
