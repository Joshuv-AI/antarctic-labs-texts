Efficacy of Kovacs digital splint for treating temporomandibular disorders: a multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jiangshan Hua, Jieyun Zhao, Chuan Lu, Xin Nie, Jian Li, Zhiyong Li, Dongmei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Kovacs digital splint is a new approach for treating temporomandibular disorders (TMD), but comparative studies on its clinical efficacy versus traditional occlusal splints are still lacking.Materials and methodsPatients from three centers were randomly assigned to the experimental group (Kovacs digital splint, KDS) or the control group (soft splint, SS). Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are common maxillofacial conditions, characterized by pain, TMJ clicking, and restricted mouth opening. Studies have shown that 34% of the population experience symptoms or signs of TMD [1]. In severe cases, it affects eating, speech, and quality of life. TMD treatments can be categorized into conservative and surgical options. The commonly used conservative treatments include cognitive behavioral therapy, manipulation, acupuncture, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and occlusal splint [2]. After wearing an occlusal splint, joint pressure is reduced [3], alleviating joint-related pain. At the same time, harmful occlusal factors such as deep overbite, deep overjet, and premature contacts are excluded, reducing muscle-related pain [4, 5]. Due to differences in the design, fabrication methods, and the reliance on clinicians’ experience for subsequent adjustments of the traditional splints, their effectiveness rates vary significantly, ranging from 44.8% to 72% [6–9]. Moreover, some randomized controlled studies have shown that the efficacy of traditional occlusal splints is not superior to that of placebo splints [4, 5].

In recent years, digital occlusal splints have emerged. These splints are created using digital processes, eliminating the errors associated with traditional impression-taking and casting, as well as issues such as material porosity, monomer residues, and polymerization shrinkage during manufacturing. This also reduces the clinical operation time for clinicians [10–12]. Studies have shown that abnormal occlusions, such as deep overbite, deep overjet, and dental defects, can affect the activity of masticatory and cervical muscles, influence mandibular and head posture, and subsequently impact the function of the orofacial system [13–15]. Therefore, adjusting the mandibular position and restoring muscle balance play an important role in occlusal splint therapy. Kovacs digital splint (KDS) used a unique muscle-deprogramming process based on fully digital manufacturing to determine a stable and comfortable therapeutic occlusal relationship for patients, reducing the reliance on the clinician’s experience and shortening the time spent on clinical adjustments [16]. Preliminary retrospective studies have shown that the pain improvement rate after treatment with KDS ranges from 69.8% to 71.6% [16, 17]. However, there is still a lack of comparative studies between KDS and traditional splints.

This study aims to compare the clinical efficacy of the KDS and soft splints (SS) in treating TMD through a multicenter randomized controlled trial.

Method

Trial design

This study is a multicenter, single-blind, randomized controlled trial conducted at three centers. Ethics approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of our institution (SH9H-2021-C74-1). Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn) (ChiCTR2200064940).

Participants

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age between 18 and 65 years; (2) Diagnosed with TMD according to the 2014 DC/TMD diagnostic criteria and MRI examination, and meeting at least one of the following conditions: (a) Pain visual analogue scale score ≥ 3 (0 to 10); (b) Maximum mouth opening ≤ 30 mm; (c) Presence of clicking in the joint that affects the quality of life (score = 6) (scoring rules are described in Data Collection and Blinding); (3) Willing to participate in the study, with good compliance, and signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Received intra-articular drug injections or invasive procedures within the past 3 months; (2) Diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis, traumatic arthritis, suppurative arthritis, joint cysts, joint tumors.

Randomization and allocation

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to experimental group (Kovacs digital splint, KDS) or control group (soft splint, SS). Randomization was performed using block randomization with a block size of four. The random allocation was generated by third-party statisticians using a centralized randomization system.

Interventions

For the KDS group, an auxiliary device called balancer was first custom-designed for each patient to determine the therapeutic jaw position. When wearing the balancer, a one-millimeter gap was maintained between the upper and lower molars. Patients then underwent a specialized muscle deprogramming process while wearing the balancer, included: small mouth opening, medium mouth opening, maximum mouth opening, maximum laterotrusion, and protrusion. The jaw position achieved during this process was recorded as a stable therapeutic position and subsequently imported into Exocad software for splint design. The detailed design and manufacturing process of the splint has been described in our previous study [16]. (Figs. 1 and 2A).

For the SS group, after oral scanning, a 3D printer (Xianyang Holy Medical, China) was used to create the model of the lower dental arch. The soft splint material used was polyvinyl chloride (PVC) with a thickness of 2 mm. The model was placed in a vacuum-pressing machine to fabricate the soft splint. The splint was trimmed so that its posterior edge covered both sides of the second molars, and its lower edge was aligned with the gingival margin (Fig. 2B). Both groups were required to wear the splint for at least 12 h per day.

Data collection and blinding

Patient’s age and gender were recorded. Based on pre-treatment MRI results, they were categorized as myogenous TMD (normal disc-condyle relationship), and arthrogenous TMD (anterior disc displacement with or without reduction (ADDwR, ADDwoR). If the condyle displayed cortical bone discontinuity, concave resorption, flattening, osteophyte formation or sclerosis, the patient was further diagnosed with osteoarthritis (OA) [18]. Evaluations were conducted at pre-treatment (T0), 2 weeks post-treatment (T1), 5 weeks post-treatment (T2).

Assessment included pain using the visual analogue scale (VAS) (0–10, with 0 being no pain and 10 being unbearable pain), maximum mouth opening (MMO), and joint clicking score (0: never, 1: occasionally, 2: 1–2 times/month, 3: once/week, 4: multiple times/week, 5: daily, 6: almost every time opening the mouth) [19].

In this trial, patients were asked to take off the splints before their symptom evaluated. So blinding was applied to the clinical evaluators and statisticians and was not applied to the patients and clinicians adjusting the splints. Patients were unaware of the differences in the materials and fabrication methods of the two types of occlusal splints until the end of the trial, so there was no placebo effect from the digital fabrication process.

At the end of treatment, the overall effective rates and each effective rates among different subgroups based on gender, age (≤ 20 years, > 20 to ≤ 40 years, > 40 years), disease stage (normal condylar relationship, ADDwR, ADDwoR, ADDwoR + OA), and symptom types (pain VAS ≥ 3.0, maximum mouth opening ≤ 30 mm, clicking score = 6) between the two groups were calculated and compared.

The secondary outcomes were the changes in pain, mouth opening, and clicking at different time points (T1 and T2) for the two groups.

Sample-size calculation

This study employs a non-inferiority design, with the primary outcome being treatment effective rate. Based on our previous evaluation of the effective rate of the Kovacs digital splint [16], the treatment effective rate for both groups were set at 80%. The significance level (α) was set at 0.025 (one-sided), the power (β) at 0.8, and the non-inferiority margin at -15%. The minimum sample size per group was calculated to be 112, for a total of 224 participants. Considering an approximate 10% dropout rate and random block size of four, the total sample size was 252.

Statistical analysis

For MMO, interrater reliability was expressed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For joint clicking score, interrater reliability was expressed using the Fleiss kappa coefficient. Both the primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed in the per-protocol set (PPS). The chi-square test was used to analyze: (1) The difference of the overall effective rates between KDS and SS. (2) The differences of effective rates between KDS and SS across different subgroups of gender, age, disease stage, and symptom type. A paired sample t-test was used to compare the clinical symptoms of both groups before and after treatment (T0,T1 and T2).

Results

Participants

A total of 264 patients from three centers were enrolled in the study, with the enrollment period from February 23, 2022, to May 25, 2023. Patients’ follow-up ended on July 13, 2023. KS group and SS group each included 132 patients. Due to the pandemic, 19 patients in KDS group and 14 patients in SS group were unable to complete the follow-up. Ultimately, 231 patients (113 in KDS group and 118 in SS group) completed the study, exceeding the minimum sample size of 224 participants. Outcomes

Interrater reliability was evaluated among three evaluators, with one evaluator from each center. For MMO, the ICC was 0.896 (95% CI, 0.820 to 0.945). For the clicking score, the Fleiss’ kappa coefficient was 0.896 (95% CI, 0.893 to 0.899). Both metrics demonstrated excellent interrater reliability.

At the end of follow-up (T2), in the KDS group, 69 out of 113 patients were deemed effective, resulting in an effective rate of 61.1%. In the SS group, 56 out of 118 patients were deemed effective, resulting in an effective rate of 47.5%. The chi-square test showed a significant difference in effective rates between the two groups (P = 0.019), which was 13.6%, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.9% to 26.3%. Since the lower bound was greater than 0%, the KDS group was determined to be superior to the SS group (Table 3).

Discussion

Splints are an important conservative treatment for TMD, widely used due to their non-invasive and reversible characteristics. They can be classified into different types, including stabilization splints, SS, and anterior repositioning splints (ARS). Stabilization splints and SS are the most commonly used traditional occlusal splints in clinical practice. Both have proven effective in treating pain, clicking, and limited mouth opening in TMD patients [4]. Soft splint is easy to acquire but susceptible to wear and tear which is only used for short-term. Stabilization splint is hard and wear-resisting but requires numerous clinical adjustments based on the doctor’s experience including the determination of jaw position and occlusal adjustment etc. The indications for ARS are relatively limited, primarily targeting ADDwR. The KDS utilizes a fully digital manufacturing workflow and a balancer was used to isolate harmful occlusal factors and relax the masticatory muscles through a process of muscle deprogramming to achieve a stable therapeutic jaw position. The semi-anatomical design, which maintains a certain cusp-fossa relationship with the opposing teeth, guides the lower jaw into the therapeutic position while also allowing further adjustments [16]. In our previous retrospective study on KDS efficacy, we mainly assessed the subjective symptoms of patients, including those with mild symptoms (pain VAS score < 3, MMO > 30 mm) during 5 months follow up. The results showed that the effective rates for pain, diet, mandibular movement, and quality of life were 71.6%, 56.4%, 55.3%, and 54.1%, respectively [16], with the highest effective rate observed for pain relief. The condylar bone predominantly remained stable on MRI during 7-month follow-up. However, there is still a lack of comparison between the efficacy of KDS and traditional splint.

In this study, we chose SS as a control group because compared with stabilization splint (hard splint), it is easy to acquire without differences among doctors across the 3 centers. Furthermore, according to Al-Moraissi et al. meta-analysis in 2020 [4], there was no significant difference between hard and soft splint in treating arthrogenous and myogenous pain. The results in this study showed that KDS had superior clinical outcomes compared to SS, especially in patients over 40 years of age and those with normal condylar relationships. This may be due to the higher proportion of muscle-related pain in older patients with normal condylar relationships [20]. Although both KDS and SS alleviate arthrogenous pain by occlusal elevation and joint decompression, KDS additionally positions the mandible in the muscle-relaxed position established through muscle deprogramming. Therefore, it provides a direct therapeutic benefit for relaxing the masticatory muscles, resulting in better outcomes for myogenous pain. Furthermore, the effective rate for clicking improvement was higher in the KDS group compared to SS (45% vs. 34.8%), and the clicking scores in the KDS group continued to decrease over time, while the clicking scores in the SS group showed no significant change from T1 to T2. This may be due to KDS’s ability to reposition the jaw and restore and stabilize the disc-condyle relationship in patients with ADDwR. However, the efficacy of KDS in reducing joint clicking is lower than anterior repositioning splint (ARS), which achieves success rates exceeding 90% [21]. This disparity arises because the ARS requires mandibular advancement to a specific position where the clicking is eliminated to ensure definitive disc recapture, which is typically located significantly anterior to the muscle-relaxed position. Since the KDS does not effectively control the mandible in clicking-free position, its efficacy in resolving clicking is inherently inferior to that of the ARS.

TMD is 1.5-2 times more prevalent in women than in men, and that 80% of patients treated for TMD are women [22]. Therefore, the enrolled population had a gender imbalance. However, no evidence suggests that the efficacy of occlusal splint therapy differs between male and female patients. Subgroup analysis revealed that the treatment efficacy rates for KDS versus SS were 58.3% vs. 40% in males and 61.8% vs. 50% in females. In both genders, KDS demonstrated a comparatively higher efficacy rate. Therefore, while the enrolled population had a gender imbalance, it reflects the real-world epidemiological profile of TMD and is unlikely to influence the study’s conclusions. Meanwhile, we found that the proportion of patients aged 20 to 40 and with ADDwoR was higher than the other type. List et al. [23] found that the incidence of TMD is highest in women aged 20–40, which is consistent with the proportion of patients included in our study. Valesan et al. [24] found that the prevalence of ADDwR was the highest in adults, reaching 33.2%. However, ADDwR patients often have milder symptoms and a lower propensity to seek treatment compared to those with ADDwoR, which explains the higher proportion of ADDwoR patients in our study. Nevertheless, the epidemiological profile of TMD may vary across different regions, which could influence the overall effectiveness rate of KDS therapy. Our subgroup analysis, however, clarified that KDS demonstrates superior efficacy for patients over 40 years old with myogenous pain. In clinical practice, we can select targeted treatments based on the patient’s age and diagnosis.

Although this study used a 5-week follow-up period, which is relatively short, previous research shows that patients undergoing splint treatment experience the most significant symptom improvement in the first 2–3 weeks, with symptoms stabilizing by 6 weeks [25, 26]. Between 3 and 12 months, patients’ symptoms generally do not change significantly [6, 7, 26]. This study similarly found that patients experienced more significant symptom improvements in the first two weeks, with continued improvement from 2 to 5 weeks, though the changes began to decrease. Therefore, the 5-week follow-up is sufficient for assessing the treatment efficacy. Since this study mainly evaluated the improvement in clinical symptoms and had a short follow-up period, radiographic changes were not significant and were not assessed.

The digital design and machining process of the KDS provides higher accuracy and improves patient comfort, but it increases the cost of fabrication. However, as a stable jaw position was achieved through muscle deprogramming prior to fabrication and the splint was manufactured with high precision, it required significantly fewer adjustment appointments during treatment compared to other hard splints. This reduction in follow-up visits lowers the time and travel burden for patients and conserves chairside time for clinicians. Therefore, KDS demonstrates both economic feasibility and clinical applicability.

In this study, pain level was evaluated by subjective scoring. However, previous research has shown that psychosocial factors, such as pain catastrophizing, can influence patients’ subjective pain assessments [27]. In this study, patients with confirmed diagnosis of mental disorders were excluded, and random grouping was used to minimize the impact of these factors on the analysis of treatment efficacy differences. Still, future studies should explore more objective indicators for evaluating treatment outcomes. Also, this study did not differentiate patients based on the specific location of masticatory muscle pain or arthrogenous pain, and the short follow-up period did not allow for assessment of potential symptom recurrence in a long time period. Future research will further refine the classification of clinical symptoms and include long-term radiographic follow-up for comparison.

Conclusion

The clinical efficacy of KDS for treating TMD was superior to SS, especially for myogenous TMD.
