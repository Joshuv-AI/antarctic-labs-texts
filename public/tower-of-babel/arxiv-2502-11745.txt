Understanding RowHammer Under Reduced Refresh Latency: Experimental Analysis of Real DRAM Chips and Implications on Future Solutions

Abstract
Read disturbance in modern DRAM chips is a widespread weakness that is used for breaking memory isolation, one of the fundamental building blocks of system security and privacy. RowHammer is a prime example of read disturbance in DRAM where repeatedly accessing (hammering) a row of DRAM cells (DRAM row) induces bitflips in physically nearby DRAM rows (victim rows). Unfortunately, shrinking technology node size exacerbates RowHammer and as such, significantly fewer accesses can induce bitflips in newer DRAM chip generations. To ensure robust DRAM operation, state-of-the-art mitigation mechanisms restore the charge in potential victim rows (i.e., they perform preventive refresh or charge restoration). With newer DRAM chip generations, these mechanisms perform preventive refresh more aggressively and cause larger performance, energy, or area overheads. Therefore, it is essential to develop a better understanding and in-depth insights into the preventive refresh to secure real DRAM chips at low cost. In this paper, our goal is to mitigate RowHammer at low cost by understanding the preventive refresh latency and the impact of reduced refresh latency on RowHammer. To this end, we present the first rigorous experimental study on the interactions between refresh latency and RowHammer characteristics in real DRAM chips. Our experimental characterization using 388 real DDR4 DRAM chips from three major manufacturers demonstrates that a preventive refresh latency can be significantly reduced (by 64%) at the expense of requiring slightly more (by 0.54%) preventive refreshes. To investigate the impact of reduced preventive refresh latency on system performance and energy efficiency, we reduce the preventive refresh latency and adjust the aggressiveness of existing RowHammer solutions by developing a new mechanism, Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation (PaCRAM). Our results show that by reducing the preventive refresh latency, PaCRAM reduces the performance and energy overheads induced by five state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation mechanisms with small additional area overhead. Thus, PaCRAM introduces a novel perspective into addressing RowHammer vulnerability at low cost by leveraging our experimental observations. To aid future research, we open-source our PaCRAM implementation at https://github.com/CMU-SAFARI/PaCRAM .

DRAM organization.
Fig.

Each DRAM chip has multiple DRAM banks each of which contains multiple subarrays. A DRAM bank is organized as a two-dimensional array of DRAM cells, where a row of cells is called a DRAM row. A DRAM cell consists of i) a storage capacitor, which stores one bit of information in the form of electrical charge, and ii) an access transistor.

Effect of Charge Restoration Latency on the Half-Double Access Pattern

An aggressor row can cause bitflips in victim rows that are not physically adjacent
[
2
,
63
]
. The impact of RowHammer on a victim row decreases and eventually disappears as the physical distance between the victim and the aggressor rows increases. To account for this characteristic, prior works define
blast radius
as the distance between an aggressor row and its furthest victim row
[
2

38

[

To mitigate Half-Double bitflips, existing RowHammer mitigation mechanisms perform preventive refreshes on all four rows in
\pm 2
-row distance of the aggressor row

8
PaCRAM

To demonstrate the potential benefits of our experimental observations, we propose a new memory controller-based mechanism,
Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation (PaCRAM)
. PaCRAM works with an existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism and leverages our experimental observations to safely reduce the latency of preventive refreshes that the existing mitigation mechanism performs. By doing so, PaCRAM
introduces a novel perspective into addressing RowHammer vulnerability at low cost.

8.1
Overview of PaCRAM

Fig.
15
illustrates a high-level overview of PaCRAM.
When a DRAM row is activated
1
, PaCRAM and the existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism
are provided with the activated row address.
The mitigation mechanism decides whether to perform a preventive refresh
and issues a preventive refresh if it detects a potential aggressor row
2
.
Simultaneously, PaCRAM determines whether preventive refresh latency can be reduced and provides the memory controller with reduced or nominal preventive refresh latency
3
.
The memory request scheduler schedules a preventive refresh (generated by the existing mitigation mechanism) using the preventive refresh latency (determined by PaCRAM)
4
.

Figure 15
:
Overview of PaCRAM

8.2
Reducing Preventive Refresh Latency

PaCRAM uses two preventive refresh latencies: i)
nominal latency
for full charge restoration
and ii)
reduced latency
for partial charge restoration.
5
5

5

PaCRAM does
not
affect the latency of periodic refreshes.

To safely perform partial charge restoration, PaCRAM overcomes two challenges.
First, reducing preventive refresh latency can reduce the row’s
N_{RH}{}
(Takeaway
5.1
).
To address this challenge,
PaCRAM configures the existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism with a reduced
N_{RH}{}
, based on experimental characterization of the DRAM chips that are being controlled.
Second, repeated consecutive partial charge restoration might cause failures (Takeaway
5.4
). To address this challenge,
PaCRAM limits the number of consecutive partial charge restorations.
To this end, PaCRAM assigns each DRAM row into one of two states: i)
F
: the row has to be refreshed using
full
charge restoration and ii)
P
: the row can be refreshed using
partial
charge restoration. Initially, all rows are in the
F
-state
and PaCRAM periodically pulls each row’s state to
F
.
When a row is refreshed using
full
charge restoration, PaCRAM switches the row’s state to
P
, so that future preventive refreshes can be performed using
partial
charge restoration.
The time interval for resetting rows to
F
-state is the smallest time window in which the maximum number of refreshes with partial charge restoration can be safely performed (§
8.3
).
By doing so, PaCRAM performs full charge restoration for the first preventive refresh targeting a DRAM row and partial charge restoration for the rest of the preventive refreshes until the row’s state is periodically reset into
F
.

Security.

PaCRAM safely reduces charge restoration latency of preventive refreshes by applying two modifications to the existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism, based on experimental characterization data: i) reducing
N_{RH}{}
and ii) limiting the maximum number of consecutive refreshes using partial charge restoration. Therefore, PaCRAM guarantees that the existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism is correctly configured based on the DRAM chip’s vulnerability level. An existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism’s security against DRAM read disturbance-based exploits is identical to that of the mechanism integrated with PaCRAM.

8.3
Detailed Implementation and Configuration

Full charge restoration interval (
\bm{t_{FCRI}{}}
).

To ensure robust execution, we calculate the smallest time interval at which a DRAM row should be refreshed using full charge restoration. We assume the worst case, where a DRAM row is hammered as frequently as possible (i.e., activated every
t_{RC}
).
The latency of a preventive refresh that performs partial charge restoration is computed as the sum of the reduced charge restoration latency and the nominal precharge latency (
t_{RAS(Red)}{}
+
t_{RP}{}
). The RowHammer mitigation mechanism performs a preventive refresh after
N_{RH}{}
row activations. Therefore,
the smallest interval of such refreshes is the cumulative latency of performing i)
N_{RH}{}
activations (
N_{RH}\times t_{RC}
) and ii) the preventive refresh (
t_{RAS(Red)}{}
+
t_{RP}{}
), resulting in (
N_{RH}{}

t_{RC}
+
t_{RAS(Red)}{}
+
t_{RP}{}
).
From experimental characterization data (Fig.
12
), we obtain
the maximum number of consecutive partial charge restorations (
N_{PCR}{}
)
.
The smallest time window that can contain
N_{PCR}{}
of such preventive refreshes is
N_{PCR}{}(N_{RH}{}t_{RC}{}+t_{RAS(Red)}{}+t_{RP}{})
, which we denote as the
full charge restoration interval (
t_{FCRI}{}
)
.
6
6

6

If
t_{FCRI}{}
is larger than
t_{REFW}{}
, PaCRAM uses the reduced charge restoration latency for
all
preventive refreshes since periodic refresh performs full charge restoration before a row can receive
N_{PCR}{}
partial charge restorations.

For example, the tested module S6 exhibits the
N_{RH}{}
and
N_{PCR}{}
values of 3.9K and 2K, respectively, at
t_{RAS(Red)}=0.36t_{RAS}
and thus it requires rows to be refreshed with full charge restoration at an interval of
374ms
.

Maintaining row states.

To maintain each row’s state at low cost, PaCRAM implements a bit vector, called
fully restored bit vector (
FR
)
.
FR
stores a single bit for each row in the DRAM module. Each bit in
FR
indicates whether a row should be refreshed using nominal latency within a
t_{FCRI}{}
.
FR
can be implemented as an SRAM array in the memory controller.

8.4
Hardware Complexity

PaCRAM’s metadata storage (i.e.,
FR
) can be implemented using SRAM in the memory controller. The metadata size is independent of
N_{RH}{}
, making PaCRAM scalable with the increasing RowHammer vulnerability.
PaCRAM stores one bit of information per DRAM row on the fully restored bit vector (
FR
). We evaluate PaCRAM’s chip area and access latency overheads using CACTI
[
222
]
. Our results show that PaCRAM has an area cost of
0.0069mm^{2}
per DRAM bank where a DRAM bank consists of 64K DRAM rows.
When configured for a dual rank system with 16 banks at each rank, PaCRAM requires storage of
8KB
per bank,
corresponding to a
0.09\%
overhead on a high-end Intel Xeon processor
[
146
]
(
1.35\%
of the memory controller area
[
223
]
).
The metadata access latency in SRAM is
0.27ns
, which is significantly smaller than the latency of a DRAM row activation (e.g.,
14ns

[
224
]
), and can be hidden by the row activation latency.

12
Conclusion

We present the first rigorous experimental study on the effects of reduced refresh latency on RowHammer vulnerability of modern DRAM chips. Our analysis of 388 real DDR4 DRAM chips from three major manufacturers demonstrate that charge restoration latency of RowHammer-preventive refreshes can be significantly reduced
at the expense of requiring slightly more preventive refreshes.
We propose a new mechanism,
Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation
(
PaCRAM
), which robustly reduces the preventive refresh latency in existing RowHammer mitigation mechanisms. Our evaluation demonstrates that PaCRAM significantly enhances system performance and energy efficiency when used with five state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation mechanisms while introducing small additional area overhead. By doing so, PaCRAM enables new trade-offs in RowHammer mitigation.
We hope and expect that the understanding we develop via our rigorous experimental characterization and the resulting PaCRAM mechanism will inspire DRAM manufacturers and system designers to efficiently and scalably enable robust and efficient operation as DRAM technology node scaling exacerbates read disturbance.

Appendix B
PaCRAM for Other Types of

Charge Restoration

PaCRAM can be extended to also reduce the charge restoration latency for
periodic refreshes and dynamic accesses
, in addition to preventive refreshes (as we have done so far).
To exemplify the potential benefits of such extensions,
we analyze the impact of reducing charge restoration latency for periodic refreshes, which restore every cell’s charge in the module every
t_{REFW}{}
to prevent data retention failures, similarly to prior work
[
200
]
.
To do so, we modify PaCRAM such the latency of
all
periodic refreshes is reduced since our experimental characterization demonstrates that charge restoration latency can be reduced significantly without causing any data retention failures (Takeaway
7
).
We use a configuration that does
not
employ any RowHammer mitigation mechanism (i.e., no preventive refreshes are issued) and sweep the charge restoration latency for periodic refreshes.

Fig.
19
demonstrates multi-core system performance (left subplot) and DRAM energy consumption (right subplot) of PaCRAM with reduced periodic refreshes and the baseline system with nominal periodic refreshes normalized to a hypothetical system which
does
not
perform any periodic refreshes (y-axis) for different DRAM chip densities (x-axis). As DRAM chip density increases, the number of rows per bank increases. Hence, the number of rows refreshed with a single periodic refresh and the periodic refresh latency increase
[
137
,
138
,
139
,
140
,
141
,
142
,
143
,
144
]
. Six curves represent different periodic refresh latencies normalized to the nominal periodic refresh latency, the baseline system with nominal refresh latency is where periodic refresh latency is 1.00 (marked with
\bm{\times}
). The shades show the variation across tested workloads.

Figure 19
:
System performance (left) and energy consumption (right) versus different DRAM chip capacities for different periodic refresh latencies

We make three observations from Fig.
19
.

First, PaCRAM significantly improves system performance and energy efficiency for all DRAM chip densities and reduced periodic refresh latencies (i.e.,
<1.00
)
compared to the baseline system that uses nominal charge restoration latency, across all tested workloads.
For example, reducing periodic refresh latency by 64% (i.e.,
PaCRAM-H
’s best-observed
charge restoration latency) improves multi-core system performance and energy efficiency by 23.31%, and 36.49% of a 512Gb DRAM chip, respectively.
Second, performance improvement and energy efficiency increase as periodic refresh latency decreases. For example, system performance increases by 8.83% when periodic refresh latency decreases from 64% to 36%.
Third, for all periodic refresh latency values, periodic refresh overheads increase as DRAM chip capacity increases.
Based on these observations, we conclude that reducing the latency of periodic refreshes improves PaCRAM’s performance benefits.

To robustly employ this extension of PaCRAM to periodic refreshes, PaCRAM needs to guarantee that a DRAM row does
not
receive
N_{PCR}{}
consecutive periodic refreshes using reduced charge restoration latency. As each DRAM row is refreshed with periodic refresh once every refresh window (
t_{REFW}{}
), PaCRAM can safely use reduced charge restoration latency for
N_{PCR}{}
refresh windows. After periodically refreshing each DRAM row using reduced charge restoration latency for
N_{PCR}{}
times, PaCRAM uses nominal charge restoration latency for the next refresh window to fully restore each cell’s charge. To do so, PaCRAM implements a single counter that counts the number of refresh windows using reduced charge restoration latency and increments the counter every
t_{REFW}{}
. When the counter reaches
N_{PCR}{}
, PaCRAM uses nominal charge restoration latency for that refresh window and resets the counter to zero.
