Whole-genome analysis of Pantoea alfalfae HF1 isolated from shot hole lesions in peach leaves and symptom reproduction by wound inoculation
Authors: Yi-Run Wu, Xue Xu, Yan Hu, Jin-Wei Zhou, Xiao-Juan Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial leaf shot-hole disease has become an increasingly serious threat to peach production and related industries. Here, we isolated a bacterial strain, designated HF1, from symptomatic peach leaves infected with shot holes in Hefei City, Anhui Province, China. 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that the isolate belongs to the genus Pantoea. Furthermore, we used Illumina and MinION Nanopore sequencing technologies to analyze the whole-genome sequence of this strain. The complete genome comprises four distinct genomic elements: one circular chromosome measuring 4,008,864 bp (G + C content of 55.37%) and three circular plasmids measuring 527,106, 161,006, and 104,931 bp long, with G + C contents of 53.13%, 52.34%, and 53.92%, respectively. Multilocus sequence analysis of five housekeeping genes revealed that strain HF1 belongs to a clade containing Pantoea alfalfae CQ10, which was isolated from alfalfa. Average nucleotide identity and DNA-DNA hybridization analyses supported the phylogenetic proximity of HF1 to P. alfalfae CQ10. Based on these results, strain HF1 was reclassified as P. alfalfae HF1. In addition, HF1 was compared with three genetically related representative strains: P. alfalfae CQ10, P. ananatis DZ-12, and P. vagans C9-1. A comparative analysis was conducted using a bacterial pan-genome pipeline, and virulence factors were identified using a virulence factor database. The findings revealed that HF1 contains a number of genes associated with potential virulence traits involved in bacterium-host interactions, including motility, biofilm formation, the type VI secretion system (T6SS), and nutrient acquisition. Compared with its closest pathogenic relative, P. alfalfae CQ10, HF1 possesses three distinct T6SS clusters, including several copies of genes encoding predicted effector proteins such as Hcp, VgrG, and PAAR. Interestingly, incomplete AHL-mediated quorum-sensing system was identified in both P. alfalfae strains, and HF1 was unable to synthesize AHL; in vitro experiments corroborated these findings. Under wound inoculation conditions, strain HF1 reproduced symptoms resembling shot hole disease in peach leaves. Collectively, these findings indicate that strain HF1 is a Pantoea species associated with leaf shot hole symptoms in peach trees. This strain harbors diverse genetic features potentially related to virulence, including motility, biofilm formation, and the T6SS, which may contribute to its ability to induce symptoms under the tested conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-026-02067-9.Keywords: Pantoea alfalfae HF1, Bacterial leaf shot hole disease of peach, Virulence factors, T6SS

Introduction

Peach (Prunus persica L.) is a globally significant stone fruit crop of high economic importance and is widely cultivated in China and other countries. However, in the past few years, several bacterial diseases, including bacterial leaf spot (also called bacterial leaf shot hole), have emerged as a growing threat to peach production and related industries. This may be due to changes in climate and planting patterns. Previous studies have revealed that Xanthomonas alboricola and X. campestris are the causal pathogens of bacterial leaf spot (Robe et al. 2018; Ryan et al. 2011); they primarily infect plant hosts via chemotaxis, motility, carbohydrate metabolism, exopolysaccharide production, biofilm formation, extracellular enzyme synthesis, effector proteins, and type II and III secretion systems (T2SS and T3SS) (Garita-Cambronero et al. 2018).

Novel bacterial species associated with peach leaf shot hole symptoms, including P. agglomerans and P. ananatis, have been reported (Zang et al. 2021). The genus Pantoea, first described by Gavini et al. in 1989, comprises a highly diverse group of Gram-negative bacteria that inhabit a broad range of environments worldwide, such as contaminated materials, soil, water, plants, seeds, and fruits (Doni et al. 2021; Weller-Stuart et al. 2017). The genus comprises 20 recognized species, among which P. agglomerans, P. ananatis, P. stewartia, and P. alfalfae have been reported to be associated with diseases in many economically important plants, including maize, rice, onion, and alfalfa (Doni et al. 2021; Morohoshi et al. 2007; Shin et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2023). In fact, several Pantoea species, including P. vagans C9-1, P. agglomersans CPHN2, and P. agglomerans UADEC20, are used as biocontrol agents and growth promoters to modify the plant environment (Elizondo-Reyna et al. 2025; Kumar et al. 2022; Smits et al. 2010). Therefore, the ability of Pantoea strains to express both beneficial and pathogenic traits underscores the complex role of microbes in the structure and dynamics of plant communities. Importantly, comparative genomic analyses have revealed distinct infection pathways between the Xanthomonas and Pantoea genera; however, their symptoms on peach trees are relatively similar (Doni et al. 2019; Garita-Cambronero et al. 2016). Previous studies have shown that the virulence of Pantoea species in plant hosts is primarily attributed to motility, biofilm formation, effector proteins, and secretion systems (Shyntum et al. 2015; Weller-Stuart et al. 2017; Yao et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2024).

In P. ananatis, T6SSs play key roles in onion leaf pathogenesis: T6SS-1 directly modulates pathogenicity, while T6SS-2 and T6SS-3 are essential for modulating T6SS-1 and indirectly enhancing host colonization (Shyntum et al. 2015). P. stewartii is associated with Stewart’s vascular wilt and leaf blight in sweet corn and maize, and its pathogenicity depends primarily on T3SS (Koutsoudis et al. 2006). Notably, WtsE, an effector protein secreted via the T3SS, plays a central role in inducing host cell death (Ham et al. 2008). The Hrp gene cluster, which encodes the T3SS and associated effectors, enables the bacterium to colonize and proliferate within the xylem of maize, ultimately leading to the onset of disease (Roper 2011). In addition, the HiVir gene cluster serves as a key determinant in P. agglomerans during onion pathogenesis (Shin et al. 2025). While this cluster is essential for inducing symptoms, it is genetically linked to the copper resistance gene cluster (copABCDRS), which promotes bacterial survival in the presence of copper-based antiseptics (Rademacher and Masepohl 2012). Together, these functionally complementary clusters may contribute to both virulence-related traits and environmental adaptability, potentially supporting the persistence of P. agglomerans in agricultural niches.

In July 2023, we observed shot hole symptoms in peach leaves in Hefei City, Anhui Province, China. Bacterial strains were isolated from symptomatic leaves. Pathogenicity testing on ex vivo peach fruits and leaves, together with 16S rRNA gene sequencing, indicated that the Pantoea sp. is associated with the observed symptoms. Although Pantoea species have been reported as being associated with peach tree disease in China (Zang et al. 2021), the detailed mechanisms underlying these association remain largely unknown. Therefore, in this study, we characterized a Pantoea strain associated with peach leaf shot hole symptoms through pathogenicity testing under wound inoculation conditions, along with phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses.

Materials and methods

Isolation and characterization of bacteria associated with shot hole symptoms in peach leaves

In July 2023, bacteria were isolated from peach leaves with bacterial shot hole symptoms collected in Hefei City, Anhui Province, China. Isolation was performed as previously described, with minor modifications (Tan et al. 2022). Briefly, peach leaves with shot hole symptoms were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. Afterward, their surfaces were sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1 min. Thereafter, the tissue was cut into small pieces and immersed in 10 mL of sterile water. Serial dilutions of the mixture were performed with sterile water and then spread onto nutrient agar (NA) plates followed by incubation at 30 °C for 48 h. Then, single colonies were isolated and purified by three successive subcultures on NA plates. All isolated bacterial strains were stored in glycerol at −80 °C for long-term preservation at Anhui Normal University, China.

Bacteria associated with shot hole symptoms in peach leaves have also been reported to be associated with fruit rot (Robe et al. 2018). Therefore, peach fruits were rinsed with sterile distilled water, after which the surface was sterilized with 75% ethanol and air-dried in a safety hood for 30 min. A sterile puncher was used to make wells (5 mm in diameter) on the fruit surface. Afterward, 25 μL of overnight culture was transferred to each well. The peach fruit was subsequently placed in a sterile drain basket and incubated at 30 °C in a growth chamber. Lesions were observed daily for 48 h. Meanwhile, 25 μL of NB was added to the wells as a control.

The peach fruit rot-associated strains were subsequently evaluated for their ability to induce symptoms in peach leaves. Briefly, fresh, healthy peach leaves were first rinsed with sterile water, followed by surface sterilization using 75% ethanol. Finally, they were dried for 15 min in a biosafety hood. Next, sterile needles were used to create wounds on the tissue, after which 10 μL of each overnight culture was inoculated at the center. The leaves were placed on sterilized and wet filter paper within a sterile double-layer drain basket and incubated in a growth chamber at 30 °C and 75% relative humidity. After 14 days of incubation, symptoms were observed. The same volume of sterile water was inoculated on the leaves as a negative control. The inoculated bacterium was re-isolated from leaves displaying shot hole-like symptoms.

Finally, strains that induced peach fruit rot-like symptoms and leaf shot hole lesions under the tested conditions were identified by 16S rRNA sequencing using the 27F/1492R primer pair.

Sequencing, assembly, and annotation of the whole genome

The genomic DNA was extracted with the TIANamp Bacterial DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), and DNA libraries were prepared for both Illumina and MinION Nanopore sequencing. Sequencing was performed (Illumina, USA) at Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd., China, with NovaSeq 6000 and PromethION sequencers.

Short reads were assembled into scaffolds using SOAPdenovo2 software (Luo et al. 2012), while long reads were processed with Unicycler (version 0.5.0) software to obtain complete chromosome and plasmid sequences (Wick et al. 2017). PLASMe software was used to identify plasmids from the genomic assembly (Tang et al. 2023), and Prokka (version 1.14.6) software was used for gene prediction (Seemann 2014). tRNAscan-SE (version 2.0) was used to predict tRNAs in the genome (Lowe and Chan 2016), whereas Barrnap0.9 software was used to predict genomic rRNA sequences. Genes were functionally annotated via a local BLAST approach. The predicted gene sequences were aligned against the NR, Swiss-Prot, Pfam, EggNOG, GO, and KEGG databases to obtain the corresponding functional annotations.

Phylogenetic analysis of Pantoea alfalfae HF1

Twenty-four strains with complete genome sequences were selected, and the sequences of five housekeeping genes-aptD, fusA, gyrB, leuS, and pyrG-were extracted for multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA). A phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021) on the basis of concatenated sequences of these five genes, with 1000 bootstrapped replications. For higher-resolution phylogenetic analysis, we further constructed a phylogenetic tree based on the core genome. The detailed methodology is described as follows: OrthoFinder (v2.5.4) (Emms and Kelly 2019) was employed to perform orthogroup analysis on the predicted protein sequences of 24 strains, followed by the identification and extraction of single-copy core genes. A rooted species tree was integrated and directly generated via its built-in STAG algorithm (Lai et al. 2017). The visualization of the tree was implemented using MEGA11, with the branch support values evaluated by gene tree concordance. To calculate the overall genomic similarity between the HF1 genome and 23 other Pantoea strains, pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) comparisons were performed using the Jspecies Web server (Richter et al. 2016). Furthermore, the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) was used to calculate in silico DNA-DNA hybridization (isDDH) values (Meier-Kolthoff et al. 2013). The cutoff values for ANI and isDDH were 96% and 70%, respectively (Goris et al. 2007).

Comparative genomics of P. alfalfae HF1 and other Pantoea strains

The complete genome of strain HF1 was compared with those of three representative Pantoea strains: P. alfalfae CQ10 (Yao et al. 2023), P. ananatis DZ-12 (Zhao et al. 2024), and P. vagans C9-1 (Klein-Gordon et al. 2023). Their genome sequences and basic genomic characteristics were obtained from the NCBI GenBank database. To identify similar and unique gene clusters, the Pangenome Analysis Pipeline (PGAP) was used to perform pangenome analysis, and the Gene Family (GF) method with default parameters was used to identify orthologous gene clusters (Zhao et al. 2012). Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) encoding putative secondary metabolites were identified in all four genomes using antiSMASH 4.0.2 (Blin et al. 2017) under standard settings. All alignment parameters were set to the default values recommended by the official website. Previous studies have reported genetic features associated with virulence-related traits, including motility, extracellular polysaccharides, flagella, chemotaxis, biofilm formation, and T6SSs, in Pantoea strains (Weller-Stuart et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2024). In contrast, dapdiamide E, siderophores, cellulose, phosphate dissolution, and indole acetic acid have been reported to be associated with biocontrol and plant growth promotion in other Pantoea species (Kumar et al. 2022; Shariati et al. 2017; Smits et al. 2010; Sulja et al. 2022). In this study, the genetic features potentially related to virulence in strain HF1 were compared with those of three representative Pantoea strains to identify similarities, differences, and absences.

Flagella-associated motility assays

The flagella-associated motility assays were performed according to previous methods (Boluk et al. 2021). A 1 µL aliquot of each overnight culture was spot-inoculated at the center of a 60 mm diameter plate containing the corresponding medium, followed by incubation at 30 °C. After 24 h, the diameters of the motility zones were detected. Dickeya zeae WH1, which exhibits strong motility (Tan et al. 2025), was used as a positive control under the same conditions. All experiments were repeated independently at least three times, with ten plates per experiment.

Biofilm formation assays

In vitro biofilm formation was assessed as previously described with slight modifications (Jiao et al. 2021; Qin et al. 2014). After static incubation in test tubes at 30 °C for 18 h, the culture medium was aspirated, and the biofilms were gently rinsed three times with sterile PBS. The biofilms were subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. Finally, the unbound dye was removed by washing the biofilms three times with 1 mL of water, after which photographs were taken.

In addition, the formation of biofilms was further verified via in vitro fluorescence staining of the biofilms in this study. Thereafter, the biofilms were grown on glass coverslips in 48-well microtiter plates at 30 °C for 18 h under static conditions. Afterward, the coverslips were gently rinsed three times with PBS, and the adherent biofilms were stained with an AO/EB staining kit (Sango Biotech, Shanghai, China), followed by visualization using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Acylated homoserine lactone (AHL) production assay

Acylated Homoserine Lactone (AHL) production was detected using the biosensor strain Chromobacterium violaceum CV026, which is capable of sensing C4 to C8 AHLs secreted by gram-negative bacteria (Pu et al. 2022; Sheng et al. 2015). The detailed protocol for the AHL production assay was performed strictly according to our previously published study (Tan et al. 2025). Briefly, 25 µL of the overnight culture of the tested strain was added to the wells of the biosensor plates. Moreover, 25 µL of sterile LB medium served as the negative control, and 25 µL of an overnight culture of D. zeae WH1 (a strain producing abundant AHL signals on the biosensor plates) was used as the positive control (Tan et al. 2022). After incubation at 28 °C for 48 h, the violacein halos were observed. All the assays were performed with at least three biological replicates, and ten plates were prepared for each replicate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times unless otherwise stated. Moreover, all phenotypic assays in this study were qualitative, and no quantitative data analysis or statistical evaluation was performed for these tests. The results are summarized in the figures.

Results

Association of Pantoea sp. strain HF1 with shot hole symptoms in peach leaves

Fourteen bacterial strains were isolated and purified from peach leaves with shot hole symptoms in Hefei City, Anhui Province, China. To assess whether these isolates were associated with the observed symptoms, inoculated peach fruits were screened; one isolate (No. 6) was observed to induce fruit rot-like symptoms (Fig. S1). Next, this isolate was inoculated on peach leaves wounded with sterile needles. After 7 days of incubation, water-soaked spots appeared in the inoculated leaves, and after 14 days, dry rot and hole-like lesions were observed (Fig. 1). 16S rRNA gene sequence-based phylogenetic analysis revealed that strain No. 6 belongs to the genus Pantoea (Fig. S2). The strain was re-isolated from the inoculated peach leaves. Therefore, we designed this strain as Pantoea sp. strain HF1. Collectively, under wound inoculation conditions, these findings indicate that Pantoea sp. strain HF1 is associated with shot hole symptoms in peach leaves in Anhui Province, China.

Taxonomic identification of P. alfalfae HF1

Strain HF1 is phylogenetically distinct from the other strains, as revealed by a tree constructed using core genome analysis and a concatenated alignment of five housekeeping genes. Among the Pantoea species examined, HF1 and P. alfalfae CQ10 isolated from alfalfa were grouped into the same branch and separated from the other Pantoea strains (Fig. 2). Furthermore, ANI analysis revealed that the ANI value between HF1 and CQ10 was 99.5%, which was higher than the cutoff value (96%). In contrast, the ANI values between HF1 and other previously identified Pantoea strains ranged from 84 to 95%, which were lower than the cutoff value. Moreover, the isDDH value between HF1 and CQ10 was 95.7%, which was also higher than the cutoff (70%) (Table S1). Collectively, these findings suggest that strain HF1 belongs to the species P. alfalfae. Therefore, we renamed it P. alfalfae HF1.

Genomic comparison of strain HF1 with other Pantoea strains

The depths of the assemblies were 268.55 and 267.87 for the Illumina and Nanopore platforms, respectively. The complete genome of strain HF1 consisted of a single circular chromosome spanning 4,008,864 bp with a 55.37% GC content and three plasmids measuring 527,106, 161,006, and 104,931 bp long, with GC contents of 53.13%, 52.34%, and 53.92%, respectively. Table 1 summarizes the general features of the genome, and detailed circular maps are shown in Fig. 3. The genome of strain HF1 was annotated using the PROKKA pipeline. In total, 76 tRNA and 22 rRNA genes were identified, with protein-coding regions accounting for 85.77% of the total. A total of 4,358 coding DNA sequences (CDSs) were identified, with an average length of 945.09 bp. Among these, 3,683 CDSs were assigned to functional categories within 26 clusters of orthologous groups (Table 1). Furthermore, 3,489 CDSs were annotated within the KEGG database, whereas 345 open reading frames, accounting for 7.92% of the total, were annotated as hypothetical proteins.

Pan-genome analysis was performed to compare the genomes of HF1 and three closely related strains. The core genome comprised 3,159 clusters, while the numbers of unique clusters in HF1, CQ10, DZ-12, and C9-1 were 281, 213, 1,595, and 525, respectively (Fig. 4). The unique clusters in HF1 harbored several key encoding genes, including those for conjugal transfer pilus assembly proteins, type VI secretion system proteins, and bacteriophages (Table S2). The unique clusters in CQ10 included genes encoding DNA phosphorothioation-dependent restriction proteins, DNA sulfur modification proteins, formate hydrogenlyase subunits, and type I fimbrial proteins (Table S3). The unique clusters in strain DZ-12 primarily included genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, iron complex transport systems, regulators, chemotaxis proteins, and type VI secretion system proteins (Table S4). However, the unique clusters in strain C9-1 included genes associated with dapdiamide synthase, iron-siderophore transport system proteins, and tellurite resistance proteins (Table S5). Therefore, pan-genome analyses revealed potential differences in unique clusters among Pantoea strains.

In total, 321 genes encoding potential virulence-related features were found across the three Pantoea strains (HF1, CQ10, and DZ-12). Analysis of genetic features potentially related to virulence in strain HF1

Analysis of the genome of strain HF1 for genes potentially involved in bacterium-host interactions revealed 1,124 functionally annotated CDSs. We noted that 13.4% of these genes have been reported in other bacteria to be associated with virulence-related or effector-like functions. Strain HF1 harbors genes encoding potential virulence-related features that have been previously associated with phytopathogenic bacteria, including those linked diseases such as bacterial leaf blight, bacterial wilt, black rot, and fire blight.

As illustrated in Table S6, 138 carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were identified in strain HF1 genome. Among them, glycoside hydrolases (GHs) constituted the most prominent family, accounting for 39.9% of all annotated CAZymes. Glycosyl transferases (GTs) were the second largest family (32.6%), followed by carbohydrate esterases (16.7%), auxiliary activities (9.4%), and carbohydrate-binding modules (1.5%). In particular, GH13 (14.5% of all GHs), GH109 (12.7%), GH1 (9.0%), and GH23 (9.0%) were the most predominant families among GHs. Among the GTs, GT2 (22.2%), GT4 (13.3%), GT9 (13.3%), and GT41 (11.1%) were the most predominant (Table S7). Notably, we identified a set of genes associated with GHs on the plasmid of strain HF1.

The antiSMASH database was used to assess the genome of strain HF1. Two and five.

Biosynthesis Gene Clusters (BGCs) were identified on the plasmids and chromosome, respectively (Table S8). Among the identified genes, those involved in arylpolyene, turnerbactin, and carotenoid biosynthesis were highly similar to the gene arrangement of the corresponding BGCs in the minimum information on a biosynthesis-related gene cluster (MIBiG) database. Notably, genes involved in hserlactone and redox cofactor synthesis in strain HF1 did not have similar gene arrangements in the MIBiG database. Comparative genomic analysis revealed that the arylpolyene biosynthesis cluster, encoded by 52 genes, was present only in strain HF1 genome (Table S9). One study revealed that arylpolyenes function as fitness factors that increase the contribution to biofilm formation and protection against oxidative stress in Escherichia coli (Johnston et al. 2021). Therefore, the environmental adaptability of P. alfalfae HF1 may be enhanced by the synthesis of acrylpolyene. In addition to the aforementioned adaptation mechanisms, iron acquisition capacity is critical for the environmental adaptation and pathogenicity of phytopathogens. A siderophore-associated gene cluster (Cluster_7) encoding a non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) was identified in the HF1 strain using the antiSMASH tool (Table S10). Sequence alignment against the VFDB database revealed that the cluster shares high similarity (> 80%) with the gene encoding the enterobactin receptor protein FepA. Whole-genome analysis revealed that cluster_7 contains a complete set of enterobactin biosynthesis genes (entA-F), indicating that the full functionality of both the synthesis and uptake of enterobactin is available for strain HF1. Thus, this system is likely to increase the efficiency of iron acquisition in strain HF1. Previous studies have indicated that enterobactin is a crucial virulence factor for Enterobacter species during infection of rice plants (Franza et al. 2005). The complete enterobactin synthesis and uptake system identified in this study may be crucial for strain HF1, facilitating host colonization, enabling competition for iron, and promoting the establishment of a growth advantage.

Secretion systems

Generally, bacteria employ an array of secretion systems, designated type I through VI (T1SS-T6SS), to direct their virulence factors to specific locations, such as the bacterial cell envelope, the extracellular environment, or the interior of host cells. However, T3SS-,T4SS- and T5SS-related genes were absent from all four analyzed Pantoea genomes. A single T2SS-related gene was identified in strain HF1. In contrast, three T6SS gene clusters were encoded by the chromosome of strain HF1 (Fig. 6). Notably, these genes encode certain effectors, including Hcp, VgrG, and PAAR, are present in strain HF1. Previous study demonstrated that T6SSs play essential roles in the pathogenicity and bacterial competition of Pantoea (Zhao et al. 2024). However, only one complete T6SS gene cluster (T6SS-1) was identified in P. alfalfae CQ10 (Yao et al. 2023). Through systematic analysis of the T6SSs in P. alfalfae HF1, two complete T6SS gene clusters (T6SS-1 and T6SS-3) were identified in its genome. No significant hits were identified for either cluster by MultiGeneBlast. These clusters contain all core structural genes, such as tssA-tssH and tssJ-tssM, as well as hcp, vgrG, and clpV, with some structural genes present in multiple copies. However, a complete T6SS-2 gene cluster could not be detected, and many essential structural genes were lacking. Structural information for the complete gene clusters is provided in Table S11.

Schematic representation of the type VI secretion system gene cluster on the chromosome of Pantoea alfalfae HF1

Within these two intact T6SS systems, key structural proteins, including Hcp, VgrG, and PAAR, exhibit homology within the same system but lack obvious homology between different systems. The results of amino acid sequence homology alignment (BLASTp) and phylogenetic tree construction for the structural proteins of the two systems show that Hcp-1 in T6SS-1, a conserved tube protein, has an extremely short phylogenetic branch length, indicating that this protein is highly conserved during evolution. In contrast, Hcp-3 in T6SS-3, in addition to functioning as a tube protein, can also act as an effector protein secreted into host cells, thus resulting in relatively high sequence variability. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Hcp-3 of HF1 and that of strain CQ-10 are distributed in different clades with long genetic distances, representing distinct subtypes of the T6SS secretion system (Fig. 7).

Phylogenetic analysis of key structural proteins in T6SS-1 and T6SS-3

Multiple copies of the vgrG gene exist in the HF1 genome; copies within the same T6SS system are homologous, whereas significant differences are observed between copies from different systems. VgrG-1 of HF1 clusters closely with that of P. agglomerans ROTS050421, whereas VgrG-1 of the conspecific strain CQ-10 clusters with that of P. vagans GM2 (Fig. 7). This suggests that VgrG, as the tip protein of the T6SS, may have undergone rapid evolution under natural selection pressure. Although VgrG-3 of HF1 clusters with homology from P. agglomerans strains CFBP13600, BIMB 2003, and DBM 3797, an obvious evolutionary distance still exists, resulting in the formation of an independent clade in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 7). Separate sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that PAAR-1 shares 91.86% identity with the polymorphic toxin type 46 domain-containing protein encoded in the genome of P. agglomerans FC61912 B, which implies that PAAR-1 may act as a T6SS effector protein and participate in host pathogenesis. In contrast, the sequence variability of PAAR-3 was high, and homologous proteins were detected in 11 out of the 22 species examined (Fig. 7).

In summary, strain HF1 maintains two complete T6SS systems with distinct evolutionary origins, reflecting high genomic plasticity. The high degree of structural gene conservation in T6SS-1 may correspond to a basic, universal secretion mechanism. In contrast, the diversity and independence of structural genes in T6SS-3, particularly those encoding Hcp-3 and VgrG-3, suggesting that this system may be adapted to more specific ecological interactions or host environments, performing biological functions distinct from those of T6SS-1.

Quorum sensing system

Many gram-negative bacteria employ the LuxI/LuxR-type quorum sensing system to regulate virulence and biofilm formation. A previous study revealed that P. agglomerans produces N-3-oxo-hexanoyl-homoserine lactone via the LuxI homolog PagI (Morohoshi et al. 2007). The homologous pagI and pagR were present in other Pantoea pathogens, including P. ananatis DZ-12. BLAST searches against the genomes of strains HF1 and CQ10 revealed no homologs of canonical pagI. However, both strains harbored other LuxI-type AHL synthase homologs: an rhlI homolog was detected in HF1, and a putative uncharacterized AHL synthase-encoding gene was found in CQ10 (Table S12). Strain HF1 harbors only an rhlI homolog without its cognate paired receptor gene rhlR, suggesting that it cannot form a complete rhlI/rhlR-type quorum sensing autoinduction circuit. Consistent with this genomic observation, strain HF1 did not produce detectable AHL signals under the tested conditions, as determined using the C. violaceum CV026 biosensor (data shown below).

Phenotype assays

The genome of strain HF1 harbors genes associated with flagella biosynthesis and biofilm formation, which have been reported to play roles in bacterial virulence-related traits in other species. The associated phenotypes were compared with those of D. zeae WH1, a strain associated with foot rot in rice. Under the tested conditions, strain HF1 exhibited moderate swimming motility when incubated at 30 °C for 24 h; however, it did not exhibit swarming motility when incubated for 36 h (Fig. 8a). Furthermore, strain HF1 produced visible biofilms in tube assays, and this observation was supported by fluorescence images (Fig. 8b). Using the C. violaceum CV026 biosensor, strain HF1 did not produce detectable AHL signals under the tested conditions (Fig. 8c).

Discussion

Bacterial shot hole has become a serious disease in peach trees and poses a severe threat to the peach industry. Various perspectives exist on the microorganisms associated with shot hole disease, with some studies identifying Xanthomonas arboricola pv. pruni as causal agents, whereas others have reported association with P. ananatis and P. agglomerans (Sharma et al. 2025; Zang et al. 2021). In this study, we found that strain P. alfalfae HF1, isolated from peach leaves with shot hole symptoms in Anhui Province, China, induced shot hole-like lesions in peach leaves in ex vivo wound inoculation assays. Previous studies revealed that P. ananatis was associated with peach bacterial leaf shot hole symptoms in Henan Province, China; however, they did not identify virulence factors (Zang et al. 2021). Furthermore, Yao et al. reported that P. alfalfae CQ10 is associated with bacterial leaf blight symptoms in alfalfa and predicted genetic features potentially related to virulence, including motility, biofilm, T6SS, and nutrient acquisition via comparative genomics (Yao et al. 2023). Under the same ex vivo wound inoculation conditions, our findings indicate that P. alfalfae HF1 is associated with bacterial leaf shot hole symptoms in peach. To our knowledge, no record exists of P. alfalfae being associated with peach bacterial shot hole symptoms. Therefore, our study may expand the known pathogenic spectrum of bacteria associated with this disease and contribute to the relevant literature on peach pathogen taxonomy. Additional genomic analysis revealed that strain HF1 harbors diverse genetic determinants potentially related with virulence. Although it shares many of these genetic features with its closest phylogenetic relative, P. alfalfae CQ10, we observed notable differences; for instance, strain HF1 harbors three distinct T6SS gene clusters in its genome.

Motility plays an essential role in the virulence of pathogenic bacteria, particularly in the early phase of infection (Tan et al. 2025). We observed that strain HF1 possesses several genes involved in flagella synthesis, similar to those of P. alfalfae CQ10 and P. ananatis DZ-12. In vitro motility assays showed that compared with D. zeae WH1, which is a pathogen that causes foot rot in rice (Tan et al. 2022, 2025), strain HF1 exhibited moderate swimming motility under the tested conditions. In swarming motility assays, strain HF1 did not show detectable swarming until 36 h of incubation. This observation indicates that under the current in vitro conditions, swarming motility of HF1 is delayed relative to its swimming motility.

Biofilm formation has been reported to play an important role in the colonization of host tissues by various bacteria. Whole-genome analysis revealed that strain HF1 harbors a complete chromosome-encoded β-glucan synthesis gene cluster (including homologous genes such as mdoH, mdoC, and mdoG). This cluster is closely associated with bacterial extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) synthesis, serving as the foundation for biofilm formation (Koutsoudis et al. 2006). Consistent with the presence of these genes, in vitro phenotypic assays showed that strain HF1 exhibits a robust biofilm-forming ability under the tested conditions. Unlike P. stewartii, which relies on other types of EPS for host colonization (Koutsoudis et al. 2006), HF1 possesses a β-glucan synthesis gene cluster. Whether HF1 primarily utilizes β-glucan for host surface colonization, and whether this represents a distinct biofilm-associated strategy within the genus Pantoea, remain to be investigated.

AHL-mediated quorum sensing has been reported to play an important role in regulating virulence-related traits in many gram-negative bacteria. Previous studies have revealed that esaI/esaR, which encodes AHL synthase and its receptor protein, is present in P. stewartii subsp. stewartii and governs biofilm formation and host colonization (Koutsoudis et al. 2006). In contrast, in-depth genomic analysis of strain HF1 revealed that it lacks a complete, canonical quorum sensing (QS) system for the autonomous regulation of group behaviors. In stead, HF1 harbors an adjacent gene module consisting of rhlI (encoding a putative AHL synthase) and sdiA (encoding a LuxR-family sensor protein). Although rhlI gene is present, its predicted AHL synthase activity remains to be experimentally verified. Moreover, HF1 lacks the corresponding receptor protein RhlR; therefore, it cannot form a complete, functionally autonomous regulatory loop. In some gram-negative bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., homologous SdiA proteins are known to “eavesdrop” on QS signals produced by other bacteria (Schwieters and Ahmer 2025). Given that HF1 possesses SdiA but lacks a complete AHL synthase-receptor pair, it is possible that strain HF1 may similarly respond to exogenous AHL signals rather than producing its own. Consistent with this genomic prediction, our in vitro assays detected no measured AHL production by HF1. Whether the absence of an autonomous AHL-mediated system affects motility, biofilm formation, or other traits in P. alfalfae remains to be determined. Future studies will investigate whether exogenous AHL signals can induce virulence-related gene expression or phenotypic changes in strain HF1.

Secretion systems, notably the T2SS, T3SS, and T6SS, are key mediators of bacterial virulence. As documented in prior studies, the T2SS characterized in Dickeya and Pectobacterium genera mediates the secretion of critical virulence factors, including a suite of extracellular enzymes responsible for degrading plant cell wall components, thereby facilitating host tissue colonization and pathogenicity (Tan et al. 2022). In contrast, the T3SS, which is found in many gram-negative bacteria, can deliver effector proteins into host cells to induce hypersensitivity responses in tobacco (Shariati et al. 2017). Notably, in contrast to these well-documented pathogenic bacteria, all Pantoea strains analyzed in the present study lacked the core structural genes encoding the T2SS and T3SS, suggesting that these secretion systems may not be present among the strains examined. In particular, all analyzed Pantoea strains harbored one to three distinct T6SS structural clusters in their genomes, indicating that the T6SS is commonly found in this taxon. T6SS has been reported to function as an injectome for effector proteins, such as hemolysin coregulated protein (Hcp), valine-glycine repeat protein (VgrG), and proline-alanine-alanine-arginine repeats (PAAR) (Carobbi et al. 2022; De Maayer et al. 2011). Additionally, accumulating evidences have established that the T6SS is functionally pleiotropic and governs a spectrum of biological processes in various gram-negative bacteria, including bacterial virulence, host-symbiont interactions, biofilm formation, environmental stress adaptation, and interbacterial competitive fitness (Allsopp and Bernal 2023; Mijatović Scouten et al. 2025; Zang et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2024). Comparative genomic analyses of three Pantoea strains examined in this study revealed the presence of three distinct T6SS clusters in the chromosome of P. alfalfae HF1, T6SS-1 and T6SS-3 in the chromosome of P. ananatis DZ-12, and T6SS-2 in the plasmid of P. ananatis DZ-12 (Zhao et al. 2024); whereas only one cluster was found in the P. alfalfae CQ10 genome (Yao et al. 2023). Prior functional studies in other Pantoea strains have suggested that T6SS-1 may represent a core functional cluster contributing to T6SS activity, and has been associated with virulence and interspecies competitive fitness (Shyntum et al. 2015). In contrast, T6SS-2 has been reported to display strict host adaptation and has been detected in Pantoea strains recovered from diseased plant tissues, suggesting a potential role in plant interactions (Weller-Stuart et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2024). However, the distribution of T6SS-3 within the Pantoea genus appears to be limited. Consistent with previous reports, multiple copies of genes encoding predicted T6SS structural and effector proteins (Hcp, VgrG, and PAAR) are distributed across distinct T6SS clusters in P. alfalfae HF1 and P. ananatis DZ-12, whereas only a single copy of each gene is present in P. alfalfae CQ10 and P. vagans C9-1. Whether the different Hcp, VgrG, and PAAR proteins encoded by these genes play distinct roles as effectors, structural elements, or both remains to be determined.

In summary, under wound inoculation conditions, P. alfalfae HF1 is associated with shot hole symptoms in peach leaves. The strain possesses multiple genetic features potentially related to virulence, including motility, biofilms, the T6SS, and nutrient acquisition. These mechanisms have been previously implicated in plant-associated interactions in Pantoea species. Phenotype analyses indicate that strain HF1 exhibits moderate motility and enhanced biofilm-forming ability under the tested conditions. Furthermore, it possesses a partial AHL-mediated quorum sensing regulatory system due to the absence of a cognate synthase-receptor pair, and consequently fails to produce detectable AHL signals in vitro. Comparative genomic analyses show that strain HF1 carries three distinct T6SS clusters, which differ from those of the phylogenetically related strain P. alfalfae CQ10 isolated from alfalfa. Given these findings, further studies are warranted to investigate whether and how these genetic features contribute to symptom development under natural infection conditions, to assess the strain’s effects on other host plants, and to explore potential control strategies for peach bacterial shot hole disease. We also acknowledge clear limitations of the present study. First, our genomics comparison was restricted to a limited number of Pantoea strains with available high-quality genomic resources, which constrains the generalizability of our evolutionary inferences to broader Pantoea populations. Second, and more importantly, our pathogenic-related conclusions are based exclusively on artificial wound inoculation assays, which do not replicate natural infection routes. Therefore, while strain HF1 consistently reproduced shot hole-like symptoms under the tested laboratory conditions, causality under field conditions remains to be established. Future work will require expanded sampling of geographically diverse isolates, additional comparative genomic datasets, and, critically, natural infection or field-based experiments to further validate and extend the current findings.
