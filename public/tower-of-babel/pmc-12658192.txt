Dietary Azolla meal enhances growth, immune status, and liver health in thin-lip grey mullet (Liza ramada) fingerlings: a sustainable approach to Aquafeed optimization
Authors: Mohamed M Toutou, Amr Elkelish, Komla Mawunyo Dossouvi, Yasmin M Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed the nutritional, immunological, and hepatic impacts of dietary Azolla meal (Azolla pinnata) as a sustainable alternative to conventional plant-based feed ingredients in Liza ramada (thin-lip grey mullet) aquaculture. Fingerlings were fed isonitrogenous and isocaloric diets where Azolla meal progressively replaced soybean meal and yellow corn at inclusion levels of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% over a 10-week period. Results revealed that the 10% inclusion level optimally enhanced growth performance (final body weight, weight gain), feed conversion ratio (1.78), and protein efficiency ratio. Hematological analysis revealed significant improvements in red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, and hematocrit levels, indicating enhanced systemic oxygen transport and improved metabolic status. Additionally, fish in the 10% Azolla group exhibited superior liver function, characterized by reduced ALT, AST, and urea levels, as well as enhanced albumin and globulin profiles. Immune markers, including lysozyme and IgM were markedly elevated, alongside improved antioxidant enzyme activities. Digestive enzyme analysis revealed improved protease and amylase activity, particularly at the 10% level. However, a 20% inclusion induced signs of metabolic stress, suggesting a threshold beyond which Azolla may be detrimental. This study conclusively demonstrates that a 10% dietary inclusion of Azolla meal is optimal for enhancing growth, immunity, and hepatic health in thin-lip grey mullet, providing a viable strategy for sustainable Aquafeed formulation. Introduction

Aquaculture plays a crucial role in providing nutrition, generating income, facilitating trade, and creating employment opportunities, especially in developing nations. Enhancing productivity requires the development of affordable, nutritionally optimized feeds, as this account for over 50% of operational expenditures in intensive aquaculture1. Protein, vital for growth, metabolic functions, and health1–3, represents the most costly dietary element, driving unsustainable fishmeal demand and rising costs. While plant protein sources (35–46% protein) are commonly used alternatives in Egypt, their escalating prices, fueled by competition from livestock, poultry, and industrial sectors4, highlight the urgent need to identify cheaper, sustainable alternatives to reduce costs and improve farmer profitability5.

The thin-lip mullet (Liza ramada) is a highly valued species in Mediterranean aquaculture due to its rapid growth and broad salinity tolerance. It inhabits coastal waters, estuaries, and lagoons, feeding on detritus and algae, which contributes to ecosystem balance. This species is ecologically important as prey for larger predators and holds significant economic value for both its flesh and roe in commercial and recreational fisheries6–10.

Azolla spp., a widespread freshwater fern in Egyptian aquatic ecosystems11, presents a promising solution. Azolla meal offers up to 30% crude protein, along with beneficial vitamins, minerals, biopolymers, and probiotics1,5,12–16, although its anti-nutritional factors require mitigation strategies17. Studies indicate successful partial substitution of fishmeal with Azolla meal, enhancing growth in species such as Nile tilapia, rohu, common carp, and catfish1,5,13,15,18, and it holds additional potential for bioremediation.

Despite this potential, research on Azolla supplementation remains notably absent for the commercially valuable thinlip grey mullet (Liza ramada). This euryhaline species, favored in Mediterranean aquaculture for its rapid growth and market value19, readily consumes both natural detritus and formulated feeds20–22. Furthermore, the aquaculture sector faces increasing challenges from global warming, as elevated water temperatures detrimentally affect fish growth, physiological processes, immune competence, and oxidative balance23–27, underscoring the need for dietary interventions like supplements to bolster resilience25,26,28.

Addressing the significant lack of data on Azolla's effects on L. ramada and the critical demand for cost-effective, climate-adaptive feeds, this study investigates the influence of dietary Azolla meal supplementation on growth performance, feed utilization efficiency, whole-body biochemical composition, general health status, and hematological parameters in thinlip grey mullet fingerlings.

Fish and experimental design

Five hundred L. ramada fry were obtained from a private nursery in Alexandria. A 70-days feeding trial was conducted at El-Max Station for Applied Aquatic Research, NIOF Alexandria Branch, Egypt. After a two-week adaptation period to the basal diet, fish (mean initial weight 1.36 ± 0.02 g/fry) were distributed into five experimental groups in triplicate (20 fry per replicate). Groups were randomly assigned to 15 glass aquaria (30 × 40 × 100 cm; width × height × length) supplied with continuously aerated marine water. Water quality parameters (temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, and salinity) were measured weekly. Throughout the 70-day trial, water temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pH averaged 20.0 ± 2.0°C, 39 ± 1.3 ppt, 7.4 ± 2.0 mg/l, and 7.9 ± 0.3, respectively. Fish were fed the test diets three times daily (09:00, 12:00, 15:00 h) to satiation. Feed consumption per aquarium was recorded daily. Fish were bulk-weighed biweekly.

Dietary supplementations and feed formulation

The dietary protein level (25% CP) was selected based on previous successful studies with mullet species of a similar size and life stage in our region, which demonstrated satisfactory growth on moderate protein diets. Furthermore, this level aligns with our goal of developing a cost-effective feed, as protein is the most expensive component.

Five isonitrogenous (~ 25% crude protein) and isocaloric (~ 18.6 kJ/g gross energy, GE) experimental diets were formulated (Table 1). The control diet (Az0, ~ 25% CP, ~ 13.3% lipid) contained no Azolla meal. Azolla meal progressively replaced a portion of the soybean meal and yellow corn at inclusion levels of 0% (Az0), 5% (Az1), 10% (Az2), 15% (Az3), and 20% (Az4). All ingredients were ground, thoroughly mixed, moistened, and pelleted using a laboratory pelleting machine1.0 mm die. Pellets were dried at 60°C for 24 h in a thermostatically controlled oven and stored in plastic bags at − 20°C until use. Proximate composition of the diets was analyzed on a dry matter basis (Table 1).

Proximate analyses of fish

At the end of the 70-days feeding trial, five fish from each aquarium (15 fish per treatment) were pooled and homogenized for analysis of their proximate chemical composition. Moisture, crude protein (Kjeldahl method; Velp Scientifica, Italy), lipid (chloroform–methanol extraction), and ash (incineration at 600°C for 4 h) contents were determined in triplicate according to AOAC30 procedures. Diet samples were similarly analyzed in triplicate.

Blood sampling and hematological analysis

At the end of the 70-day feeding trial, after a 24-h fast, three fish per aquarium (9 per treatment) were anesthetized using buffered tricaine methane sulfonate (MS-222, 30 mg/l). Approximately 0.5–1.0 mL of blood was drawn via cardiac puncture using heparinized syringes and immediately transferred to K3-EDTA coated tubes (1.5 mg EDTA per mL of blood) to prevent coagulation. Hematological parameters, including hematocrit (Hct, %), hemoglobin concentration (Hb, g/dl), red blood cell count (RBCs, 10⁶/µl), white blood cell count (WBCs, 103/µl), mean corpuscular volume (MCV, fl), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH, pg), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC, g/dl), were determined using a Fully Automatic Blood Cell Counter (Model PCE-210 N, Erma Inc.).

Statistical analysis

The sample size (n = 3 replicates per treatment) was selected based on common experimental designs in aquaculture nutrition research and was sufficient to detect significant differences, as confirmed by post-hoc power analysis. Prior to ANOVA, all data were checked for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). The data met these assumptions. Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among treatment means were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05 and separated using Duncan’s multiple range test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Laboratory personnel conducting the biochemical and enzymatic analyses were blinded to the dietary treatment groups to minimize bias.

Results

Growth performance and feed utilization

Dietary Azolla meal inclusion significantly influenced growth performance and feed utilization (Table 2). Final body weight (FBW), weight gain (WG), and specific growth rate (SGR) followed a quadratic response, with the 10% inclusion level (Az2) yielding the highest values, significantly greater (P ≤ 0.05) than the control (Az0), Az1, Az3, and Az4 groups. Although overall ANOVA for SGR was non-significant, the numerical maximum occurred at 10% Az. Feed intake (FI) was significantly lower in the Az2 and Az3 groups compared to Az0, Az1, and Az4. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was significantly lowest at Az2 (1.78), followed by Az3 (1.90), both superior to the control and other groups. Protein efficiency ratio (PER) was significantly higher in the Az2 and Az3 groups compared to Az0, Az1, and Az4. Survival rates (85.00–91.67%) were not significantly different among treatments.

Whole-body proximate composition

Dietary Azolla significantly altered the whole-body composition of thinlip mullet (Table 3). Moisture content was significantly reduced in fish fed Az1, Az2, and Az3 diets compared to the control (Az0) and Az4 groups. Crude protein (CP) content was significantly higher in fish fed Az2, Az3, and Az4 diets compared to Az0 and Az1. Ether extract (EE) content decreased progressively and significantly with increasing Azolla inclusion, with the lowest value in the Az4 group. Ash content did not differ significantly among treatments.

Liver function, metabolic, and immune biomarkers

Liver and metabolic biomarkers (Table 5) revealed that 10% Azolla meal (Az2) significantly elevated serum albumin (P < 0.05) and A/G ratio while suppressing AST and ALP activity, indicating enhanced hepatic biosynthetic capacity and reduced cellular damage. Concurrent reductions in urea (4.17 mg/dL) and creatinine (0.43 mg/dL) demonstrated improved nitrogen utilization. Crucially, Az2 amplified innate immunity (lysozyme: 2.80 U/mL) and antioxidant defense (SOD: 43.00 U/mg protein), despite moderate lipid peroxidation (MDA: 37.00 nmol/g). This hormetic response underscores Azolla’s role in modulating oxidative balance. At 20% inclusion (Az4), rising urea and declining albumin signaled metabolic strain, aligning with reduced growth performance (Table 2).

Discussion

Selecting appropriate feed ingredients is crucial for the formulation of commercial Aquafeed40,41. Fishmeal, valued for its high protein and essential amino acids, is more expensive than plant proteins42,43. Declining availability due to demand from livestock necessitates reducing fishmeal in diets. Substitution with accessible, cost-effective alternative plant proteins supports sustainable aquaculture growth44–46. While Azolla cultivation offers, environmental benefits like wastewater phytoremediation and carbon sequestration, its prolific growth rate necessitates careful management in open systems to prevent it from becoming an invasive species. Therefore, controlled, contained cultivation systems are recommended for large-scale Aquafeed production. This study evaluated the effects of dietary Azolla meal (Az) on the growth, feed utilization, and survival of thinlip grey mullet. A 10% Az inclusion significantly enhanced growth performance (FBW, WG, SGR; P ≤ 0.05), aligning with Bharti et al.47 on the growth benefits of Azolla in common carp. Optimal growth occurred at 10% Az, while 20% showed no growth promotion. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was significantly improved at 10% Az versus the control and 20% groups, indicating superior feed utilization. Magouz et al.15 similarly noted Azolla's positive effects on feed utilization in Nile tilapia.

In contrast, Gangadhar et al.48 reported optimal common carp utilization at 20% Az after two months, potentially reflecting species-specific responses or seasonal dietary shifts49. Das et al.50 found Azolla could replace 30% of groundnut oil cake in Indian major carps without adverse effects, supported by findings of effective inclusion up to 40%14,50. Survival rates (85.00–91.67%) remained unaffected across treatments, consistent with prior studies13,51, mirroring high survival and low FCR in other carps at 40% Az5,51,52. Growth reduction at 20% Az may stem from high fiber and antinutritional factors (ANFs) impairing digestibility and efficiency53–57. Palatability remained unaffected, and growth was maintained up to 15% in tilapia diets. Optimal Azolla levels depend on species, size, inclusion rate, and experimental conditions, as evidenced by enhanced carp growth at a 25% inclusion rate12.

Fish fed Azolla (Az) diets showed reduced moisture and body lipid content, attributed to the readily available energy, which potentially spares lipid storage58. However, high Az levels can hinder growth15 and significantly affect lipid content59. Optimal carcass moisture and protein occurred at 10–15% Az meal, while diet protein peaked at 20%. Body fat generally decreased with increasing dietary Az, possibly due to the deamination of excess protein into fat2, which contrasts with reports of negative correlations between carcass protein/lipid levels with Az in tilapia60. While duckweed increased lipid61, Az incorporation (15–35%) didn’t affect L. rohita protein5. Optimal common carp fry performance occurred at 30% Az.

Azolla meal (Az) inclusion significantly enhanced hematological parameters (elevated RBCs, Hb, Hct, blood total protein, albumin) in the current study and tilapia62,63, indicating improved oxygen transport and no anemia. Similar immunomodulatory effects, including increased serum lysozyme activity and IgM levels (key humoral defense components), were observed in thinlip grey mullet (Az2, Az3) and other species, supporting Az’s immunostimulatory role40,45,64–67.

While 20% Az-fed fish exhibited higher urea and creatinine levels, these remained within normal ranges, indicating no kidney impairment68,69. This group also showed the lowest glucose levels, suggesting reduced stress70. Lipid metabolism varied by species: Nile tilapia had significantly lower cholesterol and triglycerides at 20% Az, whereas thinlip grey mullet showed the highest levels at 0% Az, reflecting species-specific metabolic responses to dietary components71. Conversely, the reduced growth and signs of metabolic stress observed at the 20% inclusion level (Az4) are likely attributable to the elevated levels of fiber and antinutritional factors in Azolla meal, such as tannins, which can compromise nutrient digestibility and protein bioavailability, thereby increasing nitrogenous waste.

Efficient Az utilization up to 15% was demonstrated, supported by high protease and amylase activity, indicating improved protein and carbohydrate digestion and metabolic regulation4,15. The main carbohydrate digestion product, glucose, is absorbed via intestinal transporters72. Digestibility varies with species, carbohydrate complexity, source, processing, and temperature73–75. Lipase activity was unaffected by Az level in Nile tilapia but decreased at high levels in thinlip grey mullet, likely due to contrasting feeding habits and inherent amylolytic enzyme capacities76. However, very high Az inclusion (> 15%) may hinder digestion by disrupting enzyme function77,78 or interfering at the emulsion interface79.

Common carp fry exhibited negative allometric growth (b value: 2.1 to 1.6) up to 40% Az, consistent with previous findings5,80. Growth pattern deviations from the cube law are influenced by biological (stomach fullness, gonad maturity, sex, health, food availability, fatness) and physicochemical environmental factors81,82.

Azolla meal can replace 30% of MOC in common carp diets, enhancing immune function and potentially improving health and meat quality15. Further research is needed on Azolla digestibility and anti-nutritional factor effects across growth stages. Our findings on Liza ramada align with Nile tilapia studies, showing Azolla improves immunity, but our optimal 10% inclusion is lower than for carps (20–30%), highlighting need for species-specific diets83,84. Practically, cultivating Azolla on wastewater offers a sustainable, cost-effective feed ingredient. A 10% substitution could reduce feed costs by 5–10%, though scaling requires solutions for consistent yield and drying infrastructure.

Conclusion

This study concludes that a 10% dietary inclusion of Azolla is optimal for thinlip grey mullet fingerlings. This level significantly enhances growth performance, digestive enzyme activity, and immune responses while maintaining affordability. The recommendation is based on a comprehensive evaluation of multiple biomarkers. Longer-term studies are advised to verify these benefits throughout the full production cycle and assess economic sustainability.
