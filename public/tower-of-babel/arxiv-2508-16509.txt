ML-PWS: Estimating the Mutual Information Between Experimental Time Series Using Neural Networks
Manuel Reinhardt, Gašper Tkačik, Pieter Rein ten Wolde

Abstract
The ability to quantify information transmission is crucial for the analysis and design of natural and engineered systems. The information transmission rate is the fundamental measure for systems with time-varying signals, yet computing it is extremely challenging. In particular, the rate cannot be obtained directly from experimental time-series data without approximations, because of the high dimensionality of the signal trajectory space. Path Weight Sampling (PWS) is a computational technique that makes it possible to obtain the information rate exactly for any stochastic system. However, it requires a mathematical model of the system of interest, be it described by a master equation or a set of differential equations. Here, we present a technique that employs Machine Learning (ML) to develop a generative model from experimental time-series data, which is then combined with PWS to obtain the information rate. We demonstrate the accuracy of this technique, called ML-PWS, by comparing its results on synthetic time-series data generated from a non-linear model against ground-truth results obtained by applying PWS directly to the same model. We illustrate the utility of ML-PWS by applying it to neuronal time-series data.



I
Introduction

The canonical measure for quantifying information transmission via time-varying signals is the information transmission rate
Shannon 1948
;
Tostevin and ten Wolde 2009
. It is defined as the rate at which the path mutual information between the input and output trajectories of the system increases with the trajectory duration. It quantifies the speed at which distinct messages are transmitted through the system, taking into account the correlations that are present in the input and output signals.
The information rate has been used to
to quantify
biochemical signaling performance

Mattingly et al. 2021
;
Hahn et al. 2023
;
Moor and Zechner 2023
, to perform model reduction

Schmitt et al. 2023
, to detect the causality of interactions

Frenzel and Pompe 2007
;
Hlaváčková-Schindler et al. 2007
, to test for
nonlinearities in time series
Paluš 1995
,
to assess dependencies between
stock prices or market indices over time

Marschinski and Kantz 2002
;
Dimpfl and Peter 2013
;
Dimpfl and Jank 2016
, or to quantify information exchange
between different regions of the brain
Rad and Paninski 2011
;
So et al. 2012
. In the absence of
feedback the rate also equals the multi-step transfer entropy

Massey 1990
;
Schreiber 2000
.

Yet, computing the path mutual information between input and output
trajectories is a notoriously difficult problem because trajectories are
high-dimensional objects. Conventional methods to compute the mutual
information rely on non-parametric estimates of the joint probability
distribution of input and output, e.g. via histograms or kernel
density methods
Fraser and Swinney 1986
;
Moon et al. 1995
. These methods are, however, infeasible for high-dimensional data and hence cannot be used for computing the path mutual information

Paninski 2003
;
Cepeda-Humerez et al. 2019
. More advanced
non-parametric estimators such as the k-nearest-neighbor (KNN)
estimator
Kraskov et al. 2004
are better suited for high-dimensional
data, but suffer from uncontrolled biases as the dimensionality of
data increases
Gao et al. 2014
;
Cepeda-Humerez et al. 2019
;
Das and Wolde 2024
.
Consequently, the rate is often computed using approximate schemes, such as the Gaussian framework
Tostevin and ten Wolde 2009
;
Tostevin and ten Wolde 2010
, moment-closure approximations
Duso and Zechner 2019
;
Moor and Zechner 2023
, or techniques limited to specific systems
Sinzger et al. 2020
;
Sinzger-D’Angelo and Koeppl 2023
;
Gehri et al. 2024
. Schemes based on machine learning have also been introduced, such as decoding-based approaches
Cepeda-Humerez et al. 2019
, Information Noise-Contrastive Estimation (InfoNCE)
Oord et al. 2018
and the Difference-of-Entropies (DoE) estimator
McAllester and Stratos 2018
.

We recently introduced Path Weight Sampling (PWS)
Reinhardt et al. 2023
, which is a computational technique that makes it possible to compute the information transmission rate
exactly
for any stochastic model, be it described by a master equation or stochastic differential equations. The principal idea is
to use the stochastic model to evaluate the path likelihood, i.e. the conditional probability of an individual output trajectory for a given input trajectory, and then average this quantity via Monte Carlo sampling in trajectory space to obtain the path mutual information. While the scheme is exact and does not rely on uncontrolled approximations, it does require a stochastic model of the system of interest to evaluate the path likelihood. The scheme therefore cannot be directly applied to experimental time series data.

In this manuscript, we show how PWS can be combined with Machine Learning (ML) to obtain the information rate directly from experimental data. The new idea of our technique, called ML-PWS, is to first use machine learning to develop a generative model that is able to describe the time series data, allowing us to compute the path likelihood. As in conventional PWS, this path likelihood is then averaged to obtain the information transmission rate.
Here, we develop the generative model based on a neural autoregressive sequence prediction, as is used e.g. in speech synthesis
van den Oord et al. 2016
or text generation
Sutskever et al. 2011
, but we emphasise that the principal idea is more generic. We demonstrate the power of ML-PWS by applying it to synthetic time series data generated from a non-linear model, and by comparing the
rate thus obtained against the ground-truth result obtained by applying PWS directly to
this model. We illustrate the utility of ML-PWS by applying it to neuronal time-series data
Marre et al. 2015
.

Path Weigth Sampling—
The information rate
R(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{X})
is defined as the speed at which the mutual information
I(S_{1:n},X_{1:n})
between an input trajectory
S_{1:n}
and output trajectory
X_{1:n}
increases with the trajectory duration
T=n\delta t

R(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{X})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\delta t}I(S_{1:n},X_{1:n}),

(1)

where

\displaystyle I(S_{1:n},X_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\ln\frac{\prob(s_{1:n},x_{1:n})}{\prob(s_{1:n})\prob(x_{1:n})}\right\rangle_{\prob(s_{1:n},x_{1:n})},

(2)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\ln\frac{\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})}{\prob(x_{1:n})}\right\rangle_{\prob(s_{1:n},x_{1:n})}

(3)

with
\prob(s_{1:n},x_{1:n}),\prob(s_{1:n}),\prob(x_{1:n})
the joint and marginal distributions of the trajectories
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{X}
, respectively, and
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
the conditional distribution of the output given the input trajectory, discretized with a timestep
\delta t
. PWS is based on the idea that for systems described by a master equation or a stochastic differential equation, the path likelihood
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
can be computed on the fly, which makes it possible to estimate the mutual information as a Monte Carlo average over trajectory space:

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{\rm MC}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\ln\frac{\prob(x^{i}_{1:n}|s^{i}_{1:n})}{\prob(x^{i}_{1:n})},

(4)

with

\displaystyle\prob(x^{i}_{1:n})\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{j=1}\prob(x^{i}_{1:n}|s^{j}_{1:n}).

(5)

In the brute-force version of PWS, called Direct PWS (DPWS), the mutual information is indeed obtained via two nested Monte Carlo averages, in which
N
pairs of
(s^{i}_{1:n},x^{i}_{1:n})
are generated from
\prob(s_{1:n},x_{1:n})
, and for each output trajectory
x^{i}_{1:n}

M
input trajectories
s^{j}_{1:n}
are generated from
\prob(s_{1:n})

Reinhardt et al. 2023
. The main challenge is the computation of the marginal
\prob(x^{i}_{1:n})
, Eq.
5
, for which special techniques from polymer sampling and free energy estimation are presented in Ref.
Reinhardt et al. 2023
.

While PWS is an exact Monte Carlo scheme, in the sense that it
provides an unbiased statistical estimate of the mutual information,
it does require a stochastic model from which the path likelihood

\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
can be obtained: this quantity is at the
heart of the PWS computation of the mutual information, see
Eqs.
4
and
5
. The information transmission rate can indeed not
be obtained directly from experimental data: a model-free estimate is not possible, since the rate depends on the full
probability distribution of signal trajectories, which cannot be
estimated experimentally because of the high-dimensionality of the
trajectory space. We need, inevitably, a stochastic model that
describes the statistics of the input and output signal
trajectories. While the list of systems for which an accurate
mechanistic or a phenomenological model is available is rapidly
growing, in many cases no such model is available. To overcome this
problem, ML-PWS first learns a stochastic model from
experimental time-series data,
enabling the computation of the path
likelihood
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
, from which the mutual information
is then computed using PWS.

Machine Learning PWS—
Our generative stochastic model is based on autoregressive neural networks which have been used for sequence prediction
Graves 2013
, image modeling
van den Oord et al. 2016
, natural language processing
Vaswani et al. 2017
as well as many other tasks. All of these models factorize the joint probability of a sequence
x_{1:n}
as

\displaystyle\prob(x_{1:n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:i-1}).

(6)

To obtain the path likelihood
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
, we also need to include the conditioning on the input signal, taking into account the causal relationship between
s_{1:n}
and
x_{1:n}
. Since
s_{1:n}
and
x_{1:n}
represent time-series,
x_{i}
does not depend on any of the future inputs
s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{n}
. Therefore, instead of conditioning on the full input
s_{1:n}
, we only condition on
s_{1:i}
:

\displaystyle\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i}).

(7)

Up to this point no approximation has been made. To make further progress, we model each conditional distribution
\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})
in Eq.
7
using a parametric distribution defined by parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
. An autoregressive neural network then predicts the values of these parameters at time step
i
based on the observed sequence of inputs
s_{1:i}
and the sequence of past outputs
x_{1:i-1}
. This network can be trained efficiently from experimental data using the negative log likelihood loss.

To train the model, we exploit that the training data consists of
N
pairs of trajectories
(s^{k}_{1:n},x^{k}_{1:n})
for
k=1,\ldots,N
that represent independent draws from the true data distribution. The loss function to be minimized is then given by the sum of the individual negative log likelihoods for the trajectory pairs:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\sum^{N}_{k=1}\ln\prob(x^{k}_{1:n}|s^{k}_{1:n},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,.

(8)

Here we made explicit that the conditional probability depends on the neural weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
While generating a trajectory using an autoregressive model is inherently a sequential process, for a given pair of trajectories
(s_{1:n},x_{1:n})
the conditional probability in Eq.
7
can be evaluated in parallel, since the computations for
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
are independent of each other. This allows for efficient training on a GPU.

The above ML approach makes it possible to develop a generative model
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
, which can be combined with PWS to obtain the mutual information (Eqs.
4
and
5
). Yet, ML can also be used to optimize PWS itself, by improving its most challenging step, which is the computation of
\prob(x_{1:n})
(see Eq.
5
). The improvement is based on the observations that a)
\prob(x_{1:n})
is also given by

\displaystyle\prob(x_{1:n})=\left\langle\frac{\prob(s_{1:n})\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})}{q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})}\right\rangle_{q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})},

(9)

where
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
is an importance sampling distribution over
s_{1:n}
, and b) the variance in the estimate of
\prob(x_{1:n})
is minimized when
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
equals the true posterior distribution
\prob(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
. In the spirit of variational autoencoders, the key idea is then to train a second neural network that parametrizes
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
, the
inference
model, by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler difference between
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
and
\prob(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
, by optimizing the Evidence Lower Bound Objective (ELBO)
Kingma and Welling 2013
. Although the estimate of
\prob(x_{1:n})
is always unbiased, independent of the choice of
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
, optimizing
q(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
using this variational inference approach improves the efficiency of PWS. For this inference model we also use an autoregressive neural network, which is optimized jointly with the forward network.

Benchmarks—
We test ML-PWS by applying it to synthetic data generated from a minimal non-linear model and comparing the result against the exact result obtained by directly applying PWS to the same model. The model to generate the synthetic data

(s_{1:50},x_{1:50})
consists of an Auto-Regressive input of order 1, AR(1), and a nonlinear output with a degree of nonlinearity that is characterized by the gain parameter
\gamma
, see
Fig.

1

To obtain the information rate using ML-PWS, we use this data to train a Gaussian autoregressive neural network, where each conditional probability distribution
\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})
in Eq.
7
is Gaussian with mean
\mu_{i}(x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})
and standard deviation
\sigma_{i}(x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i}))
. While each
conditional
distribution is Gaussian, the whole sequence is not, due to the nonlinear nature of the neural network (see SM).

Application to neural data—
To demonstrate the utility of ML-PWS, we apply it to neuronal time series data
Marre et al. 2015
. The data was recorded from retinal ganglion cells of a salamander, responding to a horizontally moving bar displayed on a screen
Marre et al. 2015
. The dataset consists of spike trains from 230 neurons, sampled at a temporal resolution of
0.1{\rm ms}
. These cells were stimulated by 136 repeated trials of
T=30.08\,{\rm s}
during which the same stimulus was given. The dynamics of the stimulus, the moving bar, is that of an underdamped particle in a harmonic well, which means that the distribution of inputs,
\prob(s_{1:n})
, is known.
For analysis, the time series of the output was discretized at a resolution of
\Delta t=10\,{\rm ms}
, yielding
n=T/\Delta t=3008
time bins per trial. The number of spikes per time bin is assumed to be Poisson distributed. These discretized trajectories were used to train a neural network, yielding a generative model that was then combined with PWS to obtain the information rate.
We trained a neural network for each cell separately, yielding a generative model for the path likelihood
\prob(x^{(i)}_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
for each cell
i
, from which we then computed the path mutual information
I(S_{1:n},X^{(i)}_{1:n})
for each individual cell. In addition, we trained a neural network for the collective response of the population of cells, yielding the path likelihood
\prob({\bf x}_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
for the population cells, with
{\bf x}_{1:n}=(x^{(1)}_{1:n},\dots,x^{(230)}_{1:n})
; this yields the mutual information
I(S_{1:n},{\bf X}_{1:n})
between the input
S_{1:n}
and the collective response
{\bf X}_{1:n}
of the population of cells.

Figure

Figure

(b) shows the path mutual information as a function of trajectory duration, for 50 individual cells (blue lines), their sum (green line), as well as between the input and the collective response of the population of 50 cells (red line). After a transient caused by the delayed response of the system, the mutual information rises linearly with the trajectory duration, with a slope that defines the information transmission rate. It is seen that the mutual information varies substantially from cell to cell, as was also observed by the authors of Ref.

. Moreover, the mutual information

between the input

(red line) is significantly lower than the sum of the mutual information of the individual cells,

Discussion—
We demonstrated how autoregressive sequence prediction models can be trained on time-series data to learn a generative model, which can be combined with PWS to compute the information rate.
By applying ML-PWS to a nonlinear model of information processing, we showed that it provides more accurate mutual information estimates than the Gaussian approximation.
While this example serves as a proof of concept, it shows the potential of advanced machine learning techniques to automatically derive stochastic models from experimental data, and to enable the computation of information-theoretic measures for complex, high-dimensional data.

While ML-PWS shares similarities with the DoE estimator

McAllester and Stratos 2018
, ML-PWS has the benefit that
\prob(x_{1:n})

is obtained via an exact marginalisation of

\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})\prob(s_{1:n})
using PWS, and not by training a second neural network as in DoE; this ensures that
the marginal statistics
x_{1:n}
are consistent with the input
distribution
\prob(s_{1:n})
, as determined by the setup of the
experiment, and the conditional input distribution

\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
of the generative model.
Moreover, ML-PWS also enables the efficient computation of the mutual information
I(\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})
between a input signal
\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime}
with different statistics and the corresponding output
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
, without needing to re-train the marginal model.
This is particularly useful if one is interested in the channel capacity, as determined by the input distribution that maximizes the mutual information or information rate for the system of interest.
Indeed, in systems without feedback,
\prob(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{s})
is a property of the system and does not change upon changing the input.
The generative model then remains the same, such that
ML-PWS can directly recompute the mutual information for different input statistics.

In conclusion, ML-PWS represents a promising advance for estimating the mutual information of high-dimensional data, a notoriously difficult problem.
By enabling the accurate computation of the mutual information rate, we anticipate that ML-PWS could contribute to a deeper understanding of complex dynamical systems, with potential applications spanning neuroscience, biology, physics, and machine learning.

Appendix A
Autoregressive RNN model

Let the input sequence be
s_{1:n}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
. A recursive neural network (RNN) takes this sequence as well as an initial state
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and generates a sequence
h_{1:n}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
from a recursive relation

h_{i}=f_{\theta}(s_{i},h_{i-1})

(10)

where
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and an activation function
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The activation function
f_{\theta}
could for instance be parameterized by a simple neural network layer

f_{\theta}(s,h)=\tanh(Ws+Vh+b)

(11)

with parameters
\theta=(W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1},V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},b\in\mathbb{R}^{d})
and applying
\tanh
element wise. Other possible choices for
f_{\theta}
include LSTM units
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
or GRU units
Cho et al. 2014
which allow the model to better learn long-term dependencies. In any case, given an initial state
h_{0}
and the parameters
\theta
, a RNN represents a deterministic map from one sequence
s_{1:n}
to another sequence
h_{1:n}
.

From the sequence
h_{1:n}
we can obtain a stochastic representation of the output sequence
x_{1:n}
given an input sequence.
To represent a stochastic sequence
x_{1:n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we decompose the joint distribution as a product of conditional probabilities

\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})\,.

(12)

Specifically, we extend the recursive relation above by adding a sampling step to obtain
x_{i}

\displaystyle h_{i}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(s_{i},x_{i-1},h_{i-1})

(13)

\displaystyle x_{i}\mid h_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\phi}(h_{i}),\sigma_{\alpha}(h_{i}))

such that each
x_{i}
is a normal-distributed random variable whose mean
\mu_{\phi}(h_{i})
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\alpha}(h_{i})
are obtained from the current hidden state
h_{i}
.

The autoregressive model represents a generative model for the distribution
\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})
. Generating
x_{1:n}
given
s_{1:n}
is done sequentially. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, the sampling procedure alternates between computing the parameters of the conditional distribution,
\hat{\mu}_{i}=\mu_{i}(x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})
,
\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}(x_{1:i-1},s_{1:i})
, and sampling the next
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\sigma}_{i})
. Thus, the conditional probability of the resulting output sequence is given by

\displaystyle\prob(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\prob(x_{i}|x_{1:n-1},s_{1:n})

(14)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\hat{\sigma}_{i}}\exp\left(\frac{(x_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i})^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}\right)\,.

In practice we use the following form form for
\mu_{\phi}
and
\sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\mu_{\phi}(h)

\displaystyle=W_{\mu}h+b_{\mu}

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}(h)

\displaystyle=\exp(W_{\sigma}h+b_{\sigma})

(16)

where the weights
W_{\mu}
and
W_{\sigma}
are
d
-dimensional row vectors and the exponential function ensures that the standard deviation is always positive. For brevity of notation we use
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta,W_{\mu},b_{\mu},W_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})
to refer to all weights of the model.

The recursive relation (
13
) defines the conditional probability distribution
P(x_{1:n}|s_{1:n},\boldsymbol{\theta})
of the output sequence given the input sequence for our generative model.
Note that since
h_{i}
depends on
s_{i}
and
x_{i-1}
, as well as
h_{i-1}
, it encodes information about the entire past
s_{1:i}
and
x_{1:i-1}
and thus the conditional probability of the sequence
x_{1:n}
is given by
Eq.

14
.

A.1
Training

There are a few practical considerations for efficiently training the model.
Training is performed in iterations and it is often beneficial to introduce stochasticity between iterations to speed up gradient descent and regularize the loss function
Bottou 2010
;
Feng and Tu 2021
.
For this reason, as typically done for training neural networks, the loss function in Eq.
8
is only computed for a subset of the training data, in mini-batches of size
M
, instead of the whole training set of size
N
. At the beginning of each iteration, the subset that is used is then randomly resampled, effectively making the loss function stochastic.

Appendix B
Details of the First Study

B.1
Model

To generate training data for the neural network, we combine a linear auto-regressive input, with a stochastic nonlinear output model.
Specifically, we considered an input that evolves according to AR(1) statistics:

S_{t}=\phi S_{t-1}+\xi_{t}

(17)

where
\xi_{t}
are iid random variables from a unit Gaussian distribution, and
\phi\in[0,1)
is a parameter. In steady state, the autocovariance of this process is given by

\langle S_{\tau}S_{\tau+t}\rangle=\frac{\phi^{|t|}}{1-\phi^{2}}\,.

(18)

The output
X_{t}
is governed by the equation

X_{t}=\sigma(\gamma S_{t})+\rho X_{t-1}+\vartheta\eta_{t}

(19)

where
\eta_{t}
are iid Gaussian random numbers,
\gamma
,
\rho
and
\vartheta
are positive real parameters, and

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}

(20)

is the logistic function. The gain
\gamma
effectively controls the strength of the nonlinearity; see Fig.
1
.
This process models a response that saturates as the input grows. In fact,
\sigma(x)
is equivalent to the Hill function commonly used in biochemistry to describe saturating enzyme kinetics
Edelstein-Keshet 2005
.

B.2
Training the Neural Networks

We trained our machine learning model with synthetic data generated according to
Eq.

19
for various values of the gain, denoted by
\gamma
.
For each value of
\gamma
, we created a distinct training set of 1000 pairs of time series
(s_{1:50},x_{1:50})
and trained one autoregressive model per training set.
Once the models were trained, we estimated the mutual information for each of them using PWS, employing variational inference to perform the marginalization.

For the generative model, we use a recurrent neural network (RNN). Using Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) cells
Cho et al. 2014
with a hidden size of 64 for temporal processing, along with a dense layer that outputs two values, representing the mean
\mu
and log-variance
\ln\sigma^{2}
of a Gaussian distribution. For each time step
i
, the model receives input signals
s_{i}
and
x_{i-1}
and predicts the next output value
x_{i}
by sampling from this Gaussian. The model is trained by iteratively optimizing over 100 epochs over the entire training set. The training set is split into mini-batches of size 64 which are shuffled after each epoch. We optimize the model using the Adam optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2014
(
b_{1}=$0.9$,b_{2}=$0.999$
) with weight decay regularization
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
of
1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
and using a cosine decay learning rate schedule
Loshchilov and Hutter 2016
that smoothly decreases the learning rate from
1\text{\times}{10}^{-2}
to
1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
throughout the training process.

The inference model used for marginalization is also modeled as a RNN.
This model consists of an encoder-decoder architecture for approximating the posterior
\prob(s_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
.
The encoder first processes the input sequence
x_{1:n}
in reverse using a GRU cell with 64 hidden units to create a latent sequence
h_{1:n}
.
The decoder then uses another RNN with a hidden size of 64 (using a GRU cell) to autoregressively generate a sequence
\tilde{s}_{1:n}
using the context sequence
h_{1:n}
.
The sequence
\tilde{s}_{1:n}
is generated probabilistically by sampling from a mixture of logistic distributions with five components, as suggested in Ref.
Ho et al. 2019
.
The inference network is trained for 100 epochs with mini-batches of size 64.
For each
x_{1:n}
in the training set, 16 Monte Carlo draws from the inference network
\tilde{s}_{1:n}\sim q(\tilde{s}_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
are used to estimate the ELBO loss. The loss function gradient is estimated using SGVB
Kingma and Welling 2013
.
The model is optimized using the ADAM optimizer with weight decay regularization (same parameters as above). We use an initial learning rate of
5\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
that decays exponentially by a factor of
0.5
as training progresses.

To compute the marginal probability
\prob(x_{1:n})
from our models, we use the inference network to generate
2^{14}=$16\,384$
samples
\tilde{s}_{1:n}
for each sequence
x_{1:n}
. From these samples
\tilde{s}_{1:n}
, the marginal probability is estimated using Eq.
9
.
We monitor the convergence of the variational marginalization procedure by computing the effective sample size from the importance weights
Martino et al. 2017
. In our case, the effective sample size always remained above 85% of the actual sample size, indicating that the inference network approximates the posterior well.

Appendix C
Details of the Second Study

C.1
Dataset

We analyze neuronal responses to a moving bar stimulus, recorded from retinal ganglion cells of a salamander. The dataset consists of spike trains from 230 neurons, sampled at a temporal resolution of
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
. Recordings span a total duration of
8148\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
(approximately 2 hours) and are structured into
136
repeated trials, each lasting
T=$30.08\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
. In each trial, the same stimulus is presented.

The stimulus is a horizontally moving vertical bar displayed on a screen. The
x
-position of the bar is varied stochastically. Its dynamics are given by a stochastically driven harmonic oscillator model.
We rescale the input data, such that the variance
\langle x^{2}\rangle
is 1.
This model is defined by the following equations

\displaystyle\tau\frac{dx}{dt}

\displaystyle=v

(21)

\displaystyle\tau\frac{dv}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}}x-v+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\gamma}\eta_{v}(t)

(22)

where the time-scale is given by
\tau=$50\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
and the damping coefficient is given by
\gamma=(2\omega_{0}\tau)^{-1}=$1.06$
for a value of
\omega_{0}=$9.42\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
. Since
\gamma>1
, the oscillator is in the over-damped regime (albeit close to critically damped).

For analysis, we discretize the data at a resolution of
\delta t=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
, yielding
n=T/\delta t=$1504$
time bins per trial. Sampling times are denoted as
t_{i}=i\delta t
for
i=0,\dots,n
. The spike count of neuron
k
in the interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
is represented as
x^{k}_{i}
.

C.2
Neuron model

In each time-interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1})
we assume the spike counts of each neuron are Poisson distributed.
The firing intensity of neuron
k
at time
t_{i}=i\Delta t
is denoted by
\lambda^{k}_{i}
. The random variable
X^{k}_{i}
denotes the number of spikes of neuron
k
in the time-interval
t_{i:i+1}=[t_{i},t_{i+1})
and is assumed to be Poisson-distributed. The probability of observing
x
spikes in this interval is thus given by

\mathrm{P}(X^{k}_{i}=x)=\frac{(\lambda^{k}_{i}\Delta t)^{x}}{x!}e^{-\lambda^{k}_{i}\Delta t}\,.

(23)

Therefore, the negative log likelihood is given by

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i}-n_{i}\ln\lambda_{i}\Delta t+\lambda_{i}\Delta t+\ln(n_{i}!)

(24)

We use a one-dimensional convolutional neural network for predicting
the spiking activity of retinal cells in response to a visual
stimulus. The network processes the time-varying stimulus
s(t)
,
which represents the visual input over time, using a series of

causal convolutional layers
. Each convolutional layer applies a
1D convolution with a specified kernel size, followed by batch
normalization and a ReLU activation, allowing the network to extract
temporal features from the input. To enforce causality—ensuring
that predictions at a given time step only depend on past
inputs—a zero-padding layer is added to the data before the
convolutions, offsetting the receptive field appropriately. The final
layer is a 1x1 convolution that maps the extracted features to spike
intensity predictions for each neuron by modeling spiking activity as
a Poisson process, where the predicted intensities determine the
firing probability of each neuron.

During each training step, the model receives the stimulus
s_{1:N}
and the observed spike counts
\boldsymbol{x}_{1:N}
at every timestep, predicts the time-varying firing intensity of each neuron, and computes the loss between the predicted and actual spike counts.
The loss function used in the model is the Poisson negative log-likelihood loss.
Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer, and the learning rate is dynamically adjusted using a OneCycleLR scheduler
Smith and Topin 2018
, which initially increases the learning rate before gradually decaying it to improve convergence.
