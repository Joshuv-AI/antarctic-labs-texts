Dynamic Target Attack
Kedong Xiu, Churui Zeng, Tianhang Zheng, Xinzhe Huang, Xiaojun Jia, Di Wang, Puning Zhao, Zhan Qin, Kui Ren

Abstract
Existing gradient-based jailbreak attacks typically optimize an adversarial suffix to induce a fixed affirmative response, e.g., “ Sure, here is… ”. However, this fixed target usually resides in an extremely low-density region of a safety-aligned LLM’s output distribution conditioned on diverse harmful inputs. Due to the substantial discrepancy between the target and the original output, existing attacks require numerous iterations to optimize the adversarial prompt, which might still fail to induce the low-probability target response from the target LLM. In this paper, we propose D ynamic T arget A ttack (DTA), a new jailbreaking framework relying on the target LLM’s own responses as targets to optimize the adversarial prompts. In each optimization round, DTA iteratively samples multiple candidate responses directly from the output distribution conditioned on the current prompt, and selects the most harmful response as a temporary target for prompt optimization. In contrast to existing attacks, DTA significantly reduces the discrepancy between the target and the output distribution, substantially easing the optimization process to search for an effective adversarial prompt. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superior effectiveness and efficiency of DTA: under the white-box setting, DTA only needs 200 200 optimization iterations to achieve an average attack success rate (ASR) of over 87 % 87\% on recent safety-aligned LLMs, exceeding the state-of-the-art baselines by over 15 % 15\% . The time cost of DTA is 2 ∼ \thicksim 26 times less than existing baselines. Under the black-box setting, DTA uses Llama-3-8B-Instruct as a surrogate model for target sampling and achieves an ASR of 85 % 85\% against the black-box target model Llama-3-70B-Instruct, exceeding its counterparts by over 25 % 25\% . Background

2.1
Paradigms in White-box Jailbreaking

Jailbreaking aims to craft prompts that bypass an LLM’s safety alignment, inducing it to generate harmful or restricted content. In the white-box setting, the foundational goal is to optimize an adversarial suffix
S
, appended to a user’s harmful prompt
P
, to maximize the conditional probability of the target LLM generating a predefined, affirmative target prefix (e.g., “
Sure, here is …
”)
T=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k})
. This objective can normally be formulated as minimizing the negative log-likelihood (or cross-entropy loss):

S^{*}=\arg\min_{S}\mathcal{L}(S)=\arg\min_{S}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log p(t_{i}\mid P,S,t_{<i})\right).

(1)

Existing methods, such as GCG
[
6
]
and COLD-Attack
[
7
]
are prominent implementations of this paradigm, employing token-level and global-level optimization processes, respectively, to find an effective
S^{*}
.

However, recent researchers have recognized that constraining the optimization to a single, fixed target prefix is overly restrictive and can lead to inefficient optimization. To mitigate this limitation, methods like AdvPrefix
[
8
]
are proposed. The core idea of AdvPrefix is to relax the objective by introducing a set of multiple, curated target prefixes,
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{m}\}
. The optimization goal then becomes maximizing the probability of generating any of these targets, often approximated by minimizing the loss of the most likely target in the set:

S^{*}=\arg\min_{S}\left(\min_{T_{j}\in\mathcal{T}}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{|T_{j}|}\log p(t_{j,i}\mid P,S,t_{j,<i})\right)\right)

(2)

This multi-target approach provides more flexibility but still relies on a predefined set of external targets.
Furthermore, researchers have proposed jailbreaking methods based on Reinforcement Learning (e.g., RLbreaker
[
9
]
) and adaptive jailbreaking methods (e.g., llm-adaptive-attacks
[
10
]
. However, all existing methods rely on
externally-defined objectives
, which may not adaptively align with different target LLMs’ natural harmful responses.

2.2
Language Model Decoding and Sampling Strategies

Given a prompt
x
, the Large Language Model (LLM) generates a response by autoregressively sampling tokens from a conditional probability distribution, i.e.,

p_{T}(y^{i}_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)=\frac{e^{f^{i}(x,y_{<t})/T}}{\sum_{j}e^{f^{j}(x,y_{<t})/T}},

(3)

where
y_{t}
refers to the token output at position
t
and
y_{<t}
denotes the generated sub-sequence before
t
. In general, there are two commonly-used strategies for sampling
y_{t}
,
i.e.,
greedy sampling and random sampling. For greedy sampling, the LLM selects the next token at position
t
by
i=\arg\max_{i}p(y^{i}_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)
. For random sampling, the LLM samples the token according to the probability distribution in Eq.
3
with a non-zero temperature
T
.

In practice, most LLMs adopt greedy sampling or random sampling with a low temperature, which forms a narrow output space. A safety-aligned LLM usually generates refusal responses from this narrow space to harmful prompts.
However, recent work
[
11
]
indicates that a broader output space randomly sampled from a safety-aligned LLM usually contains harmful responses. Inspired by this observation, we propose DTA to exploit the potentially harmful responses randomly sampled from the LLM as targets to optimize the adversarial suffix, aiming to induce similar responses under the modes of greedy sampling or random sampling with a small temperature.

3
Methodology

3.1
Overview of Dynamic Target Attack

The design of DTA begins from a simple intuition: optimizing the adversarial suffix
S
toward harmful responses that have relatively high probability in the target model’s conditional output distribution is more efficient than using a fixed, relatively low probability response as the target. Given a harmful prompt
P
, DTA learns an adversarial suffix
S
such that the target LLM produces a complete harmful response under
standard
decoding. As shown in Figure
2
, DTA proceeds in short
exploration–optimization
cycles:

1.

Dynamic Target Exploration.
Temporarily relax decoding to explore a broader output region of the target model and
directly sample
native harmful responses; select the most harmful as the
dynamic target

r^{*}
.

2.

Target-Conditioned Optimization.
Update
S
with a small number of gradient optimization iterations to increase the likelihood of producing
r^{*}
under standard decoding while preserving suffix fluency and avoiding refusal triggers.

Each cycle updates
r^{*}
through re-sampling, which (i) anchors optimization in high-density regions of the
current
conditional distribution and (ii) solves the problem of distribution shift induced by optimizing
S
. Formally, the optimization in each cycle solves

\displaystyle\min_{S}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DTA}}(P,S;r^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}(P,S;r^{*})+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{suffix}}(S),

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}
steers the model toward
r^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{suffix}}
is a regularizer that promotes fluency while penalizing proximity to refusal-related tokens. The scalar
\lambda>0
balances these terms.

shows the details of our DTA.

3.2
Exploration — Dynamically Sampling a Harmful Target

The cornerstone of DTA is that targets are
directly sampled from the model’s conditional output distribution, rather than a fixed, low-probability response
. Given a harmful prompt
P
and the current suffix
S
, we query the target model
f
with
a relaxed, high-entropy decoding strategy
that increases sampling diversity, thereby
directly sampling
candidate responses from the target model. Conditioned on
P{+}S
, we draw
N
candidates
\{{r_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from the target model
f
:

r_{i}\sim f\big(\cdot\mid P{+}S;\ \text{relaxed decoding}\big),\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

(5)

Each
r_{i}
is scored by a harmfulness judge
J
(e.g., GPTFuzzer), yielding
J(r_{i})\in[0,1]
. We select the most harmful candidate as the cycle’s dynamic target:

r^{*}=\arg\max_{r_{i}}J(r_{i}).

(6)

By anchoring optimization to
r^{*}
—a
high-density, model-native
harmful response—DTA aligns its objective with what the target model already deems plausible, improving its efficiency and stability.

3.3
Optimization — Efficiently Steering Toward the Dynamic Target

Given
r^{*}
, DTA updates
S
via a small number of gradient optimization steps to minimize
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DTA}}
. This objective is composed of two primary components: a response likelihood loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}
and a suffix regularization term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{suffix}}
. Instead of matching the entire, potentially noisy sequence, we
truncate
r^{*}
to a fixed length of response, i.e.,
r^{*}_{L}
.
We employ this truncation strategy because (1) it mitigates the influence of noisy or incoherent tokens that often appear in the latter part of long generated responses, ensuring that the optimization focuses on more reliable and meaningful content, and (2) it reinforces early-stage control, encouraging the suffix to steer the model output from the very beginning of generation toward harmful behaviors.

The response-likelihood term is then the negative log-likelihood of
r^{*}_{L}
under
standard
decoding (temperature
\tau_{\text{eval}}{=}0.7
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}(P,S;r^{*}_{L})=-\sum_{t=1}^{L}\log p_{\tau_{\text{eval}}}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},,P{+}S).

(7)

To preserve stealthiness and avoid triggering refusals, we add a suffix regularizer composed of the fluency loss
\mathcal{L}_{flu}
and the refusal loss
\mathcal{L}_{rej}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{flu}}(S)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}\log p_{\tau_{\text{eval}}}(s_{j}\mid s_{<j}),

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{rej}}(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}\ \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{rej}}}\log p(s_{j}{=}v\mid s{<j}),

(9)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\text{rej}}
is a set of common refusal tokens/phrases. We combine them as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{suffix}}(S)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{flu}}(S)-\mathcal{L}_{\text{rej}}(S),

(10)

so that optimization jointly improves fluency while
moving away
from refusal-inducing regions in logit space. The overall objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DTA}}(P,S;r^{*})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}(P,S;r^{*}_{L})+\lambda\cdot(\mathcal{L}_{\text{flu}}(S)-\mathcal{L}_{\text{rej}}(S)),

(11)

and we update
S
by gradient descent (Adam
[
12
]
in practice):

S\leftarrow S-\eta\nabla{S}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DTA}}(P,S;r^{*}).

(12)

3.4
Iterative Re-Sampling

We note that the first sampled target in a cycle can still be safe. In that case, continued optimization toward this safe target may stall progress and fail to jailbreak the target model. For example, in Figure
3
, the first target
T^{1}_{S}
is more harmful than the initial refusal response
R_{\text{initial}}
but remains safe, and the first update still yields a safe output
R^{1}_{S}
. After each short optimization round, DTA
re-samples
from the model’s
current
conditional distribution given the harmful prompt and the updated suffix. It then selects the most harmful candidate as the next temporary target. This refresh keeps the target anchored to what the model now considers plausible, and it is repeated every cycle rather than focusing to a single target.

Figure 3:
Illustration of the core difference between DTA and existing methods.

The brief update toward a model-native target shifts probability distribution away from refusal and
reduces the target–distribution discrepancy
, which increases the chance that the next re-sampling step surfaces a genuinely harmful, higher-density target. In Figure
3
, the second re-sampled target
T^{2}_{S}
is more harmful than
T^{1}_{S}
. In the second round, DTA optimizes the adversarial suffix
S
towards
T^{2}_{S}
, successfully inducing the harmful response
R^{2}_{S}
. By alternating short updates with re-sampling, DTA stays aligned with the evolving output space, shortens the optimization path, and converges faster to an effective adversarial suffix. Empirically, this pattern matches Table
4
, where increasing the number of re-sampling cycles improves ASR under a fixed compute budget.

\mathbb{I}

When using GPT-4 as the judge, we pass a standardized judge template and the response

\text{ASR}_{O}=\frac{\sum_{R}\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{J}(\text{template},R)\geq 4)}{\#\text{Total Count}}.

(14)

Implementation Details.
In each sampling phase, we sample
N=30
candidate responses from the target LLM. In the optimization phase, we set the reference model under a standard decoding strategy,
e.g.,
a low decoding temperature of
\tau_{eval}=0.7
to ensure stable generation. For each of the
M=20
sampling rounds, the adversarial suffix is optimized for
T=10
iterations using the Adam optimizer. We apply an early stopping condition, terminating the process for a given prompt if a generated response achieves a harmfulness score above 0.9. All experiments are conducted on two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, each with 48 GB of memory.

ttack (DTA), a novel jailbreak method that relies on

In each optimization round, DTA iteratively samples multiple responses and selects the most harmful one as the current round target. Appendix A
Related Work

Here, we introduce six white-box and three black-box methods as our baselines in this paper:

A.1
White-box baselines

GCG

[
6
]
(Greedy Coordinate Gradient) is a foundational white-box attack. It employs a greedy, coordinate descent-based optimization to find an adversarial suffix
S
that, when appended to a harmful prompt
P
, maximizes the probability of the model generating a fixed, predefined target response (e.g., “Sure, here is…”).

COLD-Attack.
To address the limitations of high-perplexity attacks like GCG, Guo et al. (2024) introduced COLD-Attack, a framework that formally reframes controllable jailbreaking as a controllable text generation problem. By adapting the COLD algorithm, this method operates in the continuous logit space rather than on discrete tokens. It defines a composite energy function to unify multiple differentiable constraints, such as attack success, fluency, and semantic similarity. The framework uses Langevin dynamics to sample optimal logits that balance these competing objectives, and then employs a specialized LLM-guided decoding process to ensure the final text is coherent. This unified approach enables the generation of highly stealthy, low-perplexity attacks and introduces novel, controllable scenarios like paraphrasing and position-constrained attacks, which are not achievable by previous methods.

llm-adaptive

[
10
]
is an adaptive methodology for efficiently generating successful jailbreaking attacks against state-of-the-art, safety-aligned large language models. Operating under gray-box or black-box assumptions, its core strategy combines customized, handcrafted prompt templates with simple optimization algorithms, like random search, to iteratively refine attack strings based on model feedback. A key component of its effectiveness is the "self-transfer" technique, where successful attack patterns discovered on one harmful behavior are used to rapidly accelerate the attack on others, and it further exploits model-specific vulnerabilities, such as optimizing against token log-probabilities or leveraging unique API features like prefilling, to bypass safety filters with surprisingly few queries.

I-GCG.
Jia et al.
[
13
]
proposed I-GCG, enhancing optimization-based jailbreaks by addressing the limitations of GCG. They found that GCG’s simple target template (e.g., "Sure, here is…") was insufficient and instead introduced diverse target templates containing harmful self-suggestions (e.g., "my output is harmful") to more effectively mislead the LLM. To improve optimization efficiency, they developed an automatic multi-coordinate updating strategy that adaptively replaces multiple tokens per step, accelerating convergence. Additionally, they employed an easy-to-hard initialization technique, using a suffix generated from a simple malicious query as the starting point for more difficult ones.

AdvPrefix

[
8
]
is an optimization objective used in gradient-based adversarial attacks like GCG to "jailbreak" Large Language Models (LLMs). The goal is to find a short, often nonsensical, string of characters (the "prefix") that, when attached to a user’s prompt, hijacks the model’s output generation. This objective function is optimized to find the prefix that maximizes the probability of the LLM generating a specific, malicious target response (e.g., harmful instructions or hate speech), thereby bypassing its safety alignments.

RLbreaker

[
9
]
is an automated jailbreak prompt generation method that leverages Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to systematically bypass the safety alignments of Large Language Models (LLMs). The system features three components: a DRL agent (attacker) that iteratively generates and refines prompts, a target LLM (victim), and an evaluator LLM (judge) that provides a reward signal based on the attack’s success. During training, the agent continuously modifies a prompt (up to 5 steps) and attacks the target, learning from the evaluator’s feedback through this trial-and-error process to master strategies for creating effective jailbreak prompts.

A.2
Black-box baselines

ReNeLLM.
To address the limitations of manual and optimization-based jailbreaks, Ding et al.
[
18
]
proposed ReNeLLM, an automated framework that leverages LLMs themselves to generate effective and transferable jailbreak prompts. Their method generalizes the attack into two main stages: (1) Prompt Rewriting, which automatically applies a series of linguistic transformations (such as paraphrasing, misspelling sensitive words, or partial translation) to disguise the harmful intent of an initial query, and (2) Scenario Nesting, which embeds the rewritten prompt into a seemingly benign and common instruction-following scenario, such as code completion, table filling, or text continuation. This combined approach is shown to effectively shift the target LLM’s execution priority from safety to usefulness, enabling it to bypass safeguards and achieve high attack success rates on state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 and Claude-2.

PAP.
Zeng et al.
[
19
]
introduce a novel jailbreak methodology, Persuasive Adversarial Prompts (PAPs), which reframes the attack vector by treating LLMs as human-like communicators susceptible to social influence rather than as mere instruction followers. Their approach is systematically grounded in a comprehensive taxonomy of 40 persuasion techniques derived from decades of social science research. The method employs a two-stage framework: first, they train a "Persuasive Paraphraser" by fine-tuning an LLM (e.g., GPT-3.5); second, this trained model is deployed to automatically generate new, human-readable PAPs at scale. The effectiveness of these prompts in bypassing safety alignments is then systematically evaluated across various LLMs, including Llama 2 and the GPT series, using a GPT-4 Judge to classify the harmfulness of the outputs.

TAP.
Mehrotra et al.
[
20
]
propose Tree of Attacks with Pruning (TAP), an automated, black-box method for generating interpretable jailbreaks for LLMs. TAP utilizes an attacker LLM to iteratively generate and refine candidate prompts in a tree structure (branching) and an evaluator LLM to assess them. This process involves two pruning phases: first, pruning off-topic prompts before querying the target, and second, pruning low-scoring prompts after assessing the target’s response.
