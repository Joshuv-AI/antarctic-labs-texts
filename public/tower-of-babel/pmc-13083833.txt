Target-less registration of UAV-LiDAR point clouds based on graph matching of tree locations in forest environments
Authors: Reda Fekry, Eslam Ali, Abubakar Sani-Mohammed, Mohamed Abdallah, Doha Amr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate forest mapping is essential for understanding and managing forest ecosystems. In recent years, Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) technology has become a powerful tool for forest monitoring. However, registering UAV-LiDAR strips remains challenging in GNSS-degraded forest environments due to sparse ground features, canopy occlusion, and limited availability of reliable control points. Existing registration methods often rely on surface-based or feature-plane matching, which can be suboptimal in structurally heterogeneous forests. To address this gap, we present a robust and target-less registration framework for UAVLS data that leverages tree localization as structural anchors. Each detected tree is encoded into a graph using three spatial descriptors: (a) planimetric distance, (b) vertical distance, and (c) height difference relative to neighboring trees. Initial correspondences between trees across overlapping strips are established using the Hungarian algorithm, which maximizes a similarity function derived from these descriptors. Subsequently, a three-dimensional rigid transformation (rotation and translation) is estimated via Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to refine the spatial alignment. The method was validated on eight circular forest plots (30 m in diameter) derived from two overlapping UAVLS strips. The root mean square error (RMSE) of residual distances between registered tree pairs ranged from ±17.6 cm to ±27.4 cm for poplar plots and from ±17.5 cm to ±24.3 cm for dawn blackwood plots. Furthermore, the proposed method improved the tree matching ratio by 17%–27% compared to a baseline approach, demonstrating higher alignment accuracy. By integrating graph-based correspondence with swarm-based optimization, this study contributes a scalable, accurate, and GNSS-independent registration solution tailored to the complexities of forest environments. The framework has strong potential to support UAV-LiDAR-based forest mapping, monitoring, and inventory tasks in both research and operational settings.

Introduction

Forests are a key part of the earth’s critical ecosystems therefore, their continuous monitoring is essential. Initially, field observations are employed to measure the forest parameters at the tree or plot levels. However, their high accuracy is time-consuming and can not be made in danger wildlife1. In contrast, remote sensing techniques particularly LiDAR provide fast data collection with reasonable accuracy in tree parameter estimation2. In addition to high-density data, LiDAR can be installed on different platforms such as airplanes or vehicles. Lately, developments in small-scale technology have facilitated the usage of UAVs as one of LiDAR systems, allowing a distinctive combination of very high-resolution data collection at a relatively lower survey cost3,4.

UAVs have various applications including agriculture, forestry, archaeology, and environment. They are used in forests for assessments of woodlots, fire investigation, vegetation monitoring5, species detection, volume calculation6, and silviculture7. Forest structure, consisting of the spreading and physical appearance of trees, branches, understory, and canopy gaps, has a helpful linkage to numerous ecosystem purposes8. The forest structure and canopy can be measured and modeled using point cloud data from UAVs9. UAVs offer high-quality point clouds of reasonably small areas usually a few hectares. They have a wide field of view and ensure point cloud data with fewer occlusions10.

Similar to earlier photogrammetric and airborne data acquisitions, UAV captures data in adjacent strips. Accordingly, some system-related errors (e.g., trajectory errors) affecting the proper alignment of the whole data are expected11–13. Therefore, optimizing the relative and absolute orientation of the UAV strips14,15 is necessary. This can be done through scan-to-scan geometric registration or strip adjustment10. Combining point cloud data from numerous, co-registered laser scans from the same or several platforms is a well-known pre-processing stage to overcome the restrictions of data obscuration due to laser occlusion16.

Recent advances in UAV-LiDAR technology have enabled the integration of Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) GNSS systems, significantly improving the absolute georeferencing accuracy of collected point clouds. These enhancements often reduce the magnitude of misalignments between overlapping flight strips, potentially simplifying the coregistration process. However, reliance on RTK-based solutions can be challenged by GNSS signal obstructions in dense forest canopies, terrain-induced multipath errors, or degraded signal quality in dense canopy forest. In such cases, residual alignment errors persist, and robust registration methods remain essential. The framework proposed in this study is designed to complement RTK systems, offering high-precision refinement and reliable operation even when absolute positioning is suboptimal. This study addresses the critical challenge of both relative and absolute orientation of UAV-LiDAR strips, which is essential for constructing seamless and spatially accurate point clouds. Our framework offers a robust solution particularly suited to GNSS-denied forest environments, making it relevant not only to forest ecologists and silviculturists, but also to geospatial analysts and mapping professionals working with UAV-based LiDAR data.

Early methods for UAV-LiDAR strip registration relied heavily on external markers and control points to establish positional references.17 utilized building corners to align strips in urban areas, achieving vertical control through flat regions. However, the applicability of this method is limited in dense, natural forests where features are less defined.18 attempted to overcome vertical discrepancies between strips but did not address the horizontal misalignments that are often more significant in forest environments. The method of19, which used least squares matching based on discrete points interpolated into a triangulated network, encountered limitations due to its reliance on interpolation, which can be less effective in forested areas with variable canopy density.

The complexity of forest environments has led to the development of feature-based and marker-less approaches. For instance,10 proposed an iterative closest point (ICP)-based algorithm for airborne laser (ALS) scanning data that corrected for geometric inconsistencies but was constrained by the initial alignment quality.20 explored marker-less terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) data registration using view-invariant feature descriptors and confidence metrics, providing improvements for terrestrial applications but facing challenges when adapted to UAV-LiDAR data due to differences in perspective and data density.21 presented a multi-source approach for integrating TLS, UAV, and photogrammetric data through coarse-to-global registration strategies, incorporating iterative refinement and graph-based adjustments. This method, while robust in mixed-source contexts, accumulated errors during the multi-stage process and required further refinement for full 3D transformations.

Recent advancements have sought to enhance registration accuracy by exploring alternative feature-matching strategies. For example,22 introduced a DEM-ICP-based approach tailored for mountainous terrains, leveraging DEM features to guide the registration process. While effective in certain scenarios, this method encountered challenges in densely vegetated areas, where canopy occlusion hindered reliable feature extraction. Similarly,23 proposed a technique using canopy shape matching and binary image projections, which demonstrated strong performance in structured forests but proved less effective in handling heterogeneous or mixed-species canopies. Another notable approach by24 employed spherical shell point features to align UAV-LiDAR data in non-urban environments, achieving improved performance. However, it struggled with the complexities of diverse forest structures, particularly in accurately localizing trees across varied conditions.

Tree-based registration methods have typically relied on matching tree descriptors built from planimetric and vertical distances. For example,25 focused primarily on 2D transformations and employed heuristic optimization, which yields reasonable alignment in controlled settings but does not generalize well to full 3D transformations or dense canopy conditions. Complementary work on strip offset estimation using linear features26 highlighted persistent challenges in maintaining alignment accuracy where vertical references are sparse.Beyond descriptor matching, clustering-based strategies have been proposed for forest environments.27 registered UAV and terrestrial LiDAR point clouds via hierarchical clustering of trunk points to generate accurate tree maps, demonstrating robustness across forty subtropical plots. At the platform–workflow level,28 integrated a mobile laser scanning (MLS) unit with a forest harvester, using SLAM to detect stems in real time with an initial localization error of 2.44 m that improved to 0.21 m after post-processing. Together, these studies underscore two gaps: (i) existing pipelines often decouple detection/localization from transformation estimation, and (ii) optimization is frequently heuristic, limiting adaptability to complex 3D deformations and heterogeneous stands.

This work addresses these gaps by developing a robust UAV–LiDAR/terrestrial-LiDAR (UAVLS) registration framework grounded in explicit tree localization and graph reasoning. The objectives are threefold: (1) design feature descriptors that jointly encode spatial layout and vertical structure; (2) perform reliable correspondence under canopy variability; and (3) estimate full 3D transformations without external markers. Our main contributions are: Graph-based registration without markers. We introduce a tree-graph formulation that leverages stable inter-tree geometry (e.g., geodesic and angular relationships) to achieve marker-free registration that remains reliable under occlusion and sampling imbalance. Height-aware descriptors for forest structure. We propose descriptors that fuse planimetric spacing with vertical attributes (trunk height profiles), improving distinctiveness relative to purely 2D or heuristic metrics25.

We integrate particle swarm optimization (PSO) into a robust estimation loop to solve for 6-DoF transformations with quantified residuals, replacing ad-hoc heuristics and improving convergence in cluttered scenes. On eight diverse forest plots, the method achieves higher matching ratios and lower residual distances than baseline tree-descriptor and strip-alignment approaches, with consistent improvements in dense canopy and uneven terrain. These advances demonstrate that coupling tree-level semantics with graph structure and principled optimization yields accurate, generalizable 3D registration for forest mapping. The framework has direct utility for inventory, ecological assessment, and long-term environmental monitoring where multi-platform point clouds must be fused. Methodology

The proposed framework, as shown in (see, Fig. 3) performs target-less registration of UAV-LiDAR strips based on the spatial configuration of individual trees. As a first step, ground filtering is applied using the Cloth Simulation Filter (CSF) to separate ground and non-ground points, enabling accurate normalization of point cloud heights and reliable tree height estimation. Tree localization is then performed to detect individual trees from the normalized UAV-LiDAR point clouds, serving as structural anchors for subsequent processing. Next, spatial descriptors, including planimetric distances, vertical separations, and height differences relative to neighboring trees, are computed to characterize each tree’s spatial relationship within its scan. These descriptors are used to form a graph representation in which nodes correspond to trees and edges encode their relative geometry. Tree correspondences across overlapping strips are established using graph matching, formulated as an assignment problem solved via the Hungarian algorithm to maximize structural similarity. Following correspondence identification, transformation parameters (rotation, translation, and scaling) are refined through Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) by minimizing the geometric discrepancy between matched trees. The full workflow is designed to operate without reliance on external control points or GNSS signals, making it particularly effective in forested or GNSS-denied environments. Evaluation metrics such as mean residual distance and matching ratio are employed to quantitatively assess registration performance. Tree localization

Prior to tree detection, ground points were extracted using the Cloth Simulation Filter (CSF) implemented in CloudCompare. This step separates ground and non-ground points to allow accurate tree height estimation. The CSF was parameterized using a cloth resolution of 0.1 m, a classification threshold of 0.5 m, and a maximum of 500 iterations with smoothing enabled. These values were adjusted per plot to ensure robust performance under varying canopy densities and terrain complexities. In plots with dense understory or irregular terrain, manual correction was applied based on visual inspection and slope-corrected terrain models to improve reliability of ground surface reconstruction.

The ground points extracted by CSF were rasterized into a Digital Terrain Model (DTM), while the highest returns within each grid cell formed the Digital Surface Model (DSM). The difference between DSM and DTM was then used to calculate canopy height and localize individual tree tops. This terminology is consistently applied throughout the manuscript to distinguish terrain and surface elevations in accordance with standard LiDAR processing conventions.

To detect individual tree tops, a local maximum filter was applied to the high-resolution DSM (0.25 m grid size), using a minimum peak height of 2 m and a minimum horizontal separation of 1.5 m. These parameters were chosen to ensure accurate apex localization in leaf-on conditions with dense canopy coverage. Tree localization is a critical process in forest mapping, serving as a prerequisite for downstream analyses such as tree modeling, biomass estimation, and forest structure characterization. Accurate identification of individual tree locations is particularly important in complex forest environments, where traditional methods face challenges due to variable canopy densities and overlapping structures. Existing tree segmentation approaches are typically categorized into raster-based, point-based, and hybrid methods, each with its own strengths and limitations. For this study, a point-based segmentation method was chosen to leverage the high-resolution capabilities of UAV-LiDAR data. The UAV-LiDAR data acquisition is conducted over the study area using a GreenValley UAV system, capturing detailed canopy structures from an aerial perspective. The method of31 is employed to segment the single trees and identify the tree locations. The algorithm assumes there is always a gap between adjacent trees and starts building the single trees from the tops. The method is implemented in the GreenValley LiDAR360 software30. The data filtering process began with the removal of outliers, followed by the extraction of ground points. From the filtered dataset, a 0.5 m resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) was generated. The DEM was then used for data normalization, enabling the accurate calculation of individual tree heights. Single tree locations were identified by projecting treetops onto the ground surface. It is important to note that the number of extracted trees from the source and target point clouds may differ due to variations in data collection perspectives, forest characteristics, and overlap ratios. The extracted trees from each plot serve as nodes in the graph-matching process used for strip registration. These localized extractions were used for experimental consistency, but the method remains scalable to full strip-wise datasets.

The optimal matching is determined using the Hungarian Algorithm33, a combinatorial optimization method that guarantees global optimality for the bipartite assignment problem. The algorithm operates with a worst-case computational complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^3)$$\end{document}, where n is the number of nodes (trees). The cost function S to be maximized is defined as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S = \sum _{i \in \mu _N, j \in \mu _M} S(i, j) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}By maximizing S, the algorithm identifies the combination of matches that yields the highest total similarity across all corresponding trees. This ensures that the registration process minimizes ambiguities and provides a consistent mapping between the datasets.

The use of the Hungarian Algorithm is particularly advantageous for this application because it guarantees an optimal solution for the bipartite matching problem while maintaining computational efficiency4. This is critical when dealing with large forest datasets, where the number of potential matches grows exponentially with the size of the input sets. The computational complexity of the Hungarian algorithm used in our framework is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^3)$$\end{document} in the worst case, where n is the number of nodes (trees) involved in the assignment. While this classical formulation guarantees globally optimal matching, it can become computationally expensive with very large datasets. In this study, our point sets were modest in size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \in [20, 85]$$\end{document}), and runtime remained negligible. However, future implementations may consider accelerating the assignment step using spatial filtering techniques. For example, sparsifying the similarity matrix via radius-based gating or k-nearest-neighbor search using a kd-tree structure can reduce the number of edge comparisons, approaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n \log n)$$\end{document} practical performance. Additionally, pre-pruning candidate matches using Delaunay triangulation or geometric hashing could further improve efficiency without compromising matching accuracy. These extensions will be explored in subsequent work to support real-time or large-scale forest applications.

To account for unequal numbers of trees in the source and target datasets, dummy vertices are introduced into the smaller set to convert the bipartite graph into a square matrix. Edges connected to these dummy vertices are assigned a high constant cost (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^6$$\end{document}), ensuring that such assignments are only selected when no real correspondence is viable. This maintains matrix completeness while preventing spurious pairings. The resulting cost matrix is passed to the Hungarian algorithm, which yields a globally optimal one-to-one assignment that maximizes the total similarity score. These initial correspondences form the foundation for the subsequent transformation refinement step using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO).

Transformation estimation

The proposed registration framework operates in two sequential stages: (i) initial correspondence assignment via the Hungarian algorithm and (ii) refinement of transformation parameters using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). In the first stage, edge-weighted graphs are constructed using similarity descriptors such as planimetric distance, height difference, and elevation offset. The Hungarian algorithm then identifies a globally optimal one-to-one assignment from the cost matrix, which includes dummy vertices with high edge costs to handle unmatched nodes and ensure graph completeness.These correspondences obtained from the Hungarian algorithm serve as the initialization for the PSO stage. While the Hungarian method ensures an optimal discrete assignment of tree pairs, it does not directly account for geometric alignment in 3D space. Therefore, PSO solves a continuous optimization problem to estimate the rigid transformation (rotation and translation) that minimizes spatial discrepancies between matched trees. This division of tasks allows the framework to effectively combine logical correspondence matching with precise spatial refinement, thereby improving registration accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency, particularly in complex forest environments.

The one-to-one correspondence set between source and target tree locations cannot be guaranteed to contain only true matches. To address this, an optimal 3D transformation is employed. This involves estimating rotation parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{xyz}$$\end{document} and translation vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{xyz}$$\end{document} that minimize misalignment errors. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)34 is employed as an effective strategy to optimize these parameters. PSO is particularly suitable for this task as it efficiently handles non-linear, non-differentiable optimization problems without requiring gradient-based solutions. Unlike traditional optimization techniques, PSO uses a population of particles, each representing a potential solution, to explore the search space. These particles iteratively update their positions based on both global and local best solutions, ensuring a balance between exploration (global search) and exploitation (local refinement). This makes PSO highly adaptable to the complex and multi-dimensional nature of UAV-LiDAR registration.

The optimization begins with the initialization of a swarm of particles, where each particle represents a candidate set of transformation parameters. For each iteration, particles evaluate their performance by computing the alignment error, defined as the Euclidean distance between corresponding tree locations in the source and target datasets. Outliers are identified using threshold-based filtering and excluded to ensure the reliability of the optimization process.

The swarm consisted of 30 particles, optimized over 100 iterations. An early stopping criterion was enforced when the RMSE between corresponding tree locations fell below 0.01 m. This configuration yielded consistent convergence across all test scenes, balancing speed and accuracy while handling non-differentiable spatial alignment objectives. This step minimizes the influence of erroneous matches, which could otherwise skew the results. The root mean square error (RMSE) is used as the primary metric to evaluate transformation accuracy. By iteratively refining particle positions and selecting the global best parameters, the algorithm ensures convergence to an optimal solution. The integration of outlier filtering further enhances the robustness of the optimization, enabling accurate alignment even in dense forest environments with complex canopy structures. Experimental design

Eight forest plots were selected to evaluate the performance of the proposed UAV-LiDAR registration framework. The tree locations from the source and target point clouds were matched and analyzed using the described methodology. This section outlines the critical pre-processing steps, parameter setup, and accuracy assessment protocols, ensuring a robust and standardized evaluation process.

Data pre-processing

The pre-processing stage plays a pivotal role in ensuring that the input data for the registration process is accurate and reliable. This stage focused on refining the UAV-LiDAR point cloud data using a structured workflow implemented with CloudCompare software35. The following steps were performed: First, outlier removal was conducted based on statistical analysis, where noise points and spurious data were systematically excluded to retain only meaningful and high-quality points. This step significantly enhanced the clarity and reliability of the dataset, ensuring that only valid data points contributed to the registration process. Next, ground filtering was applied to separate ground points from tree structures. This filtering was essential to accurately normalize tree heights and focus the analysis on non-ground features, particularly the trees. The filtered points were subsequently used for tree localization, matching, and registration. Additionally, a DEM was generated at a 0.5-meter resolution to normalize tree heights within each plot. This ensured that tree height descriptors were consistent across all datasets, allowing fair and accurate comparisons during the registration process. The effectiveness of the pre-processing is illustrated in Fig. 4, which shows the transformation of tree locations before and after registration. These steps established a standardized and robust dataset, enabling consistent and accurate analysis in the subsequent stages of the methodology.

Parameter setup

The success of the proposed framework heavily relies on the proper configuration of its key parameters. In this study, the kernel bandwidths controlling the similarity computations for planimetric distance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _{xy}$$\end{document}), elevation difference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _{z}$$\end{document}), and height difference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _{H}$$\end{document}) were carefully tuned to 0.568, 1.24, and 0.73, respectively. These values were selected based on preliminary evaluations and the characteristics of the forest plots, ensuring a balance between sensitivity to tree attributes and robustness to noise or variability in the data. Moreover, a transformation threshold of 1 meter was applied during the registration process to identify corresponding tree pairs. This threshold was chosen to represent the maximum allowable deviation between matched tree locations while ensuring high precision in alignment. These parameters were instrumental in optimizing the performance of the similarity function and the subsequent registration steps, tailoring the framework to effectively handle the diversity in forest conditions.

Accuracy assessment

The evaluation of the proposed framework was carried out across eight forest plots representing diverse structural characteristics, enabling a robust assessment of its generalization. Metrics used included RMSE for residual alignment error and the matching ratio between predicted and reference trees. A baseline method using coarse centroid-based ICP was implemented for comparison. The improvement in both accuracy and correspondence completeness demonstrates the effectiveness of our graph- and optimization-based strategy, particularly in scenarios with spatial variability and occlusion.

Three complementary metrics were used to quantitatively assess the registration performance, capturing different aspects of spatial alignment between tree locations in the source and target datasets as follows:

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) quantifies the average deviation between matched tree locations. It places a higher weight on larger deviations, making it sensitive to significant misalignments. The RMSE is calculated using the equation:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum _{i=1}^{n} (x_i - x_t)^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_t$$\end{document} represent the coordinates of the matched tree pairs in the source and target datasets, respectively.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) measures the average magnitude of alignment errors without considering their direction. It provides a straightforward metric for understanding the overall precision of the registration and is computed as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} MAE = \frac{1}{n} \sum _{i=1}^{n} |x_i - x_t|. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Matching Ratio evaluates the proportion of successfully matched tree pairs with a Euclidean distance below the defined threshold (1 meter). It reflects the effectiveness of the algorithm in identifying true correspondences between tree locations.

By combining these metrics, the framework’s performance was evaluated from multiple perspectives, ensuring that both large-scale errors and finer alignment deviations were addressed. These metrics not only validate the accuracy of the registration method but also provide insights into its applicability across different forest plots with varying conditions.

Results and discussion

Eight forest plots, encompassing diverse tree species, ages, stand densities, and distributions, were selected to evaluate the proposed framework. The experimental design consisted of three sequential phases: (a) tree segmentation and tree localization, (b) graph matching of tree locations, and (c) transformation estimation. Tree localization

Single tree segmentation was performed on both source and target scans for each plot, with results summarized in Table 1. Dawn redwood plots, particularly those with lower canopy densities, demonstrated high consistency in the localization of corresponding tree features between the source and target datasets. This consistency can be attributed to the relatively simple and uniform crown structures of dawn redwood species, as previously noted in segmentation algorithms31. The distinct and well-defined treetop patterns facilitated clear tree identification, resulting in precise initial counts and improving subsequent registration accuracy. Conversely, high-density coniferous plots, such as Plot 6, presented notable challenges. The densely packed canopies in these plots exacerbated the difficulty of tree localization due to the segmentation algorithm’s inherent assumption of clear spacing between adjacent trees. In dense configurations, branch overlaps and crown interconnectivity reduce the algorithm’s ability to delineate individual trees accurately. This misidentification impacts not only the initial tree count but also the alignment and matching of tree features during the registration process.

Plots dominated by deciduous species, such as poplar and Zelkova serrata, exhibited additional variability in tree segmentation results. Specifically, high-density deciduous plots like Plot 3 faced complications due to the diffuse and interconnected canopy structures characteristic of these species. Zelkova serrata, in particular, presented segmentation challenges due to ambiguous treetop patterns, where overlapping crowns hindered the algorithm’s ability to define distinct maximum points. As described by31, segmentation models rely on clear maxima to differentiate individual trees. In cases where such maxima are obscured, segmentation performance is significantly degraded.

Medium-density plots, both deciduous (e.g., Plots 2 and 8) and coniferous (e.g., Plot 4), reflected more balanced results between the source and target tree counts. The relatively moderate spacing in these plots allowed for clearer segmentation without the extremes of low-density (which risk over-segmentation) or high-density settings (which risk under-segmentation). As a result, these medium-density plots achieved higher matching ratios and improved alignment outcomes during the registration phase. Overall, the analysis of tree localization across different plots highlights that while the proposed method performs well in low- and medium-density settings with clear canopy separation, it encounters limitations in high-density environments. The performance in coniferous plots with uniform canopies, such as Plot 6, underscores the need for potential adaptations in the algorithm to better handle overlapping structures. Similarly, the ambiguous treetops of deciduous species, particularly in dense settings, further emphasize the challenges of accurately segmenting trees and aligning point clouds. Conclusions

This study presents a robust and comprehensive framework for UAV-LiDAR registration, leveraging individual tree locations as natural anchor points. By integrating advanced feature descriptors encompassing planimetric, vertical, and height differences, along with the Hungarian algorithm for optimal tree correspondence, the framework addresses key challenges in forest data alignment. The transformation process, refined through Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), significantly improves the precision of 3D rigid transformations, overcoming limitations inherent in traditional heuristic methods.

The framework’s efficacy was validated across eight distinct forest plots varying in tree species, ages, and densities. The observed mean residual distances—42.5 cm for poplar, 31.2 cm for dawn redwood, and 68.5 cm for Zelkova serrata—underscore its robust performance in medium-density coniferous and deciduous plots with well-defined canopies. The incorporation of tree height into feature descriptors, coupled with PSO’s iterative optimization, proved critical for achieving accurate registration, particularly in complex forest environments, and marks a substantial improvement over baseline methods.

While the proposed method consistently outperformed the baseline, certain limitations were identified. Dense deciduous plots, such as those containing Zelkova serrata, posed challenges due to ambiguous and overlapping canopies, highlighting the need for more advanced segmentation techniques. Additionally, the computational demands of PSO, while yielding high accuracy, may constrain its applicability to large-scale or real-time forest assessments. Future exploration into hybrid optimization strategies could mitigate these limitations and improve computational efficiency.

The proposed PSO-based registration framework represents a significant advancement in UAV-LiDAR data registration for forest analysis. Its ability to align point cloud data with high precision sets a new benchmark for applications in forest monitoring, biodiversity studies, and environmental management. With continued development and validation, this framework holds the potential to become a critical tool for ecological studies and sustainable forest management, paving the way for enhanced accuracy, scalability, and adaptability in UAV-LiDAR registration.

To further extend its capabilities, future efforts should validate the framework on datasets from diverse forest types, including natural forests with heterogeneous structural characteristics and complex understory vegetation. Expanding the scope to encompass tropical, temperate, and boreal forests could provide deeper insights into the method’s adaptability and uncover region- or species-specific modifications to optimize performance. Additionally, incorporating supplementary tree attributes, such as crown diameter and canopy shape, could further improve its efficacy in challenging environments, underscoring the method’s potential to handle increasingly complex forest scenarios effectively. This study presents a proof-of-concept evaluation of a graph-based registration framework using data from eight UAV-LiDAR plots. While these plots were selected to reflect a variety of forest types and structural conditions, we acknowledge that the relatively small dataset limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research will focus on large-scale validation across diverse geographic locations, tree species compositions, and seasonal variations to more comprehensively assess the robustness and applicability of the proposed method in operational forest monitoring contexts. Additionally, the present study was conducted under leaf-on conditions, which ensured dense canopy surfaces and reliable treetop detection. However, we recognize that under leaf-off conditions, particularly in deciduous forests, the canopy structure may be sparse, potentially reducing the reliability of apex identification. Future developments will investigate adaptive treetop detection strategies that leverage structural cues such as branching patterns or stem density to enhance robustness under varying phenological states. Although the proposed method demonstrates high registration accuracy suitable for general forest mapping and canopy-level monitoring, it is not optimized for applications that necessitate millimeter-level precision, such as tree stem diameter measurement. Future integration with complementary high-resolution ground-based LiDAR may bridge this gap for multi-scale forestry applications.
