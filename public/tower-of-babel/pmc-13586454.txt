Rapidly Progressive Gastric Poorly Differentiated Neuroendocrine Carcinoma Mimicking Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor: A Case Report
Authors: Yonghoon Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoorly differentiated gastric neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) represents a rare but highly aggressive malignancy, typically diagnosed at advanced stages, which are associated with poor prognosis. We present a case of a 71-year-old male with a history of distal gastrectomy who developed recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding from a subepithelial gastric mass. The lesion initially measured approximately 2 cm and demonstrated endoscopic and radiological features reminiscent of gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST). Repeated endoscopic biopsies indicated weak c-KIT and DOG1 positivity; however, the mass enlarged rapidly to 8 cm within 2 months, accompanied by multiple hepatic metastases. Ultrasound-guided liver biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of poorly differentiated gastric NEC. Cisplatin-based palliative chemotherapy was initiated, but the patient died within 3 weeks due to persistent hemorrhage and rapid clinical decline. Although uncommon, gastric NEC may closely mimic GIST both clinically and pathologically. Accurate differentiation between these entities is essential, given their markedly divergent prognoses and therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Stomach neoplasms, Carcinoma, Gastrointestinal stromal tumor, Neuroendocrine proto-oncogene proteins c-kit

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract, accounting for approximately 80% of clinically relevant GI mesenchymal tumors [1]. In the stomach, GISTs typically appear as subepithelial lesions (SELs) with smooth, dome-shaped surfaces on esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and are characteristically positive for c-kit (CD117) and DOG1 on immunohistochemistry, reflecting their origin from the interstitial cells of Cajal [2].

Gastric neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) comprise a heterogeneous group of tumors classified by the 2022 World Health Organization and current ENETS guidelines into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NET G1, G2, and G3) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NEC), which are further subclassified into small cell NEC and large cell NEC, as well as mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms [3,4]. While gastric NETs (particularly type 1 and type 2) are generally indolent, poorly differentiated NEC is highly aggressive, characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and poor prognosis despite systemic chemotherapy [5].

The distinction between gastric GIST and NEC has important prognostic and therapeutic implications but can be challenging when NEC presents as a SEL with equivocal immunohistochemical findings. Several reports have described gastric NETs mimicking GISTs on EGD and imaging [6,7]. In addition, DOG1, traditionally regarded as a highly specific marker for GIST, has been reported to be aberrantly expressed in gastrointestinal NENs, posing a potential diagnostic pitfall [8].

Here, we report a case of a 71-year-old man with poorly differentiated gastric NEC that initially presented with GIST-like endoscopic and immunohistochemical features, resulting in delayed diagnosis and ultimately fatal outcome. This case highlights the importance of pursuing definitive tissue diagnosis in rapidly growing gastric SELs with atypical features, regardless of initial immunohistochemical results.

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old male presented to the emergency department of an outside hospital with melena. His medical history was significant for hypertension and a distal gastrectomy with gastrojejunal anastomosis performed approximately 50 years prior for a perforated gastric ulcer. He had no other notable medical history and no family history of malignancy. EGD performed on the day of admission revealed a SEL measuring approximately 2 cm near the gastrojejunal anastomosis, with a central ulceration identified as the likely bleeding source (Fig. 1A and B). Hemostatic interventions including endoscopic clip ligation were performed on the day of admission and on hospital day 3 (Fig. 1C and D); however, bleeding persisted. Due to refractory hemorrhage, an exploratory laparotomy for bleeding control was performed on hospital day 3, after which the patient was stabilized and discharged. CT of the abdomen at that time confirmed a 2 cm SEL without evidence of distant metastasis (Fig. 2A). The initial endoscopic findings were not typical of a specific disease entity, and differential considerations included gastric ulcer, gastric adenocarcinoma, gastric lymphoma, and SELs, including GIST. Based on the combined endoscopic and CT findings, a bleeding GIST was considered the most probable diagnosis. Because a definitive histologic diagnosis had not been established at the time of surgery, the patient was hemodynamically unstable due to hemorrhagic shock from gastric bleeding, and severe adhesions from the previous gastrectomy were encountered intraoperatively, curative tumor resection was not attempted and only bleeding control was performed. Short-term follow-up was therefore planned after the bleeding had resolved and the patient had stabilized clinically. The patient re-presented to the same outside hospital in one month after discharge with recurrent melena. Repeat EGD and CT imaging revealed dramatic interval growth of the mass to approximately 5 cm (Fig. 2B). Endoscopic biopsy specimens demonstrated some atypical cells but were not diagnostically conclusive. The patient was subsequently referred to our institution for further evaluation and management.

CT findings at initial presentation (A) and 1-month follow-up (B). The mass lesion demonstrated rapid interval growth, measuring approximately 2 cm and 5 cm at each respective time point (white circles).

At our institution, repeat EGD 2 months after the initial presentation revealed a large, ulcerated SEL at the anastomosis site, with endoscopic features suggestive of either a gastric GIST or a rapidly growing advanced gastric carcinoma (Fig. 3A and B). Targeted biopsies for histologic diagnosis were performed at the interface between the ulcer base and margin of the ulcerated lesion. Repeat endoscopic biopsies were performed at 1-week intervals, targeting the interface between the ulcer base and margin to minimize the risk of false-negative results. Four and six tissue specimens were obtained during the first and second procedures, respectively, all of which were considered adequate for assessment. Histology revealed atypical cells with weak positivity for both c-kit (CD117) and DOG1 on immunohistochemistry, raising the possibility of GIST (Fig. 4A-C); however, the findings were not considered conclusive for a definitive diagnosis. Follow-up CT performed 2 months after the initial presentation confirmed that the mass measured approximately 8 cm and was accompanied by multiple hepatic metastases (Fig. 3C).

A and B: The mass lesion near the gastrojejunostomy had markedly increased in size and appeared to measure at least 5 cm, with notably deeper central ulceration compared to the initial presentation (white circles). C: On the 2-month follow-up CT scan, the mass had increased in size to approximately 8 cm, with multiple hepatic metastases becoming evident (yellow arrowhead).

Histopathological findings from endoscopic biopsy specimens (A-C) and ultrasound-guided liver biopsy specimens (D-F). A: Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the endoscopic biopsy revealed poorly differentiated atypical cells lacking the typical spindle cell morphology seen in GIST (×580). B and C: Immunohistochemistry showed weak positivity for both c-kit (B) and DOG1 (C), raising the initial suspicion of GIST (red arrowheads) (B, ×620; C, ×480). D: Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the liver biopsy specimen demonstrated clusters of poorly differentiated malignant tumor cells distinct from the surrounding normal hepatocytes, suggestive of metastatic neuroendocrine carcinoma; distinction between small cell type and large cell type was difficult (×630). E: Immunohistochemistry confirmed strong diffuse positivity for synaptophysin (×350). F: Ki-67 immunostaining was positive in up to 90% of tumor cells (×330).

Given the diagnostic uncertainty and the presence of hepatic metastases amenable to biopsy, after two non-diagnostic endoscopic biopsies, an ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was ultimately performed. Histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry of the liver biopsy specimen confirmed the diagnosis of poorly differentiated NEC (Fig. 4D-F). The patient was initiated on palliative chemotherapy with a cisplatin–etoposide regimen approximately 3 months after the initial presentation. However, persistent gastrointestinal hemorrhage and rapid clinical deterioration ensued, the patient could not complete the first cycle and died approximately three weeks after the initiation of chemotherapy.

DISCUSSION

SELs of the stomach represent a broad differential diagnosis, including GIST, leiomyoma, schwannoma, ectopic pancreas, and less commonly, NENs. Among these, GIST is by far the most frequent and is typically diagnosed based on strong positivity for c-kit (CD117) and/or DOG1 on immunohistochemistry [1,2]. The diagnosis of GIST in the setting of a gastric SET with c-kit or DOG1 positivity is therefore often made with relative confidence. Gastric NETs, however, can occasionally present as SELs without the classic yellowish mucosal changes or umbilicated surface typically described for well-differentiated type 1 gastric NETs, and may therefore be clinically indistinguishable from GIST on EGD or CT [6].

In 2021, Lee and Oh [6] reported a case of a gastric NET presenting as a 2.3 cm homogeneous, hypoechoic SET on endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) without typical NET surface features, which was initially presumed to be a GIST and ultimately diagnosed only after surgical resection. Similarly, Lee et al. [7]. described a gastric spindle cell NET that mimicked GIST on histomorphology, with spindle-shaped tumor cells that were initially attributed to a mesenchymal neoplasm before immunohistochemistry for neuroendocrine markers (chromogranin, synaptophysin) established the correct diagnosis. Although the number of reported cases is limited and most involve NET rather than NEC, these reports suggest that gastric NENs can closely mimic GIST in their gross endoscopic and radiologic appearances.

A particularly challenging aspect of the present case was the weak positivity for both c-kit (CD117) and DOG1 in the gastric biopsy specimens, which directed the diagnostic workup toward GIST rather than NEC. While c-kit positivity is the hallmark of GIST and is found in over 90% of cases [2], CD117 expression has been documented in various other neoplasms, including NETs. Equally important, recent evidence has demonstrated that DOG1—long regarded as a highly specific marker for GIST—can be expressed in NENs of the gastrointestinal tract. A recent study systematically evaluated DOG1 expression in a series of NENs and found statistically significant DOG1 expression in gastrointestinal tract NETs, concluding that DOG1 positivity in a GI neoplasm should not be reflexively interpreted as evidence of GIST [8]. Additionally, Jansen et al. [9] demonstrated that DOG1 immunostaining is not entirely specific for GIST, being detected in 66 other tumor types, including colonic NEC and diffuse-type gastric adenocarcinoma. Although DOG1 is regarded as a highly sensitive and specific marker for GIST, its expression should be interpreted in the context of the overall histomorphologic findings. In the present case, weak co-positivity for c-kit and DOG1 in the gastric biopsy specimen containing atypical tumor cells initially raised suspicion for GIST, potentially delaying the establishment of the correct diagnosis. However, the presence of poorly differentiated tumor cells rather than the characteristic spindle-cell morphology of GIST prompted further diagnostic evaluation, and a liver biopsy was subsequently performed to establish a definitive diagnosis. The possibility of synchronous GIST and NEC, while extremely rare, has also been described [10]; however, this was ultimately not the case in our patient. The immunohistochemical hallmarks that would have supported a neuroendocrine diagnosis—synaptophysin, chromogranin A, CD56, and a high Ki-67 index—were not adequately assessable from the small, superficial endoscopic biopsy specimens, highlighting the limitations of endoscopic biopsy for SELs where the neoplastic component lies beneath an intact mucosal surface.

Retrospectively, several features in this case should have raised suspicion for a diagnosis other than GIST. First, the rate of tumor growth was extraordinary: the lesion expanded from approximately 2 cm to 8 cm within 2 months, accompanied by the development of multiple hepatic metastases. While highrisk GISTs can metastasize, such rapid interval growth from a 2 cm lesion to an 8 cm lesion with liver metastasis is far more consistent with a high-grade malignancy such as NEC. Poorly differentiated gastric NEC is characterized pathologically by a high mitotic rate and Ki-67 index exceeding 20%, reflecting an extremely rapid proliferative capacity [3,4]. Studies on gastric NEC have highlighted its tendency to exhibit deep ulceration, extensive necrosis, and surface hemorrhage, features that reflect its highly aggressive and invasive biological behavior. These findings are in line with the present case, which demonstrated rapid progression accompanied by recurrent severe gastrointestinal bleeding [11]. The response to repeated endoscopic biopsies yielding only “atypical cells” without definitive diagnosis also should have prompted earlier escalation to alternative biopsy strategies. Given the subepithelial nature of the lesion, superficial endoscopic forceps biopsies are prone to sampling only the overlying non-neoplastic mucosa or necrotic tumor surface. In such cases, EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration/biopsy or, when distant metastases are present, image-guided biopsy of a metastatic site should be considered earlier in the diagnostic algorithm. In this case, the definitive diagnosis was ultimately achieved only by ultrasound-guided liver biopsy, which was performed approximately 2 months after the patient’s initial presentation with melena—a delay that precluded timely initiation of appropriate therapy.

Although platinum-based chemotherapy with 4–6 cycles of cisplatin or carboplatin plus etoposide remains the recommended first-line treatment for advanced gastric NEC, the prognosis remains poor, with a reported median overall survival of approximately 11–12 months despite systemic therapy [4,5,12]. In the present patient, cisplatin-based chemotherapy was initiated, but he died within 3 weeks. Given the aggressive biology and advanced stage of NEC at diagnosis, the overall outcome may not have been substantially different even if the diagnosis had been established earlier. However, the initial suspicion of GIST complicated the diagnostic process and may have delayed discussions regarding prognosis and treatment expectations. As NEC and GIST differ markedly in both prognosis and management, accurate diagnosis is important for appropriate clinical decision-making and patient counseling.

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing gastric NEC from GIST. Although uncommon, NEC should be considered in rapidly progressive SELs, particularly when accompanied by recurrent bleeding or early metastasis. Because NEC carries a substantially poorer prognosis and requires different therapeutic strategies than GIST, establishing an accurate diagnosis through adequate tissue acquisition is essential.
