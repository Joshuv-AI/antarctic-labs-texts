Roadmap of remote photoplethysmography from heart rate measurement toward clinical translation
Authors: Mohamed Elgendi, Shuo Li, Carlo Menon, Edmund Y. Lam, Basem Al-Omari, Stefan Borik, Kirk H. Shelley, Aymen Alian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) enables non-contact heart rate (HR) and waveform measurement from facial video, offering advantages over contact methods while contending with motion, illumination changes, compression, and frame-rate variability. This roadmap integrates a systematic review, technical synthesis, and a clinic-acquired demonstration to accelerate rPPG research and translation. We outline the measurement pipeline and synthesize progress across eight domains: datasets, device factors, data scarcity, skin/ROI detection, model- and data-driven algorithms, filtering, and evaluation metrics. Strategies for improved robustness include targeted dataset expansion, augmentation, optimized ROI policies, motion and photometric normalization, and hardware configuration. A proof-of-feasibility using data collected in a routine clinical setting alongside reference monitors demonstrates high signal-level agreement under a multi-metric framework (correlation/concordance, absolute errors, and Bland–Altman bias/dispersion). We conclude with recommendations for accuracy, efficiency, and deployment in real-world and clinical contexts, providing a consolidated resource for researchers and clinicians and a pragmatic path toward reliable clinical adoption.

Introduction

In recent years, with increasing public awareness of physical health, physiological signal monitoring technologies have received growing attention. Such technologies can facilitate the measurement of various physiological indicators, enabling health status assessment and proactive management. Among vital physiological indicators, heart rate (HR) holds particular significance. It serves as a valuable measure of fatigue levels1, emotional state2, and body functions3. Consequently, improving the accuracy and efficiency of HR measurement under different circumstances has become a key research focus in this field.

The widely accepted gold standard4,5 for HR measurement is the electrocardiogram (ECG). As shown in Fig. 2, ECG signals are recorded by placing electrodes on specific body locations and connecting wires to the recording machine6. Each heartbeat induces myocardial depolarization, altering skin surface potentials that can be captured by electrodes and amplified into ECG signals. A typical ECG waveform contains three main components (P wave, QRS complex, and T wave), each reflecting specific phases of cardiac electrical activity7,8. Since ECG signals contain abundant cardiac information, clinicians can evaluate HR, cardiac rhythm, PR interval, QT interval, and other aspects by analyzing the ECG waveform in order to determine the physical condition of the patient and potential heart diseases9–11. However, the need for professional setup of traditional 12-lead ECG restricts its suitability for long-term monitoring outside clinical settings. Ambulatory ECG techniques such as Holter monitors or adhesive patch recorders have been developed to enable continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring, and ECG-enabled smartwatches allow users to obtain recordings without professional guidance. Nevertheless, all these approaches still rely on contact-based devices for signal acquisition, which could impose burdens on daily activities.

Contact-based photoplethysmography (cPPG) offers a more convenient alternative by detecting blood volume changes through optical sensors placed on the skin12. A typical setup for cPPG signal acquisition includes an LED light source and a photosensitive sensor. The light emitted by the LED is absorbed by blood vessels during the process of penetrating through the skin and tissue13. Variations in arterial blood flow caused by the rhythmic heartbeat lead to changes in the absorption intensity over time. The photosensitive sensor captures these periodic changes in light intensity and then converts them into an electronic signal, which is the resulting PPG signal and is also called blood volume pulse (BVP) signal. As presented in Fig. 2, cPPG techniques are widely used in smartwatches (reflective) and fingertip oximeters (transmissive)14. Compared with ECG, cPPG devices are easier to operate but provide less clinical information, making them suitable mainly for consumer health monitoring.

While contact-based methods such as ECG and cPPG have been widely used for HR monitoring, their reliance on skin contact can cause discomfort, limit long-term wearability, and restrict applications in certain scenarios such as telemedicine or large-scale public health screening. This limitation has motivated the development of fully non-contact alternatives. Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) techniques were proposed to meet this need by estimating HR from videos of exposed skin regions, where blood flow induces subtle color variations that can be analyzed to extract the blood volume pulse (BVP) signal. The earliest exploration of imaging-based photoplethysmography was conducted by Wu, Blažek, and Schmitt in 200015, who demonstrated that periodic changes in subcutaneous blood flow could produce measurable intensity variations in reflected light. Using a high-sensitivity video camera, they visualized dermal perfusion in a completely non-contact manner, laying the groundwork for later developments in rPPG. Building on this concept, Verkruysse et al.16 extended the approach to facial videos illuminated by ambient light, marking the emergence of modern rPPG.

Since then, the field has rapidly evolved, with researchers developing increasingly sophisticated methods and frameworks to improve accuracy and robustness in HR estimation17,18. Recent advances have expanded beyond algorithmic optimization to address fairness19, regional signal contributions20,21, privacy-preserving signal removal22, clinical translation through healthcare kiosks23, and improved signal quality assessment24. Collectively, these developments highlight the rapid maturation of rPPG from experimental validation to real-world healthcare applications.

rPPG pipeline

A typical rPPG-based HR estimation pipeline comprises several key steps (Fig. 2). First, a facial video is acquired using a standard camera. Next, the region of interest (ROI) is automatically identified; the forehead and cheeks are the most commonly chosen ROIs25,26. Pixel values within these regions are then converted into red, green, and blue (RGB) traces, from which blood volume pulse (BVP) signals are derived using established algorithms. Before and/or after BVP extraction, temporal and spectral filtering can be leveraged to improve signal quality. HR is then estimated from the BVP using frequency-domain analysis27, peak detection28, Hilbert transform29, or machine-learning methods that extract higher-order features30. A windowing operation is often employed to select specific time segments during this process31–33. Finally, standard performance metrics are used to evaluate the estimated BVP and HR signals against the ground truth.Fig. 2General pipeline for the use of rPPG algorithms in HR measurement and algorithm performance validation.RGB (red, green, and blue); ECG (electrocardiogram); cPPG (contact-based photoplethysmography); rPPG (remote photoplethysmography); HR (heart rate); BPM (beats per minute); ROI (region of interest); BVP (blood volume pulse). ECG signals are acquired via electrocardiographs. Datasets

Throughout the evolution of rPPG techniques, several challenges related to input videos have emerged as central concerns. First and foremost, effectively mitigating motion artifacts remains a critical issue. Different types of subject movement, such as talking, head rotation, and nodding, introduce perturbations in skin pixel illumination, resulting in reduced measurement accuracy. Second, achieving robust performance under varying illumination conditions presents another major challenge. Ideal lighting conditions, characterized by the proper placement of cameras34,35, uniformly diffused facial illumination, and stable, moderate light intensity, are often unattainable in real-world applications. Datasets constructed for the development of rPPG techniques should serve as evaluation platforms to assess algorithm performance across a broad range of measurement scenarios. Moreover, demographic factors such as gender, age, and skin tone require careful consideration, as the underlying anatomical and physiological differences may potentially affect the measurement accuracy. In sum, during the formulation of an rPPG dataset, researchers ought to consider these facets and aim to enhance the representativeness of data distributions to better reflect real-world conditions. Several research groups have created and released rPPG datasets tailored to specific objectives. These public datasets serve as valuable resources for both algorithm development and performance validation. In 2011, the multimodal database for affect recognition and implicit tagging (MAHNOB-HCI) was introduced36. It became the first public dataset widely utilized for assessing the performance of rPPG techniques. However, as it was primarily designed for multimedia tagging research, the MAHNOB-HCI dataset was not specifically constructed for rPPG algorithm evaluation. In 2014, Stricker et al.37 proposed the PURE dataset, which was used for pulse rate measurement on a mobile service robot. The VicarPPG dataset38 was released in the same year. These two datasets provide various motion types to facilitate rPPG algorithm validation across a broad range of scenarios.

To support evaluation under different emotional states and skin tones, Zhang et al.39 released the MMSE-HR dataset in 2016. In the same year, the COHFACE dataset40 was introduced to promote reproducible research in rPPG techniques; it was collected under two lighting conditions: studio light and natural light. In 2017, the UBFC-rPPG dataset was constructed to assess an unsupervised skin tissue segmentation method41. In 2018, following the development of the local group invariance (LGI) algorithm42, the LGI-PPGI dataset was made publicly available. Additionally, the VIPL-HR43 and ECG-fitness44 datasets were also released to the public. The VIPL-HR dataset includes visible light videos of 107 subjects. To our knowledge, it is the largest public rPPG dataset, comprising 2378 videos across nine measurement conditions. The ECG-fitness database was created to comprise recordings of various physical activities on fitness machines. It was originally used to evaluate the performance of a convolutional neural network (CNN) for HR measurement in gym settings.

In 2020, the VicarPPG2 dataset45 was introduced to expand the evaluation scope of rPPG technologies to a wider variety of subject motions. Nevertheless, datasets specifically designed to assess rPPG algorithms under different light intensities had remained notably absent. To address this gap, Yang et al.46 created the BH-rPPG dataset in 2021, which includes recordings at three distinct illumination levels. Moreover, in 2022, Xi et al.47 proposed the BUAA-MIHR dataset, defining 11 illumination levels through quantitative illuminance measurements. A more comprehensive dataset comprising multiple application domains across various skin tones, subject movement, illumination conditions, and video resolutions, namely the multi-domain mobile video physiology dataset (MMPD), was proposed by Tang et al.48 in 2023. It was constructed based on common measurement scenarios of rPPG defined in previous studies. The most recent contribution is the imaging blood volume pulse (iBVP) dataset that was released by Joshi et al.49 in 2024. The dataset construction offers novel insights from two perspectives: first, the ground truth cPPG signals were recorded from the ear, which are more synchronized with the facial rPPG signals; the other is the per-sample signal quality labels provided by the dataset to discard noisy segments of ground truth and video sequences.

Overall, existing public datasets have covered quite a few common scenarios, such as offices and fitness environments. It is worth noting that other domains such as remote medical consultation, in-vehicle monitoring, and gaming sessions, hold considerable commercial value but remain underexplored50. As exhibited in Fig. 3, most public datasets are not freely available to the research community. With the consideration regarding subject privacy and the risk of data misuse, applicants are typically required to possess academic qualifications and contact the data distributor. For datasets with stricter access requirements, applicants may also need to sign an end-user license agreement (EULA) to formalize the application process. Currently, only the LGI-PPGI42 and UBFC-Phys51 datasets can be directly accessed via public online resources.

Device-related factors

In the context of rPPG algorithms, the method of data acquisition typically involves capturing facial videos using standard RGB cameras. Webcams are commonly employed due to their practicality and affordability, as opposed to more advanced cameras that are often prohibitively expensive. As shown in Fig. 3, the UBFC-rPPG, LGI-PPGI, COHFACE, BUAA-MIHR, VIPL-HR, ECG-fitness, VicarPPG-2, and BH-rPPG datasets selected webcams of the Logitech series, which have also been widely used in other self-constructed datasets. Moreover, the landscape of video recording tools has expanded with the advent of mobile HR monitoring, prompting researchers to utilize the integrated cameras of smartphones for facial video acquisition52. For example, both the VIPL-HR and MMPD datasets employ standard smartphone cameras to record facial video sequences. In comparison to webcams, using smartphones for video collection introduces additional challenges. Mobile phone cameras often incorporate built-in algorithms and filters aimed at enhancing facial aesthetics. However, such enhancements may introduce unintended perturbations that affect video authenticity, making it necessary to disable them during recording. Rear cameras are preferred over front-facing cameras due to their generally higher image quality and resolution. Beyond these considerations, stabilizing the recording position using a tripod or a stand helps mitigate motion artifacts caused by camera shaking.

Resolution and FPS

Video resolution and frames per second (FPS) are two critical factors in determining the video quality, but the research community has different opinions on whether they significantly influence the quality of extracted rPPG signals. Some scholars argue that enhancing image resolution improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), thereby yielding more accurate HR measurement53–55. Notably, the majority of the included rPPG datasets, as depicted in Fig. 3, have a resolution of 640 × 480. The highest resolution observed is 1920 × 1080, which is commonly achieved via smartphone cameras. However, these resolutions are still considered low in the context of super-resolution video reconstruction. In addition to super-resolution techniques, a more straightforward approach is to use advanced cameras capable of capturing higher resolutions videos. However, the increasing cost related to hardware and storage pose significant barriers. Alongside resolution, FPS is another determinant that may affect measurement accuracy. According to the findings of Rouast et al.56, a higher FPS rate results in smoother video sequences, thereby facilitating the extraction of HR-related signals. The frame rate should also remain stable to ensure the temporal consistency. This is one reason that rPPG algorithms sometimes exhibit significant errors in real-time video streaming scenarios57,58. Álvarez Casado et al.59 quantitatively analyzed the effects of reduced frame rate, video resolution degradation, and frame rate instability on rPPG signal quality under limited network bandwidth and computing resources. They also developed mitigation strategies from both the transmitter and receiver sides to maintain acceptable measurement accuracy even under resource-constrained conditions. Nevertheless, Blackford et al.60 argued that the influence of resolution and FPS under multiple camera settings is actually very slight, probably due to the complementary information provided by the camera array. In their experiments, they reduced the FPS from 120 to 60 and 30, and the resolution from 658 × 492 to 329 × 246. The results demonstrated that the HR estimation accuracy remained stable even after decreasing both resolution and FPS.

Video compression methods

In addition to resolution and FPS, video compression methods may have a greater impact on HR measurement accuracy. Mcduff et al.61 conducted a systematic study of video compression methods, revealing a linear relationship between the SNR of extracted BVP signals and the bit rate. Hyanda et al.62 concluded that lossless compression methods, such FFV1, HuffYUV, v410, and v210, tend to yield better HR estimation accuracy compared with x264, x265, MPEG-4, and VP9, which belong to lossy compression methods. The VP9 video codec was recommended among lossy compression methods for its ability to balance HR estimation accuracy and video size. Furthermore, different encoding modes like constant bitrate (CBR) and constant rate factor (CRF) have also been proven to affect the rPPG signal quality63. Instead of relying on conventional video compression methods, Wang et al. developed a specialized compression scheme that can effectively preserve the rPPG signal quality while reducing video size to approximately 1/60 of the original64. A recent study by Nguyen et al.65 conducted a comprehensive evaluation of key factors influencing the performance of rPPG algorithms, including video resolution, frame rate, and compression methods. Their results supported the significant impact of all three factors on the accuracy of remote HR measurement. They also proposed a range of strategies to mitigate these negative effects and enhance the robustness of rPPG algorithms.

Camera exposure

The influence of the camera exposure process is generally considered a minor factor. The impact of exposure time—also referred to as shutter time—has been demonstrated in several studies66–68. These studies also reported that regulating the exposure time range can mitigate the occurrence of saturation within selected ROIs. Additionally, the rolling shutter effect stemming from the readout process of CMOS sensors can introduce distortions and phase shifts during video recording. Several experiments have provided evidence showing that this effect has an impact on rPPG signal quality69.

Ground truth signal acquisition

The collection of synchronized ground truth signals is a crucial step during dataset construction. The ground truth signals are typically provided in the form of ECG or cPPG recordings, which are then used to compute ground truth HR values. The methods of ECG and cPPG signal acquisition have been schematically illustrated in Fig. 2. As presented in Fig. 3, the MAHNOB-HCI and ECG-fitness datasets, used ECG signals as the ground truth. In contrast, the VicarPPG, PURE, COHFACE, UBFC-rPPG, VIPL-HR, LGI-PPGI, BH-rPPG, UBFC-Phys, BUAA-MIHR, MMPD, and iBVP datasets employed cPPG signals. In order to provide more comprehensive ground truth data, the MMSE-HR and VicarPPG2 datasets collected both ECG and cPPG signals. To guarantee data fidelity, most physiological signal acquisition instruments were selected among mature products under the approval for clinical use from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Strategies for addressing data scarcity

Over the years, deep learning (DL) algorithms have shown superior potential and significant performance compared to model-driven algorithms. Beyond the algorithm structure, the effectiveness of DL-based algorithms depends largely on the training dataset. A larger dataset often enables the algorithm to capture a broader array of patterns, leading to improved accuracy. Various strategies have been proposed to counterbalance the risk of overfitting and to improve the generalization ability of rPPG algorithms. In addition to data deficiency, the diversity of the dataset is another determinant. Some public datasets suffer from a lack of diversity in participant demographics, such as age, gender, and ethnicity70.

To mitigate demographic imbalance and dataset-induced bias, future rPPG datasets should adopt standardized design and reporting protocols. At a minimum, datasets should (i) report participant age ranges, sex, and skin tone using standardized scales (e.g., Fitzpatrick skin type); (ii) ensure coverage across multiple demographic strata rather than single-group convenience sampling; (iii) document health status and exclusion criteria where applicable; (iv) provide transparent dataset access policies with clearly defined academic or ethical use conditions; and (v) report demographic stratification in performance evaluation to expose potential bias. Adoption of such protocols would facilitate fairness-aware benchmarking and improve the translational reliability of rPPG algorithms.

Without such standardized protocols, this lack of diversity could potentially lead to bias when applying DL algorithms to underrepresented groups. Furthermore, the dearth of application scenarios (e.g., varying lighting conditions and subject motions) may pose similar challenges.

Expand the dataset size

The most straightforward and effective solution to overcome the challenge of data scarcity is to collect an expanded dataset encompassing diverse facets; however, this approach is encumbered by practical constraints. Firstly, collecting facial videos raises significant privacy concerns. Ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines and secure data storage is challenging. Moreover, recruiting a sufficient number of participants with diverse demographic features is difficult. The recruitment scope of participants may lead to potential biases and must be carefully considered. Building a comprehensive rPPG dataset is also highly resource-intensive. Costs associated with instrument maintenance, participant recruitment, data management, and infrastructural support tend to grow with the data volume.

Data augmentation

An alternative and pragmatic approach involves performing data augmentation based on existing datasets, i.e., generating new data from available resources. Cubic spline interpolation offers a simple yet effective solution for data augmentation71. This interpolation method is used to compress or stretch the extracted RGB traces and corresponding cPPG signals. Thus, multiple synthetic samples with different HR values can be generated. Similarly, Li et al.72 and Vance et al.73 directly synthesize video frames and cPPG or HR signals through window stretching and shrinkage. In order to tackle varying illumination conditions, Yang et al.46 proposed generating augmented lighting variation data during training, followed by image enhancement strategies. Moreover, to improve algorithm adaptability to diverse motion types, motion transfer techniques have been introduced74. This approach embeds synthetic PPG signals within spatial masks, upon which first-order motion models are applied, thereby infusing motion into static facial videos. In addition to environmental illumination and subject movement, skin tone diversity is another critical factor75–77. To address this, a joint optimization framework was designed to generate facial videos with various skin tones78. This bio-realistic skin tone translation mechanism helps mitigate imbalances in skin color representation. However, recent studies have highlighted that synthetic data generation may introduce extraneous artifacts, leading to noticeable distribution shifts between synthetic and real-world data. Interpolation methods used for HR variation can generate noise with non-physiological features that might be erroneously captured by DL models71. For motion transfer techniques, the problem of the synthetic-to-real gap was also identified, where visual inconsistencies or boundary artifacts manifest in synthetic data, deviating from authentic video distributions74. Furthermore, skin-tone translation mechanisms have the potential to introduce visual biases that compromise the integrity of the underlying physiological signals, thereby undermining the reliability and realism of the augmented datasets78. To circumvent this issue, an alternative approach is to use labeled real-world images for pre-training79. Particularly for Transformer-based architectures, which are extremely data-hungry, pre-training on a large-scale image dataset has been shown to help enhance the generalization ability80.

Advanced techniques for algorithm generalization improvement

The development of DL technologies has also enabled researchers to apply more advanced methods to leverage available real-world data. Gao et al.81 proposed an instance-based deep transfer learning technique that improves the generalization performance of HR estimation algorithms by detecting and emphasizing high-quality cPPG signals during the training process. Domain adaptation is another essential branch of transfer learning. Du et al.82 introduced a domain adaptation method based on a dual-bridging structure, which can generate spatiotemporal maps with different types of random noise. Through adversarial training, their method enables the algorithm to adapt to diverse noise patterns, thereby promoting its robustness. To further address the challenge of domain shifts during inference, Lee et al.83 developed a continual test-time adaptation (TTA) method, which updates only the batch normalization (BN) layers and uses a non-contrastive unsupervised learning loss in the frequency domain to improve algorithm stability against catastrophic forgetting.

Skin detection methods

Before extracting RGB signals or other relevant features, skin detection is an essential step that can significantly affect the results, particularly in non-learning-based algorithms. The importance of automatic skin detection is further highlighted in challenging scenarios involving low-light conditions, subject movement, or facial occlusions. Several papers have underscored that segmenting facial skin and excluding non-skin pixels can improve the rPPG signal quality84,85. An effective skin detection method can identify high-quality regions that are critical for accurate HR estimation. According to the reviewed studies, skin detection methods can be categorized into two main types: keypoint-based methods and pixel-based methods. Keypoint-based methods

Keypoint-based methods generally encompass two main steps: face detection and keypoint extraction. The face detection step involves locating the subject’s face and generating a bounding box for each frame. High-precision localization is important to suppress motion artifacts. The subsequent keypoint extraction step aims to segment and track facial ROIs with greater precision. In addition to accuracy, detection speed is also an important factor. Detection speed can directly influence the real-time performance during measurement, which is a basic requirement for practical applications. The Viola-Jones face detector86,87 is widely used in rPPG pipelines due to its recognized real-time performance. It utilizes Haar-like features and a cascaded classifier to efficiently detect human faces. Tracking algorithms such as the KLT tracker88, Kalman filter89,90, and MOSSE tracker91 are typically applied for continuous localization, and the stability of the tracking algorithm has been emphasized92. Multiple algorithms and packages are available for keypoint extraction. Classical feature point extractors (e.g., Shi-Tomasi corner detector93 and SURF94) were applied in earlier studies. However, such types of keypoints are not specifically designed for facial features. A multi-task convolutional neural network (MTCNN)95 was utilized in several of the included articles for face detection and keypoint extraction78,96–99. This model can help deal with the problem of occlusion and motion variations, which often cause failures when using the Viola-Jones face detector. MTCNN can extract five facial landmarks that aid in locating different ROIs. Some open-source libraries specifically built for face detection can extract more facial landmarks and thus have been intensively used in recent studies. Dlib100, developed in C++, is a Python API for cross-platform development. The facial landmark extractor of dlib defines 68 keypoints, providing an improved ROI segmentation accuracy. Similar APIs, including FaceReader101, SeetaFace102, OMRON OKAO103, CLNF OpenFace104, and MediaPipe Face Mesh105, were also employed in some papers. Among these APIs, MediaPipe Face Mesh offers 468 pre-defined facial landmarks and real-time detection speed, thus providing a representative face detection framework for rPPG applications. A comparative study106 has shown that the accuracy, processing speed, and flexibility of MediaPipe Face Mesh have all surpassed those of dlib, Viola-Jones, and MTCNN methods.

Many of the collected articles incorporated an ROI selection step after detecting ROI candidates to evaluate the signal quality of each candidate ROI and select one or more high-quality ROIs for extracting more informative BVP signals. Pixel-wise filtering offers a relatively straightforward approach. It involves computing the mean and standard deviation for pixel values within each ROI and subsequently removing outlier pixels that significantly deviate from the mean values38. Kossack et al.107 proposed a reliability score based on the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and the maximum magnitude in the frequency domain. They calculated the reliability of each ROI and selected the one with the highest score for BVP extraction. Wang108 ranked the candidate ROIs using a quality metric based on singular spectrum analysis (SSA). He recommended selecting one-half to one-fourth of the candidate patches. In addition, SNR, which is a commonly used measurement of signal quality, was also utilized for ROI selection109–112. Considering the high computing cost, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was introduced as a replacement113. With a complexity of O(n), the BIC metric was shown to be faster and more resilient to subject movement, thereby providing superior selection capability. In a quantitative comparison of commonly used SQIs, the results evaluated on the PURE and LGI-PPGI datasets showed that SNR can more accurately detect high-quality cardiac signals (with a suggested threshold of 0.293), while kurtosis is more effective in distinguishing between acceptable and unfit signals24. Rather than using a single pre-defined ROI, Boccignone et al. developed a method that improves ROI selection by clustering signals from multiple facial regions based on their spectral characteristics114. In the work of Zhang et al.115 and Zhao et al.116, the advantage of using multiple ROIs was also highlighted. At the physical modeling level, a signal quality metric based on surface normal vectors in 3D facial landmarks was developed117. This metric was inspired by the theory that the surface orientation of keypoints can significantly affect the ratio between specular reflection and diffuse reflection, and that only diffuse reflection carries physiological information. Their results showed that a small reflection angle can provide a higher rPPG signal quality. The experiments of Cantrill et al. also supported this phenomenon118. However, Fan et al.119 collected facial videos of different reflection angles and found that the highest HR estimation accuracy was at 60 degrees instead of 0 degrees. This suggests that an optimal range of reflection angles may exist, which should handle a tradeoff between reducing specular reflection and keeping enough diffuse reflection to capture BVP variations.

Pixel-based methods

In contrast to keypoint-based approaches, pixel-based methods do not rely on facial landmark localization. As shown in Fig. 4b, the algorithms can directly classify image pixels into skin pixels and non-skin pixels. Thus, the entire image is divided into several large regions called superpixels (groups of adjacent pixels in an image that share similar properties, like color, texture, and intensity). This division process automatically skips the face-detection step, thus simplifying the detection process. Among pixel-based methods, thresholding is the simplest approach to distinguish skin from non-skin pixels. Some studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of using thresholding in the hue, saturation, and value (HSV) color space, as well as in the luma, red-difference chroma, and blue-difference chroma (YCrCb) color space120,121. In addition to thresholding, clustering methods can also be applied for superpixel segmentation. Jaiswal et al.122 and Li et al.123 utilized the simple linear iterative clustering (SLIC) method to segment each frame into superpixels. In the method of Jaiswal et al., the least mean squares (LMS) filter was applied in parallel to suppress the influence of illumination variation. However, SLIC methods may impose large computational burdens. This problem can be mitigated by implicitly identifying the superpixel boundaries and only processing a fraction of the image pixels124. Certain studies85,125–127 adopted Conaire’s method128 for skin detection, which maximizes the agreement between independent data sources in HSV space iteratively. Moreover, DL-based semantic segmentation methods have demonstrated strong performance for selecting skin pixels72,129. In contrast to traditional segmentation algorithms, DL-based methods exhibit greater adaptability within unconstrained environments and demonstrate enhanced flexibility.

Model-driven algorithms

Model-driven algorithms refer to those developed based on prior knowledge or deterministic signal processing techniques. Through explicit mathematical and physical modeling, these algorithms extract physiological signals depending on theoretical foundations such as optical properties of skin, color space variations, and blind source separation. As illustrated in Fig. 2, after detecting the ROIs, RGB signals—also referred to as RGB traces—are computed by averaging the selected pixels in each color channel. Subsequently, dimensionality reduction techniques are employed to transform RGB traces into BVP signals, which contain more robust and precise HR-related information. Most model-driven rPPG methods aim to enhance the dimensionality reduction process, serving as fusion strategies that integrate HR-related signals from RGB channels. An ideal rPPG method should extract high-quality signals that can capture the cardiac activity at a deeper level while suppressing noise and artifacts130. The simplest and most straightforward approach is to select the channel that exhibits the highest similarity with ground truth cPPG signals. Prior investigations16,131–134 consistently highlighted the alignment of the G channel with PPG signals, due to its superior correspondence compared to the R and B channels. This conclusion is further supported by the anatomical fact that hemoglobin shows better absorption of green light than red light135. Nonetheless, relying solely on the G channel may not be optimal, as the R and B channels also contain complementary information useful for BVP signal extraction and should be appropriately utilized84. A simple and efficient ratio-based algorithm called GRGB rPPG was proposed by Haugg et al.136. In this method, rPPG signals are estimated by taking the sum of the ratios of the green-to-red (GR) channel and the green-to-blue (GB) channel. This algorithm was demonstrated to be effective and computationally efficient. Additionally, as a classical blind source separation (BSS) technique, an independent component analysis (ICA) algorithm was applied to extract BVP signals from raw RGB traces137, where the normalized traces are decomposed into three independent source signals. As a rule of thumb, it is recommended that the second component be extracted, which commonly reveals a dominant HR-related signal. The ICA method can be further improved by adding two priors: one based on periodicity using autocorrelation, and the other based on the chrominance (CHROM)-based constraint of skin pixels138,139. Similarly, principal component analysis (PCA) has also been applied as a BSS technique140. Unlike ICA, PCA does not assume statistical independence or any specific underlying structure in the original features. The experimental accuracy is comparable to ICA, with the advantage of lower computational complexity. A spatial subspace rotation (2SR) method was presented by Wang et al.141 without the requirement of skin-tone or pulse-related priors. It extracts pulse signals by estimating the spatial subspace of skin pixels and then measuring the temporal rotation. They also developed the plane-orthogonal-to-skin (POS) method142, which employs a projection plane orthogonal to the skin tone for pulse signal extraction. Departing from popular interpretations based on optical and spectral analysis, the local group invariance (LGI) method was designed to utilize invariant features, rearranging and concentrating the BVP signals in vector space42. Beyond the RGB space, the exploration of alternative color spaces reveals the efficacy of the H channel in the HSV color space and the Q channel in the luma, in-phase, and quadrature (YIQ) color space143.

Mitigating artifacts resulting from motion and illumination variability is crucial for the development of more robust rPPG algorithms. De Haan et al.144 proposed a robust CHROM-based rPPG method to address motion-induced distortions frequently encountered in BSS techniques. The algorithm demonstrated consistent performance under varying measurement conditions. To continuously improve motion robustness, the PBV method145 was introduced after one year, showing superior performance over CHROM across diverse datasets. This method assumes that pulse signals can be reconstructed by a linear combination of normalized RGB traces. It utilizes the relative pulsatile properties of the color channels to extract HR-related signals and remove distortions. Feng et al.84 devised an automatic selection procedure based on the ICA method to suppress motion artifacts. Rather than picking the second component by default, the independent component with the highest correlation to the reference sine function was recommended. Various studies proposed strategies to estimate subject movement and cancel out motion-induced artifacts. These motion compensation techniques include using facial optical flows146 or tracking non-skin pixels that move in sync with skin pixels147. Some researchers focused on refining existing algorithms by introducing a priori knowledge. For instance, the projection vector based on the spectral characteristics (PSC) algorithm148, inspired by the POS algorithm, constrained the projection vector using channel signal variance, demonstrating improved robustness under strong disturbance cases. Macwan et al.125 improved the ICA method by introducing multi-objective optimization with autocorrelation. The proposed method, MAICA, has shown outstanding performance in the presence of motion artifacts. ICA-based algorithms have also been improved by incorporating nonlinear components. In the nonlinear mode decomposition (NMD) technique, motion artifacts are suppressed by leveraging its inherent noise-robust characteristics149. Focusing on uneven lighting interference, Song et al.150 developed the ensemble empirical mode decomposition with multiset canonical correlation analysis (EEMD-MCCA) method. The EEMD is employed to decompose the G channel, and the MCCA is used to determine the underlying candidate pulses. A retinex-based method was employed to enhance the image quality of frames captured in dark illuminance environments. The experimental results have quantitatively confirmed that this image enhancement technique can improve the performance of common rPPG algorithms. Moreover, the study highlighted the potential reliability of the GREEN and ICA methods over POS under inadequate illumination conditions.

Besides noise caused by motion and illumination, video compression artifacts have been identified as another influential factor in rPPG algorithms. The video compression artifacts commonly appear in three forms: amplitude degradation, structured high-frequency noise, and trace interruptions151. To address these challenges, a framework based on SSA was devised by the researchers. This framework comprises three essential steps: single-channel band-pass filtering, SSA decomposition and reconstructed component (RC) selection, and spectral masking refinement. Furthermore, the orthogonal matrix image transformation (OMIT) method, which is specially tailored for heavily compressed videos, was demonstrated to be more robust than the GREEN and POS methods152. The OMIT algorithm extracts an orthogonal matrix with uncorrelated components. To improve computational efficiency, it employs thin QR factorization in place of full QR factorization. Additionally, Eulerian video magnification153 can be applied to attenuate video compression artifacts and enhance imperceptible signals.

Data-driven algorithms

For data-driven algorithms, considerable amounts of data are generally required for the optimization of learnable parameters. Instead of relying on explicit signal modeling, ML and DL techniques are commonly leveraged to automatically obtain the mapping relationship between the input raw data and the output physiological signals. With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and associated hardware capacity, increasing attention has been paid toward data-driven rPPG methods. Compared to conventional approaches, data-driven methods demonstrate superior capabilities for extracting higher-order features and offer promising generalization ability across diverse application scenarios. These algorithms can be categorized into two major types based on their integration stage within the processing pipeline. Figure 2 illustrates that end-to-end algorithms can directly estimate HR from input video sequences that are preprocessed under simple operations such as resizing, cropping, frame difference, warping, and color space transformation. Some algorithms first estimate the BVP signals and then derive HR values using frequency spectrum analysis or peak detection methods. In this roadmap, such algorithms are also classified as end-to-end approaches. The second type is referred to as hybrid methods, which apply ML or DL techniques at one or multiple stages within the workflow. End-to-end structure

DeepPhys is the first end-to-end deep CNN-based algorithm developed for HR measurement from facial videos without relying on explicit signal processing stages154. This framework employs the VGG architecture and adopts the temporal difference between consecutive frames as input. By jointly training motion and appearance branches, DeepPhys achieved enhanced performance across different illumination conditions and subject motion types. However, the utilization of VGG-based architectures in real-time applications is hindered by their large number of parameters. To improve on-device performance, the multi-task temporal shift convolutional attention network (MTTS-CAN) architecture was proposed, which can be deployed on mobile platforms with a real-time inference speed155. In a similar vein, Comas et al.99 presented a more lightweight architecture inspired by a Taylor series expansion and also introduced the temporal adaptive location shift (TALOS) loss to learn latent shift probabilities of the ground truth during training. Their method achieved comparable accuracy to DeepPhys, while the model size is 200 times smaller. Beyond the aforementioned 2D-CNN structure, 3D-CNN frameworks inherently capture temporal characteristics of input videos and are thus more powerful for extracting spatiotemporal features156–158. Nonetheless, 3D-CNNs are more computationally intensive due to their increased dimension, resulting in greater computational and memory demands, as well as the need for more training data. In response to this problem, an ultralight 3D-CNN architecture named real-time rPPG (RTrPPG) was proposed, which has a substantially faster inference speed compared with other state-of-the-art 3D-CNN methods159. Speth et al.160 developed a weakly supervised method to train 3D-CNNs using HR labels without the need for synchronized rPPG signals as the ground truth.

Beyond considerations of model size and inference speed, denoising ability is a critical aspect of rPPG algorithms. Liu et al.161 applied an adversarial correlation analysis learning strategy based on the background region for noise removal. Yue et al.53 aimed to improve HR estimation accuracy for low-resolution facial videos using a two-stage network. Their strategy involves first enhancing input video resolution using an rPPG information recovery network (rPPGRNet), followed by HR estimation via a temporally aware HR measurement network (THRNet). Illumination noise can also be suppressed via temporal normalization on RGB channels and direct current (DC) removal162. To address the issue of phase delay when using ground truth cPPG signals collected using fingertip or wristband oximeters, recently proposed methods163,164 often incorporate the spectrum distribution similarity supervision mechanism to mitigate the negative impact of phase shift during training.

In addition to CNN architectures, researchers have explored other DL structures to capture contextual features in both spatial and temporal domains. Long short-term memory (LSTM) modules can be integrated into CNN-based methods, and a mask branch can be applied for noise suppression98. With the emergence and rapid advancement of the Transformer architecture in vision tasks since 2020165, Transformer-based rPPG algorithms were developed as powerful solutions for HR measurement166–169. However, as a common problem for Transformer-based architectures, the inference speed often falls short of real-time requirements. Recent studies introduced computationally efficient algorithm architectures or modules such as Mamba, Kolmogorov-Arnold network (KAN)170, graph neural network (GNN)171, and spiking neural network (SNN)172 to address this issue while maintaining superior noise suppression capability during measurement.

Hybrid structure

In hybrid methods, structures with learnable parameters are embedded into one or more specific stages of the rPPG workflow. In 2014, Hsu et al.173 first introduced the concept of estimating HR using ML algorithms. They used a support vector regression (SVR) algorithm to leverage mid-level features and designed fusion strategies to exploit both feature and temporal redundancies. Chowdhury et al.174 integrated ML techniques into the post-processing step for rPPG signal refinement. They employed the LGI algorithm for raw signal extraction and a segmentation technique to obtain the final rPPG estimation. To mitigate the influence of facial expressions, which are recognized as the predominant source of temporal signal noise, the AND-rPPG approach was developed175. This method integrates action units (AUs) for facial expression analysis and temporal convolution networks (TCNs) for denoising distinct facial regions. For in-vehicle monitoring scenarios with luminance variations, Wu et al.176 proposed two ML-based approaches: the convolution autoencoder (CAE) and the multi-channel convolution autoencoder (Multi-CAE). These methods first transformed input videos into spectrograms via the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and then processed spectrograms using the CAE modules for noise mitigation. In another paper, they developed an adaptive neural network model selection (ANNMS) method by personalizing for different drivers using an automatic model selection mechanism177.

Beyond the RGB space, transforming RGB traces into other color spaces has shown tangible benefits178. An adaptive color space transformation (CoST) layer featuring learnable parameters for color channel transformation offers a viable alternative179. The pre-trained CoST layer transforms RGB traces into an optimal color space and thus provides more informative signals under various lighting conditions. To eliminate the effect of illumination variations, Jaiswal et al. incorporated multi-scale retinex (MSR) as a complementary color space alongside RGB traces180. Subsequent to this preprocessing step, the proposed rPPG-FuseNet takes the fused spatiotemporal feature map as input and estimates the HR values. The retinex theory can also be combined with DL architectures to estimate the illumination and reflectance components181. In fact, it is a common approach for many data-driven algorithms of hybrid structure to first transform facial video sequences into different kinds of spatiotemporal feature maps as the input of subsequent networks182–185.

Filtering techniques

The choice of filters plays a crucial role in enhancing the quality of extracted BVP signals in rPPG-based HR measurement and thus requires careful consideration. They can be applied either as pre-filtering to the RGB traces or as post-filtering to the estimated rPPG signals. Filters used in rPPG algorithms are typically designed based on prior knowledge of HR frequency ranges and are employed to suppress noise caused by subject movement and complex lighting conditions.

In our roadmap, band-pass Butterworth filters of different orders were found to be the most commonly used. The Butterworth filter186 is a type of linear filter recognized for its smooth frequency response and efficient processing speed. It is designed to offer a maximally flat frequency response within the passband. This frequency characteristic helps minimize amplitude and phase fluctuations. According to a recent comparative analysis of different common unsophisticated filters, Butterworth filters may be particularly suitable for rPPG methods compared to cPPG approaches187. Apart from Butterworth, elliptic, and Chebyshev filters, with their sharp transition properties, yield superior SNR scores. In contrast, window-based filters, such as moving averages, median filters, and Hamming windows, tend to perform less effectively, especially when processing low-quality raw signals. Their experiments also highlighted the impact of filter order. In complex scenarios requiring dynamic adjustment of filter parameters, the infinite impulse response (IIR) filter was recommended for robust rPPG-based HR estimation188. Raw RGB traces or estimated rPPG signals without post-filtering often exhibit baseline drifts and low-frequency fluctuations. Tarvainen et al.189 designed the detrending filter to eliminate long-term variations that are unrelated to cardiac activities, such as the sensor time drift and illumination changes. This approach is valued for its ability to efficiently suppress distortion effects.

Several studies have proposed more advanced filtering methods. A color-distortion filter (CDF) was designed based on the reflection spectra differences between pulsatile and non-pulsatile components121. In challenging fitness scenarios, the CDF outperformed the band-pass filter (BPF), confirming its superior performance. The CDF can also be integrated with an amplitude-selective filter (ASF) to jointly process RGB traces190. The ASF can serve to select RGB signals containing HR information, while the CDF removes color distortion. Beyond temporal domain filtering, filter banks might be more effective for processing frequency components97. Different frequency components can be adaptively adjusted by constructing a set of exponential smoothing filters with various frequency bins. Moreover, wavelet transform techniques have also exhibited application values to suppress motion artifacts and other types of noise191. In data-driven approaches, it is recommended that filters be embedded within the algorithm architecture, serving as learnable modules. For instance, multilayer perceptron (MLP)96, 1D-CNN192, and LSTM structures127 can all serve as filters with learnable parameters.

The mean absolute error (MAE), also known as the difference in beats per minute (ΔBPM), and the root mean square error (RMSE) are among the metrics most frequently used in HR values. These metrics quantify overall prediction errors, with lower values indicating higher measurement accuracy. To provide more statistical insight, standard deviations (STDs) of these metrics are often reported alongside them to establish confidence intervals for BPM estimates. In comparison, the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) metric provides a normalized and dimensionless metric to reflect algorithm performance across different HR ranges and subjects. In some studies, researchers set a threshold for MAPE to indicate the practical applicability of the proposed algorithms. Additionally, the Bland–Altman analysis is commonly used as a visualization tool to assess the measurement agreement between the proposed algorithm and standard instruments.

Besides these error-based metrics, correlation-based metrics have also been leveraged for evaluating HR estimation performance. In the pyVHR framework193,194, Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) are recommended to measure the agreement between estimated and reference HR values. Template matching correlation (TMC) has been proposed to measure correlation in a fragmentary manner. Dividing the complete signal into multiple equal-length segments allows detection of regularity in a time window, thus mitigating the impact of abnormal values at specific time points. These metrics provide complementary perspectives by assessing the trajectory agreement and are particularly useful for reflecting measurement error in HR dynamics under large-scale variation. It is worth noting that PCC, CCC, and cosine similarity are sometimes leveraged to capture the discrepancies between estimated rPPG and ground truth cPPG signals, in order to provide a PPG signal-level performance reflection. Maximum cross-correlation (MCC) has also been introduced as both a loss function and evaluation metric for rPPG-supervised learning195. Integrating MCC as a term of the loss function can enhance its robustness against random temporal offsets in the ground truth. FFT-based techniques are often used to compute MCC more efficiently. In addition to formulated metrics, DL-based approaches have also been explored to quantify rPPG signal quality. Song et al.196 proposed and compared both Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) and deep ensemble methods to evaluate the uncertainty of rPPG signals in HR estimation. These methods can be useful for providing confidence estimates in reference-free real-world applications. To complement these metric-based evaluations, we include a brief illustration in the following section on clinical translation.

Translation in clinical settings

According to our literature search, only three articles (3/151, 1.99%) specifically addressed clinically related topics. Among them, two focused on paediatric care and one on long-term, non-contact monitoring during liquid bolus administration. Hatib et al.198 conducted a two-phase validation study in children and neonates. Their research suggested that the measurement results obtained on children under 10 years old (including newborns) did not reach clinically acceptable accuracy; by contrast, older paediatric cohorts exhibited higher measurement reliability, with the subgroup of 12–16 years old achieving a Spearman correlation of 0.82 with reference measures of HR recordings. The study conducted by Wegerif et al.199 presented a similar phenomenon of age-dependent performance characteristics in paediatric care settings. They collected facial videos from 346 children across diverse health conditions and applied the POS algorithm142 for rPPG signal extraction. Their method achieved RMSEs of 15.4 BPM for subjects below 5 years old, 7.5 BPM for those aged 5–11 years, and 5.4 BPM in adolescents older than 11 years.

To bridge the performance gap in younger cohorts, these studies proposed concrete data collection and algorithmic strategies. These included the implementation of interactive gamification (e.g., animations to encourage the compliance of children), specialized overhead recording hardware setups for supine neonates, and explicit privacy/safety protocols (e.g., offline data processing and addressing concerns regarding infrared radiation). At the algorithmic level, optimizations included recommending the mid-face as a superior ROI over the forehead, expanding the HR search space up to 200 BPM, and redesigning screening filters to mitigate systematic bias arising from the mismatch between the adult and paediatric distributions.

Beyond paediatrics, Momeni et al.200 reported the first feasibility exploration of rPPG-based cardiac activity monitoring during long-term fluid bolus administration. Their developed pipeline incorporated MTCNN95 for face and keypoint detection, with the forehead and cheeks selected as ROIs for RGB signal extraction, and applied both CHROM138,139 and POS algorithms for rPPG signal conversion. The HR measurement results showed their rPPG-based methods achieved MAEs of 4.27 BPM (CHROM) and 4.28 BPM (POS), which are close to clinical standards. Importantly, the measurement setup enabled synchronous monitoring of hemodynamic parameters, including stroke volume (SV) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR). The authors also underscored that the vasoconstriction induced by cold fluid administration (15 ∘C) can potentially result in increased measurement error compared with warm fluid administration (37 ∘C).

To illustrate the capability of video-based rPPG in capturing HR dynamics under clinically relevant stress conditions, we compared cPPG and rPPG signals acquired during a lower body negative pressure (LBNP) protocol (Fig. 7). The correlation-based metrics were exceptionally high (PCC = 99.85%, CCC = 99.85%, and cosine similarity = 99.99%), while amplitude-based metrics also confirmed high accuracy (MAE = 0.60 BPM, RMSE = 1.16 BPM, MAPE = 0.76%). In Fig. 7b, the comparison demonstrates good agreement over a narrower HR range. The correlation remained high (PCC = 92.05%, CCC = 91.97%, cosine = 99.98%), with low absolute errors (MAE = 0.54 BPM, RMSE = 1.02 BPM, MAPE = 0.92%). The Bland–Altman plot again revealed minimal bias and tight dispersion, confirming that even under low-amplitude or motion-stable conditions, rPPG yields consistent HR estimates.

Together, these results illustrate the ability of the CHROM-based rPPG method to reproduce cPPG-based HR recordings across both dynamic and stable scenarios within this experimental setting. The combination of correlation, concordance, and absolute error metrics underscores the importance of a multi-metric evaluation framework: while correlation quantifies waveform similarity, absolute metrics (MAE, RMSE) capture physiological accuracy. When interpreted jointly, they provide a comprehensive depiction of agreement that is useful for methodological evaluation.

However, these strong agreement metrics were obtained under deliberately controlled experimental conditions-including fixed camera–subject geometry, disabled automatic camera settings, standardized cross-polarized illumination, supine positioning with minimal motion, and a predefined staged LBNP protocol in a homogeneous cohort of healthy volunteers-and should therefore be interpreted as upper-bound estimates under idealized conditions rather than representative of expected performance in unconstrained clinical or real-world environments.

This proof-of-feasibility demonstration is intended solely to illustrate the practical acquisition and signal-level agreement of facial rPPG recordings in a controlled clinical research environment. Given the limited sample size, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical efficacy, diagnostic accuracy, or readiness for clinical deployment. Large-scale, prospective clinical studies across diverse patient populations are required to establish clinical validity and regulatory suitability.

Discussion

Despite rapid technical advances, direct clinical evidence for rPPG remains limited. Among the 151 reviewed studies, only a small subset explicitly involves clinical populations or clinically grounded endpoints. Consequently, the current body of evidence primarily supports technical feasibility rather than validated clinical utility. This roadmap therefore, emphasizes the identification of translational gaps and the methodological prerequisites required to progress from laboratory validation toward rigorous clinical evaluation.

In this paper, we conducted a literature review that focuses on rPPG techniques that use a single RGB camera to record facial videos for HR measurement. To gain a comprehensive understanding of this field, we examined research advances in eight components of the rPPG pipeline and conducted extensive searches on mainstream academic databases. Although rPPG technology has developed over just a decade, it has evolved from low initial accuracy to performance comparable to cPPG technology in simple scenarios, demonstrating its significant development potential and application prospects. Compared to ECG and cPPG, rPPG enables non-contact measurement, substantially reducing both measurement costs and operational complexity. However, the non-contact feature of rPPG technology also makes it susceptible to various noise sources, including complex lighting conditions, subject movement, and video compression artifacts. As mentioned in this article, the main challenges encountered in the development of rPPG algorithms can be summarized in terms of four aspects: algorithm effectiveness, application environment, hardware restrictions, and insufficient data. Over the past decade, researchers have overcome many issues related to these challenges through a variety of approaches. Nonetheless, moving towards real-world applications will require continuous efforts from the research, industrial, and clinical communities.

In practical scenarios that involve deploying algorithms on hardware terminals, certain requirements for computation speed arise. This is particularly true in some use cases that have strict safety requirements, like automatic driving and intelligent surveillance. These applications often demand processing speeds at the millisecond level. However, a certain degree of latency is regarded as acceptable in the field of HR measurement; thus, the inference speed is less tightly constrained. Nonetheless, the real-time performance of rPPG algorithms still attracted significant attention from the research community. Some model-driven rPPG methods focus on reducing the operation time while maintaining measurement accuracy. According to the experimental results of Lewandowska et al.140, it took 223 ms and 94 ms for the ICA algorithm to process the full-face ROI and the forehead ROI, respectively. Their proposed PCA algorithm achieved improved computing times of 1.4 ms and 1.2 ms. The OMIT algorithm also achieved a computation speed of 17 ms per frame152. In terms of algorithmic complexity, the GRGB algorithm136 with a time complexity of O(1) outperforms the POS algorithm, which has a complexity of O(n2). These results indicate that model-driven rPPG algorithms have evolved in both measurement accuracy and computation speed, reaching millisecond-level performance.

Data-driven methods, especially those based on DL approaches, often require substantial computational resources due to their large number of parameters. Therefore, improving inference speed through lightweight architectures has become an important research direction. While most end-to-end architectures use 3D-CNNs to process facial videos, Comas et al.99 employed a 2D-CNN as the spatial encoder. Their algorithm achieved an HR measurement accuracy comparable to that of PhysNet156 while containing only 0.7% of its parameters. Lee et al.201 compared the inference time and HR measurement accuracy of several data-driven methods. Their results show that DeepPhys154, MTTS-CAN155, PhysNet156, EfficientPhys202, as well as their proposed algorithm, were all able to maintain an MAE within 2.1 BPM while having an average computation speed of less than 70 ms per frame. These results indicate that state-of-the-art DL-based rPPG algorithms can meet the general requirement when adequate computational resources are available.

From the perspective of algorithm structure, end-to-end models directly take facial videos as input and output estimated rPPG signals or HR values, simplifying the pipeline and eliminating multiple intermediate steps. Such a design streamlines the process and reduces the complexity without the need to decompose the main task into multiple substeps. However, end-to-end algorithms typically involve a larger number of parameters, requiring substantial data and computational resources during both training and real-world deployment. Additionally, as “black boxes”, the interpretability of end-to-end algorithms is relatively poor, thus making it difficult to adapt performance under specific challenging conditions. To address this, future research should consider integrating attention-map visualization203 or physically-constrained techniques, ensuring model decisions are consistent with known physiological and optical principles. In contrast, a hybrid structure involves separate processing steps that can serve as structured regularization with prior knowledge, leading to improved interpretability and robustness. The research of Zhan et al.204 has demonstrated this point by incorporating priors of knowledge-based algorithms such as CHROM and POS into CNN architectures to enhance their robustness against motion artifacts. Moreover, the smaller size allows hybrid algorithms to achieve a faster training speed. Nevertheless, this may limit their model capabilities compared to end-to-end models given sufficient training data, especially as more well-established datasets have become available in recent years. On the other hand, most end-to-end models involve a large number of parameters and require parallel computing resources like graphics processing units (GPUs). In resource-constrained environments, deploying model-driven or hybrid algorithms might serve as a more realistic solution. Figure 7 provides a synthesis view of how HR agreement between camera-derived rPPG and reference contact PPG should be evaluated and reported. The four complementary analytical panels—time-series overlay, scatter plot, Bland–Altman comparison, and the multi-metric summary—together reveal dimensions of fidelity that no single statistic can capture. Correlation-based indices (PCC, CCC, cosine similarity) quantify linear and concordant association between HR estimates, but remain sensitive to polarity, scaling, and temporal misalignment. In contrast, magnitude-based metrics (MAE, RMSE) directly quantify absolute differences in BPM and are invariant to sign. The Bland–Altman analysis complements both by characterizing bias and dispersion across the HR operating range, thereby exposing systematic under- or over-estimation that may otherwise be hidden.

In Fig. 7a, rPPG achieves near-perfect correspondence with contact PPG (PCC = 99.85%, CCC = 99.85%, MAE = 0.60 BPM, RMSE = 1.16 BPM, MAPE = 0.76%), demonstrating that under well-controlled illumination and motion-stable conditions, video-based HR estimation can closely reproduce reference values across a wide dynamic range. In Fig. 7b, a narrower HR range still yields high concordance (PCC = 92.05%, CCC = 91.97%, MAE = 0.54 BPM, RMSE = 1.02 BPM, MAPE = 0.92%), confirming that the approach remains robust even when HR variability is minimal. These examples together highlight that high correlation does not always coincide with minimal error, and conversely, low absolute errors can persist even when correlation is reduced by constrained HR variability. Hence, both association- and error-based metrics are indispensable for a complete interpretation.

Beyond numerical agreement, the figure underscores how acquisition and preprocessing decisions fundamentally shape rPPG fidelity. Region-of-interest (ROI) selection—whether focusing on the forehead, cheeks, or fused multi-ROI signals—along with illumination stability, compression, frame rate, and motion artifacts, all influence the extracted waveform prior to HR computation. A rigorous pipeline should therefore combine photometric normalization, motion compensation, and multi-ROI fusion with outlier rejection, followed by signal-quality gating using spectral or harmonic consistency criteria. Explicit documentation of every stage—color-space transform (e.g., CHROM, POS), temporal filtering, detrending, synchronization with reference signals, and sign-alignment policy—remains critical to ensure reproducibility and comparability across studies. Specifically, regarding the phase delay between the measured and reference signals, we recommend a two-fold protocol: (i) for dataset developers, providing synchronized timestamps for each video frame and reference signal sample to prevent hardware-level time offsets; and (ii) for algorithm developers, explicitly reporting the alignment strategies or compensation methods for the pulse transit time (PTT) shift (e.g., cross-correlation based alignment195 or spectrum distribution similarity supervision mechanisms163,164) to ensure fair comparisons across different studies. For actionable reproducibility, Supplementary Information (Table S3) provides a reproducibility checklist covering acquisition details, preprocessing steps, synchronization and alignment with reference signals, validation design, and code/model availability, together with minimum and recommended hardware conditions for deployment.

Overall, Fig. 7 serves as a template for standardized reporting of PPG–rPPG agreement in HR estimation. Beyond presenting summary statistics, a comprehensive evaluation should include: (i) waveform overlays to visualize temporal alignment, (ii) synchronous scatter plots to assess monotonicity and polarity, (iii) Bland–Altman analysis for bias and limits of agreement, and (iv) a balanced metric set spanning correlation, concordance, and absolute errors. Reporting both percentage-based (e.g., MAPE) and unit-based errors (e.g., MAE, RMSE) alongside Bland–Altman results enables a more transparent and physiologically interpretable assessment. Such multi-metric frameworks are essential for bridging technical validation and clinical translation of rPPG-based monitoring systems.

Non-contact rPPG can improve comfort and compliance—particularly for paediatric and elderly patients and for prolonged observation—by reducing adhesive sensors and cabling. While the present illustration is limited to two subjects and HR trends, it motivates prospective studies comparing rPPG-derived HR trajectories against reference monitors across diverse skin tones, lighting, and motion, and testing whether rPPG trends can flag decompensation earlier or with fewer artifacts in real-world workflows. These considerations are especially salient in neonatal and paediatric care, where minimizing sensor burden is a priority. In addition to adult monitoring, rPPG has shown promising potential in neonatal and paediatric environments. Hatib et al.198 conducted a two-phased clinical study evaluating rPPG deployment in paediatric wards, demonstrating its ability to reduce sensor burden in neonates. Similarly, Wegerif et al.199 compared rPPG-based pulse rate estimation methods in infants and confirmed its feasibility in clinical paediatric settings, including intensive care units. These findings suggest that rPPG can complement existing practices in neonatal ICUs by enabling continuous, non-contact monitoring without disturbing fragile patients.

Furthermore, with the increasing trend towards telemedicine, rPPG offers a viable solution for remote HR monitoring of patients, enabling timely interventions and reducing the need for frequent hospital visits205,206. The insights provided by our clinical partners were instrumental in highlighting these applications and ensuring that our roadmap addresses the practical needs of healthcare providers. A comprehensive review207 highlighted the challenges and prospects of applying computer vision and rPPG-based monitoring in clinical environments, while a recent clinical study208 demonstrated the feasibility of such techniques for diagnosing respiratory infections in paediatric emergency room settings. Together, these works reinforce the applicability of rPPG in acute care scenarios where conventional monitoring is often impractical or resource-limited.

While rPPG holds promise for integration into telemedicine and remote monitoring workflows, translation into regulated clinical use requires adherence to established medical device pathways. Depending on intended use, rPPG systems may fall under wellness monitoring or regulated medical device categories, each associated with distinct evidence requirements. Beyond algorithmic validation on public datasets, clinical translation necessitates staged evaluation, including (i) technical verification under controlled conditions, (ii) pilot feasibility studies in representative clinical environments, and (iii) large-scale prospective clinical trials demonstrating safety, accuracy, and clinical benefit relative to reference standards. Engagement with regulatory frameworks (e.g., FDA, CE marking) and alignment with data governance, privacy, and interoperability standards are essential prerequisites for approval and adoption in clinical practice.

Beyond physical health, rPPG has been increasingly applied in affective computing and mental health assessments. Several studies have demonstrated that rPPG-derived metrics, such as heart rate variability and signal amplitude patterns, can help detect emotional states, including stress, anxiety, and depression209,210. These applications open the door for rPPG to support mental health monitoring in home-based and outpatient settings. One of the key factors driving the growth of rPPG applications in public health is its integration into smartphones, security cameras, and other visual inspection devices211. Several studies have suggested that such noninvasive remote monitoring significantly reduces hospitalizations in chronic heart failure patients and may benefit other chronic disease populations212,213. A recent field validation concluded that wireless health monitoring devices are convenient, user friendly, and effective for mass screening in community settings214. To maximize the global impact of remote monitoring and mobile health technologies, it is vital to address remaining technical challenges, foster standards for data interoperability, and promote equitable access in low resource environments215,216.

Although the technical and clinical potential of rPPG is increasingly demonstrated in research, its adoption in regulated clinical practice remains limited. For rPPG-based systems to be approved for medical use, they must undergo rigorous validation and meet regulatory requirements. In the United States, such systems would typically require FDA clearance as Class II medical devices, demonstrating substantial equivalence to predicate technologies through clinical trials and safety evaluations. Similarly, Conformité Européenne (CE) marking under the European Medical Device Regulation requires conformity with safety and performance standards. Clinical validation studies comparing rPPG output with gold-standard ECG or cPPG readings across diverse populations and use conditions are essential.

Moreover, adherence to data interoperability frameworks and privacy regulations (e.g., HIPAA, GDPR) is critical for deployment in clinical and telehealth environments. Beyond regulatory compliance, large-scale deployment of facial video–based physiological monitoring requires explicit governance and risk-mitigation strategies. Privacy-preserving design principles-including data minimization, purpose limitation, and preferential use of on-device processing-should be adopted to reduce reliance on centralized storage of identifiable facial video. Where feasible, raw video frames should be treated as transient input, with only de-identified physiological signals or derived features retained. Clear consent frameworks, role-based access control, encryption, retention limits, and audit mechanisms are essential to mitigate misuse and support trustworthy deployment. Importantly, governance strategies should remain context-dependent, reflecting whether an rPPG system is intended for wellness monitoring, research, or regulated clinical decision support.

In the feasibility example presented in this work, facial video data were collected under IRB approval with explicit participant consent, stored on restricted local systems, and processed separately from raw video storage, illustrating practical safeguards for handling identifiable visual data in controlled research settings.

Emphasizing clinical safety requires not only minimizing false alarms but also implementing robust “fail-safe” mechanisms that trigger contact-based verification when rPPG signals are deemed unreliable for clinical decision-making. Beyond improving signal quality, future work must prioritize the development of standardized validation frameworks that enforce rigorous evaluation strategies (e.g., external validation or subject-wise partitioning) to mitigate data leakage and prevent overoptimistic performance reporting. Such rigor is essential to support regulatory approval and seamless integration into clinical and electronic health systems. Importantly, given the heterogeneity in datasets, acquisition protocols, and validation strategies, cross-study MAE comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive summaries rather than directly comparable indicators of algorithm performance. HR measurement devices based on cPPG algorithms have been widely adopted in consumer electronics, including smart wristbands and portable pulse oximeters. In contrast, techniques based on rPPG remain largely confined to laboratory environments, and their accuracy often lags behind cPPG systems, particularly under complex real-world conditions involving motion, illumination variability, and heterogeneous user populations. Future research should therefore prioritize bridging the performance gap between rPPG and established contact-based technologies while leveraging the unique advantages of rPPG, including its non-contact nature, low cost, and potential for passive monitoring using widely available imaging devices.

Drawing on the insights from this roadmap, including the literature review, methodological analyses, and emerging clinical demonstrations, we outline a prioritized translational pathway for advancing rPPG-based heart rate (HR) monitoring toward clinical and regulatory validation. In the near term, the most critical priority is the construction of large, standardized public datasets with synchronized reference electrocardiogram (ECG) signals, demographic diversity, and controlled acquisition conditions. Such datasets are essential to enable robust subject-wise validation, reduce bias associated with skin tone, age, and environmental variability, and facilitate fair benchmarking across algorithms. In particular, datasets capturing dynamic physiological conditions, such as rapid HR fluctuations during exercise, will be necessary to support emerging real-world applications217. In high-impact domains, including telemedicine, in-vehicle monitoring, and virtual reality, publicly available datasets will also accelerate the transition from laboratory research to scalable deployment by enabling consistent evaluation protocols and reproducibility. Furthermore, large-scale datasets are indispensable for training data-intensive deep learning models, for which existing validation frameworks remain incomplete and require further development218.

In the intermediate stage, improving algorithm robustness and generalizability remains a central challenge. This includes the development of standardized preprocessing pipelines, motion-resilient signal extraction methods, and rigorous cross-dataset validation strategies. In addition to real data collection, systematic data augmentation frameworks will play a critical role in enhancing model performance. Future work should move beyond simple interpolation-based approaches and instead incorporate standardized validation criteria that ensure both physiological fidelity and visual realism. For example, synthetic waveform generation should preserve pulse morphology and temporal dynamics, while image-based augmentation should maintain realistic skin texture characteristics. A dual-validation strategy combining physiological consistency metrics and visual fidelity measures may help bridge the synthetic-to-real gap and ensure that augmented datasets improve generalization without introducing artifacts78,219,220.

Further improvements are also required in the stages preceding rPPG signal estimation and following initial HR extraction. In particular, the selection and tracking of regions of interest (ROIs) remain critical determinants of measurement accuracy, as performance varies across facial regions under different illumination conditions and motion levels221,222. While prior work has introduced signal quality index (SQI) benchmarks24, additional validation under diverse real-world scenarios is necessary to support adaptive and real-time ROI selection. Emerging vision foundation models, such as Sapiens223, offer promising opportunities to enhance robustness through improved face detection, keypoint localization, and surface modeling. In parallel, incorporating uncertainty quantification and structured failure-mode analysis may enable systems to identify unreliable measurements and provide low-confidence warnings under adverse conditions such as excessive motion or poor illumination196. The integration of auxiliary signals, such as inertial measurements, may further support motion artifact compensation, which remains underexplored in current rPPG pipelines224–226.

Clarifying the role of rPPG within the broader ecosystem of cardiac monitoring technologies is another essential step toward clinical translation. While 12-lead ECG remains the gold standard for diagnosis, and wearable contact PPG enables continuous monitoring, rPPG offers a distinct advantage as a non-contact modality. Its potential lies in large-scale screening, telehealth integration, and passive monitoring in everyday environments. Identifying the appropriate “ecological niche” for rPPG, where its unique advantages outweigh its current limitations, will be critical for guiding both research priorities and clinical adoption.

In the longer term, the deployment of rPPG algorithms on mobile and edge platforms represents a key opportunity for real-world impact. Achieving this will require lightweight models, efficient inference, and robustness to heterogeneous user conditions. Model compression and pruning techniques may help reduce computational complexity while maintaining performance227–229. Integration with multimodal sensing frameworks, such as combined RGB and mmWave systems, may further improve reliability in challenging environments and address limitations related to motion, privacy, and signal variability230. Importantly, prospective real-world studies will be required to evaluate system performance in practical deployment scenarios, including telemedicine platforms, mobile devices, and ambient monitoring systems, while reporting clinically meaningful metrics aligned with emerging regulatory standards for AI-enabled medical technologies.

Beyond healthcare settings, incorporating rPPG into real-world application platforms may unlock additional use cases. For example, integration into virtual reality environments can enable adaptive biofeedback systems that respond dynamically to users’ physiological states, enhancing immersion and user experience. Similarly, multi-task learning frameworks that jointly perform identity recognition and physiological estimation have demonstrated potential for improving signal robustness and reliability231.

Future research should explore unsupervised and self-supervised learning paradigms to reduce reliance on labeled datasets, which remain scarce and difficult to acquire. Recent studies have demonstrated that deep neural networks can estimate HR with minimal or no annotations, suggesting a promising direction for improving generalization across diverse environments and populations195,232,233. Leveraging large-scale unlabeled video data may therefore play a key role in advancing rPPG toward robust, scalable, and clinically applicable solutions.

There is no doubt that we are entering a new era characterized by more advanced information technologies. Driven by the synchronous progress in both software and hardware, the real-world application of more flexible in-situ health monitoring has become increasingly feasible. With the support of rPPG techniques, extracting cardiac rhythm from a person only requires using a standard RGB camera, which is widely available today. The scientific principles behind the measurement process have exhibited significant research value. While several trial-stage platforms have emerged, the rPPG technology remains confined to laboratory settings, with a considerable gap to large-scale productization. Within the remote HR estimation pipeline, each substep can exert substantial influence on the final measurement accuracy. It is therefore necessary to systematically review and summarize different components within the rPPG pipeline. Except for algorithmic improvements, deeper considerations are required regarding noise mitigation from the signal source, efficient utilization of available datasets, and deployment at the edge-computing side. This article has methodological limitations, including database and language restrictions and manual inclusion decisions that may introduce selection bias. Additionally, further exploration of clinical applications requires closer cooperation among industry, academia, and clinical practitioners. Any aspect that can achieve a greater breakthrough would accelerate the adoption of more cost-effective HR measurement tools, eliminating the need for purchasing dedicated devices.

Clinical demonstration in simulated hypovolemia: data and processing

We analyzed video and reference signals from the Yale lower-body negative pressure dataset (IRB No. 2000020057; 24 healthy adults). The reference for rPPG evaluation was the cPPG signal, from which ground-truth HR was obtained using the physiological monitor. rPPG signals were extracted from the forehead regions of interest using facial landmark-based tracking. The signals were detrended and processed with a wavelet-based method to derive HR. In the Results, we present data from two representative subjects to illustrate the clinical application of rPPG, alongside the simultaneously recorded ground-truth HR for comparison.
