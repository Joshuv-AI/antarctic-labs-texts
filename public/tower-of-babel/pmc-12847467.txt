CTHRC1/TGF-β/Smad axis serves as therapeutic targets in inhibiting oral squamous cell carcinoma by mediating ferroptosis
Authors: Shihao Huang, Yiming Jiao, Yongchun Chang, Yu Feng, Guixian Mu, Linli Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollagen triple helix repeat containing 1 (CTHRC1) is an oncogene in numerous human cancers including oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). However, whether and how CTHRC1 influences OSCC progression through ferroptosis has not been completely clarified.MethodsqRT-PCR were conducted to assess CTHRC1 expression in OSCC. The proliferation of OSCC cells were assessed through 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay, colony formation, and CCK-8 assays. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the apoptotic cells of OSCC cells. Ferroptosis was assessed based on the amounts of MDA, lipid ROS, intracellular Fe2+, and ferroptosis-related proteins. TGF-β/Smad pathway-related protein were detected by Western blot.ResultsPronounced overexpression of CTHRC1 was observed in OSCC cell lines (SCC-9, CAL-27 and SCC-25). Knockdown CTHRC1 overtly inhibited cell proliferation but induced apoptosis in OSCC cells. Moreover, silencing of CTHRC1 could stimulate ferroptosis, as well as inhibit the activation of TGF-β/Smad pathway. Treatment with SRI-011381, a TGF-β/Smad pathway specific agonist, significantly reversed the promoting effect of CTHRC1 silencing on ferroptosis in OSCC cells.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that knockdown of CTHRC1 stimulates ferroptosis in OSCC through inhibiting TGF-β/Smad pathway, which provides important experimental data and theoretical basis for the management, diagnosis, and treatment of OSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04354-6.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for 95% of all the head and neck cancer cases, arising from the squamous tissues as a form of malignant oral cancer [1]. This is a devastating and fatal disease that typically affects people aged 50 to 80, but since the beginning of the 21st century, its incidence rate has been on the rise, especially among the younger population [2]. The onset of OSCC is affected by multiple factors, consisting of smoking, drinking alcohol products, betel nut consumption, and unhealthy dietary habits [3, 4]. Although there has been substantial advancement in the detection and management of OSCC, a considerable proportion of patients succumb to the disease within a span of five years [5]. Moreover, even after successful intervention, late initial presentation of advanced tumors and frequent recurrence adversely impact the long-term prognosis [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to gain a thorough understanding of the development of OSCC and explore other therapeutic approaches.

Ferroptosis was initially identified by Dixon et al. in 2012 [7]. It represents a newly discovered type of controlled cell death triggered by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, characterized by elevated intracellular Fe2+ levels and accumulation of lipid peroxides. This results in a buildup of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent oxidative cell death [8, 9]. Emerging studies have revealed that targeted ferroptosis could be an effective approach to combating the resistance of cancer cells [10, 11]. Whether employed in isolation or synergistically with other chemotherapeutic agents, agents that elicit ferroptosis are expected to trigger this form of cell death in cancer cells, particularly those that are resistant to traditional treatments [12, 13]. Several human cancers have been reported to be sensitive to ferroptosis, such as breast cancer [14], pancreatic cancer [15], ovarian cancer [16], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [17] and of course, OSCC [18–24]. The CTHRC1 gene, which encodes the collagen triple helix repeat containing 1, is located on chromosome 8q22.3, and has the capacity to encode a 30 kDa secreted protein [25]. It is significantly expressed and serves as an oncogene in various types of human cancers including non-small cell lung cancer [26], colorectal cancer [27], and melanoma [28]. In gastric cancer, CTHRC1 is regarded as a ferroptosis-related modular gene and associated with patient prognosis [29]. More importantly, CTHRC1 has been associated with unfavorable outcomes of OSCC patients and can induce the migration of OSCC cells [30, 31]. However, it remains unclear whether and how CTHRC1 affects OSCC progression through ferroptosis. In this study, we uncovered a novel mechanism of CTHRC1 to affect OSCC progression, indicating that silencing of CTHRC1 can induce ferroptosis through inhibiting TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thus inhibiting the development of OSCC. These results may broaden the horizons of underlying mechanism of CTHRC1 in OSCC pathogenesis and provide more therapeutic targets for OSCC patients.

Methods

Cells and reagents

Three OSCC cell lines (SCC-9, CAL-27, and SCC-25) and the normal oral keratinocyte cell culture (NHOK) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection Company (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Gibco (Rockville, MD, USA) provided us DMEM/F12 medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS). TGF-β/Smad pathway agonist SRI-011381 was procured from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). General Biol (Chuzhou, China) synthesized small-interfering RNA (siRNA)-CTHRC1-1/-2 (si-CTHRC1-1/-2) and its negative control (si-NC). Transfection reagent Lipofectamine 3000 was obtained from Invitrogen (Karlsruhe, Germany). The total RNA Extraction Kit was acquired from Promega (Madison, WI, USA), while the First Strand Kit and QuantiFast SYBR® Green PCR Kit were supplied by Qiagen (Dusseldorf, GER). Dojindo Laboratories (Shanghai, China) provided the CCK-8 solution and FerroOrange solution. From Abcam (Cambridge, UK), 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay Kit, primary antibodies (p-Smad3, p-Smad2, Smad3, Smad2, TGF-β, FTH1, SLC7A11, GPX4, ACSL4 and GAPDH), and the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for western blotting were available. Iron Assay Kit, Molecular Probes C11-BODIPY581/591, and crystal violet were available from Sigma‒Aldrich (San Luis, MO, USA). Beyotime (Shanghai, China) supplied cell malondialdehyde (MDA) assay Kit. The apoptosis detection Kit, RIPA lysis buffer, BCA Kit, and ECL Kit were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Rockford, MD, USA).

Bioinformatics analysis

GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) and TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer) was adopted to observe the expression of CTHRC1 in tumors.

Cell culture, transfection and treatment

NHOK and OSCC cell lines were cultured in DMEM/F12 containing with 10% FBS (5% CO2 at 37 °C). With the aid of Lipofectamine 3000, CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were co-transfected with the synthetic reagents (si-CTHRC1-1/-2 or si-NC) for 48 h. CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were pretreated with Z-VAD-FMK (ZVAD, an apoptosis inhibitor) and ferrostatin1 (Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor) at a concentration of 10 µM for 2 h. The cells that were transfected received treatment with SRI-011381 (10 µM) for one day. Thereupon, cells were gathered for subsequent experiments.

Isolation of total RNA and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from OSCC cells using a total RNA Extraction Kit, after which cDNA was synthesized with the help of the First Strand Kit. For qRT-PCR analysis, we employed the QuantiFast SYBR® Green PCR Kit according to the instructions provided. Gene relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with normalization performed using GAPDH. Table 1 listed the primers utilized in this research.

Cell proliferation assays

The OSCC cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/mL for various time intervals (0, 24, 48, and 72 h) before the addition of the CCK-8 solution (15 µL). After incubating 2 h, we employed microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Experiments were conducted to assess the proliferative abilities of OSCC cells through colony formation. Approximately 1 × 103 CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were placed in plates with 6-well for incubating 2 weeks, with the medium being replaced twice a week. The resulting cell colonies were then fixed in methanol, followed by staining for 20 min using 0.1% crystal violet. We then counted cell colonies using an Olympus light microscope (Tokyo, Japan) after being washed with PBS.

EdU-positive cells were assessed using a EdU proliferation assay Kit. OSCC cells were first exposed to 50 µM EdU and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Afterwards, they were fixed using a solution containing 4% formaldehyde, and permeabilized by treating them with 0.5% Triton X-100 for a period of 20 min. Following this, the cells were treated with a 1× Apollo reaction cocktail and left at room temperature for 30 min. To visualize DNA, DAPI (4’,6-diamidino2-phenylindole) was employed as a staining agent for an additional duration of 30 min. A fluorescence microscope was utilized to examine the cells that were positive for EdU (scale bar = 50 μm).

Apoptosis determination

According to the manufacturer’s guidelines of apoptosis Kit, the apoptotic OSCC cells were evaluated. The re-suspended CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells (2 × 105) were labeled with Annexin V-FITC (10 µL), followed by incubation with PI (5 µL) at 25 °C in the absence of light for 15 min, then mixed with 500 µL binding buffer. Subsequently, the apoptotic cells were measured with a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Western blotting assay

CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were meticulously lysed utilizing RIPA buffer, and the protein concentrations were determined by a BCA Protein Kit. The resultant proteins were subsequently subjected to separation via SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis before being transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (p-Smad3, p-Smad2, Smad3, Smad2, TGF-β, FTH1, SLC7A11, GPX4, ACSL4 and GAPDH; all diluted at 1:1000) and subsequent incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies (at 1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. With the aid of an ECL detection Kit, immunoblotting results were visualized.

Measurement of ferroptosis signatures

The relative levels of MDA in OSCC cells were assessed using a commercially available Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Besides, The Iron Assay Kit (Sigma- Aldrich, USA) was used to measure total iron and Fe2 + content in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells. Lipid ROS measurement was carried out using the Molecular Probes C11-BODIPY581/591. Cells (1 × 105) were plated in 12-well plates, and exposed to the Kit for 30 min without exposure to light. Subsequently, cells were rinsed three times with PBS. Lipid ROS was examined by flow cytometer.

Immunofluorescent tests

The OSCC cells in different groups were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were exposed to a solution containing FerroOrange at a concentration of 1 mM in PBS for 1 h at a temperature of 37 °C. Subsequently, the cell nuclei were treated with DAPI for a duration of 30 min. Finally, the samples were immediately visualized using a fluorescence microscope (scale bar = 50 μm).

Statistical analysis

The disparities within the data were meticulously evaluated using one-way ANOVA and Student’s t-tests as appropriate. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software v22.0. The data were indicated as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was counted as statistically significant.

Results

CTHRC1 is at high expression levels in OSCC cell lines

In order to investigate if CTHRC1 plays a pivotal role in OSCC, the TCGA and GEPIA databases were utilized to examine the expression differences of CTHRC1. The data revealed notably higher CTHRC1 expression in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) tissues (Fig. 1A). CTHRC1 expression in OSCC cell lines and NHOK was examined, indicating that CTHRC1 was highly expressed in OSCC cell lines, especially in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells (Fig. 1B, P < 0.001). Therefore, CAL-27 and SCC-25 cell lines were selected for subsequent experiments.

Silencing of CTHRC1 inhibits the proliferation but stimulates apoptosis of OSCC cells

To evaluate the roles of CTHRC1 in the progression of OSCC in vitro, CTHRC1 expression was firstly silenced in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, transfected with si-CTHRC1-1 or si-CTHRC1-2 resulted in a significant reduction of CTHRC1 expression in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells in contrast to the si-NC group (P < 0.001), whilst the inhibition of CTHRC1 expression was relatively higher in the si-CTHRC1-1 group than that in the si-CTHRC1-2 group. Therefore, we have opted to proceed with si-CTHRC1-1 for the subsequent experiments.

We observed that the silencing of CTHRC1 led to a significant suppression of the proliferative capacities of CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells, as evidenced by a notable decrease in cell viability (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001), colony number (Fig. 2C, P < 0.01), and EdU-positive cells (Fig. 2D, P < 0.05, P < 0.01) in the si-CTHRC1-1 group compared to the si-NC group. Additionally, by contrast to CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells transfected with si-NC, the apoptosis rate of CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were remarkably enhanced following transfection of si-CTHRC1-1in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells (Fig. 2E, P < 0.01, P < 0.001).

Silencing of CTHRC1 induces ferroptosis in OSCC cells

Ferroptosis-related proteins in OSCC cells were then measured through western blotting following transfection of si-CTHRC1-1 or si-NC. As manifested in Fig. 3A, in contrast to the si-NC group, anti-ferroptosis-related protein levels in the si-CTHRC1-1 group including FTH1, SLC7A11, and GPX4 were dramatically reduced, whilst the ferroptosis-promoting protein ACSL4 expression was markedly elevated. Concurrently, the signatures of ferroptosis, such as elevated levels of total iron and Fe2+ (Fig. 3B, P < 0.01, P < 0.001), lipid ROS (P < 0.001), and MDA (P < 0.01, P < 0.001) were observed in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells after transfection with si-CTHRC1-1, providing further evidence for the initiation of ferroptosis. FerroOrange is a dye that does not emit fluorescence when it is not bound to ferrous ions (Fe2+), but shows a fluorescent signal once it binds to these ions and enters the cell. We found that compared to the si-NC group, the average fluorescence intensity of Fe2+ was significantly enhanced in the si-CTHRC1-1 group (Fig. 3C, P < 0.001). To investigate the potential relationship between the antiproliferative effect of CTHRC1 and cell death, CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells were pretreated with ZVAD (an apoptosis inhibitor) and Fer-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor) at a concentration of 10 µM for 2 h, followed by treatment with si-CTHRC1-1. We found that the cytotoxic effect of CTHRC1 on proliferation was partially alleviated by pretreatment with ZVAD and Fer-1(Fig. 3D, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001).

Silencing of CTHRC1 inactivates TGF-β/Smad pathway

TGF-β/Smad pathway is generally activated during the pathogenesis of various types of cancers [32, 33]. We therefore explored the relationship between CTHRC1 and TGF-β/Smad pathway in the progression of OSCC in vitro. As illustrated in Fig. 4, silencing of CTHRC1 significantly reduced the expression levels of of p-Smad2/Smad2, p-Smad3/Smad3 and TGF-β (P < 0.01, P < 0.0001), which are pivotal indicators of TGF-β/Smad pathway activity. However, it did not have any impacts on the protein levels of Smad2 and Smad3.

TGF-β/Smad pathway activation reverses the promoting effect of CTHRC1 Silencing on ferroptosis in OSCC cells

SRI-011381, a TGF-β/Smad pathway specific agonist, was subjected to CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells, and the interaction of CTHRC1 with TGF-β/Smad pathway during OSCC cell ferroptosis was then ascertained. It was found that treatment with SRI-011381 in CAL-27 and SCC-25 cells markedly reversed the regulatory effects of CTHRC1 silencing on ferroptosis-related protein expression (Fig. 5A). Subsequent experiments demonstrated that treatment with SRI-011381 mitigated the enhancing effects of CTHRC1 silencing on the generation of total iron (Fig. 5B, P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001), Fe2+(P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001), lipid ROS (P < 0.05, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001), and MDA (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001). Notably, silencing of CTHRC1 resulted in an increase of fluorescence intensity of Fe2+ in OSCC cells, which was similarly attenuated by SRI-011381 treatment (Fig. 5C, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001).

Discussion

OSCC is a significant public health issue and considered the primary cause of mortality related to oral diseases in numerous nations. It has been documented that the prevalence tends to be higher in lower/middle income countries or developing nations compared to developed ones [34]. Recent global assessments indicate that there were more than 360,000 fresh instances and approximately 180,000 fatalities recorded in 2020 [35]. The findings of a systematic review revealed that in certain developed western countries, the economic burden of OSCC amounted to an average of 75% of the Gross Domestic Product per capita [36]. The mean cost for treating OSCC can range from $3,343 in early stages to $24,890 in advanced stages in the United Kingdom alone [37]. The economic burden associated with global healthcare is progressively escalating. Therefore, it is necessary to extensively seek effective methods for treating OSCC. Our findings indicated that silencing of CTHRC1 can promote ferroptosis through inactivating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby attenuating the development of OSCC.CTHRC1, highly expressed in various malignant tumors, is shown to be a carcinogenic agent to promote cancer progression through inducing growth and metastasis. For example, high level of CTHRC1 exhibit a profound correlation with the carcinogenesis and metastasis in gastric cancer patients [38]. CTHRC1 is overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancer and enhances migratory and invasive potentials of cancer cell [26]. In addition, the highly expressed of CTHRC1 is reported to serve as a liver metastasis-associated gene in colorectal cancer [27]. In this study, pronounced overexpression of CTHRC1 was also observed in OSCC cell lines, suggesting that it may be associated with the progression of OSCC. Additionally, our functional experiments indicated the inhibiting effect of CTHRC1 silencing on the proliferative capacities, and its promoting effect on the apoptosis of OSCC cells. These results implied that CTHRC1was also a carcinogenic factor in OSCC progression, while it silencing can attenuate OSCC development through dampening proliferation and potentiating apoptosis in cancer cells.

In recent years, studies have suggested that ferroptosis was implicated in the initiation and advancement of tumors. Several genes associated with tumors have been found to regulate ferroptosis in OSCC, such as SLC3A2 [39], CDH4 [18], and NFE2L1 [40]. Given the suppressive effect of CTHRC1 silencing on OSCC cell growth, we speculated that CTHRC1 may also be implicated in modulating ferroptosis in OSCC. As expected, it was shown that knocking-down CTHRC1 markedly reduced the amounts of anti-ferroptosis proteins (FTH1, SLC7A11, and GPX4), while promoted the ferroptosis-promoting protein ACSL4 expression. Moreover, further experiments were conducted to evaluate the contents of MDA, lipid ROS, total iron and intracellular Fe2+, the signatures of ferroptosis. Our findings unveiled a remarkable escalation in the amounts of MDA, lipid ROS, total iron and intracellular Fe2+ upon CTHRC1 knockdown, suggesting that silencing of CTHRC1 can induce ferroptosis in OSCC cells. T The dysregulation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway is significantly involved in the development of different types of cancers including OSCC [41, 42]. Furthermore, it has been reported to be implicated in modulating ferroptosis in lung cancer [43]. We therefore speculated that there may be certain interaction between CTHRC1 and TGF-β/Smad pathway in modulating ferroptosis in OSCC. In the present study, silencing of CTHRC1 significantly reduced the expression levels of TGF-β, p-Smad2, and p-Smad3, indicating knocking-down CTHRC1 suppressed the activation of TGF-β/Smad pathway. Notably, TGF-β/Smad pathway agonist (SRI-011381) reversed the regulatory effect of CTHRC1 silencing on ferroptosis signatures. Collectively, our findings indicated that silencing of CTHRC1 promotes ferroptosis through inactivating TGF-β/Smad pathway in OSCC.

Certain constraints are evident in the present study. First, only CTHRC1 silencing was employed in cell culture. To enhance the rigor of our findings, future studies should include an investigation into the impact of CTHRC1 overexpression on cells. Second, it may be necessary to conduct in vivo tests to investigate the influences of CTHRC1 on TGF-β/Smad signaling pathways. Third, it is possible that there are variations in homology between humans and mice. It is essential to conduct additional clinical experiments to confirm these discoveries.

Conclusion

To recapitulate briefly, the present study uncovers a novel action mechanism of CTHRC1 in OSCC tumorigenesis, indicating that silencing of CTHRC1 can inactivate TGF-β/Smad pathway to induce ferroptosis in cancer cells, thereby attenuating the development of OSCC. This could provide additional details and perspectives on potential clinical treatments for OSCC.
