Serum baseline tryptase levels across chronic kidney disease stages: A retrospective report
Authors: Emily Gansert, Ricardo J Estrada-Mendizabal, Dayne H Voelker, Evamaria Anvari, Mia E Truman, Claire I Yee, Sergio E Chiarella, Alexei Gonzalez-Estrada
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a prevalent disorder affecting more than 1 in 7 adults in the United States.1 It has been associated with elevated serum baseline tryptase (sBT) levels independent of an allergic pathology, which may exceed the standard reference value of less than 11.5 ng/mL as kidney function declines. This increase has been attributed to several factors, including impaired renal clearance and increased mast cell activation associated with renal fibrosis and inflammation.2, 3, 4, 5 Despite the clinical utility of sBT level, the existing data on the changes occurring across CKD stages are limited and there are no standard reference values. A prior study in Spain demonstrated a correlation between worsening kidney function and elevated sBT level, especially in CKD stages 4 and 5, and in patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) versus in patients with CKD stages 1 and 2, as well as an increase in mean tryptase values in men versus in women.6 This study aimed to characterize sBT levels across different CKD stages in a United States–based cohort.

We performed a retrospective chart review of consecutive outpatient nephrology patients across the Mayo Clinic enterprise from January 2020 to July 2025. Patients with a confirmed CKD diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, Revision 10, codes N18.1-N18.5) and an sBT level measured within 40 days of calculation of the patient’s estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were included. Patients were then individually screened by an investigator to confirm the measurement values and dates, CKD stage, and stability of the eGFR over a 3-month period. For patients receiving HD, screening also verified dialysis status. Patients who were hospitalized or experiencing an acute reaction such as anaphylaxis were excluded. CKD stages were defined by eGFR (mL per minute/1.73 m2): stage 1, greater than or equal to 90; stage 2, 60-89; stage 3a, 45-59; stage 3b, 30-44; stage 4, 15-29; and stage 5, less than 15. eGFR was calculated in the patient’s electronic health record by using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation, which is a race-free formula that utilizes serum creatinine, age, and sex. Stages 3a and 3b were combined into stage 3 for statistical analysis. sBT level was measured by utilizing the fluorescence enzyme immunoassay method. Those individuals with conditions known to elevate sBT level, including systemic mastocytosis, hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (HAT), and renal transplant, were excluded. Linear regression was performed with CKD stage as a continuous variable, and group comparisons were analyzed by using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni adjustment.

Results and discussion

A total of 120 patients met the inclusion criteria. The majority were female (51%), White (84%), and not Hispanic/Latino (93%). The median sBT levels (ng/mL) by CKD stage were as follows: stage 1, 4.2; stage 2, 5.1; stage 3, 6.7; stage 4, 10.7; and stage 5, 6.8 (Table I). Of the 6 patients in stage 5, 5 were undergoing HD treatment. A box plot (Fig 1) of median sBT level by CKD stage was performed. There was a statistically significant increase in sBT values associated with an increase in CKD staging (R2 = 0.09 [P = .001]). When the linear assumption was removed, a statistically significant difference was still observed for differences in median sBT levels between the groups by CKD stage (P = .002). The median number of days between measurement of tryptase level and calculation of eGFR was 0 days.

In our cohort, there was a significant association between CKD stage and sBT level. These findings suggest that declining kidney function may influence circulating tryptase concentrations. These same findings have also been noticed in other studies. When Sirvent et al6 examined serum tryptase levels in a population of 153 outpatients with CKD and 35 patients undergoing HD, they found that tryptase levels were higher in stages 4 and 5 and in patients undergoing HD than in stages 1 and 2 (ie, 12.7 ± 7.3 μg/L; 13.8 ± 7.8 μg/L; and 15 ± 8.9 μg/L vs 6.7 ± 5.1 μg/L [P < .01]). These mean values are higher across all stages than in our cohort. Sirvent et al6 also noted that tryptase levels were higher in men than in women. However, compared with our population, their population was mostly male (66.5%) and younger (mean age 60.83 ± 16.13 years). Additionally, in patients undergoing HD, tryptase levels may be elevated because of mast cell–derived tryptase forming complexes with heparin, thus increasing the concentration of active tryptase and further worsening kidney function.2

Furthermore, tryptase has been noted as a proinflammatory marker, and significantly increased levels of tryptase have been observed in patients with declining kidney function versus in those with stable kidney function.7 In 2020, Owens et al7 noted its utility in their 5-biomarker predictive panel alongside serum creatinine, osteopontin, urea, and eGFR. This model outperformed traditional clinical measures such as eGFR or albuminuria to predict future progressive CKD. This study suggests that tryptase could be utilized as a prognostic indicator for CKD progression.

It is important to account for renal function when interpreting elevated sBT values. There are numerous factors that can account for the tryptase level elevation in patients with CKD, including impaired renal clearance and increased mast cell activation. Rising tryptase levels may indicate inflammation and may be linked to renal failure progression, as mast cells release mediators such as endothelin, growth factors, and activation of the renin-angiotensin system.2, 3, 4, 5 A study by Valent et al noted an expected sBT value range of 10 to 50 ng/mL in patients with renal failure.3 Our results showed a higher median sBT level in CKD stage 4 than in stage 5. Although an overall association between advancing CKD stage and higher sBT level was observed, this finding may reflect heterogeneity related to HD exposure in patients with stage 5 CKD.

This study has several limitations, including a small sample size in CKD stages 1 and 5, potential tertiary referral bias, and retrospective design. Future studies should include larger and more balanced stage-specific cohorts to better define reference ranges or develop adjustment models incorporating eGFR. Inclusion of additional institutions may help reduce referral bias and improve generalizability. Prospective studies could further clarify the relationship between renal function and sBT level.

Overall, our findings support an association between CKD stage and sBT level, with higher concentrations observed in patients with more advanced disease. In patients with CKD, sBT level results should be interpreted in the context of renal function. Clinicians should consider obtaining an eGFR concurrently with measurement of sBT level and exercise caution before initiating an extensive evaluation of mast cell disorder, as elevated sBT level in this population may reflect reduced clearance and chronic inflammation rather than systemic mastocytosis or HAT.

Clinical implications

This study’s findings highlight the importance of interpreting sBT levels in the context of kidney function. By incorporating renal function into tryptase level interpretation, unnecessary diagnostic evaluations for conditions such as systemic mastocytosis or HAT may be avoided in patients with CKD.
