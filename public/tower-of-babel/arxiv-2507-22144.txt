Rheological modeling with GENERIC and with the Onsager principle
Miroslav Grmela

Abstract
In this paper we compare three frameworks for modeling flows of complex fluids: (i) local conservations of mass, momentum and energy, (ii) GENERIC, and (iii) Onsager principle. The first is based on the mass, momentum, and energy conservation implied by mechanics, the second on the observed approach of externally unforced fluids to equilibrium states at which their behavior is well described by equilibrium thermodynamics, and the third on the minimal resistance to external influences. The comparison is illustrated on isothermal and incompressible polymeric fluids.



1
Introduction

How do complex fluids (like for instance polymeric fluids and suspensions) flow? This question gave birth to rheology. The same question but with simple fluids (like for instance water) replacing the complex fluids gave birth to hydrodynamics
[
1
]
. Rheology is an extension of hydrodynamics from simple fluids to complex fluids. The mathematical models of fluids that are emerging in hydrodynamics and in rheology are expected to reproduce first of all their observed flow behavior. There are however also other types of the behavior of fluids that can be readily observed.
For instance it is the overall mass, momentum, and energy conservation or the approach of externally unforced fluids to thermodynamic equilibrium at which their observed behavior is found to be well described by the classical equilibrium thermodynamics. While this type of behavior is not of a direct interest to hydrodynamics and rheology, correct mathematical models have to reproduce it. Due to the universal nature of some of the ”hydrodynamically and rheologically uninteresting” observations the requirement that the governing equations reproduce them can be expected to provide a universal framework for the governing equations. The requirement of the mass, momentum, and energy conservation does indeed provide a framework for the hydrodynamic equations (the equations have the form of local conservation laws: time derivative equals divergence of a flux). This framework can be extended to rheological equations by requiring in addition either the compatibility with the equilibrium thermodynamics
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
or the compatibility with the Onsager variational principle
[
6
]
. In this paper we compare these two types of requirements.

3
Onsager principle

In this section we put our attention directly on modeling the rheological behavior. How do complex fluids react to an imposed flow?
The physical basis that will give us the framework for our investigation in this section is the Onsager principle according to which the fluids react to external forces in such a way that their resistance is minimal.
The resistance is measured by the entropy production, the minimization is subjected to constraints. The momentum field
{\boldmath\mbox{$u$}}({\boldmath\mbox{$r$}})
is not an unknown field in this investigation but a quantity with which the imposed flow is expressed. The unknown quantity that emerges in solutions to the governing equations is solely the internal structure
{\boldmath\mbox{$c$}}({\boldmath\mbox{$r$}})
through which the extra stress tensor (that is a directly measured quantity) is expressed.

The Onsager principle is a lift of the MaxEnt principle (acting in the state space) to the space of vector fields
[

,

The physical basis of the Onsager principle is the approach, in the space of vector fields, from a mesoscopic description involving time evolution to another mesoscopic description that also involves time evolution but takes into account less details. We shall investigate the relation between the MaxEnt and the Onsager principle in Section

4
Relation between GENERIC and the Onsager principle

MaxEnt principle expresses approach of a mesoscopic well established and autonomous theory to the classical equilibrium thermodynamics. The approach is made by maximizing the entropy subjected to constraints. The maximization is made in the course of the time evolution governed by Eq.(
1
).

According to the Onsager principle,
macroscopic systems react to imposed external forces by minimizing their resistance. Where does this principle come from? It certainly does not come from the Hamiltonian mechanics but it comes from the dissipative mechanics that is compatible with thermodynamics (i.e. from GENERIC (
1
)). We have already demonstrated it in the previous section. The strategy of solving Eq.(
2
) by first solving the second equation with zero on its left hand side, then inserting the solution to the first equation and solving it, is from the physical point of view, the statement that the time evolution proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, described by the second equation, a well established mesoscopic level on which
{\boldmath\mbox{$c$}}({\boldmath\mbox{$r$}})
serves as the state variable (Level
c
) approaches another well established mesoscopic level on which
{\boldmath\mbox{$u$}}({\boldmath\mbox{$r$}})
serves as the state variable (Level
u
). The time evolution in the second stage is described by the first equation with
{\boldmath\mbox{$c$}}=hat{{\boldmath\mbox{$c$}}}({\boldmath\mbox{$u$}})
that is the internal structure reached, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, in the first stage.
This means that the MaxEnt principle and the Onsager principle are principles of the same type, both expressing the compatibility of two well established and autonomous levels. In the former these levels are a mesoscopic level and the equilibrium level, in the latter they are a mesoscopic level (Level
c
) and another mesoscopic level with less details (Level
u
). The time evolution on the Level
c
is assumed to be faster than the time evolution on the Level
u
.

Summing up, we list the advantages and disadvantages of the GENERIC and the Onsager-principle rheological modeling. The explicit involvement of mechanics (in the Poisson bracket) in the GENERIC modeling makes to appear both the force with which the imposed flow acts on the internal structure (the type of advection) and the way the internal structure acts on the flow (the extra stress tensor) as explicit expressions. They both appear as a consequence of the Hamiltonian kinematics. In the Onsager-principle modeling such expressions have to be found separately in an analysis based on mechanics that is missing in the Onsager principle. The danger in the separate mechanical analysis is that two expressions may not be mutually compatible. The Hamiltonian structure (in particular the skew symmetry of the Poisson bivector
L
) clearly relates one to the other. A change in the advection (for instance by introducing Schowalter
[
20
]
modification) has to be accompanied by a corresponding change in the expression for the extra stress tensor.

The advantage of the Onsager-principle modeling is that it addresses also other external forces than imposed flows. The Onsager-principle modeling is focused on the internal structure. The overall flow is just one possible way to act on it. The thermodynamics emerging in the Onsager-principle analysis is the rate thermodynamics generated by the dissipation potential. On the other hand, the GENERIC modeling is focused on the overall flow. The internal structure is a one way to influence it. The emerging thermodynamics (the classical equilibrium thermodynamics) is generated by the entropy.
