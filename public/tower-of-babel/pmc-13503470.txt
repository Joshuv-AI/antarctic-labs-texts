Pregnancy Ambivalence, History of Pregnancy, Condom Use, and Contraceptive Use Among U.S. Youth Experiencing Homelessness
Authors: Annalynn Galvin, Jennifer Jones, Utsav Arandhara, Mary Lingwall, Nikhil Padhye, Adeline Nyamathi, Marguerita Lightfoot, Mary Paul, Yasmeen Quadri, Michael Businelle, Diane Santa Maria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesYouth experiencing homelessness (YEH) are 5–8 times more likely than housed peers to become pregnant. A more nuanced examination of YEH pregnancy desire may better inform pregnancy and pregnancy prevention guidance. This study examined pregnancy ambivalence, pregnancy history, condom use, and contraceptive use among sexually active YEH.MethodsWe utilized baseline data of YEH aged 16–25 from a randomized controlled trial published elsewhere (Santa Maria et al., 2021). Pregnancy desire was categorized as ambivalence (43%) and non-ambivalence: desiring pregnancy (10%) and not desiring pregnancy (47%). We calculated adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) between pregnancy ambivalence with three outcomes (pregnancy history, condom, and any contraceptive use), stratified by male/female participants, controlling for demographics (e.g., gender, sexual orientation).ResultsAmong YEH (n = 446, 44% female, 50% male, 6% another gender; mean age 21.1 years), 39% reported pregnancy history, 41% reported recent condom use, and 15% reported contraceptive use. Adjusted prevalence ratios stratified by male/female participants indicated that pregnancy ambivalence was notsignificantly associated with pregnancy history, recent condom use, and reported contraceptive use.Conclusions for PracticeBy characterizing YEH pregnancy ambivalence, condom and contraceptive use, and the pregnancy history, providers may develop tailored policies and programs that respond to the preferences and sexual decision-making needs of YEH who are more vulnerable to undesired pregnancy, regardless of pregnancy desire.

Introduction

In the U.S., approximately 150,000 children experienced homelessness on a single night in 2024 (De Sousa et al., 2024). With 30–60% of youth experiencing homelessness (YEH; aged 18–25) reporting a current or past history of pregnancy (Kachingwe et al., 2019), YEH are 5–8 times more likely to become pregnant than their housed peers (Winetrobe et al., 2013). This may be attributable to YEH’s higher comparative rates of sexual experience, younger age of sexual debut, increased number of sexual partners, increased likelihood of substance use during sex, and increased engagement in survival sex (Hathazi et al., 2009; Semborski et al., 2021). Nearly three-fourths of these pregnancies are considered unintended (e.g., unwanted, mistimed; Gelberg et al., 2008), compared to less than half among youth in general (Kost et al., 2023). While unintended pregnancies do not necessarily result in negative child outcomes, parents reported significant mental health challenges as a result (Mark & Cowan, 2022). Pregnant and parenting YEH are more vulnerable to mental health challenges based on higher violence exposure and prolonged durations of homelessness (Eapen et al., 2023). YEH are also at high risk for sexual assault, with 22% reporting sexual assault and 24% forced sex (Santa Maria et al., 2020). Since sexual trauma is associated with pregnancy complications and poor outcomes (Rodgers et al., 2003), understanding the complexity behind contraception and pregnancy desire is critical for YEH.

Gender and partner dynamics also affect contraception preferences and non-use for women experiencing homelessness, particularly with discretion and reproductive coercion (Thompson et al., 2023). Prioritizing basic needs for food and physical safety may make sexual and reproductive prevention behaviors difficult to prioritize (Eapen et al., 2023). Despite most women preferring not to become pregnant while homeless (Galvin et al., 2024; Kozlowski et al., 2022), YEH consistently report higher contraception non-use and resultant pregnancies, compared to youth stably housed (Begun, 2015; Eapen et al., 2023). Rather than effectiveness, contraception non-use was more attributed to side effects, fertility resumption, and accessibility (Thompson et al., 2023; Galvin et al., 2022).

Under a reproductive justice lens that prioritizes individual fertility preferences, we must also account for YEH who either desire pregnancy or feel ambivalent toward pregnancy, who may require different or unique preventive guidance. Some YEH and women experiencing homelessness viewed pregnancy as an opportunity to access supportive services and housing and forge a new, hopeful path in life (Kozlowski et al., 2022; Smid et al., 2010; Winetrobe et al., 2013). These perceived benefits may also contribute to pregnancy ambivalence, an understudied category of desire for future pregnancy (Bruckner et al., 2004; Galvin et al., 2022; Sheeder et al., 2010).

Youth and young adults who report “ambivalent” pregnancy intentions and prevention behaviors may fail to successfully prevent unintended pregnancy because they may be more likely to “not care enough about not becoming pregnant” to try to avoid conceiving (Sheeder et al., 2010; Stevens-Simon et al., 2001). Pregnancy ambivalence may also explain the paradoxical underutilization of contraception, especially among Latinas, women of low socioeconomic status, Black women, and YEH (Begun et al., 2019; Jones, 2017; Manze et al., 2021). Since YEH also comprise a higher proportion of gender diverse youth (Hail-Jares et al., 2023), certain YEH subpopulations may be potentially more vulnerable to harm related to undesired pregnancy.

Less is known about how these factors related to pregnancy intention (e.g., pregnancy history, condom use, and contraception) vary across genders, with gaps in understanding male YEH’s reproductive experiences. It is critical to examine reproductive health behaviors for YEH, regardless of gender (Hail-Jares et al., 2023). To inform personalized sexual and reproductive healthcare guidance for YEH, we first need to characterize pregnancy intention (and ambivalence), contraception, and pregnancy history among YEH. Thus, this study examined YEH pregnancy desire and ambivalence, contraceptive use behaviors, and pregnancy history.

Methodology

Participants and Procedures

This cross-sectional study examined baseline data from a YEH cohort (aged 16–25; n = 450) in a randomized wait-list controlled trial (Santa Maria et al., 2020). Eligibility criteria included being aged 16–25, English-speaking, experiencing homelessness per the McKinney-Vento Homelessness Assistance Act, and not planning to leave the local area while enrolled. Exclusion criteria included a Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine-Short Form (Davis et al., 1991) score < 4 or experiencing mental distress or noticeable intoxication when screened. Eligible YEH had to report sexual intercourse in their lifetime for this study since we did not ask abstinent YEH about pregnancy and contraception, and thus excluded them from this study.

We conducted research in accordance with the principles stated in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and later amendments, with approval from the UTHealth Houston Center for the Protection of Human Subjects Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent before study enrollment. Recruitment sites in Houston, TX, included shelters, drop-in centers, and federally qualified healthcare centers. Additional recruitment and procedures are described elsewhere (Santa Maria et al., 2021).

Measures

Pregnancy ambivalence comprised three items: “I think I would be happier if I got pregnant or got someone pregnant now”; “I think it would be good for me to get pregnant or get someone else pregnant at this time”; and “I would really like to get pregnant or get someone else pregnant now.” Responses included “very true”, “sort of true”, “not very true”, or “not at all true”. The adapted ambivalence items demonstrated excellent construct validity and reliability (α = 0.95).

Following the ‘mid-response count’ methodology (Sheeder et al., 2010), we categorized participants who responded to any of the questions with “sort of true” or “not very true” responses as “ambivalent” (i.e., ambivalent to becoming pregnant at this time). We chose categorization, versus using a total or mean score, to ensure clarity in interpretability of results comparing pregnancy ambivalent and pregnancy non-ambivalent groups. We further categorized non-ambivalent participants due to potential differences between YEH unambivalently desiring to avoid pregnancy and unambivalently desiring pregnancy. We categorized participants who consistently responded “very true” as unambivalently desiring pregnancy and participants with consistent responses of “not true at all” as unambivalently not desiring pregnancy after sensitivity analyses and examining response distributions.

Outcomes included (1) current contraceptive use (using ‘birth control’ for YEH interpretability): “Are you or your sexual partner(s) currently using birth control?” and responses were “yes” (where participants then specified the specific type of contraception like pills, patches, long-acting reversible contraception, etc.), “no”, and “I don’t know” (with “no” and “I don’t know” combined based on response distributions), (2) condom use during most recent intercourse: “The last time you had sexual intercourse, did you or your partner use a condom?” and responses were “yes”, “no”, and “I’ve never had sex” (operationalized as “yes” and “no” since we excluded participants who have not had sex), and (3) lifetime pregnancy: “Have you ever been pregnant or gotten someone else pregnant?”, with responses of “yes” or “no”.

We also examined covariates associated with pregnancy ambivalence and unintended pregnancy: age in years (continuous, range 16–25), gender identity (categorical: female, male, transgender, genderqueer, neither exclusively male nor female, or another gender), race (categorical: Black only, Hispanic/Latino/a/x only, White only, Asian, American Indian or Alaskan native, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, and multiple races), and sexual orientation (categorical: lesbian, gay, heterosexual, bisexual, pansexual, or something else). Based on response distributions, we collapsed some gender, race, and sexual orientation categories together (e.g., Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Data Analysis

Using R in the RStudio environment, we conducted multiple imputation with chained equations with the mice package (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011), assuming data were missing at random, to reduce potential bias and loss of power due to missing values. Sensitivity analyses examining for possible not-missing-at-random (MNAR) effects were examined, where only gender was associated with missingness patterns. While MNAR could not be excluded completely, sensitivity analyses and stratification of regression models help mitigate potential residual bias.

We calculated univariate statistics (e.g., means, standard deviations, frequencies, percentages) for each variable, then stratified by ambivalence category. Multivariable modified Poisson regression analyses examined adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% confidence intervals, due to the higher frequency of outcomes, to mitigate overestimation of associations. Three models examined associations between pregnancy desire/ambivalence and three reproductive health outcomes, adjusting for demographic covariates. Models were stratified by male or female gender to better capture variation between potentially getting someone pregnant or becoming pregnant, respectively. Given the smaller cell sizes, participants who did not report male or female gender were excluded from regression analyses.

Results

From the original 450 participants, we analyzed 446 participants (Table 1), excluding four with discrepant responses related to ever having sex. The average age was 21.1 years (SD, 2.1, range 16–25), with 50% male participants, 44% female participants, and 6% reporting another gender. Black participants made up most of the sample (61.8%), followed by Another/Mixed Race (17.8%), White (11.2%), and Hispanic/Latino/a/x (9.2%). Most were heterosexual (64.1%), then bisexual (20%). Approximately 43% of participants were ambivalent about pregnancy, and of the 57% who were non-ambivalent, 47% felt non-ambivalent about avoiding pregnancy, and the remaining 10% non-ambivalently desired pregnancy.

Of the 192 ambivalent participants, 44% had a pregnancy history, 37% used a condom at their last sexual intercourse, and 14% were either currently on a contraceptive or had a partner they knew was on contraception (Table 2). For the 208 non-ambivalent participants desiring to avoid pregnancy, 31% had a pregnancy history, 45% used a condom at their last sexual intercourse, and only 12% reported current contraception use. Of the 46 participants who non-ambivalently desired pregnancy, about 46% had a lifetime history of pregnancy or getting someone else pregnant, 33% used condoms at their last sexual intercourse, and 17% were either currently on contraception or with a partner who was on contraception.

Pregnancy ambivalence among participants was not significantly associated with any of the three outcomes, even when controlling for covariates or stratifying by sex (Table 3).

Discussion

This study uniquely characterizes pregnancy ambivalence, non-ambivalent desire to avoid pregnancy, and non-ambivalent desire to become pregnant among a sample of YEH in Houston, TX, who have engaged in sex. When comparing condom use, contraceptive use, and lifetime history of pregnancy by ambivalence categories, we did not find pregnancy desire or ambivalence associated with outcomes after adjusting for gender, age, race/ethnicity, and sexual orientation. We found more pregnancy-ambivalent YEH than other studies, perhaps due to the inclusion of younger, more gender diverse participants: one study found a significant minority of sampled adolescents endorsed ambivalent attitudes toward pregnancy at baseline (Jaccard et al., 2003). While inclusion of both male and female YEH broadens research related to reproductive health practices and pregnancy desire, the absence of statistically significant findings in adjusted gender-stratified models limits conclusions regarding whether pregnancy attitudes and behaviors differ across genders. Future research with larger samples and measures designed to capture gender-specific reproductive experiences may clarify whether meaningful differences exist.

Notably, pregnancy ambivalence was not significantly associated with contraception and condom use at last sex nor lifetime history of pregnancy. These findings build on current literature suggesting that pregnancy ambivalence may be conceptually separate decision-making processes despite being interrelated behaviors (Sundstrom et al., 2017). Pregnancy desire and ambivalence may be more salient for YEH due to structural constraints related to healthcare access and competing needs, where desire matters less when options are less available (Eapen et al., 2023). Further inconsistencies or decision changes in contraception and condom use may occur at a different frequency than changing the desire to become pregnant or avoid pregnancy (Begun et al., 2019).

In the current study, only 15% of participants reported using contraception. Another study found that fewer than 25% YEH reporting contraceptive use versus 60% of the general youth population (Bruckner et al., 2004). More youth (41%) in this current study reported using condoms at last sex. This may be partially explained by YEH’s contraception attitudes on side effects (Thompson et al., 2023) and higher dependence on barrier-only contraception like condoms (Eapen et al., 2023). Future research should account for other YEH who may further benefit from variable pregnancy planning guidance, such as YEH engaged in commercial sex exploitation, given the high pregnancy rates among girls with histories of commercial sexual exploitation (Barnert et al., 2020). Additionally, examining the consistency of condom and contraceptive use or examining contraception and condom use over time may better capture in-the-moment sexual and reproductive decision-making, to allow clinicians and public health professionals to provide tailored guidance and support.

About 39% of participants reported a lifetime history of pregnancy. Another study found that youth with negative baseline attitudes toward pregnancy had a next-year pregnancy rate less than 5%, versus over 25% among those with the most favorable attitudes (Jaccard et al., 2003). While pregnancy may be an entry point for housing instability for some YEH forced to leave their original homes when becoming pregnant (Begun et al., 2015), pregnancy history may also shape current pregnancy attitudes; even a pregnancy scare can alter desires and contraceptive behavior, rather than the reverse (Barber at al., 2021; Gatny et al., 2018). Future studies may need to examine other related histories, such as trauma history or sexual assault, in the context of housing instability, contraception decision-making, and pregnancy desire or ambivalence. Studies examining gender perspectives from a dyadic perspective may also better explain relational dynamics amidst housing instability (Arteaga et al., 2020).

This study has a few limitations. We sampled YEH from a single, urban metropolitan area in the south, thus limiting generalizability. This study also had missing data, and if the data were not missing at random, there may be bias. However, sensitivity analyses and multiple imputation with chained equations with our robust sample size is a more rigorous attempt to address missingness than standard listwise deletion or simple mean imputation (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Another limitation is the possibility that male YEH included in the sample do not have perfect information on whether their partners became pregnant, thus limiting the ability to determine whether their behaviors are being influenced by experiences of their partners. This study’s ambivalence measure also does not capture simultaneous positive and negative feelings (Miller et al., 2016). Future research would benefit from a temporal examination of conflicting and simultaneous pregnancy desires, contextualized by demographic and housing history.

This study examines pregnancy ambivalence, condom and contraceptive use, and the lifetime prevalence of pregnancy in a gender-diverse sample of YEH. Despite a larger proportion of YEH reporting pregnancy ambivalence, pregnancy ambivalence did not correlate with condom use, contraceptive use, or lifetime history of pregnancy. This work is a next step toward future longitudinal studies that can inform accessible programs that respond to the evolving needs and preferences of all YEH, inclusive of ambivalent and pregnancy-desiring YEH. Understanding variability in pregnancy attitudes and behaviors may help clinicians and community health workers support continual contraception decision-making, especially for YEH most vulnerable to undesired pregnancy.
