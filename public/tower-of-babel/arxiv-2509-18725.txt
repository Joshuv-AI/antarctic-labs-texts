Barycentric decompositions for extensive monotone divergences
Erkka Haapasalo

Abstract
. We study sets of divergences or dissimilarity measures in a generalized real-algebraic setting which includes the cases of classical and quantum multivariate divergences. We show that a special subset of divergences, the so-called test spectrum, characterizes the rest of the divergences through barycentres and that the extreme points of relevant convex subsets of general divergences are contained within the test spectrum. Only some special parts of the test spectrum may contain non-extreme elements. We are able to fully characterize the test spectrum in the case of classical multivariate divergences. The quantum case is much more varied, and we demonstrate that essentially all the bivariate and multivariate quantum divergences suggested previously in literature are within the test spectrum and extreme within the set of all quantum (multivariate) divergences. This suggests that the variability of quantum divergences is real since all the previously suggested divergences are independent of each other.



1.
Introduction

Both in classical and quantum information theory we need to compare the information contained in a set of states (i.e., probability measures in the classical case and density operators in the quantum case). In the classical case a set
P=\{p^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
of probability measures, i.e., a statistical experiment, is more informative than another statistical experiment
Q=\{q^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
if
P
attains a higher expected payoff in different tests that
Q
. According to Blackwell
[
5
,
6
]
, this is equivalent with the existence of a stochastic operator (Markov kernel)
T
such that
Tp^{\vartheta}=q^{\vartheta}
for all
\vartheta\in\Theta
. In this case we denote
P\succeq Q
. We may study this comparison of experiments also in the quantum regime by defining that a quantum experiment
\vec{\rho}=\{\rho^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
with states (density operators)
\rho^{\vartheta}
is more informative than another experiment
\vec{\sigma}=\{\sigma^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
, symbolically
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
, if there is a quantum channel (a completely positive trace-preserving linear map)
\Phi
such that
\Phi(\rho^{\vartheta})=\sigma^{\vartheta}
for all
\vartheta\in\Theta
.

We are often more interested in more relaxed settings of the comparison of experiments where we have access to multiple copies of the experiments or to suitable ‘catalytic’ experiments. This means that we may ask when
\vec{\rho}=\big\{\rho^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
yields more information than
\vec{\sigma}=\big\{\sigma^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}

in large samples
, i.e.,

\big\{\big(\rho^{\vartheta}\big)^{\otimes n}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}\succeq\big\{\big(\sigma^{\vartheta}\big)^{\otimes n}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. On the other hand, we say that
\vec{\rho}
yields more information than
\vec{\sigma}

catalytically
if there is an experiment
\vec{\tau}=\big\{\tau^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
such that

\big\{\rho^{\vartheta}\otimes\tau^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}\succeq\big\{\sigma^{\vartheta}\otimes\tau^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}.

Some catalysts are essentially universial, i.e., they can catalyze the information content comparison order for any inputs and outputs. This is the case, e.g., for a fully orthogonal catalyst where, for any
\vartheta,\vartheta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,
\vartheta\neq\vartheta^{\prime}
,
\tau^{\vartheta}
and
\tau^{\vartheta^{\prime}}
are orthogonally supported. This is why such trivial catalytic experiments are typically excluded.

We may attempt to approach the problem of comparing the information content in classical and quantum experiments (in the large-sample or catalytic regime) by using particular quantifiers which we call
divergences
: A map
D
on quantum experiments is a divergence if
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
implies
D(\vec{\rho})\geq D(\vec{\sigma})
(data-processing inequality) and, for any experiments
\vec{\rho}=\{\rho^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
and
\vec{\sigma}=\{\sigma^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
,

D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\rho^{\vartheta}\otimes\sigma^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}}}\right)=D(\vec{\rho})+D(\vec{\sigma})\quad\textrm{(tensor-additivity or extensivity)}.

The data-processing inequality is a natural requirement for a measure of information content since such a measure should reflect the information ordering
\succeq
of experiments. Extensivity means that the information content is additive under non-correlated products of experiments. The insistence on extensivity comes from the large-sample and catalytic comparison of experiments: ultimately we want to characterize large-sample or asymptotic majorization through inequalities
D(\vec{\rho})>D(\vec{\sigma})
which are obviously unchanged in the large-sample and catalytic regimes if
D
is extensive.

Let us recall that, if
\Theta=\{1,2\}
, divergences as defined above are often also called
relative entropies
which is arguably the term of choice in this particular setting where the term ‘divergence’ is often used for any monotone map vanishing on repeating pairs
(\rho,\rho)
. This means that also certain one-shot quantities, e.g., smoothed hypothesis-testing quantities, are often called divergences although they are not extensive. However, for us, all the divergences are henceforth assumed to be extensive.

In a generalized setting which includes both the classical and quantum information ordering of experiments, where the set
\Theta=\{1,\ldots,d\}
is finite (and the sample spaces of the probability measures are finite and the Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional), it turns out that there is a special subset
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
within the set of divergences which gives a necessary condition for the large-sample ordering which, in the quantum case, has the following form: whenever
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
are (finite) quantum experiments such that
\Delta(\vec{\rho})>\Delta(\vec{\sigma})
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big(\rho^{(1)}\big)^{\otimes n},\ldots,\big(\rho^{(d)}\big)^{\otimes n}}}\right)\succeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big(\sigma^{(1)}\big)^{\otimes n},\ldots,\big(\sigma^{(d)}\big)^{\otimes n}}}\right)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. This result is a consequence of a real-algebraic Vergleichsstellensatz derived in
[
10
]
. Related results have also been proven in
[
11
]
.

This condition for large-sample information ordering implies in a straightforward manner that, given finite quantum (or classical) experiments
\vec{\rho}
and
\vec{\sigma}
, the condition
\Delta(\vec{\rho})\geq\Delta(\vec{\sigma})
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
implies that
D(\vec{\rho})\geq D(\vec{\sigma})
for all divergences
D
. In the classical case, for
d=2
, this was used to prove that any classical relative entropy (i.e., a bivariate extensive divergence) can be considered as a barycentre over
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
in
[
20
]
. In this special case the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
consists of the maps
(p,q)\mapsto D_{\alpha}(p\|q)
and
(p,q)\mapsto D_{\alpha}(q\|p)
for all
\alpha\in[1/2,\infty]
, where the maps
D_{\alpha}
are the bivariate Rényi relative entropies
[
25
]
. Recall that, for probability measures
p
and
q
on a standard Borel measurable space with supports
{\rm supp}\,p
and
{\rm supp}\,q
and a dominating measure
\nu
(e.g.,
\nu=p+q
), we have

(1)

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\log{q({\rm supp}\,p)},&\alpha=0,\\
\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log{\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dp}{d\nu}}}\right)^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dq}{d\nu}}}\right)^{1-\alpha}}\,d\nu,&\alpha\in(0,1)\\
&{\rm or}\ \alpha\in(1,\infty)\ {\rm and}\ p\ll q,\\
\int\frac{dp}{dq}\log{\frac{dp}{dq}}\,dq,&\alpha=1\ {\rm and}\ p\ll q,\\
\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{q-{\rm ess}\,\sup\frac{dp}{dq}}}\right\}},&\alpha=\infty\ {\rm and}\ p\ll q,\\
\infty&{\rm otherwise},\end{array}}}\right.

with the convention
\log{0}=-\infty
and
p\ll q
means that
p
is absolutely continuous with respect to
q
. Recall that
D_{1}=:D_{\rm KL}
is also called the
Kullback-Leibler relative entropy
and
D_{\infty}=:D_{\rm max}
is called the
max-relative entropy
. Thus, according to
[
20
]
, for any relative entropy
D
, there are inner and outer regular finite measures
\mu_{\pm}:\mathcal{B}\big([1/2,\infty]\big)\to[0,\infty)
such that

(2)

D(p\|q)=\int_{[1/2,\infty]}D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\,d\mu_{+}(\alpha)+\int_{[1/2,\infty]}D_{\alpha}(q\|p)\,d\mu_{-}(\alpha).

In the present work we generalize this result, so that, as special cases, we may characterize multivariate (
d
-variate) classical and quantum divergences as barycentres over special sets
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
of divergences. In the classical case, the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
can be identified with the set of classical multivariate Rényi divergences studied in
[
9
,
31
]
where also the above large-sample result was derived. In the quantum case (as well as in more general settings), the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
consists of a few parts: a subset we denote
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
(‘nd’ for ‘nondegenerate’ or ‘non-derivation’) and subsets
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, of ‘derivations’. We have a normalization function
N:\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that, for all
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
, there is a positive function
\Phi_{\Delta}
on non-normalized experiments (i.e.,
d
-tuples of positive semi-definite operators of any non-zero trace) such that

(3)

\Delta(\vec{\rho})=N(\Delta)\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho})}.

The function
\Phi_{\Delta}
is a monotone homomorphism in the sense that

(ND1)

we have either

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{(1)}\oplus\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\oplus\sigma^{(d)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho})+\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\sigma})\qquad{\rm or}

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{(1)}\oplus\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\oplus\sigma^{(d)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho}),\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\sigma})}}\right\}

for all experiments
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
which can be non-normalized,

(ND2)

we have

\Phi_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{(1)}\otimes\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\otimes\sigma^{(d)}}}\right)=\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho})\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\sigma})

for all experiments
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
which can be non-normalized,

(ND3)

\Phi_{\Delta}(1,\ldots,1)=1
where we consider
1
as a state on a one-dimensional Hilbert space, and

(ND4)

whenever
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
, then
\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho})\geq\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\sigma})
when
N(\Delta)>0
and
\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\rho})\leq\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\sigma})
when
N(\Delta)<0
.

The set
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
consists of divergences
\Delta
which has the form
\Delta(\vec{\rho})=\Delta^{\prime}(\vec{\rho})/\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(k)}}}\right]
for any non-normalized
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
where the map
\Delta^{\prime}
additionally satisfies

(k1)

\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{(1)}\oplus\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\oplus\sigma^{(d)}}}\right)=\Delta^{\prime}(\vec{\rho})+\Delta^{\prime}(\vec{\sigma})
and

(k2)

\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{(1)}\otimes\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\otimes\sigma^{(d)}}}\right)=\Delta^{\prime}(\vec{\rho})\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma^{(k)}}}\right]+\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(k)}}}\right]\Delta^{\prime}(\vec{\sigma})

for all non-normalized experiments
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
.

The set
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
is further divided into four subsets depending which of the two instances in conditions (ND1) and (ND4) hold. Note that, when
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
, conditions (ND2) – (ND4) just mean that
\Delta
is a divergence. Condition (ND1) is the special extra condition which characterizes
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
within the special set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Conditions (k1) and (k2) are both extra conditions for any
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
. We may impose an additional normalization condition for all (quantum) divergences: Let us pick an experiment
\vec{\tau}=\big(\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(d)}\big)
of states
\tau^{(k)}
such that
k\neq\ell
implies
\tau^{(k)}\neq\tau^{(\ell)}
. Let us consider the convex set
\mathfrak{D}
of general (quantum) divergences
D
such that
D(\vec{\tau})=1
. For the function
N:\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
it is now opportune to choose
N(\Delta)=\big(\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(\vec{\tau})}\big)^{-1}
for all
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
. We show that, in the general setting including the classical and quantum cases,

(4)

{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}\subseteq\hat{\mathfrak{D}},\quad\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}\subseteq{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}

for the set
{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}
of convex extreme points of the normalized set
\mathfrak{D}
of divergences. The latter inclusion above means that, in the quantum case, all the quantum divergences
\Delta
having the form presented in (
3
) where
\Phi_{\Delta}
satisfies (ND1) – (ND4) (especially the special property (ND1)) are extreme divergences. This means that (ND1) can be seen as an extremality condition for a divergence. Particularly all these special quantum divergences are independent of each other as none of them can be expressed as a barycentre with support outside a singleton. This prompts one to think that at least all
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
(and all
\Delta\in{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
) are relevant when characterizing the information order in quantum experiments. Since most of the proposed bivariate and multivariate quantum divergences are within
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
(
k=1,\ldots,d
) (as we shall demonstrate), this means that the large variability of quantum divergences presented in existing literature is non-redundant. Examples of
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
include the
\alpha
-
z
quantum Rényi divergences (
d=2
)
[
2
,
15
]
including the quantum divergences of the Petz-type
[
23
,
24
]
and of the ‘sandwiched’ type
[
21
]
as well as the Kubo-Ando divergences (
d=2
)
[
17
]
and the general multivariate divergences arising from matrix means
[
4
]
. Examples of
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{k}
include the Umegaki quantum divergence and the Belavkin-Staszewski divergence
[
3
]
(
d=2
in both). Several multivariate generalizations of these were introduced, e.g., in
[
7
,
19
]
.

The theoretical background of this work lies in the theory of preordered semirings and asymptotic spectra. This theory has been highly useful especially for information theory ever since Volker Strassen found the first subcubic matrix multiplication algorithm, a find giving rise to his theory of asymptotic spectra
[
26
,
27
,
29
,
28
]
. This theory has been applied to varied fields of information theory and computer science since then, even quantum information theory, and some extensions to Strassen’s
Positivstellensatz
are represented by the results of
[
11
,
10
,
32
]
. An overview of the theory of asymptotic spectra and its uses is provided by
[
33
]
. In this current work, we especially utilize a
Vergleichsstellensatz
(‘comparison set theorem’) from
[
10
]
which characterizes large sample ordering in a preordered semidomain by inequalities involving a (suitably compact) set of conditions (a ‘test spectrum’) whose elements can be seen as special divergences and it coincides in the quantum and classical cases with the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
of those
\Delta
(or
\Phi_{\Delta}
) satisfying (ND1)–(ND4) or those
\Delta
satisfying (k1) and (k2),
k=1,\ldots,d
. We apply similar machinery to this large-sample result as that used in
[
20
]
to derive the barycentric decompositions for general divergences. In the quantum case, this can be briefly stated as follows: for any (multivariate) quantum divergence
D
there exists a finite positive (inner and outer regular) measure
\mu
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
(w.r.t. to a particular compactifying topology of
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
) such that

D(\vec{\rho})=\int_{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}}\Delta(\vec{\rho})\,d\mu(\Delta)

for all quantum experiments
\vec{\rho}
consisting of trace-one density operators. Let us here underline the fact that although we know that the above barycentric expression exists for quantum divergences, we are currently unable to fully characterize the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. This set however contains the majority of quantum divergences proposed in literature as we have just specified. The set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
for the classical multivariate case is characterized in detail in Example
9
based on earlier results in
[
9
]
.
The barycentric result is then used to prove the extremality result summed up in (
4
).

,

]
needed for the

2.
Background

We use the following basic notations throughout this work:

•

\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}

•

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
: the set of all strictly positive real numbers.

•

\mathbb{R}_{+}
: The set of non-negative real numbers seen as a preordered semiring (see the definition of this notion later) with the natural addition, multiplication, and (total) order
\geq
.

•

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
: as a set the same as
\mathbb{R}_{+}
above but with the opposite order
a\geq^{\rm op}b

\Leftrightarrow

b\geq a
.
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
together are often called
temperate non-negative reals
.

•

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
: otherwise the same as
\mathbb{R}_{+}
above but with the tropical sum
a+^{\prime}b=\max\{a,b\}
.

•

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
: the same as
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
above but with the opposite order.
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
together are often called as
tropical non-negative reals
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the 1-norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
through

\|x\|_{1}=|x_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{n}|,\qquad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We also define, for all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\rm supp}\,x
as the set of those
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
x_{k}\neq 0
. For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define
x\oplus y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
and
x\otimes y\in\mathbb{R}^{nm}
through

\displaystyle x\oplus y

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}),

\displaystyle x\otimes y

\displaystyle=(x_{i}y_{j})_{i,j}

where, in the latter formula, we identify
\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\{1,\ldots,m\}
with
\{1,\ldots,nm\}
in an unspecified way which we keep fixed throughout this work. In the sequel, we usually restrict these definitions for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
.

2.1.
Preordered semirings and a Vergleichsstellensatz

Preordered semirings are essentially otherwise like commutative rings with the exception that the elements of a (preordered) semiring typically do not have additive inverses and there is a preorder which respects the algebraic structure. To formalize this, a preordered semiring is an ordered tuple
(S,0,1,+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq)
where
S\neq\emptyset
,
(S,0,+)
and
(S,1,\cdot)
are commutative semigroups where the multiplication distributes over the addition, and
\trianglerighteq
is a preorder (a reflexive and transitive binary relation) on
S
such that

x\trianglerighteq y\ \Rightarrow\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x+a\trianglerighteq y+a,&\forall a\in S,\\
xa\trianglerighteq ya,&\forall a\in S.\end{array}}}\right.

For a preordered semiring
S
and
x,y\in S
, we denote
x\sim y
whenever there are
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in S
such that

x\trianglerighteq z_{1}\trianglelefteq z_{2}\trianglerighteq\cdots\trianglelefteq z_{n}\trianglerighteq y.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is of
polynomial growth
when there is
u\in S
such that

x\trianglerighteq y\ \Rightarrow\ \exists k\in\mathbb{N}:\ yu^{k}\trianglerighteq x.

Such an element
u
is called a
power universal
. A preordered semiring
S
is
zerosumfree
if

x+y=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0=y.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is a
preordered semidomain
if

xy=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ {\rm or}\ y=0

and

0\trianglerighteq x\trianglerighteq 0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0.

Given two preordered semirings
(S,0_{S},1_{S},+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq_{S})
and
(T,0_{T},1_{T},+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq_{T})
, we say that a map
\Phi:S\to T
is a
monotone homomorphism
if

•

\Phi(0_{S})=0_{T}
,
\Phi(1_{S})=1_{T}
,

•

\Phi(x+y)=\Phi(x)+\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in S
,

•

\Phi(xy)=\Phi(x)\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in S
, and

•

x\trianglerighteq_{S}y

\Rightarrow

\Phi(x)\trianglerighteq_{T}\Phi(y)
.

We say that a monotone homomorphism
\Phi:S\to T
is
degenerate
if
x\trianglerighteq y
implies
\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)
. Otherwise
\Phi
is
nondegenerate
. We are primarily interested in monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
where
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
. Given a monotone homomorphism
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we say that a map
\Delta:S\to\mathbb{R}
is a
monotone derivation at
\Phi
if
x\trianglerighteq y
implies
\Delta(x)\geq\Delta(y)
,
\Delta(x+y)=\Delta(x)+\Delta(y)
for all
x,y\in S
, and

\Delta(xy)=\Delta(x)\Phi(y)+\Phi(x)\Delta(y)\qquad{\rm(Leibniz\ rule)}

for all
x,y\in S
. We are only interested in monotone derivations at degenerate homomorphisms
\Phi
in which case we may view
\Phi
as a monotone homomorphism of
S
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
or, equivalently,
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
.

Definition 1
.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is
of degeneracy
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
if there is a surjective homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
with trivial kernel such that

a\trianglerighteq b\ \Rightarrow\ \|a\|=\|b\|,\quad\|a\|=\|b\|\ \Rightarrow\ a\sim b.

In this situation, we denote the component homomorphisms of
\|\cdot\|
by
\|\cdot\|_{(k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
.

Let us briefly note that, if
S
is of degeneracy
d
, we have
x\sim y
if and only if
\|x\|=\|y\|
. Naturally,
\|x\|=\|y\|

\Rightarrow

x\sim y
. For the converse, assume that
x\sim y
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in S
are such that
x\trianglerighteq z_{1}\trianglelefteq z_{2}\trianglerighteq\cdots\trianglelefteq z_{n}\trianglerighteq y
. The first inequality in the chain implies
\|x\|=\|z_{1}\|
, the second implies
\|z_{1}\|=\|z_{2}\|
, and so on until
\|z_{n-1}\|=\|z_{n}\|
and
\|z_{n}\|=\|y\|
. Thus,

\|x\|=\|z_{1}\|=\|z_{2}\|=\cdots=\|z_{n-1}\|=\|z_{n}\|=\|y\|.

Definition 2
.

Let
S
be a preordered semiring of polynomial growth and of degeneracy
d
where we fix a power universal
u
and the vector-valued homomorphism
\|\cdot\|
of Definition
2
. We denote the set of all nondegenerate monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
by
\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})
and the set of all the monotone derivations
\Delta:S\to\mathbb{R}
at
\|\cdot\|_{(k)}
(
k=1,\ldots,d
) with
\Delta(u)=1
by
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)
. For
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
, we define

\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{K}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\log{\Phi(\cdot)}}{\log{\Phi(u)}}\,\middle|\,\Phi\in\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})}}\right\}

as a set of maps
S\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we define

\mathfrak{D}_{k}(S):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\setminus\{0\}\ni x\mapsto\frac{\Delta(x)}{\|x\|_{(k)}}\,\middle|\,\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)}}\right\}.

Finally, we define the
test spectrum
through

\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S):=\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})\cup\mathfrak{D}_{1}(S)\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{D}_{d}(S).

In
[
10
]
, the test spectrum was defined as the union of the sets
\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. However, it is useful for our future discussion to define the test spectrum as the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)
defined in Definition
2
. The following central result (
Vergleichsstellensatz
) for this work is Theorem 8.6 in
[
10
]
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
S
be a zerosumfree preordered semidomain of polynomial growth and of degeneracy
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We fix a power universal
u\in S
and the vector-valued homomorphism
\|\cdot\|
of Definition
1
to define the test spectrum
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)
. Suppose that
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
are such that
\|x\|=\|y\|
. If

(5)

\Delta(x)>\Delta(y)\qquad\forall\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S),

then

(a)

x^{n}u^{k}\trianglerighteq y^{n}u^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
when
n\in\mathbb{N}
is sufficiently large and

(b)

xz\trianglerighteq yz
for some
z\in S\setminus\{0\}
which can be chosen according to

z=u^{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}x^{\ell}y^{n-\ell}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large.

If
x
is a power universal, we may omit the appearance of
u^{k}
in items (a) and (b) above. Conversely, if condition (a) or (b) above holds, then the inequalities in (
5
) hold non-strictly.

Before going on, let us note that any power universal
u\in S
has
\|u\|=(1,\ldots,1)
as long as
\|\cdot\|
satisfies the conditions of Definition
1
. This is naturally equivalent with
u\sim 1
. To see this, assume that
x,y\in S
are such that
x\trianglelefteq y
, so that there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
xu^{k}\trianglerighteq y
. Since
x\trianglelefteq y
, we have
\|x\|=\|y\|
and, since
xu^{k}\trianglerighteq y
, we have
\|x\|\cdot\|u\|^{k}=\|y\|=\|x\|
where we have used the first observation in the final equality. From this we immediately see that
\|u\|=(1,\ldots,1)
.

3.
Extensive monotone divergences over preordered semirings

Throughout this section, we work with a preordered semidomain
S
of polynomial growth and of degeneracy
d
with a fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
. To be explicit, let
(S,0,1,\trianglerighteq)
be a preordered semidomain of polynomial growth with a power universal
u
and
\|\cdot\|:S\to\{(0,\ldots,0)\}\cup\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
be as in Definition
2
. We also denote by

S_{N}:=\{x\in S\,|\,\|x\|=(1,\ldots,1)\}=\{x\in S\,|\,x\sim 1\}

the set of
normalized elements of
S
. In most of the applications of this theory,
S_{N}
is the physically (or, rather, information-theoretically) relevant part of the preordered semiring since normalization corresponds to, e.g., trace-1 positive operators or probability measures. The power universal
u
is within this normalized set. We simply denote the test spectrum as

\hat{\mathfrak{D}}:=\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)

and its constituent parts as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{K}}

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{K}),\quad\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{D}_{k}(S),\quad k=1,\ldots,d,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{D}_{d}.

We use the fixed power universal
u
in the definition of all these sets. The set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
is a compact Hausdorff space in the topology of pointwise comparison
[
10
, Proposition 8.5]
. This means that
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
is compact w.r.t. the coarsest topology where the comparison maps
f_{x,y}:\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(6)

f_{x,y}(\Delta)=\Delta(x)-\Delta(y),

with
x\sim y
(i.e.,
\|x\|=\|y\|
) are continuous. Especially the evaluation maps
f_{x}=f_{x,1}
,
f_{x}:\Delta\mapsto\Delta(x)
, are continuous for
x\in S_{N}
.

Definition 4
.

We say that
D:S\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
monotone divergence
if

(i)

D(xy)=D(x)+D(y)
for all
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
(extensivity),

(ii)

x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
,
x\trianglerighteq y

\Rightarrow

D(x)\geq D(y)
(monotonicity), and

(iii)

D(u)=1
(normalization)

We denote the set of monotone divergences by
\mathfrak{D}(S)
. We often simply denote
\mathfrak{D}(S)=:\mathfrak{D}
when there is no risk of confusion.

Remark 5
.

The normalization condition
D(u)=1
for all
D\in\mathfrak{D}
and the fixed power universal can also be lifted. The price to pay is that the hope of finding extreme points for the convex set
\mathfrak{D}
is lost and the statement of Theorem
7
will change a little.

One easily sees that
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}
. We will show that actually all monotone divergences are barycentres of Borel probability measures over
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
.

3.1.
Divergences as barycentres over the test spectrum

First, we present and prove a central lemma which utilizes the characterization of asymptotic ordering in
S
through
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
D:S\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a monotone divergence. If
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
,
x\sim y
, are such that
\Delta(x)\geq\Delta(y)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
, then also
D(x)\geq D(y)
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
,
x\sim y
, are such that
\Delta(x)>\Delta(y)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. According to Theorem
3
, this means that there are
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u^{k}x^{n}\trianglerighteq u^{k}y^{n}
. Using the monotonicity of
D
, we now have

kD(u)+nD(x)=D(u^{k}x^{n})\geq D(u^{k}y^{n})=kD(u)+nD(y)\ \Rightarrow\ D(x)\geq D(y).

Assume next that
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
,
x\sim y
, are such that
\Delta(x)\geq\Delta(y)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Let us make the inequality strict by adding the power universal. Indeed, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\Delta(ux^{m})=\Delta(u)+m\Delta(x)=1+m\Delta(x)>m\Delta(x)\geq m\Delta(y)=\Delta(y^{m})

for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. According to what we proved earlier, this means that
D(ux^{m})\geq D(y^{m})
, i.e.,

mD(x)+D(u)\geq mD(y)\ \Rightarrow\ D(x)+\frac{1}{m}D(u)\geq D(y).

Since this holds for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
D(x)\geq D(y)
.
∎

The above lemma is the crucial ingredient in the proof of the following result; the rest of the proof is straightforward functional analysis. The proof closely follows the idea of the proof of Theorem 2 of
[
20
]
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
D\in\mathfrak{D}
. Denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
w.r.t. the topology of pointwise comparison by
\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
. Suppose that, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
, there is
a_{z}\in S
with
\|a_{z}\|=z
such that
z\mapsto\Delta(a_{z})
and
z\mapsto D(a_{z})
are measurable. There is an inner and outer regular probability measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})\to[0,1]
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
x\in S\setminus\{0\}
,

(7)

D(x)=\int_{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}}\Delta(x)\,d\mu(\Delta)+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}.

Proof.

Denote by
C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
the set of continuous functions on the compact Hausdorff space
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
(w.r.t. the topology of pointwise comparison) which we equip with the sup-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. For all
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
with
x\sim y
, recall the comparison maps
f_{x,y}
as defined in (
6
). By the definition of the topology on
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
, these maps are continuous, i.e.,
f_{x,y}\in C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
for all
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
. Moreover, these functions have a couple of important properties: When
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
are such that
x\trianglerighteq y
, then

f_{x,y}(\Delta)=\Delta(x)-\Delta(y)\geq 0

for all
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
and similarly for
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}
. Thus,
f_{x,y}\geq 0
. Furthermore, when
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in S
, where
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
and
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
, are such that
f_{x_{1},y_{1}}\geq f_{x_{2},y_{2}}
, then
\Delta(x_{1}y_{2})\geq\Delta(x_{2}y_{1})
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. We also find that, if
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
and
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
, then

f_{x_{1}x_{2},y_{1}y_{2}}=f_{x_{1},y_{1}}+f_{x_{2},y_{2}}.

Our strategy is to define a subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
containing
f_{x,y}
with all
x,y\in S
with
x\sim y
and a positive linear functional
H:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
through
H(f_{x,y})=D(x)-D(y)
and then extend it into a positive linear functional
I:C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})\to\mathbb{R}
using a result due to Kantorovich related to the Hahn-Banach theorem. Riesz representation theorem for positive linear functionals on functions vanishing at the infinity then implies the existence of the measure of the claim. The main ingredient in this proof is Lemma
6
.

Let us first show that the ansatz
F(f_{x,y})=D(x)-D(y)
for a linear functional makes sense. Denote by
\mathcal{F}\subseteq C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
the set of
f_{x,y}
with
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
. Assume that
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
, are such that
f_{x,y}=0
, i.e.,
\Delta(x)=\Delta(y)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. According to Lemma
6
, this means that
D(x)=D(y)
, and we may define the map
F:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
through
F(f_{x,y})=D(x)-D(y)
for all
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
. As we have seen above,
f_{x_{1},y_{1}}\geq f_{x_{2},y_{2}}
for
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
and
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
implies
\Delta(x_{1}y_{2})\geq\Delta(x_{2}y_{1})
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
which, according to Lemma
6
means that
D(x_{1}y_{2})\geq D(x_{2}y_{1})
; note that
x_{1}y_{2}\sim x_{2}y_{1}
. This means that
F(f_{x_{1},y_{1}})\geq F(f_{x_{2},y_{2}})
. Thus,
F
is monotone. It is also additive. Indeed, for all
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in S
with
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
and
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
,

\displaystyle F(f_{x_{1},y_{1}}+f_{x_{2},y_{2}})

\displaystyle=F(f_{x_{1}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2}})=D(x_{1}y_{1})-D(x_{2}y_{2})

\displaystyle=D(x_{1})+D(y_{1})-D(x_{2})-D(y_{2})=F(f_{x_{1},y_{1}})+F(f_{x_{2},y_{2}}).

Next we extend
F
onto the cone generated by
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
the cone generated by
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., the set of combinations
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{x_{i},y_{i}}
with
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
,
x_{i},y_{i}\in S
,
x_{i}\sim y_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Because
\mathcal{F}
is closed under sums, it is easy to see that

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{F}.

Thus, it suffices to extend
F
for functions of the form
(1/n)f_{x,y}
. Assume that
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in S
,
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
,
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
(1/n)f_{x_{1},y_{1}}=(1/m)f_{x_{2},y_{2}}
. This is equivalent with
f_{x_{1}^{m},y_{1}^{m}}=f_{x_{2}^{n},y_{2}^{n}}
, i.e.,
\Delta(x_{1}^{m}y_{2}^{n})=\Delta(x_{2}^{n}y_{1}^{m})
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Since
x_{1}^{m}y_{2}^{n}\sim x_{2}^{n}y_{1}^{m}
, Lemma
6
implies that
D(x_{1}^{m}y_{2}^{n})=D(x_{2}^{n}y_{1}^{m})
, i.e.,
(1/n)F(f_{x_{1},y_{1}})=(1/m)F(f_{x_{2},y_{2}})
. Thus, we may define
G:\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathbb{R}
through
G(n^{-1}f_{x,y})=n^{-1}\big(D(x)-D(y)\big)
. It follows easily that
G
is still monotone and additive, i.e.,
G(f)\geq G(g)
whenever
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
are such that
f\geq g
and
G(f+g)=G(f)+G(g)
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Using again the fact that
\mathcal{F}
is closed under sums, one may also show that
G(f+\alpha g)=G(f)+\alpha G(g)
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
.

Next we want to extend
G
onto the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-closure
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
which includes the full positive cone
\mathcal{C}
generated by
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., the set of combinations
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{x_{i},y_{i}}
with
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x_{i},y_{i}\in S
,
x_{i}\sim y_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To do this, we show that
G
is uniformly continuous. For
x\in S_{N}
(especially
x=u
), we define
f_{x}:=f_{x,1}
,
f_{x}(\Delta)=\Delta(x)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We have, for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
q\geq\|f-g\|_{\infty}
,

\displaystyle g(\Delta)\leq f(\Delta)+\|f-g\|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle f(\Delta)+\|f-g\|_{\infty}\underbrace{\Delta(u)}_{=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\Delta)+\|f-g\|_{\infty}f_{u}(\Delta)\leq f(\Delta)+qf_{u}(\Delta).

Due to the monotonicity of
G
,

G(g)\leq G(f+qf_{u})=G(f)+qG(f_{u})=G(f)+qD(u)=G(f)+q.

Letting
q\to\|f-g\|_{\infty}
, we have
G(g)\leq G(f)+\|f-g\|_{\infty}
. Similarly, due to symmetry, we have
G(f)\leq G(g)+\|f-g\|_{\infty}
, i.e.,

|G(f)-G(g)|\leq\|f-g\|_{\infty}.

Thus,
G
extends into a uniformly continuous functional
\overline{G}:\overline{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\to\mathbb{R}
. Especially,
\overline{G}
is uniformly continuous on
\mathcal{C}
.

Next we show that
\overline{G}
is still monotone and positive-linear. Assume that
f,g\in\mathcal{C}
are such that
f\geq g
. For
i=1,2
, there are sequences
(a_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of natural numbers and
(x_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
(y_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
S
with
x_{i,k}\sim y_{i,k}
for all
k
such that
a_{1,k}^{-1}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}\to f
and
a_{2,k}^{-1}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}\to g
in the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm as
k\to\infty
. Thus, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}\geq f-\frac{1}{2n},\quad\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}\leq g+\frac{1}{2n}

for all
k\geq k_{n}
. Recalling that
f_{u}
is the constant function 1 and
f-g\geq 0
, we obtain

\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}\leq f-g+\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}\leq\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}+\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}+\frac{1}{n}f_{u}

for all
k\geq k_{n}
. Using the additivity and monotonicity of
G
, we have

G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{n}D(u)\geq G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}}}\right)

for all
k\geq k_{n}
. Letting
k\to\infty
, we have
\overline{G}(f)+n^{-1}D(u)\geq\overline{G}(g)
and, letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain
\overline{G}(f)\geq\overline{G}(g)
. Thus,
\overline{G}
is monotone. For positive-linearity, pick
f,g\in\mathcal{C}
and
\alpha\geq 0
. Let sequences
(a_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
,
(x_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, and
(y_{i,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be as above and, additionally,
(\alpha_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
converging to
\alpha
. It is clear that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f+\alpha g-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}}}\right)}}\right\|_{\infty}\to 0

as
k\in\infty
. Thus,

\displaystyle\overline{G}(f+\alpha g)=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}}}\right)+\alpha_{k}G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}}}\right)}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1,k}}f_{x_{1,k},y_{1,k}}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}}}\right)\lim_{k\to\infty}G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{2,k}}f_{x_{2,k},y_{2,k}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{G}(f)+\alpha\overline{G}(g),

implying that
\overline{G}
is positive-linear.

Let us define the vector space
\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}\subseteq C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
. We may define the linear functional
H:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
through
H(f-g)=\overline{G}(f)-\overline{G}(g)
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}
since, as an affine functional,
\overline{G}
extends uniquely into a linear functional defined on the linear hull. It is easy to verify that
H
is positive, i.e.,
H(f)\geq 0
whenever
f\in V
is positive (
f(\Delta)\geq 0
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
). Indeed, we may approximate any positive element of
\mathcal{V}
with differences
n^{-1}f_{x_{1},y_{1}}-m^{-1}f_{x_{2},y_{2}}\geq 0
with
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{1}\sim y_{1}
, and
x_{2}\sim y_{2}
. By now familiar calculations show that
D(x_{1}^{m}y_{2}^{n})\geq D(x_{2}^{n}y_{1}^{m})
, i.e.,
H(n^{-1}f_{x_{1},y_{1}})\geq H(m^{-1}f_{x_{2},y_{2}})
. Let
f\in C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
and pick
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
. Thus,

f(\Delta)\leq\|f\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{\infty}f_{u}(\Delta)\leq nf_{u}(\Delta)=f_{u^{n}}(\Delta)

for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Thus, all elements of
C(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
are bounded by elements of
\mathcal{V}
. According to a result due to Kantorovich
[
16
]
(a modern version also in Theorem 8.32 of
[
1
]
), this means that there is a positive linear functional

Remark 10
.

It should be emphasized that the main ingredient for Theorem
7

6

3
which we mainly use as a setting of our results and as a means to prove the pivotal Lemma

]

were first used, is not real-algebraic in nature. As long as we have something like Lemma

at our disposal, we obtain a barycentric result following the proof of Theorem

or Theorem 2 of
[
20
]

Let us give an example of this. Denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of finite probability vectors of varying length. We say that
p\in\mathcal{P}
majorizes

if one of the following equivalent conditions is satisfied:

(a)

Reorganizing the entries of

(respectively
q
) in a non-increasing order

(respectively
q^{\downarrow}=(q^{\downarrow}_{1},\ldots,q^{\downarrow}_{n})

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{\downarrow}_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}q^{\downarrow}_{i}

for
k=1,\ldots,n
.

(b)

There is a bistochasic matrix
S
such that
Sp=q
. Recall that a matrix is bistochastic when its entries are non-negative and the entries in any of its rows or columns sum up to 1.

When either of the conditions (a) or (b) is satisfied, we denote

. Let us call a map

a generalized entropy if it is extensive, i.e.,
H(p\otimes q)=H(p)+H(q)
for any

and monotone, i.e.,

.

Let us introduce the generalized Rényi entropies

for

(the attribute ‘generalized’ referring to allowing the
\alpha
-parameter to be negative) by determining their values for

with
{\rm supp}\,p=:I
:

H_{\alpha}(p)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\min_{i\in I}\log{p_{i}},&\alpha=-\infty,\\
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log{\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}^{\alpha}},&\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)\cup(1,\infty),\\
\sum_{i\in I}\log{p_{i}},&\alpha=0,\\
-\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\log{p_{i}},&\alpha=1,\\
-\max_{i\in I}\log{p_{i}},&\alpha=\infty.\end{array}}}\right.

Corollary 46 of
[
9
]
gives us a sufficient condition for the large-sample majorization of probability vectors: Let

. If
H_{\alpha}(p)<H_{\alpha}(q)
for all
\alpha\in[-\infty,\infty]
, then

for any sufficiently large

. Proceeding just as in the proof of Lemma
6
, we may show that, given

for all generalized entropies

if

]
(Recalling also Remark

for all

.

3.2.
Extreme points of the set of divergences

We immediately see that the set
\mathfrak{D}
of monotone divergences
D:S\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, i.e., for any
D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathfrak{D}
and
t\in[0,1]
, the map
tD_{1}+(1-t)D_{2}
defined through
\big(tD_{1}+(1-t)D_{2}\big)(x)=tD_{1}(x)+(1-t)D_{2}(x)
for all
x\in S
is also a monotone divergence. We notice that, as long as we are only interested in the restriction of
D
onto
S_{N}
,
D
is fully determined by a probability measure
\mu
on
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
according to (
9
). We know that the extreme points of the convex set of these measures are the point measures
\delta_{\Delta}
giving
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
as their barycentres. However, since for one monotone divergence
D
there might exist
a priori
many probability measures providing a barycentric representation for
D
, we cannot deduce that the extreme points of
\mathfrak{D}
are exactly those in
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Moreover, we notice that we may ‘disturb’ any divergence with a
transition function

S\ni x\mapsto a_{1}\log{\|x\|_{(1)}}+\cdots+a_{d}\log{\|x\|_{(d)}}\in\mathbb{R}

with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\mathfrak{D}
is clearly not compact in any meaningful sense. Thus the existence of extreme points is questionable. This is why, in the sequel, we implicitly view
\mathfrak{D}
modulo transition functions.

Definition 11
.

From now on we assume that there is a function
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}\ni z\mapsto a_{z}\in S

z\mapsto\Delta(a_{z})
is measurable for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. We denote by
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
the subset of those

modulo transition functions such that

is measurable.

Note that, if we defined the transition functions simply as functions

such that
\delta(uv)=\delta(u)+\delta(v)
for all
v,w\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
(where the product of vectors is defined entrywise), then we could remove the measurability assumption above and consider
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
as the coset space of
\mathfrak{D}
over the set of these more general transition functions.

We now embark on identifying the extreme points of
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
. First we show that, the set
{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}
of extreme points of
\mathfrak{D}
is contained in
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
(modulo transition functions). Note that, if we restrict the divergences onto
S_{N}
which is usually the set of interest, we do not need the definition for the restricted set
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
of divergences.

Lemma 12
.

{\rm ext}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\subseteq\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
.

Proof.

Let
D\in{\rm ext}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
and

7
) holds where we ignore the transition part, i.e., set
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{d}=0
. Let us make the counter assumption that there are
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in{\rm supp}\,\mu
such that
\Delta_{1}\neq\Delta_{2}
. Following the proof of Proposition 8.5 of
[
10
]
, we find
x_{0}\in S_{N}
such that
\Delta_{1}(x_{0})\neq\Delta_{2}(x_{0})
. For
i=1,2
and
\varepsilon>0
, we define the subsets
U_{i,\varepsilon}\subseteq\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
,

U_{i,\varepsilon}:=\{\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\,|\,|\Delta_{i}(x_{0})-\Delta(x_{0})|<\varepsilon\}.

Clearly,

are open neighbourhoods of
\Delta_{i}
for all

and

. This means that
\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})>0
for

and all
\varepsilon>0

,
U_{1,\varepsilon}\cap U_{2,\varepsilon}=\emptyset

\varepsilon>0
. From now on, we only consider these sufficiently small

Let us define, for

and all

D_{i,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\int_{U_{i,\varepsilon}}\Delta(x)\,d\mu(\Delta)

for all
x\in S
. We immediately see that
D_{i,\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{D}
. For
i=1,2
and all
\varepsilon>0
, we may also define
D^{\prime}_{i,\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{D}
through

D^{\prime}_{i,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\int_{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\setminus U_{i,\varepsilon}}\Delta(x)\,d\mu(\Delta)

for all
x\in S
; note that, when
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then
U_{1,\varepsilon}
and
U_{2,\varepsilon}
are disjoint and they have a positive measure in
\mu
, so also
\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})<\mu(U_{1,\varepsilon})+\mu(U_{2,\varepsilon})\leq\mu(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})=1
, so the definition of
D^{\prime}_{i,\varepsilon}
makes sense. We now have

D=\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})D_{i,\varepsilon}+\big(1-\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})\big)D^{\prime}_{i,\varepsilon}

for all
x\in S_{N}
,
\varepsilon>0
(sufficiently small), and
i=1,2
. Since
0<\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})<1
for any
\varepsilon>0
(sufficiently small), the extremality of
D
implies that
D=D_{i,\varepsilon}
for
i=1,2
and any
\varepsilon>0
(sufficiently small). Furthermore, for
i=1,2
and
\varepsilon>0
(sufficiently small), we have

\displaystyle|D_{i,\varepsilon}(x_{0})-\Delta_{i}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\int_{U_{i,\varepsilon}}\Delta(x_{0})\,d\mu(\Delta)-\Delta_{i}(x_{0})}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{U_{i,\varepsilon}}\big(\Delta(x_{0})-\Delta_{i}(x_{0})\big)\,d\mu(\Delta)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\int_{U_{i,\varepsilon}}|\Delta(x_{0})-\Delta_{i}(x_{0})|\,d\mu(\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\mu(U_{i,\varepsilon})=\varepsilon,

where the second inequality follows from the definition of
U_{i,\varepsilon}
. Recall that, since
x_{0}\in S_{N}
, the expressions for
D_{i,\varepsilon}(x_{0})
simplify. Thus, for
i=1,2
,
D(x_{0})=D_{i,\varepsilon}(x_{0})\to\Delta_{i}(x_{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
so that
\Delta_{1}(x_{0})=D(x_{0})=\Delta_{2}(x_{0})\neq\Delta_{1}(x_{0})
, a contradiction. Thus there cannot be distinct elements in the support of
\mu
, i.e.,
{\rm supp}\,\mu
is a singleton, say
\{\Delta_{0}\}
with some
\Delta_{0}\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. This means that
\mu
is the point measure concentrated at
\Delta_{0}
, so that
D=\Delta_{0}\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
.
∎

Remark 13
.

The inclusion
{\rm ext}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\subseteq\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
proven in Lemma
12
may be strict, i.e., not all elements of the test spectrum are extreme in
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
. Note, e.g., that the sets
\mathfrak{D}_{k}(C^{d})
of derivations of the semiring discussed in Example
9
essentially consist of proper convex mixtures of the (normalized) Kullback-Leibler divergences

\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)\mapsto\frac{D_{\rm KL}\big(p^{(k)}\big\|p^{(\ell)}\big)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|p^{(k)}}}\right\|_{1}}

which provide the extreme points of
\mathfrak{D}_{k}(C^{d})
. However, the non-derivation part
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
of the test spectrum is within
{\rm ext}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
, as the following theorem states.

Theorem 14
.

{\rm ext}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}=\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}\cup{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}_{d}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
12
, it suffices to prove that, whenever
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}
or
\Delta\in{\rm ext}\,\mathfrak{D}_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, then
\Delta
is extreme in
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
. We start by considering
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. According to Theorem
7
, there is a probability measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})\to[0,1]
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(13)

\Delta(x)=\int_{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}

for all
x\in S
. We keep this representation fixed and first prove the following implications:

(i)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

(ii)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

(iii)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}

(iv)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

(v)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{0}
,

(vk)

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{k}

\Rightarrow

{\rm supp}\,\mu\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d

In the proofs of these implications, we will often utilize the fact that we may view
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
as an element of the test spectrum of
\mathsf{Frac}(S)
when we consider entries
x/y
with
x\sim y
; see also the proof of Proposition 8.5
[
10

. This allows us to consider elements of the form
(u^{k}+1)/2
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
2:=1+1\in S
; note that
\|u^{k}+1\|=(2,\ldots,2)=\|2\|
, so that
u^{k}+1\sim 2
.

Before going on with the proof, let us show that the sets
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{K}}
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{0}
are measurable subsets of
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Indeed, it is easily seen that

•

\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

\Rightarrow

\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{u+1}{2}}}\right)=0
,

•

\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

\Rightarrow

0<\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{u+1}{2}}}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
,

•

\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}

\Rightarrow

\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{u+1}{2}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}
,

•

\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\Rightarrow

\frac{1}{2}<\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{u+1}{2}}}\right)<1
, and

•

\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\Rightarrow

\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{u+1}{2}}}\right)=1
.

This means that each of the sets
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{K}}
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{0}
are suitable preimages in the evaluation function at
(u+1)/2
. Thus, it makes sense to restrict
\mu
on each of these sets and

1=\mu(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})=\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}})+\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}})+\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}})+\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}})+\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{0}).

This result will come in handy also later. We discuss the separation of the different sets
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, later.

Let us start by proving implication (i). Let
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
and consider

with

Let

\Phi_{\Delta}=\exp{\big(\Delta(\cdot)\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}\big)}
. We find that, for
\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x_{k})=\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}+1\big)/2

as

when

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}

\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x_{k})=\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(\Phi_{\Delta}(u)^{-1}+1\big)=

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}(x_{k})=\exp{\big(\Delta(x_{k})\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}\big)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}\int_{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}}\Delta^{\prime}(x_{k})\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\rm nd}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x_{k})}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{0}}\tilde{\Delta}(x_{k})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\tilde{\Delta})}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}+1\big)}}\right]}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}+1\big)}}\right]}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{1}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-k\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{k}{2}\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{0}}\,d\mu(\tilde{\Delta})}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}+1\big)}}\right]}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(u)^{-k}+1\big)}}\right]}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}})+\frac{1}{2}\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{0})}}\right)\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}}}\right]}.

The left-hand side of the above equation converges to

\Phi_{\Delta}(y_{k})=\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(y_{k})}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}\exp{\big(k\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}})\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}\big)},

For the implication in (ii), the proof is essentially the same as with the implication in (i). In this case, we just consider

Using Jensen’s inequality, we have, for all

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(x)}}=\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}f(\Delta^{\prime})\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)}\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\exp{\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)}}\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime})=\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime}).

Let us now consider
x(s):=sx+(1-s)1
for a fixed
x\in S_{N}
and

. Naturally, the following derivative can be equivalently viewed as the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}k\big[\Phi_{\Delta}\big(x(1/k)\big)-1\big]

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}\Phi_{\Delta}\big(x(s)\big)}}\right|_{s=0}=\Phi_{\Delta}(x)-1

together with

\Phi_{\Delta}\big(x(s)\big)=\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\log{\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}\big(x(s)\big)}\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right)}

implies (together with the Leibniz integral rule)

\Phi_{\Delta}(x)-1=\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\big(\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)-1\big)\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime})=\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)\,d\nu(\Delta^{\prime})-1.

This means that we actually have an equality in (
15
). This means that
\nu
is supported by a set
B\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
such that

is affine, where

is the image of the map

. Thus,

must be a singleton. This means that all
\Phi_{\Delta^{\prime}}(x)
,
\Delta^{\prime}\in{\rm supp}\,\nu
, coincide. This holds for all

, so all
\Delta^{\prime}
in the support of
\nu
coincide. Thus, this support is a singleton, obviously

\Delta
is not a convex combination of different elements of

nor of the rest of
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}

\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}}
is dealt with in a similar fashion.

Let us now assume that
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}
. For all

\displaystyle\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)}}{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}}=\frac{\log{\max\{\Phi_{\Delta}(x),\Phi_{\Delta}(y)\}}}{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\max\{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(x)},\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(y)}\}}{\log{\Phi_{\Delta}(u)}}=\max\{\Delta(x),\Delta(y)\}.

Moreover, for all

\Delta(x)=\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}},

since

. We shall subsequently use these facts.

Let us make the counter assumption that there is
\Delta_{1}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}\setminus\{\Delta\}
such that
\Delta_{1}\in{\rm supp}\,\mu
. Again following the proof of Proposition 8.5 of
[

]
, we find

(i.e.,

), such that

. We may assume that
\Delta(x_{0})>\Delta_{1}(x_{0})

such that
\Delta(x_{0})>n/m>\Delta_{1}(x_{0})
. Replacing

with

, the desired inequalities hold.

We may define the sets

which are clearly neighbourhoods of
\Delta_{1}
. As

\mu(V_{\varepsilon})>0
for all

and, for suitably small

and

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(V_{\varepsilon})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{V_{\varepsilon}}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}}}\right),

\displaystyle D^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}\setminus V_{\varepsilon})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}\setminus V_{\varepsilon}}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}}}\right),

so that

\varepsilon>0

D_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})<0
since this number is
\varepsilon

\Delta_{1}(x_{0})<0
. Thus, we find
t\in(0,1)
and divergences

and

with

where
\Delta\neq D
and, even more,
D(x_{0})<0
. Moreover, there is a set
B\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathfrak{D}})
with

and

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}}}\right),

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}}\setminus B}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\log{\|x\|_{(k)}}}}\right).

x,y\in S_{N}

\displaystyle D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{B}\Delta^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{B}\max\{\Delta^{\prime}(x),\Delta^{\prime}(y)\}\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\int_{B}\Delta^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime}),\,\int_{B}\Delta^{\prime}(y)\,d\mu(\Delta^{\prime})}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\{D(x),D(y)\}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\max\{\Delta(x),\Delta(y)\}

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)=tD\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)+(1-t)D^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}}}\right)

implying that we have equalities in all the inequalities above. Especially,

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\max\{\Delta(x_{0}),0\}=\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{2}}}\right)=tD\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{2}}}\right)+(1-t)D^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\max\{D^{\prime}(x_{0}),0\}=(1-t)D^{\prime}(x_{0}),

where the final equality is due to the fact that, if

we arrive at the contradictory

. Thus,

must be a singleton, i.e.,

. Consequently,

. The case for

4.
Implications for quantum divergences and relative entropies

Theorems
3
,
7
, and
14
can be applied in many settings, including the case of the comparison of finite statistical experiments which was discussed in Example
9
. These results may also be applied in a quantum-theoretic counterpart of this classical problem which we shall call
quantum majorization
. For the duration of this section, we fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and study tuples
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(k)}\big)_{k=1}^{d}
of positive semi-definite operators
\rho^{(k)}
on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Typically these operators are quantum states, i.e., they are of unit trace. From now on, by ‘positive operator’ we mean ‘positive semi-definite operator’.

Definition 15
.

We say that a tuple
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(k)}\big)_{k=1}^{d}
of positive operators
majorizes
another tuple
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(k)}\big)_{k=1}^{d}
of positive operators if there is a quantum channel (completely positive trace-preserving linear map)
\Phi
such that
\Phi\big(\rho^{(k)}\big)=\sigma^{(k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. The finite-dimensional Hilbert space where
\sigma^{(k)}
reside may differ from the finite-dimensional Hilbert space where
\rho^{(k)}
reside. In this case, we denote
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
. Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{n}
the set of non-zero positive operators on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{d}
the set of maps
D:\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

For any tuples
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(k)}\big)_{k=1}^{d}
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(k)}\big)_{k=1}^{d}
of positive operators, we have

D\big(\rho^{(1)}\otimes\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\otimes\sigma^{(d)}\big)=D(\vec{\rho})+D(\vec{\sigma})\quad\textrm{(extensivity).}

(b)

Whenever
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
, we have
D(\vec{\rho})\geq D(\vec{\sigma})
(data processing inequality).

We call elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{d}

d
-variate quantum divergences
. In the
d=2
case, we call these
quantum relative entropies
since this term has already been firmly established in the bivariate case.

The set
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{2}
(the bivariate case) has been extensively studied. This set includes, e.g., the following relative entropies:

(1)

\alpha
-
z
relative entropies

D_{\alpha,z}

[
2
,
14
,
15
]
,

(16)

D_{\alpha,z}(\rho\|\sigma)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2z}}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{z}}\rho^{\frac{\alpha}{2z}}}}\right)^{z}}}\right]}.

These always satisfy condition (a) and, according to
[
8
,
13
,
34
]
, condition (b) is satisfied if and only if

(17)

0<\alpha<1,\ \max\{\alpha,1-\alpha\}\leq z\quad{\rm or}\quad\alpha>1,\ \max\{\alpha/2,\alpha-1\}\leq z\leq\alpha.

The choice
z=1
results in the Petz-type quantum relative entropies
[
23
,
24
]
and
\alpha=z
leads to the ‘sandwiched’ quantum Rényi relative entropies
[
21
]
. The pointwise limit
(\alpha,z)\to(1,1)
of these divergences coincides with the Umegaki quantum divergence
D_{\rm U}
,

(18)

D_{\rm U}(\rho\|\sigma)=\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho(\log{\rho}-\log{\sigma})}}\right].

This is also an element of
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{2}
.

(2)

Matrix means and the related Kubo-Ando quantum relative entropies

D^{\rm mm}_{\alpha}

[
3
,
17
]
,

(19)

D^{\rm mm}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}}}\right)^{\alpha}\sigma^{1/2}}}\right]}.

These relative entropies always satisfy condition (a) and, when
\alpha\in[0,2]
they also satisfy (b). The expression inside the trace coincides with the unique positive operator minimizing the
\alpha
-weighted Euclidean distances to
\rho
and
\sigma
. At the pointwise limit
\alpha\to 1
these divergences converge to the Belavkin-Staszewski quantum relative entropy

(20)

D_{\rm BS}(\rho\|\sigma)=-\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}}}\right)}}}\right]

\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{2}

(3)

The quantum max-relative entropy

D_{\rm max}
,

(21)

D_{\rm max}(\rho\|\sigma)=\inf\big\{\lambda\geq 0\big|\rho\leq\exp{(\lambda)}\sigma\big\}.

The matrix means of item (2) above can also generalized in the general
d\in\mathbb{N}
case
[
4
]
: Define the Euclidean distance
\delta_{2}
between positive operators
\rho

\sigma

\delta_{2}(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{\sum_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log{\lambda_{j}(\rho^{-1}\sigma)}}}\right)^{2}}

where
\lambda_{j}(\tau)

. We may now define the

(22)

These divergences satisfy both (a) and (b) whenever
\underline{\alpha}
is a non-extreme probability vector. The additivity property (a) is easily seen and the monotonicity property (b) follows from Theorem 4.1 and the discussion of Section 5 of
[
4
]
.

A number of multivariate quantum divergences (barycentric Rényi divergences) were introduced in
[

(23)

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{a})_{a\in A},

(24)

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}^{a}

\displaystyle=(\beta^{a}_{1},\ldots,\beta^{a}_{d}),\quad a\in A.

Using these and fixing

for all

Using this, one can show that the map

, is monotone under the quantum majorization preorder

In what follows we will see that, according to Theorems

and
14
we may express every divergence

as a barycentre over the set of extreme points of

which coincides with a subset of a test spectrum of a particular preordered semiring. The bulk of the test spectrum is extreme in
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{d}
and its elements are characterized by an extra additivity property. Interestingly, in the case

, all the

. Moreover, the

and the ‘staged’ divergences

4.1.
The quantum majorization semiring

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}
the set of
d
-tuples
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
of
\rho^{(k)}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with coinciding supports, i.e.,

{\rm supp}\,\rho^{(1)}=\cdots={\rm supp}\,\rho^{(d)}.

Furthermore, we denote

\mathcal{Q}^{d}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}.

For
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
, we define

\displaystyle\vec{\rho}\boxplus\vec{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{(1)}\oplus\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\oplus\sigma^{(d)}\big),

\displaystyle\vec{\rho}\boxtimes\vec{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{(1)}\otimes\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\otimes\sigma^{(d)}\big).

We also denote
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
whenever there exists a quantum channel (a completely positive trace-preserving linear map)
\Phi
such that
\Phi\big(\rho^{(k)}\big)=\sigma^{(k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Note that this preorder is exactly the quantum majorization order set up in Definition
15
.

Suppose now that
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{m}
. We denote
\vec{\rho}\approx\vec{\sigma}
if there is
\ell\geq n,m
and isometries
U:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
and
V:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
such that
U\rho^{(k)}U^{*}=V\sigma^{(k)}V^{*}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. We denote the
\approx
-equivalence class defined by
\vec{\rho}
by
[\vec{\rho}]
. We lift
\boxplus
,
\boxtimes
, and
\succeq
to the coset space
\mathcal{Q}^{d}/\!\approx
to obtain the
quantum majorization semiring

Q^{d}=(\mathcal{Q}^{d}/\!\approx,0,1,+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq)

where
0=[(0,\ldots,0)]
and
1=[(1,\ldots,1)]
where the equivalence classes are determined here by elements residing in
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{1}
. From now on, with a slight abuse of notation, we identify elements of
Q^{d}
with their representatives in
\mathcal{Q}^{d}
to lighten our notation. This should cause no confusion.

We easily see that
Q^{d}
is a preordered semidomain. We may also define the map
\|\cdot\|:Q^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
through

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(1)}}}\right],\ldots,\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(d)}}}\right]}}\right).

Clearly,
\|\vec{\rho}\|=(0,\ldots,0)
if and only if
\vec{\rho}=(0,\ldots,0)
, i.e.,
\|\cdot\|
has trivial kernel. If
\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
, then
\|\vec{\rho}\|=\|\vec{\sigma}\|
. This is because the operators in
\vec{\sigma}
are obtained from the operators of
\vec{\rho}
through a trace-preserving map. Let now
\|\vec{\rho}\|=\|\vec{\sigma}\|=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
. Since the trace is a (completely) positive linear map and
\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(k)}}}\right]=a_{k}=\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma^{(k)}}}\right]
for
k=1,\ldots,d
, we have

\vec{\rho}\succeq(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\preceq\vec{\sigma},

i.e.,
\vec{\rho}\sim\vec{\sigma}
. Thus,
\|\vec{\rho}\|=\|\vec{\sigma}\|

\Rightarrow

\vec{\rho}\sim\vec{\sigma}
, and
\|\cdot\|
satisfies the conditions of Definition
1
, i.e.,
Q^{d}
is of degeracy
d
. We also know that, if
\vec{\tau}\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
is a power universal, then
\|\vec{\tau}\|=(1,\ldots,1)
. The next lemma shows that power universals indeed exist (i.e.,
Q^{d}
is of polynomial growth) and that the power universals are plentiful and can be easily characterized.

Lemma 16
.

The preordered semidomain
Q^{d}
is of polynomial growth and
\vec{\tau}=\big(\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(d)}\big)
is a power universal if and only if
\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau^{(k)}}}\right]=1
for all
k
and
\tau^{(k)}\neq\tau^{(\ell)}
whenever
k\neq\ell
.

Proof.

Let us introduce some notation that we use throughout this proof. A map
M:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
is a (finite-outcome) POVM in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
denoting the set of effect operators, i.e., the operator interval
[0,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}]
) if
M(1)+\cdots+M(m)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
. For any POVM
M:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
and any positive (trace-class) operator
\tau
, we define the vector
p^{M}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
according to

p^{M}_{\tau}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau M(1)}}\right],\ldots,\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau M(m)}}\right]}}\right).

When
\tau
is a state,
p^{M}_{\tau}
is a probability vector describing the outcome statistics of the quantum observable described by
M
in the state
\tau
.

Let us pick
\vec{\tau}=\big(\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
where
\tau^{(k)}
are trace-one operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and, whenever
k\neq\ell
,
\tau^{(k)}\neq\tau^{(\ell)}
. According to
[
18
]
(see also
[
22
]
for the case
d=2
), there exists a sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of POVMs
M_{n}:\{1,\ldots,d\}\to\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})
such that, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\tau^{(k)}\big)^{\otimes n}M_{n}(\ell)}}\right]}}\right)_{\ell=1}^{d}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon_{k,1}^{(n)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k,k-1}^{(n)},1-\varepsilon_{k,k}^{(n)},\varepsilon_{k,k+1}^{(n)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k,d}^{(n)}}}\right)

where
\varepsilon_{k,\ell}^{(n)}\leq\exp{\big(-nC(\vec{\tau})\big)}
where, in turn,

\displaystyle C(\vec{\tau})

\displaystyle:=\min_{k,\ell:k\neq\ell}\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\log{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\tau^{(k)}\big)^{s}\big(\tau^{(\ell)}\big)^{1-s}}}\right]}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{k,\ell:k\neq\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\log{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\tau^{(k)}\big)^{1/2}\big(\tau^{(\ell)}\big)^{1/2}}}\right]}}}\right\}>-\max_{k,\ell:k\neq\ell}\log{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau^{(k)}}}\right]\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\tau^{(\ell)}}}\right]}}=0

where the final strict inequality is due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, strictness following from the fact that the states
\tau^{(k)}
all differ from each other. When we view

U^{(n)}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(1)})^{\otimes n}},\ldots,p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(d)})^{\otimes n}}}}\right)

as a
(d\times d)
-matrix with columns
p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}}
, we see that
U^{(n)}
converges to the
(d\times d)
identity matrix
I_{d}
as
n\to\infty
. Thus, when
n
is large enough,
U^{(n)}
enters a neighbourhood of the identity matrix where all matrices are invertible, Thus, for
n
large enough,
U^{(n)}
has an inverse
T^{(n)}
whose columns we denote by
t^{(n,k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Since
U^{(n)}
is a stochastic matrix, the columns of
T^{(n)}
sum up to 1. Let us quickly show this. First, defining the horizontal row
e:=(1\,\cdots\,1)
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}t^{(n,k)}_{\ell}}}\right)_{k=1}^{d}=eT^{(n)}=eU^{(n)}T^{(n)}=e,

where the second equality is due to the fact that, since the columns of
U^{(n)}
sum up to 1, then
eU^{(n)}=e
and the second equality is due to the fact that
U^{(n)}T^{(n)}=I_{d}
.

Let us consider any
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
where
\sigma^{(k)}
are states on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
the set of operators on which we denote by
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{K})
. We next show that there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
. Let
n
be large enough so that
T^{(n)}
exists. We define the linear preparation map
\Phi^{(n)}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{K})
through
\Phi^{(n)}(e_{k})=\sigma_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
where
e_{k}
are the natural basis vectors having 1 in the
k
th slot and zeros everywhere else. We also define the preparation map
\Psi^{(n)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{K})
through
\Psi^{(n)}(p)=\Phi^{(n)}(T^{(n)}p)
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\Phi^{(n)}
is naturally positive and
\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Phi^{(n)}(p)}}\right]=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{d}
for all
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Due to the fact that the columns of
T^{(n)}
all sum up to 1, we see that the latter condition holds also for
\Psi^{(n)}
. Since
T^{(n)}\to I_{d}
as
n\to\infty
(due to the fact that
U^{(n)}\to I_{d}
as
n\to\infty
and the continuity of the matrix inverse) we see that the absolute value of the possible negative matrix entries in
T^{(n)}=\big(t^{(n,k)}_{\ell}\big)_{k,\ell=1}^{d}
converge to 0. We now have, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

\displaystyle\Psi^{(n)}(e_{k})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(n)}(T^{(n)}e_{k})=\Phi^{(n)}(t^{(n,k)})=\sum_{ell=1}^{d}t^{(n,k)}_{\ell}\Phi^{(n)}(e_{\ell})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}t^{(n,k)}_{\ell}\sigma^{(\ell)}\geq 0

where the final operator inequality holds for
n
large enough so that the negative numbers among
t^{(n,k)}_{\ell}
have small enough absolute values so that the sum operator is positive semi-definite; recall that
\sigma^{(\ell)}
all share the same support. Thus, with
n
large enough,
\Psi^{(n)}
is a positive state preparator. Thus, when
n
is sufficiently large, we may define this positive state preparator
\Psi^{(n)}
which combined to the quantum-to-classical map
\Phi_{M_{n}}:\rho\mapsto p^{M_{n}}_{\rho}
gives us

\displaystyle(\Psi^{(n)}\circ\Phi_{M_{n}})\big((\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}\big)

\displaystyle=\Psi^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T^{(n)}p^{M_{n}}_{(\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}}}}\right)=\Phi^{(n)}(e_{k})=\sigma^{(k)}

for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Since positive linear quantum-to-classical and classical-to-quantum maps are completely positive, so is
\Lambda^{(n)}:=\Psi^{(n)}\circ\Phi_{M_{n}}
, i.e.,
\Lambda^{(n)}
is a quantum channel (completely positive trace-preserving linear map). Thus,
\Lambda^{(n)}\big((\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}\big)=\sigma^{(k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
, i.e.,
\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\succeq\vec{\sigma}
for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us assume that
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
are such that
\vec{\rho},\vec{\sigma}\notin 0
and
\vec{\sigma}\succeq\vec{\rho}
. Thus,
\|\vec{\rho}\|=\|\vec{\sigma}\|=:(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
. We also have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{d}}}}\right)\boxtimes\vec{\sigma}\succeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{d}}}}\right)\boxtimes\vec{\rho}

where the operator tuples consist of trace-one operators. Thus, we may freely assume that
\rho^{(k)}
and
\sigma^{(k)}
are of unit trace. Now
\vec{\rho}\succeq(1,\ldots,1)
, which combined with our earlier observation yields

\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes\vec{\rho}\succeq\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes(1,\ldots,1)=\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\succeq\vec{\sigma}

for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
showing that
\vec{\tau}
is a power universal.

Let us show the ‘only if’ part of the claim. Suppose that
\vec{\tau}=\big(\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(d)}\big)
is a power universal. We have
\|\vec{\tau}\|=(1,\ldots,1)
, meaning
\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma^{(k)}}}\right]=1
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Let us make the counter assumption that there are
k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
k\neq\ell
, such that
\tau^{(k)}=\tau^{(\ell)}
. Let
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
be such that
\|\vec{\rho}\|=(1,\ldots,1)
and
\rho^{(k)}\neq\rho^{(\ell)}
. Since
\vec{\rho}\succeq(1,\ldots,1)
, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\vec{\tau}^{\boxtimes n}\succeq\vec{\rho}
. This means that there is a channel
\Phi
such that

\rho^{(k)}=\Phi\big((\tau^{(k)})^{\otimes n}\big)=\Phi\big((\tau^{(\ell)})^{\otimes n}\big)=\rho^{(\ell)}\neq\rho^{(k)},

a contradiction. Thus,
\vec{\tau}
does not contain repeating operators.
∎

satisfies all the conditions of Theorem

Lemma 17
.

\mathfrak{D}(Q^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

When
\Phi\in\Sigma(Q^{d},\mathbb{K})
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
, we can prove in exactly the same way as in the proof of Proposition 13 of
[
9
]
that, whenever
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
,

\Phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{\alpha_{d}},&\underline{\alpha}\in A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{d}\ {\rm and}\ \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
&{\rm or}\ \underline{\alpha}\in A_{+}\ {\rm and}\ \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\\
a^{\beta_{1}}\cdots a^{\beta_{d}},&\underline{\beta}\in B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{d}\ {\rm and}\ \mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}.\end{array}}}\right.

Especially, when
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
,
\Phi(\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)=1
for all
\lambda>0
.

Let now
\Phi\in\Sigma(Q^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})
. We have, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\vec{\rho},\vec{\sigma}\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}
, and
t\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\Phi\big(t\vec{\rho}+(1-t)\vec{\sigma}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi\big(t\vec{\rho}\boxplus(1-t)\vec{\sigma}\big)=\max\big\{\Phi(t\vec{\rho}),\Phi\big((1-t)\vec{\sigma}\big)\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\Phi(t,\ldots,t)\Phi(\vec{\rho}),\Phi(1-t,\ldots,1-t)\Phi(\vec{\sigma})\big\}

(26)

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\Phi(\vec{\rho}),\Phi(\vec{\sigma})\big\}.

We next show that
\Phi(\vec{\rho})
only depends on the common support of the operators in
\vec{\rho}
. Let
\vec{\rho}=\big(\rho^{(1)},\ldots,\rho^{(d)}\big)
and
\vec{\sigma}=\big(\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(d)}\big)
be elements of
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

{\rm supp}\,\rho^{(1)}=\cdots={\rm supp}\,\rho^{(d)}={\rm supp}\,\sigma^{(1)}=\cdots={\rm supp}\,\sigma^{(d)}=:P

where we view
P
as a projection which we assume to be non-zero. There are
\vec{\tau_{s}}=\big(\tau_{s}^{(1)},\ldots,\tau_{s}^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{n}
with
s=1,2
and
s,t\in(0,1)
such that
\vec{\rho}=s\vec{\sigma}+(1-s)\vec{\tau_{1}}
and
\vec{\sigma}=t\vec{\rho}+(1-t)\vec{\tau_{2}}
. Using (
26
), we may evaluate

\displaystyle\Phi(\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle\geq\max\{\Phi(\vec{\rho}),\Phi(\vec{\tau_{1}})\}\geq\Phi(\vec{\rho})\geq\max\{\Phi(\vec{\sigma}),\Phi(\vec{\tau_{2}})\}\geq\Phi(\vec{\sigma}).

Thus,
\Phi(\vec{\rho})=\Phi(\vec{\sigma})
. Denoting
\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P}}\right]=:m
, we now have

\displaystyle\Phi(\vec{\rho})

\displaystyle=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m}P,\ldots,\frac{1}{m}P}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}}}\right)=1

Thus,
\Phi(\vec{\rho})=1
whenever
\vec{\rho}
is non-zero. Naturally,
\Phi(0)=0
. We have now seen that this is the only monotone homomorphism of

into
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}

\Sigma(Q^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})=\emptyset=\mathfrak{D}(Q^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})

through

\Delta^{(k)}_{\underline{\gamma},{\rm U}}(\vec{\rho})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho^{(k)}}}\right]}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\gamma_{\ell}D_{\rm U}\big(\rho^{(k)}\big\|\rho^{(\ell)}\big).

We may define similar divergences w.r.t. the Belavkin-Staszewski divergence or mixing both Umegaki and Belavkin-Staszewski divergences. When
\underline{\gamma}
is supported in more than one entry, these divergences are naturally not extreme in
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}^{d}
.

5.
Conclusions

We have defined extensive divergences on a preordered semiring and shown that, when the semiring
S
is a preordered semidomain of polynomial growth and degeneracy
d\in\mathbb{N}
(i.e., the equivalence relation generated by the preorder is characterized by a vector-valued degenerate homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{0\}
), then all the extensive divergences have a barycentric decomposition over the test spectrum of
S
. The test spectrum consists of extensive divergences that either satisfy certain extra additivity properties or Leibniz-type rules. We also show that the ‘bulk’ of this test spectrum can be viewed as the set of convex extreme points of the convex set of extensive divergences.

All the above results are applicable to the problems of finite comparing classical or quantum experiments. Whereas we can fully characterize the test spectrum in the classical case, the quantum case poses problems that, for now, seem difficult to solve. However, we have shown that, once fully characterized, the quantum test spectrum is all that is needed to describe all extensive multivariate quantum divergences (or quantum relative entropies). However, most of the quantum divergences (relative entropies) proposed in the literature, such as the
\alpha-z
relative entropies (including the Petz-type and sandwiched quantum Rényi relative entropies, especially the Umegaki relative entropy) and the matrix mean relative entropies (Kubo-Ando relative entropies), are within this test spectrum and hence, due to our extremality result, essentially independent of each other. This strongly suggests that all of these varied quantum relative entropies are meaningful in some quantum information processing tasks, although we cannot say so definitively based solely on their extremality.

It is important to note that the real-algebraic methods and results are not so important for the barycentric result of Theorem
7
. When perusing the proof of Theorem
7
, the reader may notice that the only thing is needed is a variant of Lemma
6
which identifies a set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
of extensive (when suitably defined) and monotone maps on the set
S_{N}
of resources of interest which is compact in the topology of pointwise convergence such that, when
\Delta(x)\geq\Delta(y)
for some
x,y\in S_{N}
, then
D(x)\geq D(y)
for any extensive monotone map
D:S_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. Proceeding in exactly the same way as in the proof of Theorem
7
, one proofs similar barycentric results for extensive monotone maps on
S_{N}
.

One may also notice that we have proven a barycentric result first followed by an extremality result in Theorem
14
. This seems a bit odd since usually order is the opposite: we first identify the extreme points of a convex (and compact) set and then use a Choquet-like theorem to express any element of the convex set as a barycentre over the extreme points. It may well be that there is a more straight way to the extremality result Theorem
14
without using the barycentric result Theorem
7
. From Theorem
14
one could then prove Theorem
7
using Choquet integrals. We leave this possible more direct proof as an open problem for now.
