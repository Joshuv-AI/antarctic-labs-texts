The predictive value of the ARC-HBR criteria for in-hospital bleeding risk following percutaneous coronary intervention in patients with acute coronary syndrome
Authors: Juan Liu, Hui He, Hong Su, Jun Hou, Yan Luo, Qiang Chen, Qiao Feng, Xiufen Peng, Maoling Jiang, Long Xia, Hanxiong Liu, Zhen Zhang, Shiqiang Xiong, Lin Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Academic Research Consortium for High Bleeding Risk (ARC-HBR) criteria were proposed for predicting bleeding risk in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). However, there is a lack of research evaluating the risk of in-hospital bleeding following PCI for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) utilizing the ARC-HBR criteria.Methods and resultsThis study involved 1013 ACS patients who underwent PCI and dual antiplatelet therapy. There were 63 cases of in-hospital bleeding events (6.22 %). According to the ARC-HBR criteria, patients classified as HBR had a significantly greater bleeding rate than non-HBR patients (15.81 % vs. 1.99 %, p < 0.001). As the CRUSADE score category increased, the risk of bleeding also increased. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of the ARC-HBR criteria was significantly greater than that of the CRUSADE score for bleeding (0.751 vs. 0.696, p < 0.0001). Subgroup analysis revealed that the ARC-HBR criteria exhibited better predictive ability for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI, AUC 0.767 vs. 0.694, p = 0.020) but comparable predictive ability in patients with unstable angina (AUC 0.756 vs. 0.644, p = 0.213), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (AUC 0.713 vs. 0.683, p = 0.644), and non-ST-segment elevation ACS (AUC 0.739 vs. 0.687, p = 0.330).ConclusionCompared with the CRUSADE score, the ARC-HBR criteria demonstrate superior predictive ability for in-hospital bleeding events during PCI in ACS patients. Routine assessment of the ARC-HBR score might be helpful for identifying high-risk individuals in this specific population.Keywords: High bleeding risk, ARC-HBR criteria, CRUSADE score, Percutaneous coronary intervention

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) serves as the principal treatment method for the majority of patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [1]. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is crucial for the management of coronary artery disease (CAD) to mitigate stent thrombosis following PCI and reduce the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events. Nonetheless, there is growing concern regarding the increasing incidence of bleeding complications linked to antiplatelet therapy [2]. The 2023 ESC guidelines for ACS have made substantial revisions to antithrombotic treatment strategies to mitigate the risk of bleeding, and high bleeding risk (HBR) patients are advised to consider switching to monotherapy with either aspirin or a P2Y12 receptor inhibitor after completing 1 month of DAPT [3]. Most studies have suggested that early P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy is associated with a decreased risk of bleeding complications, with no significant increase in ischemic events compared with standard DAPT, which is recommended for HBR patients undergoing PCI [4], [5], [6]. Moreover, a systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that ticagrelor or clopidogrel monotherapy after 1 to 3 months of DAPT was not inferior to standard DAPT in terms of all-cause death, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke; it was superior in preventing major bleeding, and ticagrelor monotherapy potentially reduced the number of adverse clinical events [7]. Therefore, adequate identification of HBR patients post-PCI during hospitalization while balancing bleeding and ischemic risks remains the foundation of decision-making.

In recent years, several scoring systems have been employed to assess bleeding in ACS patients. The PARIS, PRECISE DAPT, and PRAISE risk scores are primarily used to evaluate the risk of outpatient bleeding in patients with ACS [8], [9], [10]. Although the CRUSADE bleeding risk score is the most extensively employed in-hospital bleeding scoring tool in clinical settings [11], [12], it does not account for patients using anticoagulants. In 2019, the Academic Research Consortium for High Bleeding Risk (ARC-HBR) published consensus guidelines outlining criteria for identifying patients at HBR undergoing PCI [13]. These criteria are primarily utilized to predict the risk of bleeding within one year of PCI. Due to the increased ischemic risk in ACS patients [14], there is a need for intensified anticoagulant therapy and improved compliance, which in turn increases the risk of bleeding. To the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of research examining the predictive value of the ARC-HBR criteria in assessing in-hospital bleeding in individuals with ACS who are receiving DAPT following PCI. Moreover, the majority of clinical studies and scoring systems within the ARC-HBR consensus are primarily based on Western populations. Given the variations in bleeding and thrombotic risks between the Chinese population and Western populations, alongside the substantial number of ACS patients in China, it is crucial to investigate the suitability of applying the ARC-HBR criteria to assess in-hospital bleeding within these populations.

Thus, this study aims to investigate the predictive value of the ARC-HBR score for assessing in-hospital bleeding risk in ACS patients undergoing PCI and receiving DAPT, and compare it to the most commonly used CRUSADE score.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective, single-center investigation conducted at the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu. The participants consisted of patients diagnosed with ACS, including acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA), who underwent PCI and received DAPT from May 2018 to December 2020. The prescription of DAPT followed the recommendations of international guidelines [3]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients diagnosed with ACS undergoing PCI; and 2) patients who received DAPT post-procedure (some patients switched to single antiplatelet therapy due to bleeding complications). The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with active bleeding, peptic ulcers, or other contraindications to DAPT; 2) patients who were allergic to antiplatelet drugs; and 3) patients with hematological disorders. As shown in Fig. 1, we excluded 17 patients due to missing important clinical data that prevented bleeding scoring and excluded one patient who experienced post-PCI hemoptysis, leading to the inability to use DAPT. Ultimately, 1013 eligible patients were included in this analysis. The baseline data collection for all participants was independently conducted by clinically trained researchers to ensure the objectivity and accuracy of the collected data.

Flow chart of the study population. ACS, acute coronary syndrome; PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention; DAPT, dual antiplatelet therapy.

This study received approval from the local ethics committee and adhered rigorously to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was waived. In addition, personally identifiable information related to patient identities is concealed.

Clinical data and definitions

Information regarding demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, medical history, smoking and drinking habits, laboratory test results, and medical and surgical details of the participants was retrieved from the electronic medical records. The formula for calculating body mass index (BMI) is as follows: BMI = weight(kg)/[height(M)]^2. Creatinine clearance (Ccr) is as follows: Ccr = [(140-age) × weight(kg)]/[0.818 × Scr(μmol/L)], and for females, the result is multiplied by 0.85.

The medical history of the participants encompassed a range of conditions, including PCI, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and atrial fibrillation. ACS, UA, NSTEMI, and NSTE-ACS are defined according to the 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of ACS [3].

After an overnight fast (>8 h), peripheral venous blood samples were collected. Standard laboratory methods in the clinical laboratory of the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu were used to measure cardiac troponin T (cTnT), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, Ccr, uric acid, fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein-C (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein-C (HDL-C) levels. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated via the two-dimensional modified Simpson’s method.

Definition

The CRUSADE scores and ARC-HBR criteria were evaluated on the basis of the patients’ clinical characteristics. The CRUSADE score was determined via an online calculator with variables collected upon patient hospital admission (sex, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, hematocrit, Ccr, chronic heart failure indicators, vascular disease history, and diabetes history) [15]. The CRUSADE score classification delineates the following risk categories: 0–20 points, very low risk (n = 104, 10.3 %); 21–30 points, low risk (n = 276, 27.2 %); 31–40 points, moderate risk (n = 335, 33.1 %); 41–50 points, high risk (n = 177, 17.5 %); and > 50 points, very high risk (n = 121, 11.9 %). A higher CRUSADE score is associated with an increased risk of bleeding in patients.

We used the original ARC-HBR criteria but replaced the eGFR with the CCr. The ARC-HBR score comprises 14 major criteria and six minor criteria. The major criteria include anticipated use of long-term oral anticoagulation, severe or end-stage chronic kidney disease (CKD, Ccr < 30 mL/min), severe anemia (hemoglobin < 11 g/dL), spontaneous bleeding requiring hospitalization or transfusion in the past 6 months or at any time, a platelet count < 100 × 109/L, chronic bleeding diathesis, liver cirrhosis with portal hypertension, active malignancy within 12 months and/or ongoing requirements for treatment (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer), previous spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, previous traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage within the past 12 months, the presence of moderate-to-severe ischemic stroke associated with a brain arteriovenous malformation within the past 6 months, nondeferrable major surgery on DAPT, and recent major surgery or major trauma within 30 days before PCI. The minor criteria include age ≥75 years, moderate CKD (Ccr 30–59 ml/min), mild anemia (male hemoglobin 11–12.9 g/dL, female 11–11.9 g/dL), spontaneous bleeding necessitating hospitalization or transfusion within the previous 12 months that does not meet the major criterion, long-term oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or steroids, and any past ischemic stroke not meeting the major criterion. Patients who meet at least one major or two minor criteria are classified as having high bleeding risk (HBR) (n = 310, 30.6 %), whereas those meeting ≤ 1 minor criterion are classified as having non-HBR (n = 703, 69.4 %).

Clinical endpoints

The primary endpoint of the present study was bleeding events categorized on three distinct severity scales: Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) scores of 2, 3, or 5 [16], whereas the secondary endpoint was all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed metric data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD), whereas nonnormally distributed metric data are presented as the median with the interquartile range. Count data are expressed as frequencies (percentages). Group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted via analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas binary variables were assessed via the chi–square test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the associations between the two scoring models and bleeding. The predictive performance of the ARC-HBR criteria and the CRUSADE score for in-hospital bleeding events was compared through the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). All the statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted via IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Among the 1013 patients, males accounted for 72.0 %, with a median age of 67 (60, 76) years, and 63 patients (6.22 %) experienced in-hospital bleeding events. Compared with patients in the nonbleeding group, those in the bleeding group were older, smoked, had longer hospital stays, and were more likely to have hypertension, angina, diabetes, and a history of blood dialysis. These patients presented elevated levels of BNP and white blood cells, but reduced levels of TC, LDL-C, red blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets. Additionally, these patients had higher incidences of cardiogenic shock and MI, poorer liver and kidney function, and compromised LVEF.

Effects of the ARC-HBR criteria and CRUSADE scores on clinical outcomes

According to the ARC-HBR criteria, 30.6 % (n = 310) of patients were classified as HBR. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the primary criteria that meet the definition of the ARC-HBR include moderate to severe anemia (29 %), thrombocytopenia (18 %), severe/end-stage CKD (11.6 %), and oral anticoagulant use (4.2 %). The most prevalent secondary criteria were age ≥75 years (54.5 %), mild anemia (38 %), moderate CKD (21 %), and a history of stroke within the past year (8.7 %).

Distribution of the ARC-HBR criteria. CKD, chronic kidney disease.

As shown in Fig. 3, patients meeting the HBR criteria presented a significantly greater risk of BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding compared to non-HBR patients (15.81 % vs. 1.99 %). As the ARC-HBR score increased, the incidence of bleeding also increased. However, no bleeding events were observed among patients scoring 3 points or more (because only two patients had scores of 3 and 3.5). Logistic regression analysis revealed that the bleeding risk for patients in the HBR group was 9.226 times (95 % CI: 5.009–16.994; P < 0.001) greater than that for patients in the non-HBR group, as shown in Table 4.

In the CRUSADE scoring stratification, there were no instances of bleeding in the very low-risk group (n = 104), 7 cases of bleeding in the low-risk group (n = 276, the bleeding proportion was 2.54 %), 20 cases of bleeding in the medium-risk group (n = 335, the bleeding proportion was 5.77 %), 22 cases of bleeding in the high-risk group (n = 177, the bleeding proportion was 12.43 %), and 14 cases of bleeding in the very high-risk group (n = 121, the bleeding proportion was 11.57 %) (Fig. 4). Logistic regression analysis revealed that, compared with the very low- and low-risk groups, the medium-risk group had a 3.383-fold greater risk of bleeding (95 % CI: 1.412–8.105; P = 0.006), the high-risk group had a 7.563-fold greater risk (95 % CI: 3.166-18.069; P < 0.001), and the very high-risk group had a 6.972-fold greater risk (95 % CI: 2.744-17.713; P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Comparison of the predictive performance of the ARC-HBR criteria and CRUSADE scores

The area under the ROC curves revealed that the ARC-HBR criteria had better predictive performance than the CRUSADE scores (0.751 vs. 0.696, P < 0.0001) and exhibited notable variance in sensitivity (77.80 % vs. 57.14 %), with a minor difference in specificity (72.50 % vs. 72.42 %) (Fig. 5, Table 5).

Subgroup analysis

Further subgroup analysis based on the area under the ROC curves for different types of ACS patients revealed consistent findings: the ARC-HBR score had greater predictive value for in-hospital bleeding in various types of ACS patients than did the CRUSADE score, with values for UA (0.756 vs. 0.644, P = 0.213), NSTEMI (0.713 vs. 0.683, P = 0.644), NSTE-ACS (0.739 vs. 0.687, P = 0.330), and STEMI (0.767 vs. 0.694, P = 0.020). Notably, a substantial difference existed in predicting in-hospital bleeding among STEMI patients (Fig. 6, Table 6).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comparative analysis of the ability of the ARC-HBR criteria and the CRUSADE score to predict the incidence of in-hospital bleeding following DAPT in ACS patients undergoing PCI. The main findings are as follows: Both the ARC-HBR criteria and the CRUSADE score demonstrate good predictive value for in-hospital bleeding in these patients, with the ARC-HBR exhibiting superior predictive sensitivity, particularly in NSTEMI patients.

It is well known that administering standard DAPT to ACS patients classified as HBR may result in severe bleeding and potential fatality [17]. Despite updates to the duration of DAPT treatment in the most recent ACS guidelines, specific recommendations for the early identification and management of HBR patients during the in-hospital phase remain insufficient. The CRUSADE score was initially employed for the prediction of in-hospital bleeding events among patients with NSTEMI [15]. The 2020 ESC guidelines for the management of ACS in patients presenting without persistent ST-segment elevation reiterated the importance of the CRUSADE scoring system in informing accurate antithrombotic strategies for the post-PCI management of CAD [12]. The study by Ariza-Sole et al. [18] validated the predictive capacity of the CRUSADE scoring model in a cohort of patients with STEMI who underwent PCI, further broadening the clinical utility of the CRUSADE score. Nonetheless, the CRUSADE scoring system does not assess patients on anticoagulant therapy, leading to specific limitations. Administering standard DAPT post-PCI in these individuals could markedly increase the likelihood of early bleeding events and mortality.

The ARC-HBR criteria represent the inaugural expert consensus for defining high bleeding risk in patients undergoing PCI [13], with significant clinical and research implications. Research suggests that the ARC-HBR criteria are more sensitive than the PRECISE-DAPT, PARIS, and CREDO-KYOTO criteria for long-term bleeding events in patients undergoing PCI [19], [20], [21]. Gragnano et al. [22] conducted a comparative analysis of bleeding events over one year in patients with ACS and chronic coronary syndrome (CCS). These findings indicated that the discriminatory power of the ARC-HBR score was weaker for ACS patients than for CCS patients. Nevertheless, the overall performance of the score exhibited numerical improvement when ACS was considered an additional minor risk criterion. In research on MI patients, Fujii T et al. [23] reported that both the ARC-HBR criteria and the PRECISE-DAPT score exhibited strong predictive capabilities. These studies all focused on the predictive value of the HBR criteria for long-term bleeding in patients after PCI. Tsukizawa et al. [24] conducted a study on the relationship between the incidence of in-hospital events and ARC-HBR scores in acute MI patients. The results revealed that the incidence of all in-hospital events (including major bleeding) in the HBR group was significantly greater than that in the non-HBR group, which is consistent with our subgroup analysis results. Our study highlighted the strong predictive ability of the ARC-HBR criteria for assessing in-hospital bleeding risk among STEMI patients. In contrast to their research, our focus was on analyzing in-hospital bleeding characteristics in ACS patients post-PCI and comparing them with the widely utilized CRUSADE score for in-hospital bleeding. The outcomes indicated increased sensitivity, indicating significant value in evaluating the application of the HBR criteria in predicting early bleeding.

In contrast to Western cohorts, East Asian populations present a lower BMI and genetic variations related to thrombotic factors [25], [26], [27]. The Chinese population comprises a significant number of patients with ACS, with a notably lower proportion undergoing emergency CABG surgery compared to other countries. The delay in medical care for ACS patients in China is generally greater than that in developed nations. Furthermore, owing to the absence of intravenous P2Y12 receptor inhibitors (such as cangrelor), many Chinese specialists advocate administering P2Y12 receptor inhibitors as a pretreatment for ACS patients before PCI to reduce the risk of perioperative thrombosis. This approach diverges from the assessment of bleeding risk in European and American patients, as the ARC-HBR criteria primarily stem from randomized trials conducted in Western countries. Given the pronounced variations in the occurrence and distribution of bleeding and thrombotic events between the Chinese population and their Western counterparts, the present study confirmed the prognostic ability of the ARC-HBR scoring system for predicting in-hospital bleeding in post-PCI patients within this demographic range. This validation holds considerable epidemiological importance in shaping suitable antithrombotic strategies. Furthermore, in light of the limited positive predictive value of the ARC-HBR criteria for BARC 3 or 5 bleeding, this study adopted BARC 2, 3, or 5 bleeding as the primary endpoint.

Compared with the CRUSADE score, the ARC-HBR has superior predictive value for the STEMI cohort, whereas there is no significant variance in assessing in-hospital bleeding risk among NSTEMI patients. This disparity may stem from the distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying STEMI and NSTEMI-ACS. Most STEMI patients exhibit single-vessel CAD along with multiple bleeding risk factors, whereas NSTEMI-ACS patients present with varying degrees of CAD complexity, ranging from none to severe triple-vessel disease, necessitating diverse clinical assessments and treatment strategies. We observed that the ARC-HBR score has strong predictive value for in-hospital bleeding in both male and female patients, possibly due to the influence of early anticoagulation and DAPT. Moreover, the study by Alessandro Spirito et al. [28] found that female patients initially had lower ARC-HBR scores than male patients did. However, after the ARC-HBR scoring model was updated to analyze age, renal function, and hemoglobin values as continuous variables instead of dichotomous variables, similar effects were observed in both sexes.

While an increase in risk factors for HBR bleeding corresponds to a gradual elevation in bleeding risk, there is currently a lack of adequate research data, hindering the establishment of relative weighting for HBR scoring criteria. Regarding the intensity and duration of DAPT, deliberation is warranted on whether varying scoring values for “high-risk” patients should be applied, or if equivalent consideration should be given to “moderate-risk” patients within the “low-risk” group, constituting a secondary criterion, and “truly low-risk” patients without criteria. Further exploration is essential in the realms of clinical decision-making and research analysis. Nonetheless, this study provides evidence-based support for the evaluation of in-hospital bleeding risk via the ARC-HBR among post-PCI patients with ACS, thereby providing clinicians with personalized and precise antiplatelet therapy reference points.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, and future validation through prospective, multicenter, and large cohort studies is needed. Second, the study predominantly targeted Chinese participants who may exhibit heightened apprehension toward bleeding, leading to a potential inclination to utilize lower doses of antiplatelet medications, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the bleeding risk within the Asian population. A significant issue is the dynamic and ongoing nature of risk assessment in ACS patients, which is influenced by various factors, such as liver and kidney dysfunction. Notably, in this study, some patients shifted to single antiplatelet therapy due to bleeding complications, and critically ill patients receiving IABP or intubation were not excluded, potentially affecting the outcomes of the two scoring systems. Another key aspect is the focus of the study on a comparatively vulnerable population with ACS, wherein the proportion of patients with a single risk factor is lower than that in CCS patients. This scenario of vulnerability may heighten the risk of bleeding or thrombosis. Additionally, ischemic risk was not evaluated in this study because of the presence of shared risk factors for both ischemia and bleeding, including advanced age and renal insufficiency. Therefore, future large-scale randomized controlled trials utilizing scoring systems are warranted to confirm the efficacy of ARC-HBR in managing the delicate balance between bleeding and thrombosis.

Conclusion

Compared with the CRUSADE score, the ARC-HBR criteria have a more robust ability to predict in-hospital bleeding events in ACS patients undergoing PCI and receiving DAPT. The use of the ARC-HBR criteria may have the potential to assist clinicians in promptly identifying high-risk patients and facilitating the development of tailored treatment strategies.
