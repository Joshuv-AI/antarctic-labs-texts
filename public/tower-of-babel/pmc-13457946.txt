Impact of Changes in Chlamydia Treatment Guidelines on Recurrent/Persistent Rectal Chlamydia trachomatis Infections
Authors: Mary Bridget Waters, Anjuli D Wagner, Tim W Menza, Anna Berzkalns, Lisa E Manhart, Christine M Khosropour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrior to 2021, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommended either doxycycline or azithromycin as first-line treatments for Chlamydia trachomatis (CT). By 2016, there was evidence showing that doxycycline was more effective than azithromycin for the treatment of rectal CT. This motivated the Public Health—Seattle & King County Sexual Health Clinic to update their clinic's protocol to treat rectal CT with exclusively doxycycline in mid-2016. The CDC later recommended this in July 2021.MethodsLeveraging health records from the clinic, we performed a controlled interrupted time series analysis to evaluate the impact of updated treatment guidelines at the clinic on recurrent/persistent (RP) rectal chlamydia (RP-CT) infections among men who have sex with men using RP rectal gonorrhea (RP-GC) as a control outcome. We also evaluated if there were patient demographic and behavioral correlates associated with receipt of azithromycin before and after the change in clinic guidelines.ResultsOur analysis revealed a significant relative trend change between RP-CT and RP-GC when comparing the period of full implementation to preimplementation, showing a 16.9% greater reduction in the quarterly trend of RP-CT cases compared to RP-GC cases. Self-identifying as White and with human immunodeficiency virus were positively associated with receiving azithromycin for treatment of rectal CT after the change in guidelines.ConclusionsThe significant reduction in the trend change for RP-CT versus RP-GC when comparing the fully implemented period of exclusive doxycycline to the preimplementation period demonstrates the impact of the new treatment guidelines in reducing incidence of RP-CT infections.Keywords: chlamydia, controlled interrupted time series, doxycycline, rectal chlamydia, STI treatment guidelines

Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) is the most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection globally, with estimated 128.5 million CT infections worldwide in 2020 in people aged 15–49 years [1]. C. trachomatis can cause severe reproductive tract sequelae, including pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility [2].

Prior to 2021, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommended either doxycycline (100 mg BID × 7 days) or azithromycin (1 g single dose) as first-line treatments for CT [3] because studies of urogenital CT demonstrated that both regimens were similarly efficacious [4]. By 2016, there was mounting observational evidence [5], including a study conducted at the Public Health—Seattle & King County Sexual Health Clinic (PHSKC SHC) [6], showing that doxycycline was more effective than azithromycin for the treatment of rectal CT. This motivated the PHSKC SHC to update their clinic's protocol to treat rectal CT with exclusively doxycycline beginning in April 2016. In 2021, 2 randomized controlled trials demonstrated doxycycline's superiority over azithromycin for the treatment of rectal CT [7, 8]. This prompted an update to national treatment guidelines in July 2021, which removed azithromycin as a first-line therapy and recommended only doxycycline for the treatment of CT infections in people who are not pregnant [2].

While it is established that doxycycline is more effective than azithromycin for the treatment of rectal CT, it is unknown whether changes to clinic protocols and national guidelines have led to a decline in trends of recurrent/persistent (RP) rectal CT (RP-CT). The goal of this study was to estimate the impact of changes in treatment policy on RP rectal CT infections among men who have sex with men (MSM) at the PHSKC SHC: first when the policy was being implemented at the clinic level and second when the policy was fully implemented at the clinic level and national recommendations were updated. Additionally, we examined correlates associated with receipt of azithromycin before and after the change in clinic guidelines to assess whether providers were more likely to prescribe azithromycin to certain patients based on sociodemographic characteristics.

METHODS

Study Design, Setting, and Population

We conducted a controlled interrupted time series (cITS) analysis to evaluate how the change in CT treatment guidelines impacted RP-CT infections at the PHSKC SHC. Interrupted time series (ITS) analyses apply segmented regression to estimate the impacts of a clinic or policy implementation. Interrupted time series models allow for estimation of both a level change (ie, change in the outcome immediately following an intervention) and a trend change (ie, change in the trend of the outcome after the intervention). Using a control group may provide an improved estimation of the impacts of an intervention when there were other changes that may have impacted an outcome during the time period [9].

Our study population included all MSM who tested positive and were treated for either rectal CT or rectal Neisseria gonorrhea (GC) at the PHSKC SHC from 1 January 2012 through 31 December 2024. All data from the study were from the electronic health record (EHR), which includes patient demographics, diagnoses, and treatment as well as data from the clinic's electronic intake form, which includes sexual behaviors, drug use, and health insurance. We performed all analyses in R (Version 4.4.3) [10] and RStudio [11] using the “tidyverse” [12], “MASS' [13], “gtsummary” [14], and “geepack” [15] packages. This study was approved by the University of Washington Human Subjects Division Institutional Review Board.

Controlled Interrupted Time Series Analysis Definitions and Analyses

Our primary analysis used a cITS design to evaluate the impact of the change in treatment guidelines on RP-CT infections. We defined 3 different time periods in our cITS analysis: (1) “preimplementation” (before the change in guidelines; January 2012 through June 2016), (2) “implementation phase” (implementation of the change in clinic guidelines; July 2016 through September 2021), and (3) “fully implemented” (full implementation of the change in clinic guidelines along with updates to national guidelines; October 2021 through December 2024).

Our outcome was RP-CT infections, since these are infections that would be impacted by changes in CT treatment policies. Our control outcome was RP rectal gonorrhea (RP-GC) infections. We selected RP-GC as a control outcome because RP-GC should not have been impacted by the change in chlamydia treatment guidelines. Although GC treatment guidelines were updated in the middle of our study period (December 2020), this was due to an increase in the detection of highly azithromycin-resistant GC isolates, new ceftriaxone pharmacokinetic data, and considerations related to antimicrobial stewardship, rather than an increase in RP-GC infections [16]. In fact, there were no reported GC treatment failures in the United States using the therapy recommended prior to December 2020 [17]. For both GC and CT, the term RP includes those whose infections cleared but were re-infected and those who had a persistent infection that did not clear after therapy; we are unable to disentangle these 2 scenarios.

We extracted all rectal CT and rectal GC diagnoses at PHSKC SHC from January 2012 through December 2024. Our primary analysis defined RP-CT and RP-GC infections as a second diagnosis of CT or GC via nucleic acid amplification test 14–90 days after an initial diagnosis in the EHR. We aggregated the counts of these infections by calendar quarter (January to March, April to June, July to September, and October to December for each year). Coinfections of RP-CT and RP-GC were included as both an RP-CT infection and an RP-GC infection, and they represent 2.3% of RP infections.

We fit a negative binomial model that accounted for seasonality using quarterly indicator variables and population using an offset. The populations for the RP-CT group and RP-GC group were the number of individuals with a rectal CT infection or rectal GC infection within the same quarter, respectively. We used segmented regression with 3 time periods and visualized our model by plotting the observed quarterly counts of RP CT and GC, the model output, and the linear trend of the model output.

We examined the following potential confounders for the cITS model (Table 1): age, self-reported multiselect race (Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Black, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and White), Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, health insurance status, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, number of male sex partners in the last year, ever having received money or drugs for having sex with someone, and used methamphetamine in the last year. We used generalized linear models that included an interaction term for the guideline implementation period and CT or GC with the potential confounder as an outcome. This allowed us to determine which variables had a significant “difference-in-difference” of the confounder using t-tests and Wald tests, meaning that the confounder changed by guideline implementation period across the CT and GC groups [9]. Our results showed that age and White race were potential confounders, so we fit a cITS model adjusted for mean age and all race groups as binary variables.

We performed a sensitivity analysis where we fit additional versions of this cITS model varying the definition of RP infection to 14–30, 14–60, and 14–120 days after the initial diagnosis (primary analysis definition = 14–90 days after initial diagnosis). Additionally, we performed an ad hoc sensitivity analysis that excluded data from 2024 to account for the rollout of doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis (doxy PEP) at the SHC.

Analyses of Correlates Associated With Receipt of Azithromycin

Given the removal of azithromycin as first-line therapy for CT, we sought to determine whether correlates associated with receipt of azithromycin for treatment of rectal CT changed across the clinic implementation period, which may indicate provider bias rooted in concerns about patients completing the entire course of doxycycline. We examined this association in the period before and after the change in clinic guidelines (ie, before vs after April 2016). The analytic population for this analysis included all MSM with a rectal CT diagnosis (not necessarily RP); those coinfected with rectal GC and those who were prescribed both azithromycin and doxycycline were excluded. Correlates of interest for this analysis were the same variables that we examined as potential confounders in the cITS analyses (delineated in Table 1). We fit generalized estimating equations with a binomial family using an independent correlation structure to account for repeated cases of CT among the same person. We fit these models for the 2 time periods separately and for both periods combined using an interaction term for time period (to compare differences before and after the change in guidelines).

RESULTS

Impact of Changing Treatment Guidelines on Recurrent/Persistent C. trachomatis

There were 211 RP-CT infections and 222 RP-GC infections at the SHC between January 2012 and December 2024 (Table 1). The median age of participants differed significantly across time periods and by RP-CT or RP-GC infection. During preimplementation, those with RP-CT infections had a median age of 33 years, compared to those with RP-GC infections who had a median age of 26 years. This differed from the median ages for those with RP-CT and RP-GC during the implementation and fully implemented periods, which were both 29 years during implementation and were 30 (RP-CT) and 35 years (RP-GC) during the fully implemented period. There was a significant difference across time and by RP-CT or RP-GC group in the percentage of participants who self-identified as White (ranged from 31% to 82%, P value for “difference-in-difference” <.001). All other demographic and behavior variables did not vary significantly between the CT and GC groups and across time.

The level changes in Table 2 represent the number of quarterly cases immediately after the intervention timepoint divided by the number of quarterly cases immediately before the intervention timepoint. The level changes for RP-CT and RP-GC comparing implementation to preimplementation, the fully implemented period to preimplementation, and the fully implemented period to implementation all showed decreases in the number of RP infections across each of the intervention timepoints immediately following the intervention implementation, but none of these decreases was statistically significant.

The trend changes in Table 2 represent the ratio of the trend in quarterly RP cases during the period after and before the relevant timepoint. The trend in RP-CT decreased by 5.6% when comparing implementation to preimplementation (0.944; 95% CI: .887, 1.004; P = .065) and decreased significantly by 14.7% when comparing the fully implemented period to preimplementation (0.853; 95% CI: .765, .951; P = .004). The comparison between the fully implemented and implementation periods showed a 9.6% decrease in the trend in RP-CT (0.904; 95% CI: .816, 1.000; P = .050). For RP-GC, we did not observe any statistically significant changes in the trend of RP-GC during or after the implementation of CT treatment changes.

When comparing the trend change for RP-CT to RP-GC, the trend change from preimplementation to implementation decreased by 6.8% for RP-CT compared to RP-GC (0.932; 95% CI: .850, 1.021; P = .130), meaning that the trend for RP-CT decreased by 6.8% relative to the trend for RP-GC. From preimplementation to the fully implemented period, the trend change for RP-CT decreased significantly by 16.9% as compared to the trend change for RP-GC (0.831; 95% CI: .719, .957; P = .011), and from implementation to the fully implemented period, the trend change for RP-CT decreased by 10.8% (0.892; 95% CI: .785, 1.013; P = .079) as compared to the trend change for RP-GC.

Correlates Associated With Receipt of Azithromycin

In the time after the clinic implemented the new guidelines, self-identifying as White and with HIV were positively associated with receiving azithromycin for rectal CT, while self-identifying as Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander and Hispanic or Latino were inversely associated with receiving azithromycin (Table 4). These associations did not significantly differ before and after implementation, but self-identifying as Hispanic or Latino was also inversely associated with receiving azithromycin before the clinic's change in prescribing guidelines. Self-report of ever receiving money or drugs for having sex with someone was inversely associated with receipt of azithromycin before the change in guidelines but not after, and the difference between these associations across the change in guidelines approached statistical significance.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used cITS to assess the impact of changing treatment guidelines on RP-CT. We found that updating clinical protocols to recommend only doxycycline for CT resulted in a steady change in the clinic's prescribing practices, which corresponded to a decreasing (but nonsignificant) change in RP-CT cases during implementation. We found that when the clinic protocol was fully implemented—which coincided with a national policy change—there was a further decrease in RP-CT cases, which was a significant decline from the period prior to implementation. This suggests that the clinic's guidelines, when fully scaled and implemented with fidelity, had a substantial and significant impact on reducing RP-CT cases. Our sensitivity analysis, which varied the definition of RP-CT, demonstrated the robustness of these findings.

Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies have demonstrated the superiority of doxycycline relative to azithromycin for rectal CT, our study assessed whether implementing clinic guidelines to recommend doxycycline would ultimately lead to declines in RP-CT, an outcome we would expect to decline in the setting of widespread doxycycline use. In our main model, we found that the trend of RP-CT significantly decreased by 16.9% per quarter compared to RP-GC in the fully implemented period versus the implementation period. For the 14–120 days definition of RP infection (sensitivity analysis), we found that there were significant reductions in the trend change when comparing the fully implemented period to both implementation and preimplementation. These results are likely influenced by the number of RP cases according to the RP definitions: the 14–120 days definition had more cases compared to the 14–90 days definition. It is also possible that the 14–120 days definition is more likely to represent recurrent infections given the longer timeframe, but we could not accurately disentangle recurrent and persistent infections in this study.

Our findings are particularly robust when considering our control group. There were undoubtedly time-varying factors that influenced incidence and diagnosis of RP-CT during this time, namely the COVID-19 pandemic [18, 19], mpox outbreaks [20], and the rollout of doxy PEP at the SHC beginning in June 2023 [21]. Some of these time-varying factors influencing RP-CT would be expected to similarly affect RP-GC, which displayed a nearly identical trend to RP-CT prior to any implementation of new treatment guidelines. Therefore, we have more confidence that the changes in RP-CT trends we observed were related to changes in treatment guidelines and not other time-varying factors. The primary exception to this is the rollout of doxy PEP. The impact of doxy PEP on RP-CT and RP-GC is substantially different, as doxy PEP taken within 72 hours after sex reduces risk of CT by ∼70% and GC by 50% [22]. In this study, we were not able to disentangle the impacts of full implementation of using exclusively doxycycline for rectal CT and the rollout of doxy PEP on RP-CT infections, though in our clinic, use of doxy PEP did not substantially increase until 2024 (∼50% of clinic patients on doxy PEP), which was the last year of our primary analysis. However, our ad hoc sensitivity analysis that excluded 2024 did not show substantial changes in the level and trend changes for RP-CT versus RP-GC other than a lack of statistical significance for the trend change comparing the fully implemented period to preimplementation, which was likely due to fewer timepoints.

The lack of significant level changes in our cITS model (ie, changes to RP-CT immediately following the beginning of implementation) substantiates the notion that changes in clinical recommendations often take time to be fully implemented. At our clinic, the initial memo to recommend doxycycline over azithromycin for rectal CT was circulated in April 2016, but only about one-third of patients diagnosed with rectal CT in 2017 received doxycycline (Supplementary Table 4). Similarly, implementing changes to national guidelines also took several years, with 1 study of national data demonstrating that fewer than 60% of people diagnosed with CT in the United States received doxycycline in 2022 [23]. In our population, the percent prescribed doxycycline climbed incrementally each year from 2018 through 2020 and reached 93.1% during 2021 when the national guidelines were updated, contributing to the significant trend decrease when comparing RP-CT versus RP-GC in the national implementation and preimplementation periods. We are not entirely sure if this trend was observed because the strongest evidence of doxycycline's efficacy to treat rectal CT was published in 2021, because national guidelines were updated at that time or because it took time for providers’ prescribing patterns to change.

We hypothesized that correlates such as age and race would predict the receipt of azithromycin after the change in prescribing guidelines due to clinicians' implicit biases surrounding medication adherence [24, 25]. Azithromycin is prescribed as a 1 g single dose for the treatment of CT, while doxycycline is prescribed twice daily for 7 days. Accordingly, clinicians would be more likely to prescribe azithromycin to patients they believed may not adhere to the full course of doxycycline. At the SHC, both medications were available for free for patients. Although we did find some correlates associated with receipt of azithromycin after the change in clinic guidelines, our results did not suggest that there were significant differences in factors associated with azithromycin before and after the implementation of the change in clinic guidelines.

A major strength of our study was the substantial sample size for rectal CT and GC cases and the number of calendar quarters within each implementation period. We were also able to use an appropriate control outcome that accounts for time-varying influences on the incidence of RP-CT infections at the clinic. Our study is limited in terms of the true understanding of RP-CT and RP-GC infections as we utilized EHR data, meaning that diagnoses of RP infections were dependent on which patients chose to return for re-testing. Although the CDC recommends re-testing for CT within 3 months of a diagnosis [2], the majority of people do not re-test; estimates of re-testing range from 10% to 20.5% in various populations of men [26–28]. We were unable to ascertain the percentage of patients initially diagnosed with CT or GC who returned for re-testing in this study, but the participants in our study are likely substantially different from those who do not re-test for CT or GC. As previously mentioned, we were also unable to isolate the impacts of full implementation of the new prescribing guidelines from the implementation of doxy PEP at the SHC, although this was only meaningfully implemented near the end of our study. As these results come from a single sexual health clinic, they may not be generalizable to other settings.

Despite these limitations, we found that after the intervention was fully implemented, there was a significant reduction in the trend of RP-CT at the SHC as compared to RP-GC. This study builds on the existing RCTs demonstrating the effectiveness of doxycycline over azithromycin for the treatment of rectal CT by estimating the impact of the implementation of new prescribing guidelines on reducing subsequent RP-CT.
