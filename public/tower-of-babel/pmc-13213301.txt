The glenoid track in question: biomechanical considerations for combined inferior glenohumeral ligament repair with glenoid bone loss
Authors: Dr Parth Aphale, Himanshu Shekhar, Shashank Dokania
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent article by Diop et al1 on the “Biomechanical impact of repair of the posterior band of the inferior glenohumeral ligament on anterior stabilization of the shoulder with a large glenoid bone defect.” The authors provide compelling biomechanical evidence suggesting that a complementary repair of the posterior band (PB) of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL) significantly enhances glenohumeral stability compared to isolated anterior repair, even in the presence of a 20% anterior glenoid bone defect (GBD). This finding challenges the traditional focus of Bankart repair and highlights the critical role of the PB in resisting anteroinferior displacement, particularly under higher loads (30 N and 40 N).

While the data supporting increased stability with combined repair are robust, the clinical context of the 9 o'clock (0 to 9 o'clock) injury model warrants critical discussion. Anterior shoulder instability typically results from a 3-6 o'clock Bankart lesion alongside GBD, which may subsequently result in stretching or attenuation of the PB rather than an outright tear extending to 9 o'clock. Therefore, we question whether the “major instability” observed in the 9 hour injury model accurately reflects the most common failure pathway of an isolated soft-tissue Bankart repair (ie, failure due to GBD and lack of concavity, rather than a pre-existing 0-9 h tear). Does the necessary injury mechanism in this model risk overstating the clinical indication for routine PB repair? Future investigations could benefit from differentiating between acute 9 hour tears and chronic PB stretch/attenuation in the presence of GBD.

A key omission in the experimental design, which limits direct translational application, is the lack of consideration for the humeral side injury, specifically the Hill-Sachs lesion (HSL) and its relationship with the glenoid track concept.2 The failure of soft-tissue only repair in the presence of GBD is often a consequence of HSL engagement at the anterior glenoid rim. Given that the authors established a 20% GBD a threshold generally considered “critical” or “subcritical” by modern standards, particularly when coupled with an HSL3,4—it is imperative to know if the 20% GBD model created an “off track” lesion in the 90° abduction and 60° external rotation (ER) test position. The biomechanical efficacy of the PB repair in reducing anteroinferior displacement might be fundamentally different if the mechanism of failure shifts from pure soft-tissue laxity to bony engagement.5 How does the added PB repair influence the critical shoulder range of motion required to prevent HSL engagement, and was HSL engagement monitored during the testing sequences?

Finally, the study's conclusion that the 9h repair provided stability “comparable or higher than the one of intact specimens” without statistically significant restriction in ER is noteworthy. However, the nonstandard repair technique utilizing transosseous tunnels and Endobuttons to suture the IGHL/labrum onto the resected glenoid rim, explicitly avoiding tightening (which the authors highlight as a departure from plication) may limit the generalizability of this ER finding. Given that modern arthroscopic repair failure is often linked to overtensioning and subsequent ER loss, was the tension applied to the PB repair quantitatively standardized and measured, or was it based purely on the knot-tying technique? Understanding the exact tension achieved is crucial for clinical implementation.

Diop et al successfully advance the understanding of the IGHL's posterior component in anterior instability management. Nevertheless, future work should integrate the bipolar bony lesion complex, specifically the glenoid track, with these soft-tissue repair concepts to produce a truly clinically comprehensive assessment.
