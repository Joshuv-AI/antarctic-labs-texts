Major financial problems, low mental well-being and reduced HIV/STI testing among sex workers in the Netherlands during the COVID-19 pandemic: a repeated cross-sectional survey
Authors: Mariëlle Kloek, Daphne van Wees, Jane Bakker, Mischa Tyd, Julia Raxon Rosales, Tomas Geling, Evelien Spek, Jan A C Hontelez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine associations between the banning of sex work during the COVID-19 pandemic, and work, financial problems, mental well-being and HIV/sexually transmittable infection (STI) testing among sex workers in the Netherlands.DesignTwo cross-sectional online surveys. The first survey covered two time-periods: pre-COVID-19 (1 January 2019 to 31 December 2019) and period 1 (15 March 2020 to 1 July 2020). The second survey covered period 2 (1 January 2021 to 31 December 2021).SettingThe NetherlandsParticipantsIn total, 106 (first survey) and 196 (second survey) sex workers participated. Most of the participants in the first and second survey were cisgender women (respectively, 76.4% and 66.5%), followed by cisgender men (respectively, 12.3% and 15.7%) and the combination of transgender men, transgender women, non-binary or other (respectively, 11.3% and 17.6%). Most participants were born in the Netherlands (respectively, 61.4% and 69.7%).Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe provide descriptive statistics of self-reported work during and prior to COVID-19 measures, financial problems due to COVID-19 measures and HIV/STI testing and mental well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic. We also performed logistic and linear regression analyses to identify risk factors associated with reporting financial problems due to COVID-19 measures, not testing for HIV/STIs and lower mental well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic.ResultsIn periods 1 and 2, respectively, 69.6% and 62.0% reported financial problems due to the COVID-19 measures. Among those who reported to have had sex with clients, the percentage not HIV/STI testing was: 4.5% (95% CI: 0.9; 12.5) pre-COVID-19, 28.2% (95% CI: 15.0; 44.9) in period 1, and 15.2% (95% CI: 9.7; 22.3) in period 2. In the multivariate analysis, reported financial problems due to the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with not HIV/STI testing (OR: 12.1, p<0.001) and lower mental well-being (B: −2.7, p<0.001).ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic control measures in the Netherlands were associated with major financial problems, low mental well-being and reduced HIV/STI testing among sex workers.Keywords: COVID-19, Health Services, Sexually Transmitted Disease, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

COVID-19, a disease caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, caused a global pandemic and crisis since December 2019 and put health systems worldwide under tremendous pressure.1 On 15 March 2020, the Dutch government decided on a lockdown with accompanying COVID-19 containment measures to keep the spread of COVID-19 and the pressure on the healthcare system manageable.2 3 These measures included, among others, the closure of several occupational groups, such as the travel, hotel and catering industry, and contact professions (eg, sex workers, hairdressers and masseurs). A financial support package was made available by the government for the vast majority of these occupational groups, however not including most of the sex workers.4

The sex work community in the Netherlands is diverse in age, gender, social–economic status and migration background. About 60% are migrant sex workers, mainly with an Eastern European or Latin American background.5 Sex workers can work in clubs, private houses, massage saloons (CPM), as an escort, in brothel windows, on the streets, from home and via the internet. The legal framework regarding sex work in the Netherlands is generally considered as ‘regulated’, that is, sex work is not prohibited, but is regulated through permits and licensing, and is comparable to other European countries (eg, Germany, Austria and Switzerland).6 Whether or not a sex worker can work legally and licensed is complex and depends on age, legal status, work permit in the Netherlands, a license for the sex work location, a permit from the municipality and registration at the chamber of commerce.7 In practice, the complexities of these regulations often make it difficult for sex workers to work legally.7,11 Illegally selling sex can result in penalties from the internal revenue service, arrests, home evictions or deportations (in case of illegal immigrants selling sex). Furthermore, sex workers in the Netherlands experience high levels of stigma and violence.12 Literature shows that the criminalisation of sex work and unsafe work conditions negatively impact sex workers’ well-being and access to care for HIV and sexually transmittable infections (STI).13,16

As a consequence of the control measures, sex work basically was an illegal contact occupation for long periods during the COVID-19 pandemic. It was forbidden for almost 12 months, from 15 March 2020 to 26 February 2022.17 During the periods when sex work was allowed, sex work was possible with observance of the COVID-19 measures implemented by the government, such as conducting a health check.18 Unlike other professions that had to close or scale down their businesses, less than 10% of sex workers accessed governmental financial support.19 In addition, while all other contact professions were allowed to resume service by 3 March 2021, sex work remained illegal for another two and a half months.17 This likely caused severe economic hardship among sex workers and increased illegal and unsafe sex work.7 11 On top of that, many sexual health centres (SHCs) temporarily scaled down their HIV/STI services, such as outreach activities, consultations and testing, to facilitate COVID-19-related activities, likely affecting the sex workers’ access to essential HIV/STI care.10 11 20 The prohibition of sex work during the COVID-19 pandemic was strictly enforced, with police actively searching for those who continue to sell sex. Those who were arrested received fines, were at risk of home evictions, or at risk of deportation.

The aim of our study was to determine the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and the containment measures, and work, financial problems, mental well-being and HIV/STI testing among sex workers in the Netherlands. Studying the impacts of COVD-19 and its containment measures on sex work is essential to allow policymakers, social workers and healthcare professionals to better prepare for future comparable crises and better mitigate potential collateral damage. Next to that, the impacts of delegalisation of sex work during the COVID-19 pandemic can provide important lessons for future policy changes imposing further restrictions on sex work. One such change, a new law about sex work—‘Wet Regulering Sekswerk’ (WRS)—is currently being prepared by Dutch lawmakers.21 22 In short, this law will oblige sex workers to undergo a self-reliance assessment with a civil servant where at least the following elements will be scored: sex work experience, presence of a social network in the Netherlands, housing situation, motivations, relationships, indicators of coercion and exploitation, language proficiency, knowledge of rights and obligations, health and mental capacity. If approved, the sex worker will be registered in a database for sex workers.21 22 It is expected that the vast majority will refuse registration and all these sex workers will then work illegally and are punishable by law, as well as their clients and other people working with these sex workers.

Methods

Study design

Our study had a cross-sectional design, consisting of two online retrospective surveys during the COVID-19 pandemic. The first survey was conducted from 18 January 2021 to 15 March 2021 and covered two time-periods: pre-COVID-19 (1 January 2019 to 31 December 2019) and period 1 (15 March 2020 to 1 July 2020). The second survey was conducted from 13 January 2022 to 15 March 2022, and covered period 2 (1 January 2021 to 31 December 2021). A schematic overview of survey timings and COVID-19 containment measures during the study period is given in figure 1. There were three periods in which sex work was forbidden: from March 2020 to June 2020; November 2020 to May 2021 and December 2021 to February 2022. In November 2021, sex work was partially allowed, but only between 6 am and 6 pm. The first survey covered two periods: pre-COVID-19 (1 January 2019 to 31 December 2019) and period 1 (15 March 2020 to 1 July 2020). The only exception was for the number of HIV/STI tests per year in period 1. Here, we asked whether a person was tested between 15 March 2020 up to the date of filling in the survey (between 18 January 2021 and 15 March 2021). So that we could assess whether someone did an HIV/STI test during the time of approximately 1 year since the COVID-19 pandemic started. The second survey covered the year 2021 (1 January 2021 to 31 December 2021) and was called ‘period 2’. This period consisted of months where sex work was forbidden, allowed and partially allowed (figure 1). More details on sex work-specific timelines of measures can be found at ‘SekswerkExpertise’.17

Data collection

The questionnaires were developed through Limesurvey online survey tool,23 and the topics covered differed slightly between the two surveys. Commonly covered topics were: gender, age, country of birth, work experience, number of sex clients (ie, clients with which the sex worker had sexual intercourse), work locations and HIV/STI testing. In addition, we specifically asked to which extent participants had experienced financial problems due to the COVID-19 pandemic: no (ie, enough money for comfortable living), minor (ie, no comfortable living next to the costs for housing and living) or major financial problems (ie, not enough money to pay the costs for housing, bills and groceries). Due to experiences from focus group discussions in the first survey and expert opinion, we added the following items to the second survey: questions on mental well-being (the average mental well-being score from 0 to 10, and the occurrence of seven symptoms of poor mental well-being), reported STI occurrence (if a person had a diagnosed STI) and violence (if a person experienced physical violence, sexual violence and/or sextortion). The surveys could be completed anonymously. They were available in Dutch, English and Spanish, making them accessible to the largest part of sex workers in the Netherlands. The English version of both questionnaires is given in online supplemental material-questionnaire 1 and online supplemental material-questionnaire 2.

All sex workers in the Netherlands aged 18 years or older, regardless of sex and gender (eg, cisgender women, cisgender men, transgender women, transgender men, intersex persons, non-binary persons and persons who identify differently), and active in 2019 and/or during the pandemic, were eligible to participate in the study, regardless of whether they worked legally or illegally and licensed or unlicensed (see definitions in appendix panel 1). We did not perform an a priori sample size calculation, and aimed to include as many as possible within the data collection timeframes.

We employed a convenience sampling strategy by recruiting participants through online network engagement and advertisements. The community researchers drove the recruitment of participants, mainly through various online platforms such as sex work advertising websites Kinky24 and Hookers,25 on the website sekswerk.info,26 through Twitter (@sekswerk.info), through professionals networks (Region Experts Prostitution), contacts with SHC staff, sex work organisations, WhatsApp groups and mutual sex work networks, accountants and operators and the operators association ‘Vereniging Exploitanten Relaxbedrijven’.27 Participant recruitment was explicitly focused on representing different subgroups within the various sex work communities based on gender, type of workplace, age and country of birth. There was no financial compensation for participation in the first survey. Participants in the second survey received a €20 voucher for an online retail store. Given the sampling strategy and anonymity of the survey, we could not assess whether individuals participated in only one, or both surveys. Advertisements in Dutch, English and Spanish are given in the online supplemental material - advertisements.

The study received medical ethical committee approval by Erasmus MC Ethical Review Board (MEC-2020-0849). Informed consent was not needed, as the data collection procedure was through an anonymous online survey. No personal data were ever handled.

Analysis

The data from the surveys were analysed using descriptive statistics; numbers (N), percentages (%) and 95% CIs. For binary variables, we compared the 95% CIs for periods 1 and 2 with the 95% CI pre-COVID-19 and defined differences to be significant if CIs were not overlapping. For categorical variables a χ2 goodness of fit analysis was performed to identify significant (p<0.05) differences between the pandemic periods (periods 1 and 2) and pre-COVID-19. All analyses were done on the full sample, except for the question regarding HIV/STI testing. Here, we only assessed results for those who reported to have had sex with clients over the survey period, as those who reported to have had no sex with clients may have perceived no need for testing due to lower risks.

We performed three regression analyses based on the data from period 2. First, we determined risk factors for reporting financial problems due to COVID-19 through a logistic regression, with reported financial problems (major and minor vs no) as the dependent variable, and age, gender, country of birth, number of clients and violence as potential risk factors. Next, we determined to which extent the mental well-being score and not testing for HIV/STI were associated with reporting financial problems due to COVID-19 through a linear and logistic regression, respectively. For all three models, we first determined the univariate associations, and then tested potential confounders (age, gender, country of birth, number of clients, financial problems and violence) by constructing a multivariate model through backwards selection, and choosing the model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion.28 All analyses were performed in R software V.4.0.0.

Patient and public involvement statement

Three sex workers were part of the research team as community researchers. The community researchers actively participated in all research phases, including study design, development of data collection tools, participant recruitment, data interpretation and reviewing of the paper.

Results

A total of 106 sex workers participated in the first survey, and 196 sex workers participated in the second survey. There were some significant differences between the participants in the two surveys: participants in the first survey were older (p=0.015), more likely born in the Netherlands (p=0.046), and had more work experience (p<0.001) compared with the second survey. Most of the participants in the first and second survey were cisgender women (respectively, 76.4% and 66.5%), followed by cisgender men (respectively, 12.3% and 15.7%) and the combination of transgender men, transgender women, non-binary or other (respectively, 11.3% and 17.6%). In both surveys, most participants (89.3% and 81.5%) were 25 years or older, while only 10.7% and 18.5% were between 18 and 24 years of age. Most participants were born in the Netherlands (respectively, 61.4% and 69.7%). More details on participant characteristics can be found in the online supplemental material table S1.

Work and financial problems

During period 1 of our study, the participants reported that the number of sex clients had declined substantially compared with the pre-COVID-19 period: 45.2% (95% CI: 35.4%; 55.3%) of sex workers reported no clients and 4.8% (95% CI: 1.6%; 10.9%) reported more than 10 clients per week during period 1, compared with 3.8% (95% CI: 1.1%; 9.6%) reporting no clients and 29.8% (95% CI: 21.2%; 39.6%) reporting more than 10 clients pre-COVID-19. In period 2, 17.4% (95% CI: 12.2%; 23.8%) reported no clients, and 11.8% (95% CI: 7.5%; 17.5%) reported more than 10 clients per week (figure 2A, online supplemental table S2). In addition, there were substantial shifts in work locations of sex workers during the COVID-19 periods. During period 1, the proportion working at escort, CPM and window reduced from 38.7% (95% CI: 29.4; 48.6), 29.2% (95% CI: 20.8; 38.9) and 14.2% (95% CI: 8.1%; 22.3%), respectively, pre-COVID-19 to 20.8% (95% CI: 13.5%; 29.7%), 0.0% (95% CI: 0.0%; 3.4%) and 0.0% (95% CI: 0.0%; 3.4%) in period 1. During period 2, many of these locations were reported as working locations more often, even compared with pre-COVID-19: 50.5% (95% CI: 43.3%; 57.8%) for escort and 38.7% (95% CI: 31.8%; 45.9%) for CPM. Other locations such as at home or hotel/holiday park remained more stable over the different time periods (figure 2B, online supplemental table S2).

During periods 1 and 2, respectively, 29.0% (95% CI: 18.7%; 41.2%) and 29.8% (95% CI: 23.1%; 37.3%) reported major financial problems due to COVID-19 and the pandemic control measures, meaning they had no enough money to pay for housing, bills and groceries. Another 40.6% (95% CI: 28.9%; 53.1%) and 32.2% (95% CI: 25.2%; 39.7%) of sex workers in periods 1 and 2, respectively, reported minor financial problems, meaning they could not have a comfortable living next to the costs for housing and living (figure 2C, online supplemental table S3). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, those reporting to have experienced violence were more likely to report any COVID-19 related financial problems (adjusted OR (aOR)=2.33, 95% CI: 1.02; 5.33), while those reporting more than 10 clients per week were significantly less likely to report any financial problems due to COVID-19 compared with those reporting 1–10 clients per week (aOR=0.23, 95% CI: 0.07; 0.80) (figure 3, online supplemental table S4).

Health

Self-reported mental well-being, HIV/STI testing and STI occurrence among sex workers are shown in table 1. In period 2, sex workers scored their mental well-being on average with a 5.6 out of 10 (IQR: 4.0 to 7.0), with 13.8% (95% CI: 9.0%; 19.8%) of sex workers reporting thoughts of death or suicide. Among sex workers who reported to have had sex with clients, in the period pre-COVID-19, only 4.5% (95% CI: 0.9%; 12.5%) did not do an HIV/STI test over the period of 1 year, but this number increased to 28.2% (95% CI: 15.0%; 44.9%) in period 1 and 15.2% (95% CI: 9.7%; 22.3%) in period 2. We also found that in period 2, 61.2% (95% CI: 54.1%; 68.3%) tested at least once at an SHC, 24.0% (95% CI: 17.9%; 30.2%) at the general practitioner and 12.6% (95% CI: 7.8%; 17.4%) through online services. Also, 28.7% (95% CI: 21.9%; 36.2%) reported a diagnosed STI in period 2 and an additional 1.8% (95% CI: 0.4%; 5.3%) thought having had an STI, but did not test.

Discussion

We showed that about 65% of sex workers in the Netherlands reported financial problems due to COVID-19 pandemic lockdown measures, half of which reported not having enough money to pay the costs of housing, bills and groceries. Reported annual HIV/STI testing reduced significantly among people who reported to have had sex with clients, from 95% pre-COVID-19 to 70% and 85% in the first and second periods, respectively. Major financial problems due to COVID-19 lockdown measures appeared strongly associated with a decreased mental well-being (−2.8 points out of 10, p<0.001) and not HIV/STI testing (OR: 12.1, p<0.001). Finally, violence during the pandemic was associated with financial problems and a lower mental well-being.

Our study confirms earlier findings by de Wildt and Sondeijker,29 who showed that among 16 sex workers in the Netherlands, more than half suffered from financial problems due to COVID-19 measures. Likewise, multiple international studies found that high proportions of sex workers worldwide suffered severe financial distress due to a lack of governmental financial support.30,34 Our findings of low HIV/STI testing are also in line with findings from the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM), who found a 45% reduction in the number of HIV/STI testing consultations at SHCs among sex workers during the pandemic.20 During the pandemic, many SHCs temporarily scaled down their HIV/STI services to facilitate COVID-19-related activities. It is alarming that especially the sex workers with the highest numbers of clients were not HIV/STI testing. Reporting financial problems due to COVID-19 was strongly associated with not testing in our study, further emphasising the importance of free HIV/STI testing at SHCs. This is in line with multiple international studies,31,33 pointing at financial problems—caused by the lack of governmental financial support—as a major underlying driver of reduced HIV/STI testing for sex workers.913,16 31 33

Despite reduced HIV/STI testing, 30% reported having had at least one diagnosed STI during the pandemic. In addition, RIVM identified an increased STI positivity rate among sex workers who accessed testing through SHCs from 9% in 2017 to 13% in 2021.20 Considering the number of sex workers not HIV/STI testing, and the observation that those reporting more than 10 clients per week were more likely to not test, the number of undiagnosed STIs has probably increased during the pandemic. This is in line with a study by van den Dries and van Oorschot, who found increased sexual risk behaviour among male sex workers in the Netherlands during the COVID-19 pandemic.34 In addition, Tan and colleagues found increased sexual risk behaviour among sex workers in Singapore due to financial hardship.31 These findings highlight that it is vital for a future crisis that sex workers receive sufficient governmental financial support and sexual health services remain open, inclusive and safe for all sex workers regardless of circumstances (eg, whether sex workers work legal or illegal) and personal conditions.10 35

The mental well-being score of sex workers (5.6 out of 10) was low during the pandemic, much lower than the average 7.3 out of 10 well-being score among the Dutch general population during the same period.36 Among the general Dutch population, mental well-being reduced significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic.37 It is likely that the same happened for sex workers, as reported financial problems due to the COVID-19 lockdown measures were significantly associated with a lower mental well-being score.

The results of our study can be interpreted as a natural experiment into the effects of criminalising sex work, and support the notion that safety and health are compromised when policies and regulations lead to illegal work.713,16 Sex work policies in the Netherlands have become increasingly restrictive over the past decades. Up to 75% of licensed workplaces were shut down between 2000 and 2020.27 38 The upcoming ‘WRS’ would oblige sex workers to conduct a self-reliance assessment with a civil servant, and would likely result in increased illegality of sex work in the Netherlands, as similar regulations did in Sweden.39 Our research and the international literature show the potential detrimental consequences of such policies for the health and well-being of sex workers.31,3340 41

Our study has some limitations. First, the results rely on self-reported data. We cannot exclude the potential influence of social desirability bias, even though we tried to mitigate this effect by setting up fully anonymous online surveys. Second, there are no population estimates of sex workers in the Netherlands, making it challenging to assess whether our sample is fully representative. Third, the results are based on two repeated cross-sectional surveys, which means that the results from the first and second survey are not directly comparable. In the first survey, more participants were older, born in the Netherlands, and had more work experience compared with the second survey. It is not clear whether this difference represents a (temporary) change in the sex work community, or whether we recruited a different group of sex workers (perhaps as a consequence of using an incentive in the second survey). Fourth, despite extensive online outreach activities, recruiting participants proved even more difficult than before COVID-19, when we conducted a research into stigma and violence.12 In the Netherlands, sex workers are generally hesitant to participate in research, as previous research has been misused by anti-sex work politicians. In addition, COVID-19 measures forced us to use an online questionnaire and multiple online platforms for recruitment, while usually in person outreach work is an essential part of our recruitment strategy and trust from the community. In addition, the usual online platforms for sex workers were subject to police checks for working sex workers, further complicating participant recruitment. In our study, sex workers and community representatives were part of the research activities throughout the process, mitigating some of these challenges. In addition, we translated the surveys into Dutch, English and Spanish, the three most commonly spoken languages among sex workers in The Netherlands.

We conclude that many sex workers faced severe financial problems during the COVID-19 pandemic control measures in the Netherlands, which was associated with reduced HIV/STI testing and mental well-being. Similar policies in future crises should include measures for adequate financial support, accessible mental health and HIV/STI care and the protection of the health and safety of sex workers. In addition, potential future temporary lockdown measures, or permanent restrictive or criminalising polices for sex work, should be better weighted against its collateral damage, and it is key that sex workers are involved in formulating policies and measures to prevent negative consequences as much as possible.
