HOXC6 drives a therapeutically targetable pancreatic cancer growth and metastasis pathway by regulating MSK1 and PPP2R2B
Authors: Parmanand Malvi, Suresh Chava, Guoping Cai, Kai Hu, Lihua Julie Zhu, Yvonne JK Edwards, Michael R Green, Romi Gupta, Narendra Wajapeyee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the deadliest cancers, which lacks effective therapies. Here, we demonstrate that the transcription factor, homeobox C6 (HOXC6), is overexpressed in most PDACs, and its inhibition blocks PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. HOXC6 transcriptionally activates tumor-promoting kinase MSK1 and suppresses tumor-inhibitory protein PPP2R2B in PDAC. HOXC6-induced PPP2R2B suppression causes mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway activation, which facilitates PDAC growth. Also, MSK1 upregulation by HOXC6 is necessary for PDAC growth because of its ability to suppress apoptosis via its substrate DDX17. Combinatorial pharmacological inhibition of MSK1 and mTOR potently suppressed PDAC tumor growth and metastasis in PDAC mouse models. PDAC cells with acquired resistance to MSK1/mTOR-inhibitors displayed activated insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) signaling and were successfully eradicated by IGF1R inhibitor. Furthermore, MEK inhibitor trametinib enhanced the efficacy of dual MSK1 and mTOR inhibition. Collectively, these results identify therapeutic vulnerabilities of PDAC and an approach to overcome acquired drug resistance to prolong therapeutic benefit.Keywords: pancreatic cancer, PDACs, HOXC6, tumor growth, metastasis, MSK1 kinase, protein phosphatase 2 regulatory subunit B beta, PPP2R2B

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common form of pancreatic cancer and accounts for more than 400,000 deaths worldwide annually.1 The 5-year overall survival rate for patients with advanced/unresectable PDAC is estimated to be approximately 12% for all Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results stages combined and decreases to approximately 3% for patients with distal metastasis.2,3 Over the last two decades, PDAC treatment has evolved, but surgical resection, when possible, remains the only potential cure for this cancer.3 Current therapies include FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents such as albumin-bound paclitaxel, erlotinib, or immunotherapies.3 However, each of these therapies has shown significant limitations in providing durable clinical benefits for PDAC patients.4 Therefore, a better understanding of PDAC dependencies is required to develop more effective therapies for these patients.

Transcriptional deregulation plays a crucial role in the growth and metastasis of several cancer types.5 Among these, the overexpression of transcription factors is a prevalent mechanism that leads to transcriptional dysregulation in cancer cells.5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Therefore, it is anticipated that the discovery of previously undocumented transcription factor-driven oncogenic molecular networks will reveal alternative cancer treatment options.

The homeobox (HOX) family of transcription factors comprises DNA-binding proteins that can either activate or repress transcription.12 HOX genes have been shown to be deregulated in cancer and other human diseases.13,14 In particular, HOX genes are mutated or overexpressed in cancer and play a direct role in the development and progression of some cancers.14,15,16,17

Here, we demonstrate that the transcription factor HOXC6 is overexpressed in most PDACs and is essential for tumor growth and metastasis. Additionally, we demonstrate that HOXC6 inhibits protein phosphatase 2 regulatory subunit B beta (PPP2R2B), a regulatory subunit of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), and activates ribosomal protein S6 kinase, also known as MSK1, to promote PDAC growth. Combining pharmacological inhibition of MSK1 with an inhibitor of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) effectively inhibits PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. Furthermore, modeling the acquired resistance to MSK1 and mTOR inhibition in PDAC cells reveals that PDAC cells acquiring resistance to both MSK1 and mTOR inhibitors become sensitive to insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) inhibition. Finally, we show that MEK inhibitor trametinib further enhances the efficacy of SB-747651A and everolimus combination therapy. Collectively, these results demonstrate the role of HOXC6 as a driver of PDAC tumor growth and metastasis, and identify alternative therapeutic targets for PDAC treatment.

Results

HOXC6 is overexpressed in the majority of PDACs in a PI3K/AKT pathway-dependent manner

Because transcription factors are essential mediators of cancer growth and metastasis,5 we analyzed publicly available PDAC patient samples to identify overexpressed transcription factors. We found that HOXC6 mRNA was significantly overexpressed in PDACs relative to normal pancreas samples (Figures 1A and S1A).18,19,20,21,22,23 To validate these findings, we measured HOXC6 protein expression using immunohistochemistry in a tissue microarray composed of PDACs and normal pancreatic tissues (Table S1). Consistent with our analysis, we discovered that HOXC6 protein expression was significantly greater in a large majority of patient-derived PDACs than in normal-matched pancreatic tissues (Figures 1B and 1C; Table S1). Previous studies have classified PDACs into distinct transcriptional subtypes based on their mRNA profiles.24,25,26,27 Therefore, we asked whether HOXC6 overexpression was associated with a specific PDAC transcriptional subtype by analyzing four previously published datasets.24,25,26,27 However, we did not observe significant enrichment of HOXC6 in any specific transcriptional PDAC subtype (Figures S1B–S1E), which indicates that its expression is not restricted to a single PDAC subtype.

Next, we investigated the mechanism underlying HOXC6 overexpression. In 95% of PDACs, oncogenic mutations in the KRAS gene result in constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways downstream of KRAS.28 Therefore, we hypothesized that the PI3K/AKT or MAPK pathways are responsible for the overexpression of HOXC6 in PDAC. PDAC cells were treated with the MEK inhibitor trametinib to inhibit the MAPK pathway and PI3K inhibitor buparlisib to inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway to test this hypothesis. Buparlisib treatment decreased mRNA and protein levels of HOXC6 in PDAC cells (Figures 1D and 1E). Additionally, we identified a significant positive correlation between HOXC6 expression and pAKT1 level in PDAC patient samples (Figure 1F),29 which further supports the role of PI3K-AKT pathway in upregulating HOXC6 in PDAC cells. However, HOXC6 mRNA and protein levels were not decreased in PDAC cells treated with trametinib (Figures S2A and S2B). Finally, to complement experiments with buparlisib, we ectopically expressed a constitutively active PI3K catalytic subunit alpha (PI3KCA) in immortalized human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (HPNE-hTERT). Consistent with the effects of buparlisib, ectopic PI3KCA expression increased HOXC6 mRNA and protein levels (Figures 1G and 1H). Altogether, these findings demonstrate that HOXC6 is overexpressed in PDAC in a PI3K/AKT pathway-dependent manner. Next, to determine the mechanism by which the PI3K/AKT pathway stimulates HOXC6 transcription in PDAC, we analyzed the HOXC6 promoter to identify transcription factor (TF)-binding sites using two independent TF-binding site prediction programs (PROMO 3.0 and rVista 2.0),30,31 which identified 10 common TFs as potential direct regulators of HOXC6 (Figure S2C). Then, using these commonly identified TFs, we determined which of these, similar to HOXC6, were also upregulated in PDAC and were correlated with a high p-AKT level. These analyses shortlisted CEBPB and ETS1 as potential transcriptional activators of HOXC6 in PDAC (Figures S2C–S2E). We next determined whether PI3K/AKT regulates the expression of CEBPB or ETS1 by treating PDAC cells with buparlisib. We found that buparlisib treatment inhibited CEBPB expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures S2F and S2G). Furthermore, a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay showed that CEBPB was associated with the HOXC6 promoter (Figure 1I), and its knockdown decreased HOXC6 mRNA and protein expression in PDAC cells (Figures 1J and 1K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PI3K stimulates HOXC6 expression in PDAC cells via the TF CEBPB.

HOXC6 expression is necessary for PDAC tumor growth and metastasis

Given that HOXC6 was overexpressed in PDAC, we hypothesized that HOXC6 may be responsible for PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. We knocked down HOXC6 expression in PDAC cell lines to test this hypothesis using two sequence-independent short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) (Figures S3A and S3B). These PDAC cell lines expressed significantly higher levels of HOXC6 than HPNE-hTERT cells (Figure S3C). The ability of these HOXC6 knockdown-validated PDAC cell lines to grow in an anchorage-independent manner was then evaluated using soft agar assays.32 PDAC cells expressing nonspecific (NS) shRNA were used as a negative control. We found that HOXC6 knockdown significantly decreased the ability of PDAC cells to form colonies in soft agar (Figures S4A and S4B). Next, we examined the biological consequences of HOXC6-loss in PDAC cells. Knockdown of HOXC6 resulted in increased apoptosis induction and decreased cellular proliferation, as evidenced by an increase in cleaved caspase-3 (Figure S4C), increased annexin V-positive cells (Figure S4D), and decreased EdU incorporation in PDAC cells expressing HOXC6 shRNAs compared with NS shRNA-expressing cells (Figure S4E). These findings demonstrate that HOXC6 enhances an anti-apoptotic and a pro-proliferative response to facilitate PDAC growth.

We then investigated whether HOXC6 knockdown also inhibits PDAC tumor growth in vivo. We first used a xenograft-based mouse model of PDAC based on injecting human PDAC cells subcutaneously into immunocompromised mice. PDAC cells expressing either HOXC6-specific shRNAs or NS shRNA were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of immunocompromised mice, and tumor growth was measured. We observed that HOXC6 knockdown inhibited the growth of PDAC tumors in vivo in this mouse model (Figure S4F).

Next, we evaluated the effect of HOXC6 knockdown on the metastatic characteristics of PDAC cells. We began by analyzing the effects of HOXC6 knockdown on the invasion of PDAC cells. In a Matrigel invasion assay, HOXC6 knockdown significantly inhibited the invasiveness of PDAC cells relative to cells expressing NS shRNA (Figures S4G and S4H). Using a PDAC mouse model of lung metastasis, we investigated the effect of HOXC6 knockdown on PDAC lung metastasis in vivo. We injected firefly luciferase (F-Luc)-labeled PDAC cells expressing control NS shRNA or HOXC6 shRNAs into the tail vein of NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice. Compared with PDAC cells expressing the NS shRNA, HOXC6 knockdown significantly decreased the PDAC lung metastatic growth (Figures 2A–2C).

To further demonstrate the essential role of HOXC6 in PDAC tumor growth and metastasis, we used a more stringent orthotopic PDAC mouse model to recapitulate tumor growth in the pancreas and to model the multi-step metastasis of PDAC to distant organs, as observed in human PDAC patients.33,34 F-Luc-labelled PDAC cells expressing NS shRNA or HOXC6 shRNA were injected into the pancreas of mice, and bioluminescence imaging was used to image the pancreas. The lungs and livers of mice were also imaged at the end of the experiments to monitor metastatic progression. We observed that HOXC6 knockdown significantly impaired pancreatic tumor growth and attenuated metastatic progression to the lungs and liver compared with mice injected with PDAC cells expressing NS shRNA (Figures 2D–2G and S5A–S5D), further validating the results observed in subcutaneous and tail vein-based PDAC models.

Loss of HOXC6 inhibits the mTOR pathway via upregulation of PPP2R2B

Next, we sought to identify the mechanisms by which HOXC6 inhibition suppresses PDAC growth. We hypothesized that, because HOXC6 is a TF, it influences PDAC tumor growth and metastasis by regulating the expression of its target genes. Therefore, we analyzed PANC1 cells expressing HOXC6 or NS shRNA using transcriptome-wide RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). RNA-seq identified PPP2R2B as the most highly upregulated gene in HOXC6 shRNA-expressing PANC1 cells (Figure S6A and Table S2). Brain-specific regulatory subunit B of PP2A, a multiple-protein phosphatase with a catalytic subunit, structural subunit, and regulatory subunit, is encoded by the PPP2R2B gene.35 Previous research has demonstrated that PPP2R2B is inactivated in breast cancer, and its genetic variant is associated with an increased risk and recurrence of breast cancer,36,37 suggesting a tumor suppressor-like function in cancer.

RNA-seq data validation confirmed the upregulation of PPP2R2B mRNA and protein in PDAC cells in response to shRNA-mediated HOXC6 knockdown (Figures 3A and 3B). PPP2R2B protein expression was substantially decreased in the vast majority of PDAC patient samples (<25% of samples expressed PPP2R2B) (Figures S6B and S6C). Furthermore, our ChIP experiments demonstrated that HOXC6 was directly associated with the PPP2R2B promoter (Figure S6D), which shows that PPP2R2B is a direct target of HOXC6. Similarly, single-cell analysis of PDAC cells with NS shRNA or HOXC6 shRNA revealed a negative correlation between HOXC6 and PPP2R2B expression (Figure S6E). We also performed a HOXC6 and PPP2R2B correlation analysis using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis to examine The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset and found a significant negative correlation between HOXC6 and PPP2R2B expression (Figure S6F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PPP2R2B is directly repressed by HOXC6 in PDAC.

We then hypothesized that HOXC6-mediated repression of PPP2R2B in PDAC may play a significant role in PDAC development and that the PPP2R2B upregulation in PDAC cells after HOXC6 knockdown may influence mTOR signaling; previous studies have demonstrated that decreased PPP2R2B levels correlate with AKT/mTOR pathway activation and that PPP2R2B expression sensitizes colon cancer cells to rapamycin.38,39 Initially, we measured levels of phosphorylated ribosomal S6 kinase (phospho-p70 S6K) and discovered that it was significantly decreased in HOXC6 shRNA-expressing PDAC cells (Figure 3B), indicating decreased mTOR pathway activity. Since increased mTOR pathway activity also results in increased protein translation,40 we evaluated the protein translation using a technique that relies on the incorporation and detection of the puromycin analog O-propargyl-puromycin.41 PDAC cells expressing HOXC6 shRNA consistently exhibited decreased global protein synthesis (Figures 3C and 3D).

Next, we hypothesized that HOXC6 knockdown-mediated upregulation of PPP2R2B in PDAC cells could result in tumor suppression. Therefore, we simultaneously knocked down PPP2R2B and HOXC6 in PANC1 cells using shRNAs. In these double knockdown cells, we first measured phospho-p70 S6K levels to assess the mTOR pathway. The double knockdown cells demonstrated restored mTOR signaling, as indicated by a higher level of phospho-p70 S6K compared with PDAC cells expressing HOXC6 shRNA alone (Figure 3E). Next, we examined whether double knockdown cells exhibited a growth advantage in soft agar assays and discovered that the simultaneous knockdown of PPP2R2B partially restored the ability of PDAC cells expressing HOXC6 shRNA to grow in soft agar (Figures 3F and 3G). Furthermore, the double knockdown of PPP2R2B and HOXC6 partially rescued protein translation (Figures 3H and 3I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PPP2R2B-mediated inhibition of the mTOR signaling pathway contributes to the growth inhibition of PDAC in response to HOXC6 loss.

HOXC6 inhibition suppresses MSK1 expression and inhibits PDAC tumor growth

Our RNA-seq analysis revealed the downregulation of MSK1 in HOXC6 shRNA-expressing PANC1 cells (Table S2). Previous studies show that MSK1 overexpression is associated with tumor aggression and poor overall survival in patients with certain types of cancer,42 as well as PI3K/mTOR inhibitor resistance in glioblastoma.43 Therefore, we hypothesized that HOXC6-mediated upregulation of MSK1 might be required for PDAC development. Consistent with our RNA-seq findings, we found that HOXC6 shRNA-expressing cells showed decreased MSK1 mRNA and protein levels (Figures S6G and S6H). Furthermore, MSK1 protein was overexpressed in more than 75% of PDAC samples (Figures S6I and S6J). Similarly, the expression levels of MSK1 protein in PDAC a tissue microarray were positively correlated with HOXC6 expression (Figures 4A and 4B; Table S3). Furthermore, our ChIP experiments demonstrated that HOXC6 was directly associated with the MSK1 promoter (Figure S6D), which indicates that MSK1 is a direct target of HOXC6. Similarly, single-cell analysis of PDAC cells (PANC1 and AsPC1) with NS shRNA or HOXC6 shRNA showed a positive correlation between HOXC6 and MSK1 expression (Figure S6E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HOXC6 directly stimulates the expression of MSK1 in PDAC.

Next, we hypothesized that HOXC6-mediated MSK1 upregulation may be necessary for PDAC growth. Therefore, we used shRNA to knockdown MSK1 expression in PDAC cells (Figure S7A) and analyzed the effects on PDAC cell growth. MSK1 knockdown significantly inhibited PDAC cell growth in soft agar assays (Figures 4C and 4D). Additionally, we conducted experiments to establish MSK1’s role as a downstream mediator of HOXC6’s function. Therefore, we ectopically expressed MSK1 in HOXC6 shRNA-expressing PDAC cells (Figure S7B) and examined whether MSK1 expression could reverse the HOXC6 loss-induced PDAC growth inhibition. We discovered that ectopic MSK1 expression partially restored PDAC growth in soft agar (Figures 4E and 4F).

We hypothesized that, as a kinase, MSK1 exerts PDAC-promoting activity by phosphorylating its protein targets. However, to date, only a few downstream substrates have been identified.44,45,46 Additionally, the proteins that MSK1 targets to mediate its growth-promoting effects on PDAC are unknown. To identify the phosphorylation targets of MSK1 and the mediators of its function in PDAC cells, we utilized an unbiased TMT10-based phosphoproteomics analysis47 (Figure S7C). PDAC cells were treated with DMSO or the MSK1 inhibitor SB-747651A. SB-747651A is a potent ATP-competitive inhibitor that targets the N-terminal kinase domain of MSK1 and has a half maximal inhibitory concentration of 11 nM.48 We found that 1 μM SB-747651A inhibited the MSK1 kinase activity as effectively as the other, higher concentration tested (Figure S7D); therefore, this concentration was used for the TMT10-based phosphoproteomics analysis. The TMT10-based phosphoproteomics identified 39 and 40 distinct proteins in PANC1 and AsPC1 cells, respectively, whose phosphorylation was significantly reduced by SB-747651A treatment (Figure S7E; Tables S4 and S5). Notably, combining TMT10-based phosphoproteomics data from both PANC1 and AsPC1 cells revealed two common targets of MSK1 in PDAC cells: dead box helicase 17 (DDX17) and ribosomal protein L34 (RPL34) (Figure S7E). We first validated our phosphoproteomics results by measuring the phosphorylation status of DDX17 and RPL34 in both PANC1 and AsPC1 cells after SB-747651A treatment. After treatment with SB-747651A, the phosphorylation of DDX17 protein was significantly decreased (Figure 4G). Because of the unavailability of an appropriate antibody, we were unable to validate RPL34 as a target. Therefore, we concentrated our analyses on DDX17. RNA helicase DDX17 controls transcription, splicing, ribosomal RNA processing, and miRNA processing.49,50,51 Previous studies indicate that DDX17 promotes cancer.52,53 We initially investigated whether DDX17 knockdown (Figures S7F and S7G) phenocopies MSK1 loss. We found that, similar to MSK1 loss, DDX17 loss decreased colony formation in the soft agar assay (Figures S7H and S7I) and invasion in the Matrigel invasion assay (Figures S7J and S7K). Next, to directly test the role of DDX17 downstream of MSK1, we examined whether ectopic expression of DDX17 could prevent the loss of MSK1-driven phenotypes (Figure 4H). Our results showed that DDX17 ectopic expression partially rescued both the MSK1 loss-driven decrease in soft agar colony formation (Figures 4I and 4J) and the MSK1 loss-driven reduction in invasiveness (Figures 4K and 4L). In particular, we found that DDX17 ectopic expression partially prevented MSK1 loss-induced apoptosis (Figures 4M and 4N). Altogether, these findings demonstrate that DDX17 is an MSK1 target that suppresses apoptosis to mediate its PDAC tumor- and metastatic attribute-promoting effects.

Previous studies have shown a key role of DDX17 in transcription regulation.51,54,55,56 Therefore, we first performed an RNA-seq analysis of MSK1 knockdown PDAC cells. RNA-seq identified 319 upregulated and 357 downregulated genes that were commonly deregulated in both PANC1 and AsPC1 cell lines after MSK1 knockdown (Figure S8A and Table S6). We then asked whether MSK1 target genes were also regulated by DDX17. We first analyzed the expression of previously known TFs that are regulated by DDX17.51,54,55,56 We found that the TF YBX1, previously known to interact with DDX17,54 was significantly overexpressed in PDAC (Figure S8B). Therefore, we examined the interaction of DDX17 with YBX1 without or with SB-747651A treatment. MSK1 inhibition blocked DDX17 and YBX1 protein interaction (Figure S8C). Next, we determined whether knockdown of DDX17 or YBX1 affected the expression of MSK1 target genes. We tested a subset of MSK1 target genes identified from our RNA-seq analysis based on their known cancer-related functions (Figure S8D) and found that the expression of four (AFAPL1, BIRC3, FBOX32, and RAD51B) of 14 validated MSK1 target genes was reduced following knockdown of DDX17 and YBX1 (Figures S8E and S8F). To determine whether this effect was due to the direct binding of YBX1 and DDX17 on MSK1 target gene promoters, we performed ChIP assays. The ChIP assay results showed that SB-747651A reduced the binding of YBX1 and DDX17 to the promoters of MSK1 target genes (Figure S8G), consistent with their downregulation following YBX1 and DDX17 knockdown. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MSK1 promotes the interaction of YBX1 and DDX17, which in turn regulates a subset of MSK1 target genes that may in part explain the role of DDX17 as a downstream mediator of MSK1 in PDAC.

Pharmacological inhibition of MSK1 and the mTOR pathway impairs PDAC tumor growth and metastasis

Because HOXC6 stimulated the expression of MSK1 and increased mTOR pathway activity by suppressing PPP2R2B, we sought to determine whether pharmacological inhibition of MSK1 and the mTOR pathway could be used to inhibit PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. To ascertain this, we analyzed the effects of the MSK1 inhibitor SB-747651A and the mTOR inhibitor everolimus on PDAC tumor development. We found that SB-747651A inhibited PDAC cell growth in soft agar assays in a concentration-dependent manner (Figures S9A and S9B). Similarly, we observed that everolimus inhibited PDAC growth (Figures S9C and S9D). Next, we postulated that SB-747651A and mTOR inhibitors could be combined to achieve a more potent inhibitory effect on PDAC tumor growth because they inhibit PDAC via two distinct mechanisms. Consistent with this rationale, our results demonstrated that the combination of SB-747651A with everolimus inhibited PDAC growth in soft agar assays with greater potency than either drug alone (Figures S9E and S9F). Furthermore, the combination of SB-747651A with everolimus was more effective in suppressing PDAC cell invasion compared with a single drug alone (Figures S9G and S9H).

Next, using the orthotopic mouse model of PDAC tumor growth and spontaneous metastasis, we evaluated the efficacy of SB-747651A and mTOR inhibitors on PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. To accomplish this, we injected F-Luc-labeled PDAC cells orthotopically into the pancreas of NSG mice and treated them with either SB-747651A, everolimus, or a combination of both. These mouse studies demonstrated that SB-747651A and everolimus significantly inhibited PDAC tumor growth and metastasis (Figures 5A–5D and S10A–S10D). To further fortify these findings, we examined the efficacy of the combination of everolimus and SB-747651A in four distinct PDAC patient-derived organoids (PDOs) (PDM-38, PDM-179, PDM-24, and PDM-168) and two different PDAC patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) (TM01212 and TM01098). These PDOs and PDXs showed HOXC6 overexpression (Figures S10E and S10F). We also found that the combination of SB-747651A and everolimus significantly inhibited PDAC growth in both PDOs (Figures 5E and 5F) and PDXs (Figure 5G). Collectively, these studies identify HOXC6-mediated activation of MSK1 and mTOR pathways as pharmacological targets for treating PDAC.

To investigate the reason why this combination therapy provides a superior benefit, we measured apoptosis induction following either single drug or combination therapy (SB-747651A, everolimus, or both). We found that PDAC cells treated with both SB-747651A and everolimus showed significantly greater apoptosis induction than that observed with a single agent (Figure S10G). We also observed greater decreases in the anti-apoptotic protein MCL1 after combination therapy compared with that observed with a single drug alone (Figure S10H). mTOR has been previously shown to promote survival via translational control of MCL1.57 Thus, the increase in apoptosis, potentially via reduction in MCL1 levels, may in part contribute to the increased efficacy of combination therapy.

MSK1 and mTOR inhibitor-resistant PDAC cells acquire sensitivity to IGF1R inhibitor

Cancer cells can acquire resistance to numerous anti-cancer treatments, limiting their long-term benefits in clinic.58,59,60,61 Targeting resistant cells is crucial for preventing disease progression and recurrence, as well as for prolonging the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Therefore, to address the potential likelihood of the emergence of acquired resistance to combined everolimus and SB-747651A treatment, we treated PDAC cells for 4 weeks and isolated surviving cells (Figure 6A). These PDAC cells exhibited resistance to everolimus and SB-747651A treatment (Figure 6B). We then used the everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells to investigate changes in the phosphorylation of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) to identify the potential upregulation of one or more RTK signaling pathways.

Previous studies have demonstrated that RTK pathways play a causal role in drug resistance,62 and numerous drugs targeting RTKs have been developed over the years and are used clinically to treat a wide variety of cancers.63,64 Thus, we postulated that, if an RTK is identified as a driver of dual MSK1/mTOR inhibitor resistance in PDAC cells, it could potentially be used to target drug resistant PDACs. Using a human phospho-RTK array that can detect changes in the phosphorylation of 49 different human RTKs, parental PANC1 cells (PANC1-P) and PANC1 cells resistant to everolimus and SB-747651A (PANC1-R) were analyzed. PANC1-R cells exhibited elevated phosphorylation levels of IGF1R, insulin receptor (INSR), and EPH receptor A2 (EPHA2) (Figures 6C and 6D). We then compared the phosphorylation of IGF1R, INSR, and EPHA2 in everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells (PANC1-R and AsPC1-R) with that of parental PDAC cells (PANC1-P and AsPC1-P). Only IGF1R was consistently upregulated in PDAC cells resistant to everolimus and SB-747651A, compared with parental cells (Figures 6E and S10I–S10J). Next, we examined whether PDAC cells resistant to everolimus and SB-747651A were more sensitive to IGF1R inhibitors. To test this, we conducted a clonogenic assay to determine the impact of the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-754807 on the ability of parental or everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells to form colonies.

BMS-754807 is a potent and reversible IGF-1R inhibitor and is currently in phase II of a clinical trial for the treatment of estrogen-dependent breast cancer with hormonal therapy.65,66 Compared with parental PDAC cells, everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells were more sensitive to BMS-754807 (Figure 6F). Next, we tested the impact of BMS-754807 treatment on parental or everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells in mice, using the orthotopic model of PDAC tumor growth and spontaneous metastasis. F-Luc-labeled AsPC1-P or AsPC1-R cells were injected into the pancreas of mice, which were then treated with vehicle or BMS-754807. We found that treatment of AsPC1-R orthotopic tumors with BMS-754807 inhibited their growth and ability to metastasize to the lungs or liver, whereas AsPC1-P orthotopic tumor growth and metastatic progress were largely unaffected (Figures 6G–6K). Altogether, these findings identify a strategy to impede tumor growth and metastasis of everolimus- and SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells by inhibiting IGF1R, thus providing an approach for extending the duration of therapeutic efficacy in PDAC patients.

MSK1 and mTOR inhibitors in combination with the MEK inhibitor trametinib more potently inhibit PDAC tumor growth and metastasis

Based on these results, we speculated that it was possible to further enhance the therapeutic benefit of MSK1 and mTOR inhibitors in PDAC. Because the MAP kinase pathway is activated in PDAC cells due to KRAS mutations, we determined whether the MEK inhibitor trametinib can be combined with MSK1 and mTOR inhibitors to achieve a superior therapeutic effect. To test this, we used three PDAC model systems, PDOs, an orthotopic xenograft-based mouse model, and a PDX model, which were treated with the MEK inhibitor trametinib alone, a dual combination of SB-747651A and everolimus, or a triple combination of SB-747651A, everolimus and trametinib. We found that the triple combination therapy more potently inhibited the growth of PDAC PDOs (Figures 7A and 7B). Furthermore, in the orthotopic model of PDAC tumor growth and metastasis, the triple combination therapy was significantly more effective than either trametinib alone or dual combination of SB-747651A and everolimus therapy (Figures 7C–7F). Finally, we tested these drugs and combinations in a KRAS-driven PDAC PDX model and found that the triple combination therapy was significantly more effective than either trametinib alone or SB-747651A and everolimus combination therapy (Figure 7G). These results demonstrate that the addition of trametinib further enhances the efficacy of SB-747651A and everolimus combination therapy.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of TFs drives tumor growth and metastasis in various cancer types, including PDAC.5,6,7,8,9,10,11,67,68,69,70,71 HOXC6 has also been shown to be overexpressed in some cancer types and promotes their growth.72,73,74 Similarly, HOXC6 overexpression is identified as an early biomarker for detecting some cancers.75,76 However, the role of HOXC6 in PDAC tumor growth and metastasis has not been previously documented. Here, we demonstrated that HOXC6 is overexpressed in the vast majority of PDACs and is required for tumor growth and metastasis (Figure 7H).

More than 90% of PDAC tumors harbor mutations in the KRAS gene, leading to its constitutive activation.27 This constitutive oncogenic activation of KRAS stimulates the expression of multiple oncogenic pathways, most notably the MAPK and PI3K pathways.77 We found that the PI3K/AKT pathway was responsible for the upregulation of HOXC6 transcription in PDACs. These findings indicate that HOXC6 is a crucial TF that mediates some of the oncogenic effects of oncogenic KRAS in PDAC via the PI3K/AKT pathway. Our study also identified that the TF CEBPB is regulated through the PI3K/AKT pathway and was necessary for HOXC6 expression.

Our mechanistic analyses revealed that HOXC6’s ability to activate the mTOR pathway by repressing PPP2R2B expression in PDAC cells contributed in part to its growth-promoting effect on PDAC cells. The mTOR pathway functions as a metabolic, cell proliferation, and survival regulator, and its activity is elevated in several cancer types, which in turn is essential for tumor growth and progression.78,79 mTOR pathway activation is also observed in PDAC.27 Notably, the mTOR pathway can be pharmacologically targeted, and several mTOR inhibitors have been evaluated in clinical trials to treat various cancer types,80,81 supporting the driver role of this pathway in cancer. PPP2R2B encodes the brain-specific regulatory subunit B of PP2A, a heterotrimeric serine/threonine phosphatase implicated in numerous regulatory processes, including cell growth and division, as well as transcription regulation. PP2A comprises a 36-kD catalytic subunit, a highly homologous 65-kD structural subunit, and numerous regulatory subunits, including PPP2R2B, which regulates its specificity.35 Previous studies have shown that PPP2R2B is epigenetically silenced by DNA hypermethylation in colorectal cancer, and its loss leads to compensatory MYC phosphorylation in a PDK1-dependent manner, resulting in rapamycin resistance.38 Additionally, decreased PPP2R2B expression is associated with AKT/mTOR activation, thereby promoting cancer cell survival and proliferation.39 PP2A also negatively regulates AKT activity, primarily by preventing the phosphorylation of Thr-308.82 Furthermore, similar to our findings, a previous study of HER2+ breast cancer showed possible regulation of the mTOR pathway via PPP2RB.83 We show that HOXC6 knockdown in PDAC decreased mTOR pathway activity, which was directly related to HOXC6’s ability to regulate PPP2R2B expression. Additionally, we demonstrated that the PPP2R2B→mTOR axis regulates HOXC6-mediated PDAC growth.

MSK1 was identified as a key downstream mediator of HOXC6 in PDAC. MSK1 is an overexpressed serine/threonine kinase in colorectal cancer, especially in patients with stage III colorectal cancer.42 Its overexpression is also associated with a poor prognosis for patients and tumor aggressiveness.42 Glioma patients with a higher MSK1 expression are also more susceptible to adverse clinical outcomes and prognoses.84 Few MSK1 targets have been identified, most of which are known transcriptional regulators.85,86,87,88,89 We discovered that HOXC6 is required for the expression of MSK1 in PDAC cells. MSK1 was required for the HOXC6-driven growth and metastasis of PDAC tumors. Furthermore, we identified DDX17 as an MSK1 phosphorylation target. DDX17 is a member of the DEAD box (asp-glu-ala-asp/his) protein family of RNA helicases that are involved in diverse cellular functions.53,90,91,92 Additionally, previous studies have implicated DDX17 in cancer.52,53,93 Here, we demonstrated that DDX17 is an MSK1 target and a mediator of its downstream action in PDAC, and functions by suppressing apoptosis. Based on our results, we speculate that YBX1/DDX17-mediated transcription regulation of MSK1 target genes might be necessary for the observed phenotypes downstream of MSK1. Consequently, these studies identify MSK1→DDX17 as an axis that contributes significantly to the growth of PDAC downstream of HOXC6. As both the mTOR pathway and MSK1 can be pharmacologically inhibited, our findings present a therapeutic opportunity for PDAC treatment. According to our study, the combination of an MSK1 inhibitor and an mTOR inhibitor inhibited PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. We would also like to note that, because we observed suppression of primary tumor growth following HOXC6 knockdown in our mouse studies, it is possible that suppression of metastasis could be partly attributed to the suppression of primary tumor growth.

In cancer, the emergence of resistance to therapeutic agents is a common phenomenon. We also modeled acquired resistance to dual MSK1 and mTOR pathway inhibition in PDAC to lengthen the duration of therapeutic outcomes using our approach. Our findings identified IGF1R as a therapeutic target for PDAC cells resistant to MSK1 and mTOR inhibitors. IGF1R has been shown to confer resistance to other targeted therapeutic agents in other types of cancer. In addition, clinical-grade inhibitors, such as BMS-754807 used in our study, are available, and some are being used in clinical trials, for the treatment of a range of naive and drug-resistant cancers.65,66 Consequently, our findings emphasize the significance of RTKs, such as IGF1R, as therapeutic agents for the treatment of drug-resistant cancers. Our findings provide primary and secondary treatment strategies for the long-term suppression of PDAC, thereby expanding treatment options for patients with PDAC. Furthermore, combination treatment with the MEK inhibitor trametinib, an MSK1 inhibitor, and an mTOR inhibitor was able to further enhance the suppression of PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. These results have important implications, because this treatment might delay the emergence of drug resistance to MSK1/mTOR inhibitors. We suggest that similar experiments can be performed in the future with approved and investigational PDAC therapies to achieve better outcomes. For example, a future study combining MSK1/mTOR inhibitors with adaptive immune checkpoint blockade therapies may yield better results due to targeting of both cell-extrinsic and cell-intrinsic pathways. Furthermore, it is important to note that the clinical response in human subjects is a complex issue and depends on the type of cancer and therapy.94 A definitive conclusion can only be empirically determined in a controlled clinical trial in PDAC patients. Collectively, our results uncover HOXC6 and its downstream targets as facilitators of PDAC tumor growth and metastasis that are pharmacologically amenable to PDAC therapy.

Limitations of the study

Our study utilizes an orthotopic model of PDAC tumor growth and spontaneous metastasis. However, in this model, we have not fully dissected the role of HOXC6 on metastatic progression because of its primary effect on PDAC tumor growth as well. Therefore, it will be important to perform experiments to precisely determine the impact of HOXC6 independent of its effect on primary tumor growth. Additionally, we focused on MSK1 and PPP2R2B as primary HOXC6 targets by which it mediates its tumor- and metastasis-promoting functions. However, additional HOXC6 targets were identified and may be tested for assessing their relative role in driving PDAC tumor growth and metastasis. In particular, it was not tested if different HOXC6 targets may mediate primary tumor promoting versus metastasis promoting functions of HOXC6 and would be worth testing in future studies.

Experimental model and subject details

Cell culture

PANC1, AsPC1, MIAPaCa2, SU.86.86, HPNE-hTERT and 293T cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 Medium (Life Technologies), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Life Technologies) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies). Information regarding cell culture reagents is listed in the key resources table.

PDAC organoid culture experiments with inhibitor treatments

The PDAC organoids HCM-CSHL-0091-C25 (ATCC; PDM-38), HCM-BROD-0232-C25 (ATCC; PDM-179), HCM-CSHL-0073-C25 (ATCC; PDM-24), and HCM-BROD-0229-C25 (ATCC; PDM-168) were acquired from ATCC and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The organoids were maintained in complete growth medium in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 37°C. The detailed composition of the complete organoid growth medium is provided in Table S7. Organoids were dissociated into single-cell suspensions for experiments involving inhibitors using TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were then resuspended in ice-cold Geltrex Matrigel (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10-μL drops of the Matrigel/cell suspension (3,000 cells per drop) were seeded into 48-well plates. The Matrigel/cell suspension was then solidified in the cell culture incubator for 20 min by inverting the plates. Then, 250 μL of complete organoid growth medium containing 10 μM of the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was added to each well. Three days after the initial medium change, inhibitor treatments were initiated. For 2 weeks, the medium containing the vehicle or inhibitors was changed every 3 days. Subsequently, using light microscopy, brightfield images were captured at the conclusion of the experiment. CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay Kit was utilized to quantify cell viability (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The conditions of treatment are described in the figure legends.

Mouse tumorigenesis experiments

Mouse tumorigenesis experiments with shRNA-expressing cells

Immunocompromised mice were subcutaneously injected in the flank with 5 × 106 PANC1 and AsPC1 cells expressing different HOXC6 shRNAs or control NS shRNA. Tumor volume was measured every week. The size of the tumor was determined using the following formula: length × width2 × 0.5. The protocol for these mouse experiments was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yale University.

Tail vein injection of shRNA-expressing cells for lung metastasis experiments

As previously described, PANC1 or AsPC1 cells expressing firefly luciferase under the control of the cytomegalovirus promoter were generated by cotransfection with the PiggyBac GFP-F-Luc transposon vector and Act-PBase helper plasmid.95 Next, using blasticidin S, cells with stable transposon integration were selected (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, PANC1-F-Luc or AsPC1-F-Luc cells (5 × 104) expressing NS or HOXC6 shRNAs were injected intravenously into NSG mice (stock no. 005557; The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). The IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System was used to capture photographs of mice (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The total luminescence of tumor-bearing areas was measured using the Living Image in vivo imaging software (PerkinElmer). The protocol for these mouse experiments has been approved by the IACUC of Yale University.

Orthotopic pancreatic injection of shRNA-expressing PDAC cells

The NSG mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. The spleen and underlying pancreas of each mouse were located through an intra-abdominal incision. The tail of the pancreas was injected with a Matrigel suspension (40 μL) containing PANC1-F-Luc or AsPC1-F-Luc cells (5 × 104) expressing NS shRNA or HOXC6 shRNAs. The abdominal wall and skin were subsequently sutured shut. Injections of D-luciferin into mice were used to perform imaging weekly until the completion of the experiment. The total luminescence of the tumor-bearing regions was measured using Living Image in vivo imaging software. Method details

Plasmids

The V5-tagged MSK1 and V5-tagged DDX17 lentiviral expression constructs in the pLX304-Blast-V5 plasmid purchased from Horizon Discovery (Waterbeach, UK), and PIK3CA retroviral expression construct in the pBabe puro plasmid was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA) are listed in the key resources table.

Lentivirus and shRNA preparation

Horizon Discovery supplied the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector-based shRNAs for specific target genes and nonspecific (NS) control shRNA. The shRNAs are described in the key resources table. As described in detail at https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gpp/public/resources/protocols, lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting 293T cells with gene-specific or NS shRNA plasmids and lentiviral packaging plasmids. Effectene Transfection Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was utilized for every lentiviral transduction. PDAC cells were infected with shRNA lentiviral particles in 12-well plates to generate stable cell lines. For cell selection, appropriate concentrations of puromycin (0.5–1.5 μg/mL) were used. For the pLX304-Blast-V5-based lentivirus, 2 μg/mL blasticidin was used to select successfully transduced PDAC cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA preparation, complementary DNA (cDNA) preparation, reverse transcription (RT), and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and purified with an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cDNA was synthesized using the M-MuLV First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Then, qPCR was performed using gene-specific primers and the Power SYBR-Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), per the manufacturer’s instructions. The beta-actin (ACTB) gene served as a control for normalization. The primer sequences for all genes analyzed in the study are provided in the key resources table.

Immunoblot analysis

Using IP Lysis Buffer (Pierce Chemical, Rockford, IL, USA) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail whole-cell protein extracts were prepared (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The lysed samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 40 min, and the clarified supernatants were stored at ˗80°C. Protein concentrations were measured using Bradford Protein Assay Reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Subsequently, using a wet-transfer apparatus, equal amounts of protein samples were electrophoresed on 10% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gels and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) containing 5% skimmed milk and then probed with primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (1:2,000; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). The blots were developed using SuperSignal West Pico or Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All antibodies used for the immunoblot analyses are listed in the key resources table.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from PANC1 cells stably expressing one of two different shRNAs targeting HOXC6 or a control nonspecific (NS) shRNA were isolated for gene expression analysis using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system (San Diego, CA, USA). For MSK1 knockdown RNA-seq, total RNA was isolated from PANC1 and AsPC1 cells stably expressing one of two different shRNAs targeting MSK1 or a control nonspecific (NS) shRNA were isolated for gene expression analysis using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system (San Diego, CA, USA). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and purified using RNAeasy mini columns (Qiagen). The mRNA was purified using oligo-dT beads from approximately 500 ng total RNA and then sheared by incubation at 94°C. In order to generate strand-specific sequencing libraries, the first strand was synthesized with random primers, followed by the second strand with dUTP. The cDNA library was subjected to end repair and poly(A) tailing. Adapters were then ligated, and uracil-DNA-glycosidase was used to digest the second strand. Using a commercially available RT-qPCR kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA), indexed libraries that met appropriate thresholds for both were quantified. LabChip GX Nucleic Acid Analyzer (PerkinElmer) or an Agilent Bioanalyzer was used to determine the insert size distribution (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). On the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system, samples with a yield of ≥0.5 ng/μL were sequenced.

Sample concentrations were normalized to 3 nM were loaded into the Illumina High-Output Flow Cell to generate 500–550 million reads per flow cell. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, samples were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 using 150-bp paired-end sequencing. After completing the first read, the 6-bp index was automatically determined using a second read. Data generated during sequencing runs were simultaneously transferred to the Cheaha high-performance computing cluster at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB). During runs, the Real-Time Analysis (RTA) software of the system converted signal intensities to individual base calls. The Bcl2Fastq software was used to convert base call log files (.bcl) files to FASTQ files on the UAB Cheaha computing cluster.

The RTA 1.18.64.0 base-calling pipeline converted sequencer-generated images into nucleotide sequences that were stored in the FASTQ format. The FASTQ files containing raw sequencing data were subjected to a quality check (FastQC), removal of adapter content, and quality thresholding (removal of reads with Phred scores <30). Reads that passed the quality thresholds were mapped to the latest stable version of human reference genome hg38 (GRCh38.p12, Ensembl, Bowtie2 and Tophat 2.1.1). Subsequently, using Cufflinks 2.2.1, the of the assembled transcriptomes was estimated (6). Briefly, the transcriptome assembly quality was evaluated, and gene and transcript expression profiles were calculated for each sample. The standard fragments per kilobase per million fragments (FPKM) method was used for normalization, followed by log2 transformation. Next, differential gene expression between conditions was estimated using the log2-transformed FPKM values of transcripts sharing each gene’s ID. For differentially expressed genes, the uncorrected p value of the test statistic and the false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p value of the test statistic (q-value) were estimated. After applying the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing, any gene with a p value greater than the FDR was deemed to be differentially expressed between the test and control conditions. The data were submitted to Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE146944 for HOXC6 RNA-seq, and GSE150222 and 150223 for MSK1 RNA-seq).

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue microarray slides containing PDAC, and matched normal pancreatic tissues were obtained from US Biomax (Cat. No. HPan-Ade150CS-02 and PA601; Derwood, MD, USA). Briefly, following deparaffinization of the slides, antigen retrieval was conducted in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 97°C for 20 min using the Lab Vision PT Module (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The slides were then washed with 1 × Tris-buffered saline and incubated with hydrogen peroxide for 30 min to inhibit the activity of endogenous peroxides. The slides were then incubated with 0.3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min to prevent nonspecific binding, followed by incubation with an anti-HOXC6 antibody (1:500 dilution) or an anti-MSK1 antibody (1:40 dilution) and an anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Dako, Jena, Germany). Afterward, the slides were stained with the Dako Liquid DAB+ Substrate Chromogen System and counterstained with Dako Automation Hematoxylin Histological Staining Reagent. HOXC6 staining was scored by Dr. Guoping Cai in a blinded experiment, which concealed the identity of each slide. All antibodies used for immunohistochemistry analyses are listed in key resources table.

Soft agar assay

Soft agar cell growth is a surrogate for tumorigenicity and can be used to predict the role of a gene in cancer.32 Soft agar assays were carried out by seeding 5 × 103–2 × 104 PDAC cells, which stably expressed the indicated shRNA or cDNA constructs onto a 0.4% low-melting-point agarose gel (Sigma-Aldrich), which was layered on top of a 0.8% agarose gel. After 3–4 weeks of incubation, colonies were stained with 0.005% crystal violet and photographed with a brightfield microscope. Colony sizes were measured using ImageJ software (NIH; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) and plotted.

Matrigel invasion assay

PDAC cells expressing the indicated shRNAs were used for invasion assays in BioCoat Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel Invasion Chambers (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Cells were serum starved for 6 h, and then 5 × 104 cells/insert were seeded in triplicate into the top chambers containing low-serum medium. These cells were incubated for 20 h to allow invasion toward the serum-rich medium in the bottom wells and DAPI staining and imaging were used to detect the invading cells. Nuclei quantification was performed using ImageJ software (NIH; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) and plotted.

PDAC datasets for gene expression analysis

The Oncomine cancer profiling database was queried to identify datasets that compare gene expression in pancreatic cancer and normal pancreatic tissues. The Badea, Grutzmann, Ishikawa, Pei, and Jimeno datasets were used for data analysis.18,19,20,21,29 The Badea dataset included paired samples of PDAC (n = 39) and normal pancreas tissue (n = 39) from 36 patients, three of which were duplicates. Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array analysis was conducted on the samples. The Grutzmann dataset consisted of 11 PDAC samples and 11 normal pancreatic duct samples analyzed on Affymetrix U133 A/B microarrays with cancer type, grade, and TNM stage metadata, as well as patient age and sex. The Ishikawa dataset comprised 24 PDAC samples and 25 normal pancreatic duct samples analyzed on Affymetrix U133 A/B microarrays with metadata, such as cancer type, patient age and sex, atypical cell proportion, clinical stage, and cytological grade. The Pei dataset included 36 pancreatic carcinoma samples and 16 paired, normal samples analyzed on a Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array with patient age and sex as metadata. Jimeno dataset included 24 pAKT1 IHC negative and 12 pAKT1 IHC positive samples an used in our analysis. In addition to cetuximab and erlotinib sensitivity, sample data included EGFR, KRAS, and PI3KCA mutation status. Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) was used to analyze the expression of HOXC6 in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset at http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn. 179 PDAC tumors and 171 normal samples were analyzed for HOXC6 (Match TCGA + GTEx data). HOXC6 mRNA expression was also analyzed in various PDACs datasets to determine if its expression is significantly enriched in a specific PDAC transcriptional subtypes.24,25,26,27

Protein synthesis assay

Using the Protein Synthesis Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), a non-radioactive method employing the cell-permeable, alkyne puromycin analog O-propargyl-puromycin, protein synthesis was measured (OPP). When applied to cells, OPP probes are incorporated into the C-termini of polypeptide chains undergoing translation, halting translation. These truncated C-terminal alkyne-labeled proteins are subsequently detected by 5-FAM-azide-catalyzed copper-catalyzed click chemistry. In order to perform the assay, cells were seeded into 96-well, black, clear-bottom plates at a density of 1 × 104–5 × 104 cells/mL. The cells were either treated with a vehicle or with inhibitors. Kit-supplied cycloheximide was diluted 1:1,000 in a culture medium and used as a negative control. Cells were incubated in cycloheximide for 30 min at 37°C. The plates were then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was aspirated with care, and 100 μL of an OPP working solution containing the inhibitors was added and mixed into the wells. The plates were then incubated for 30 min at 37°C and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was aspirated carefully, and 100 L of the fixative for cell-based assays from the kit was added to each well and mixed.

The plates were then incubated at room temperature for 5 min and again centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Again, the supernatant was aspirated carefully, and 100 L of the kit’s cell-based assay wash buffer was added to each well and mixed. The cells were then incubated for 5 min at room temperature before being centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was aspirated with care, and the washing process was repeated twice more. After adding 5-FAM azide staining solution (100 μL) to each well, the plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The plates were then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated. After adding and mixing 100 μL of cell-based assay wash buffer to each well, the cells were incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The plate was then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated. The washing process was repeated twice more. Finally, 100 μL of assay buffer (1×) was added to each well, and the cells were immediately examined using a filter designed to detect FITC (excitation/emission = 485/535 nm).

TMT10-based phosphoproteomics analysis using SB-747651A

Treatment with SB-747651A

PANC1 and AsPC1 cells were plated in 100-mm dishes and treated with SB-747651A. At approximately 80% confluence, cells were treated with 1 μM SB-747651A for 24 h. Untreated and treated cells were collected using a scraper and stored at −80°C for phosphoproteomics analysis. The phoshoproteomics data was submitted to PRIDE database (Accession #PXD041238 for PANC1 and Accession # PXD041239 for AsPC1 cells).

Sample preparation

Cell pellets were dissolved in DF Covaris buffer, and protein was extracted for 120 s at 10% power using a Covaris S220 focused ultrasound device (Woburn, MA, USA). Proteins that were extracted were precipitated in ice-cold methanol/chloroform and then air-dried. For reduction and alkylation, samples were resolubilized in a 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer and then digested for 5 h in a buffer containing 10 μg trypsin (Promega). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, samples of digested peptides were desalted using Zeba Micro Spin Desalting Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and enriched using the High-Select TiO2 Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). TMT11plex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to label enriched phosphopeptides according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled samples were fractionated before mass spectrometry analysis.

Hi-pH separation and mass spectrometry analysis

Before being submitted to LC-MS/MS, each sample was separated into 20 fractions using a Hi-pH column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each fraction was subjected to a single LC-MS/MS experiment on a Lumos Tribrid (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a 3000 Ultima Dual nano HPLC pump (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated using a microcapillary trapping column with a 150-μm inner diameter packed with approximately 3 cm of C18 Reprosil resin (5 μm, 100 Å, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany) and then a PharmaFluidics (Belgium) 50 cm Micro-PAK analytical column. The separation was achieved by applying a 90-min, 200-nL/min gradient of 5%–27% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. Electrospray ionization was accomplished by applying a voltage of 1.8 kV to a homemade electrode junction at the end of a microcapillary column sprayed with stainless steel tips (PepSep, Denmark). For mass spectrometry, the Lumos Orbitrap was operated in a data-dependent mode. The mass spectrometry survey scan was performed in the Orbitrap in the range of 400–1,800 m/z at a resolution of 6 × 104. The 20 most intense ions (TOP20) were chosen for collision-induced dissociation (CID)-MS2 fragmentation in the ion trap using a precursor isolation width window of 2 m/z, an automatic gain control (AGC) setting of 10,000, and a maximum ion accumulation time of 50 ms. Singly charged ion species were not fragmented via CID. With an activation time of 10 ms, the normalized collision energy was set to 35 V. Ions within a 10-pm m/z window surrounding ions chosen for MS2 were excluded from further selection by fragmentation for 90 s. The same TOP20 ions were fragmented by higher-energy-collisional-dissociation (HCD)-MS2 in Orbitrap with a fragment ion isolation width of 0.8 m/z, an AGC setting of 50,000, and a maximum ion accumulation time of 150 ms. For each HCD-MS2 scan, the normalized collision energy was set to 38 V with an activation time of 1 ms.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Raw data were submitted for mass spectrometry analysis in Proteome Discoverer 2. 4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Byonic 3.5 and ptmRS nodes. MS/MS spectra were assigned using the Sequest HT algorithm by querying the data against a protein sequence database containing all entries from the Human Uniprot database (SwissProt 19,768 2019) and other known contaminants, such as human keratins. Sequest HT queries were conducted with a 10-ppm precursor ion tolerance and required each peptide’s N/C-termini to specifically bind with trypsin protease, allowing for up to two missed cleavages. Eleven-plex TMT tags on peptide N termini and lysine residues (+229.163 Da) were set as static modifications, while methionine oxidation (+15.99492 Da) was set as a variable modification. Subsequently, using a target-decoy database search, a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was achieved at the protein level for the assignment of MS2 spectra. The data was filtered using a Percolator (64-bit version).96 For quantification, a 0.02 m/z window was centered on the theoretical m/z value of each of the six reporter ions, and the closest signal intensity was recorded. Reporter ion intensities were exported as Microsoft Excel tables from the Proteome Discoverer 2.4 search engine. Using ptmRS, the precise location of the phospho-moiety was determined and analyzed using the ptmRS program. To perform statistical analyses and identify differentially expressed proteins between sample groups, in-house R scripts based on Bioconductor packages (https://www.bioconductor.org/) were developed and used.

Generation of mTOR inhibitor everolimus/MSK1 inhibitor SB-747651A-resistant PDAC cells

PDAC cells resistant to the mTOR inhibitor everolimus/MSK1 inhibitor SB-747651A were derived from PANC1 or AsPC1 cells by 4 weeks of continuous culture in the presence of 100 nM everolimus and 10 μM SB-747651A. Until colonies formed, the medium was replaced every 3 days and supplemented with fresh everolimus and SB-747651A. In order to isolate resistant cells, individual colonies were trypsinized, expanded, and subjected to clonogenic assay in the presence of everolimus and SB-747651A to confirm their resistant phenotype (PANC1-R or AsPC1-R) relative to their parental counterparts (PANC1-P or AsPC1-P).

Clonogenic assay

Under DMSO or inhibitor-treated conditions, the clonogenic potential of PDAC cells (PANC1-P/PANC1-R or AsPC1-P/AsPC1-R) was assessed. In 6-well culture plates, cells were seeded for these tests at 5 × 103 cells/well. In the presence of inhibitors, cells were treated for drug treatment experiments. The medium containing inhibitors was changed every 3 days. After 2 weeks of treatment, surviving colonies were stained with a solution of 40% methanol, 10% acetic acid, and 0.005% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (Sigma-Aldrich), and the plates were scanned with an Epson Perfection V850 Pro Photo Scanner (USA).

Human phospho-RTK array analysis

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-RTK Array Kit (R&D Systems) was used to determine the relative levels of tyrosine phosphorylation for 49 distinct RTKs. PANC1-P/PANC1-R or AsPC1-P/AsPC1-R cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Invitrogen) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors [protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich)]. After blocking for 1 h with Array Buffer 1 (R&D Systems), the arrays were incubated overnight at 4°C with 200 μg of protein lysate. Arrays were washed with wash buffer (R&D Systems) and incubated for 1 h with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated phosphotyrosine detection antibody (1:5000). Using the SuperSignal West Pico Reagent (Pierce), arrays were created to detect phosphorylation changes. ImageJ software was used to measure signal intensities. Positive controls on the membranes were used to standardize and compare the results of various membranes.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The promoter sequences for the genes were downloaded from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) genome browser and analyzed using PROMO 3.030 and rVista 2.031 software. ChIP experiments were performed as described previously.97 Cell lysates were incubated with specific antibodies (IgG, CEBPB, HOXC6, YBX1 or DDX17) as required (details listed in key resources table), with the IgG antibody used as a control. Normalized cycle threshold (ΔCt) values were calculated by subtracting the Ct of input DNA from that of immunoprecipitated DNA (ΔCt = Ct[IP] − Ct[input]). The relative fold-enrichment of a transcription factor at its target site was calculated using the formula 2 − (ΔCt(T) − ΔCt(Actb)), where ΔCt(T) and ΔCt(Actb) are the ΔCt values of the target and ACTINB (negative control promoter) primers, respectively.

Annexin V staining assay using flow cytometry

The apoptosis rates of shRNA- or open reading frame (ORF)-expressing or inhibitor-treated PDAC cells as indicated in respective figure legends were determined using an annexin V staining kit (BD Pharmingen #559763) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 3 × 105 cells/well were seeded in six-well cell culture plates (cell culture plates with ultra-low attachment were used for shRNA- or ORF-expressing PDAC cells). After the incubation period, the cells were collected, washed twice with 1× PBS, resuspended in 1× binding buffer, stained with 5 μL FITC-annexin V and 5 μL of PI, and incubated for 15 min in the dark. Next, 400 μL of 1× binding buffer was added, and the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using an LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Ashland).

Co-immunoprecipitation

PDAC cells (3 × 106) were plated in 100 mm cell culture dishes. After 24 h, cells were treated with inhibitor for further 48 h. Next, cells were lysed using ice-cold IP lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA; Cat# 87788) containing protease inhibitor (Roche Applied Science, USA; Cat# 11836170001). The lysed samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 40 min, and clarified supernatants were stored at −80°C. Protein concentrations were determined using Bradford Protein Assay Reagent (Bio-Rad, CA, USA; Cat# 5000006). The supernatants containing 25 μg of protein were used as the co-immunoprecipitation input. For co-immunoprecipitation, equal amounts of protein (600 μg) was aliquoted, and lysates were pre-cleared using 50 μL recombinant Protein G Agarose (Invitrogen, USA; Cat# 15920–010) for 30 min. Agarose beads were washed with ice-cold PBS, and pelleted. The supernatants were incubated with a protein-specific antibody or a control IgG (2 μg antibody per sample) overnight at 4°C. Then, 50 μL of protein A/G-plus agarose was added and shaken gently for 4 h at 4°C. Antigen-antibody complexes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were washed twice with IP lysis buffer. The target and its associated proteins were separated by boiling the sample in 2× protein-loading buffer for 10 min. Samples were electrophoresed on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, MA, USA; Cat# 88518). Membranes were blocked and probed with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with TidyBlot Western Blot Detection Reagent: HRP (1:200) (Bio-Rad, USA; Cat# STAR209P), and blots were developed using luminescence detection reagents (Thermo Scientific, USA; Cat# 34080 or Cat# 34096). All antibodies used for co-immunoprecipitation are listed in key resources table.

EdU incorporation assay using flow cytometry

EdU incorporation assay in PDAC cells was performed using Click-iT Plus EdU Flow Cytometry Kit (Invitrogen; Cat# C10635). Briefly, PDAC cells (1 × 103/well for PANC1 and 3 × 103/well for AsPC1) expressing the indicated shRNAs were plated in a 6-well plate. After 48 h, 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU; 10 μM), a thymidine analog which is incorporated during DNA replication, was added to the cells and incubated in a humidified CO2 incubator at 37°C for 2 h. Next, cells were trypsinized and washed with 3 mL of PBS containing 1% BSA and then were fixed with 100 μL of Click-iT fixative (Component D). After 15 min of incubation at room temperature in dark, the cells were washed with 3 mL of PBS containing 1% BSA. Cells were then permeabilized using 100μL of 1X Click-iT saponin-based permeabilization and wash reagent (Component E) for 15 min. Thereafter, the cells were incubated with 500μlL of Click-iT Plus reaction cocktail containing Alexa Fluor 647 picolyl azide for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. The cells were then washed with 3mL of 1X Click-iT saponin-based permeabilization and wash reagent. Further, cells were resuspended and incubated in 1X Click-iT saponin-based permeabilization and wash reagent (containing RNase A and propidium iodide) for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. Thereafter, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry using LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Ashland).

MSK1 kinase activity assay

MSK1 kinase activity was determined using the MSK1 Kinase Enzyme System kit (Promega# V5093) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the kinase reaction was performed in the presence or absence of different concentrations of MSK1 inhibitor SB-747651A for 1 h. The kinase reaction was terminated by adding ADP-Glo Reagent (incubation time: 40 min at room temperature). Finally, Kinase Detection Reagent was added (incubation time: 1 h at room temperature) and the luminescence was measured with the Biotek Synergy MX Multi Format Microplate Reader (Biotek).

Chemical inhibitors

Trametinib, buparlisib (BKM120), everolimus, and IGF1R inhibitor (BMS-754807) were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). SB-747651A, an MSK1 inhibitor, was acquired from Tocris (Bristol, UK). The information about inhibitors is listed in the key resources table. The conditions of treatment are described in the figure legends.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment included at least three biological replicates for RT-qPCR and for ChIP studies. For other experiments the number of replicates is indicated in the figures and/or figure legends. Individual experiment data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Next, using GraphPad Prism for Macintosh, version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA; www.graphpad.com), the area under the curve was analyzed for statistical analyses of tumor progression measurements in mice. For other experiments, GraphPad Prism was used to calculate p values using two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-tests. In order to analyze the correlation between HOXC6 and MSK1 protein expression levels based on IHC data, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using GraphPad Prism software.
