A determinantal formula for cluster variables in cluster algebras from surfaces
Javier De Loera

Abstract
For cluster algebras of surface type, Musiker, Schiffler and Williams gave a formula for cluster variables in terms of perfect matchings of snake graphs. Building on this, we provide a simple determinantal formula for cluster variables via the weighted biadjacency matrix of the associated snake graphs, thus circumventing the enumeration of their perfect matchings.



1
Introduction

Cluster algebras are a broad class of commutative algebras introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in
[
5
]
as a framework for the study of total positivity. They are recursively defined from an initial set of data by a combinatorial process called mutation. In
[
4
]
, Fomin, Shapiro and Thurston introduced and studied a class of cluster algebras whose combinatorics are governed by flips of (tagged) triangulations in surfaces with marked points. In
[
8
]
, Musiker, Schiffler and Williams gave a cluster expansion formula for cluster variables in these cluster algebras in terms of the perfect matchings of certain planar graphs called
snake graphs
, see Theorem
3.1
. These graphs were studied further in
[
2
]
, where a surprising connection to continued fractions was developed. In this paper, we apply a classical idea in matching theory to provide a reformulation of Musiker’s, Schiffler’s and Williams’ cluster expansion formula into a determinantal formula. This provides a way to circumvent the enumeration of the perfect matchings of the relevant snake graph.

We begin by briefly recalling the definition of cluster algebras (with trivial coefficients) obtained from surfaces, following
[
4
]
. Let
\Sigma
be a Riemann surface with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, and let
\mathbb{M}\subset\Sigma
be a non empty finite set of
marked points
such that
\mathbb{M}\cap C\neq\emptyset
for each connected component
C
of
\partial\Sigma
. We will call the pair
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
a
bordered surface
. Call the marked points in
\partial\Sigma

ideal points
, and those in the interior of
\Sigma
are
punctures
. A
boundary arc
of
\Sigma
is a curve contained in
\delta\Sigma
with endpoints in
M
.
An
arc
in
\Sigma
is a curve
\gamma
with ideal points as endpoints, no self intersections, and not homotopic to a boundary arc or to a point; similarly, a
All such curves are considered up to homotopy.
Two arcs are
compatible
if they do not intersect in their relative interiors, up to homotopy. A maximal collection
T
of pairwise compatible arcs in
\Sigma
along with all of the boundary arcs of
\Sigma
is an
ideal triangulation
of
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
. All triangulations of
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
have
6g+3b+3p+c-6
arcs.

Fix a surface with marked points
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
and an ideal triangulation
T_{0}
of
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
with valency at least 3 at each puncture with arcs
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
. For each triangle
\Delta
in
T_{0}
, let
B^{\Delta}=(b_{i,j}^{\Delta})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq n}
, where

b_{i,j}^{\Delta}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $t_{j}$ follows $t_{i}$ in clockwise order,}\\
-1&\text{if $t_{j}$ follows $t_{i}$ in counterclockwise order,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The
signed adjacency matrix

B=B(T_{0})
is given by
B(T_{0})=\sum_{\Delta}B^{\Delta}
, where the sum is over all non self-folded triangles.
Take a set of
n
algebraically independent rational functions, one for each arc in
T_{0}
,
x_{0}=\{x_{\tau_{1}},\ldots,x_{\tau_{n}}\}
,
that generate the field
\mathbb{C}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
. Such a set is called an initial
cluster
. The pair
(B(T_{0}),x_{0})
is called an
initial seed
. For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
define the
mutation in direction k
by the following rule. Let
\mu_{k}(x_{\tau_{i}})=x_{\tau_{i}}
if
i\neq k
, and

\mu_{k}(x_{\tau_{k}})=\frac{1}{x_{\tau_{k}}}\left(\prod_{B_{k,j}>0}x_{\tau_{j}}^{B_{k,j}}+\prod_{B_{k,j}<0}x_{\tau_{j}}^{-B_{k,j}}\right)\,,

so that
\mu_{k}(x_{0})=x_{0}\setminus\{x_{k}\}\cup\{\mu_{k}(x_{k})\}
. Define

\mu_{k}(B)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-B_{i,j}&\text{if }k\in\{i,j\}\\
B_{i,j}+\frac{|B_{i,k}|B_{k,j}+B_{i,k}|B_{k,j}|}{2}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Write
\mu_{k}(B(T_{0}),x_{0}):=(\mu_{k}(B(T_{0})),\mu_{k}(x_{0}))
. Observe that
\mu_{k}(B(T_{0}))
is the adjacency matrix of the triangulation
T^{\prime}
obtained by flipping the edge
\tau_{k}
. The set
\mu_{k}(x_{0})
is again a cluster.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of
cluster variables
; that is, all variables contained in all possible clusters obtained by sequences of mutations starting from
x_{0}
. The
cluster algebra
(with trivial coefficients) associated to the surface with marked points
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
is the algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(T_{0},x_{0})=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{X}]
. It is independent of the chosen triangulation
T_{0}
, so it really is associated to the surface
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
. Furthermore, there is a biyective correspondence between clusters of
\mathcal{A}
and triangulations of
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
, as well as between cluster variables and arcs in
\Sigma
.

2
Perfect matchings

All graphs considered in this paper will be finite and loopless. Let
\mathcal{G}
be such a graph with vertex set
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
and edge set
E
. Denote by
E(i,j)
the set of edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
. Write
s(G)
for the underlying simple graph of
G
, and
m(i,j)=|E(i,j)|
. Of course, if
G
is simple then
s(G)=G
, and
m(i,j)\in\{0,1\}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. If
\overrightarrow{G}
is an orientation of
G
, then
E(i,j)
denotes the set of arrows with tail
v_{i}
and head
v_{j}
.

A
perfect matching
of
G
is a subset
M\subseteq E
such that each
v\in V
is an endpoint of exactly one member of
M
.
Thus,
|M|=|V|/2
, so a graph that admits a perfect matching must have an even number of vertices. Call such a graph
even
. Denote by
\operatorname{Match}(G)
the set of perfect matchings of
G
, and let
\Phi(G):=|\operatorname{Match}(G)|.

We will use a simple but useful device to keep track of all perfect matchings of
G
, the so-called
method of variables
as in
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

(Weighted adjacency matrix)
Let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. For each edge
e
of
s(G)
, consider the formal variable
z_{e}
, and let
a_{i,j}=m(i,j)z_{e}
.
The matrix
A_{G}(z):=(a_{i,j})
is called the
weighted adjacency matrix
of
G
. Furthermore, if
\overrightarrow{G}
is an orientation of
G
with no oriented 2-cycles, write

b_{i,j}:=\epsilon(i,j)m(i,j)z_{e}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\epsilon(i,j):=\begin{cases}1&E(i,j)\neq\emptyset\\
-1&E(j,i)\neq\emptyset\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z):=(b_{i,j})
is the
weighted skew-symmetric adjacency matrix
of
\overrightarrow{G}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The matrices of definition
2.1
are defined up to a relabeling of the vertex set of
G
, which corresponds to a simultaneous permutation of their rows and columns. By setting each variable
z_{e}=1
, we recover the usual adjacency matrices of the graphs
G
and
\overrightarrow{G}
, so we denote them by
A_{G}(1)
and
B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(1)
, respectively.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph and
E^{\prime}
be the edge set of
s(G)
. The
matching generating function
of
G
is the polynomial
\phi_{G}\in\mathbb{Z}[z_{e}\mid e\in E^{\prime}]
given by

\phi_{G}:=\sum_{\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\in\operatorname{Match}(G)}z_{e_{1}}\cdots z_{e_{k}}

Definition 2.3
.

Let
C=(c_{i,j})
be a skew-symmetric matrix of size
2n\geq 2
over a commutative unit ring
A
. For each partition in pairs
P=\{\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\ldots,\{i_{n},j_{n}\}\}
of the set
\{1,2,\ldots,2n-1,2n\}
, define

\sigma_{P}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&2n-1&2n\\
i_{1}&j_{1}&\ldots&i_{n}&j_{n}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad c_{P}:=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P})c_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots c_{i_{n},j_{n}}\,,

where
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P})
denotes the sign of the permutation
\sigma
.
The
pfaffian
of
C
is
\operatorname{pff}(C):=\sum_{P}c_{P}\,.

Remark 2.2
.

The term
c_{P}
is independent of the order of labeling of each part in
P
. Indeed, switching the roles of
i_{k}
and
j_{k}
changes the sign of
\sigma_{P}
by
-1
, but
c_{j_{k},i_{k}}=-c_{i_{k},j_{k}}
. Thus,
c_{P}
really does depend only on the partition
P
. Note that such a partition corresponds to a perfect matching of
K_{2n}
, the complete graph in
2n
vertices, and that
c_{P}\neq 0
if and only if such a potential matching corresponds to an actual matching of
G
.

The pfaffian and determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix are closely related. The following lemma is due to Cayley
[
3
]
. A proof can be found in
[
1
]
, where it appears as Theorem 5.6.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
B
is a skew-symmetric matrix of even size,
\det B=\operatorname{pff}(B)^{2}
.

The next lemma gives a way to keep track of the perfect matchings of a graph
G
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be an graph with an even number of vertices, and let
\overrightarrow{G}
be an arbitrary orientation of
G
. The sum of the absolute value of the coefficients of
\operatorname{pff}(B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z))
is
\Phi(G)
.

Proof.

Take the vertex set
V
of
G
to be
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and consider the simple graph
s(G)
with edge set
E^{\prime}
. Let
k=n/2
. Each partition
P=\{\{i_{l},j_{l}\}\mid 1\leq l\leq k\}
as in definition
2.3
corresponds to a possible set of independent edges in the simple graph
s(G)
. For
C=B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z)
, the associated term
c_{P}
is

c_{P}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\{i_{s},j_{s}\}\notin E^{\prime}\text{ for some }1\leq s\leq k\,,\\
\prod_{l=1}^{k}\epsilon(i_{l},j_{l})m(i_{l},j_{l})z_{\{i_{l},j_{l}\}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus,
c_{P}\neq 0
if and only if
P
corresponds to a perfect matching
M
in
s(G)
, as
P
is a partition. Note that the coefficient
\prod_{l=1}^{k}m(i_{l},j_{l})

is the number of ways of getting the matching
M
in
s(G)
from a matching in
G
.
Since different matchings of
s(G)
differ by at least one edge, terms corresponding to distinct matchings cannot cancel each other. This completes the proof.
∎

As Tutte first observed
[
9
]
, the previous argument and Lemma
2.1
imply that a graph
G
admits a perfect matching if and only if there is some orientation
\overrightarrow{G}
for which
\det(B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z))\neq 0
. Call the polynomial
\operatorname{pff}(B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z))
the pfaffian of
\overrightarrow{G}
, and denote it by
\operatorname{pff}(\overrightarrow{G})
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\overrightarrow{G}
be an oriented graph. Then
|\operatorname{pff}(B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(1))|\leq\Phi(G)
.

An orientation
\overrightarrow{G}
of
G
for which the equality is attained in the previous corollary is called a
pfaffian
orientation. Likewise, a graph is
pfaffian
if it admits a pfaffian orientation. Observe that for such an orientation, the pfaffian of
\overrightarrow{G}
is a polynomial whose coefficients all have the same sign. Indeed, in such a case
\operatorname{pff}(\overrightarrow{G})=\pm\phi_{G}
. We cite a useful characterization of pfaffian orientations taken from
[
7
]
. Call a cycle
C
of a graph
G

nice
if
G-V(C)
admits a perfect matching.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 8.3.2)
Let
G
be an even graph and
\overrightarrow{G}
an orientation of
G
. Then
\overrightarrow{G}
is pfaffian if and only if, when traversing each even nice cycle of
G
in an arbitrary direction, it has an odd number of arrows that agree with the orientation
\overrightarrow{G}
.

Furthermore, the following theorem is classic, and a proof can be found in
[
7
]
where it appears as Theorem 8.3.4.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Kasteleyn)
Every even planar graph is pfaffian.

Let
\overrightarrow{G}
be bipartite with bipartition
V=U\cup W
such that
|U|=|W|
(note that if
\overrightarrow{G}
has a perfect matching, this is the case). The weighted skew symmetric adjacency matrix of
\overrightarrow{G}
takes the form

B_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&M_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z)\\
-M_{\overrightarrow{G}}(z)^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

for any labeling
V=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},w_{1},\ldots,w_{l}\}
such that
u_{i}\in U
and
w_{j}\in W
for
1\leq i\leq k
,
1\leq j\leq l
.

M_{\overrightarrow{G}}(x)
is called the
weighted biadjacency matrix
of
\overrightarrow{G}
.
The following lemma is given as an exercise in
[
6
]
(though there seems to be a sign missing therein). We include a proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
M
be a square matrix of size
n
. If

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&M\\
-M^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

then
\operatorname{pff}(B)=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\det M
.

Proof.

Let
V=\{1,\ldots,2n\}
,
U=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
W=\{n+1,\ldots,2n\}
. Fix a partition
P=\{\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\ldots,\{i_{n},j_{n}\}\}
. By remark
2.2
, we can suppose that
j_{i}=n+i
for each
1\leq i\leq n
, so

\sigma_{P}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&\ldots&2n-1&2n\\
i_{1}&n+1&i_{2}&n+2&\ldots&i_{n}&2n\end{pmatrix}

Note that
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leq n<n+1=j_{1}
, so the indices
i_{l}
amount to
n-1
inversions with respect to
j_{1}
. Similarly,
n-2
of the
i_{l}
give
n-2
inversions with
j_{2}
, and so on. This gives a total of
n(n-1)/2
inversions. Thus,

\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P})=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&n-1&n&n+1&\ldots&2n-1&2n\\
i_{1}&i_{2}&\ldots&i_{n-1}&i_{n}&n+1&\ldots&2n-1&2n\end{pmatrix}\,.

It follows that
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P})=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P}^{\prime})
, where

\sigma_{P}^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&n-1&n\\
i_{1}&i_{2}&\ldots&i_{n-1}&i_{n}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Thus,
b_{P}=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{P}^{\prime})b_{i_{1},n+1}\cdots b_{i_{n},2n}
. Summing over all partitions
P
, we obtain the result.
∎

In particular, calculating the pfaffian of a bipartite graph amounts to computing the determinant of its biadjacency matrix.

Snake graphs and cluster algebras

A
tile
is a graph isomorphic to a square drawn in the plane. A
snake graph

\mathcal{G}
is a plane graph obtained from a sequence of
d
tiles
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
such that each successive tile is placed on top or to the right of the previous tile, as in figure
1
. That is, for
1\leq i\leq d-1
, tiles
G_{i}
and
G_{i+1}
share their right and left edge, respectively, or their top and bottom edge, respectively. The edges of
\mathcal{G}
that lie on its unbounded face are called
boundary edges
, and the rest of the edges are
internal
. It is easily seen by induction that snake graphs are bipartite and always admit perfect matchings, and that a snake graph with
d
tiles has
2d+2
vertices and
3d+1
edges.

Snake graphs were used in
[
8
]
as a crucial combinatorial tool to compute the cluster expansions of cluster variables in surface cluster algebras. Fix a bordered surface
(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
and an initial seed
(T,x_{0})
. As discussed in the introduction, each arc
\gamma
in
\Sigma
corresponds to a cluster variable
x_{\gamma}
, and if
\gamma\notin T
, then
x_{\gamma}
is not part of the initial cluster
x_{0}
, so one must perform a series of mutations to find the expansion of
x_{\gamma}
with respect to
x_{0}
. This can be avoided by virtue of the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

(
[
8
]
, Theorem 4.9)
Orient
\gamma\notin T
so that it crosses the edges
\tau_{i_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{i_{d}}
of
T
in order. Each
\tau_{i_{j}}
is contained in exactly two triangles
\Delta_{j}
and
\Delta_{j}^{\prime}
. Let
G_{j}
be the quadrilateral formed by the triangles
\Delta_{j}
and
\Delta_{j}^{\prime}
. Give each side of
G_{j}
the weight
x_{k}
if it is the internal diagonal of
T
associated to
x_{k}
, and give it the weight 1 if it is a boundary edge of
T
. There is a weighted snake graph
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
with tiles
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
such that

x_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cross}(\gamma)}\sum_{P\in\operatorname{Match}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})}w(P)\,

where
\operatorname{cross}(\gamma)=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}
and
w(P)
is the product of the weights of the edges in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
that occur in
P
.

For more details on how the tiles
G_{j}
are glued together to obtain
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
, see
[
8
]
.

\mathcal{G}
with the pfaffian orientation

By Theorem
2.2
, snake graphs are pfaffian, and Theorem
2.1
suggests a way to produce a pfaffian orientation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be snake graph with tiles
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{d}
. Define the orientation
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
as follows:

1.

Orient the boundary edges of
\mathcal{G}
cyclically and counterclockwise.

2.

Orient every internal vertical edge from bottom to top, and every internal horizontal edge from right to left.

3.

Reverse the orientation of the left edge of
G_{1}

The following is readily seen on account of Theorem
2.1
. We give a direct proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every snake graph
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
is a pfaffian orientation.

Proof.

We proceed by induction in
d
, the number of tiles. When
d=1
, say
\mathcal{G}
has vertices
u_{1},w_{1},u_{2},w_{2}
listed in cyclic order. The weighted biadjacency matrix of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
is

\begin{pmatrix}z_{u_{1}w_{1}}&-z_{u_{2}w_{1}}\\
z_{u_{1}w_{2}}&z_{u_{2}w_{2}}\end{pmatrix}

so that
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))=-z_{u_{1}w_{1}}z_{u_{2}w_{2}}-z_{u_{2}w_{1}}z_{u_{1}w_{2}}
.
Take
\mathcal{G}
with tiles
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d+1}
,
d\geq 1
and give it the orientation
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be the snake graph with tiles
G_{2},\ldots,G_{d+1}
obtained by deleting the two leftmost vertices of
\mathcal{G}
. Observe that the induced orientation on
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
, so by induction the coefficients of the pfaffian of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
all have the same sign
\epsilon\in\{1,-1\}
. By circling the boundary of
\mathcal{G}
counterclockwise, we obtain a bipartition
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}\cup\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}
of its vertices. Label
V(\mathcal{G})
so that
G_{1}
has vertices
w_{n+1},u_{n},w_{n},u_{n+1}
as in figure
1
, and write
b_{i,j}
and
b_{i,j}^{\prime}
for the entries of
B_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})}(z)
and
B_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})}(z)
, respectively. Note that
B_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})}(x)
is obtained from
B_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})}(z)
by adding two rows and two columns corresponding to vertices
u_{n+1}
and
w_{n+1}
.

Any perfect matching
P
of
\mathcal{G}
either contains the edge
u_{n+1}w_{n+1}
or the edges
u_{n+1}w_{n}
and
u_{n}w_{n+1}
.
In the first case,
P
restricts to a perfect matching of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, and its associated term in
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))
is

\displaystyle b_{P}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&2n-1&2n&2n+1&2n+2\\
i_{1}&j_{1}&\ldots&i_{n}&j_{n}&2n+1&2n+2\end{pmatrix}b_{i_{1},j_{1}}\ldots b_{i_{n},j_{n}}(-z_{u_{n+1}w_{n+1}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&2n-1&2n\\
i_{1}&j_{1}&\ldots&i_{n}&j_{n}\end{pmatrix}b_{i_{1},j_{1}}\ldots b_{i_{n},j_{n}}(-z_{u_{n+1}w_{n+1}})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon z_{u_{i_{1}}w_{j_{1}}}\cdots z_{u_{i_{n}}w_{j_{n}}}z_{u_{n+1}w_{n+1}}

In the second case,
P
contains edges
u_{n+1}w_{n}
and
u_{n}w_{n+1}
. Such matchings are in bijective correspondence to matchings
P^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
that contain the edge
u_{n}w_{n}
. The associated term in
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))
is

\displaystyle b_{P}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&2n-1&2n&2n+1&2n+2\\
i_{1}&j_{1}&\ldots&2n-1&2n+2&2n+1&2n\end{pmatrix}b_{i_{1},j_{1}}\ldots b_{i_{n-1}j_{n-1}}(-z_{u_{n}w_{n+1}})(-z_{u_{n+1}w_{n}})\,.

The corresponding term
b_{P^{\prime}}
is

\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&2n-1&2n\\
i_{1}&j_{1}&\ldots&2n-1&2n\end{pmatrix}b_{i_{1},j_{1}}\ldots b_{i_{n-1}j_{n-1}}z_{u_{n},w_{n}}.

Note that
b_{P}
can be obtained from
b_{P^{\prime}}
by substituting
z_{u_{n},w_{n}}
with
-z_{u_{n}w_{n+1}}z_{u_{n+1}w_{n}}
, since their associated permutations have opposite signs. Thus,
b_{P}=-\epsilon z_{u_{i_{1}},w_{j_{1}}}\cdots z_{u_{i_{n-1}},w_{j_{n-1}}}z_{u_{n}w_{n+1}}z_{u_{n+1}w_{n}}
, so every term of
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))
has coefficient
-\epsilon
.

The previous proof also shows that the sign of each coefficient in
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))
is
(-1)^{d}
, where
d
is the number of tiles in
d
. Observe that the snake graph
T
with one tile has pfaffian equal to
-\phi_{T}
. By induction, we see that
\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))=(-1)^{d+1}\phi_{G}
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a snake graph with
d
tiles, and let
M(z)
be the weighted biadjacency matrix of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
. Then

\det M(z)=(-1)^{(d+1)(d+2)/2}\phi_{\mathcal{G}}\,.

Proof.

\mathcal{G}
has
2d+2
vertices, so
M(z)
has size
d+1
. By Lemma
2.3
,

\det M(z)=(-1)^{d(d+1)/2}\operatorname{pff}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}))=(-1)^{d(d+1)/2+d+1}\phi_{\mathcal{G}}=(-1)^{(d+1)(d+2)/2}\phi_{\mathcal{G}}\,.

∎

Applying this corollary to Theorem
3.1
, we obtain our determinantal formula.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma,\mathbb{M})
be a surface cluster algebra with trivial coefficients, and let
(T,x_{0})
be an initial seed. For every arc
\gamma\notin T
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
be its associated weighted snake graph, and
d
be its number of tiles. Let
M
be the matrix obtained by substituting each
z_{e}
in
M_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})}(z)
with the weight of
e
in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
. The cluster expansion of
x_{\gamma}
is given by

x_{\gamma}=\frac{(-1)^{(d+1)(d+2)/2}}{\operatorname{cross}(\gamma)}\det M\,.

.

. The snake graph

associated to arc

, so

-x_{2}&x_{4}&1&0\\

and
\det(M)=x_{4}^{2}x_{6}+x_{4}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{5}+x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}x_{6}+x_{1}x_{3}
.
