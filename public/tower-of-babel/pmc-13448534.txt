READYCOM: protocol for a 2-year prospective natural history and cross-sectional muscle-fatigability study for improving trial readiness in congenital myopathies

AbstractIntroductionCongenital myopathies (CMYO) are a group of rare hereditary muscle diseases defined by characteristic abnormalities on muscle biopsy. Several types, including the core myopathies central core disease and multi-minicore disease, nemaline myopathy and centronuclear myopathy, have been identified based on the characteristic histopathological changes and attributed to various genetic backgrounds. The most prominent clinical features are generalised muscle weakness often pronounced axially, variable cardiorespiratory and bulbar impairment, and skeletal and joint involvement. Currently, no curative therapies are available for CMYOs; however, a few phase I and II trials have been performed or are expected in the near future. To reach trial readiness, an informed understanding of the disease course and a selection of relevant and sensitive clinical and functional outcome measures, and blood and imaging biomarkers is necessary. Furthermore, additional symptoms such as muscle fatigability have been recognised but not investigated systematically yet. For study 2, we will assess the muscle fatigability and neuromuscular junction transmission. Baseline data will be analysed using descriptive statistics and correlation analysis. Introduction

Congenital myopathies (CMYO) are a rare group of hereditary muscle diseases defined by characteristic abnormalities in the muscle fibre.1 2 The estimated prevalence of CMYO is 1.5:100 000.3 Several types, including the core myopathies central core disease (CCD) and multi-minicore disease (MmD), nemaline myopathy (NM) and centronuclear myopathy (CNM), have been identified based on distinctive histopathological changes and attributed to various genetic backgrounds.1 The most common clinical features in the CMYOs as a group are generalised muscle weakness often pronounced axially, variable cardiorespiratory and bulbar impairment, and skeletal and joint involvement.1 2 The most mutated gene in patients with a core myopathy is RYR1, which encodes the skeletal muscle ryanodine 1 (RyR1) receptor.4 5 Most pathogenic RYR1 variants implicated in CCD/MmD reduce the function of the RYR1 receptor and result in muscle weakness.6 CNM can also be caused by variants in RYR1 but is more commonly caused by variants in other genes, in particular MTM1, BIN1 and DNM2.7 8 The phenotype is genotype-dependent, with the X-linked variant resulting in the most severe cases in males. Other variants more commonly result in milder symptoms.9 NM is characterised by nemaline rods on muscle biopsy and caused by variants in a wide range of genes, including NEB, ACTA1 and KBTBD13.10–13 Several subtypes can be distinguished based on onset, severity, distribution of weakness and other features.13 Due to the clinical similarities in phenotypes between CCD/MmD, NM and CNM, we will use a basket natural history study and a cross-sectional study towards fatigability.

Currently, no curative therapies are available for the CMYOs; however, a few therapies based on expert opinion and a few proof-of-concept studies are being used, for example, to treat muscle fatigability using salbutamol or pyridostigmine.14 15 A few phase I and II trials have been performed or are expected in the near future.16–21 To reach trial readiness, improved understanding of the disease course and a selection of the most sensitive and relevant clinical and functional outcome measures and blood and imaging biomarkers is necessary.

Various outcome measures exist in practice to determine the presence and severity of CMYO,22 but it is yet unknown which outcome measures are most sensitive to detect changes in muscle strength and motor abilities over the timeframe of a clinical trial in CMYO. In addition, the presence of potential systemic diseases (eg, liver and renal function) should be investigated since gene therapies can negatively affect these organ systems.21 23

It has been increasingly recognised that CMYOs may have symptoms other than progressive muscle weakness, including muscle fatigability.24 25 Fatigability is not equal to experienced fatigue and may have a different pathophysiology. Consequently, experienced fatigue and fatigability are assessed differently.26 27 Muscle fatigability is defined as the inability to perform repetitive tasks at the same intensity without a deterioration in performance and is primarily a result of functional failure of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ).28 The occurrence and severity of fatigability have not been investigated systematically in CMYO. Furthermore, muscle fatigability has been managed successfully with easily accessible, low-cost drugs such as pyridostigmine and salbutamol, with few side effects in several neuromuscular disorders, including CMYO and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).29 A few studies have reported CMYO patients experiencing increased endurance or stamina through treatments that affect the NMJ.2 24 25 30 The lack of evidence through systematical assessments on fatigability in CMYO calls for a study assessing muscle fatigability and NMJ transmission in CMYO.

Objectives

This study involves two parts; (1) a prospective cohort study with five 6-monthly visits over a 2-year period; and (2) an exploratory study on muscle fatigability. For both parts, patients from the same study population will be included. The muscle fatigability is only measured cross-sectionally because before measuring it longitudinally we need to assess if we can indeed measure the muscle fatigability with the proposed design. Both parts of the study have their own primary objective and study 1 has additional secondary objectives.

Methods/design

Study design

Our study, named READYCOM, will focus on CCD/MmD, NM and CNM and aims to investigate the natural history and outcome measures. The study consists of two parts, both of an explorative nature; (1) a prospective, observational study with repeated measurements (natural history study) performed at the (Pediatric) Neurology Department at Radboud University Medical Center (Radboudumc); and (2) a cross-sectional study to explore the presence of muscle fatigability at the Department of Neurology and the Child Development and Exercise Center of the University Medical Center Utrecht (UMC Utrecht) and Radboudumc. Patients can choose to participate in either one of the studies or both (figure 1).

Flow chart of recruitment and inclusion of nemaline myopathy, centronuclear myopathy, and central core disease/multi-minicore disease patients.

For the natural history study, patients will be invited for five 6-monthly study visits over a period of 2 years at Radboudumc. If patients are unable to visit the hospital, we offer a home visit in which we perform the clinical and functional outcome measures, and questionnaires. For the fatigability study, patients will be invited for two visits with a maximum of 4 weeks in between, with one visit at Radboudumc and one visit at UMC Utrecht. Patients participating in both studies will have six visits: five visits at an interval of 6 months and one visit within 4 weeks after visit 2, 3 or 4. Our study has been approved by the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (CCMO, registration number NL83069.000.23) and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06157268).

Sample size calculation

In the Netherlands, 40–50 patients per CMYO group have been identified. Based on previous experience in research regarding rare diseases, we estimated that 50% of the patients would participate in the study. Therefore, we will include 15 patients per CMYO group for a total of 45 patients in the natural history study.

To study the fatigability, we will perform repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS), which has been shown to be specific and sensitive in other neuromuscular disorders.31 In the general population, 6% of people have a positive fatigability score.32 Based on clinical experience, we expected that around 30% of patients present with increased muscle fatigability. We want to include enough patients for a power of 80% and a one-sided type 1 error probability α of 5%. This results in a sample size of 25 patients per CMYO group for a total of 75 patients for the fatigability study. Based on the fatigability studies in SMA, the cohort is expected to be sufficiently large to show an effect.33

Patient and public involvement

The patient organisation Spierziekten Nederland has been included from the initial conception through the grants submission stage and project execution. They have contributed to important topics, feasibility and outreach to patients. After collecting the data, we will invite patients and patient representatives to collaborate in interpreting and disseminating the results.

Recruitment

Patients known at Radboudumc and UMC Utrecht will be approached about the study. Other (paediatric) neurologists, rehabilitation specialists and clinical geneticists will be invited to inform other potential participants. Additionally, the study will be promoted through the patient organisation in the Netherlands (Spierziekten Nederland) by digital newsletters. The inclusion for study 1 started in March 2024 and has closed in November 2025. The inclusion for study 2 started in May 2025 and is ongoing until 2027.

Study procedures

The studies consist of multiple parts: patient-reported, clinical and functional outcome measures, laboratory studies, muscle imaging and fatigability measures (tables 1 and 2). The sequence of measurements will, to the extent possible, be standardised across all participants and visits, taking into account the availability of materials and personnel. Due to the subjective nature of the clinical and functional outcome measures, these will be performed by the same researcher for each participant at each visit. If children object to a certain procedure, this will be marked as missing data. Also, not all participants will be able to undergo an MRI due to metal objects in their body. These patients are not excluded from the study.

Clinical and functional outcome measures (at each visit)

Accelerometry. After each visit, patients will be given an accelerometer (GENEActiv, Activinsights Ltd, Kimbolton, UK) to wear on their non-dominant hand for 8 days. The patients will also be asked to fill in a diary with their activities in the same period: sleeping hours, working and/or school hours and exercise. The GENEActiv captures tri-axial acceleration data, set at 87.5 Hz sampling. The captured raw data will be processed into 1 s epochs by using the GENEActiv software. The gravity-subtracted sum of vector magnitudes (SVMgs) will be used as the activity measure. To calculate the SVMgs the following equation will be used: ∑x2+γ2+z2−1g.49 50 All data will be analysed using MATLAB (The Mathworks, Inc, Natick, Massachusetts, USA). Total activity, percentage of sedentary, light, moderate and vigorous activity will be analysed.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan will be performed in patients aged ≥6 years. At the first and last visit, bone density will be assessed with a DXA scan of the right femoral neck and lumbar spine, including vertebral fracture assessment using the Hologic Horizon DXA System (Hologic Inc, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA).

Muscle MRI will only be performed in patients aged ≥10 years who satisfy the inclusion and exclusion criteria for MRI. At the first and last visit, leg muscles will be qualitatively and quantitatively assessed (1.5 T Avanto fit, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) in accordance with a locally-developed scanning protocol, including 6-point chemical-shift-based water-fat-separation, or ‘Dixon’ imaging, and multi-echo-spin-echo-based T2 mapping. Fat-fraction maps will be calculated from the Dixon data using a Python processing pipeline developed in-house. The Dixon opposed-phase images will be semi-automatically segmented using the Dafne63 to generate regions of interest (ROIs) for muscles of the upper and lower legs. Each ROI will be used to calculate muscle volumes, fat fractions, contractile volumes and water T2 values.

At the first and last visit, leg muscles will be qualitatively and quantitatively assessed (1.5 T Avanto fit, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) in accordance with a locally-developed scanning protocol, including 6-point chemical-shift-based water-fat-separation, or ‘Dixon’ imaging, and multi-echo-spin-echo-based T2 mapping. Fat-fraction maps will be calculated from the Dixon data using a Python processing pipeline developed in-house. The Dixon opposed-phase images will be semi-automatically segmented using the Dafne63 to generate regions of interest (ROIs) for muscles of the upper and lower legs. Each ROI will be used to calculate muscle volumes, fat fractions, contractile volumes and water T2 values.

Muscle ultrasound will be performed for all patients at the first and last visit. Muscle thickness and muscle echogenicity of a previously used set of paired muscles on each side64 will be assessed by using a Canon Aplio i800 with a PLT-1005BT Probe (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan), according to a predefined measurement protocol.65–72 The mean grayscale level within a ROI in the ultrasound image will be calculated using an in-house developed software package to determine the muscle echogenicity.73 Both muscle echogenicity and thickness will be standardised by calculating their z-score. The predicted grayscale level will be determined using a reference equation that includes age, height, sex and weight.72 74 Finally, the ultrasound images will be scored using a 3-point Heckmatt grading scale.75

Data collection

Data management and data collection will be performed in the Castor EDC platform V.2024.2.2.0 (Castor Electronic Data Capture, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) via direct and indirect entry through the electronic patient system (Epic, version May 2023). Clinical outcome assessments were completed on paper and stored at Radboudumc. Raw data such as muscle imaging, accelerometry data will be stored on a department server in a secured folder. Data will be stored for 15 years. All data will be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Statistical analysis

Natural history data

Due to the explorative character of the study, we will use descriptive statistics to summarise our data. To test correlation between age and difference between ambulatory and non-ambulatory patients, we will perform (Spearman’s) correlation analysis. To assess disease progression between longitudinal measurements, we will use mixed-models. Multiple linear regressions will be used to explore the relationship between potential disease-modifying variables and disease severity. We will regard p <0.05 as statistically significant.

RNS data

Measurement of decrement in RNS is a quantitative test. The outcome is either positive (decrement-pathologic) or negative (no decrement=normal). A 10% or higher decrement reflects NMJ post-synaptic dysfunction. The percentage of decrement in the participants will be compared with the 6% false positive decrement in the normal population.32 82 The difference between the two groups will be tested with a Z-score (z=(observed value-expected value)/se(observed value)). Quantitative differences in decrement between the ages and ambulatory and non-ambulatory patients will be tested with analysis of variance. Correlation analysis of the degree of decrement and CMAP amplitude, disease duration and total MRC sum score will be performed with the Spearman rank correlation test. To control for false discovery rate, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure will be applied.

Endurance test data

The EST has two outcomes; drop-out (yes/no) and time to limitation (seconds). Drop-out is defined as the inability to endure the maximum test duration of 20 min. Kaplan-Meier analyses will be performed to assess the probability of drop-out since test initiation of the shuttle test. Pearson’s correlation will be used to assess the correlation between muscle fatigability, defined by the drop-out time, and muscle function, motor function and experienced fatigue.76 To control for false discovery rate, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure will be applied.

Isokinetic dynamometry and sEMG data

The decrease in power and work, measured with isokinetic dynamometry, between the start and end of the endurance protocol will be assessed. The same will be assessed for RMS of the sEMG amplitude and median frequency. We will perform correlation analyses between the parameters measured during the isokinetic dynamometry (power and work) and sEMG parameters (amplitude and frequency). To control for false discovery rate, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure will be applied.

Discussion

We present the protocol of our 2-year natural history study in children and adults with various CMYO, including a cross-sectional study on muscle-fatigability (READYCOM). The longitudinal assessment of various patient-reported, clinical and functional outcomes, and blood and imaging biomarkers will provide the basis for future clinical trials in CMYO. Additionally, the study will offer detailed insights into the severity of muscle fatigability in these patients.

Several initiatives worldwide have started to gather data from CMYO patients to create a broad overview of their characteristics. During a dedicated European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC; https://www.enmc.org/) workshop in 2019 for nemaline myopathies18 and the RYR-1-Related Diseases International Research Workshops in 202220 and 2025, the importance of international collaboration was discussed. A large portion of the proposed outcome measures from the ENMC workshop will be assessed during our study at 6-month intervals. A natural history study for patients with CNM has been conducted by Dynacure in Belgium, France, Germany, Italy and Spain.83 However, this study was less extensive than the READYCOM study. We also include patient-reported outcomes, extensive medical history, graded and timed function tests, dynamometry and imaging and blood biomarkers. In the USA, a natural history study of RYR1-related disorders is imminent.84 Several study outcomes align with the outcomes from our study. Using the same outcome measures will increase the data for this rare disease and result in a more robust dataset.

Strengths and limitations

In the READYCOM study, we will assess three different types of CMYO. Given the shared characteristics of CCD/MmD, NM and CNM—such as reduced muscle tone, muscle weakness, respiratory and bulbar difficulties and skeletal features—along with their genetic heterogeneity, we have decided to group these conditions together in a basket natural history study. This approach will also expand the number of patients we can include, given that these are rare diseases. In addition to creating a larger cohort, our approach will also provide valuable insights into the similarities and differences between these groups. This design has been beneficial in a previous natural history study and clinical trials.85–87

Another strength of our study is our collaboration with the patient organisation, Spierziekten Nederland, from the initial conception through to the grant submission stage and project execution. First, they identified key topics important to patients beyond just muscle strength and movement, such as fatigue and pain. These have been incorporated into our study design as additional outcome measures.88 They also contributed to the study’s design, particularly in assessing the feasibility of having patients complete all the measurements in a single day and in dissemination of the invitation to participate. Furthermore, the patient organisation plays a crucial role in reaching the patient community for ongoing research, which is why we will promote our study via Spierziekten Nederland. After collecting the data, we will invite patients and patient representatives to collaborate in interpreting and disseminating the results.

At Radboudumc, we have extensive experience in conducting natural history studies in patients with facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy and Selenoprotein N-related congenital myopathy (SELENON-RM)/Laminin-α2-related muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-MD).86 89 90 Based on our experience, most patients find the study protocols manageable and not overly demanding. We have also reviewed the clinical trial experience of patients.91 92 One key point emphasised by patients was the importance of gaining knowledge about their condition and potential treatment options. Natural history studies are the first step in expanding knowledge about these diseases and act as stepping stones towards clinical trials for potential treatments.

Patients also raised concerns about the logistical challenges of participating in trials, especially the long travel distances of up to a few hours to Radboudumc or UMC Utrecht. To mitigate this, we aim to schedule study visits alongside annual neurologist appointments. Additionally, we offer patients the option to stay overnight at the hospital or a nearby hotel before a full day of examinations to minimise fatigue. Patients highlighted several crucial aspects of effective communication during the study.93 Therefore, we aim to maintain close contact with Spierziekten Nederland to enhance patient engagement and connect with the broader patient community, including those not participating in the READYCOM study.

The clinical and functional outcome measures can be influenced by multiple factors such as the motivation and level of fatigue of the patients. That is why we try to maintain the same order of measurements on the research days. Unfortunately, this is not always possible due to availability of materials and personnel. To take fatigue into account, we will ask the participants to rate their fatigue before the measurements.

Because the study consists of multiple visits over a 2-year period and at Radboudumc, there is a possibility that the more severely affected and less independent individuals will choose not to participate. This will skew the results in a more positive direction. To minimise this shift to milder phenotypes, patients are also given the opportunity to participate in a subset of the natural history study, which can be performed at home. The subset contains clinical and functional outcome measures and questionnaires.

The READYCOM study will yield valuable natural history data and a well-characterised baseline cohort of patients with CMYO for future studies and clinical trials. This information will be essential in selecting relevant and sensitive clinical and functional outcome measures, as well as blood and imaging biomarkers, to improve trial readiness. Furthermore, the study will provide in-depth insights into the severity of muscle fatigability in these patients.

Findings will be shared with the participating patients and the funder. It will be presented at conferences and shared through peer-reviewed publications.
