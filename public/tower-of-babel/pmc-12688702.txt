Core-shell structured magnetic γ-Fe2O3@Cu-La composite material for enhanced arsenic removal: Behavior and mechanism
Authors: Wei Zhang, Guanghuan Jia, Xinlu Li, Jianbo Lu, Chunting Chen, Jiyu Zhang, Juan Li, Jun Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryArsenic removal has attracted growing attention due to its high toxicity, mobility, and carcinogenicity. For efficient arsenic uptake, we successfully fabricated a magnetic γ-Fe2O3@Cu-La composite material (CLFO). The CLFO exhibited high arsenic adsorption capacity through the synergistic effect of its Cu-La composite oxide phase, while the γ-Fe2O3 component endowed it with superior magnetic separation functionality. The core-shell structured CLFO consisted of smaller nanoparticles of irregular sizes and shapes. Results from batch experiments demonstrated that the CLFO exhibited effective removal capacities for both As(V) (160.4 mg/g) and As(III) (113.5 mg/g), with an optimal adsorption pH range of 6.0–7.0. The spent CLFO was successfully regenerated and used for arsenic removal from real water samples. Both As(V) and As(III) were taken up through the creation of inner-sphere surface complexes resulting from replacing the surface hydroxyl groups. Overall, the CLFO appeared to be an excellent option for eliminating arsenic in engineering applications.Subject areas: Applied sciences, Composite materials, Materials science, Materials synthesis

Introduction

Arsenic (As), a potentially carcinogenic contaminant, poses substantial hazards to both the environment and human well-being.1 The contamination of water with arsenic is primarily caused by arsenic release from As-containing minerals and ores, industrial effluents, and agricultural pesticide application, as well as animal feed additives.2,3 In aqueous environments, arsenic typically occurs as pentavalent arsenate (As(V)) and/or trivalent arsenite (As(III)), in accordance with the solution pH and redox potential (Eh). In reducing environments (i.e., low Eh values), As(III) predominantly exists as the nonionized H3AsO3 species, whereas when oxidation is dominant (i.e., high Eh values), As(V) predominates as the ionized H2AsO4− and HAsO42− species.4,5 As(III) forms exhibit greater toxicity, mobility, and solubility in comparison to As(V) forms.6 Persistent ingestion of water contaminated with arsenic may cause serious health problems, ranging from arsenicosis and malignancies to cancers in the bladder, lung, and kidney.7 Currently, concentrations in source waters from various regions globally (e.g., China, Bangladesh, USA, Mexico, and Brazil) have been found to exceed the regulatory limit of 10 μg/L.8 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a cost-effective and efficient remediation strategy for addressing arsenic contamination, thereby ensuring water safety and safeguarding public health.

Adsorption is considered an applicable technique for the efficient elimination of arsenic in distributed aqueous systems owing to its ease of implementation, economic viability, and environmental friendliness.9,10 To date, several nanomaterials, such as rare-earth oxide nanoparticles, have been engineered for arsenic sequestration from aqueous media.10,11,12 Amongst them, lanthanum (hydro)oxides demonstrate excellent performance on the removal of arsenic even at trace levels, mainly on account of the high concentration of -OH groups on the adsorbents' surface.13 Lanthanum (La), as a non-toxic and environmentally benign element, is the most prevalent rare-earth element in Earth’s lithosphere, and it is essential to bacterial and some antimicrobial activities.14,15 Hence, La-based adsorbents have received considerable scientific interest. Nevertheless, the release of La3+ ions under neutral and acidic environments significantly restricts its application in water treatment.16 Most recently, substantial work has focused on developing new La-based composite adsorbents, such as Fe-La and Ti-La adsorbents.17,18,19,20 Those adsorbents possess large specific surface areas and exceptional affinities toward certain anionic contaminants. Moreover, the synergistic effect between the La component and other components can suppress the leakage of La3+, which probably decreases biological incompatibilities. However, all the aforementioned La-based adsorbents are in powder form, making them difficult to separate and recover from aqueous solutions and prone to causing filter clogging.21

Compared to traditional filtration, magnetic separation is faster, and the adsorbents can be readily retrieved from water. Furthermore, magnetite-based composites combine the benefits of magnetic separation with excellent adsorption ability.2,22,23,24 For example, Zeng et al. reported an innovative core-shell magnetic nanoparticle fabricated using iron-containing water treatment residues via a heterogeneous nucleation technique, which demonstrated high efficiency in eliminating As(V) from aqueous media.2 Rashid et al. fabricated humic acid-coated magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (HA-MNPs), and these showed promise for remediating As(III) and As(V).22 Zhang et al. showed that two kinds of modified adsorbents had strong affinities for arsenic and can be effortlessly separated from aqueous solutions via magnetic means.23 Shan et al. revealed that Fe-Mn binary oxide-modified magnetic nanoparticles showed remarkable improvements in arsenite removal.24 These findings indicated that magnetic composite nanoparticles could be regarded as attractive adsorbents for arsenic immobilization.

Copper (Cu) is abundant in nature and exists in the form of metal oxides/sulfides.25 Copper oxide (CuO) has also been employed as an adsorbent for arsenic remediation because of its strong redox behavior, high pHZPC (9.4), and good chemical stability. Additionally, the antibacterial property of Cu also reduces the microbial load.26,27 Therefore, it is reasonably expected that adding the CuO component may enhance the physicochemical properties of the La-based composite adsorbent, thus promoting the removal efficiency of arsenic contaminants. A core-shell structured magnetic γ-Fe2O3@Cu-La composite material (CLFO) was preliminarily prepared in our lab. The synthesized CLFO adsorbent demonstrated exceptional efficiency in adsorbing both As(V) and As(III) across a broad pH spectrum, exhibiting strong resistance to acidic and alkaline conditions. Moreover, the application of an external magnetic field facilitated the convenient separation of the adsorbent from the solution. These findings indicated that the CLFO adsorbent holds significant potential as a practical material for arsenic removal in drinking water treatment. Nevertheless, as far as we know, arsenic (As(V) and As(III)) removal using the CLFO material has not been documented. Hence, the major goals of this study were (1) to prepare a core-shell structured CLFO adsorbent under room temperature conditions; (2) to analyze the prepared CLFO adsorbent with multiple techniques; (3) to investigate the arsenic adsorption efficiency and material reusability; and (4) to explore the potential removal pathways of As(V) and As(III) via the CLFO material.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the CLFO adsorbent

Figure 1A illustrated the schematic diagram of the preparation process for the CLFO adsorbent. The SEM (Figure 1B) and TEM (Figure 1D) images demonstrated that the prepared γ-Fe2O3 component was uniform quasi-cubical particles (around 50 nm) with relatively rough and porous surfaces. After being coated with Cu-La composite oxides, the quasi-cubical particles disappeared, giving rise to larger tight ball-like nanoparticles and nanoflakes (as shown in Figure 1C). This transformation could be attributed to the encapsulation of γ-Fe2O3 particles by the generated Cu-La composite oxides. Moreover, due to the characteristics of the facile co-precipitation method, the synthesized CLFO particles exhibited non-uniform sizes and might contain multiple γ-Fe2O3 cores. Therefore, it could be inferred that the CLFO adsorbent consisted of the quasi-cubical γ-Fe2O3 cores and a shell composed of ball-like and flake-like Cu-La composite oxides. Much like the SEM image, the TEM micrograph (Figure 1E) demonstrated that the CLFO adsorbent (approximately 100–200 nm) was composed of irregularly shaped nanosized particles and lacked a clearly defined boundary between the core and the shell. The particle size distributions of the γ-Fe2O3 component and the CLFO adsorbent were depicted in Figure S1, indicating that the main particle sizes of these two materials fell within the ranges of 1–20 nm and 10–200 nm, respectively. In addition, the energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) mappings (Figure 1F) of the CLFO material revealed that Cu, La, Fe, and O elements were distributed homogeneously in the composite.

The XRD diffraction patterns of the γ-Fe2O3 component and the CLFO adsorbent were compared in Figure 1G. For the pure γ-Fe2O3 sample, diffraction peaks at 2θ = 30.2°, 35.6°, 43.3°, 53.7°, 57.3°, and 62.9° were in good agreement with the crystal planes of the maghemite-C structure (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440), respectively (PDF No. 39–1346).28 For the prepared CLFO sample, the new broad peaks located at approximately 15.8° and 28.0° were consistent with standard La(OH)3 diffraction peaks (PDF No. 36–1481) for (100) and (101) lattice planes, respectively.17 Moreover, the pattern exhibited two new characteristic peaks at approximately 38.7° (111) and 48.7° (−202), sharing some similarities with the typical XRD pattern of CuO (PDF No. 45–0937).29 This result also suggested that several target components were successfully prepared.

Porosity and specific surface area were analyzed through N2 adsorption-desorption experiments. The adsorption isotherms within Figure 1H showed a hysteresis phenomenon in the high relative pressure regime and presented a type-IV isotherm, which was characteristic of the typical mesopore distribution according to the IUPAC classification.30 The CLFO material possessed a specific surface area of 17.4 m2/g and a pore volume of 0.064 cm3/g. The CLFO adsorbent featured a calculated pore size of 14.8 nm determined via the adsorption average pore width (4V/A by BET), which fell within the range of mesoporous dimension (2–50 nm). In addition, the differential pore size distribution of the CLFO adsorbent was presented in Figure 1H (inset graph). It could be seen that the adsorbent showed a broad range of pore sizes spanning from 256 nm to below 2 nm. The saturation magnetization (Ms) of CLFO nanoparticles reached 50.1 emu/g (Figure 1I). Generally, a material can be completely separated from a water-based solution by using a conventional magnet when its saturation magnetization exceeds 16.3 emu/g.31 Similar phenomena of efficient magnetic separation from suspension were discovered by Shan et al.24 and Zeng et al.2 for Mag-Fe-Mn (23.2 emu/g) and c-MNPs (36.4 emu/g), respectively. Indeed, our magnetic nanoparticles could be readily separated from water in 1 min using a simple external magnet (Figure 1I). Thus, it is expected that the CLFO adsorbent may serve as a magnetic adsorbent used to eliminate arsenic from water.

Adsorption isotherms

Tests under isothermal conditions were conducted to assess the arsenic adsorption capacities of the CLFO adsorbent. As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, the CLFO adsorbent exhibited good adsorption capacities for both As(V) and As(III), with a relatively better adsorption efficiency of As(V) than As(III), especially at low equilibrium concentrations. The isotherm data of adsorption were applied to Langmuir, Temkin, and Freundlich models,32,33,34 with the resulting adsorption constants presented in Tables 1 and S2. The findings suggested that the Langmuir model was shown to align better with the experimental data relative to the Freundlich and Temkin models, as evidenced by a high Adj. R2, a low residual sum of squares, and a high F value. On the whole, the Langmuir model presumes adsorption takes place on a homogeneous surface,32 which further confirms the even coating of Cu-La composite oxides on the γ-Fe2O3 core. The CLFO adsorbent possessed maximal adsorption capacities (calculated based on the Langmuir mode) of 160.4 and 113.5 mg/g toward As(V) and As(III), respectively, which were higher than those of most similar composite materials, related magnetic materials, and carbon-based materials (e.g., c-MNPs, mag-Fe-Mn, Zr/Mn/C, FeOx-GOCS-0.08, and MIO-CNTs), but lower compared to those of other metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) materials (e.g., CoFe2O4@MIL-100(Fe), FeSx@MOF-808, and Zn-MOF-74) (Table S3). However, these MOF materials currently face challenges such as high fabrication costs, complicated synthesis procedures, and difficulties in achieving stable large-scale production. Therefore, the above results suggested that the CLFO adsorbent might be a promising material for As removal.

Adsorption kinetics

The adsorption kinetics of As(V) and As(III) by the CLFO adsorbent are shown in Figures 2C and 2E. As could be seen, a rapid adsorption was observed during the initial stage (0–4 h), then the balance was attained following 24 h for As(V) and As(III). Kinetic modeling by using both the pseudo-first-order model and pseudo-second-order model35,36 well fitted the adsorption kinetics of As(V) and As (III) with high Adj. R2 values (Tables 2 and S4). In addition, the adsorption data could also be matched with the intra-particle diffusion model (Adj. R2 > 0.99), which effectively identified the reaction pathways and predicted the rate-controlling step.37 As shown in Figures 2D and 2F, the adsorption process might be split into three stages, namely (i) the rapid movement of arsenic ions from bulk solution to particle surface via a hydrodynamic boundary layer or film by the driven force of concentration gradient in the first stage (0–1 h); (ii) the gradual arsenic ion diffusion from the exterior to the pores of the CLFO adsorbent during the subsequent phase (1–12 h); and (iii) the final equilibrium stage resulting from the spatial obstruction caused by adsorbed molecules (12–24 h). The multiple stages of adsorption of arsenic ions by the CLFO adsorbent suggested a complex process therein.

Effect of pH and ionic strength on arsenic adsorption

Figures 3A and 3B suggested that arsenic ion adsorption was a pH-dependent process. The elimination efficiency of As(V) initially rose with pH increasing from 5.0 to 6.0, and afterward it remained relatively stable with pH increasing to 7.0. However, the elimination efficiency of As(V) gradually decreased as pH further rose from 7.0 to 10.0 (Figure 3A). For instance, the adsorption capacities of As(V) at pH 5.0, 7.0, and 10.0 were around 75.3, 84.3, and 35.4 mg/g, in each case. The pH-dependent adsorption efficiency of arsenic ions by the CLFO adsorbent might be attributed to the electrostatic interactions between As(V) ions and the adsorbent surface. Within the tested pH range, As(V) ions predominantly exist in the form of H2AsO4− and HAsO42− anions, respectively (pK1 = 2.2, pK2 = 6.98, pK3 = 11.5).38 Under elevated pH conditions, the concentration of hydroxide ions in the solution increases, promoting the deprotonation of surface functional groups (e.g., -OH) on the oxide. This deprotonation leads to a negatively charged surface, thereby enhancing the electrostatic repelling force between the negatively charged As(V) species and the adsorbent surface.38 Consequently, a decreased As(V) uptake capacity was observed at higher pH values. To be noted, a lower pH-sensitivity of the adsorption capacity of As(III) by the CLFO adsorbent than that of As(V) was observed in Figure 3B. The results might be accounted by the fact that As(III) mainly existed as neutral H3AsO3 molecules over the investigated pH ranges (pK1 = 9.2); hence, a weaker electrostatic interplay between the adsorbent surface and As(III) would be expected. Additionally, the adsorption capacity of both As(V) and As(III) decreased slightly as pH dropped from 6.0 to 5.0, possibly owing to the leaching of certain La ions. As depicted in Figure S2, slight leaching of La ions (about 1.4 mg/L) from the CLFO adsorbent was observed at pH 5.0 but not pH > 5.0. The leaching of Fe and Cu ions from the CLFO adsorbent was negligible over the pH range of 5.0–10.0, demonstrating that the CLFO adsorbent was sufficiently stable. Obviously, CLFO demonstrated significant potential as an adsorbent for eliminating arsenic from the majority of water sources, where the pH typically ranges from 6.5 to 8.5.

In general, the adsorption of anions takes place mainly via outer-sphere association or inner-sphere complex pathway. The former exhibited high sensitivity to ionic strength, and the adsorption would be inhibited by weakly adsorbing anions (e.g., NO3− or Cl−) due to competitive adsorption via electrostatic forces. In contrast, the latter was either inert toward ionic strength or displayed a higher uptake capacity under higher ionic strength.39 In this study, varying the ionic strength from 0.001 M to 0.1 M had a moderate effect on As(V) and As(III) adsorption by the CLFO adsorbent within the pH range of 5.0–10.0 (Figures 3A and 3B), suggesting that the inner-sphere adsorption mechanism was probably implicated in As(V) and As(III) adsorption therein.

Influence of coexisting ions on arsenic adsorption

The impacts of common ambient water cations (e.g., Ca2+) and anions (e.g., F− and CO32−) at different concentrations on arsenic adsorption by the CLFO adsorbent were analyzed. As shown in Figures 3C and 3D, the presence of Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, and SO42− ions had a negligible effect on As(V) adsorption by CLFO, whereas adsorption was partially impeded by F−, CO32−, and SiO32−. In contrast, the existence of PO43− remarkably hindered As(V) adsorption, particularly at elevated concentration levels. This outcome was primarily attributed to the competitive adsorption between PO43− and As(V) for active sites, which may be due to the fact that phosphorus and arsenic belong to the same main group in the periodic table, resulting in structural similarities. Therefore, the appropriate pretreatment of the water body is necessary when substantial concentrations of phosphate ions are present. For As(III), the As(III) removal was less influenced by coexisting ions in comparison to that of As(V), which might be due to the molecular structure of As(III) in water. The robust adsorption behavior of the CLFO adsorbent for both As(V) and As(III) across varying ion concentrations and types demonstrated its excellent selectivity toward arsenic species, highlighting its potential as a promising strategy for arsenic remediation in realistic waters.

Reusability and application

The reusability and optimum dosage of CLFO adsorbent were further studied to gain better insight into its possibility for practical application, and the results were displayed as Figures 3E and 3F. Cycle 0 in Figure 3E corresponded to the arsenic adsorption capacity of the virgin CLFO adsorbent. As could be seen, the adsorption capacity of both As(III) and As(V) gradually decreased as the cycling treatment increased from 0 to 4 cycles. For instance, the adsorption capacity of As(V) decreased from 125.6 mg/g to 101.4 mg/g as the cycling increased from 0 to 4 cycles, while that of As(III) declined from 68.9 mg/g to 32.3 mg/g. Despite that the adsorption capacity of As(V) and As(III) declined as recycling times increased, the total adsorption capacities of both As(V) and As(III) were >46% of the original adsorption capacity even after 4 cycles. As shown in Figure S3, no significant release of the relevant ions was observed throughout the entire recycling experiment, and the levels of released individual ions from the material were all below the respective safety limits. Moreover, a minor loss of the CLFO adsorbent itself (approximately 2%) was noted during each adsorption-desorption cycle (Figure 3E), which was attributed to operational errors during the filtration of the spent adsorbent. Therefore, it could be concluded that the synthesized adsorbent was capable of repeated use, and its active adsorption sites could be effectively restored through treatment with a NaOH solution. Additionally, at the laboratory scale, the synthetic cost of the material was approximately CNY 400 per 500 g based on chemical accounting, which was comparable to the price of commercially available activated alumina adsorbents (CNY 147–624 per 500 g, laboratory grade) and significantly lower than that of MOF materials. Furthermore, considering that the synthesized adsorbent could be recycled, its practical cost could be further reduced, indicating substantial potential for application in water treatment. Solutions containing arsenic (49 μg/L) and various ions (i.e., Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, NO3−, SiO32−, and PO43−) were prepared to simulate the authentic waters. The residual arsenic concentrations after treatment with CLFO adsorbent at different dosages were monitored. As shown in Figure 3F, the residual arsenic concentrations decreased as the CLFO adsorbent dosages increased, and the concentration was below the maximum permissible arsenic level (10 μg/L) at the adsorbent dosage of 30 mg/L. This suggested that the CLFO adsorbent was feasible for the removal of arsenic in natural water, and the optimum adsorbent dosage should be carefully evaluated during the treatment.

Zeta potential measurement

The zeta potentials of the CLFO adsorbent both prior to and following exposure to arsenic solution in relation to solution pH were presented in Figure 4A. It was observable that the isoelectric point (IEP) of the fresh adsorbent approximated 7.4, and it decreased to around 6.7 after As(V) adsorption. Comparatively, the IEP underwent minimal alteration after As(III) adsorption.40 The decreased IEP of CLFO after As(V) adsorption suggested a specific adsorption instead of a merely electrostatic interaction between the two. The relatively stable IEP of CLFO after As(III) adsorption might be explained by the pure adsorption of uncharged neutral As(III) molecules by CLFO, which did not notably change the IEP of adsorbents.41 The result otherwise indicated that the uncharged As(III) remained untransformed into As(V) throughout its adsorption procedure and primarily existed as neutral species under the tested pH conditions.

Analysis of Fourier transform-infrared spectra and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of the CLFO adsorbent prior to and post-reaction with arsenic were collected to validate the vibrations and rotations of molecular functional groups caused by energy transfer during the interaction between radiation and the sample. As illustrated in Figure 4B, regarding the pristine CLFO adsorbent, the absorption peak at 1633 cm−1 was linked with the bending of water molecules, signifying the existence of physically adsorbed water on the adsorbent.5 The peak at 1396 cm−1 was attributed to the vibration of adsorbed CO32− resulting from the dissolution of CO2 from the air.42 The absorption band observed at 1489 cm−1 served as a characteristic peak of the lanthanum (hydr)oxide, while two absorption peaks located at 1086 cm−1 and 1043 cm−1 were largely assigned to the bending vibration of the hydroxyl groups attached to M-OH.17,42,43 For the As(V)-treated sample, the signal of M-OH bending bands weakened or disappeared, with a new band emerging at 852 cm-1, and it could be attributed to the vibration of As(V)-O-M groups.17,44 However, no new band could be detected within the As(III)-loaded adsorbent’s spectrum compared to the original adsorbent, mainly due to the inherent difficulty in the detection of adsorbed As(III) by the FTIR spectroscopy.44 Similar observations were reported by Ren et al.45 and Li et al.46 for iron-zirconium binary oxide and Ce-Ti oxide, respectively.

The elemental chemical states within the topmost several atomic layers on solid surfaces are amenable to analysis via the XPS technique. Figure 4C presents the As3d core-level spectra for the CLFO adsorbent’s surface prior to and following the adsorption of As(V) and As(III). As could be seen, a distinct As3d core-level signal on the adsorbent became distinctly visible following reaction with As(V) or As(III), signifying the successful adsorption of As(V) or As(III) onto the adsorbent. The As3d binding energies for the adsorbent were 45.2 eV and 44.4 eV for As(V) and As(III), respectively. In general, reported binding energies for the As3d core level of As(V) and As(III) in arsenic oxides are 45.2–45.6 eV and 44.3–44.5 eV, correspondingly,21,47 This suggests that the arsenic species adsorbed onto the CLFO surface remained as As(V) and As(III) after uptake. For the Fe2P region of the virgin CLFO adsorbent (as illustrated in Figure 4D; Table S5), distinct characteristic peaks were observed at 711.1 eV and 724.8 eV, which corresponded exactly to the Fe2p3/2 and Fe2p1/2 of the γ-Fe2O3 particles. After interacting with As(V) and As(III), only negligible changes were detected in both the Fe2p peak shape and binding energy, indicating minimal interaction between arsenic species and the Fe phase. In other words, the γ-Fe2O3 component in the CLFO adsorbent might primarily serve a magnetic separation role. Figure 4E and Table S6 depicted the characteristic La3d high-resolution XPS spectra of the samples. For the virgin material, the characteristic peaks situated at 834.7 eV and 851.4 eV could be ascribed to the La3d5/2 and La3d3/2, as evidenced by the standard spectrum of lanthanum(III) oxide.13 After the adsorption of As(V) and As(III), the binding energies of the La3d peaks shifted to higher values (835.5 eV and 835.5 eV for La3d5/2, and 852.2 eV and 852.2 eV for La3d3/2), likely resulting from the formation of inner-sphere La-O-As complexes.48 As revealed by the high-resolution Cu2p spectra (Figure 4F and Table S7), the two peaks occurring at 933.1 eV and 953.0 eV were ascribed to the Cu2p3/2 and Cu2p1/2 levels of Cu2+, respectively, verifying the presence of CuO in the CLFO adsorbent.49 Following As(V) adsorption, obvious shifts in the Cu2p XPS peaks were observed to 933.7 eV and 954.3 eV, respectively. Similarly, after As(III) adsorption, slight shifts were detected at 933.5 eV and 953.5 eV. These findings indicated that Cu-associated hydroxyl groups played a crucial role in arsenic removal.50 In addition, the O1s deconvoluted spectra of the samples prior to and following reaction with As(V) and As(III) were shown in Figure 4G, and the spectra were subjected to fitting via a nonlinear least-square curve fitting program (XPSPEAK41 Software). The XPS spectrum of O1s showed the presence of three overlapped peaks at about 529.5, 531.0, and 532.0 eV, which corresponded to the binding energies of oxide oxygen (O2−), -OH, and H2O, respectively.38,51 The proportion of -OH groups in the pristine CLFO adsorbent was approximately 43.5%, whereas it dropped to around 40.7% and 41.6%, respectively, after the As(V) and As(III) adsorption. Meanwhile, the O2− percentage significantly increased from about 9.9% to 17.0% and 29.6%, respectively. Thus, it could be inferred that -OH groups on the CLFO adsorbent surface were partially replaced by As(V) or As(III) during adsorption, resulting in the formation of inner-sphere surface complexes.

Reasonable mechanisms for As(V) or As(III) adsorption by the CLFO adsorbent could be proposed as Figure 5 on the basis of the experimental findings from ionic strength and FTIR/XPS analysis. As(V) or As(III) in bulk solution were first transported to the protonated surface of the CLFO adsorbent, driven by convection or diffusion forces. Subsequently, the arsenic species are specifically adsorbed through the substitution of surface hydroxyl groups, generating bidentate binuclear inner-sphere surface complexes.

Schematic diagram of the arsenic removal mechanism by the CLFO adsorbent

Limitations of the study

Despite its high arsenic adsorption capacity and excellent magnetic separation performance in aqueous environments, the CLFO adsorbent still encounters several challenges. The current research was carried out under controlled laboratory conditions, without pilot-scale experiments to evaluate its effectiveness in real-world engineering applications. Furthermore, this study primarily investigated the adsorption potential of the CLFO adsorbent for As(V) and As(III), whereas natural water systems typically contain a variety of coexisting contaminants. Therefore, future studies will focus on establishing dynamic adsorption-magnetic separation models to assess its long-term stability and reusability in actual water bodies, as well as developing integration with complementary technologies (such as pre-treatment adsorption combined technology, and adsorption membrane combined technology) to explore integrated treatment strategies for multiple pollutants.

Method details

Preparation of magnetic CLFO adsorbent

The magnetic CLFO adsorbent was synthesized via the following procedures (shown in Figure 1A): (1) a mixed solution was first prepared by dissolving the required amount of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4⋅7H2O, 0.01 mol) and ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3⋅6H2O, 0.03 mol) in 400 mL of DI water. The mixed solution was heated to 60°C and stirred slowly. Under vigorous stirring, sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH, 1 mol/L) was added dropwise into the mixed solution at 60°C until the pH reached a value of 11. After that, the produced precipitate (named as γ-Fe2O3) was stirred continuously for 20 min, aged at 60°C for 2 h, rinsed several times with DI water, and then dried at 80°C for use in subsequent steps. (2) 1.0 g of the obtained γ-Fe2O3 particles were dispersed in polyethylene glycol solution (PEG, 5 %) by ultrasonic stirring for 30 min, and then a certain amount of lanthanum chloride (LaCl3·nH2O, 0.01 mol) and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4⋅5H2O, 0.01 mol) was added to this mixture at 60°C. 1 M NaOH solution was added under stirring to raise the pH of the solution to about 7.5 and to form magnetic CLFO particles, which were separated magnetically, washed several times with DI water, and dried at 60°C for 24 h. After crushing, the dry adsorbent was placed in a desiccator to maintain its function. The obtained material was in the form of a fine powder.

Characterization of magnetic CLFO adsorbent

The morphology of the synthesized magnetic CLFO adsorbent was characterized by a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-1400, Japan) and a field scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Hitachi S-4800, Japan). Elemental mappings were obtained with SEM-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) in a 20 min acquisition time. The crystalline structure of the formed CLFO adsorbent was analyzed by a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku D/Max-3A, Japan) using Ni-filtered copper K-alpha radiation. The material diffracted over a 2θ range from 10° to 80°. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined by an automatic physical adsorption apparatus (BET, Micromeritics ASAP 2000, USA) in the presence of N2 atmosphere. The specific saturation magnetization of the CLFO adsorbent was measured via a VSM model Lakeshore 7307 magnetic meter (Lakeshore Co., USA). In addition, the zeta potentials of CLFO particles before and after arsenic adsorption were measured through a zeta potential analyzer (Zetasizer 2000, Malvern, UK). The Fourier transform-infrared (FTIR) spectra of CLFO adsorbent before and after treatment with arsenic solution were collected on a Nicolet IS10 spectrometer (Thermo scientific, USA) using a transmission model. Samples for FTIR determination were prepared via grinding 1 mg of the adsorbent with 80 mg of spectral grade KBr in an agate mortar. All IR measurements were carried out at room temperature. The chemical state of elements in samples were conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (ESCALab-220i-XL, VG Scientific, UK) with a monochromatic Al Ka X-ray source (1486.6 eV). C1s peaks were used as an inner standard calibration peak at 284.7 eV. For wide-scan spectra, an energy range of 0-1100 eV was used with pass energy of 80 eV and step size of 1 eV. The high-resolution scans were conducted according to the peak being examined with pass energy of 40 eV and step size of 0.05 eV. XPS data process and peak fitting were performed using a nonlinear least-squares curve-fitting program (XPSPEAK41 Software).

Batch adsorption and desorption experiments

In the following series of experiments, 2 mg of synthesized magnetic CLFO adsorbent was added to 100 mL plastic vessels containing 50 mL of 1-30 mg/L arsenic solution unless otherwise specified. The ionic strength of the solution was controlled by NaNO3 (0.01 M). 0.1 M HNO3 and NaOH solutions were used to adjust the pH value (7.0 ± 0.1) during the entire reaction period. The mixture was shaken in a thermostatic shaker (170 r/min) at room temperature (25 ± 1°C) for 24 h. After the treatment process, all suspensions were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and analyzed for As concentration by an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (7800, Agilent Co., USA). Prior to analysis, the aqueous samples were acidified and diluted to a suitable arsenic concentration range using 3% concentrated HNO3. The calibration curve of arsenic element and the key parameter indicators (such as linearity, LOD, BEC and R value) were shown in Figure S4.

For the adsorption kinetics, the experiments were carried out by loading 20 mg of the CLFO adsorbent in a 1 L glass vessel containing 500 mL of 1-5 mg/L arsenic solution. Approximately 5 mL aliquots were taken from the vessel at specified time intervals until 24 h. To investigate the influence of pH and ionic strength on arsenic adsorption, experiments were performed at designated pH values (5.0-10.0), and the ionic strength of the solutions was varied from 0.001 to 0.1 mol/L by adding NaNO3. The effect of common coexisting ions Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl-, SO42-, F-, CO32-, SiO32-, and PO43- on arsenic adsorption was assessed by batch experiments with varying concentrations of coexisting ions from 0.1 to 10 mmol/L. For the regeneration and reusability of the CLFO adsorbent, (1) 200 mg of the CLFO adsorbent was added into a glass vessel containing 5 L of solution with about 15 mg/L arsenic. The suspension was stirred for 12 h, and its pH was maintained at pH 7.0 ± 0.1. Then, the spent adsorbent was separated from the solution, and the equilibrium As concentration in the filtrates was measured. (2) The As-containing CLFO adsorbent was regenerated using 1 M NaOH for 4 h. Then, the adsorbent was filtered, washed, and dried for use in the next adsorption-desorption cycle (four cycles). In the simulated water tests, the arsenic stock solution was diluted to 50 mL of about 50 μg/L arsenic solution with Songhua river water (Heilongjiang Province) in 100 mL glass vessels. A detailed description of Songhua river water quality is provided in Table S1. The dosage of the CLFO adsorbent ranged from 1 mg/L to 100 mg/L.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In the manuscript, all experimental data presented are derived from the mean values of triplicate experimental readings. The statistical analysis and data processing were conducted using OriginPro 2024b Learning Edition software.
