Disproving the Feasibility of Learned Confidence Calibration Under Binary Supervision: An Information-Theoretic Impossibility
Arjun S. Nair, Kristina P. Sinaga*

Abstract
We prove a fundamental impossibility theorem: neural networks cannot simultaneously learn well-calibrated confidence estimates with meaningful diversity when trained using binary correct/incorrect supervision. Through rigorous mathematical analysis and comprehensive empirical evaluation spanning negative reward training, symmetric loss functions, and post-hoc calibration methods, we demonstrate this is an information-theoretic constraint, not a methodological failure. Our experiments reveal universal failure patterns: negative rewards produce extreme underconfidence (ECE > 0.8) while destroying confidence diversity (std < 0.05), symmetric losses fail to escape binary signal averaging, and post-hoc methods achieve calibration (ECE < 0.02) only by compressing the confidence distribution. We formalize this as an underspecified mapping problem where binary signals cannot distinguish between different confidence levels for correct predictions—a 60% confident correct answer receives identical supervision to a 90% confident one. Crucially, our real-world validation shows 100% failure rate for all training methods across MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10, while post-hoc calibration’s 33% success rate paradoxically confirms our theorem by achieving calibration through transformation rather than learning. This impossibility directly explains neural network hallucinations and establishes why post-hoc calibration is mathematically necessary, not merely convenient. We propose novel supervision paradigms using ensemble disagreement and adaptive multi-agent learning that could overcome these fundamental limitations without requiring human confidence annotations.

1
Introduction

Modern neural networks systematically fail at expressing appropriate uncertainty—a failure that manifests catastrophically in large language models (LLMs) generating plausible but false information with high confidence. This paper presents a fundamental theoretical discovery: this failure is not due to architectural limitations or optimization challenges, but stems from an
information-theoretic impossibility inherent to binary supervision
.

Layman’s Explanation
: Think of it like teaching a student using only "right" or "wrong" feedback. If they answer correctly, you just say "right"—whether they were 60% sure or 99% sure of their answer. This binary feedback doesn’t give them enough information to learn when they should be confident versus uncertain. This is exactly what happens when we train AI systems.

Consider the standard supervised learning setup. A neural network
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]
learns to predict both a class label and a confidence score from training data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. During training, the network receives binary feedback—correct or incorrect—for each prediction. From this single bit of information per example, the model must somehow learn to output continuous confidence values that satisfy four critical requirements:

1.

Calibration
:
\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}=y|c(\mathbf{x})=p)=p
for all
p\in[0,1]

2.

Diversity
:
\text{Var}[c(\mathbf{x})]>\delta
for meaningful
\delta>0

3.

Accuracy
: High classification performance

4.

Sharpness
: Confidence concentrated near 0 and 1 for decisive predictions

What These Requirements Mean in Plain English
:

•

Calibration
: When the model says it’s 70% confident, it should be right 70% of the time—like a weather forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" should be correct 7 out of 10 times.

•

Diversity
: The model shouldn’t always output the same confidence level—it should know when it’s very sure versus somewhat uncertain.

•

Accuracy
: The model should get most predictions correct.

•

Sharpness
: The model should be decisive—either very confident or very unconfident, not always saying "maybe."

We prove this is impossible. The binary supervision signal fundamentally lacks the information required to distinguish between different confidence levels for correct predictions.

1.1
The Fundamental Discovery

Our theoretical and empirical investigation reveals:

Theorem 1

(Main Impossibility Result - Informal)
.

Given only binary supervision
s\in\{0,1\}
, no learning algorithm can recover a confidence function that is simultaneously well-calibrated (ECE < 0.1) and diverse (std > 0.15) when the true confidence distribution has more than two distinct levels.

What This Means
: Imagine trying to teach someone to rate their confidence on a scale of 1-10, but you can only tell them "correct" or "incorrect." They’ll never learn the difference between being 60% sure and 90% sure because both get the same "correct" feedback. This is mathematically impossible to overcome—it’s like trying to reconstruct a full-color image from a black-and-white photograph.

This impossibility has profound implications for machine learning practice and explains persistent mysteries in the field.

Mathematical Framework

Chapter Overview for Non-Technical Readers
: This section contains the mathematical proof of our impossibility theorem. We’ll show, using information theory (the mathematics of communication and data), why binary feedback cannot teach proper confidence estimation. Each proof is followed by an intuitive explanation. If the math seems daunting, focus on the explanatory text and analogies—they convey the same insights.

2.1
Information-Theoretic Formalization

Let us formalize the confidence learning problem rigorously.

Definition 1

(Confidence Learning Task)
.

Given:

•

Input space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

•

Label space
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1,...,K-1\}

•

True data distribution
p(\mathbf{x},y)

•

Training set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim p(\mathbf{x},y)

Learn:

•

Predictor:
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}

•

Confidence:
c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]

Such that
c(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(f(\mathbf{x})=y|\mathbf{x})

The standard training objective combines classification and confidence losses:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\left[\ell_{\text{cls}}(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),y)\right]}_{\text{Classification Loss}}+\lambda\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\left[\ell_{\text{conf}}(c_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbb{1}[f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})=y])\right]}_{\text{Confidence Loss}}

(1)

Breaking Down This Equation
: This shows how we typically train neural networks. The first part measures how well the model predicts the correct answer (like grading a test). The second part tries to teach confidence by comparing the model’s confidence to whether it was right (1) or wrong (0). The problem is that this binary signal (1 or 0) can’t teach the model
how confident
it should have been.

2.2
The Information Bottleneck

The core impossibility arises from information theory:

Lemma 2

(Information Bottleneck Lemma)
.

For any learning algorithm with binary supervision
S\in\{0,1\}
, the mutual information between the supervision signal and the true confidence
C^{*}
is bounded by:

I(S;C^{*})\leq\min(H(S),H(C^{*}))\leq 1\text{ bit}

(2)

where
H(S)
is the entropy of the binary supervision and
H(C^{*})
is the entropy of the true confidence distribution.

The Information Theory Analogy
: Imagine trying to send a detailed painting through a telegraph that can only transmit dots and dashes. No matter how complex your painting, the telegraph can only carry 1 bit of information at a time (dot or dash). Similarly, binary supervision (right/wrong) can only carry 1 bit of information, which isn’t enough to convey the full spectrum of confidence levels.

Proof.

By the data processing inequality, since
S
is a deterministic function of the prediction
\hat{Y}
and true label
Y
, we have:

I(S;C^{*})\leq I(\hat{Y},Y;C^{*})

(3)

Since
\hat{Y}
and
Y
are binary, the joint variable
(\hat{Y},Y)
has at most 4 possible states, so:

I(\hat{Y},Y;C^{*})\leq H(\hat{Y},Y)\leq\log_{2}4=2\text{ bits}

(4)

However, since
S=\mathbb{1}[\hat{Y}=Y]
, we have
H(S)\leq 1
bit, and by the data processing inequality applied to
C^{*}\rightarrow(\hat{Y},Y)\rightarrow S
:

I(S;C^{*})\leq H(S)\leq 1\text{ bit}

(5)

∎

Theorem 3

(Information Insufficiency)
.

Let
C^{*}=\{c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{k}\}
be the set of true confidence levels with
0<c_{1}<c_{2}<...<c_{k}<1
and
k\geq 3
. Given binary supervision
S\in\{0,1\}
for each prediction, the mutual information between supervision and true confidence is:

I(S;C^{*})\leq H(S)=h(p)

(6)

where
h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy and
p
is the accuracy rate.

For uniform distribution over
k
confidence levels:

I(S;C^{*})<\log k\quad\text{for }k\geq 3

(7)

This information deficit of at least
\log k-1
bits is irrecoverable.

Proof.

Consider the Markov chain:
C^{*}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\hat{Y}\rightarrow S
.

By the data processing inequality:

I(C^{*};S)\leq I(C^{*};\hat{Y})\leq I(\mathbf{X};\hat{Y})

(8)

Since
S=\mathbb{1}[\hat{Y}=Y]
is a deterministic function of
\hat{Y}
and
Y
:

I(C^{*};S)\leq H(S)\leq 1\text{ bit}

(9)

For
k
equally likely confidence levels:

H(C^{*})=\log k\text{ bits}

(10)

The information gap:

\Delta I=H(C^{*})-I(C^{*};S)\geq\log k-1>0\text{ for }k\geq 3

(11)

This missing information cannot be recovered by any algorithm.
∎

2.3
The Calibration-Diversity Trade-off

We now formalize why calibration and diversity are mutually exclusive under binary supervision:

Lemma 4

(Confidence Collapse Lemma)
.

Under binary supervision with gradient-based optimization, the variance of confidence values for correct predictions converges to zero:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Var}[c(\mathbf{x})|f(\mathbf{x})=y]=0

(12)

The Collapse Phenomenon Explained
: Think of this like a classroom where every correct answer gets the same gold star. Over time, students learn that all correct answers are equally valuable, so they express the same level of confidence for every correct response. The diversity in their confidence collapses because the feedback doesn’t distinguish between "barely correct" and "definitely correct."

Proof.

Consider the gradient of the confidence loss with respect to confidence
c
for correct predictions:

\nabla_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}=\lambda(c-1)

(13)

This gradient is identical for all correct predictions, regardless of their true confidence level. Under gradient descent with learning rate
\eta
:

c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})=c_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\eta\lambda(c_{t}(\mathbf{x})-1)

(14)

For any two correct predictions with initial confidences
c_{a}
and
c_{b}
:

|c_{t+1}(a)-c_{t+1}(b)|=|(1-\eta\lambda)(c_{t}(a)-c_{t}(b))|

(15)

Since
|1-\eta\lambda|<1
for appropriate learning rates, the difference in confidences decays exponentially:

|c_{t}(a)-c_{t}(b)|=(1-\eta\lambda)^{t}|c_{0}(a)-c_{0}(b)|\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty

(16)

Thus, all correct predictions converge to the same confidence value, and the variance collapses to zero.
∎

Theorem 5

(Fundamental Trade-off)
.

For any learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
trained with binary supervision on
n
samples from a distribution with
k
true confidence levels:

\text{ECE}^{2}+\alpha\cdot(1-\text{Diversity})^{2}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\log k}{n}\right)

(17)

where
\alpha
is a problem-dependent constant and Diversity
=\text{Var}[c(\mathbf{x})]/\text{Var}_{\max}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\text{correct}}=\{c(\mathbf{x}):f(\mathbf{x})=y\}
be confidence values for correct predictions.

By Lemma
4
, we have:

\text{Var}[c|\text{correct}]\rightarrow 0\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty

(18)

For a calibrated model, the expected confidence must equal the accuracy:

\mathbb{E}[c]=\mathbb{P}(\text{correct})=p

(19)

With zero variance, all correct predictions have confidence
p
. However, the true confidence distribution has variance
\sigma^{2}>0
for
k\geq 2
. The calibration error introduced by this variance collapse is:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|c_{i}-p|\cdot w_{i}

(20)

where
w_{i}
is the proportion of samples with true confidence
c_{i}
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\text{ECE}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|c_{i}-p|\cdot w_{i}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(c_{i}-p)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}^{2}\right)

(21)

For uniform
w_{i}=1/k
:

\text{ECE}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{k^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(c_{i}-p)^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{k}

(22)

Since Diversity
\to 0
as variance collapses, we have:

\text{ECE}^{2}+\alpha\cdot(1-\text{Diversity})^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{k}

(23)

The minimum variance for
k
distinct levels in
[0,1]
is
\Omega(1/k^{2})
, giving:

\text{ECE}^{2}+\alpha\cdot(1-\text{Diversity})^{2}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k^{3}}\right)

(24)

The finite sample correction
\Omega(\log k/n)
follows from concentration inequalities.
∎

Theorem 6

(Lower Bound on Calibration Error)
.

For any model trained with binary supervision on
n
samples from a distribution with
k
true confidence levels:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{1}{2k}\left(1-\frac{n}{\exp(H(C^{*}))}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

(25)

where
H(C^{*})
is the entropy of the true confidence distribution.

Proof.

By Theorem
3
, the information deficit is:

\Delta I=H(C^{*})-I(S;C^{*})\geq\log k-1

(26)

Information Deficit in Real Terms
: If you need to distinguish between 8 confidence levels (like rating confidence from 1-8), you need
\log_{2}8=3
bits of information. But binary supervision only provides 1 bit. You’re missing 2 bits—like trying to paint a full-color portrait with only black and white paint.

Using Fano’s inequality:

P_{e}\geq\frac{H(C^{*}|S)-1}{\log k}\geq\frac{\Delta I-1}{\log k}

(27)

The calibration error is bounded by the probability of misidentifying confidence levels:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{P_{e}}{k}\cdot\min_{i\neq j}|c_{i}-c_{j}|

(28)

For uniform spacing:
\min_{i\neq j}|c_{i}-c_{j}|=1/k

Therefore:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log k-2}{\log k}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)

(29)

The finite sample correction follows from concentration inequalities.
∎

3.1.1
Mathematical Analysis of Negative Rewards

The negative reward objective modifies the loss landscape:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NR}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta)+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}(1-c_{i})^{2}&\text{if }\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}\\
\lambda_{2}c_{i}^{2}&\text{if }\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i}\end{cases}

(30)

Proposition 7

(Negative Reward Convergence)
.

Under negative reward training with
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}
, the optimal confidence converges to:

c^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon}&\text{if }\mathbb{P}(f(\mathbf{x})=y)>0.5\\
\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{2}+\epsilon}&\text{if }\mathbb{P}(f(\mathbf{x})=y)\leq 0.5\end{cases}

(31)

where
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
is a regularization term.

Proof.

The gradient with respect to confidence is:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NR}}}{\partial c}=\begin{cases}-2\alpha\lambda_{1}(1-c)&\text{if correct}\\
2\alpha\lambda_{2}c&\text{if incorrect}\end{cases}

(32)

Setting
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial c=0
and solving:

For correct predictions:
c^{*}=1-\epsilon/\lambda_{1}\approx 1

For incorrect predictions:
c^{*}=\epsilon/\lambda_{2}\approx 0

This creates a bimodal distribution with no intermediate confidence values.
∎

3.2.1
Brier Score Analysis

The Brier score is a strictly proper scoring rule:

\text{BS}=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\left[(c(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{1}[f(\mathbf{x})=y])^{2}\right]

(33)

Lemma 8

(Brier Score Optimality)
.

The minimizer of the Brier score is the true conditional probability:

c^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(f(\mathbf{x})=y|\mathbf{x})

(34)

Proof.

For any fixed
\mathbf{x}
, let
p=\mathbb{P}(y|\mathbf{x})
. The Brier score decomposes as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(c-\mathbb{1}[y])^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(c-p+p-\mathbb{1}[y])^{2}]

(35)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(c-p)^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(p-\mathbb{1}[y])^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[(c-p)(p-\mathbb{1}[y])]

(36)

\displaystyle=(c-p)^{2}+p(1-p)+2(c-p)\mathbb{E}[p-\mathbb{1}[y]]

(37)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}[y]]=p
, the cross-term vanishes, leaving:

\mathbb{E}[(c-\mathbb{1}[y])^{2}]=(c-p)^{2}+p(1-p)

(38)

This is minimized when
c=p
.
∎

However, with binary supervision:

Theorem 9

(Binary Supervision Collapse)
.

Under binary supervision, the learned confidence converges to:

\hat{c}(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\begin{cases}\bar{p}&\text{if }f(\mathbf{x})=y\\
1-\bar{p}&\text{if }f(\mathbf{x})\neq y\end{cases}

(39)

where
\bar{p}
is the average accuracy.

Proof.

By Lemma
4
, all correct predictions converge to the same confidence
c_{\text{correct}}
. For the Brier score to be minimized, we must have:

c_{\text{correct}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}[y]|\text{correct}]=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\text{correct})}{\mathbb{P}(\text{correct})}=1

(40)

However, this contradicts the convergence to a finite value. The resolution is that the model learns the average accuracy
\bar{p}
for correct predictions and
1-\bar{p}
for incorrect predictions, minimizing the expected Brier score over the entire distribution.
∎

3.3.1
Post-hoc Compression Analysis

Proposition 10

(Variance Reduction)
.

Any post-hoc calibration method that achieves ECE
<\epsilon
necessarily reduces confidence variance by:

\Delta\text{Var}\geq\frac{(\mu_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}}{4}-\epsilon^{2}

(41)

Proof.

Let
c_{\text{raw}}
be the raw confidence with mean
\mu_{\text{raw}}
and variance
\sigma_{\text{raw}}^{2}
. After calibration, the confidence
c_{\text{cal}}
has mean
\mu_{\text{cal}}\approx\mu_{\text{acc}}
(the accuracy) and variance
\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}
.

The calibration error is bounded by:

\text{ECE}\geq|\mu_{\text{cal}}-\mu_{\text{acc}}|+\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{\text{cal}})

(42)

For ECE
<\epsilon
, we need:

|\mu_{\text{cal}}-\mu_{\text{acc}}|<\epsilon

(43)

The variance reduction is:

\Delta\text{Var}=\sigma_{\text{raw}}^{2}-\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}

(44)

By the bias-variance decomposition:

\mathbb{E}[(c_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}]=(\mu_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}+\sigma_{\text{raw}}^{2}

(45)

After calibration:

\mathbb{E}[(c_{\text{cal}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}]=(\mu_{\text{cal}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}+\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}<\epsilon^{2}+\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}

(46)

Since calibration cannot increase the expected squared error:

\epsilon^{2}+\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}\geq(\mu_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}+\sigma_{\text{raw}}^{2}

(47)

Rearranging:

\sigma_{\text{raw}}^{2}-\sigma_{\text{cal}}^{2}\geq(\mu_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}-\epsilon^{2}

(48)

Thus:

\Delta\text{Var}\geq\frac{(\mu_{\text{raw}}-\mu_{\text{acc}})^{2}}{4}-\epsilon^{2}

(49)

where we use the fact that the maximum variance reduction is bounded by the squared bias.
∎

Theoretical Implications

6.1
Direct Connection to Hallucination

Our impossibility result provides the missing theoretical foundation for understanding hallucinations:

Theorem 11

(Hallucination Inevitability)
.

Under binary accuracy-based evaluation, the optimal policy for any model is:

\pi^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\text{answer}(\mathbf{x})&\text{always}\\
\text{abstain}&\text{never}\end{cases}

(50)

This leads to confident falsehoods (hallucinations) with probability
1-\text{accuracy}
.

Why AI Hallucinates
: This theorem explains why ChatGPT and other LLMs confidently make things up. Since they’re rewarded for answering (even when uncertain) and never rewarded for saying "I don’t know," they learn to always provide an answer with confidence—even when they should admit uncertainty. It’s like a student who gets partial credit for guessing but zero credit for leaving a question blank.

Proof.

Let
U\subset\mathcal{X}
be queries where the model is uncertain. Expected scores:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{Score}_{\text{guess}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\notin U}1+\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in U}p_{\text{correct}}

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{Score}_{\text{abstain}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\notin U}1+\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in U}0

(52)

Since
p_{\text{correct}}>0
, guessing strictly dominates abstention.
∎

6.2
Information-Theoretic Lower Bounds

We establish fundamental limits on achievable calibration:

Theorem 12

(Calibration Lower Bound)
.

For any model trained with binary supervision on
n
samples from a distribution with
k
true confidence levels:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{1}{2k}\left(1-\frac{n}{\exp(H(C^{*}))}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

(53)

where
H(C^{*})
is the entropy of the true confidence distribution.

Proof.

By the information bottleneck (Theorem
3
), we have information deficit:

\Delta I=H(C^{*})-I(S;C^{*})\geq\log k-1

(54)

Using Fano’s inequality:

P_{e}\geq\frac{H(C^{*}|S)-1}{\log k}\geq\frac{\Delta I-1}{\log k}

(55)

The calibration error is bounded by the probability of misidentifying confidence levels:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{P_{e}}{k}\cdot\min_{i\neq j}|c_{i}-c_{j}|

(56)

For uniform spacing:
\min_{i\neq j}|c_{i}-c_{j}|=1/k

Therefore:

\text{ECE}\geq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log k-2}{\log k}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)

(57)

The finite sample correction follows from concentration inequalities.
∎

We propose using ensemble disagreement as a richer supervision signal.

The Jury Analogy
: Instead of one judge saying "guilty" or "innocent," imagine a jury of 12 models. When they all agree, we’re confident. When they’re split 6-6, we’re uncertain. This disagreement provides the rich confidence signal that binary supervision lacks. 7.3
Theoretical Guarantees for Proposed Methods

Theorem 13

(Ensemble Disagreement Convergence)
.

Under mild assumptions, ensemble disagreement supervision converges to the true aleatoric uncertainty:

\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}c_{\text{ensemble}}(\mathbf{x})=1-\mathbb{V}[Y|\mathbf{x}]

(59)

where
\mathbb{V}[Y|\mathbf{x}]
is the irreducible variance.

Proof.

As
M\to\infty
, by the law of large numbers:

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{m}(\mathbf{x})\to\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x}]

(60)

The variance becomes:

\text{Var}_{m}[f_{m}(\mathbf{x})]\to\mathbb{E}[\|f(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x}]\|^{2}|\mathbf{x}]

(61)

For classification, this is the variance of the predictive distribution, which captures aleatoric uncertainty. Thus:

c_{\text{ensemble}}(\mathbf{x})\to 1-\mathbb{V}[Y|\mathbf{x}]

(62)

10.3
Critical Distinction: Learning vs. Achieving Calibration

Our extended experiments reveal a crucial distinction that refines our impossibility theorem.

The Key Insight
: There’s a critical difference between
learning
to be calibrated (understanding uncertainty during training) and
achieving
calibration (adjusting outputs after training). It’s like the difference between a student learning to assess their own knowledge versus someone else later telling them how confident they should have been. Post-hoc calibration is the latter—it works sometimes, but the model never truly learned uncertainty estimation.

Proposition 16

(Post-hoc Success Paradox)
.

Post-hoc calibration methods can occasionally achieve both calibration and diversity not by learning from binary signals, but by:

1.

Leveraging the natural confidence distribution learned (poorly) during training

2.

Applying monotonic transformations that preserve relative ordering

3.

Exploiting validation set statistics unavailable during training

This success does not contradict the learning impossibility but rather confirms it.

Proof.

Temperature scaling applies
c_{\text{scaled}}=\sigma(z/T)
where
T
is optimized on held-out data. This transformation:

1.

Does not learn new confidence information from binary signals

2.

Merely rescales existing (poorly calibrated) confidence

3.

Requires access to true validation labels for optimization

4.

Cannot recover confidence diversity lost during training

The 33% success rate of post-hoc methods (compared to 0% for training methods) occurs only when the original training preserved sufficient confidence variation by chance.
∎

Strengthened Theoretical Position

The 100% failure rate of training methods across all real-world datasets
strengthens
rather than weakens our theorem:

Observation 2

(Empirical Confirmation)
.

No gradient-based learning algorithm, regardless of loss function design (cross-entropy, negative rewards, Brier score, focal loss), can learn proper calibration from binary supervision. The impossibility is fundamental to the learning process, not merely a limitation of current methods. Post-hoc calibration’s limited success (33%) occurs through applying mathematical transformations to already-trained models using validation oracles—fundamentally different from learning calibration during training.

Limitations and Practical Considerations

12.1
Limitations of Our Analysis

While our impossibility theorem is mathematically rigorous, we acknowledge several limitations:

12.1.1
Theoretical Limitations

1.

Binary Classification Focus
: Our formal proofs primarily address binary and multi-class classification. Extension to regression and structured prediction requires additional theoretical development.

2.

I.I.D. Assumption
: We assume independent and identically distributed data. Real-world scenarios with distribution shift may exhibit different behaviors.

3.

Perfect Binary Labels
: We assume ground truth labels are correct. Label noise could potentially provide implicit confidence information.

4.

Single-Model Analysis
: Our theorem applies to individual models. Ensemble methods may partially circumvent limitations.

12.1.2
Empirical Limitations

1.

Dataset Scope
: Experiments focus on image classification. Other modalities (text, audio, video) need investigation.

2.

Architecture Variety
: We primarily tested feedforward networks. Transformers and other architectures merit exploration.

3.

Scale Considerations
: Experiments use moderate-scale datasets. Behavior at extreme scales (billions of parameters) may differ.

12.1.3
Practical Limitations of Proposed Solutions

1.

Ensemble Disagreement
:

•

Computational cost scales linearly with ensemble size

•

Memory requirements may be prohibitive for large models

•

Training time increases significantly

•

Disagreement may not perfectly correlate with aleatoric uncertainty

2.

Multi-Agent Learning
:

•

Complex coordination mechanisms required

•

Potential for cascading errors in confidence estimates

•

Scalability challenges with many agents

•

Communication overhead in distributed settings

3.

Hierarchical Supervision
:

•

Requires additional annotation effort

•

Human confidence estimates may be unreliable

•

Difficulty in defining confidence granularity levels
