Association of BCAAs/BCKAs with pancreatic steatosis in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: A cross‐sectional study
Authors: Jie Gao, Pijun Cheng, Fusong Jiang, Wenmin Li, Yingying Shi, Wenjun Wang, Liang Wang, Tao Lei, Yunxiang Zang, Jun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo examine the association between branched‐chain α‐keto acids (BCKAs) and pancreatic steatosis in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM).MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted from January 2023 to March 2025. Upper abdominal computerized tomography was performed, pancreatic volume (PV), PV index (PVI), CT attenuation of pancreas (CTAP), and CTAp/s were then calculated. Levels of serum BCKAs and BCAAs were determined by ELISA kits. Partial correlation and linear regression analysis were performed to investigate the relationship between BCKA and pancreatic parameters. Mediating models were employed to explore the role of BMI in the relationship between BCKA and pancreatic parameters.ResultsThere were significant differences in median PV, PVI, CTAp, and CTAp/s according to BCKA tertiles (all p < 0.05). BCKAs were positively associated with PV and PVI but negatively correlated with CTAp and CTAp/s after adjustment for age and gender (all P < 0.05). Linear regression analysis showed that BCKA was associated with PV, CTAp, and CTAp/s after adjustment for confounders in all models (Model 1, Model 2, Model 3) (all P < 0.05). There existed no significant association of BCAA with pancreatic parameters after adjustment for confounders. Mediation analysis suggested that BMI played approximately 20% indirect effect in the relationship between BCKAs and PV or CTAp/s.ConclusionsSerum BCKAs rather than BCAAs were associated with PV and CTAp in T2DM patients, indicating an association between BCAA malmetabolism and pancreatic steatosis. Additionally, obesity may serve as a mediator between BCKAs and pancreatic steatosis, which should be validated by prospective cohort.Keywords: branched‐chain α‐keto acids, pancreatic steatosis, type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are essential amino acids and are found in 15–25% of protein‐based diets 1 . In addition to protein synthesis and tissue repair, BCAAs were also an important energy source and raw material for de novo synthesis of glucose and lipid 2 . However, excessive intake or abnormal metabolism of BCAAs was associated with many morbidities, such as obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and even pancreatic cancer 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 . Recently, Ma et al reported that increased levels of serum BCAAs were not inevitably associated with high‐fat diet (HFD)‐induced obesity in mice with adipose tissue knockout of Bcat2, an enzyme that converts BCAAs to branched‐chain keto acids (BCKAs) 9 . Further investigation indicated that BCKAs (like BCAAs) could also suppress the insulin signaling pathway by activation of the mammalian target of the rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, resulting in insulin resistance (IR) 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 . On the other hand, reducing the abundance of BCAA and BCKA could markedly attenuate IR in ob/ob mice 9 , 10 , 11 . BCAA‐BCKA axis was hence proposed as a cause of obesity and insulin resistance 9 , 10 .

Pancreatic steatosis is characterized as adipocyte infiltration and intracellular fat deposition in acinar and islet cells in the absence of heavy alcohol consumption 13 , 14 . Previous studies indicated that CT scan was a reliable noninvasive method to evaluate pancreatic steatosis in human beings 15 , 16 , 17 . Data showed that the CT attenuation was negatively correlated with histological pancreatic fat content 17 . In our previous studies, reduced pancreatic volume (PV) was observed in patients with type 1 diabetes, latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA), and long‐term type 2 diabetes versus nondiabetic subjects 15 , 16 . On the other hand, researchers mimicked pancreatic steatosis model using high‐fat diet in rodents 18 . Also, we reported previously that restriction of BCAA intake attenuated pancreatic steatosis in high‐fat diet‐fed mice 19 . However, it was unclear about the association of BCAA‐BCKA axis with pancreatic steatosis in human beings. Furthermore, our previous research indicated that PV was closely related to obesity parameters such as BMI, waist circumference, and hip circumference 16 . Therefore, we explored the relationship of circulating BCAAs and its primary metabolite BCKAs with pancreatic steatosis in patients with type 2 diabetes, as well as whether BMI played a role in the relationship between BCAA and pancreatic steatosis using the mediating analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The BCAAs and catabolites associated disease exploration (B‐CARE) study aimed to investigate the association of BCAAs and their catabolites with obesity, micro‐ and macro‐vascular complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. Patients aged 18–75 years were included in this study. Those with a history of acute or chronic pancreatitis (n = 2), cancer (n = 3), severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (alanine transaminase ≥105 μ/L; serum creatinine ≥450 μmol/L, n = 5), or unclear images (n = 39) were excluded. Finally, 513 patients with type 2 diabetes were enrolled in the final analysis. The prescriptions including insulin, biguanides, and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) were recorded at the time of enrollment.

All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. Informed consent was obtained from all patients for being included in the study.

Anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Height and weight were measured using standard methods. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 centimeter, and weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as weight divided by height squared. BSA (m2) was calculated as 0.0061 × height (cm) + 0.0128 × weight (kg) − 0.1529. Venous blood samples were drawn after an overnight fast (10–16 h) for test of biochemical profiles, BCAAs and BCKAs. High‐performance liquid chromatography (HLC‐73G7; Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan) was used to determine glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Lipid profiles were measured using an auto‐analyzer (Hitachi 7,600; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Fasting C‐peptide (FCP) was measured using electrochemical luminescence, and plasma glucose was measured using the glucose oxidase method (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Serum BCAA (abcam, ab83374, USA) and BCKA (Fusheng Biologicals, A137748, Shanghai, China) were tested by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay according to manufacturer's instruction. The fibrosis‐4 index (FIB‐4), a noninvasive marker of liver fibrosis, was calculated as follows: FIB−4=ageyears×aspartate aminotransferaseASTU/Lplatelet10^9×√alanine aminotransferaseALTU/L.. Patients were divided into three groups according to tertiles of serum BCKAs as: lower tertile, BCKAs <114 μmol/L; middle tertile, 114 μmol/L ≤ BCKAs <131 μmol/L; and upper tertile, BCKAs ≥131 μmol/L.

Assessment of pancreatic volume and CT attenuation

All the subjects underwent upper abdominal CT scans with the method described previously 16 . Scan images were coded and analyzed by an experienced technician, the outline of the region of interest was annotated by freehand using a Siemens Virtuoso workstation, generating a pancreatic area (cm2) and CT attenuation (HU) for each slice. The pancreatic volume (PV) was calculated by the sum of the segmental area multiplied by the interval between each slice (5 mm). PV index was calculated as PV divided by BSA. The average CT attenuation of all slices was used for final analysis. P/S was derived from CT attenuation of pancreas divided by that of spleen. The staff was blinded to the clinical information of the patients when the images were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Differences in medians were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test among three groups. Differences in proportions were tested using the chi‐square test. The general linear model was utilized to compare pancreatic indices according to tertiles of serum BCKAs and BCAAs, and LSD method was applied for post hoc between‐group comparison. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association of pancreatic parameters with clinical features. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to examine the association between pancreatic steatosis indices and BCAAs‐BCKAs axis after adjusting for age and gender (Model 1), and then duration of diabetes, smoking status, drinking status, HTN, HbA1c, TG, HDL‐C, HOMA‐IR, FCP, FIB‐4, Insulin, biguanide, GLP‐1RA (Model 2), and further BMI (Model 3). Additionally, we applied mediating models based on bootstrapping calculations to decompose the association between BCKAs and pancreatic parameters into direct and indirect effects, with BMI included as a potential bridge factor. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.); two ‐ sided p <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Comparison of pancreatic parameters among patients with various BCKAs tertiles

There were significant differences in the median PV (cm3), PV index, CT attenuation and P/S among individuals grouped by tertiles of BCKAs (all p < 0.05). The corresponding mean PVs were 45.5 cm3 (lower tertile), 44.7 cm3 (middle tertile), and 55.7 cm3 (upper tertile), respectively (P for trend <0.001). However, an opposite trend was observed concerning CT attenuation and P/S; patients with the upper tertile of BCKA had lower levels of CT attenuation and P/S in comparison with those in lower and middle tertiles (P for trend <0.05) (Table 1).

Association between BCAA‐BCKA axis with parameters of pancreatic steatosis

Partial correlation analysis indicated that PV was positively associated with BMI, BSA, triglyceride, fasting C peptide (FCP), 2‐h postprandial C peptide (PCP), and BCKA, whereas PVI was positively related to fasting C peptide (FCP), 2‐h postprandial C peptide (PCP), and BCKA. On the other side, CT attenuation of pancreas and P/S were negatively correlated with BMI, FPG, PPG, BSA, FIB‐4, and BCKA (Table 2). As shown in Table 3, linear regression analysis showed that BCKAs and BCKAs/BCAAs ratio were significantly associated with PV after being adjusted by age, gender (Model 1) as well as further adjustment for duration of diabetes, smoking status, drinking status, HTN, HbA1c, TG, HDL‐C, HOMA‐IR, FCP, FIB‐4, insulin, biguanide, GLP‐1RA (Model 2) and additionally adjusted for BMI (Model 3) (all P < 0.05). Although the association between BCKA and PV remained significant, the degree of correlation attenuated. Levels of BCKAs were negatively associated with CT attenuation of pancreas and P/S (all P < 0.05) among all three models. However, no significant association was observed between BCAAs and CT attenuation of pancreas or P/S in any model. Likewise, BCKAs/BCAAs ratio did not exhibit a significant association with either CT attenuation of pancreas or CTAp/s (all P > 0.05).

Exploratory analysis of BMI in the relationship between BCKA and parameters of pancreatic steatosis

As shown in Figure 1a, the total effect of BCKA on CTAp was statistically significant in the general population after adjusting for age, gender, duration of diabetes, smoking status, drinking status, HTN, HbA1c, TG, and HDL‐C (β, −0.061; 95% CI, −0.106 to −0.016). After stratification by sex, the total effect of BCKA on CTAp remained statistically significant merely in males but not in females (males: β, −0.072; 95% CI, −0.134 to −0.010; females: β, −0.048; 95% CI, −0.112 to 0.016). Likewise, the direct effect of BCKA on CTAp was statistically significant in the overall group as well as in males (all P< 0.01). When including BMI in the path as a mediator, it accounted for ~20% in the association of BCKA with CTAp (P< 0.05).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to examine the association between BCAA‐BCKA axis and pancreatic steatosis among a Chinese diabetic population. Our major findings are: (1) Patients with upper tertile of serum BCKAs had increased PV to subjects in lower tertile; (2) CT attenuation was reduced according to BCKAs tertiles; (3) BCKAs rather than BCAAs were associated with parameters of pancreas, including PV, CT attenuation of pancreas and CTAp/s; and (4) BMI accounts for ~20% in the association of BCKA with PV and CTAp using mediating analysis.

Pancreatic steatosis has been recognized as early as the 1930s 20 , 21 . However, the clinical implication of pancreatic steatosis has not been gradually recognized until recent years due to the development of radiography to determine the fat content of pancreas 16 , 20 , 22 , 23 , 24 . It was estimated that the prevalence of pancreatic steatosis in the general population ranges from 16% to 35%, depending on ethnicity and age 21 . Alberti 25 et al reported that the occurrence of pancreatic steatosis in the general population of adolescents was 4%, reaching almost 20% in participants with obesity. Pancreatic steatosis was associated with non‐alcohol fatty liver, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome 20 , 24 , 26 . Metabolic features including increased BMI and obesity were considered as significant risk factor for developing pancreatic steatosis 27 , 28 , 29 . Pancreatic fat was found to be positively associated with BMI and visceral adipose tissue independent of sex and age 30 , 31 , 32 . Consistent with these findings, our research identified BMI as an important factor in the relationship between BCKAs and both volume and CT attenuation of pancreas.

Regrettably, visceral adipose tissue mass was neither measured nor included in our correlation and mediation analyses, despite growing evidence indicating that visceral fat was more closely associated with metabolic dysfunction, insulin resistance, and ectopic fat deposition, including pancreatic steatosis 33 , 34 . Notably, visceral fat area has been shown to correlate more strongly with pancreatic fatty infiltration than BMI 34 , and individuals with visceral obesity exhibit significantly higher pancreatic fat volume and lower pancreatic CT attenuation values 35 . Furthermore, serum BCAAs were positively correlated with visceral fat area in both obese individuals and patients with type 2 diabetes 36 , 37 , and BCAA levels have been reported to be more closely associated with visceral fat area than subcutaneous fat area 37 . Therefore, future studies should incorporate standardized CT‐based quantification of visceral fat mass to more precisely elucidate the relationships among BCAAs/ BCKAs, visceral adiposity, and pancreatic steatosis, as well as to examine whether visceral fat mass serves as a mediator in these associations.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) are widely used methods for determining the concentrations of serum BCAAs/BCKAs 38 , 39 . LC–MS/MS is regarded as the gold standard for quantitative analysis of BCAAs/BCKAs due to its superior sensitivity, specificity, and ability to simultaneously quantify individual analytes 40 . However, the high cost of instrumentation, the requirement for professional technicians to operate, and the complexity of sample preparation limit its accessibility in large‐scale clinical application. Conversely, although ELISA detection methods for structurally similar small‐molecule metabolites such as BCAAs and their corresponding α‐keto acids are susceptible to cross‐reactivity and lack the ability to distinguish individual analytes, its advantages of low cost, simple operation, and the capacity to process a large number of samples render it more commonly used in clinical practice 41 . In addition, our previously published research had employed ELISA kits for measuring the levels of serum BCAAs/BCKAs 37 , 42 , verifying the reliability of the ELISA method for determining the levels of serum BCAAs/BCKAs.

The etiology of pancreatic steatosis remained unclear. In rodents, researchers mimicked pancreatic steatosis model using high‐fat diet 18 . Our previous research also found that branched‐chain amino acid restriction ameliorated HFD‐induced pancreatic steatosis in mice 19 . However, the role of the BCAA‐BCKA axis in the severity of pancreatic steatosis was seldomly reported in human beings. In this study, we demonstrated a close relationship between BCKA and pancreatic steatosis in patients with type 2 diabetes for the first time, but the cross‐sectional design of our study precluded the conclusions regarding causality, as obesity, diabetes‐related metabolic derangements, or pancreatic dysfunction may have contributed to elevated levels of BCAAs/BCKAs. Mosley et al. 43 found that BCAAs were not mediators of type 2 diabetes risk but were biomarkers of diabetes, with their concentrations increasing as a consequence of diabetes and insulin resistance. Furthermore, bidirectional Mendelian randomization analyses demonstrated that obesity and type 2 diabetes exerted a strong reverse causal effect on all three BCAAs including valine, isoleucine, and leucine 44 . Elevated circulating BCAA levels appeared to be a result of poor metabolic health, characterized by higher BMI, increased triglycerides, reduced HDL‐C, and an elevated risk of type 2 diabetes 45 . Additionally, BCKAs have been shown to inhibit glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion in pancreatic β‐cells, and elevated circulating BCKAs in diabetic patients were inversely correlated with insulin secretory capacity, suggesting that pancreatic dysfunction may both result from and contribute to BCKA accumulation 46 . These potential reverse causality scenarios underscore the necessity for prospective cohort studies and interventional trials to determine the temporal sequence and causal direction of these relationships.

The mechanism about the association between BCKAs and pancreatic steatosis was unclear in human beings. However, previous experimental studies demonstrated a close correlation of BCKAs with insulin resistance and catabolism of lipid. Firstly, BCKAs accumulation as well as BCAAs could activate mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORc1) and in turn suppress insulin signaling 10 , 47 , whereas insulin resistance was closely related to lipogenesis and differentiation of adipocyte. Up‐regulation of BCAA‐BCKA catabolism (e.g., activation of BCAT2 and BCKDH) or restriction of BCAA intake could significantly attenuate insulin resistance in obese mice 10 . Studies revealed that BCKAs rather than BCAAs were positively associated with insulin resistance in adolescents with obesity 48 . Our recent observational research also found that circulating BCKAs rather than BCAAs were closely associated with adipose tissue insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes 42 . Secondly, BCAA‐BCKA axis regulated white adipose tissue browning and thermogenesis by acetylation of PR domain‐containing protein 16 (PRDM16) 9 . Duan 49 et al demonstrated that limiting BCAA intake could enhance lipid β‐oxidation in muscles through the activation of AMPKα–PGC‐1α signaling pathway. Additionally, it has been reported that a diet low in isoleucine could mitigate obesity by stimulating the FGF21‐UCP1 axis, thereby increasing energy expenditure 50 . Our previous data also showed that BCAA restriction led to the upregulation of AMPKα, PGC‐1α, uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), and carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1A (CPT1A) expression in obese mice 19 . Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction is thought to play a major role in pancreatic β‐cell failure in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes 51 , 52 . β‐oxidation of fatty acids and the acetyl coenzyme A derived from fatty acids are oxidized by the TCA cycle to produce electron donors (NADH and FADH2), thereby potentially increasing the production of mitochondrial ROS production 53 . Indeed, BCAA‐BCKA, which was associated with metabolic disorders, may potentially increasing mitochondrial ROS production. Elevated levels of BCAA‐derived acylcarnitines in individuals with insulin‐resistant obesity or T2DM could be served as indicators of mitochondrial dysfunction 54 . What is more, increased BCAA levels in humans and mice could increase lipid peroxidation, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial transition pore opening, and apoptosis 54 . These findings were mainly from animal experiments, which should be verified in human beings.

Nevertheless, there were some limitations in this study. First, this study employed a cross‐sectional design and recruited patients from a single center in China; the findings could not be generalized to other ethnicities, geographic regions, or healthcare settings. The extrapolation of the conclusions of this study in a broader population should be scrupulous. Second, mass spectrometry should be employed in the future of our study, although the ELISA‐based results demonstrated high internal consistency. Third, the accuracy of the CT scan utilized may be affected by fibrosis, iron accumulation, edema, and technical parameters related to the scanning process. Hence, we use the parameter CTAp/s in order to minimize the influence of the above factors. Fourth, the interpretation of the mediating analysis was exploratory considering the cross‐sectional setting of our study. Consequently, prospective or interventional trials should be implemented to explore the causal relationship.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, levels of serum BCKAs rather than BCAAs were significantly associated with the volume and CT attenuation of pancreas in patients with type 2 diabetes in this cross‐sectional study, indicating that BCAA malmetabolism was related to pancreatic steatosis. Furthermore, obesity may play an important role in the correlation between circulating BCKAs and pancreatic steatosis, but future prospective cohort studies and interventional trials are warranted to clarify whether BCKAs are a cause or a consequence of pancreatic steatosis and to explore the underlying mechanisms.
