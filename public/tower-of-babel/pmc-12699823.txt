Multilevel retro-superior costotransverse ligament block with liposomal bupivacaine for open hepatectomy: a case report
Authors: Hao Cheng, Leiming Ding, Kun Zhang, Sufang Yang, Chuanguang Wang, Kailai Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative analgesia after open liver resection is challenging owing to both somatic and visceral pain. Retro-superior costotransverse ligament block is a novel ultrasound-guided paraspinal block that may provide a safer and effective alternative to thoracic epidural analgesia, thoracic paravertebral block, and erector spinae plane block, particularly in patients with coagulation concerns.Case presentationA 38-year-old Han Chinese woman underwent open liver resection under general anesthesia. Preoperatively, ultrasound-guided retro-superior costotransverse ligament block was performed at the T6–7, T8–9, and T10–11 levels, with 10 mL of a bupivacaine mixture injected per level (total 30 mL: 20 mL of liposomal bupivacaine and 10 mL of plain bupivacaine).The patient achieved sensory blockade from T4 to T12 in the anterior and lateral thoracic wall, and from T3 to L1 posteriorly. Intraoperative hemodynamics remained stable. Postoperatively, visual analog scale scores for both resting and coughing remained between 0 and 2 for 48 h. No rescue analgesics were required, and the patient-controlled analgesia pump was not activated.ConclusionRetro-superior costotransverse ligament block with liposomal bupivacaine may provide effective, long-lasting, and opioid-sparing analgesia after major abdominal surgery, and is a promising option.

Background

In recent years, various ultrasound-guided paraspinal and truncal blocks have been introduced as part of multimodal analgesia protocols for major abdominal surgeries. Thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) provides effective unilateral analgesia but carries the risk of pleural puncture and technical complexity. Erector spinae plane block (ESPB), as a representative of thoracic paraspinal blocks (TPSBs), is gaining popularity for its ease of use and favorable safety profile. However, TPSBs often produce variable and unpredictable sensory effects and may fail to provide consistent anterior thoracic wall analgesia owing to indirect spread of local anesthetics [1–3]. The retro-superior costotransverse ligament block (RSCTLB) is an emerging ultrasound-guided fascial plane block in which local anesthetic is deposited posterior to the superior costotransverse ligament, allowing for potential spread to the intercostal space, paravertebral space, intervertebral foramina, and the sympathetic chain [4]. This results in multilevel blockade of both dorsal and ventral rami, with potentially more reliable and reproducible sensory coverage. Compared with TEA, TPVB, and ESPB, RSCTLB may offer a balance of efficacy and safety, especially in hepatic surgery where coagulopathy is a concern. Despite its theoretical advantages, clinical evidence for RSCTLB in open upper abdominal surgery remains scarce. We report a case in which multilevel RSCTLB combined with liposomal bupivacaine provided effective and opioid-sparing analgesia following open liver resection.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 38-year-old Han Chinese woman diagnosed with a hepatic space-occupying lesion who underwent open liver resection under general anesthesia. For postoperative pain management, a multilevel ultrasound-guided retro superior costotransverse ligament space block (RSCTLB) was performed preoperatively at the T6–7, T8–9, and T10–11 levels.

Upon entering the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, and electrocardiography. The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position. A low-frequency curved-array ultrasound probe (Beidou model, Huasheng Medical Instrument Co., China) was placed parallel to the spine to identify the 12th rib. The probe was then moved cranially to locate the 6th rib and shifted medially to identify the 6th transverse process. It was subsequently rotated 90 degrees to visualize the bony landmarks of the rib and transverse process. The probe was then moved caudally to the level of the articular process to identify the inferior articular process. At this point, the parietal pleura and the retro superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL) became visible. The lateral edge of the probe was slightly tilted caudally to enhance visualization of the SCTL. Once the desired ultrasound image was obtained, an in-plane needle insertion technique was used (Fig. 1). After local infiltration with 2 mL of 1% lidocaine, a 22 G needle was advanced from lateral to medial. When the needle tip reached the space posterior to the SCTL and lateral to the inferior articular process, 10 mL of local anesthetic was injected. The same procedure was repeated at the T8–9 and T10–11 levels. A total of 30 mL of local anesthetic was administered—a mixture of 20 mL of liposomal bupivacaine (Aihengping®, Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., China) and 10 mL of plain bupivacaine (Changjiang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Anhui, China)—with 10 mL injected at each level.Fig. 1Ultrasound-guided retro-superior costotransverse ligament block. The surgical incision extended from the xiphoid process to below the right costal margin. General anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 μg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg), followed by endotracheal intubation after adequate neuromuscular blockade. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (1.5–2.0%) in a 50% oxygen–air mixture and intermittent boluses of cisatracurium. Owing to the stable hemodynamics and satisfactory intraoperative analgesia achieved with the multilevel RSCTLB, no additional opioids (such as sufentanil or remifentanil) were administered during surgery. Mean arterial pressure remained within 67–87 mmHg and heart rate ranged from 67–78 bpm, with no requirement for vasopressors or rescue analgesics throughout the procedure. The surgery lasted 186 min. After completion, the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Extubation was smooth, and the patient reported no pain or discomfort following extubation.

The patient received a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump containing 8 mg hydromorphone and 16 mg ondansetron diluted to 100 mL with normal saline. The pump was programmed with a background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, a bolus dose of 2 mL, and a lockout interval of 15 min. Notably, the patient did not activate the PCA pump or require any rescue analgesics. Postoperative analgesia was excellent, with resting and coughing visual analog scale (VAS) scores ranging from 0 to 2 over the first 48 h. The resting VAS score remained at 0 throughout, while the coughing VAS score was 0 at 0 and 6 h, 1 at 12 h, and 2 at 24, 36, and 48 h. No adverse effects were observed.

Discussion

Effective postoperative analgesia is crucial following open liver resection to enhance patient recovery, facilitate early mobilization, reduce pulmonary complications, and shorten hospital stay. Thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) remains the gold standard for such procedures owing to its reliable somatic and visceral blockade. However, its clinical application is often limited by contraindications, especially in patients with perioperative coagulopathy, because of risks including hypotension, motor block, and epidural hematoma [5].Thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) provides effective analgesia but carries risks, such as pleural puncture and hematoma formation, which limit its broader clinical application.

As safer alternatives, a variety of thoracic paraspinal blocks (TPSBs) have gained popularity in recent years. Techniques such as midpoint transverse process to pleura block (MTPB), multiple injection costotransverse block (MICB), subtransverse interligamentary plane block (STILB), costotransverse foramen block (CTFB), erector spinae plane block (ESPB), and retrolaminar block (RLB) all target anatomical planes adjacent to the thoracic paravertebral space (TPVS) [6–11]. These techniques rely on the indirect spread of local anesthetics to the TPVS to achieve analgesia without entering the paravertebral space directly. However, their clinical effects are often variable, with limited anterior spread, which may lead to incomplete ventral rami blockade and inconsistent analgesia [12, 13]. A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that ESPB may not provide any significant postoperative analgesia in liver surgery patients. While there was a tendency toward reduced opioid consumption, limited data showed no significant difference between ESPB and spinal analgesia in terms of pain scores and 24-h analgesic consumption [14].

The retro superior costotransverse ligament space block (RSCTLB), first described by Karmakar in 2022 [4], is a novel TPSB technique that targets the fascial plane posterior to the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL). This space contains loose connective tissue and fat, and anatomical studies have demonstrated that the SCTL is not a continuous barrier but includes natural fissures and channels that communicate with the intercostal space, TPVS, intervertebral foramina, and even the epidural space [8, 15]. Micro-CT evidence further shows that the proximal segments of both dorsal and ventral rami lie medial to the SCTL, suggesting that injection into this space can facilitate extensive and multidirectional spread of local anesthetic [16].

In this case, right-sided RSCTLB was performed at the T6–7, T8–9, and T10–11 levels. The injection sites were located posterior to the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL) and lateral to the inferior articular process. The surgical incision extended from the xiphoid process to below the right costal margin. Despite the extensive incision, the patient experienced excellent postoperative analgesia, with visual analog scale (VAS) scores for both resting and coughing consistently maintained between 0 and 2 during the 48-h postoperative period. No rescue analgesics were required, and the patient did not activate the PCA pump.

To prolong the duration of analgesia, a combination of liposomal bupivacaine and plain bupivacaine was administered. Liposomal bupivacaine is a multivesicular formulation known for its sustained effect of up to 72 h [17, 18]. When combined with plain bupivacaine, it offers both rapid onset and long-lasting pain control. This pharmacologic synergy likely contributed to the observed opioid-sparing effect and excellent patient comfort without any adverse events.

Compared with other regional techniques, RSCTLB appears to strike a balance between efficacy and safety. Unlike TEA and TPVB, RSCTLB is performed in an avascular, superficial plane, reducing the risks of vascular puncture, hematoma, or pneumothorax. Its ease of visualization under ultrasound and favorable anatomical spread make it a promising candidate for enhanced recovery protocols in hepatobiliary surgery.

Nevertheless, RSCTLB has certain limitations. Current clinical evidence remains limited to cadaveric studies, case reports, and small case series. The variability in individual anatomy and potential for inconsistent spread may affect its reproducibility. Contraindications such as local infection, severe spinal deformity, or poor ultrasound window must also be considered. Additionally, the technique still requires operator familiarity with ultrasound-guided thoracic anatomy for safe and effective execution. Future research should aim to validate the clinical efficacy of RSCTLB through randomized controlled trials, explore optimal dosing strategies, and evaluate long-term outcomes across various surgical populations.

Conclusion

Multilevel retro-superior costotransverse ligament block (RSCTLB) using a combination of liposomal and plain bupivacaine provided effective and long-lasting postoperative analgesia following open liver resection. The technique demonstrated favorable hemodynamic stability, extensive dermatomal coverage, and significant opioid-sparing effects. Given its superficial injection plane and ultrasound-guided safety profile, RSCTLB may serve as a valuable component of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, particularly in patients with bleeding risk or contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia.
