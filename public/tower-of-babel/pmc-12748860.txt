Reversible data hiding in encrypted images using LWE-based secret sharing
Authors: Zahra Saeidi, Sattar Mirzakuchaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The technique of reversible data hiding in encrypted images (RDH-EI) has experienced significant interest as it allows for precise extraction of embedded data without compromising the confidentiality of the original image. This research introduces a novel RDH-EI technique designed to accommodate multiple data hiders. To tackle this challenge, we propose a sophisticated RDH-EI method that integrates secret sharing Founded on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem alongside adaptive coding strategies. On the content owner’s side, the original image is first distributed to multiple data hiders using a method Founded on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem. Then, block permutation along with stream cipher encryption are performed to completely preserve the spatial correlation between image blocks. The proposed method benefits from the robust security provided by LWE. Initially, we examine the spread of the most significant bit planes to detect segments that are suitable for data embedding. Next, the data hider produces extra data and embeds errors within the encrypted image to guarantee precise image reconstruction. To increase data storage capacity, the most significant bits (MSB) The blocks suitable for embedding are adaptively compressed based on their frequency of occurrence. The extra data may be inserted into the (MSB) of the encrypted image, where it is combined with inverse Huffman codewords and supplementary auxiliary information. At the receiving side, the initial image can still be completely restored Losslessly, even if some shares happen to be damaged or missing, provided that a sufficient number of valid shares are available. Experimental findings demonstrate that the RDH-EI approach exceeds the performance of various cutting-edge methods, including those employing secret sharing (SS), in terms of embedding capacity.

Introduction

An image data hiding system involves two main components: The secret information is embedded into the cover image to produce a stego-image that appears identical to the original to the human eye. Two scenarios must be addressed in an image data hiding system. In one scenario, the focus is on safeguarding the secret data, and restoring the original cover image is unnecessary. The other scenario requires that both the secret data and the cover image are essential and must be perfectly recovered at the decoder side, which is crucial in applications like military and medical fields. It is evident that the first scenario represents irreversible data hiding, while the second corresponds to reversible data hiding (RDH). RDH has gained growing interest because of its ability to allow complete reversibility. Until now, most RDH techniques have primarily been developed using difference expansion (DE), histogram shifting (HS), lossless compression, and prediction error expansion (PEE). These RDH techniques strive to maintain an ideal compromise between the volume of embedded data and the extent of modifications made to the image. As cloud computing advances rapidly, the demand for privacy protection continues to grow. To safeguard data privacy while still allowing necessary operations, processing multimedia signals in the encrypted domain has drawn significant interest1–4.

In many scenarios, like cloud storage, image owners typically encrypt their images before being uploaded them to a cloud server machine to ensure content protection. Once the encrypted image is received, the cloud server might need to insert extra data for tasks like storage management or image annotation. As a result, the approach of reversible data hiding in encrypted images (RDH-EI) has been introduced5,6. This method enables the image owner to encrypt the image, while also permitting a data hider to insert secret information into it while keeping the original content hidden. With the appropriate encryption keys, it is possible to completely restore both the original image and the hidden data.

In 2008, Puech and colleagues proposed a (RDH-EI) technique5, in which one bit is embedded per block within an image encrypted with the (AES).

The concealed bit may be recovered by evaluating the local variation in pixel values within the block of the image.

A few years later, Zhang introduced a novel RDH-EI method in which the image is first encrypted using a stream cipher, and then extra data is embedded using a bit flipping technique6.

Building upon these methods, numerous novel RDH-EI techniques have been designed employing a range of different strategies7–9.

Reversible data hiding in encrypted images (RDH-EI) schemes are typically classified into two main types: vacating room after encryption (VRAE)6–9 and reserving room before encryption (RRBE)10–14.

In the VRAE method, the original image undergoes encryption first, after which the confidential data is embedded into the encoded picture. Conversely, the RRBE method reserves region for embedding data prior to the encryption process. Unlike the VRAE approach, the RRBE technique takes benefit of the spatial correlation within the initial image and generally offers a higher embedding space.

A prominent example of the RRBE strategy is the RDH-EI scheme introduced by Puteaux and Puech, which employs prediction of the pixels’ (MSB)14. This approach effectively leverages the local correlation present in the initial image, which causes a notable rise in the embedding capacity. Building upon this approach, Yin et al.15 proposed a adaptive-length MSB prediction technique utilizing Huffman encoding, while Mohammadi et al.16 improved the approach by including more important bits.

Zhang17 and Qian and Zhang18 suggested more adaptable distinct techniques that compress specific LSB planes of a stream cipher-encrypted image to generate space for embedding data. This method allows data extraction to be performed without the need for the encoding key and operates independently of the image reconstruction process. In19, secret bits are embedded by randomly selecting pixels from the image encrypted using a stream cipher and replacing the higher-order bit-planes. Because each high bit-plane can be either “0” or “1,” the prediction errors for both cases are examined to precisely restore the original image.

While RRBE-based techniques provide strong embedding performance, they can encounter practical challenges because the content owner might not be informed about the data hiding process that follows or may lack the computational resources needed for preprocessing.

To address this limitation, specialized encryption-based techniques called VRBE-based methods have been developed. References20,21, and23 describe generating the encrypted image through block rearrangement and block-oriented bitwise XOR, which preserves the internal correlation of each encrypted block. Following this, data hiding is performed using methods like difference histogram shifting (DHS)20, adaptive block encoding21, and difference compression23. In24 and25, The initial image has been encrypted through bit-plane rearrangement and Block shuffling, successfully transferring The extra space from the initial image into the encrypted image.

In the mentioned RDHEI techniques, the original image is first encrypted and then uploaded to the cloud. However, the encrypted image could be targeted by a third party attack, resulting in incorrect retrieval of the image at the receiver’s end. To enhance the resilience of RDHEI, various methods incorporating secret sharing (SS) have been introduced26–32.

Recently, RDH-EI schemes utilizing secret sharing methods have been presented in33–36. In the methods outlined in33–36, the owner of the image utilizes secret sharing techniques to encode the image into n separate encrypted shares, which are then stored on a cloud server. Each cloud server is capable of independently embedding data into its respective encrypted segment. The original image can be completely restored by gathering t of these encrypted segments.

Wu et al. were the pioneers in applying secret sharing techniques to RDH-EI schemes, as presented in33. They concealed the confidential data within using the encrypted image techniques like difference expansion or difference histogram shifting. Subsequently, Chen et al.34 proposed a novel reversible data hiding (RDH) approach based on secret sharing techniques. In this instance method, the data hider conceals secret data employing difference expansion combined with additive homomorphism. Nevertheless, the current approach involves a single data hider and achieves a comparatively low embedding capacity. To enhance the embedding capacity, Chen et al.35 suggested a new approach that employs several data hiders. In the process of data hiding stage, every data hider embeds private information through modifying a single pixel in every set of n pixels

In the present work, we propose a novel RDH-EI method that employs a distinct secret sharing approach. Our approach begins with an innovative sharing technique based on LWE (Learning With Errors). Using LWE, we design an RDH-EI method capable of supporting multiple data hiders. The scheme operates under a threshold model (t, n) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t \le n$$\end{document}, allowing the original image to be divided into n encrypted shares.

It is worth noting that our method employs LWE-based secret sharing, which has significant advantages over traditional methods such as Shamir’s Secret Sharing. While Shamir’s method is based on linear polynomials and Lagrangian interpolation, and its security is guaranteed only against classical computations, the LWE method relies on a well-known mathematical problem in the field of post-quantum cryptography that remains robust even against quantum attacks. In addition, LWE enables the combination of encryption and sharing processes into a single step, thereby reducing computational overhead. Another advantage is that the matrix structure of LWE naturally supports multi data hider scenarios, whereas the Shamir scheme is mainly designed for single data hider scenarios. These differences indicate that the use of LWE not only provides stronger security but also greater efficiency and flexibility for novel applications in RDH-EI.

To improve pixel correlation, we apply block permutation and a stream cipher, ensuring that each encrypted image is delivered to a specific data hider. The data hider examines the distribution of the most significant bit (MSB) layers to identify embeddable blocks and generate auxiliary data. In addition, errors are introduced into the encrypted image to facilitate accurate recovery of the original image at the receiver.

The embeddable blocks’ MSB layers are encoded adaptively according to their occurrence rates. This method enables the insertion of redundant data into the empty regions of the encrypted image, along with auxiliary information and the reverse Huffman codes corresponding to the MSB layers. At the receiver end, the processes of Data retrieval, image decoding, and image restoration can be performed depending on whether the encryption and embedding keys are available. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed scheme surpasses several existing methods regarding embedding capacity, appearance quality of the decoded images, and fidelity of image recovery.

An 8-bit pseudo-random binary sequence I is generated using the key \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_{e}^{2}$$\end{document}. Each of these encrypted images has a noise-like appearance and, based on their statistical characteristics, contains no discernible information from the original image. Figure 6g through 6k depict five specific encrypted images, each containing hidden data embedded into Fig. 6b–f, respectively. Assessing the visual imperceptibility of images with embedded hidden data is essential for reversible data hiding (RDH) techniques. This is important because the hidden data is inserted into the original image, and different embedding methods can alter the visual appearance of the resulting image in various ways. In the RDH-EI method we propose, the owner of the image transforms the initial image into an encoded version through encryption. The data hider is able to insert confidential information straight within the encrypted image and the recipient are able to extract separately this information and restore the original image.

Because the secret data is inserted directly within the encrypted image, the two encrypted images and its watermarked version exhibit a fully random appearance. Consequently, the embedded data is visually imperceptible in the watermarked image, preventing any leakage of information about the original image or the hidden data. Both the original image content and the embedded data remain hidden from potential attackers. Computational complexity analysis

In this section, we calculate the computational complexity of the design at each stage.

LWE-based sharing (share generation stage)

According to Eq. (4), generating each group of t pixels requires a matrix–vector multiplication \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A\in Z_{q}^{n \times t}$$\end{document} with the pixel vector p, which involves approximately n.t multiplications and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n.(t-1)$$\end{document} additions. Since the number of groups is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G=N/t$$\end{document}, the overall complexity of share generation is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G.O(nt)=O(N.n).$$\end{document}

Block encryption and XOR

In the bitwise encryption stage, each bit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i,j}^{y}$$\end{document} is encrypted using a pseudo-random binary sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{y}$$\end{document}. A block permutation is then applied with complexity O(N), followed by XOR operations, also with complexity O(N) per share. Therefore, if all n shares are generated and encrypted, the total complexity is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \ O(N)$$\end{document}.

MSB analysis and adaptive coding (Huffman)

For each block, the algorithm computes the MSB frequency, constructs the reverse Huffman code (with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T\le 4$$\end{document}, i.e., alphabet size at most 4), and performs bit transformations. Frequency counting per block costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(s^2)$$\end{document}, and over all blocks this gives O(N). Since building the Huffman code requires only constant time O(1), the overall cost of MSB analysis and adaptive coding is linear, O(N), with negligible overhead.

Embedding and extraction

Embedding: writing bits/patterns into the selected MSBs – simple implementation cost O(N) (practically smaller than N since only embeddable blocks are processed).

Extraction: scanning and reading bits and reconstructing auxiliary data – also O(N).

Reconstruction (image recovery) – solving the linear system

Recovery Equation. Reconstruction involves solving a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t \times t$$\end{document} linear system (Eq. 7), which can be carried out using Gaussian elimination or matrix inversion. Two scenarios can be considered:

Without precomputation. If a separate inversion or Gaussian elimination is performed for each group, the cost per group is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(t^3)$$\end{document}. With precomputation. If the coefficient matrix is fixed, its inverse can be computed once with complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(t^3)$$\end{document}. Conclusion

This article presents a novel model for (RDH-EI) based on LWE secret sharing. The initial image is transformed into several encrypted versions, which are subsequently shared to various data hiders for embedding data. This method guarantees that, despite potential loss or damage to some data hiders, the initial image can be restored by gathering enough labeled encrypted images from the remaining hiders. The data hider examines the most significant bits (MSB) of the encrypted blocks to generate additional information. They also intentionally introduce errors into the encrypted image to guarantee accurate retrieval of the data hider’s information. The data hider uses adaptive block coding based on the frequency of the most significant bits (MSB) and applies Huffman coding to create space for embedding. Extra data, together with error information, is embedded by employing inverse Huffman code sequence. Results from experiments reveal that the proposed method exceeds the performance of several leading RDH-EI methods in payload capacity.

Furthermore, extending the secret sharing approach to other formats For example voice and video presents an interesting direction for future research.
