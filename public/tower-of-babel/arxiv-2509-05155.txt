1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain.
When
\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
, it was proven in
[
25
,
30
]
that
\Omega
admits a solution to the following overdetermined system:

(1.1)

-\Delta u=1\ \text{ in }\ \Omega,\ u=0\ \text{ almost everywhere in}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\ \text{ and }\ \partial_{\nu}u=-\mathbf{c}\ \text{ on }\ \partial\Omega,

where
\mathbf{c}=\frac{|\Omega|}{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega)}
is a constant,
if and only if
\Omega
is a ball. Here,
\nu
denotes the unit outer normal of
\Omega
. Subsequent research has explored alternative proofs of this result; see, e.g.
[
4
,
6
,
23
]
.
This discovery initiated a vibrant and fruitful field of study, where the interplay of analysis and geometry has yielded numerous applications across diverse areas of mathematics and the natural sciences.

1.1.
Serrin’s theorem in rough domains

In 1992, Vogel
[
28
]
established that if
\Omega
is a
C^{1}
domain admitting a solution to (
1.1
), then
\Omega
is necessarily of class
C^{2}
and thus Serrin’s theorem applies. Specifically, Vogel assumed the following boundary behavior for the solution

u(x)\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad|\nabla u|(x)\to\mathbf{c}\ \text{ uniformly as }x\to\partial\Omega,

and the regularity theory for free boundary problems of Alt-Caffarelli type to deduce the
C^{2}
-regularity of
\Omega
.

Later, Berestycki raised the following question:

Suppose
\Omega
is
C^{2}
throughout except for a potential corner, and
u
represents a strong solution to (
1.1
) everywhere except at said corner. Does Serrin’s theorem remain applicable in this scenario?

This problem was resolved in
[
24
]
via an adapted moving plane method that systematically avoided the singular point.
Subsequently, interest grew in extending Serrin’s theorem to more general domains. In particular,
[
13
, Question 7.1]
posed the following:

Does Serrin’s theorem hold if
\Omega
is merely Lipschitz and
u
solves the equation under the weak formulation?

Let
B
be the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Recall that, a measurable set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
finite perimeter
if the distributional gradient of its characteristic function
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued Radon measure
D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}
with finite total variation, i.e.,
|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|(\mathbb{R}^{n})<\infty
.
By the Lebesgue-Besicovitch differentiation theorem, for
|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|
-a.e.
x
, the following holds:

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(x+rB)}{|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|(x+rB)}=-\nu_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad|\nu_{x}|=1.

The set of such
x
is called the
reduced boundary
of
\Omega
, denoted by
\partial^{*}\Omega
.
At these points,
\nu_{x}
is the measure-theoretic outer unit normal to
\Omega
at
x
.
By De Giorgi’s rectifiability theorem, the reduced boundary is a
(n-1)
-rectifiable set. Moreover,
\Omega
can be modified on a null set so that
\overline{\partial^{*}\Omega}=\partial\Omega
.
Finally, a set
E
of finite perimeter is
indecomposable
if for any
F\subset E
of finite perimeter satisfying

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}E)=\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}F)+\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}(E\setminus F)),

either
|F|=0
or
|E\setminus F|=0
.
For further details, see
[
3
]
and
[
16
, Sections 12, 15]
.

In
[
11
]
, the authors proved that if a bounded indecomposable set of finite perimeter
\Omega
satisfies, for some
A>0
, the measure-theoretic condition

(1.2)

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(B_{r}(x)\cap\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq Ar^{n-1}\quad\text{ for $\mathscr{H}^{n-1}$-a.e. }x\in\partial^{*}\Omega\text{ and $r\in(0,1)$,}

along with the weak formulation of (
1.1
):

(1.3)

u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=-\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\varphi\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then
\Omega
must be a ball.

Notably, Lipschitz domains satisfy the above measure-theoretic condition, thereby resolving
[
13
, Question 7.1]
. Furthermore, the same boundary assumption ensures that
u
is Lipschitz up to the boundary
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
.

1.2.
Wulff potential and nontangential limits

When
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain and
u
is harmonic, it is well known that if
u=0
on an open subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, one can understand the (outer) normal derivative
\partial_{\nu}u
on
\Gamma
in the weak formulation (
1.3
) via nontangential limits. Moreover,
\partial_{\nu}u\in L^{2+\delta}(\Gamma;\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1})
for some
\delta=\delta(n)>0
; see e.g.
[
5
]
.
This observation motivates our study of an anisotropic generalization of Serrin’s overdetermined problem, building upon tools from harmonic analysis.

Toward this end, we first introduce the
anisotropic energy
framework. Let
K
be a convex body containing the origin, and
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the corresponding Wulff potential (or
supporting function
), defined by

H(x)=\sup_{y\in K}x\cdot y,

which is
1
-homogeneous, that is,

H(tx)=tH(x)\quad\text{ for every $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

This induces a general Minkowski norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the dual function
H_{*}
of
H
as

H_{*}(x)=\sup\left\{x\cdot y:H(y)=1\right\}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Equivalently,
K
can be characterized as

K=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon H_{*}(x)\leq 1\}.

Let
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(x)
. We consider the following anisotropic overdetermined system:

u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}DV(Du)\cdot D\varphi\,dx=-\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}\varphi H(-\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx,\ \forall\,\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\mathbf{c}=\frac{|\Omega|}{P_{K}(\Omega)}
,
\nu
is the (measure-theoretic) unit outer normal, and

P_{K}(\Omega)=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}H(-\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}

denotes the anisotropic perimeter of
\Omega
. Then further denoting the anisotropic Laplacian by

\Delta_{H}u:={\rm div}(DV(Du))={\rm div}(H(Du)DH(Du)),

one may equivalently restate the equation in distributional form as

(1.4)

u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad u=0\ \text{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\quad\Delta_{H}u=\mathbf{c}H(-\nu)\mathscr{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial^{*}\Omega}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\,dx.

When
\Omega
is sufficiently smooth, ensuring that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
, i.e.,
u
is a classical solution, it was independently demonstrated in the two exemplary work
[
7
]
and
[
29
]
that
\Omega
must coincide with
K
up to an appropriate translation and dilation. These proofs elegantly refined the methodologies introduced in
[
4
]
and
[
30
]
, respectively.

In this manuscript, we introduce a novel approach rooted in harmonic analysis to establish an anisotropic variant of Serrin’s overdetermined theorem for Lipschitz domains, specifically when the convex body
K
under consideration is an ellipsoid.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz domain,
K
be a (bounded) ellipsoid and
H
be the associated Wulff potential.
Assume that
u\in W^{1,\,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
satisfies the anisotropic overdetermined system (
1.4
),
|Du|\geq c_{0}>0
in a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega,
and that
Du
is
L^{2}
-Dini-VMO up to the reduced boundary
\partial^{*}\Omega
(outside a set of
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
-measure zero). Then

u(x)=\frac{r^{2}-H_{*}^{2}(x)}{2n}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\text{ for some $r>0$,},

where
H_{*}
is the dual function of
H
. Moreover, this implies that
\Omega
and
K
are homothetic.

Remark 1.2
.

We provide a series of remarks below to clarify the context and technical aspects of our results.

(1)

Given that an ellipsoid
K
centered at the origin can be transformed into the unit ball via a linear map
L
, by precomposing
u
with
L^{-1}
, one can directly analyze the problem within the framework of the standard Euclidean norm. In fact, our method holds promise for generalization to encompass general Wulff shapes (under suitable regularity assumptions on
K
), while one has to impose several restrictive assumptions on
Du
. This is due to our reliance on harmonic analysis techniques applied to linear operators, where the linearized operator in our problem inherently involves
Du
. These additional constraints might render the problem less interesting. Nevertheless, we still present and prove the theorem using terminologies that facilitate its extension to the general Wulff shape scenario (under certain assumptions).

Additionally, our approach also yields an alternative proof for the case when
\Omega
is Lipschitz, as discussed in
[
11
]
(within more general class of domains), and introduce a novel approach to address
[
13
, Question 7.1]
. It is also crucial to acknowledge that the proof in
[
11
]
heavily depends on a set of properties unique to the Laplacian, so extending that approach to the anisotropic case appears to be non-trivial.

(2)

The core technique underlying our result involves proving that the non-tangential maximal function
N_{*}(|Du|)
belongs to
L^{2+\delta}(\partial\Omega)
for some
\delta>0
. This estimate is then applied to control the
L^{2}
-convergence of
H(Du)
to
\mathbf{c}
as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
\Omega_{\epsilon}=\{u>\epsilon\}
. Recall that in
[
11
]
,
u
is shown to be Lipschitz continuous up to the boundary (under a weaker assumption (
1.2
)), providing an
L^{\infty}
-analog of this dominance.

While the
L^{2+\delta}
-integrability of the non-tangential maximal function of
|Du|
is well established for harmonic functions in more general domains (including Lipschitz domains), the Lipschitz regularity assumption is crucial for analyzing restrictions of
W^{2,\,2}
-functions; see the proof of Proposition
2.3
. Consequently, this limitation prevents us from further extending our results to broader classes of domains.

(3)

Similar to
[
11
]
, our approach follows Weinberger’s framework
[
30
]
. Readers familiar with
[
4
]
may wonder whether its techniques could be adapted here. Notably, the proof in
[
4
]
relies solely on the minimum principle for
u
, without requiring the maximum principle for
|Du|
or the Hopf boundary lemma. However, extending their method would necessitate
L^{3}
-convergence of
|Du|
on
\partial\Omega_{\epsilon}
to the boundary value
\mathbf{c}
, a condition that remains unclear even in Lipschitz domains, especially when the dimension is high.

1.3.
Alexandrov theorem and Maggi’s conjecture

Our result is also partially motivated by a conjecture of Maggi
[
17
]
:

Conjecture 1.3

(Maggi’s Conjecture)
.

For a positive convex integrand, Wulff shapes are the unique sets of finite perimeter and finite volume that are critical points of the anisotropic boundary energy under fixed volume constraints.

This conjecture generalizes the classical Alexandrov theorem
[
1
,
2
]
and its anisotropic counterpart
[
12
]
. In Conjecture
1.3
, the notions of first variation and critical points are rigorously defined via the convexity of the functional along prescribed variational flows
[
17
, pages 35-36]
.

In full generality (for sets of finite perimeter in dimensions greater than
2
), the conjecture has been resolved in the following cases. For the standard Euclidean norm, we refer to the manuscript of Delgadino and Maggi
[
10
]
. For elliptic integrands of class
C^{2,\,\alpha}
(
\alpha>0
), see the work by De Rosa–Kolasiński–Santilli
[
9
]
. Both of the arguments extend the argument of Montiel and Ros
[
18
]
.

Alexandrov’s theorem is closely connected to Serrin’s overdetermined boundary value problem. For instance,
[
6
]
established Serrin’s theorem via Alexandrov’s result, and subsequent work
[
8
,
21
,
22
]
have further explored this deep interplay. We also recommend the insightful survey
[
20
]
, which provides a comprehensive review of these developments.

Additionally, since Alexandrov’s theorem is related to minimal surfaces, Serrin’s overdetermined problem is similarly linked to the one-phase Bernoulli free boundary problem
[
27
]
. This motivates the following anisotropic generalization of Serrin’s theorem:

Conjecture 1.4

(Anisotropic Serrin-type conjecture for rough domains)
.

For a positive convex integrand
H
, Wulff shapes are the unique (indecomposable) sets of finite perimeter (or at least among Lipschitz domains) and finite volume that satisfy the overdetermined system (
1.4
).

A key implication of this conjecture is that, the upper bound condition on the perimeter density (
1.2
) in
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
might be dispensed, though this remains an open problem. As previously highlighted in
[
11
, Remark 1.5]
, this problem presents greater challenges than its counterpart in the context of the Alexandrov theorem, even when the set under consideration is merely Lipschitz.

The manuscript is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce key terminologies from harmonic analysis. Specifically, we establish that the nonlinear operator
\Delta_{H}
is uniformly elliptic and satisfies the DKP-condition. This enables us to reformulate the weak solution in terms of nontangential limits, yielding a strong
L^{2+\delta}
-convergence of the
H(Du)
as it approaches the boundary. This result serves as the cornerstone for the generalized argument from Weinberger presented in Section 3, which culminates in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us fix some notation.
For a point
x\in\Omega
, we write

d(x)={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x,\,\partial\Omega).

We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{n}
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
and by
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
A general constant is represented by the symbol
C
, which may differ in various estimates. We incorporate all the parameters it relies on within parentheses, denoted as
C(\cdot)
.

We record some properties of
H
.
By the equivalence of norms in finite dimensional spaces, it follows that

(2.1)

C^{-1}|x|\leq H(x)\leq C|x|,

for some
C=C(K).

Moreover, the
1
-homogenuity of
H
implies

(2.2)

x\cdot DH(x)=H(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the left-hand side is taken as zero when
x=0
.
In addition, since
u\in C^{2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}
and
H\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,

D^{2}V(x)=H(x)D^{2}H(x)+DH(x)\otimes DH(x)\quad\text{ is in $L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ and zero-homogeneous},

and set
H(Du)DH(Du)=0
whenever
Du=0
. Namely
DV
is Lipschitz.
Therefore

\Delta_{H}u(x)={\rm div}(DV(Du(x)))={\rm div}\left(\int_{0}^{1}D^{2}V(sDu(x))Du(x)\,ds\right):={\rm div}(\mathcal{A}(x)Du(x)),

is uniformly elliptic with

\mathcal{A}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}D^{2}V(sDu(x))\,ds=D^{2}V(Du(x)),

since
D^{2}V
is
0
-homogeneous and
H
is uniformly convex.

Definition 2.1
.

A real matrix function
\mathcal{A}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfies the
C
-Dahlberg-Kenig-Pipher (
C
-DKP for short) condition in
\Omega
if
\mathcal{A}
satisfies that

d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=\chi_{\Omega}(x)\sup\{d(z)|D\mathcal{A}(z)|^{2}\colon z\in B(x,d(x)/2)\}d\mathcal{L}(x)

is a
C
-Carleson measure with respect to
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial\Omega}
, i.e., for any ball
B_{r}(y)
centered at
y\in\partial\Omega
,

\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(B_{r}(y)\cap\Omega)\leq C\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}(y)).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
u
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then

\mathcal{A}(x)=D^{2}V(Du(x))

satisfies the
C
-DKP condition in
\Omega
with
C=C(n,\,c_{0},\,\alpha,\,\|D^{2}V\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})},\|D^{3}V\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})},\,L)
.

Proof.

A direct computation yields

D\mathcal{A}(x)=D^{3}V(Du(x))D^{2}u(x).

As
K
is an ellipsoid, one has
D^{3}V\equiv 0
, and hence the
C
-DKP condition is satisfied automatically.

∎

Assume that
x\in\partial\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
locally is a graph of an
L
-Lipschitz function near
x
for some
L>0
. We define the vertical cone
V_{L}(x)
with vertex at
x
(of sufficiently narrow aperture) as

\left\{y\in\Omega\colon\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\cdot e_{n}\geq\frac{2L}{\sqrt{1+4L^{2}}}\right\}.

Then the
non-tangential maximal function

N_{*}(|Du|)
is defined as

N_{*}(|Du|)(x)=\sup_{y\in V_{L}(x)}|Du|(y).

The following result is a corollary of
[
15
,
19
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
and
u
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then there exists
\delta=\delta(n)>0
for which, the nontangential maximum function

N_{*}(|Du|)\in L^{2+\delta}(\partial\Omega).

In particular,

H(Du)\bigg|_{\partial\Omega_{\epsilon}}\to\mathbf{c}

strongly in
L^{2+\delta}
with respect to
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
-measure as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
\Omega_{\epsilon}=\{u>\epsilon\}
.

Proof.

As
K
is an ellipsoid,
\mathcal{A}
is constant and we can extend
\mathcal{A}
trivially in whole
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
v=\chi_{\Omega}\star G
, where
G
is the fundamental solution of the operator
L_{\mathcal{A}}:={\rm div}(\mathcal{A}D\cdot)
. Then it follows that
u-v
is
\mathcal{A}
-harmonic in
\Omega
and

(u-v)|_{\partial\Omega}=-v|_{\partial\Omega}

is the trace of a
W^{2,\,2}
-function on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of a Lipschitz domain. Thus we have
(u-v)|_{\partial\Omega}\in W^{\frac{3}{2},2}(\partial\Omega;\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1})
.
Now by recalling (
2.1
), we can apply
[
15
, Theorem 6.17]
and
[
19
]
, together with fractional Sobolev embeddings, to conclude the proposition according to Lemma
2.2
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Now following the idea of Weinberger
[
30
]
, we prove Theorem
1.1
in terms of the nontangential limit of
|Du|
.

As
u
is
C^{2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}
(indeed
C^{2,\,\alpha}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}
) in
\Omega
, by Sard’s theorem and the strong minimum principle, there exists a sequence
\epsilon_{m}\searrow 0
so that,
by writing
\Omega_{m}=\{u>\epsilon_{m}\}
, one has
\Omega_{m}
is
C^{2}
,

\Omega_{m}\subset\Omega_{m+1}\subset\Omega,

and
\Omega_{m}\to\Omega
in the Hausdorff distance.

Recall that
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(x)
has a quadratic growth by (
2.1
). We firstly show the following version of Pohozaev identity.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
u
as in Theorem
1.1
. Then

n\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}n}{2}|\Omega|+(n-2)\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx.

Proof.

As
u\in C^{2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}
and
u=\epsilon_{m}
on the boundary of
\partial\Omega_{m}
, the divergence theorem yields

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{m}}(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\,dx=\

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{m}}\left[-{\rm div}(x(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+})+n(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\right]\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\int_{\Omega_{m}}x\cdot Du\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\Omega_{m}}{\rm div}(DV(Du))(x\cdot Du)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\Omega_{m}}\left[{\rm div}(DV(Du)(x\cdot Du))-DV(Du)\cdot D(x\cdot Du)\right]\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\Omega_{m}}{\rm div}(DV(Du)(x\cdot Du))\,dx-\int_{\Omega_{m}}(Du+x^{T}D^{2}u)\cdot DV(Du)\,dx.

As the divergence theorem also gives

\int_{\Omega_{m}}{\rm div}(DV(Du)(x\cdot Du))\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}(DV(Du)\cdot\nu)(x\cdot Du))\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1},

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{m}}x^{T}D^{2}uDV(Du)\,dx=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\Omega_{m}}\left[{\rm div}(xV(Du))-nV(Du)\right]\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}V(Du)(x\cdot\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}-n\int_{\Omega_{m}}V(Du)\,dx,

we employ (
2.2
) together with

Du=|Du|\nu\quad\text{ on }\ \partial\Omega_{m}

to conclude

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{m}}(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\,dx

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}(DV(Du)\cdot\nu)(x\cdot Du))\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}-2\int_{\Omega_{m}}V(Du)\,dx-\int_{\Omega_{m}}x^{T}D^{2}uDV(Du)\,dx

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}V(Du)(x\cdot\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+(n-2)\int_{\Omega_{m}}V(Du)\,dx.

Now by letting
m\to\infty
and applying Proposition
2.3
, we eventually obtain that

n\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}x\cdot\nu\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+(n-2)\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx=\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}n}{2}|\Omega|+(n-2)\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx,

recalling that
V=\frac{1}{2}H^{2}
has a quadratic growth by (
2.1
) and hence
V(Du)\to\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}
.
∎

Now we are ready to show Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

First of all, testing (
1.4
) via
u
, we conclude from (
2.2
) that

\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}Du\cdot DV(Du)\,dx=2\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx.

This combining with Lemma
3.1
yields

(3.1)

\mathbf{c}^{2}n|\Omega|=(n+2)\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=2(n+2)\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx.

Particularly, we have

(3.2)

2\int_{\Omega}V(Du)\,dx+\frac{2}{n}\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\mathbf{c}^{2}|\Omega|.

Following the strategy of
[
11
, Proposition 3.3]
, we next show that

(3.3)

u_{e}(x)=Du(x)\cdot e\leq\mathbf{c}

for every
x\in\Omega
and any
e\in\partial K
. Towards this, we first note that (
1.4
) implies

{\rm div}(D^{2}V(Du)Du_{e})=:L_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=0\quad\text{ in }\ \Omega,\ \text{where $w=Du\cdot e$}.

Then by fixing
x\in\Omega
and setting
G_{m}(x,\,y)=G_{m,\,x}(y)
as the Green’s function of the operator
L_{\mathcal{A}}
in
\Omega_{m}
containing
x
, i.e.,

{\rm div}(D^{2}V(Du)DG_{m,\,{x}})=-\delta_{x}+\mu_{m}\mathscr{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial\Omega_{m}},

where
\mu_{m}=-D^{2}V(Du)DG_{m,\,x}\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{m}}
represents the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the
\mathcal{A}
-harmonic measure on
\partial\Omega_{m}
,
we have

(3.4)

u_{e}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}u_{e}(y)\mu_{m}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}.

Observe that, for
r>0
sufficiently small so that
B_{r}(x)\subset\subset\Omega_{m}
, we have
(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\geq 0
in
\Omega
with

G_{m,\,x}\leq M(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\quad\text{ on }\ \partial(\Omega_{m}\setminus B_{r}(x))

with
M=M(\Omega,x,\,r)>0
. Then as
(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}
solves the same equation as
u
with zero boundary value on
\Omega_{m}
, by the comparison principle, one gets

G_{m,\,x}\leq M(u-\epsilon_{m})_{+}\quad\text{ in }\ \Omega_{m}\setminus B_{r}(x).

As
\Omega_{m}
is
C^{2}
, the Hopf boundary lemma implies

|DG_{m,\,x}|\leq M|Du|\quad\text{ on }\ \partial\Omega_{m}.

Thus, Proposition
2.3
together with the
0
-homogeneity of
D^{2}v
implies

\mu_{m}\leq M\|D^{2}V\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}|Du|\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{m};\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}).

This allows us to pass
m\to\infty
in (
3.4
) as
|u_{e}|\leq H(Du)\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{m};\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1})
. Then we conclude (
3.3
) from facts that
|u_{e}|\leq H(Du)\to\mathbf{c}
in
L^{2}
and that
\mu_{m}d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial\Omega_{m}}
is a sequence of probability measures.
In particular, the arbitrariness of
e
and
x
in (
3.3
) implies

(3.5)

\sup_{\Omega}H(Du)\leq\mathbf{c}.

We now show that
P(x)=V(Du(x))+\frac{1}{n}u(x)
satisfies

(3.6)

2P(x)\leq\mathbf{c}^{2}.

First of all, recall from
[
29
, Theorem 4 & Formula (31)]
that
P
satisfies

(3.7)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle tr}(D^{2}V(Du)D^{2}P)+b_{i}P_{i}=\sum_{i,\,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}(\partial_{ij}u)^{2}-\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\partial_{ii}u)^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{n}(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}u)^{2}-\frac{1}{n}=0

where
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is a vector coefficient and
\lambda_{i}>0,1\leq i\leq n,
are the eigenvalues of
D^{2}V(Du)
; note that in a suitable coordinate system,

\Delta_{H}u=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}u=-1.

Thus,
P
attains its maximum in
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
on
\partial\Omega_{m}
for every
\epsilon_{m}>0
.
As
\Omega
is Lipschitz,
u
is continuous up to the boundary, and then (
3.5
) together with the maximum principle above yields (
3.6
).

Now we deduce from (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{2}|\Omega|=\frac{n+2}{n}\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u\left(-{\rm div}(DV(Du))+\frac{2}{n}\right)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(2V(Du)+\frac{2}{n}u\right)\,dx\leq\mathbf{c}^{2}|\Omega|,

which implies that
P
has to be constant. According to the AM-GM inequality applied in (
3.7
), this yields that
\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}u
must be the same, i.e.,

D^{2}V(Du)D^{2}u=D(DV(Du))\quad\text{is a multiple of identity.}

Thus the connectedness of
\Omega
implies

u(x)=\frac{r^{2}-H_{*}^{2}(x)}{2n},

where
r>0
is a constant and
H_{*}
is the dual function of
H
; see e.g.
[
7
, (5.17)-(5.19)]
. This concludes the theorem.
