Biomechanical comparison of titanium and Y-TZP subperiosteal implants in atrophic maxilla: a finite element study
Authors: Eda Etik, Basak Keskin Yalcin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the biomechanical behavior of subperiosteal implant systems manufactured from titanium and yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (Y-TZP) materials under different implant thicknesses and screw diameters using three-dimensional finite element analysis (FEA).MethodsEight FEA models were generated from maxillary geometry obtained using computed tomography (CT). Two implant materials (Titanium and Y-TZP), two implant thicknesses (1.0 mm and 1.5 mm), and two screw diameters (1.5 mm and 2.0 mm) were evaluated. Three loading conditions were simulated: bilateral vertical loading of 150 N on premolars and first molar, unilateral oblique loading of 100 N at 30°, and bilateral vertical loading of 150 N on the incisors. Total displacement values, maximum and minimum principal stresses in the bone; Von Mises stresses in the bone, subperiosteal implant, abutment, and metal framework were analyzed.ResultsIncreasing implant thickness reduced stress levels in the bone while increasing stresses within the subperiosteal implant and metal framework. Larger screw diameters decreased stress in the bone and implant but increased stress concentration in the metallic substructure. The higher elastic modulus of Y-TZP reduced stress transmission to the bone; however, higher stresses were observed within the implant body and framework.ConclusionsImplant thickness, screw diameter, and material stiffness influenced the biomechanical behavior of subperiosteal implants. Both materials produced bone stress values below thresholds associated with bone resorption. Y-TZP demonstrated favorable stress distribution in terms of reduced bone stress due to its material properties. In vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies are required to validate its long-term biomechanical performance and clinical applicability in subperiosteal implant systems.

Background

Dental implantology is currently regarded as one of the most widely used and predictable treatment options for the functional and esthetic rehabilitation of missing teeth [1]. However, particularly in atrophic jaws with advanced alveolar bone resorption, sufficient bone volume and density for the placement of endosseous implants cannot always be achieved [2]. In such cases, advanced surgical approaches may be applied [3–5]; these methods are associated with disadvantages including increased morbidity, prolonged treatment time, and higher costs, thereby highlighting the need for alternative treatment modalities [6–8].

Subperiosteal implants, developed as an alternative to conventional dental implants in cases of severe atrophy, owing to their design, which rests on the bone surface and is stabilized through screw fixation [9], showed a marked decline in clinical use due to the high rate of reported complications [10]. However, in recent years, they have been re-evaluated with the development of CT, CAD/CAM (Computer-Aided Design/Computer-Aided Manufacturing) systems, and additive manufacturing technologies. These advancements have enabled the production of biocompatible, patient-specific implant solutions [11]. Additive manufacturing techniques, such as Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS) and Selective Laser Melting (SLM), enable the fabrication of highly accurate, customized implant designs. These approaches reduce surgical time, improve anatomical adaptation, and increase the bone–implant contact area, thereby enhancing biomechanical stability without the need for advanced surgical procedures such as bone grafting or sinus elevation [12–13]. The requirement for passive fit, the risk of soft tissue complications, and the limited predictability of long-term biological integration represent important limitations. From a prosthetic standpoint, full-arch rehabilitation and the possibility of early loading are advantageous; however, long-term maintenance, load distribution, and stress concentration at the interface remain controversial. Biomechanical success depends on design, material properties, and interface conditions, with stress distribution and structural stability being critical determinants.

Modern subperiosteal implants are primarily manufactured from titanium, and less commonly from biomaterials such as Y-TZP. Titanium is considered the gold standard due to its high biocompatibility and mechanical strength. Y-TZP may also be preferred in patients with metal hypersensitivity, owing to its favorable material properties, such as high hardness, surface durability, and chemical stability [14]. To evaluate the mechanical performance and biomechanical behavior of different implant materials, computational simulation techniques, in addition to experimental studies, represent a valuable and widely recognized methodological approach.

FEA is a powerful engineering method for predicting stress distributions, deformation magnitudes, and the overall biomechanical behavior of implant systems under loading conditions [15]. In implant biomechanics, material elastic modulus, implant thickness, screw diameter, and loading direction are considered critical parameters. The elastic modulus determines the stiffness of the implant; increased stiffness may lead to higher stress levels within the implant while reducing the load transmitted to the surrounding bone. In contrast, materials with a lower elastic modulus tend to reduce stress concentrations within the implant while increasing load transfer to the surrounding bone tissue [16]. Although several studies have investigated subperiosteal implants using FEA, comparative studies evaluating patient-specific, dual-block subperiosteal implants used in severely atrophic maxilla with respect to different biomaterials and implant design parameters remain limited.

The present study aims to compare the biomechanical behavior of patient-specific subperiosteal implant designs fabricated from titanium and Y-TZP materials using FEA, based on three-dimensional data obtained from a patient with a severely atrophic maxilla. By evaluating implant thickness and screw diameter, the study aims to establish a scientific basis for determining optimal design parameters for clinical application. It is hypothesized that implant material, thickness, and screw diameter significantly influence stress distribution and deformation patterns in patient-specific subperiosteal implants. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the few comparative analyses investigating the use of Y-TZP as a subperiosteal implant material, thereby addressing an existing gap in the literature and providing an original contribution.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This in-silico FEA study was approved by the Istanbul University Institute of Health Sciences (Approval No: E-99984023-302.14.01-3467799) and the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Bezmialem Vakif University (Approval No: E-54022451-050.04-206894). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant included in the study.

Radiographic data acquisition

Radiographic images of the maxilla were acquired from a patient who fulfilled the predefined inclusion criteria and had severe maxillary atrophy [17]. Imaging was performed using a multidetector CT (MDCT) system operated at 120 kVp and 200 mA, with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm and an interslice interval of 0.5 mm. The radiographic data were obtained in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) (.dcm) format, comprising approximately 800 axial slices. Subsequently, the imaging dataset was exported and archived on a compact disc (CD) to facilitate three-dimensional reconstruction and further analysis.

Construction of anatomical models

The CT dataset of the maxilla was segmented using 3D Slicer software (version 5.6.1/32438; Slicer Community, Boston, MA, USA) by applying Hounsfield unit thresholds ranging from 426.50 to 3193.04 [18]. Cortical and trabecular bone were not differentiated and were modeled as a single homogeneous structure to simplify the computational analysis. The segmented data were reconstructed into a three-dimensional model and exported in STL format. Subsequently, the geometry was imported into Blender (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) for geometric refinement, surface smoothing, and preparation for finite element analysis. Mesh quality was assessed, and elements exceeding predefined thresholds (skewness > 80° or minimum edge length < 0.001 mm) were corrected. Finally, all models were aligned according to anatomical reference coordinates, completing the modeling workflow. The detailed mesh properties of all finite element models are presented in Table 1.

Construction of prosthetic and implant components

In the present study, subperiosteal implant systems were designed three-dimensionally using Blender software in accordance with the planned surgical conditions. Implant frameworks were generated with thicknesses of 1.0 mm and 1.5 mm, while the fixation screws used for anchorage to the bone were modeled with diameters of 1.5 mm and 2.0 mm. Based on these parameters, four distinct implant–screw combinations were established. Furthermore, the abutment, occlusal screws, metal framework, and prosthetic superstructure were modeled within the same computational environment. The prosthetic morphology was defined with reference to anatomical guidelines described in the Wheeler atlas [19]. To ensure reliable load transfer across the assembled components, mesh interfaces were refined and optimized using ALTAIR HyperMesh software. (Altair Engineering, Troy, MI, USA).

Development of mathematical models

The geometries generated in the Blender environment were utilized to construct FEA models suitable for numerical analysis. Initial surface meshing was performed using triangular (tria) elements with sizes ranging from 0.05 to 0.25 mm, followed by volumetric meshing with tetrahedral elements to accurately represent complex anatomical structures. The models were then imported into the ALTAIR OptiStruct solver (Altair Engineering, Troy, MI, USA) for static stress analysis. Mesh convergence analysis was performed to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the simulations. The objective was to determine an optimal mesh density that maintains the relative error below 3% while ensuring computational efficiency. Accordingly, a series of progressively refined meshes, ranging from coarse to fine, was generated and analyzed under identical loading and boundary conditions. Assembly of systems and intercomponent contact

For accurate FEA and the attainment of reliable results, the interactions at the interfaces of all model components were explicitly defined within the simulation environment. Nonlinear frictional contact conditions were assigned to the bone–subperiosteal implant interface, with a coefficient of friction (µ) of 0.3 [24], and to the interface between the subperiosteal implant screw holes and the fixation screws, with a coefficient of friction (µ) of 0.36 [25]. The implant–component interfaces, except for the defined frictional contacts, were modeled using a FREEZE-type bonded contact formulation, representing an idealized fully constrained interface without relative motion. Sliding or separation between the surfaces has been neglected. The model has been simplified, and the simulation stabilized.

Boundary conditions

Mechanical stability was achieved by fully constraining all degrees of freedom of the nodes located in the superior (cranial) and posterior regions of the maxilla. This boundary condition was applied to provide a stable reference framework for evaluating stress distribution within the bone–implant–prosthesis assembly. Translational and rotational displacements along the X, Y, and Z axes were restricted, thereby simulating the physiological fixation of the maxilla to the cranial base. Micro-movements allowed by the craniofacial sutures and surrounding soft tissues were neglected to enable mathematical modeling and solution of the system.

Loading scenarios

This study subjected all FEA models to three loading conditions replicating physiologically relevant masticatory forces, with the prosthesis modeled as a rigid component transmitting these forces to the underlying bone. The posterior vertical loading condition involved the application of a bilateral vertical force of 150 N to the cusps of the first premolars, second premolars, and first molars (Fig. 3A). Forces were allocated to the nodal points within the occlusal contact regions to reduce local stress concentrations and avoid numerical singularities. For the posterior oblique loading condition, a unilateral force of 100 N was applied at a 30° angle from the buccal toward the palatal direction on the same posterior teeth (Fig. 3B), allowing the simulation of off-axis loading and asymmetric stress distribution associated with functional mastication. In the anterior vertical loading condition, a bilateral vertical load of 150 N was applied to the central and lateral incisors to mimic anterior biting forces [26–28] (Fig. 3C).

Analysis procedure

Under the defined loading and boundary conditions, a total of 24 linear static analyses were performed by combining four different model configurations, two material types, and three loading scenarios (4 models × 2 materials × 3 loading conditions). This comprehensive approach enabled a detailed evaluation of the biomechanical behavior of subperiosteal implant systems under varying material properties, implant thicknesses, and loading conditions.

Biomechanical evaluation

This study assessed the biomechanical performance of patient-specific subperiosteal implant systems by evaluating total displacement, principal stresses, and von Mises stress. Total displacement was used to quantify the overall deformation of the implant–bone complex and to assess its structural stability under applied loads. Tensile and compressive behavior of the bone tissue was represented by the maximum principal stress (σ₁) and minimum principal stress (σ₃), respectively, allowing for a detailed analysis of load transfer at the bone–implant interface. Von Mises stress was examined to characterize the equivalent stress distribution within the implant, abutment, and metal framework components. These mechanical parameters were systematically compared across three loading scenarios and different configurations of implant framework thickness and fixation screw diameters.

Results

Total displacement

The total displacement results obtained under three different loading conditions demonstrated that Y-TZP subperiosteal implants exhibited lower displacement values than titanium implants across all model configurations.

Under posterior vertical loading, when the same design parameters were compared, Y-TZP implants showed approximately 12–13% lower displacement values than titanium implants. Increasing the implant thickness from 1 mm to 1.5 mm significantly reduced displacement in both materials, with reductions of approximately 16% for both Y-TZP and titanium. In contrast, the effect of increasing screw diameter was limited, resulting in a displacement reduction of less than 1% (Fig. 5A). Under posterior oblique loading conditions, Y-TZP implants demonstrated approximately 13% lower displacement compared to titanium. Increasing implant thickness led to a reduction in displacement of approximately 17% in both the Y-TZP and titanium groups. The influence of screw diameter remained minimal, with changes of less than 1% (Fig. 5B). Under anterior vertical loading, Y-TZP implants again exhibited lower displacement values, showing approximately 16% less deformation compared to titanium. Increasing implant thickness remained effective under this loading condition, resulting in displacement reductions of approximately 9–10% for both Y-TZP and titanium. The effect of increasing screw diameter was again limited, with a reduction of less than 1% in displacement values (Fig. 5C).

Maximum–minimum principal stress

Maximum (σ₁) and minimum (σ₃) principal stress values obtained under three loading conditions showed similar distribution in both materials, but differed in magnitude.

Under posterior vertical loading conditions, the highest maximum principal stress values were observed in the posterior alveolar crest region (Fig. 6). In this region, for the 1 mm plate–1.5 mm screw combination, the maximum principal stress was 62.933 MPa for Y-TZP and 64.585 MPa for titanium. In the same configuration, the minimum principal stress values were − 63.383 MPa (Y-TZP) and − 74.421 MPa (titanium), indicating that Y-TZP generated approximately 14.8% lower compressive stress. Increasing the implant thickness from 1 mm to 1.5 mm resulted in a reduction in maximum stress values for both materials; for instance, in the 1.5 mm plate–2.0 mm screw configuration, the maximum stress in the posterior region decreased to 49.365 MPa for Y-TZP and 51.640 MPa for titanium. In the same configuration, the minimum stress values were − 41.233 MPa for Y-TZP and − 49.922 MPa for titanium, corresponding to an approximately 17.4% lower value for Y-TZP. In the premolar region and around the piriform aperture, Y-TZP generally exhibited lower maximum and less negative minimum stress values, with minimum stress differences ranging between 3% and 25%.

Under posterior oblique loading, stress values were predominantly concentrated in the posterior and premolar regions (Fig. 7). In the posterior region, for the 1 mm plate–1.5 mm screw configuration, the maximum principal stress was 49.805 MPa for Y-TZP and 51.466 MPa for titanium, while the minimum principal stress values were − 57.209 MPa and − 66.329 MPa, respectively, indicating that Y-TZP produced approximately 13.7% lower compressive stress. With increasing plate thickness, maximum stress values decreased to 34.521 MPa for Y-TZP and 38.735 MPa for titanium, and the difference in minimum stress was reduced to approximately 3–4%. In the premolar region, titanium showed higher maximum stress values (e.g., 44.091 MPa), whereas Y-TZP exhibited lower values (36.044 MPa). In terms of minimum stress, Y-TZP demonstrated approximately 10–15% lower values. Around the piriform aperture, lower stress values were observed for both materials, with minimum stress differences generally ranging between 2% and 20%.

Under anterior vertical loading conditions, stress values were markedly lower compared to the other loading scenarios (Fig. 8). In the posterior region, for the 1 mm plate–1.5 mm screw configuration, the maximum principal stress was 15.831 MPa for Y-TZP and 19.471 MPa for titanium, while the minimum stress values were − 8.143 MPa and − 11.712 MPa, respectively, indicating that Y-TZP generated approximately 30.5% lower compressive stress. Increasing the implant thickness resulted in a limited reduction in maximum stress values; in the 1.5 mm plate–2.0 mm screw configuration, these values were 14.137 MPa for Y-TZP and 16.022 MPa for titanium. Under the same conditions, the difference in minimum stress was maintained in favor of Y-TZP at approximately 20–25%. Similar findings were observed in the premolar region and around the piriform aperture, where Y-TZP consistently exhibited lower maximum and less negative minimum stress values.

Von mises stresses

In subperiosteal implant systems, comparison of von Mises stresses between Y-TZP and titanium under different loading conditions demonstrated comparable stress distributions, whereas stress magnitudes varied between the materials.

Under posterior vertical loading, in the posterior alveolar crest region, the Y-TZP subperiosteal implant exhibited a stress of 509.3 MPa at a T/SD combination of 1.0/1.5 mm, whereas the corresponding value for titanium was 386.7 MPa, indicating that Y-TZP stress was approximately 32% higher than that of titanium. Increasing the screw diameter to 2.0 mm (1.0/2.0) maintained a similar difference of 32%, while increasing the implant thickness to 1.5 mm (1.5/1.5) reduced the difference to 25%, and simultaneous increases in both thickness and screw diameter (1.5/2.0) further reduced it to 24%. For the abutment component, Y-TZP stresses exhibited smaller differences compared to titanium, with values 6%, 4%, 3%, and 3% higher for the 1.0/1.5, 1.0/2.0, 1.5/1.5-, and 1.5/2.0-mm T/SD combinations, respectively. Differences in the metal framework were minimal, with Y-TZP demonstrating 1–5% higher stresses across all combinations. In the premolar alveolar crest and piriform aperture/nasal floor regions, subperiosteal implant stresses were 25–30% higher for Y-TZP, abutment stresses 2–10% higher, and metal framework stresses 1–8% higher than those of titanium (Table 3).

Discussion

This study utilized an in-silico methodology, emphasizing the development and verification of the computational model before conducting biomechanical analyses. Such simulations allow for a systematic evaluation of biomechanical parameters without being limited by the ethical and biological constraints associated with in vivo experiments [29]. FEA, as one of the computational methods, was employed due to its ability to accurately predict implant–bone interactions [30]. Subperiosteal implants, representing a critical treatment option for patients with severe alveolar bone atrophy where conventional dental implants cannot be applied, were evaluated [31]. Parameters directly influencing stress distribution in the bone and long-term stability, such as framework thickness and screw diameter, were also analyzed. Loading scenarios were standardized to compare the relative behavior of the system under different mechanical conditions and to evaluate performance differences between materials; thus, the obtained results are valuable for understanding clinical tendencies. It has been reported that functional masticatory forces in patients with atrophic maxilla are significantly lower compared to healthy individuals [32–34]. Although the loading values used in this study were based on masticatory force values reported in the literature, they may not fully reflect the functional forces observed in patients with severe maxillary atrophy. To simplify FE calculations and improve computational efficiency, the bone model was represented as a combined unit of cortical and cancellous bone, while still preserving a realistic representation of bone mechanics. Previous finite element studies [20, 22, 35–38] on subperiosteal implants have primarily focused on titanium frameworks and design-related parameters, without evaluating different materials under identical conditions. In contrast, zirconia has been extensively investigated in endosseous implant systems, whereas its application in subperiosteal configurations remains limited, making direct comparisons challenging. In the present study, titanium and zirconia were analyzed using the same implant design, enabling a more isolated assessment of material-dependent biomechanical behavior. The rationale for comparing Y-TZP with titanium is based on their distinct mechanical and biological characteristics in subperiosteal implant design. Zirconia is characterized by high hardness, corrosion resistance, and favorable biocompatibility, and may be considered an alternative in patients with metal hypersensitivity. However, due to its brittle nature and relatively low fracture toughness, zirconia may present mechanical limitations, particularly under increased functional loading. Wan et al. [39] reported that brittle ceramic materials such as zirconia may reduce stress transmission to the surrounding bone, while simultaneously exhibiting higher internal stress concentrations due to their limited capacity for plastic deformation. Similarly, recent findings [40] indicate that patient-specific Y-TZP subperiosteal implants can provide sufficient rigidity and load-bearing capacity, despite these inherent material constraints. The results of the present study demonstrate that zirconia reduces stress in the surrounding bone; however, this is accompanied by increased internal stress within the implant structure. This observation is consistent with previous findings on brittle materials and highlights the trade-off between minimizing bone stress and maintaining structural integrity. From a clinical perspective, the relevance of these reductions should be interpreted with caution, as the extent to which decreased stress and displacement values influence bone remodeling or long-term implant survival remains unclear. Titanium, on the other hand, demonstrates high strength, superior fatigue resistance, and well-documented long-term clinical reliability. The use of a brittle Y-TZP framework together with titanium screws may cause material incompatibility at the implant–screw interface and increase local stress concentrations compared with titanium–titanium interfaces. Therefore, special attention should be given to the design of this interface to improve mechanical compatibility and reduce potential complications. Possible solutions include surface treatment of titanium screws, zirconia screws, or titanium reinforcement at the screw contact areas. However, further experimental and clinical studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of these approaches. Considering these factors, material selection for subperiosteal implants should be based on a careful balance between mechanical performance and biological considerations. Within the limitations of this study, Y-TZP may represent a potential alternative material in selected cases; however, its mechanical behavior, particularly under functional loading conditions, warrants further investigation through experimental and clinical studies. The anatomical model was constructed based on CT data rather than CBCT, providing higher resolution and more reliable anatomical detail [41], thereby enhancing the accuracy of the three-dimensional geometries and the consistency of the FEA model. In the biomechanical evaluation of subperiosteal implants, total displacement, Von Mises stress, and maximum/minimum principal stresses were assessed.

Total displacement is a key parameter for evaluating the structural rigidity and load transfer capacity of subperiosteal implant systems. In this study, Y-TZP subperiosteal implants demonstrated a 10–25% reduction in total displacement across all loading conditions compared with titanium implants. This finding reflects the increased rigidity conferred by the higher elastic modulus of Y-TZP, which limits deformation transmitted to the bone and enhances implant stability. Reductions in displacement indicate improved mechanical performance; however, their direct clinical implications regarding bone remodeling, reduced bone resorption, or long-term implant survival cannot be conclusively established based on finite element analysis and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Under anterior vertical loading, displacement in the anterior region remained minimal, whereas posterior vertical and posterior oblique loading conditions resulted in localized deformation in the posterior region. This distribution indicates effective load transfer in anatomically supported areas and highlights the influence of higher bending moments in the posterior extensions. From a structural perspective, increasing the implant framework thickness and fixation screw diameter enhanced overall system rigidity, reducing total displacement by approximately 15–25% across all loading conditions. These findings are in line with previous studies reporting that subperiosteal implants provide sufficient mechanical stability under physiological occlusal forces, while a shift from vertical to oblique loading increases implant displacement [20, 35]. This observation aligns with literature indicating that implants and fixation components with larger cross-sectional areas distribute applied loads more effectively, thereby limiting elastic deformation [42]. Optimization of framework thickness and screw diameter increases the load-bearing cross-sectional area, reduces elastic deformation, and consequently enhances the overall stability of the implant system.

Beyond displacement behavior, principal stress analysis provides further insight into bone loading patterns and the mechanical response of the implant–bone interface. In the present study, the maximum (σ₁) and minimum (σ₃) principal stress analyses revealed similar distribution patterns for both Y-TZP and titanium implants across different loading conditions, although notable differences in stress magnitudes were observed. Across all loading scenarios, the posterior alveolar crest consistently showed the highest maximum principal stresses, reflecting the anatomical concentration of occlusal forces and the influence of bending moments in posterior extensions. Y-TZP generally generated lower compressive stresses compared to titanium, with reductions ranging from approximately 13% to 30% depending on implant thickness, screw diameter, and loading direction. Under posterior vertical loading, Y-TZP demonstrated slightly lower compressive stress than titanium in the posterior region, indicating that its higher rigidity and elastic modulus more effectively limited excessive bone compression. Increasing implant thickness and screw diameter further reduced maximum and minimum principal stresses for both materials, highlighting the critical role of structural design parameters in stress mitigation. A similar trend was observed under posterior oblique loading, with stress concentrations primarily in the posterior and premolar regions. Y-TZP exhibited lower minimum stress values (approximately 10–15% lower in premolar regions) and generally lower maximum stresses in critical areas, suggesting enhanced load distribution and reduced risk of local bone overload. Anterior vertical loading resulted in overall lower stress magnitudes compared to posterior loading, yet Y-TZP continued to demonstrate a notable reduction in compressive stresses, particularly in the posterior region (approximately 30% lower). This indicates that Y-TZP implants effectively protect alveolar bone from excessive compressive forces, even in areas with limited vertical support. Across the premolar region and around the piriform aperture, Y-TZP also showed lower maximum and less negative minimum stress values, supporting its favorable load transfer characteristics. Studies evaluating monoblock and dual-block subperiosteal implants have reported that monoblock designs, due to their higher rigidity, tend to concentrate stresses locally around fixation screws, connection necks, and the implant framework, whereas dual-block designs provide better passive adaptation to the bone surface, allowing loads to be distributed over a wider area and enhancing mechanical stability [43–46]. In severely atrophic jaws, the thinned cortical bone and limited load-bearing capacity of trabecular bone make stresses at the implant–bone interface clinically more critical. Literature indicates that the maximum tensile strength of cortical bone is approximately 130.1 ± 24.9 MPa, and its compressive strength is around 128.3 ± 29.8 MPa [47–51]; however, in the presence of advanced atrophy, these threshold values should be considered clinically lower. Maintaining tensile stresses below these critical thresholds is essential to minimize the risk of microfracture formation and bone resorption. Rigid implant materials and increased implant thickness tend to direct applied loads toward the implant framework and abutment components rather than the bone, which is clinically acceptable provided that the implant and its connecting components are mechanically robust. Previous studies have suggested that reducing stresses transmitted to the bone can decrease the risk of peri-implant bone loss and implant failure [52]. In the present study, across all three loading scenarios, the maximum tensile and compressive stresses measured in cortical bone remained below these reference thresholds, indicating that the dual-block subperiosteal implant designs evaluated are within the biomechanical tolerance limits of the bone. These findings indicate that the reduced bone stress associated with Y-TZP may be particularly beneficial in patients with severe alveolar atrophy, where preserving the remaining bone is critical. However, the concomitant increase in internal stresses within the implant framework underscores the importance of carefully optimizing implant thickness, screw diameter, and placement to balance bone protection with long-term mechanical stability. The use of dual-block designs, combined with the higher rigidity of Y-TZP, facilitates a more homogeneous stress distribution, mitigates local overload, and ensures that critical biomechanical thresholds of atrophic cortical bone are not exceeded.

While principal stress analysis reflects the loading conditions within the bone, von Mises stress provides complementary information regarding stress distribution within the implant components. Von Mises stress analysis indicated that Y-TZP subperiosteal implants generally exhibited higher stress magnitudes compared to titanium implants, while the overall stress distribution patterns remained comparable across various loading conditions. The posterior alveolar crest, particularly under posterior vertical and oblique loading, displayed the highest stress values, reflecting the influence of bending moments in the posterior extensions of the implant system. Y-TZP stresses were approximately 24–32% higher than titanium in the implant body, while differences in the abutment and metal framework components were more limited, generally ranging from 1 to 16%. Under anterior vertical loading, Y-TZP stresses in the implant body were moderately elevated, whereas abutment stresses were slightly lower than titanium in some scenarios, and metal framework stresses exhibited minimal increases. These findings indicate that the high elastic modulus and rigidity of Y-TZP reduce the load transmitted to the bone, particularly in regions with limited alveolar support, resulting in stress concentration within the implant structure. Consequently, Y-TZP offers advantages in preserving bone integrity; however, careful selection of framework thickness and screw dimensions is critical to mitigate potential mechanical risks associated with increased internal stresses. These results are supported by previous FEA studies reporting that rigid materials enhance bone preservation while increasing internal stresses within the implant framework and fixation components. Altıparmak et al. [26] compared subperiosteal implants fabricated from different materials and reported that rigid titanium implants reduced the stresses transmitted to the bone but generated higher von Mises stresses within the implant body. Similarly, El-Sawy et al. [53] demonstrated that high-modulus titanium in digitally designed subperiosteal implants decreased stresses in the underlying bone while increasing stresses in the implant body and fixation screws. In line with these findings, Demir and Çağlar [54] showed that zirconia frameworks produced lower stresses in bone, implants, and prosthetic screws, indicating a more balanced biomechanical performance. Therefore, although the use of rigid materials such as Y-TZP provides advantages for bone preservation, careful optimization of framework thickness, screw diameter, and implant positioning remains essential to ensure long-term mechanical safety and biomechanical performance.

Screw diameter in subperiosteal implants has been identified as a critical design parameter that significantly affects the biomechanical behavior of the implant–bone system. Larger-diameter screws increase the bone–screw contact area, distributing applied loads over a wider surface and reducing stress concentrations in the bone; however, this may increase stress accumulation at the implant framework and screw–framework interfaces [55]. Clinically, 2.0 mm titanium screws are commonly preferred as they provide adequate primary stability while limiting excessive stress transmission to the bone [56]. In cases of severely compromised bone quality or insufficient primary stability, 2.3–2.4 mm “emergency” screws may be used [57], although this can increase stresses within the implant framework. Kundakcioglu et al. [58] reported that 2.0 mm screws reduced bone stress but increased implant displacement, whereas 1.5 mm screws produced higher bone stress but lower displacement. In the present study, increasing screw diameter was observed to reduce stresses in both bone and implant, while transferring loads to more rigid structures led to greater stress accumulation in the metal framework. Similarly, the effect of implant thickness on biomechanical performance has been emphasized. In maxillofacial surgery, mini-plate systems are generally designed within a 1.0–2.0 mm range, with plates ≥ 2.0 mm reserved for high-load reconstruction scenarios [37]. These findings highlight that careful selection of screw diameter and implant thickness is critical to balance bone preservation with the increased internal stresses in the implant framework and connection components. Clinically, implants with 1.0–1.5 mm thickness can facilitate soft tissue adaptation while maintaining biomechanical safety, similar to mini-plate systems. Literature indicates that 1.0 mm-thick subperiosteal implants often exceed yield stress, increasing stresses transmitted to the bone, whereas implants with 1.5–2.0 mm thickness remain within safe biomechanical limits [20, 59]. Implants with a thickness of 2.0 mm have been reported to provide the most favorable stress distribution and minimal deformation, with at least 1.5 mm required for mechanical stability; however, the literature recommends keeping implant thickness as minimal as possible to reduce the risk of gingival recession associated with subperiosteal placement. Load transfer to the implant framework is particularly advantageous in severely atrophic jaws, where bone volume and density are limited. However, increased thickness may lead to contour prominence, mucosal irritation, palpable metallic sensation, and esthetic concerns in atrophic jaws with limited soft tissue volume, and may also increase wound tension and delay healing [38]. Although reducing Y-TZP thickness may improve anatomical adaptability in severely atrophic cases, the present results indicate that decreasing the framework to 1.0 mm significantly increases stress concentration and may elevate the risk of mechanical failure due to the inherent brittleness of zirconia. Therefore, clinical decision-making should rely on a patient-specific risk–benefit balance, where implant thickness is selected to ensure sufficient structural resistance without compromising anatomical fit and surgical feasibility.

This study has several inherent limitations related to the use of finite element analysis. Owing to the severely atrophic condition of the bone in the DICOM datasets, differentiation between cortical and trabecular bone could not be achieved, and the bone was therefore modeled as a homogeneous structure. Fixation screws were simulated under idealized conditions, positioned perpendicular to the bone surface to ensure methodological standardization; angled screw placements, which are frequently encountered in clinical practice, were not represented. Moreover, all analyses were conducted under static loading conditions inherent to the FEA methodology. Consequently, the results should be interpreted with caution and corroborated by in vitro mechanical testing, dynamic and fatigue analyses, as well as in-vivo long-term clinical studies.

Conclusions

Implant thickness, screw diameter, and material stiffness were found to be key determinants of the biomechanical behavior of patient-specific subperiosteal implants, particularly in severely atrophic maxilla. The comparative analysis between Y-TZP and titanium subperiosteal implants provides biomechanical evidence extending beyond the predominantly titanium-based literature. Overall, Y-TZP demonstrated favorable biomechanical performance; however, further experimental investigations and long-term clinical studies are necessary to validate these findings and to confirm their clinical relevance.
