Solutions of the thin film equation obtained in the limit of vanishing slip
Hans Knüpfer, Juan J. L. Velázquez

Abstract
. We analyze the evolution of thin liquid droplets in the lubrication approximation with different slip conditions at the liquid–solid interface. Motivated by the classical no-slip paradox which states that the Navier–Stokes equations with a no-slip boundary condition require unphysical infinite dissipation during droplet spreading, we focus on the limit of vanishing slip. We show that in the no–slip limit three fundamentally different classes of limiting solutions are approached, each of them corresponding to a different scaling of the microscopic contact angle as the regularization parameter vanishes. These findings suggest that the thin-film equation with no slip supports a rich family of physically admissible solutions, provided one interprets the no–slip thin film equation as the asymptotic limit of models which regularized slip conditions. Even though the large apparent contact angles in some of these solutions seem incompatible with the lubrication approximation, a refined analysis shows that the underlying physical variables remain consistent with the assumptions for the lubrication approximation.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Introduction

We consider the evolution of a liquid droplet on a solid substrate. In the
classical fluid dynamical description, the evolution in the fluid is described
by the Navier–Stokes equations, while a no slip condition is assumed at the
liquid–solid interface. It has been observed that such a no–slip condition
allows for droplet spreading only under the seemingly unphysical condition of
infinite dissipation of energy, e.g.
[
51
]
. This apparent

no slip paradox
has led to many discussions about possible resolutions
of this paradox. We want to discuss some related issues. As one way to
avoid the no–slip paradox different relaxed slip conditions have been proposed
where the tangential velocity, relative to the solid, at the interface is a
function of the tangential shear stress. In particular, we consider the
evolution of a thin capillary driven droplet in the so–called lubrication
approximation regime. Assuming a no slip condition, in this regime the evolution
of the droplet can be described by the thin–film equation

(2)

\displaystyle h_{t}+(h^{3}h_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{in $\Omega\ :=\ \{h>0\}$.}

Here,
h
is the droplet height and the continuity assumption
h=0
is assumed
at
\partial\Omega
. Assuming more general slip conditions, the lubrication
approximation leads to the family of equations

(3)

\displaystyle h_{t}+((h^{3}+\epsilon^{3-n}h^{n})h_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{in $\Omega\ :=\ \{h>0\}$,}

The mobility exponent
n\in(0,3)
models the slip condition at the
liquid–solid interface:
n=2
represents Navier–slip where a linear
slip/stress relationship is assumed, while
n=1
corresponds to Greenspan’s
slip condition
[
44
]
. The regularization parameter
\epsilon>0
,
which is assumed to be small, is called
slip length
(see Section

2
). We recover the equation (
2
) in the limit

\epsilon\to 0
. Equation (
3
) has to be complemented by a boundary
condition at
\partial\Omega
. We assume the constant angle condition

(4)

\displaystyle h_{x}\ =\ \theta_{\epsilon}\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{at $\partial\Omega\ =\ \partial\{h>0\}$.}

We note that the boundary condition (
4
) for (
2
) leads
to an ill–posed problem
[
23
]
. Throughout the paper, for
both equations (
2
) and (
3
) we also use the boundary
condition

(5)

\displaystyle h^{n}h_{xxx}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $x\in\Omega$, $x\to\partial\Omega$.}

This condition ensures that there is no loss of mass across the contact points

\partial\Omega
. For further references we refer e.g. to

[
29
,
51
,
49
,
24
]
, for nonlinear
slip see e.g.
[
72
]
. We note that other regularizations of
the no–slip paradox exist which are not considered here, see
e.g.
[
62
,
74
,
61
,
53
]
. For
existence of solutions for (
2
) we refer e.g. to

[
7
,
3
,
8
,
16
,
46
,
35
,
42
,
43
]
. The
literature is discussed more in detail in the next section.

More in particular, we study solutions of (
2
) which appear as a
limit of solutions for (
3
) as
\epsilon\to 0
. Our arguments are
formal in the sense that they are based on the use of matched asymptotic
expansions. For simplicity, we consider solutions with an isolated contact
point
s(t)
at the left boundary of the support, i.e

\Omega\cap B_{\delta}(s(t))=[s(t),s(t)+\delta)
for some
\delta>0
. We obtain the following three types of solutions of (
2
) and
their asymptotic behaviour near the contact point:

(a)

The support is constant,
s(t)=s_{0}
. Furthermore,

(6)

\displaystyle h(x,t)\ \approx\ \gamma(t)\left(x-s_{0}\right)\qquad\qquad\text{ as $x\to s_{0}^{+}$}.

for some smooth function
\gamma(t)
(see the paragraph on notation below
for the meaning of the symbol “
\approx
”). We recall the well–known result
that the support is constant for sufficiently regular solutions (Theorem

3.2
).

(b)

The contact point
s(t)
is an arbitrary, monotonically increasing,
prescribed smooth function (this corresponds to a shrinking droplet). The
solutions have asymptotic behaviour

(7)

\displaystyle h(x,t)\ \approx\ (3\dot{s}(t))^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(x-s(t)\right)\Big(\ln\frac{1}{x-s(t)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\qquad\qquad\text{ as $x\to s(t)^{+}$}.

(with notation
\dot{s}=\frac{d}{dt}s
) We remark that the possibility of
having travelling wave solutions with receding support, as the type (b)
solutions was already suggested in
[
11
]
and obtained by
matched asymptotics in
[
53
]
. It turns out that the same
initial data allows for infinitely many solutions.

(c)

The interface
s
is also monotonically increasing and moves in a shorter
time scale
\tau
than
t,
that will be denoted as
\tau
and the motion as

s\left(\tau\right)
. The mass accumulates in the moving interface

s(\tau)
. Therefore (with the notation
s^{\prime}:=\frac{d}{d\tau}s
)

(8)

\displaystyle h(x,\tau)=m(\tau)\delta_{s(\tau)}(x)+h_{0}(x)\chi_{\{x>s\left(\tau\right)\}}\ \ \text{with}\ m^{\prime}(\tau)=h_{0}(s(\tau))s^{\prime}(\tau)

For these solutions the interface moves so fast that the profile
h_{0}
does
not have time to change. In this case all the mass of
h_{0}
that is reduced due to the reduction of the support accumulates at the
interface boundary. This requires a more sophisticated analysis, see the
upcoming paper
[
57
]
.

The above solutions of (
2
) appear as limit of solutions of
(
3
) and are related to the asymptotic behaviour of the contact
angle
\theta_{\epsilon}
in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
.

In fact, a contact angle
\theta_{\epsilon}\sim 1
of order corresponds to type (a)
solutions. A contact angle of size
\theta_{\epsilon}\gg|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{1}{3}}

leads to type (c) solutions. A contact angle in the intermediate regime

\theta_{\epsilon}\approx\gamma|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{1}{3}}
leads to type (b)
solutions. The contact point velocity in the intermediate regime is given by

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{s}\ =\ +\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\theta_{\epsilon}^{3}}{3|\ln\epsilon|}\ =\ +\frac{\gamma}{3}\ >\ 0,\qquad\text{where $\theta_{\epsilon}\approx\gamma(-\ln\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{3}}$.}

We believe that type (a) solutions also appear in the remaining case where

1\ll\theta_{\epsilon}\ll|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{1}{3}}
but this case is not treated in this
paper. We also note that the limits above might only exist for a subsequence

\epsilon_{k}\to 0
where the limiting angle has a well–defined behaviour. Assuming
that (
3
) is a valid physical regularization of (
2
)
this implies that the three types of solutions (a), (b) and (c) are physically
feasible as well.

Remark 1.1

(Time scales)
.

The evolution exhibits two time scales. In our (non–dimensionalized) model,
the droplet profile
h
changes in
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
time scales, i.e. times scales of
order one. The motion of the contact point, however, generally lives on a
different time scale which depends on the value of the microscopic contact
angle. This second time scale is associated with large times for type (a)
solutions,
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
times scales for type (b) solutions and much shorter times
for type (c) solutions.

Equations (
2
) and (
3
) describe an evolution which is
governed by the dissipation of capillary energy. The energy–dissipation formula
for (
3
) has the form

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}h_{x}^{2}+\theta_{\epsilon}^{2}\ dx\ =\ -\int_{\Omega}(h^{3}+\epsilon^{3-n}h^{n})h_{xxx}^{2}\ dx,

assuming that the solutions are sufficiently regular. Here, the two terms on the
left–hand side describe the (relative) interfacial energies between the three
phases air, liquid and solid. The right–hand side describes the dissipation of
energy due to viscous friction. For type (b) solutions of (
2
) we
obtain that the rate of dissipation of energy near the contact point is
divergent for type (b) solutions, i.e. for any
\delta>0
we have

(11)

\displaystyle\int_{s(t)}^{s(t)+\delta}h^{3}h_{xxx}^{2}\ dx\ =\ \infty.

At a first glance this would indicate that the type (b) solutions cannot have a
physical meaning and therefore must be discharged. However, the situation is
more complicated: We note that the contact angle
\theta_{\epsilon}
is determined by
the classical Young’s condition, and hence depends on the interfacial energies of
liquid–solid, liquid-gas and solid–gas interfaces. If
\theta_{\epsilon}
diverges
logarithmically, then the energy related to liquid–solid interfaces is much
larger than the energy related to solid–gas interfaces (with a logarithmic
divergence). In the dissipation of energy formula (
10
) the
dissipation is finite for each
\epsilon>0,
but it diverges logarithmically as

\epsilon\to 0
if
\theta_{\epsilon}\sim|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{1}{3}}
. This dissipation
compensates for the increase of energy that results from the reduction of the
support of the droplet due to the reduction of the solid-liquid interface. Thus,
the divergent contributions of this term and the one due to the dissipation term
on the right–hand side cancel out in leading order as
\epsilon\to 0
. This
cancellation of the increase of energy due to the reduction of the support of
the droplet with the large dissipation will be described in detail in Subsection

2.2
. This cancellation explains why it is possible to have
physically admissible limiting solutions for which (
11
) is divergent.

Our formal arguments suggest that these three types of solutions are the only
possible solutions of (
2
) in the absence of fluxes across the
contact point. We give some formal arguments for the existence of these three
types of solutions for equation (
2
) and we will also give some
arguments how these solutions appear as a limit for solutions of
(
3
). A rigorous proof for the existence of type (a) solution will
be given in the upcoming paper
[
34
]
. We also note that the
solutions of types (a), (b) and (c) are consistent with the lubrication
approximation. This is not clear a priori in the case of the type (b) and (c)
solutions, because the angle between the liquid-gas interface and the
liquid–solid interface is large which would not be compatible with the
lubrication approximation, that requires to have nearly one-dimensional
flows. However, a more careful analysis shows that this is not the case for the
initial variables (cf. Remark
2.1
).

2.1.
Lubrication approximation

We recall the lubrication approximation that is used to derive the equation
(
3
) for
n=2
taking as starting point the free boundary problem
for the Stokes or Navier-Stokes equations describing the evolution of a droplet
on a solid substrate (see Fig.
1
). Since in the derivation of
the thin film equation, the inertial terms give a negligible contribution, we
will consider only the free boundary problem for the Stokes problem. For
simplicity, we consider the two-dimensional case. Since this derivation is
standard we will just indicate the main steps, see also

[
38
,
47
,
59
,
55
,
56
]
. We
assume that the geometry of the fluid domain is

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}(T)\ :=\ \big\{X\in(S_{1},S_{2}),\ 0<Y<H(X,T)\big\},

where
H
is the droplet height, satisfying
\spt H=[S_{1}(T),S_{2}(T)]
also
including the case
S_{2}(T)=\infty
. We write

\partial_{1}{\mathcal{U}}:=\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}\cap\{Y>0\}
for the liquid–gas interface and

\partial_{0}{\mathcal{U}}:=\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}\cap\{Y=0\}
for the solid–liquid interface. The
evolution is given by the free boundary problem driven by the Stokes flow

(15)

\displaystyle\mu\Delta V\

\displaystyle=\nabla P,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V

\displaystyle=\ 0\ \

\displaystyle\text{in\ \ }{\mathcal{U}}(t)

(16)

\displaystyle V_{1}\

\displaystyle=\ \beta^{n}|\partial_{Y}V_{1}|^{n-2}\partial_{Y}V_{1},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=\ 0

\displaystyle\text{on $\partial_{0}{\mathcal{U}}(t)$},

(17)

\displaystyle 2\mu{\mathcal{S}}N\

\displaystyle=(P-\gamma_{LG}\kappa)N

\displaystyle\text{on $\partial_{1}{\mathcal{U}}(t)$},

(18)

\displaystyle V_{N}\

\displaystyle=\ V\cdot N

\displaystyle\text{on $\partial_{1}{\mathcal{U}}(t)$},

(19)

\displaystyle H_{X}\

\displaystyle=\ \tan\Theta\

\displaystyle\text{at $\partial_{0}{\mathcal{U}}\cap\partial_{1}{\mathcal{U}}$},

where
{\mathcal{S}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla V+\nabla^{t}V)
. Here,

is the fluid

P(X,T)
is the pressure,
\kappa
is the curvature of the interface,

its normal and

is the normal velocity of the interface. Furthermore,

is the viscosity,

the surface tension of the liquid-gas

We make the following assumptions:

(i)

Horizontal length scales are much larger than vertical length scales. In
terms of the non–dimensional variable this implies

(22)

\displaystyle\delta\ :=\ \frac{{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}}\ \ll\ 1,\qquad\qquad

We assume that a corresponding relation also holds for the derivatives of the
profile, e.g.
|H_{XX}|\lesssim{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}/{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}^{2}
.

(ii)

The evolution is driven by a balance of capillary and viscous forces. The
dominance of viscous forces has been already used by the choice of Stokes flow
for the initial model

(iii)

We also assume that the maximal thickness of the droplet is much larger
than the slip length, i.e. we consider the regime

(23)

\displaystyle\epsilon\ :=\ \frac{\beta}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}}.

\epsilon\ll 1

thought as a function of
\epsilon
, i.e.

We also choose typical length scales
{\mathfrak{s}}_{p}
and
{\mathfrak{s}}_{t}

(24)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{s}}_{p}\ :=\ \frac{\gamma_{LG}{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}}\ =\ \gamma_{LG}\delta,\quad\qquad{\mathfrak{s}}_{t}\ :=\ \frac{\gamma_{LG}{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}\mu}\ =\ \frac{\gamma_{LG}\delta}{\mu}.

We introduce the rescaled (non–dimensional) variables

(25)

\displaystyle\big(x,y,h,t,v_{1},v_{2},p,\theta,\Omega\big)\ :=\ \Big(\frac{X}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}},\frac{Y}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}},\frac{H}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}},\frac{T}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{t}},\ \frac{V_{1}{\mathfrak{s}}_{t}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}},\frac{V_{2}{\mathfrak{s}}_{t}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{y}},\ \frac{P}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{p}},\frac{\Theta}{\delta},\frac{\partial_{0}{\mathcal{U}}}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}}\Big).

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}\

\displaystyle\approx\ \delta^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\delta^{2}v_{1,x}&\frac{1}{2}v_{1,y}\vskip 2.15277pt\\
\frac{1}{2}v_{1,y}&\delta^{2}v_{2,y}\end{pmatrix},\qquad N\ \approx\ \left(\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{r}-\delta h_{x}\\

\delta h_{x}\end{array}\hskip-5.0pt\right).

\displaystyle\delta^{2}(N^{\perp}\cdot{\mathcal{S}}N)\

\displaystyle=\ -\delta h_{x}v_{1,x}+\frac{1}{2}v_{1,y}(1+\delta^{2}h_{x}^{2})+\delta h_{x}v_{2,y},

(31)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}(N\cdot{\mathcal{S}}N)\

\displaystyle=\ \delta^{4}h_{x}^{2}v_{1,x}^{2}+\delta h_{x}v_{1,y}+\delta^{2}v_{2,y}.

From the boundary condition (

v_{1,y}=0

. The second momentum equation yields

for

\displaystyle\mu v_{1}\ =\ h_{xxx}\Big(\frac{1}{2}y^{2}-hy-\epsilon h\Big)\qquad\text{for $y\in(0,h)$. }

The equation for conservation of mass can be written in the form

\partial_{t}h+v_{1}h_{x}=v_{2}

(33)

\displaystyle h_{t}\ =\ -\Big(\int_{0}^{h}v_{1}\ dy\Big)_{x}\ =\ -\Big(\big(\frac{1}{6}h^{3}+\epsilon h^{2}\big)h_{xxx}\Big)_{x}.\

It might seem inconsistent that we allow for large contact angles in the
lubrication approximation. However, this contradiction is resolved by the
difference between physical and rescaled variables as indicated in Section

Remark 2.1

(Applicability and rescaled contact angle)
.

For the lubrication approximation to be a good approximation, the droplet
profile has to be flat and in particular the contact angle has to be small in
the initial variables. Due to the anisotropic rescaling, the droplet is,
however, not necessarily flat in the rescaled variables and in particular the
rescaled contact angle
\theta
is not necessarily small. In fact, we consider
the limit
\epsilon\to 0
where the angle
\theta_{\epsilon}
is a second free parameter
which can take different values in the limit. The smallness of
\delta
then
ensures the flatness of the liquid droplet.

Since the contact angle is determined by Young’s Law we can also phrase the
regimes (a),(b) and (c) from the introduction in terms of the interfacial
energies.

Remark 2.2

(Lubrication approximation and interfacial energies)
.

For the validity of the lubrication approximation we need

\frac{1}{2}\Theta^{2}\approx 1-\cos\Theta
. In view of Young’s Law
(
20
) this is the same as

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{LG}}(\gamma_{LG}+\gamma_{SL}-\gamma_{SG})\ \approx\ \frac{\Theta^{2}}{2}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\ \to\ 0^{+}\ \qquad\text{as\ $\epsilon\to 0$}

Type (b) solutions are then e.g. obtained in the regime when

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{LG}}(\gamma_{LG}+\gamma_{SL}-\gamma_{SG})\ \approx\ \gamma\delta^{2}|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}\qquad\qquad\text{as\ $\epsilon\to 0$}

In particular, the interfacial energies
\gamma_{SG}
,
\gamma_{SL}
,
\gamma_{LG}

need to be adjusted in a very careful way to obtain the dynamics of the
interface by means of (
3
). In particular, type (b) solutions
appear only if condition (
35
) holds.

2.2.
Dissipation of energy

Solutions of the initial Stokes system dissipate the total interfacial energy
(
21

(
21

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathcal{E}}[H]}{\gamma_{LG}{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}\delta^{2}}\ \hskip 0.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{energy-init}}}{{=}}\ \frac{1}{{\mathfrak{s}}_{x}\delta^{2}}\int_{\partial_{0}{\mathcal{U}}}\sqrt{1+H_{X}^{2}}-\cos\Theta\ dX\ \approx\ {\mathcal{F}}[h],

where the thin–film energy is given by

(37)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}[h]\ :=\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}h_{x}^{2}+\theta^{2}\ dx.

The thin film equation (
3

identity with respect to quantity on the right–hand side above even for weak
solutions. A definition for weak solutions in the partial wetting regime has
been given by Bertsch, Giacomelli and Shishkov in

[

. Here, we recall this identity for
classical solutions with sufficient regularity. In fact, let

,

(38)

\displaystyle\,\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{F}}[h]\ =\ -\int_{\Omega}(h^{3}+\epsilon^{3-n}h^{n})h_{xxx}^{2}\ dx.

To see these we may assume without loss of generality we assume
\Omega=(s_{-},s_{+})
. Using
(

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}h_{x}^{2}+\theta_{\epsilon}^{2}\ dx\ =\ \int_{\Omega}h_{tx}h_{x}=-\int_{\Omega}((h^{3}+\epsilon^{3-n}h^{n})h_{xxx})_{xx}h_{x}\ dx.

Integrating by parts two more times yields (
38

the integration by parts for the last identity vanish by assumption on the
regularity of the solution. We now discuss the dissipation of energy for type
(a) solutions.

Remark 2.3

(Dissipation of energy for type (a) solutions)
.

Suppose that
\Omega(t)
is a bounded interval. In this case, formal analysis
Tanner (see Section
4.2
below) indicates that

•

in the first stage where
t={\mathcal{O}}(1)
,
|\Omega(t)|
remains nearly
constant, and most of the dissipation takes place through the term

\int_{\Omega(t)}h^{3}h_{xxx}^{2}dx
via reduction of the energy

\int_{\Omega(t)}h_{x}^{2}dx
.

•

During the second stage where
t={\mathcal{O}}(|\ln\epsilon|
),
h_{xxx}
is
approximately zero in the bulk of the droplet, and most of the dissipation
takes place at
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
–distance from
\partial\Omega(t)
. This dissipation rate
is of order
|\ln\epsilon|^{-1}
and is associated to
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
changes of

|\Omega(t)|
in times of order
|\ln\epsilon|
. This can be seen with the change
of variables
\epsilon y=x-s
,

(see (

Type (b) solutions are obtained in the regime

\theta_{\epsilon}\approx\gamma|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{1}{3}}\ll 1
. For type (b) solutions
the relaxes energy by reducing the size of the solid–liquid interface. The
energy gained in this way is infinitely (logarithmically) large and it is
compensated with the infinite (logarithmic) dissipation in the fluid.

Lemma 2.4

(Energy dissipation for type (b) solutions)
.

Let
h
be a solution of (
2
) with support
(s,\infty)
for
some function
s=s(t)
. We assume that
h
is a type (b) in the sense that
for
\xi:=x-s
we have

(40)

\displaystyle h(\xi,t)\ \approx\ (3\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}\xi|\ln\xi|^{\frac{1}{3}}\qquad\text{for $\xi\ll 1$.}

We also assume that the time derivative
h_{t}
and up to three spatial
derivatives of
h
have the asymptotic behaviour, obtained by applying these
derivatives on the right–hand side above. Furthermore, the solution and its
derivatives decay sufficiently fast for
\xi\gg 1
. Then the limit below
exists and is finite and

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}h_{x}^{2}\ dx\ =\ \lim_{\delta\to 0}\Big(\frac{\dot{s}}{2}h_{x}^{2}(\delta)-\int_{s+\delta}^{\infty}h^{3}h_{xxx}^{2}\ dx\Big).

Proof.

By assumption, for
\xi\ll 1
we have

h_{\xi\xi\xi}\approx\frac{1}{3}(3\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}\xi^{-2}|\ln\xi|^{-\frac{2}{3}}
and

(42)

\displaystyle h_{\xi}h_{t}\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}(\xi|\ln\xi|^{\frac{2}{3}})\ =\ o(1),

(43)

\displaystyle h^{3}h_{\xi\xi}h_{\xi\xi\xi}\ =\ {\mathcal{O}}(|\ln\xi|^{-\frac{1}{3}})\ =\ o(1).

where
o(1)\to 0
as
\xi\to 0
. For
\xi>0
we have

h_{t}=h_{\xi}\dot{s}-(h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi})_{\xi}
. Using this identity, for

\delta>0
we hence obtain

\displaystyle\,\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h_{\xi}^{2}\ dx\

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p.i.}}{{=}}\ -\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h_{\xi\xi}h_{t}+\left[h_{\xi}h_{t}\right]_{\delta}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\hskip-3.44444pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{asy1}}}{{=}}\ -\dot{s}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h_{\xi\xi}h_{\xi}\ d\xi+\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h_{\xi\xi}(h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi})_{\xi}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\dot{s}}{2}h_{\xi}^{2}(\delta)-\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi}^{2}\ d\xi-\left[h^{3}h_{\xi\xi}h_{\xi\xi\xi}\right]_{\delta}^{\infty}+o(1)

\displaystyle\hskip-3.44444pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{asy3}}}{{=}}\ \frac{\dot{s}}{2}h_{\xi}^{2}(\delta)-\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi}^{2}\ d\xi+o(1),

where
o(1)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
. For
\delta\to 0
we obtain the asserted
identity. Furthermore, we note that we have the integral identity

(44)

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\xi|\ln\xi|^{\frac{1}{3}}}\ \approx\ \frac{3}{2}|\ln\delta|^{\frac{2}{3}}\qquad\qquad\text{for $\delta\ll 1$}

In particular, for
\xi\to 0
a straightforward calculation yields

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{s}}{2}h_{\xi}^{2}(\delta)-\int_{\delta}^{\infty}h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi}^{2}\ d\xi\ \approx\ \frac{1}{6}(3\dot{s})^{\frac{5}{3}}|\ln\delta|^{\frac{2}{3}}-\frac{1}{6}(3\dot{s})^{\frac{5}{3}}|\ln\delta|^{\frac{2}{3}}+{\mathcal{O}}(1).

i.e. the cancellation of the leading order terms shows that the limit in
leading order exists and is finite.
∎

Generally, the interfacial energies between liquid and solid might depend on the
horizontal variable. The contact angle then also depends on the position. In

then has the form

\displaystyle E(h)\ =\ \int_{s(t)}^{\infty}h_{x}^{2}\ dx+|\ln\epsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}\int_{s(t)}^{\infty}\psi(x)\ dx.

3.1.
Sufficient conditions for non–spreading of solutions

We consider sufficient conditions for the non–spreading of solutions. Since we
do not consider the asymptotic behaviour
\epsilon\to 0
here, we can equivalently
discuss the issue for the equation (
12

near the boundary. It is not possible to have smooth solutions with a finite

\displaystyle D\ =\ \left\{(x,t):s(t)<x<s(t)+\delta,\ 0<t<T\right\}

for some

and a single contact point

, i.e. we consider

(48)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &h_{t}+(h^{n}h_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ 0\qquad&&\text{in $D$},\\
&h\ =\ 0\qquad&&\text{for $x=s(t)$}.\end{aligned}

The no–slip paradox is the conjecture that the no–slip condition at the
liquid–solid interface prevents movement of the contact line since this would
lead to infinite dissipation of energy. In the perspective of the thin–film
equation we can phrase it in the following way:

Conjecture 3.1

(Finite dissipation implies fixed support)
.

Let
n\geq 3
,
\delta>0,\ T>0
. Let
h\in C^{0}(\overline{D})
and
s\in C^{0}((0,T))
be
a classical and positive in
D
solution of (
48
) with

(49)

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\int_{D}h^{n}h_{xxx}^{2}\ dxdt\ <\ \infty.

Then
s(t)=s_{0}
is constant in
(0,T)
.

We have assumed continuity of the height profile. Alternatively, one could also
assume finiteness of the thin–film energy. The next theorem was discussed
during a workshop in the Lorentz Center
[
20
]
. In particular,
J. Eggers asked if under general smoothness conditions one can prove that the
support of the solution does not expand. For the convenience of the reader we
present a short and simple proof:

Theorem 3.2

(Regularity implies fixed support)
.

Let
n\geq 3
,
\delta>0,\ T>0
and let
h\in C_{x,t}^{4,1}(\overline{D})
with

s\in C^{1}((0,T))
be a solution of (
48
) such that
(
5
) holds. Furthermore, suppose that for some positive

\gamma\in C^{1}((0,T))
and
R_{1},R_{2}\in C_{x,t}^{0,1}(D)
we have

(50)

\displaystyle h(x,t)\

\displaystyle=\ \gamma(x-s(t))+R_{1}(x,t)\qquad\text{where $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to s(t)^{+}}\frac{R_{1}(x,t)}{x-s(t)}\ =\ 0$},

(51)

\displaystyle h_{t}(x,t)\

\displaystyle=-\gamma\dot{s}(t)+R_{2}(x,t)\ \qquad\qquad\text{where $\lim_{x\to s(t)^{+}}R_{2}(x,t)\ =\ 0$.}

Then
s(t)=s_{0}
is constant in
(0,T)
.

Proof.

The expansion (
51
) implies that for any
t\in(0,T)
we have
as
x\to s(t)^{+}

(52)

\displaystyle(h^{n}h_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ -h_{t}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{exp-timeder}}}{{=}}\ \gamma\dot{s}(t)-R_{2}(x,t).

Integrating once from
s(t)
to
x
and using the assumptions we obtain

(53)

\displaystyle h_{xxx}(x,t)\

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{no-massflux}}}{{=}}\ \frac{1}{h^{n}}\Big(\gamma\dot{s}(t)(x-s(t))+\int_{s(t)}^{x}R_{2}(\widetilde{x},t)\ d\widetilde{x}\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{exp-regder}}}{{\approx}}\ \frac{\dot{s}(t)}{\gamma^{n-1}(x-s(t))^{n-1}}.\qquad\qquad

Integrating two times and if
n\geq 3
it follows that

|h_{x}(x,t)|\to\infty
as

if
\dot{s}(t)\neq 0
. This implies
\dot{s}=0
.
∎

We note that the above proof does not directly use finite dissipation of
energy.

3.2.
Type (a) solutions – fixed support

In this section, we justify why solutions of (

(54)

\displaystyle h_{t}+(h^{3}h_{xxx})_{x}\

\displaystyle=\ 0\ \ \qquad\qquad\text{for }x\in(0,\infty),\ t>0,

(55)

\displaystyle h(0,t)\

\displaystyle=\ 0\ \ \qquad\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0

with initial data

. One can construct solutions to (

near the contact point. More precisely, we assume that

(56)

\displaystyle h(x,t)\ =\ \gamma x+\varphi(x,t),

Plugging (

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}+\gamma^{3}(x^{3}\varphi_{xxx})_{x}+\dot{\gamma}x\ \approx\ 0.

Using that

we can change the time scale as

(58)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\tau}+(x^{3}\varphi_{xxx})_{x}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}x}{\gamma^{3}}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $x>0$,}

where
\dot{\gamma}=\,\frac{d}{dt}\gamma
. The leading order behaviour for

obtained by

(59)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\tau}+(x^{3}\varphi_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ 0\ \qquad\text{for $x>0$.}

Equation (

\widetilde{\varphi}
denote the Laplace transform of

in time with

(60)

\displaystyle z\widetilde{\varphi}+(x^{3}\widetilde{\varphi}_{xxx})_{x}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $x>0$, for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$}.

The possible asymptotic behaviours as

for solutions of

,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}x

. Therefore, the

We now discuss a different class of solutions of (

\displaystyle h_{t}-\dot{s}h_{\xi}+(h^{3}h_{\xi\xi\xi})_{\xi}\

\displaystyle=\ 0

\displaystyle h(\xi,t)\ =:\ \overline{h}(\xi,t)+u(\xi,t),\qquad\qquad\text{where}\quad\overline{h}(\xi,t)\ :=\ \gamma\xi(-\ln\xi)^{\frac{1}{3}}

smooth function, not necessarily increasing. The behaviour of the solution

near the contact point
x=s(t)
with
\xi:=x-s(t)
is given by

(70)

\displaystyle h(x,t)\ =\ \gamma\xi+\varphi(\xi,t),\qquad

for some smooth function
\gamma>0
and some function
\varphi
with

(71)

\displaystyle\varphi(\xi,t)=o(\xi),\ \ \varphi_{\xi}(\xi,t)=o(1),\ \ \varphi_{t}(\xi,t)=o(\xi)\qquad\text{as $\xi\to 0^{+}$}.

We examine the behaviour of the linearized problem satisfied by
\varphi
near
the contact point. Keeping the leading order terms we obtain

(72)

\displaystyle-\gamma\dot{s}+\varphi_{t}-\dot{s}\varphi_{\xi}+\gamma^{n}(\xi^{n}\varphi_{\xi\xi\xi})_{\xi}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $\xi>0,t>0$},

with
\varphi(0,t)=0
. By assumption (
5
) we
have
\xi^{n}\varphi_{\xi\xi\xi}\to 0
as
\xi\to 0^{+}
. Integrating the equation in

\xi
and using (
71
) we obtain the asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle\varphi(\xi,t)\ \approx\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{1-n}\dot{s}}{(4-n)(3-n)(2-n)}\xi^{4-n}&\text{if $2<n<3$,}\\
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{-1}\dot{s}\xi^{2}\ln\xi&\text{if $n=2$,}\\
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi_{\xi\xi}(0)\xi^{2}.&\text{if $0<n<2$}.\end{array}\right.

Notice that there are no constraints about the sign of
\dot{s}
. This is
different to the case of solutions for (
2
) where the position of
the contact point can be prescribed arbitrarily as long as the support is
shrinking (cf. Section
3.3
).

Solutions with prescribed contact line angle.

One can construct the
solution of a different type of boundary problem for (
12
) with

n<3
. More precisely, we can prescribe the value of the contact angle

\gamma\geq 0
. To impose this quantity is a very stringent condition and this
determines uniquely the value of
s(t)
. Existence of such solutions has been
proved in the complete wetting as well as partial wetting case, see
e.g.
[
7
,
3
,
8
,
16
,
46
,
35
,
42
]
. It
seems possible to use an approach similar to the one in those papers to solve
the problem with prescribed, smooth
\gamma
by reformulating the angle condition
as an integral equation for
s(t)
.

Remark 4.1

(Boundary conditions)
.

As discussed before, for our boundary value problem, we can either prescribe
two boundary conditions for
h
or we can prescribe the contact point velocity
and one boundary condition. We show how a similar issue arises from the
classical one–dimensional Stefan problem

(76)

\displaystyle u_{t}\ =u_{xx}\ \

\displaystyle x>s(t),\ t\geq 0,

(77)

\displaystyle u\ =\ 1,\quad\dot{s}\ =\ -u_{x},

\displaystyle x=s(t),t\geq 0

with initial conditions
u=u_{0}
. In terms of
U=\int_{x}^{\infty}u
for

x>s(t)
we obtain

(78)

\displaystyle U_{t}\ =U_{xx}\ \

\displaystyle x>s(t),\ t\geq 0,

(79)

\displaystyle U\ =\ \int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}u_{0}\ dx,\quad U_{x}\ =\ -1,

\displaystyle x=s(t),t\geq 0.

Indeed, for the first boundary condition in (
79
) we calculate

(80)

\displaystyle\,\frac{d}{dt}U(s(t),t)\

\displaystyle=\ U_{x}(s(t),t)\dot{s}+\partial_{t}U(s(t),t)\ =\ -\dot{s}+\int_{s(t)}^{\infty}u_{xx}(\xi,t)\ d\xi

(81)

\displaystyle=\ -\dot{s}-u_{x}(\dot{s},t)\ =\ 0.

This illustrates that the Stefan problem (
76
) – one boundary
condition for
u
and the speed
\dot{s}
is prescribed — is equivalent to the
boundary value problem (
78
) where two boundary conditions on the
function are assumed at the free boundary while the speed of the contact line is
only implicitly given.

4.2.
Approximating type (a) solutions – Cox-Voinov Law

In this subsection we discuss the type (a) solutions and their relation to the
Cox-Voinov Law which connects the speed of the contact point with the
apparent contact angle. We will see that for
\epsilon\to 0
, type (a)
solutions of (
2
) arise as limit of solutions of
(
68
) where the constant angle
\theta_{\epsilon}
does not depend on

\epsilon
. Both in the case of complete or partial wetting, the Cox–Voinov Law can
be reformulated as a law for the speed of the contact point. Our main goal is to
illustrate the fact that those computations yield a limit law for the speed of
the contact lines associated to the equation (
2
) obtained as a
limit of (
3
). In this regime the contact line moves very slowly
with speed of order
|\ln\epsilon|^{-1}
.

We consider solutions of (
68
) with a single contact point. Far
from the contact point, the solutions approximately solve (
2
) and satisfy

(82)

\displaystyle h(\xi,t)\ \hskip 0.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{S1E4}}}{{\approx}}\ \gamma\xi,\qquad\qquad\text{for $\epsilon\ll\xi\ll 1$},

where
\gamma>0
is an
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
function that depends on
initial data. The asymptotic formula (
82
) breaks down near the contact line when

h^{3}\sim\epsilon^{3-n}h^{n}
, i.e.
\xi\sim\epsilon
. To investigate the solutions in the inner
region we use the new variables

(83)

\displaystyle h\ =\ \epsilon H,\ \quad\ \xi\ =\ \epsilon y,\ \quad t\ =\ \epsilon\tau

In terms of these new variables (
68
) takes the form

(84)

\displaystyle H_{\tau}-\dot{s}H_{y}+\left(\left(H^{3}+H^{n}\right)H_{yyy}\right)_{y}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $y>0$}.

with
H_{y}(0,\cdot)=\theta
. We expect the solutions to become stationary if

\tau
is large. With the assumption (
5
) we obtain the
approximative equation

(85)

\displaystyle-\dot{s}+(H^{2}+H^{n-1})H_{yyy}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $y>0$}.

We need to match solutions of (
85
) with the asymptotic behaviour
(
82
) for
\epsilon\ll\xi\ll 1
where
\dot{s}
is one of the quantities
that must be determined. The equation (
85
) must be solved with contact
angle
\theta=0
in the complete wetting case and contact angle
\theta>0
in
the partial wetting case. This analysis to the Cox–Voinox Law in the case of
complete wetting as well partial wetting. We use the self–consistent assumption

|\dot{s}|\ll 1
for
\epsilon\ll 1
.

Partial wetting case.

We now consider solutions of (
85
) with nonzero contact angle
\theta>0

assuming that the slope is almost constant for distances to the contact point of
order
\epsilon
. We assume
n\in(1,3)
to be fixed and consider the asymptotics
\epsilon\to 0
. We make the ansatz

H=H_{0}+H_{1}+\ldots
with
|H_{1}|\ll H_{0}
for
y\to 0^{+}
. We obtain to
leading order

(86)

\displaystyle H\ \approx\ H_{0}\ =\ \theta y\qquad\qquad\text{as $y\to 0^{+}$}.

Furthermore,
H_{1}
solves to leading order

(87)

\displaystyle H_{1,yyy}\ =\ \frac{\dot{s}}{H_{0}^{2}+H_{0}^{n-1}}\ =\ \frac{\dot{s}}{\theta^{2}y^{2}+\theta^{n-1}y^{n-1}}\qquad\qquad\text{for $y>0$.}

With the assumption
|H_{1}|\ll y
for
y\ll 1
and
H_{1}\ll y^{2}
for
y\gg 1

this yields

(88)

\displaystyle H_{1}\ =\ |\dot{s}|\int_{0}^{y}\int_{0}^{y_{1}}\int_{y_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\theta^{2}y_{3}^{2}+\theta^{n-1}y_{3}^{n-1}}\ dy_{3}dy_{2}dy_{1}.

Integrating (
88
) we get the asymptotic behaviour

(89)

\displaystyle H_{1}\ \approx\ \frac{|\dot{s}|}{\theta^{2}}y\ln y\qquad\qquad\text{as $y\to\infty$.}

Using the approximation
H=H_{0}+H_{1}
and in terms of the variables
h
,

\xi
, cf. (
83
) and as in (
98
) we then obtain

(90)

\displaystyle h\ =\ \epsilon H\ \approx\ \theta y+\frac{|\dot{s}|}{\theta^{2}}y\ln y\ \approx\ \theta\xi+\frac{|\dot{s}|}{\theta^{2}}\big(\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big)\xi\qquad\qquad\text{for $\epsilon\ll\xi\ll 1$}

Matching (
90
) with (
82
) we obtain the Cox–Voinov Law

[
73
,
15
,
50
]

(91)

\displaystyle\dot{s}\ =\ \frac{\theta^{2}}{\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}}(\theta-\gamma),

which is valid with the assumption
n\in(1,3)
in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
. Notice that the speed of the contact line vanishes if the outer contact
angle
\gamma
is the same as the microscopic contact angle
\theta
. The contact
line speed logarithmically converges to zero if
\epsilon\to 0
. Hence, in real
situations the change of the droplet profile
h
and the motion of the contact
line
s(t)
could take place in similar time scales. We note that the above
computation has some analogies with the computation in Subsection

4.3
.

Complete wetting case.

Although most of the paper is concerned with
the case of a non–zero contact angle, for the convenience of the reader we also
give a short motivation of Tanner’s Law which is relevant for the zero contact
angle case. We focus on the relevant case
n=2
, associated to the Navier slip
condition. We need to obtain a solution of (
85
) with a suitable choice
of
\dot{s}
and the boundary condition
H_{y}\left(0,\tau\right)=0
such that

h=\epsilon H
satisfies the matching condition (
82
). We look for
solutions of (
85
) in the form
H=H_{0}+H_{1}+\ldots
where

|H_{1}|\ll H_{0}
for
y\ll 1
. The leading order contribution is determined
by
H_{0}H_{0,yyy}=\dot{s}
(with
\dot{s}=\,\frac{d}{dt}s
) which yields

(92)

\displaystyle H_{0}(y,\tau)\ \approx\ \sqrt{\tfrac{8}{3}}(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{\frac{3}{2}}\qquad\qquad\text{as $y\to 0^{+}$}

using a standard asymptotic argument. The nonnegativity of the solution and
(
92
) then show that
\dot{s}<0
, i.e. the support of the solutions can
only expand. In leading order (
85
) then takes the form

H_{0}^{2}H_{0,yyy}+H_{0}H_{1,yyy}+H_{1}H_{0,yyy}
=
0
which leads to

(93)

\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}y^{3}H_{1,yyy}-H_{1}\ =\ \frac{8}{3}(-\dot{s})y^{3}.

The solution of (
93
) has the form
H_{1}=H_{1,p}+H_{1,\hom}

with the particular solution
H_{1,p}=\frac{8}{45}(-\dot{s})y^{3}
and a solution

H_{1,\hom}
of the homogeneous problem. In particular,
H_{1,\hom}
is a linear
combination of the functions
y^{\beta_{i}}
,
1=1,2,3
with

\beta_{1,2}=\frac{5}{4}\pm\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{13}
and
\beta_{3}=\frac{1}{2}
. The
assumption
|H_{1}|\ll H_{0}
implies
\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=0
. This yields for some

K\in\mathbb{R}
which might depend on

the asymptotic formula

(94)

\displaystyle H\ \approx\ \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{8}{45}(-\dot{s})y^{3}+Ky^{\beta_{1}}\qquad\text{for $(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}y\ll 1$.}

The asymptotic formula (
94
) holds as long as
|H_{1}|\ll H_{0}
, i.e.

y\ll(-\dot{s})^{-\frac{1}{3}}

that

yields a similar contribution than the other two terms for
these values of
y

\eta=(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}y

matching condition

(95)

\displaystyle H\ \approx\ \sqrt{\tfrac{8}{3}}\,\eta^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{8}{45}\eta^{3}+K(-\dot{s})^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{3}}\eta^{\beta_{1}}\qquad\qquad\text{for $\eta\ll 1$.}

On the other hand, in terms of
\eta
, (
85

(96)

\displaystyle 1+(H^{2}+H)H_{\eta\eta\eta}\ =\ 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $\eta>0$},

Equation (
96

[
37
,
42
]
. The behaviour of the solutions
of (
96

K
: Except for a discrete set of values we
have either
H\approx y^{2}
as

for some positive
value of
\eta

K=K_{0}\left(-\dot{s}\right)^{\frac{\beta_{1}}{3}}

(97)

\displaystyle H\ \approx\ 3^{\frac{1}{3}}\eta(\ln\eta)^{\frac{1}{3}}\ \qquad\qquad\text{as $\eta\to\infty$}.

In terms of the initial variable
h
, cf. (
83

97

takes the form

(98)

\displaystyle h\ \approx\ 3^{\frac{1}{3}}(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}\xi\Big(\ln\frac{(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}\xi}{\epsilon}\Big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\ \approx\ 3^{\frac{1}{3}}(-\dot{s})^{\frac{1}{3}}\xi\big(\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\qquad\text{for $\xi\sim 1$}

where we used the assumption

for the last approximation which is consistent with (

). Matching the

\displaystyle\dot{s}\ =\ -\frac{\gamma^{3}}{3\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}}\qquad\qquad\text{as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

A similar argument can be worked out in the more general case

that a logarithm appears on certain critical values of

5.
Conclusion

We analyze the evolution of thin liquid droplets in the lubrication
approximation with different slip conditions at the liquid–solid
interface. Motivated by the classical no-slip paradox which states that the
Navier–Stokes equations with a no-slip boundary condition require unphysical
infinite dissipation during droplet spreading, we focus on the limit of
vanishing slip. Our analysis shows that there are three different physically
relevant limits where the solutions approximate qualitatively different
evolutions of the free boundary: We show that in the no–slip limit three
fundamentally different classes of limiting solutions are approached, each of
them corresponding to a different scaling of the microscopic contact angle as
the regularization parameter vanishes:

(a)

The first solution class are regular at the contact line and have
fixed support. This reflects the well known fact that the contact line remains
fixed for sufficiently regular no-slip solutions.

(b)

For the second solution class, the contact line recedes on a time
scale of order one while the solution’s profile exhibits a characteristic
logarithmic correction near the moving contact point. Intriguingly, for these
solutions the dissipation near the contact point diverges, but this divergence
is balanced by the energetic cost associated with the shrinking solid–liquid
interface.

(c)

A third class of solutions appears when the contact angle diverges
even faster in which case the contact line recedes on a much smaller time
scale, and the mass removed from the support accumulates at the boundary.

These findings suggest that the thin-film equation with no slip supports a rich
family of physically admissible solutions, provided one interprets the no–slip
thin film equation as the asymptotic limit of models which regularized slip
conditions. Even though the large apparent contact angles in solutions of type
(b) and (c) seem incompatible with the lubrication approximation, a refined
analysis shows that the underlying physical variables remain consistent with the
assumptions for the lubrication approximation.
