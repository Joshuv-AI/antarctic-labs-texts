Rumen microbial dysbiosis is associated with productive lifespan decline in dairy cows via metabolic-inflammatory crosstalk
Authors: Panping Yang, Lei Yan, Zhuoxuan Wu, Yujia Li, Yuefei Yan, Feng Zhou, Shuhua Gu, Changlei Liu, Xiaoli Ren, Zhiyang Zhang, Shanshan Gao, Chaoying Huang, Yaqin Wang, Jingwen Dai, Shuai Qin, Yang Zhang, Huixia Li, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGut microbiota has been established as a critical regulator of human longevity, but the mechanistic role of rumen microbiota in dairy cow productive lifespan remains unexplored. This study investigated differences in rumen microbial community structure and metabolic signatures in a longitudinal cohort of dairy cows with divergent productive lifespans, aiming to elucidate the correction and potential regulatory mechanisms governing dairy cow longevity through microbial-host metabolic reprogramming.ResultsOur longitudinal study identified critical trends linked to increasing parity in dairy cows: milk yield and rumen microbiota diversity declined progressively, with microbial communities restructuring to show 37% higher abundance of Alphaproteobacteria and Pseudomonadota in cows with ≥ 4 parities compared to younger cohorts (parities 1–3). A key parity threshold was observed at the 4th lactation: early-parity cows (1–3) maintained energy-efficient metabolism via glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and TCA cycle, while ≥ 4th parity cows exhibited fundamental metabolic shifts—including altered methane-related gene expression, fermentation profile transitions from propionate- to acetate-dominated VFAs, and lipid dysregulation—alongside impaired nutrient conversion efficiency despite active B vitamin biosynthesis. These changes triggered pro-inflammatory metabolite accumulation, leading to systemic inflammation (elevated IL-1β/TNF-α/IL-6 levels) and reduced antioxidant capacity. Murine rumen microbiota transplantation experiments confirmed causality, with recipients of ≥ 4th parity microbiota developing gut barrier dysfunction and hepatic inflammation via TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation.ConclusionLongitudinal analyses classify rumen microbial dysbiosis as a potential driver of reduced productive longevity and lactation performance decline in dairy cows, suggesting that the temporal dynamics of the rumen microbiota influence lactation persistence and productive lifespan. This study fills the gap in microbiota-targeted strategies to extend dairy cows' productive longevity through precision microbial consortium modulation. Introduction

Longevity traits are critically important in the realm of dairy cattle farming due to their significant economic implications [1]. The productive lifespan, a crucial measure of longevity traits, can be defined as the period from the first calving until the cow exits the herd due to insufficient productivity, which may result from disease-related death or culling by the farmer [2]. The productive lifespan of dairy cows is typically around three years, however, in advanced dairy-producing countries, the average productive lifespan can reach up to 4.5 years(New zealand:4.5, United Kingdom:3.9, The Netherlands:3.7, and Poland:3.3) whereas in China, the average parity of dairy is less than 2.7 [3]. Given that the natural lifespan of dairy cows is approximately 20 years, with peak annual production typically occurring during the fifth lactation and the highest annual profit usually realized in the sixth lactation period [4, 5], investigating the influencing factors and underlying regulatory mechanisms is of paramount importance.

As Nobel prize winner Elie Metchnikoff proposed in 1907, aging is caused by the poisoning effects of certain microbial products within the gut, and modulating these microbial communities could extend lifespan [6]. In recent years, a large number of studies in humans and animal studies have shown that gut microbiota profoundly influences host lifespan through multiple pathways. On one hand, gut symbiotic bacteria can regulate the host's immune response. For example, Bifidobacterium lactis strain LKM512 can extend the lifespan of mammals by increasing the concentration of polyamines (PAs) in the gut, inhibiting chronic inflammation, and maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier [7]. On the other hand, microbial metabolites, such as bile acids, can not only directly combat pathogens and reduce the body's inflammatory response but also act as signaling molecules to regulate immune channels by activating nuclear receptors, thus affecting the host's immune homeostasis and lifespan [8, 9]. In addition, microbial metabolism can also influence host lifespan at the cellular metabolism level by mediating the Target of Rapamycin (TOR) signaling pathway and Insulin/insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling (IIS) pathway [10, 11]. The longevity of dairy cows has been governed by a long-standing paradigm emphasizing tripartite interplay: genetic determinants (GAT1, GRINA, CPSF1, SLC2A4RG and THRB) [12], epigenetic regulation (DNA methylation at AKT2) [13], and environmental pressures (feed efficiency metrics, culling policies). Yet this framework conspicuously neglects the symbiotic microbiome—particularly the rumen ecosystem. The rumen, a distinctive and vital digestive organ in ruminants, harbors a complex and diverse microbial community which is pivotal in multiple aspects, including feed digestion and absorption [14], production performance [15], and breast health [16, 17]. However, its potential function as a regulator of lifespan has not received sufficient attention.

We first proposed that the rumen microbiome is a pivotal determinant of dairy cow productive lifespan. In this study, we conducted a large—scale screening of 274 dairy cows across different parities. First, we analyzed the correlation between rumen microbiota and dairy cow productive lifespan using 16S rRNA sequencing. Subsequently, combining pedigree information, milk production stability, and cow disease status, we selected 24 cows with different parities for in—depth multi—omics analysis, including rumen metagenomics, metabolomics, and serum metabolomics analyzing the differences in rumen structure, function, and metabolism among cows with varying productive lifespans. Finally, through rumen fluid transplantation experiments conducted in mice, we explored the potential molecular mechanisms by which rumen microbiota influences the productive lifespan of dairy cows. The findings could deepen our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying longevity in dairy cow and clarify the specific role of rumen microbiota in modulating dairy cow lifespan. Additionally, the established research frameworks could further provide a theoretical basis for developing microbiota-targeted interventions to promote healthy aging in dairy cow, facilitating the translation of basic research into strategies for extending dairy cow lifespan and improving dairy cow health outcomes.

Material and methods

Animals, phenotypic data and sample collection

This study included 274 Holstein dairy cows with different productive lifespans from the same farm, aiming to investigate animal productive longevity and collect samples (Fig. 1). The animal experiments were approved and authorized by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Approval Number: NJAU.N020190415N13). This study was conducted at a commercial dairy farm (Huahuaniu Livestock Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Henan Province, China; 34°44′55.4″N, 113°37′8.9″E). A cohort of Holstein dairy cattle was group-housed under standardized management conditions and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) diet formulated according to NRC (2001) requirements (Supplementary File 1). Longitudinal milk samples were collected at three time points (06:00, 13:00, and 20:00 h) on days 14, 28, 42, 56, and 74 of the trial periods. Samples were proportionally pooled (4:3:3 ratio by volume) and submitted to the Henan Dairy Herd Improvement (DHI) Center for compositional analysis. On day 74 prior to morning feeding, rumen fluid (~ 10 mL) was collected via oral stomach tubes using standardized protocols [18]. Immediately after collection, pH was measured with a calibrated pH meter (pH/Oxidation–Reduction Potential, USA). All rumen sampling procedures were performed by the same technician to ensure consistency. Microbial diversity sequencing and analysis

All rumen fluid samples obtained from the cows underwent 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The total microbial genomic DNA was isolated from these samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.), following the protocol outlined by the manufacturer. To assess the quality and concentration of the extracted DNA, 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States) were utilized, with the samples subsequently stored at −80 ℃ for future analysis.

Amplification of the hypervariable regions V3-V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was conducted using primer pairs 338 F (5'-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3') and 806R (5'-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3') [19], employing the T100 Thermal Cycler PCR thermocycler (BIO-RAD, USA). The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 4 μL of 5 × Fast Pfu buffer, 2 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL of each primer (5 μM), 0.4 μL of Fast Pfu polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH2O, adjusted to a final volume of 20 µL. The conditions for PCR amplification included an initial denaturation step at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles comprising denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s, concluding with a single extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min, and finally ending at 4 ℃. The PCR products were extracted from a 2% agarose gel, purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) as per the manufacturer’s guidelines, and quantified utilizing Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Purified amplicons were combined in equimolar quantities and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA), following the standard procedures established by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw sequencing data were submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Bio-Project ID: PRJNA1199669). The analysis workflow began with de-multiplexing raw FASTQ files using a custom Perl script, followed by quality filtering with fastp and merging using FLASH. Reads with low average quality scores and those shorter than 50 bp were discarded, while only overlapping sequences longer than 10 bp were assembled. Samples were differentiated by barcode and primer matching, with chloroplast sequences removed during OTU clustering using UPARSE 7.1 at a 97% similarity level. To ensure balanced diversity measurements, the number of 16S rRNA gene sequences was standardized to 20,000, achieving a Good's coverage of 99.09%. Based on the OTUs information, rarefaction curves and alpha diversity (Chao1 richness) were calculated with Mothur v1.30.1. Combining production performance data with microbial diversity, we established a relationship model between Chao1 richness and milk yield. Our hypothesis was that microbial diversity correlates with milk yield. The model is represented by Y = β0 + β1X1 + β2*X2 + … + βn*Xn + ε, where Y denotes the dependent variable, X1, X2, …, Xn denote the independent variables, β0, β1, … βn are the model coefficients, and ε is the error term. We obtained the optimal model coefficients using the least squares method. The distribution frequency of the dependent variable (Chao1 richness) and the independent variable (milk yield) was statistically analyzed using a boundary histogram. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to measure the diversity correlation between milk yield and microbial diversity. Based on the sequencing results of diversity, PCA analysis of different group communities was performed using the R package ggbiplot (GitHub—vqv/ggbiplot: A biplot based on ggplot2). In the following, genetically homogeneous cows were identified through bull ID-tracked pedigree verification (Henan DHI Center records) using left_join() function in the R package dplyr matching. Individuals exhibiting ketosis, mastitis (subclinical/clinical), or bloating within 90 pre-sampling days were excluded, with priority given to metabolically stable cows demonstrating consistent milk yield (CV < 15% over 3 months). Stratified random sampling ensured balanced representation across lactation stages (OG/GG/PG/FG; n = 6 per group).

Biochemical analysis

Volatile fatty acids (VFA), including propionate, acetate, and butyrate concentrations, were quantified using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010plus, Japan). The microbial protein (MCP) levels were determined by the Coomassie brilliant blue method. Serum concentrations of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glucose (Glu), malondialdehyde (MDA), immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin M (IgM), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were all measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jianchen Biological Technology Co., Ltd.).

Metagenome sequencing

After the aforementioned DNA extraction, the DNA was fragmented using the Covaris M220 (Gene Company, China), and fragments of approximately 400 bp were selected for constructing the paired-end library. Metagenomic sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina, USA) sequencing platform at Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. The raw data have been submitted to NCBI (BioProject ID: PRJNA1199737). The data analysis workflow includes: the workflow consists of several key steps for processing sequencing data. First, fastp (version 0.20.0) was employed to perform quality trimming on the adapter sequences at both the 3' and 5' ends of the reads (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp). It also filtered out reads shorter than 50 bp, with an average quality score below 20 or containing ambiguous bases, retaining only high-quality paired-end and single-end reads. Next, BWA (version 0.7.9a) was used to align the reads to the host DNA sequence (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net), removing any contaminant reads that showed high similarity, applicable if the sample originates from a host with a published genome.

For assembly and gene prediction, MEGAHIT (version 1.1.2) was utilized to assemble the optimized sequences (https://github.com/voutcn/megahit), selecting contigs of at least 300 bp as the final assembly result. Prodigal and MetaGene (http://metagene.cb.k.u-tokyo.ac.jp/) were then used to predict open reading frames (ORFs) from the assembled contigs, focusing on genes that are 100 bp or longer and translating them into amino acid sequences. To construct a non-redundant gene set, CD-HIT (version 4.6.1) clustered all predicted gene sequences at 90% identity and coverage (http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit/), with the longest gene from each cluster designated as the representative sequence. Finally, gene abundance was calculated by aligning high-quality reads from each sample against the non-redundant gene set using SOAPaligner (version 2.21) (http://soap.genomics.org.cn/) at a 95% identity threshold to quantify gene abundance in the respective samples. To mitigate biases introduced by gene length and sequencing depth in gene abundance quantification, we normalized the raw data using the RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) algorithm. Specifically, RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase Million) represents the number of reads mapped to each gene per kilobase, within one million reads. Functional annotation was performed using DIAMOND (v0.8.35) with BLASTP (e-value ≤ 1e-5) against three databases: (1) NR for taxonomic classification, (2) eggNOG for COG functions, and (3) KEGG for KO/Pathway/EC/Module annotation. All functional abundances were calculated from corresponding gene abundances.

Construction of random forest classifier

We performed random forest analysis using the random Forest package in R to predict dairy cow productive longevity based on rumen microbial composition (phylum, genus, and species levels) and their relative abundances. All bacterial taxa with ≥ 0.1% relative abundance in metagenomic data were included as predictive features. Following the machine learning framework described by Verhaar et al. [20], we employed a nested cross-validation approach with 30 iterations to prevent overfitting. For each iteration, the dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Model hyperparameters were optimized through five-fold cross-validation within the training set, and final model performance was evaluated on the test set using the area under the ROC curve (AUC). The reported AUC values represent the average across all 30 iterations. All analyses used default parameters in the random Forest package, with ROC curves generated by plotting true positive rates against false positive rates, and model performance was assessed based on AUC values.

Metagenome analysis

To detect features with significantly different abundances, the non-parametric factorial Kruskal–Wallis sum-rank test was used, identifying taxa with significant abundance differences. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to examine the consistency of differences in the identified species across different subgroups. Finally, LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) was used to estimate the impact size of each component's (species, genes, functions) abundance on the differences observed.

To reveal the interaction network of the rumen microbiota in cows of different parities, the MENA online tool (http://ieg4.rccc.ou.edu/MENA/) was used to construct microbial interaction networks, and ecological network interaction analysis was conducted using Gephi. We defined each node as one of four types: (1) peripheral nodes (Zi < 2.8, Pi < 0.65), (2) connectors (Zi < 2.8, Pi > 0.65), (3) module hubs (Zi > 2.8, Pi < 0.65), and (4) network hubs (Zi > 2.8, Pi > 0.65). The size of the nodes is proportional to the number of connections, and the width of the edges is proportional to the Spearman correlation coefficient, the color of the edges corresponds to positive (colored) or negative (gray) correlations.

Rumen and serum metabolomics

Rumen microbial samples were analyzed using the LS-MS/LS-GS platform (UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X/8890B-5977B), while serum samples were analyzed using the LS-MS platform (UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X). The raw data have been submitted to NCBI (BioProject ID: PRJNA1199669).

Accurately weigh 50 mg of rumen fluid or 100 µL of serum into a 2 mL centrifuge tube and add a 6 mm diameter grinding bead. Add 400 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v:v) containing four internal standards (e.g., L-2-chlorophenylalanine at 0.02 mg/mL). Grind the sample using a frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz). Extract the sample with low-temperature ultrasonication for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). Place the sample at −20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuge for 15 min (13,000 g, 4 °C). Transfer the supernatant to a sample vial with an insert for LC–MS analysis.

For GC/MS analysis, accurately weigh 50 mg of rumen fluid into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, add 0.5 mL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v: v) containing ribitol at 0.02 mg/mL as an internal standard. Add a steel bead and grind in a grinder for 3 min (−20 °C, 50 Hz). Add 200 μL of chloroform and grind for another 3 min (50 Hz). Perform ultrasonication extraction for 30 min and then let it stand at −20 °C for 30 min. Centrifuge at low temperature for 15 min (13,000 rcf, 4 °C). Transfer the supernatant into a glass derivatization vial and dry it under nitrogen gas. Add 80 μL of methoxylamine hydrochloride in pyridine (15 mg/mL), vortex for 2 min, and perform oximation in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 90 min. Add 80 μL of BSTFA (with 1% TMCS) derivatization reagent, vortex for 2 min, and react at 70 °C for 60 min. Allow the sample to stand at room temperature for 30 min before GC/MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS and GC–MS analysis

Inject 3 μL of rumen or serum sample into an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm) for chromatographic separation followed by mass spectrometry detection. Mobile phase A is 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B is 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate is 0.40 mL/min, and the column temperature is set at 40 °C. Samples are analyzed in both positive and negative ion scan modes, with a mass scan range of 70–1050 m/z. The sheath gas flow rate is 50 psi, auxiliary gas flow rate is 13 psi, auxiliary gas heater temperature is 425 °C, positive mode ion spray voltage is 3500 V, negative mode ion spray voltage is −3500 V, ion transfer tube temperature is 325 °C, and normalized collision energy is 20–40–60 V cycle collision energy. The first-level mass spectrometry resolution is 60,000, and the second-level mass spectrometry resolution is 7,500, using DDA mode to collect data.

GC–MS metabolomics analysis is performed using Agilent 8890B-5977B gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) system (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd.). Samples are injected in split mode with an injection volume of 1 µL and a split ratio of 15:1. The samples are separated on a DB-5MS capillary column (40 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm, Agilent 122-5532G) and detected by mass spectrometry. Injection port temperature is 260 °C, carrier gas is high-purity helium at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, septum purge flow rate is 3 mL/min, and solvent delay is 5.5 min. The temperature program starts at 60 °C, held for 0.5 min, then ramped to 310 °C at 8 °C/min, and held for 6 min. Mass spectrometry conditions include electron impact ionization (EI), transfer line temperature of 310 °C, ion source temperature of 230 °C, quadrupole temperature of 150 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, full scan mode (SCAN) with a mass scan range of m/z 50–500, and a scan rate of 3.2 scans/s.

LC–MS raw data are imported into Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, resulting in a data matrix of retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. The MS and MS/MS spectra are matched against public databases (HMDB, http://www.hmdb.ca/; Metlin, https://metlin.scripps.edu/) and the Majorbio in-house database for metabolite identification. GC/MS raw data are processed using MassHunter Workstation Quantitative Analysis (v10.0.707.0) software to export a CSV format three-dimensional data matrix, including sample information, metabolite names, and mass spectral response intensities. Internal standard peaks and known false positives (e.g., noise, column bleed, and derivatization reagent peaks) are removed, and peak de-duplication and merging are performed. Metabolites are identified using public databases such as NIST (2017), Fiehn (2013), and MS-DIAL (2021). To reduce errors from sample preparation and instrument instability, total ion intensity normalization is applied to the sample mass spectrometry peaks, resulting in a normalized data matrix. Variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples are removed, and log10 transformation is applied to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analysis. Preprocessed data matrices are subjected to orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) using the R package ropls (Version 1.6.2). Model stability is evaluated using sevenfold cross-validation. Significant differential metabolites are selected based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values from the OPLS-DA model and Student's t-test p-values, with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 considered as significant differential metabolites. Differential metabolites were annotated to their respective metabolic pathways using the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to identify their associated pathways. Pathway enrichment analysis is performed using Python's scipy.stats package, and Fisher's exact test identifies the biological pathways most relevant to the experimental treatment.

Analysis of the Mechanism of Rumen Microbiota Transplantation (RMT) and inflammation development

A cohort of 16 BALB/c female mice, each weighing between 24–26 g, was procured from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed under controlled ambient conditions, provided with an ad libitum standard diet and water, and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. To establish the rumen microbiota transplantation model, we followed the protocol delineated by Zhao et al. [16]. Specifically, rumen fluid samples from young cows (second parity) and aged cows (fifth parity) were centrifuged at 100 × g for 2 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected and stored for later use. The mice were subjected to daily antibiotic treatment via oral gavage. The antibiotic cocktail comprised ampicillin, metronidazole, neomycin, and vancomycin (all sourced from Solarbio, China) at a mixed solution concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, administered for 7 consecutive days to eradicate the indigenous microbiota of the mice. Following a seven-day antibiotic washout period, each mouse received 100 µL of the prepared rumen microbiota suspension daily via gavage for 14 days. The mice were subsequently stratified into four distinct groups: the untreated group receiving a standard diet (Blank Control, BC), saline gavage (Saline Gavage, SG), young bovine rumen fluid gavage (Young Bovine Rumen Fluid Gavage Group, YR), and aged bovine rumen fluid gavage (Aged Bovine Rumen Fluid Gavage Group, AR).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

Following the procedures outlined in reference, liver and colon tissues fixed in paraformaldehyde were thoroughly rinsed, dehydrated with a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. The sections underwent deparaffinization with xylene and ethanol, staining with hematoxylin and eosin, and mounting with neutral balsam for microscopic examination. The panoramic scanning of tissue sections was conducted using a 3D HISTECH scanner, with subsequent image analysis performed using the SlideViewer 2.5 software.

Western Blotting (WB)

Approximately 50 mg of liver tissue from a consistent site on the right lobe was homogenized in a grinder with an appropriate volume of RIPA lysis buffer on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 r·min−1 for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA method. The supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and heated in a constant-temperature metal bath for 6 min, and the samples were stored at −20 °C. Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes at a constant current of 200 mA for 1.5 h on ice. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h, washed three times with TBST (each wash lasting 10 min), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (dilution 1:1000). Following washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h, washed again, and developed. GAPDH was used as a loading control, and the relative expression levels of TLR4, p65, p-p65, p-lκB, and lκB proteins were analyzed using ImageJ software. The same protocol was employed to detect the expression of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 in colon tissues.

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

The Mann—Whitney U test was employed to analyze the milk yields of dairy cows at different parities (from the 1 st to the 5th parity) and different physiological stages (5—30 days postpartum, 31—100 days postpartum, 101—200 days postpartum, and 201—305 days postpartum). Linear regression was used to establish the correlations between microbial diversity (Chao1 index), milk yield, and productive lifespan. The best coefficient estimates were sought, and the least—squares method was used to minimize the sum of squared residuals between the predicted values of the model and the actual observed values. The lm() function in the R package was used for equation fitting, and the R2, F—statistic, and P—value (POLS) of the regression equation were calculated. The hist() function was used to draw histograms of sample diversity and milk yield distribution. The QIIME2 (v 2023.5) was used to process the 16S rRNA sequencing data to obtain the α—diversity indices at the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) level, including the Chao1 index. The t-test was used to compare the serum biochemical indicators and rumen volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentrations between groups.

Rumen microbiota compositional differences (domain/phylum/genus) were assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests on 16S rRNA data, with FDR-corrected P < 0.05 considered significant. The phyloseq package was used to analyze the distribution differences at the phylum level. The prcomp() function and the ggplot2 package were used to draw PCA plots, and the pie() function was used to draw pie charts. Microbial species differences were evaluated by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) and machine learning (random forest model), with the criteria of an LDA score > 3 and a P—value < 0.05. LEfSe analysis was also used to analyze the differences between groups in microbial metabolic pathways, modules, and KEGG. The significance threshold was set as an LDA score > 2 and a P—value < 0.05. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on the Bray–Curtis method was used to conduct β—diversity analysis of bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses in different groups of dairy cows to reveal the differences in bacterial communities, and the results were visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). For microbial network interaction analysis, microorganisms with a prevalence > 60% in different groups were selected. The MENA online tool (http://ieg4.rccc.ou.edu/MENA/) was used to construct microbial interaction networks, and the Gephi software was used for ecological network interaction analysis. For metabolomics data, multivariate statistical analysis was performed through the MetaboAnalyst 4.0 platform. Principal component analysis (PCA), partial least—squares discriminant analysis (PLS—DA), and the t—test were used to compare the differential metabolites between groups. Permutation validation analysis of the (O)PLS-DA model was performed to assess potential overfitting and statistical significance of the metabolomics data. The significance criteria were an FDR—corrected p- value < 0.05 and a variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1. To validate the reliability of findings given the limited sample size, post hoc statistical power analyses were performed using G-Power 3.1 [21]. For key outcomes (α-diversity indices: Chao 1 and Shannon), we computed achieved power at α = 0.05 with two-tailed testing. The analysis assumed a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.7) based on observed inter-parity differences, with sample sizes constrained to n = 6 per group.

Results

Dynamics of rumen microbiota across productive lifespans in dairy cattle

To compare the differences in production performance among cows with varying productive lifespans, we tracked the production performance data of 1,422 cows over nearly two years under the same rearing conditions, compiling a total of 45,420 performance records. Milk yield variation by parity and lactation stage was quantified, identifying significantly higher production in second- and third-parity cows (p < 0.01). Milk yield in cows with four or more parities showed a progressive decline (Fig. 2a). Notably, the most pronounced performance differences among parity groups occurred during lactation days 31–100 (p = 0.047) (Fig. 2b), and those in this period were subsequently selected for the study on the interaction between rumen microbiota and productive lifespan. To delineate rumen microbiota abundance across cows with varying productive lifespans, we selected 274 cows from a total of 1,422 cows at Huahua Dairy Livestock Technology Co., Ltd. in Henan Province representing different parities during postpartum days 31–100. A linear regression demonstrated an inverse correlation between productive lifespan and microbial diversity (Chao1, r = − 0.1317, 95%CI [0.312, 0.708], p < 0.05), whereas milk yield showed a strong positive association (Chao1, r = 0.5102, 95%CI [0.312, 0.708], p < 0.05) (Fig. 2c, d). The comprehensive post hoc power analysis for α-diversity metrics is shown in the Supplementary File 2. Serum biochemical profiles and rumen fermentation metrics in dairy cows with distinct production lifespans

To investigate parity-related metabolic differences, cows were stratified into four groups based on milk yield and parity: OG (first parity), GG (second and third parity), PG (fourth parity), and FG (fifth parity and above). Blood biochemistry and rumen fermentation parameters were analyzed in 24 cows as direct metabolic indicators. It suggests that ruminal fermentation metabolism in the OG and FG groups exhibits greater activity with stronger butyrate-producing capacity, while the GG and PG groups show reduced overall fermentation activity but significantly higher propionate contribution—a pattern consistent with propionate-type fermentation metabolism (Fig. 3b–e,Supplementary Material 4 ). Notably, compared with the GG group, PG and FG groups exhibited significantly elevated AST (38% increase) and ALT (42% increase) activities (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3h, j) demonstrating clear hepatic impairment. Examining serum antioxidant parameters, progressive oxidative stress was observed with advancing parity, including malondialdehyde (MDA) levels increase 53% compared with the GG group, superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities decrease 28–35% (p < 0.05) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels 2.4-folds higher, which indicates that cows in the PG and FG groups have a disrupted redox equilibrium (Fig. 3q–t). Regarding serum immunological markers, a decline in IgG, IgM, and IgA levels was evident with extended productive lifespan. Conversely, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) displayed an upward trend, which imply a deterioration in immune function and an escalation in inflammatory responses as the productive lifespan of dairy cow increases (Fig. 3k–p). Meanwhile, we observed that the glucose levels were higher in the PG and FG groups, which may be linked to the metabolism of inflammatory cells and the phagocytosis of nutrients (Fig. 3g). Interestingly, we observed that NH3-N and BUN showed opposite trends in dairy cows of different parities (Fig. 3f, i). This phenomenon is not contradictory but rather reflects fundamental differences in liver function and nitrogen metabolism efficiency across physiological stages. The liver is the core organ of nitrogen metabolism, responsible for converting toxic NH₃ into non-toxic BUN via the urea cycle. Thus, the processing capacity of the hepatic “ammonia–urea cycle” is critical. In high-parity cows, repeated transition periods and high-yield lactation phases expose the liver to prolonged metabolic stress, often leading to functional decline. As a result, ammonia metabolism is reduced. The organism may adapt by altering rumen microbial structure and reducing ammonia permeability across the rumen wall, thereby decreasing ammonia production and absorption. The absorbed ammonia is still converted into urea, and impaired kidney function—common in older cows—may hinder urea excretion, leading to elevated BUN concentrations. Metagenomic sequencing generated a total of 1,747,460,724 reads, with 1,738,781,916 clean reads obtained after stringent quality control and host DNA removal (Supplementary File 5). Discussion

In this study, we present the first investigation into the relationship between rumen microbiota and the productive lifespan of dairy cows, along with its underlying mechanisms. By analyzing rumen microbial composition across 274 cows with varying parities (selected from a large population), we identified significant correlations between microbial taxa abundance/diversity and parity/milk yield. To further elucidate these associations, we conducted integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses on 24 genetically similar cows, revealing functional shifts in the rumen microbiome across parities and their impact on host metabolism. While our multi-omics statistics approach mitigates small-sample biases, the restricted cohort size may limit generalizability, particularly for low-abundance taxa. Future validation in larger cohorts is recommended to confirm rare microbe-host interactions. Additionally, rumen microbiota transplantation (RMT) experiments validated a proposed model in which microbial dysbiosis triggers host inflammatory responses. Collectively, this study provides a theoretical foundation for extending dairy cow productive lifespan through targeted modulation of rumen microbial homeostasis.

Differences in cows with different parities: the structure of the rumen microbial community

In this study, cows were categorized into four groups based on parity and differences in production performance. In the OG group, g__Prevotella was associated with the metabolism of glutathione, phenylalanine, starch, sucrose, and galactose, highlighting its role in amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism [22]. Previous studies have established that Prevotella in the dairy cow rumen is involved in glutathione and phenylalanine metabolism, and modulation of host amino acid metabolism pathways including glycine, serine, threonine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, cysteine, and methionine [22]. f__Muribaculaceaewas mainly enriched in butyrate production and tryptophan metabolism pathways [23]. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid that cannot be synthesized in mammals and rumen microbe plays a crucial role in its biosynthesis, which has been shown to enhance nutrient utilization efficiency and lactation performance in dairy cows [24]. The GG group showed an enrichment of bacteria from the family of Rikenellaceae, including genera such as Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, Prevotella, and Porphyromonas, which could encode enzymes for polysaccharide and glycoside hydrolysis, aiding in the breakdown of dietary fibers and starches, releasing energy, and serving as primary sources of propionate. Additionally, Rikenellaceae participates in bile acid deconjugation, transforming primary bile acids into secondary bile acids in the human colon, and it is also a probiotic that plays a crucial role in disease resistance, immune function, and maintaining homeostasis [25]. Conversely, pathogenic bacteria such as f__Akkermansiaceae, f__Erysipelotrichaceae, Pseudomonadota, and Alphaproteobacteria were predominantly found in the PG and FG groups. f__Erysipelotrichaceae is primarily involved in redox reactions and is associated with proinflammatory responses [26]. Research using Nlrp12 knockout mice demonstrated increased basal colonic inflammation, leading to reduced microbial diversity, loss of protective gut symbionts f__Lachnospiraceae, and an abundance of the colitis-associated strain f__Erysipelotrichaceae [26]. Alphaproteobacteria can produce significant amounts of hydrogen, when combined with carbon dioxide and generates methane [27]. Pseudomonadota is an inflammation-related strain that utilizes purines as a carbon source, driving inflammatory responses [28]. Gammaproteobacteria is an enteric pathogen phylum primarily responsible for producing enterotoxins [29]. They encode chaperone proteins such as HdeA and HdeB involved in acid stress responses through glutamate, arginine, and lysine decarboxylation pathways, counteracting the extreme acidity encountered in the digestive system [30]. In archaea enrichment, the OG group included Candidatus_Micrarchaeota and s__Candidatus_Micrarchaeota_Archaeon, which reported in marine environments capable of lipid and polypeptide synthesis [31]. The GG group exhibited significant enrichment of Korarchaeota phylum members, including Candidatus Korarchaeota, Candidatus Korarchaeota archaeon, Desulfurococcales archaeon, and Acidianus manzaensis. These taxa harbor genes essential for dissimilatory sulfur metabolism—a process that reduces environmental methane volatilization while supplying bioavailable inorganic sulfur to the host. [32]. In the PG group, Thermofilum_sp__EX4484_79 contains genes encoding cellulose disaccharide hydrolase [33], while the FG group was enriched with numerous archaea associated with methane production or metabolism, including g__Methanocorpusculum and s__Methanosphaera [34]. Functional analysis of microbes revealed an enrichment of beneficial strains involved in nutrient metabolism in the OG and GG groups, whereas the PG and FG groups were predominantly characterized by pathogenic and inflammation-related bacteria. While our bioinformatics and machine learning -driven approach successfully identified differential functional microbial taxa, a key limitation of this study lies in the lack of experimental validation of their proposed pro-inflammatory functions. This critical gap underscores the need for functional validation of these candidate microbes through targeted isolation and screening from the rumen ecosystem. Future work focusing on in vitro and in vivo characterization of their inflammatory mechanisms will be essential to establish causality and advance our understanding of microbiome-host interactions in dairy cow health.

Differences in cows with different parities: energy metabolism

VFAs, constituting ~ 95% of rumen fatty acids (acetate/propionate/butyrate), provide 70–80% of ruminant energy needs [35, 36]. Their concentrations are critical indicators of rumen digestive metabolism. Acetate, propionate, and butyrate collectively comprise approximately 95% of the total volatile fatty acids, rendering them as the most vital VFAs in the metabolic processes of ruminants. Research indicates that the energy loss rates in the conversion of monosaccharides to acetate, propionate, and butyrate by rumen microbiota are 38%, −9%, and 22%, respectively [37]. Propionate production in the rumen competitively consumes hydrogen, effectively inhibiting methane synthesis, and serves as a key precursor for gluconeogenesis. Acetate and glucose exhibit a synergistic role in lipid synthesis in dairy cows, with acetate being the principal precursor for fat synthesis in ruminants [38]. Our findings reveal that the FG group exhibited the highest total VFA and acetate levels, while propionate levels were the lowest. Conversely, the GG and PG groups had significantly higher propionate levels than other groups, suggesting a predominant propionate metabolism in these groups. Through the competitive consumption of hydrogen, propionate production could effectively inhibit methane synthesis, while also functioning as an essential precursor for gluconeogenesis [39]. The metagenomic pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of methane metabolism (ko00680) in the FG group, which is likely associated with the proliferation of methanogenic archaea within this group. Specifically, taxa such as g_Methanocorpusculum (LDA > 4.5, p < 0.05), s_Methanocorpusculum_sp_ (LDA > 4.5, p < 0.05), and Methanobrevibacter_sp_ (LDA > 4.5, p < 0.05) were notably enriched, deriving energy from hydrogen molecules, which are byproducts of other obligate anaerobes, highlighting a metabolic interdependence within the microbial community of the FG group. The distinct metabolic pathways for hydrogen utilization in cows of different lactations may result in varying energy states within the body, potentially explaining the significant differences in production performance between GG and FG cows. Metabolomic profiling identified cobalamin (vitamin B12) as the most differentially abundant metabolite between FG and GG groups. Functionally, cobalamin acts as a coenzyme for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MCM; EC 5.4.99.2), catalyzing the isomerization of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA—a critical step in propionate synthesis from branched-chain amino acids, fatty acids, and cholesterol [40]. Unlike other B vitamins obtainable through dietary intake, cobalamin biosynthesis in ruminants primarily depends on microbial activity. The rumen microbiota thus serves as the principal endogenous source of this essential cofactor [41]. Notably, reduced cobalamin levels in the PG and FG groups correlated strongly with diminished Bifidobacterium abundance (Spearman r = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.68–0.92, p < 0.001), suggesting a potential association between microbial dysbiosis and impaired propionate metabolism. However, it is important to note that correlation does not imply causation. While we observed significant correlations between gut microbiota and metabolite levels, these associations do not establish definitive causal relationships. Future studies, such as causal mediation analysis or intervention experiments (e.g., Bifidobacterium supplementation), are needed to validate potential mechanistic links and determine the directionality of effects. Additionally, Metagenomic analysis revealed a progressive decline in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity (ko00020) with advancing lactation stages, while the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP, ko00030) became markedly enriched in the FG group. Concurrently, alpha diversity indices demonstrated reduced rumen microbial diversity and abundance in later lactation groups. These findings suggest lactation-stage-dependent restructuring of rumen microbiota may drive host energy metabolism shifts, ultimately affecting production efficiency. While this study preliminarily revealed associations between rumen microbial composition and host metabolic heterogeneity through multi-omics integration and serum biochemical data (e.g., enriched methane metabolism in FG group, enhanced glycolytic activity in OG/GG groups), limitations exist. A key shortcoming is the lack of systematic measurement of key metabolic enzyme activities, particularly glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, pyruvate kinase) and methane metabolism-specific enzymes (methyl-coenzyme M reductase, formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase). Liquid nitrogen levels, reflecting dietary protein degradation and microbial ammonia utilization efficiency, peaked in the PG group, indicating heightened nitrogen metabolism. Paradoxically, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels—a hepatic ammonia detoxification product and protein catabolism marker—were lowest in PG and highest in FG groups under identical diets, highlighting potential metabolic dysregulation in advanced lactation stages [42].

Differences in cows with different parities: oxidative balance and antioxidant capacity

Stress resistance plays a crucial role in regulating lifespan by helping organisms endure environmental and physiological stressors, including pathogens and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [43]. Elevated ROS levels can damage proteins, DNA, and cells, but maintaining ROS homeostasis is vital for growth and aging [44]. Key indicators of oxidative balance in animals include serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC). This study observed that as parity increased, cows showed reduced antioxidant capacity and higher oxidative stress levels. Recent studies highlight that gut microbiota profoundly influence ROS homeostasis and stress response pathways. Symbiotic probiotics in human and animal microbiota are widely acknowledged for their potential to extend lifespan. For example, Lactobacillus gasseri SBT2055 (LG2055) extends the lifespan of Caenorhabditis elegans by upregulating skn-1 and its downstream genes, which encode antioxidant proteins, via the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. This enhances antioxidant defenses [45, 46]. Similarly, dietary inclusion of Lactobacillus fermentum strain U-21 improves oxidative stress resistance and protects dopaminergic neurons, thereby extending the lifespan of C. elegans and mouse models of Parkinson’s disease [47]. Additionally, small molecules like indole, produced by Escherichia coli K-12 variants, enhance stress resilience, prolong lifespan, and extend reproductive periods in nematodes, fruit flies and mice by activating the conserved aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a sensor for exogenous molecules [48]. Collectively, these studies emphasize the role of gut microbiota in modulating oxidative stress.

Rumen microbiota communication and interaction

The rumen microbiota constitutes a dynamic microbial ecosystem that sustains gastrointestinal diversity and stability from birth in calves, which plays a crucial role in performing various critical functions, including anti-infective, anti-inflammatory, and immune-modulating activities, all of which are essential for host health [49]. Central among these host-protective mechanisms, colonization resistance emerges as a cornerstone defense strategy. This critical function mediates the dynamic equilibrium between commensal microbiota and potential pathogens via a dual containment strategy competitive exclusion through secondary bile acid production, and direct pathogen suppression by antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). [50]. When environmental stressors or pathogenic infections disrupt the microbiota, the synthesis of various antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) is rapidly upregulated. Once the concentration of AMPs in the microbiota exceeds a certain threshold, they effectively target several essential microbial functions, facilitating rapid bacterial clearance [51]. In this study, we observed a reduction in microbial abundance and diversity in both the PG and FG groups, with a notable enrichment of AMP signaling pathways in the FG group. This may indicate decreased community stability or reduced population resistance induced by pathogens, further suggesting that changes in the microbiota during the aging of dairy cows occur gradually. The spatial regulation of AMP expression across different species integrates local environmental conditions to precisely calibrate the spatial heterogeneity and bacterial phenotypes of the microbiota. Although the coding sequences and amino acid compositions of AMPs are highly conserved, there is substantial variability in their copy numbers and regulatory sequences. This is particularly significant because the proper modulation or promotion of AMP production may help mitigate or prevent imbalances in intestinal homeostasis. In fact, AMP production is regulated by quorum sensing (QS), and 767 microbial genomes in the rumen have been identified to contain QS-related genes, predominantly encoding AI-2 synthases or receptors [52]. Therefore, effectively regulating the QS system to mediate the maintenance of homeostasis presents a meaningful research avenue. Notably, Fengycin, secreted by Bacillus spp., has been found to act as a structural analog of the small molecule Arg in the QS system of Staphylococcus aureus, disrupting inter-colonial communication, destabilizing the community, and inhibiting its colonization [53]. Therefore, mediating communication between bacteria to regulate community homeostasis could be a feasible approach.

Parity-related rumen microbiota dysbiosis and inflammation

The increasing parity in dairy cows is associated with a decline in production performance and an elevated disease susceptibility, reflecting an aging process governed by intricate signaling pathways and modulated by genetic and environmental intrications. A comprehensive review has delineated twelve hallmarks of aging, which include genomic instability, dysregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and ecological imbalance [54]. Notably, "inflamm-aging" a state of age-related chronic inflammation—has emerged as a driver of gut barrier disruption and microbial dysbiosis [55]. In this study, biochemical profiling revealed lactation-dependent immune dysregulation: IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF levels rose significantly, whereas IgM, IgA, and IgG concentrations declined, suggesting progressive immune dysfunction and inflamm-aging activation. Concurrent rumen microbiota shifts included enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g. Akkermansiaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae) and opportunistic pathogens (e.g. Pseudomonadota, Alphaproteobacteria) in PG/FG groups—patterns mirroring human aging phenotypes where elevated inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6, TNF) correlate with chronic diseases and frailty [56]. The phenomenon of "inflamm-aging" impacts gut integrity and correlates with changes in microbial communities. For instance, TNF knockout mice (aged 18 months and older) did not exhibit the same degree of dysbiosis as their wild-type counterparts [57]. Similarly, in NLRP12-deficient mice, the abundance of colitis-protective Lachnospiraceae decreased, whereas colitis-promoting Erysipelotrichaceae increased in a TNF- and IL-6-dependent manner [26]. Both studies indicated that the changes in the relative abundances of these microbial communities were TNF-dependent, suggesting that their fluctuations are more closely associated with inflammation rather than aging. These microbes may be better adapted to survive fluctuations in the local redox and nutrient environments, thus providing them with a competitive advantage in the inflamed gut [58]. This observation may elucidate the correlation between chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., subclinical mastitis) and microbial dysbiosis [16, 17].

Conclusion

This study presents the first evidence establishing a link between parity-driven rumen microbiome alterations and reduced productive lifespan in dairy cows. We demonstrate that microbial dysbiosis in cows beyond the fourth lactation initiates a detrimental cascade: a metabolic shift from energy-efficient pathways toward increased methane production and acetate-dominant fermentation, concurrent with lipid dysregulation and compromised nutrient utilization (Fig. 10). Critically, these perturbations trigger systemic inflammation, impair host health, and ultimately accelerate milk yield decline, thereby shortening productive longevity. Our findings propose a novel concept of microbiota-targeted strategies to restore microbial homeostasis and extend dairy cow longevity. Future research should prioritize:(1) Causal validation of key microbial taxa and functions driving productivity decline;(2) Development of sustainable interventions to decouple lactation performance from inflammatory-metabolic dysfunction; (3) Methodological refinements (e.g., sampling frequency/timing optimization, solid-adherent microbiota profiling) to elucidate microbiome dynamics governing productivity and longevity.
