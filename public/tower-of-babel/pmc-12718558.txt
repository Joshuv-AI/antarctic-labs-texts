Challenges and strategies for early diagnosis and management of type 2 diabetes mellitus at the primary healthcare level in rural Western Vidarbha, India: a qualitative study
Authors: Umesh Kawalkar, Abhay Gaidhane, Sumit Aggarwal, Amar Mankar, Nitin Ambadekar, Priti Kogade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus presents a major public health challenge globally, with India bearing a disproportionate burden. Early detection and management at the primary healthcare (PHC) level are critical but remain underexplored in rural contexts.MethodsWe conducted a phenomenological qualitative study in Akola district from western Vidarbha region of India. Data were collected through 15 key informant interviews with healthcare providers, 11 interviews with patients and 13 focus group discussions with community health workers. Thematic analysis was performed following Braun and Clarke’s framework, and intercoder reliability was established (Cohen’s Kappa=0.85).ResultsBarriers included limited provider knowledge, patient health literacy gaps, fragmented referral systems and resource shortages. Facilitators encompassed training initiatives, home-based screening innovations, community awareness programmes and integration of specialist support. A conceptual framework was developed mapping systemic, provider and community-level determinants influencing diabetes outcomes.ConclusionStrengthening early diabetes screening and management at the PHC level requires multifaceted interventions encompassing workforce capacity development, supply chain improvements and community engagement. The findings offer a scalable model for integrating diabetes care into primary health systems in resource-constrained settings, contributing to global efforts to address the rising non-communicable disease burden.Keywords: Health Personnel, Public Health, Public Health Practice, Qualitative Research, Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus management is given special attention at the primary healthcare level because of the disease’s epidemic nature and the fact that its prevalence is continuously increasing.1 2 Globally, over the past three decades, the number of people with type 2 diabetes has more than doubled, making it one of the most important public health challenges. The situation in India is not different. India is the diabetic capital of the world.1 3 It is frequently not diagnosed until complications appear, and approximately one-third of all people with diabetes may remain undiagnosed. To date, there is strong evidence that vigorous treatment of diabetes type 2 can decrease the morbidity and mortality of the disease by decreasing its chronic complications.4 Diabetes care requires continuous patient commitment because of the complexity of treatment and the need for adherence to long-term lifestyle modification and medication. Over the past 20 years, the responsibility for the care of people with diabetes has shifted away from hospitals to primary care.5 It is the challenge for primary physicians to give type 2 diabetes patients the tools for active participation in the management of the disease. During this period, randomised trials have demonstrated that if regular review of patients is guaranteed, the standard of primary care can be as good as or better than hospital outpatient care in the short term. Several guidelines and diabetes management programmes have been developed nationally and locally to improve diabetes care in the community.

Primary healthcare is the first line of care provision and the most accessible service for the population. Primary healthcare services globally, and in India specifically, still face numerous challenges, such as lack of manpower; infrastructure inadequacy; inadequate supply of medicine; inadequate implementation of evidence-based medicine; challenges related to teamwork; impaired information systems and challenges related to ethics.6,8 However, empirical data suggest that compliance with diabetes clinical practice recommendations is inadequate in primary care and that a large proportion of patients with diabetes remain at high risk.9 10 We planned a study in our context focussing on barriers and enablers for early diagnosis of diabetes and management of patients with diabetes in primary care, outpatient and community settings.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design using a phenomenological approach to explore the experiences of healthcare providers and patients regarding the early diagnosis and management of diabetes mellitus at the primary healthcare level. A phenomenology-based inquiry enabled the capture of in-depth, lived experiences related to diabetes screening and management practices.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Akola district of western Vidarbha region, Maharashtra state. Administratively, the district comprises one municipal corporation (Akola Municipal Corporation), six municipal councils (Murtizapur, Akot, Balapur, Patur, Telhara and Barshitakli), and approximately 535 gram panchayats covering 864 villages. As per the 2011 Census, Akola has a population of approximately 1.81 million, with around 60% residing in rural areas. Primary healthcare services are delivered through 30 primary health centres (PHCs) and 178 subcentres, supported by both public and private providers across allopathic, ayurvedic and homeopathic systems.11

Participant selection

Participants were selected through purposive sampling. The study population included healthcare providers actively involved in diabetes care like medical officers (MOs), taluka health officers (THOs), that is, block level health officers, district non-communicable disease (NCD) consultant, community health officers (CHOs), auxiliary nurse midwives (ANMs), multipurpose health workers (MPWs), accredited social health activists (ASHAs) and adult diabetic patients. Inclusion criteria for healthcare providers included active engagement in diabetes screening and management services, willingness to participate and availability during data collection. For patients, inclusion criteria were: age ≥30 years, diagnosis of diabetes or identified as high-risk through PHC-based screening and willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included healthcare workers not involved in diabetes care and patients with severe diabetes complications or cognitive impairments affecting participation. Healthcare centres and participants were approached via official communication with the district health office and PHC staff rosters. No invited participants refused to participate or dropped out of the study. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

Sampling strategy

Purposive sampling was used to ensure a diverse representation across urban and rural areas. Separate focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with ASHAs and other healthcare workers (CHO, MPW and ANM) to minimise hierarchical influence during data collection. Data saturation was achieved when no new themes emerged during interviews and FGDs.

Data collection procedures

Research team and reflexivity

All interviews and FGDs were conducted by trained qualitative researchers who were not affiliated with the healthcare facilities involved in diabetes care. The researchers held advanced degrees in public health and medicine (MPH, MD, PhD scholar) and were employed as academic faculty and public-health researchers at the time of the study. The team included both male and female facilitators to minimise gender-related response bias. Each facilitator received formal training in qualitative research methods, including the use of the R package for qualitative data analysis (RQDA) software and thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s framework.12 No prior relationship existed between the researchers and participants. Participants were informed about the researchers’ roles, study objectives and independent positions to reduce social-desirability bias. Reflexive journaling and regular debriefing sessions were maintained throughout data collection to enhance credibility and reduce researcher bias.

Data were collected through key informant interviews (KIIs) and FGDs with healthcare providers, ASHAs and patients. 15 KIIs were conducted with 13 MOs, one THO and one district NCD consultant. 11 patients (six newly diagnosed and five previously diagnosed) were also interviewed about their experiences of screening, diagnosis, barriers to care and access to treatment. Although the total number of interviews was modest, data saturation was achieved, as no new codes or concepts emerged during the final interviews.

6 FGDs (8–10 participants each, total n=54) were held with ASHAs to explore the challenges they face in diabetes screening, their perceptions of community-level barriers, training needs and the feasibility of task-sharing with other health cadres. Separate FGDs were also conducted with CHOs (n=12), MPWs (n=16) and ANMs (n=15) to understand provider experiences and system-level challenges.

Semistructured interview and discussion guides were developed from existing literature on diabetes care at the primary-health-care level and aligned with the study objectives. For KIIs, open-ended questions explored awareness and practices related to early diagnosis and management of diabetes, the availability and use of diagnostic and treatment services, perceived barriers to screening and follow-up and feasible suggestions for improving diabetes care. The FGD guide includes questions related to experiences with screening and referral processes, training and supervisory support, community beliefs and misconceptions about diabetes and practical solutions for improving early detection and follow-up at the community level. The guides were reviewed by qualitative research experts and pilot-tested in one PHC for clarity, cultural appropriateness and contextual relevance. Facilitators used flexible probes to encourage detailed responses and ensure that all themes were covered.

Data management and transcription

All interviews and FGDs were conducted in private settings within PHC premises or community centres without the presence of non-participants. Field notes were maintained alongside audio recordings to capture non-verbal cues and contextual information. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min. While verbatim transcription and translation into English were performed, transcripts were not returned to participants due to concerns over feasibility, literacy barriers and confidentiality. However, member checking was conducted during the discussions to verify interpretation and ensure accurate representation of participant perspectives. Minor themes such as cultural stigma, belief in traditional medicine and scepticism about early diagnosis were included to reflect the diversity of participant views. During transcription, personal identifiers were removed, and participants were assigned unique codes to ensure confidentiality. Data were securely stored with restricted access to the research team.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis approach following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework was employed. The phases included: (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the final report.12 Data coding and organisation were supported using RQDA, an open-source qualitative analysis software within R. Three researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts. Intercoder reliability was assessed, yielding a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.85, indicating strong agreement. Regular team meetings were held to resolve discrepancies and ensure coding consistency.

Trustworthiness and rigour

Credibility was enhanced through member checking and peer debriefing. Dependability was ensured through maintaining a comprehensive audit trail. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive journaling and bracketing potential researcher biases. Triangulation of data sources (healthcare providers, ASHAs and patients) and methods (KIIs and FGDs) was employed to improve analytic rigour. Thick description of participant contexts was used to enhance transferability.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and community members were not directly involved in designing, conducting or reporting this qualitative study. However, patients diagnosed with or at risk of diabetes mellitus participated in KIIs to share their experiences regarding early diagnosis and management at the primary healthcare level. The research team plans to conduct structured feedback meetings with participating primary healthcare providers, ASHAs and community representatives in collaboration with district health authorities in Akola. These dissemination sessions will be organised through the district NCD cell to promote dialogue on feasible actions for strengthening early diabetes screening and management under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases.

As per the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases, healthcare providers from subcentres and PHCs should screen all adults above the age of 30 years once a year for type 2 diabetes mellitus as a useful strategy for early detection. A total of 11 type 2 diabetic patients were interviewed, with a mean (±SD) age of 52.4 (±6.4) years and gender (five male and six female). A total of 6 patients were newly diagnosed, and education levels ranged from no formal schooling to higher secondary. Most lived within 5 km of PHCs, using walking or public transport to access service. Based on insights from KIIs and FGDs, the study identified major enablers and barriers influencing the implementation of early type 2 diabetes mellitus screening and management at the primary healthcare level.

Barriers to early diagnosis of diabetes mellitus

Data from KIIs with MOs and district NCD staff, FGDs with ASHAs, ANMs, CHOs and MPWs and patient narratives revealed three major domains of barriers: provider-related, community-related and health-system-related. These interlinked challenges highlight the complexity of implementing early diabetes diagnosis and management at the PHC level.

Provider level barriers

Healthcare providers, including CHOs and ASHAs, demonstrated satisfactory awareness of the screening process and recognised their role in early detection.

We screened all male and female above 30 years for blood sugar and blood pressure for Diabetes mellitus and Hypertension. If results are abnormal, we refer them to the primary health center. We advise them for lifestyle modification like diet and exercise, avoid tobacco, etc, and ask them to follow-up again after 7 days. Then we test again screening is better because the disease onset is early and if undetected, could lead to complications. (CHO)

However, gaps remained in comprehensive diabetes knowledge among ASHAs, ANMs and MPWs, particularly in understanding early symptoms, risk factors and treatment protocols.

There was a middle-aged man in my village, who had been complaining of constant fatigue for several months. If we had known about these symptoms earlier, we could have asked him to get tested and started treatment on time. Many of us, even healthcare workers, fail to recognize these early signs of diabetes, leading to unnecessary suffering and complications. (ASHA)

Participants also expressed challenges in educating patients effectively about diabetes and lifestyle modification.

A middle-aged woman came to the clinic with high blood sugar levels. She said she didn’t eat sweets at all but drank 6–7 cups of tea daily, each with two spoons of sugar. She didn’t realize that this was contributing to her high sugar levels. Many patients do not understand the hidden sugars in their daily diet. (MO)

Community level barriers

From the patient and community perspective, low health literacy, cultural beliefs and misinformation emerged as strong barriers. Many individuals did not perceive fatigue, weight loss or delayed wound healing as warning signs, attributing them to ageing or hard work.

For months, I was feeling tired all the time, but I thought it was because of my routine work. I didn’t think it was serious. It was only when I felt dizzy one day and went to the hospital that I found out I had diabetes. (Patient)

Cultural norms and stigma also influenced care-seeking behaviour.

In our community, there is a stigma associated with diabetes. People hesitate to seek medical help due to fear of being labelled as ‘sick’. This cultural perception often leads to delayed diagnosis and complications. (ANM)

Preference for traditional or home remedies over allopathic treatment further delayed diagnosis and adherence.

My neighbour told me drinking bitter gourd juice every morning would cure my diabetes. I stopped taking my medicines and started doing that, but my sugar levels got worse. (Patient)

In addition, many patients were resistant to behavioural change, especially regarding tobacco use.

One of my patients, older farmer, had been smoking beedis (locally hand-rolled cigarettes made of raw tobacco wrapped in tendu leaves) for over three decades. It took multiple counselling sessions and support from his family to help him reduce his smoking, but he still struggles to quit completely. (MO)

These findings emphasise that behavioural change and diabetes awareness require ongoing community-based engagement and culturally sensitive counselling strategies.

Health system level barriers

Systemic and organisational issues significantly constrained early diabetes detection efforts. MPWs reported excessive workload due to their involvement in multiple national health programmes, leaving limited time for NCD activities.

Along with diabetes work, we are responsible for active surveillance for malaria, tuberculosis, and dengue; vaccination sessions; drinking water testing for water quality; sanitary surveys of villages; reporting under various national programs and administrative works. With so many tasks, it becomes difficult to give each patient the time needed for proper screening. (MPW)

Coordination gaps among frontline workers further weakened service delivery.

There is a lack of coordination between ASHA workers, medical officers, and other healthcare providers. This fragmentation hampers our efforts to provide holistic diabetes care at the community level. (ANM)

KIIs with programme managers also highlighted intermittent medicine shortages and insufficient diagnostic infrastructure, contributing to patient dropouts and delayed follow-up. Data from the NCD portal corroborated this, indicating that only around 40% of registered diabetic patients in rural areas consistently attend follow-up appointments across the district.

The triangulation of findings from providers, patients and programme managers reveals that barriers operate across multiple levels from knowledge and workload at the provider level, to awareness and stigma at the community level, and resource gaps within the health system. These overlapping challenges underscore the need for a comprehensive, multilevel approach to strengthen early diabetes screening and management under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases.

Facilitators to early diagnosis and management of type 2 diabetes mellitus

The qualitative findings identified multiple enablers across three interrelated domains: provider, community and health-system levels that collectively strengthen diabetes detection and management in PHC settings.

Provider level enablers

Regular in-service training programmes emerged as a key facilitator, helping healthcare providers remain updated with current diabetes management protocols.

Regular training programmes are essential for us to stay updated with the latest developments in diabetes management. It helps us improve our skills and provide better care to our patients. (MO)

Such training enhanced providers’ diagnostic confidence, improved their counselling skills and standardised screening and follow-up practices. Another major facilitator was the proactive role of community-level workers, especially ASHAs and CHOs, in linking screening with health education and counselling. Their consistent engagement allowed earlier identification of symptoms and more effective lifestyle modification counselling.

Community level enablers

At the community level, continuous support and follow-up by ASHA workers played a vital role in sustaining patient adherence to medication and lifestyle recommendations. Regular home visits reinforced the importance of medication, dietary modification and follow-up testing, gradually improving glycaemic control among patients. Community awareness and health promotion activities were also identified as key facilitators.

Awareness campaigns are powerful tools in educating communities about diabetes prevention and management. They help people understand the importance of early diagnosis and lifestyle modifications. (MO)

Public education campaigns conducted through village meetings, local media and health and wellness sessions helped bridge knowledge gaps and foster early care-seeking behaviour.

Additionally, home-based blood-sample collection improved access for individuals in remote and underserved areas, making screening more convenient.

Home-based blood-sample collection with prior intimation is a great facilitator for patients, especially those who live in remote areas. It saves them time and effort, making it more convenient to monitor their diabetes. (MPW)

This intervention minimised travel-related challenges, particularly for elderly and mobility-limited patients, thereby increasing screening coverage.

Health system level enablers

At the system level, effective logistics and stock management ensured uninterrupted availability of diagnostic tools and essential medicines.

Effective stock management ensures that we have all the necessary equipment and supplies available when needed. It makes our job easier and ensures smooth operations in diabetes care. (MO)

Regular supply audits, timely procurement and coordination with district health authorities were cited as best practices sustaining continuity of care.

Periodic specialist visits to PHCs were another important facilitator, bridging the gap between peripheral and tertiary care. Such visits allowed rural patients to access endocrinology consultations, advanced management advice and personalised care plans locally, without the burden of long-distance travel. Furthermore, integration of these enablers within existing National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases structures and the HWC platform enhanced service efficiency and accountability, offering a sustainable model for comprehensive diabetes management in rural India.

The enablers were identified through triangulation of data obtained from KIIs with MOs and district officials and FGDs with ASHAs, ANMs and MPWs. This multisource synthesis enhanced the credibility of findings and provided a comprehensive understanding of factors that strengthen early diabetes screening and management in PHC settings.

The thematic analysis provided an in-depth understanding of the complex, multilevel factors shaping early diabetes diagnosis and management at the primary healthcare level. The emergent themes were systematically organised into three interrelated domains: provider related, community related and health system related, reflecting the continuum of service delivery from individual practice to institutional governance. Provider level barriers were primarily linked to limited technical training, excessive administrative workload and insufficient diagnostic infrastructure. Community level barriers encompassed low health literacy, entrenched cultural beliefs and social stigma associated with chronic diseases, while system-level challenges included fragmented referral pathways, irregular drug supplies and weak integration of NCD services within primary care.

In contrast, several enabling factors surfaced across these domains, including periodic skill-enhancement programmes, home-based screening initiatives, improved logistics management and community engagement activities. These findings demonstrate both the constraints and adaptive capacities inherent within the health system. These measures were perceived by participants as both feasible and sustainable within existing PHC resources.

It reflects the real experiences and views shared by participants. This framework illustrates dynamic relationships between healthcare provider capacities, community awareness, health system support and patient-provider interactions.

The framework shows that challenges such as limited skills of health staff, low awareness in the community and weak support systems are closely linked. It also highlights how practical actions such as regular staff training, reliable drug and supply management and community-based education can improve diabetes care. Discussion

This qualitative study explored how provider skills, community awareness and system resources together influence the early diagnosis and management of type 2 diabetes at the PHC level. By integrating the perspectives of healthcare providers, patients and community health workers, the study identified key barriers such as limited provider training, inadequate diagnostic resources and low community awareness and feasible, context-specific solutions to strengthen early detection and continuity of care within PHCs. These findings directly align with the results presented, which showed that health-system constraints (eg, medicine stock-outs, shortage of staff), community-level barriers (low awareness and stigma) and provider-level challenges (limited skills and high workload) collectively limit the effectiveness of diabetes screening at the PHC level. Similar challenges have been reported in other countries. Studies from Oman and Uganda described weak technical support, lack of teamwork and limited patient participation in diabetes management.13 14 Our findings are consistent with these reports, particularly regarding resource shortages and poor community engagement. In India, evaluations of the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases have shown persistent shortages of trained staff, diagnostic tools and essential medicines, especially in tribal and rural regions.15 These issues were also reflected in our study, where participants reported frequent laboratory supply gaps and insufficient human resources. Studies from Belagavi Taluka and rural Jaipur similarly noted poor referral systems, inadequate staff training and low community awareness.16 17 Such parallels suggest that while contextual differences exist, the underlying barriers limiting workforce capacity, weak logistics and poor health literacy remain consistent across primary-care settings.

Based on these findings, a conceptual framework was developed to explain how provider, community and system factors interact to influence early diagnosis and diabetes management. The framework illustrates how gaps in knowledge, infrastructure and communication reinforce one another, limiting the reach and effectiveness of current screening efforts. Understanding these interconnections can help design interventions that act across multiple levels of the health system. Although this study was conducted in Akola district, challenges such as inadequate staffing, low community awareness and disrupted supply chains are common across rural India.17 18 Therefore, the insights generated here may inform similar low-resource settings with appropriate local adaptation. This study also highlights several practical strategies to improve diabetes services within existing primary-care systems. Regular modular training for healthcare providers can improve screening quality and patient counselling. Home-based fasting blood sample collection by ANMs or trained ASHAs, supported by mobile laboratory units, can increase compliance for confirmatory testing. Organising monthly ‘Diabetes Days’ at HWCs, integrating check-ups, counselling and group education, can enhance patient engagement. Real-time stock tracking and flexible procurement through the NCD portal can prevent drug shortages. Additionally, establishing peer-support groups and integrating diabetes education within Village Health and Nutrition Days can promote adherence and self-care. These actions are practical, low-cost and aligned with National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases and Ayushman Bharat HWC initiatives. Implementing them can strengthen early diagnosis, ensure continuity of care and make diabetes services more equitable and sustainable in rural India. Evidence from other countries, such as community-based self-management programmes in Oman and nurse-led care models in South Korea, demonstrates that combining workforce training with patient empowerment offers scalable and sustainable solutions.13

One of the main strengths of this study is that it included the views of healthcare providers, community health workers and patients, which helped to understand different aspects of diabetes care at the PHC level. The participation of various cadres such as MOs, CHOs and ASHAs provided a realistic picture of how diabetes screening and management are carried out in the field. The use of thematic analysis, member checking and regular team discussions improved the accuracy and reliability of the findings.

This study has some limitations. It was conducted in one district only, so the findings may not fully reflect the situation in other areas with different social or administrative settings. As a qualitative study, the aim was to gain a deeper understanding rather than to generalise results to a larger population. Also, since the information was self-reported, some responses might have been influenced by recall or social desirability. Even with these limitations, the study provides useful and practical insights for improving diabetes screening and management under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases.

Future research should test and refine this framework in varied PHC settings to identify which strategies are most effective and scalable in improving early diabetes care delivery. Strengthening capacity-building, task-sharing among providers, community mobilisation, consistent drug supply and digital reporting systems will be essential to sustain improvements in diabetes care at the primary healthcare level.18,20

Conclusion

This study contributes evidence from rural western Vidarbha, demonstrating how integrated and community-anchored primary-care strategies can effectively address diagnostic and management gaps in type 2 diabetes. Moving beyond previous descriptive assessments of NCD programme readiness, it offers context-specific, actionable solutions co-developed with frontline health workers and patients. Strengthening diabetes care in India will require coordinated efforts among policymakers, healthcare providers and communities, supported by robust conceptual frameworks and informed by successful implementation models from comparable settings. Embedding and scaling such locally responsive strategies within the evolving primary healthcare system can play a pivotal role in reducing the growing diabetes burden and advancing universal NCD care.
