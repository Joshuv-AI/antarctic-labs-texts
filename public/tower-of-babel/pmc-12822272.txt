Mechanistic exploration of bisphenol A in primary Sjögren’s syndrome using network toxicology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations and experimental validation
Authors: Zelin Wang, Jinle Zhang, Jingqi Dong, Shufen Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder marked by exocrine gland impairment and systemic manifestations. Environmental endocrine disruptors, including bisphenol A (BPA), have been associated with immunological dysregulation; however, their involvement in pSS is not well-defined. This study integrated network toxicology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vitro validation to examine the potential effects of BPA on pSS. Bioinformatics investigation revealed 25 overlapping targets between BPA-associated genes and differentially expressed genes related to pSS, with CASP3, PTGS1, and PTGS2 identified as main possibilities. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations validated robust and stable interactions of BPA with these proteins. Cellular studies with human submandibular gland epithelial cells demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity of BPA, accompanied by substantial overexpression of CASP3, PTGS1, and PTGS2 at 1 µM exposure. The data indicate that BPA enhances apoptosis and inflammatory signaling in salivary gland cells, potentially contributing to pSS progression. This study provides mechanistic insight into how BPA may contribute to autoimmune disease development, highlighting its potential role in pSS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-01070-3.Keywords: Primary Sjögren's syndrome, Bisphenol A, Molecular docking, Molecular dynamics simulations, Network toxicology

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder marked by the gradual loss of exocrine glands. It primarily impacts the salivary and lacrimal glands, sometimes presenting clinically with mucosal symptoms such as xerophthalmia and xerostomia [1], and may be associated with multi-systemic damage and a higher risk of lymphoma [2, 3]. This disease primarily impacts middle-aged women, exhibiting a male-to-female ratio of 1:9. The global prevalence rate is between 0.01 and 0.05% [4]. The etiology is intricate, encompassing various elements including genetic predisposition, environmental influences, and dysregulated immunological responses [5]. While immunosuppressants and biologics can partially mitigate symptoms, therapeutic alternatives remain comparatively constrained. Consequently, illness preventive techniques are of paramount importance.

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a prevalent industrial chemical and one of the most extensively manufactured endocrine disruptors worldwide [6]. It is frequently present in plastic items, food packaging, and medical instruments [7]. Its stable chemical qualities and inexpensive production costs have resulted in widespread application across diverse consumer products. BPA is environmentally persistent and bioaccumulative. Its non-covalent binding properties facilitate its ongoing leaching from products into the environment and human bodies, presenting possible health hazards. BPA, even at minimal concentrations, can disrupt normal physiological activities in the body via many chemical mechanisms. BPA has estrogenic activity and can interfere with androgen and thyroid hormone signaling, disrupting endocrine regulation [8, 9]. The immunotoxicity of BPA has garnered heightened scrutiny, since it can induce the release of cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ) [10] and activate critical inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB [11]. BPA influences immune function through oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death, promotes autoantibody formation, and may trigger or worsen autoimmune disorders [12, 13].

Network toxicology utilises systems biology and bioinformatics to create a multi-dimensional interaction network of “compounds - targets - pathways - diseases,” thereby elucidating the critical biological pathways through which BPA disrupts the pathogenesis of pSS, offering an effective and systematic analytical framework for environmental health risk assessment [14]. Molecular docking additionally forecasts the binding mechanism and affinity between BPA and critical target proteins, where a reduced binding energy signifies a more stable association and a heightened potential for interference [15]. Molecular dynamics simulations dynamically assess the stability and conformational alterations of complexes at the atomic level, hence augmenting the reliability of binding predictions [16]. This study aims to integrate network toxicology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vitro cellular experiments to comprehensively assess the potential targets, effects, and molecular mechanisms of the endocrine BPA on pSS. The human submandibular gland epithelial cell line (HSG) was utilised to ascertain the optimal treatment conditions via various concentrations of BPA intervention, and real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR) technology was employed to assess the expression alterations of key target genes associated with the network predictions. Materials and methods

Acquisition of BPA targets

The SMILE structure of BPA was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [17]. Toxicological data, including LD50 and toxicity scores, were obtained from the ProTox-II database (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/). Potential BPA target genes were predicted using ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) [18], STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/) [19], PharmMapper (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html) [20] and SwissTargetPrediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) [21]. All predictions were restricted to human proteins. In ChEMBL, potential targets were obtained through compound searches.In STITCH, interactions between the query compound and potential target genes (including known and predicted targets) were identified. In PharmMapper, target genes similar to the compound structure were predicted through virtual screening. In SwissTargetPrediction, potential target genes were predicted based on the chemical structure of the compound. Finally, all target genes were standardized using the Uniport (https://www.uniprot.org/) [22] database, and the database results are integrated, with duplicate items removed.

Data resources

PSS-related genes were derived from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [23] datasets GSE93683 [24] and GSE84844 [24]. The GSE93683 dataset was based on the GPL570 platform and contained the gene expression profiles of immune cells from 24 pSS patients and 24 healthy controls. The GSE84844 dataset was also based on the GPL570 platform and included the whole blood transcriptome information of 30 pSS patients and 30 healthy controls. Both datasets are microarray-based gene expression profiles generated using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array (GPL570).

Identification of pSS differentially expressed genes

The GEOquery package in R (v4.4.0) was used to load the GSE93683 and GSE84844 datasets, and the “hgu133plus2.db” package was used to convert probe ids to gene symbols, followed by log2 conversion of the expression matrix. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the pSS group and the healthy control group were analyzed using the “limma” package. The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method. The screening criteria for GSE93683 was: adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.4, while for GSE84844 was: |log 2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. The DEGs were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps.

Identification of target genes for BPA related pSS diseases

The DEGs were intersected with BPA-predicted targets, thereby obtaining the potential targets for BPA-induced pSS occurrence. The results were visualized using the Venn diagram and Cytoscape 3.7.1 [25].

GO and KEGG analysis

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis (KEGG) are two widely used bioinformatics analysis methods, which are employed to determine the functions of genes or proteins and the biological processes they are involved in. To gain a deeper understanding of the biological functions and signaling pathways of the potential targets of BPA-induced pSS, we conducted GO and KEGG analyses using the R “clusterprofiler” package [26]. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was set to ensure the accuracy of the enriched pathways and functions.

Construct the protein interaction network and screen the core genes

Input the potential interacting target genes into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) [27], select the species as “Homo sapiens”, and set the “Minimum Interaction Score” to “Medium Confidence > 0.4” to obtain the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network information. Then, use Cytocape 3.7.1 to visualize and analyze the PPI network diagram. In the Cytohubba plugin, the four commonly used algorithms (MCC, MNC, Degree, and Closeness) were employed to screen the core target genes of BPA for pSS. Finally, the top ten core target genes ranked by each of the four algorithms were interacted, and the results were visualized using a Venn diagram.

Molecular Docking

To investigate the binding ability of BPA to the core genes of pSS, we conducted molecular docking analysis using the AutoDock Vina [28] software. The structure of the BPA molecule was obtained from the PubChem database, and the structure of the core target molecule was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The docking results were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio for further analysis.

Molecular dynamics simulation

A 100-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulation was conducted using Gromacs 2022 [29] to evaluate the binding stability of the protein-ligand complex. The protein potential field was selected as CHARMM 36, and the ligand topological structure was constructed using GAFF2 force field parameters. Periodic boundary conditions were adopted, and the protein-ligand complex was placed in a cubic box. The TIP3P water model was used to fill the water molecules into the box, forming a water box with a periodic boundary of 1.2 nm. The electrostatic interactions were handled using the the PME method and Verlet al.gorithms. Initially, the steepest descent algorithm was used for energy minimization to optimize the initial conformation of the complex. All simulations were performed under periodic boundary conditions to eliminate edge effects. Subsequently, a 0.1-nanosecond equilibration was carried out under the canonical ensemble (NVT), with an integration time step of 2 fs. Then, the system was further equilibrated under the isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) to stabilize the temperature and pressure. The root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of Gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and free energy landscape (FEL) were calculated using Gromacs’ built-in tools. Subsequently, the simulation trajectories were analyzed using the GMX_MMPBSA tool. This method combines the molecular mechanics (MM) energy term with the poisson-Boltzmann surface area (PBSA) calculation, providing a reliable theoretical framework for evaluating the intermolecular binding affinity.

Cell culture and treatment

HSG (Tong Pai Technology, Shanghai, China) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1, Solarbio, Beijing) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The culture was conducted in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. BPA (≥ 99%, Macklin, Shanghai, China) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Macklin, Shanghai, China) to prepare the stock solution, and then diluted with complete medium to prepare working solutions of 0.1, 1, 10 and 50 µM; An equal volume of DMSO was added to the control group to keep the final concentration of DMSO in each group below 0.1%. After the cells adhered and grew to the logarithmic phase, they were treated with media containing different concentrations of BPA for 24 h, among which the 0 µM group was used as the solvent control. After the processing is completed, the cells are collected for subsequent experiments.

Cell proliferation assessment

HSG cells were inoculated in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Then, they were treated with 0 µM, 0.1 µM, 1 µM, 10 µM, and 50 µM of BPA for 24 h to determine the optimal concentration of BPA. Cell viability was evaluated using CCK-8 (Beyotime, China), and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm (MD SpectraMax 190, USA).

Expression of mRNA detected by the RT-PCR

The optimal concentration was selected based on CCK-8 to treat the cells. qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green I staining method to detect the core target genes. After extracting the total cell RNA, reverse transcription was completed using the TransGen ER501 reagent kit (TransGen, China). Real-time PCR was conducted on the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, China). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary file 1.

Result

Molecular structure and network toxicological analysis of BPA

The chemical structure information of BPA (including the chemical formula, molecular Structures and SMILES structure) was obtained from the PubChem database (Fig. 2A). ProTox-II predicted an LD50 of 4950 mg/kg for BPA (toxicity class 5), suggesting low acute oral toxicity (Fig. 2B). The toxicity model report showed that BPA mainly targeted Estrogen Receptor Alpha (ER), Estrogen Receptor Ligand Binding Domain (ER-LBD), Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP), and Cytochrome P2C9 (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, 107, 102, 10 and 165 toxic targets for BPA were retrieved from the SwissTargetPrediction, ChEMBL, STITCH and PharmMapper databases respectively. After integrating the targets obtained from the four databases and deleting duplicate data, a total of 318 toxic targets of BPA were identified (Supplementary Table 1).

Identification of DEGs in pSS

Using the “limma” software package, DEGs between pSS patients and healthy controls were analyzed in the GSE93683 and GSE84844 datasets respectively. In GSE93683, 624 DEGs were identified, including 342 upregulated and 282 downregulated genes. In GSE84844, 887 DEGs were identified, with 831 genes upregulated and 56 genes downregulated (Supplementary Table 2). The top 20 differentially expressed genes in each dataset were illustrated using volcano plots and heatmaps (Figs. 3A, B, C, D).

Identification of BPA related pSS targets

The DEGs were intersected with the predicted BPA targets resulting in a total of 25 overlapping genes. These 25 genes were considered potential BPA-related targets in pSS. The interaction results were represented by a Venn diagram (Fig. 3E).

Functional enrichment analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of the biological processes and signaling pathways related to the mechanism of action of the 25 potential targets in BPA induced pSS, we conducted GO and KEGG analyses. The Gene Ontology enrichment analysis covered three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) (Fig. 4A). The BP enrichment analysis revealed that response to corticosteroid, response to steroid hormone, prostaglandin biosynthetic process, prostanoid biosynthetic process and positive regulation of interleukin-2 production is the primary biological process involving potential targets. In the CC, the top three components included protein kinase complex, nuclear outer membrane, cyclin-dependent protein kinase holoenzyme complex and nuclear inner membrane. In the MF category, cyclin-dependent protein serine/threonine kinase regulator activity, cysteine-type endopeptidase activity involved in apoptotic signaling pathway and cyclin-dependent protein serine/threonine kinase inhibitor activity. played critical roles.KEGG pathway analysis showed that the potential targets were significantly enriched in Serotonergic synapse, Ovarian steroidogenesis, Arachidonic acid metabolism, Efferocytosis and Glioma. (Fig. 4B).

Construction of protein interaction networks and acquisition of core targets

The STRING database was used to construct the PPI network. PPI networks were visualized in Cytoscape. This network consisted of 25 nodes and 36 edges, with an average node degree of 2.88 (Fig. 5A). The CytoHubba plugin generated the top 10 potential targets ranked by MCC (Fig. 5B), MNC (Fig. 5C), Degree (Fig. 5D), and Closeness (Fig. 5E). Integrating the four algorithms identified eight core targets: CASP3, PTGS2, PTGS1, CCNA2, TYMS, CASP1, CDK6, and KIF11 (Fig. 5F).

Molecular docking of BPA to core targets

To investigate the affinity between environmental pollutants and the core targets, molecular docking was employed for analysis. Using the Autodock Vina software, the binding sites and interactions between BPA and 8 core targets were discovered. A lower binding energy indicated a more stable protein–ligand complex. A docking score lower than − 4.25 kcal/mol indicates a certain binding activity, while a score lower than − 5.0 kcal/mol indicates significant binding activity. Docking results showed strong affinity between BPA and all core targets, with binding energies below − 6 kcal/mol (Table 1). The docking results were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio (Fig. 6).

Molecular dynamics simulations of CASP3-BPA, PTGS1-BPA and PTGS2-BPA

Based on the binding energies obtained from molecular docking, we conducted molecular dynamics simulations on the complexes of CASP3(5ibp)-BPA, PTGS2(5f19)-BPA, and PTGS1(6y3c)-BPA with higher binding energies, in order to further verify their binding stability.

RMSD reflects the degree of conformational fluctuation of proteins and can be used to evaluate the stability of ligand-protein binding. The smaller the value, the more stable the binding [30]. All complexes reached RMSD equilibrium after ~ 20 ns and remained stable thereafter (Fig. 7A). RMSF reflects the fluctuation of amino acid residues in proteins. RMSF fluctuations (0.05–0.20 nm) further supported complex stability (Fig. 7B). Rg results showed that the overall Rg of the CASP3-BPA complex slightly decreased, indicating that the protein tended to be compact after binding, while the overall compactness of PTGS1-BPA and PTGS2-BPA did not change much, but PTGS2 showed more local flexibility around the binding pocket. (Fig. 7C). SASA values remained stable across complexes, but the numerical levels were significantly different. Overall, CASP3-BPA was the most tightly bound, PTGS1-BPA was in the middle, and PTGS2-BPA was the loosest (Fig. 7D). Hydrogen bond numbers fluctuated between 1 and 4, with PTGS1 showing occasional transient increases, indicating that the binding of BPA was mainly driven by hydrophobic forces, van der Waals forces and electrostatic forces (Fig. 7E).

Molecular dynamics analysis of BPA binding to CASP3, PTGS1, and PTGS2. (A) RMSD. (B) RMSF. (C) Rg. (D) SASA. (E) Number of hydrogen bonds

To gain a more intuitive and in-depth understanding of the dynamic behavior and stability of the complexes, we constructed a three-dimensional FEL (Fig. 8A) based on the RMSD values and Rg values of the protein receptor complexes, combined with the Gibbs relative free energy along the Z-axis. FEL analysis revealed well-defined low-energy basins for all complexes, confirming overall stability.

Free energy landscape, the blue and green regions represent low-energy conformational states, suggesting stable configurations reveals the three complexes remain highly stable throughout the simulation: (A) CASP3, (B) PTGS1, and (C) PTGS2

To further explore the binding mechanism, we conducted MM/PBSA combined free energy calculations. The binding energies of CASP3, PTGS1 and PTGS2 with BPA were − 88.110 kJ/mol, -69.081 kJ/mol and − 157.124 kJ/mol respectively (Fig. 9). The results indicated that the binding affinities of these three targets to BPA were relatively high, and they all had good binding energies. This further verified the molecular docking results.

BPA affects the proliferation of HSG cells

To evaluate the effect of BPA on the viability of HSG cells, this study used the CCK-8 method to detect the changes in cell viability after treatment with different concentrations of BPA (0, 0.1, 1, 10, 50 µM) for 24 h. The results showed that as the concentration of BPA increased, the cell viability gradually decreased. Among them, the cell viability in the 10 µM BPA treatment group had already shown a significant decrease, and the 50 µM treatment group showed a more obvious decrease (Fig. 10). Data are presented as mean ± SD of three independent biological experiments (n = 3). Error bars indicate the standard deviation. BPA reduced HSG viability in a dose-dependent manner. It is worth noting that 1 µM BPA had a significant inhibitory effect on cell viability without causing excessive cytotoxicity, and it was subsequently selected for further experiments.

To further explore the effects of BPA on the expression of inflammatory and apoptotic-related genes in HSG cells, a qRT-PCR experiment was conducted 24 h after treatment with 1 µM BPA. Discussion

This study integrated network toxicology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and cellular experiments to show that BPA may modulate core pSS-related genes (e.g., CASP3, PTGS1, PTGS2) by influencing inflammatory and apoptotic pathways. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the potential toxicological effects of BPA in pSS.

BPA is a widely used environmental endocrine disruptor that can be released from consumer and dental materials [30]. Because the oral cavity is directly exposed, salivary glands may represent a primary site of BPA-associated inflammatory and apoptotic changes. Research indicates that BPA can leach into saliva during the curing or degradation of dental materials, sustaining elevated concentrations for a certain duration [31]. It can directly influence the salivary glands, provoking localised inflammatory responses and death of glandular cells, therefore worsening the d ysfunction of glandular output [32, 33].

To investigate the potential targets of BPA in pSS, we analysed the GEO dataset to find differentially expressed genes in pSS. We intersected BPA-predicted targets with pSS DEGs and identified 25 overlapping genes. GO and KEGG analyses showed enrichment in steroid and prostaglandin pathways, arachidonic acid metabolism, IL-2 regulation, and apoptosis, all of which are central to inflammatory and immune dysregulation in pSS.

Eight core targets (CASP3, PTGS2, PTGS1, CCNA2, TYMS, CASP1, CDK6, KIF11) were identified across all four algorithms. The molecular docking studies demonstrated that BPA displayed a robust binding affinity with all eight targets, with binding energies consistently below − 6 kcal/mol, indicating a stable association with the primary targets. To enhance the validation of the dependability and biological relevance of the docking outcomes, the three foremost complexes were chosen for molecular dynamics simulation. MD simulations further supported stable binding of BPA to CASP3, PTGS1, and PTGS2, consistent with docking results. The MM/PBSA calculation results demonstrated substantial binding energy between BPA and the three targets, thus validating the molecular docking findings.

CASP3 is a key executor of apoptosis. Excessive activation may result in heightened apoptosis of glandular cells and facilitate the death of glands in pSS. Several studies have established the significant role of CASP3 in the pathological alterations of salivary glands in pSS.A histological analysis revealed that in the ducts and acinar cells of pSS salivary glands, the activated form of Caspase-3 and the cleaved form of PARP were greatly elevated, whereas in healthy tissues, their expression was minimal [34]. CASP3 was similarly shown to be highly expressed in the small salivary gland tissues of the lips, with its levels significantly associated to γδ T cell infiltration, indicating that CASP3 may play a crucial role in the destruction of glandular epithelial cells [35]. Furthermore, TLR3 stimulates salivary gland epithelial cells and triggers apoptosis via caspase-3, so elucidating the relationship between external stimuli and the mechanism of glandular injury [36]. In a model experiment, the use of caspase inhibitors can postpone the autoimmune damage associated with pSS, underscoring the essential role of the caspase family in the pathogenesis of pSS [37].

PTGS1 regulates prostaglandin synthesis [38] and is aberrantly expressed in pSS salivary glands, particularly in infiltrating macrophages [39]. Its upregulation may contribute to prostaglandin-mediated inflammatory injury, suggesting a potential link between BPA exposure and PTGS1-driven inflammation. PTGS2 mediates PGE₂ synthesis and is implicated in salivary gland dysfunction. Autoantibodies in pSS can enhance PTGS2 expression and impair mucin secretion [40, 41]. BPA-induced PTGS2 activation may therefore exacerbate inflammatory responses and epithelial damage.

The pervasive presence of environmental contaminants, particularly BPA as a prevalent plasticizer, may have led to an underestimation of its potential impact. This study suggests a potential mechanism through which BPA may aggravate pSS, underscoring the possible contribution of environmental exposures to autoimmune disease susceptibility.

This study has limitations. Bioinformatic predictions require in vivo confirmation to establish causality. Although HSG cells showed transcriptional changes, protein-level validation and functional assays were not performed. In vitro BPA concentrations do not fully reflect environmental exposure, and future studies integrating animal models and epidemiological data are needed.

Conclusions

In summary, our integrated analysis combining network toxicology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and in vitro validation identified CASP3, PTGS1, and PTGS2 as key molecular targets potentially linking BPA exposure to the pathogenesis of primary Sjögren’s syndrome. BPA was found to promote inflammatory signaling and apoptosis in salivary gland epithelial cells, providing mechanistic insight into how environmental endocrine disruptors may contribute to glandular dysfunction in pSS. Although additional in vivo and epidemiological validation is required, these findings highlight BPA as a potential environmental risk factor for pSS and offer a theoretical foundation for future studies on exposure control and disease prevention.
