Ankle arthrodesis with non-vascularized fibular graft for neglected comminuted talar body fracture: A case report
Authors: Asep Santoso, Bagus Iman Brilianto, Totok Siswanto, Hillan Akbar, Dykall Naf'an Dzikri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Talus injuries present a diversified and distinct set of care issues, having a significant impact on the patient's short- and long-term functional outcomes. Talus fractures are extremely uncommon, accounting for only around 1% of all foot and ankle fractures. The degree of initial fracture displacement and the accuracy of fracture reduction are both factors that influence the result. This case report describes a case of a 30 year-old male patient with neglected close fracture talus on the left foot. The patient presented to the outpatient department with chief complaint of progressive pain over the left foot while walking for the past 4 months. The diagnosis was made based on radiograph, and managed with reconstruction and arthrodesis of the ankle with non-vascularized fibular graft. On 1-year follow up, the radiograph showed complete union and no neurovascular complication was reported. Ankle Arthrodesis with Non-vascularized fibular graft a can be viable options for managing neglected comminuted talar body fracture.

Introduction

Talus injuries present a diversified and distinct set of care issues, having a significant impact on the patient's short- and long-term functional outcomes. The talus is coated in articular cartilage to the extent of 60 to 70%, however it lacks muscle attachments and articulates with neighboring bony structures via capsuloligamentous constraints [1].

Talus fractures are extremely uncommon, accounting for only around 1% of all foot and ankle fractures. The talus neck is where the majority of talus fractures occur, accounting for 50% of all talus fractures [2]. The incidence of talus fractures varies between 0.1 and 0.85% of all fractures. The degree of initial fracture displacement and the accuracy of fracture reduction are both factors that influence the result [3]. The talus fracture is a medical emergency that has grown in prominence in recent years due to its rarity and unique blood supply [4].

The anastomotic ring around the talar neck is quite likely to be injured during the fracture, which will obstruct blood flow to the talus body. As a result, bone healing is slowed, and the healed fracture's integrity is compromised, resulting in a poor functional outcome. Osteonecrosis of the talus body, which eventually leads to collapse of the body, is the most feared consequence following talar neck fracture. Following a talar neck fracture, subtalar arthritis is the second most prevalent consequence [5].

The importance of early detection and treatment in preventing these consequences cannot be overstated. According to several research, talus fractures are easily missed during primary examination. Advanced imaging modalities such as computed tomography, ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance imaging can all help with early diagnosis and therapy, reducing long-term problems and morbidity in these individuals [6], [7].

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male presented to the outpatient department with chief complaint of progressive pain over the left foot while walking for the past 4 months. There was a history of motorcycle accident in July 2020. The patient did not seek medical advice because the pain was not significant. Patient was mobilizing over the affected foot without any support. X-ray of the affected foot is shown on Fig. 1. Discussions

Talus fractures are a relatively uncommon injury, accounting for 0.1–2.5% of all fractures and 3% to 5% of foot and ankle fractures. Because plain radiographs have a sensitivity of just 74% and a high rate of missed diagnoses, the true incidence of talar fractures may be underestimated. The most prevalent anatomic site for injury is the talar neck, which accounts for 45% to 50% of all talus fractures [[8], [9], [10]]. Talar body fractures have various classifications, and these classifications does not help in making treatment choices or predict outcome.

Despite the fact that plain radiographs are the gold standard for initial screening for bone fractures around the foot and ankle, their sensitivity for any talar injury is just 74%, with displacement being the most important factor in radiographic sensitivity. The most commonly missed fracture sites are talar dome osteochondral fracture, lateral process fracture, and posterior process fracture. Given these findings, computed tomography (CT) is the gold standard for diagnosis, despite the fact that only 91% of fractures at a level 1 trauma center were adequately assessed with a CT scan. Because 6.9% of talus fractures go undetected at the time of presentation, CT should be part of routine surveillance of ankle injuries with swelling and pain disproportionate to radiography findings [11].

Because talus fractures are mainly articular, maintaining a decreased joint line and stable articulation is critical for short-term functional status and long-term post-traumatic arthritis risk mitigation. As a result, nonoperative treatment should only be considered in patients with nondisplaced neck and body fractures, some nonambulatory patients, and those who are medically unable to endure surgery. In body fractures, even displacements of less than 1 mm are cause for surgical reduction and fixation. Without articular surface displacement, talar neck fractures can be treated non-operatively with rigid immobilization and stringent non-weightbearing, but fragment shift must be constantly monitored [12], [13].

Casting the foot and ankle in a neutral position for 6 weeks can be used to treat nondisplaced head and body fractures. For about 8 to 10 weeks, partial weightbearing is required until radiographic confirmation of fracture union is attained [12].

When soft tissue injury and/or the patient's medical condition prevents safe open reduction and internal fixation, external fixation should be performed to support a reduced talus fracture and/or dislocation. External fixation is described using a variety of ways. The medial safe zone lies along the anteromedial neck, proximal to the talonavicular joint and superior to the tibialis posterior tendon when pins must be placed into the talus itself. The safest zone on the lateral aspect of the talus is a tiny non-articular area of the neck, albeit due to the difficulties of precise palpation, this requires fluoroscopic localization [14].

The majority of therapy for articular talus fractures is anatomic reduction and rigid fixation to avoid changing contact pressures between the ankle and hindfoot articulations and reducing the risk of osteonecrosis; malunion tolerances typically fewer than 3 degrees. Level of fracture comminution, contemporaneous bone and/or soft tissue injury, and fracture pattern are additional surgical variables that govern approach, implant use, and fixation technique [14].

Even when treated promptly, talus neck fractures are associated with a high rate of sequelae, and in the long run, they can lead to chronic discomfort and stiffness in the ankle joint. The quantity of soft-tissue injury and the level of fracture displacement have been shown to be directly related to the development of osteonecrosis. Treatment options for talar osteonecrosis vary depending on the severity of the condition. Core decompression, bone grafting, arthrodesis, partial or whole talar replacement, and, in extreme cases, total ankle replacement are among the therapy options available [[4], [15], [16]].

Conclusion

Ankle Arthrodesis with Non-vascularized fibular graft can be viable options for managing neglected comminuted talar body fracture.
