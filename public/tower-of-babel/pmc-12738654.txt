DDRN: DETR with dual refinement networks for autonomous vehicle object detection
Authors: Jiayao Li, Chak Fong Cheang, Zhaolong Du, Xiaoyuan Yu, Suigu Tang, Qianxiang Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObject detection is an essential part of autonomous driving perception, as its effectiveness directly affects subsequent decision making and control. However, the difficulty of detecting small objects and the complexity of the driving environment poses challenges that prevent existing detectors from fully meeting the safety and efficiency requirements of autonomous driving systems. To address these challenges, we propose three components: Feature Pyramid Network- (FPN-), Classification Refinement Networks (CRN), and Localization Refinement Networks (LRN), and organize them into DETR with Dual Refinement Networks (DDRN). FPN- determines to what degree deep layers in FPN participate in the learning of shallow layers by reweighting during gradient back propagation, which addresses background misdetection in small object detection. CRN and LRN integrate FPN- features with predictions from advanced end-to-end detectors to further optimize classification and localization, respectively. Compared to several advanced autonomous driving object detectors, our method achieved an improvement of 1.4% and 1.1% in average precision (AP) on two public autonomous driving datasets, respectively. Extensive experiments have demonstrated the effectiveness, robustness, and applicability of DDRN.Keywords: Autonomous driving, Object detection, Refinement networks, Background misdetectionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Related work

Autonomous driving object detection techniques have evolved from universal object detection techniques. This section will first introduce the enlightening universal object detection techniques and small object detection that pose difficulties for autonomous driving perception, and finally focus on the recent autonomous driving object detectors.

Universal object detectors

Traditional object detectors based on deep learning can be divided into two categories: two-stage detectors11,12 and one-stage detectors13,14. Two-stage detectors typically involve two steps: first, generating region proposals from the image, and then refining these proposals to produce the final object bounding boxes15,16. Fast R-CNN17 first runs the image through a CNN to extract features and returns region of interest (ROI). It then applies pooling layers to the ROI to ensure that each region has the same size. Finally, the features of these regions are passed through a fully connected network and the results are returned using Softmax and linear regression. FPN18 proposed a top-down network architecture with lateral connections to build high-level semantic information across all layers with different scales. The introduction of FPN greatly promoted the improvement of detection network accuracy. One-stage object detection algorithms do not require a region proposal stage19,20. They directly generate the probability of object classes and position coordinates, thereby obtaining the final detection results in a single stage, resulting in faster detection speed. YOLOX14 proposed a unique decoupled head processing method and anchor-free design, which distinguishes it among the YOLO series algorithms21. Overall, two-stage detectors generally achieve higher accuracy than one-stage detectors. Although two-stage detectors perform well, they rely heavily on many manually designed components, such as non-maximum suppression (NMS) or anchor box generation.

The emergence of DETR22 aims to address the manual dependency problem in two-stage detectors and to achieve a more flexible end-to-end detector23. DETR treats object detection as a set prediction problem and introduces a one-to-one set matching method based on Transformer24. DINO25 adopts the advanced query formulation of DAB-DETR26 and combines it with improved contrastive denoising techniques to achieve excellent performance. Chen et al.10 proposed RefineBox, a module that uses a lightweight refinement network to optimize the localization performance of DETR-like detectors. DDQ27 first arranges dense queries similar to traditional detectors, then selects different queries for one-to-one mapping. DDQ integrates the advantages of traditional and end-to-end detectors, significantly improving the performance of detectors. CO-DETR28 adopts a collaborative hybrid assignment training architecture, the key of which is to use versatile one-to-many label assignment to improve the training efficiency and effectiveness of the encoder and decoder. Currently, CO-DETR is the state-of-the-art (SOTA) on COCO, with its highest AP reaching 66%. DETR-like models have significantly improved the performance of detectors, even outperforming classical convolutional models. For the foreseeable future, DETR-like models will remain a research hotspot in the field of object detection.

Small object detectors

The prosperity of universal object detectors cannot hide the slow development in the field of small object detection29,30. The inherent structural characteristics of small objects, coupled with their poor visual appearance and noisy representations, make small object detection an extremely challenging task. C3Det31 relates the full image context with annotator inputs in a local and global manner via late-fusion and feature-correlation, respectively. Gong et al.32 used fusion factors to control the information passed from deep layers to shallow layers, enabling FPN to adapt to the detection of small objects. Chen et al.33 pointed out that feature prediction heads of different scales can also remain effective in small object detection, using multi-scale predictions is more effective than single-scale predictions. Zhu et al.34 proposed a new Dynamic Sparse Vision Transformer, which achieves more flexible computation allocation through dual-layer connections and achieves very significant results on small objects, especially in dense prediction tasks. These methods improve small object detection from different perspectives, but do not consider the negative impact of complex scenes in road traffic.

Autonomous vehicle object detectors

MFR-CNN35 proposed a new convolutional neural network model for autonomous vehicle object detection by using multi-scale local and global feature representations. The model proposed by MFR-CNN consists of two parts: a region proposal network that generates candidate object regions, and an object detection network that integrates multi-scale features and global information. Zhang et al.36 divide the image into patches and select patches containing objects of different scales, this way is based on a transformer-based network and integrates diffusion models to improve the detection accuracy. DRMNet37 can be applied to autonomous driving scenarios, which can simultaneously complete the tasks of vehicle detection, lane detection, and drivable area detection. Firstly, the backbone of the model is designed as a two-pathway structure, which is used to extract shallow detail information and deep semantic information, respectively. Secondly, a multi-scale feature fusion module is designed to fuse the extracted shallow detail and deep semantic information. Then, different detection branches are designed according to the different characteristics of each detection task. CTAFFNet38 employs dual branches of CNN and Transformer to extract local and global features from images, which are then adaptively fused. Subsequently, a novel adaptive feature fusion strategy is designed to integrate local high-frequency features with global low-frequency features, thereby obtaining comprehensive feature representations. Finally, CTAFFNet adopts an encoder-decoder architecture to facilitate the propagation of fused local-global information across different stages, ensuring the model’s generalization capability. C2FDA39 proposed a novel category-induced coarse-to-fine domain adaptation approach, which consists of three pivotal components: (1) Attention-induced coarse-grained alignment module; (2) Attention-induced feature selection module and (3) Category-induced fine-grained alignment module. The aforementioned methods have improved the field of autonomous vehicle object detection from various perspectives and have achieved promising results. However, there is still a considerable gap between current autonomous vehicle detectors and the safe and efficient implementation of autonomous driving systems, leaving significant room for improvement.

Inspired by the enlightening universal detectors mentioned above and the recent autonomous driving object detectors, we propose DDRN, which consists of CO-DETR, FPN-, CRN, and LRN. CO-DETR is the state-of-the-art end-to-end universal detector; FPN- balances the feature learning of objects with different sizes by incorporating fusion factors, thus addressing the problem of background false positives in small object detection; CRN and LRN fuse pyramid features with the predictions of CO-DETR and further optimize classification and localization, respectively. Unlike existing one-stage and two-stage object detectors, DDRN does not adhere to a strict stage-wise architecture but shares similarities with two-stage detectors. It divides the detection into two stages: first, end-to-end detection, and then feature fusion and prediction. In the first stage, the end-to-end detection model acts as the region proposal network. In the second stage, the parameters of CO-DETR are frozen, and only the weights of FPN- and Dual Refinement Network are updated. DDRN addresses two key aspects: (1) In FPN, each layer must handle objects of varying scales (shallow layers focus on small objects, deep layers on large ones). However, autonomous driving scenarios predominantly feature small objects, necessitating a fusion factor to balance learning between shallow and deep layers. DDRN adaptively learns to control the degree to which deep layers reweight their contribution to shallow layer learning during backpropagation, thereby improving small object detection performance in traffic scenes; (2) For the two detection subtasks: classification and localization, DDRN proposes Dual Refinement Networks to specifically enhance performance. There is inconsistency between the well-trained model and the randomly initialized refinement network. Therefore, refinement networks only need to fit the output features of the frozen detector. DETR-like models are difficult to learn multiscale features due to the quadratic complexity of self-attention. DDRN integrates and learns multi-scale features with the predictions of CO-DETR, further increasing the potential for performance improvement of the autonomous vehicle object detector.

Method

Overview

The framework of the DDRN designed for autonomous driving scenarios is shown in Fig. 2. It consists of four components: the frozen CO-DETR, FPN-, CRN, and LRN. CRN and LRN are dedicated to improving the prediction classification and localization, respectively. We will provide a detailed description of the components and offer insights into why it can work well.

CRN and LRN fuse pyramid features with the predictions of CO-DETR and further optimize classification and localization, respectively.

Method details

The Dual Refinement Networks perform two main steps. First, the refinement networks use the feature pyramid network FPN- to extract features from the well-trained backbone of the CO-DETR detection model. Second, through a series of refinement modules, they effectively refine the classifications and localizations using multi-scale features predicted by the detector. During the training process, we freeze the parameters of the pre-trained object detector and only update the weights of FPN- and the Dual Refinement Networks.

Dual refinement network

Once DDRN obtains multi-scale features, DDRN applies m Classification Refinement Networks to improve class prediction and n Localization Refinement Networks to improve bounding box prediction. CRN and LRN have the same network structure, but they are distinguished by the purposes of classification prediction and localization prediction respectively. Dual Refinement Networks take the predictions from CO-DETR and the feature pyramid as input. DDRN corrects classes and bounding boxes by predicting the deltas between predictions and ground truth. Each refinement network consists of two ROI align layers40, two residual modules41 (as shown in Fig. 4), a gate unit that generates weights to select useful information, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The residual module of refinement networks consists of several bottleneck blocks. Each block stacks a 11 convolutional layer, a 33 convolutional layer, and a 11 convolutional layer. The bottleneck channels correspond to the input channels of the 33 convolutional layer. We use L1 loss and GIOU loss42 as regression losses, and Focal Loss43 as classification loss. Training and inference

During training, we select predictions that match the ground truth and improve the corresponding classification and localization. Other predictions that do not match the ground truth are ignored. For inference, we refine the top k predictions based on confidence scores. Since the ground truth is not accessible, the value of k is set to the maximum number of objects in a single image in the dataset, which is set to 100 in DDRN. The rationale for setting the value of k is provided in the ablation experiments.

Why DDRN works

Because of the fusion operations between adjacent layers, FPN is indeed multitask learning. If the top-down connections were removed from FPN, each layer would only need to focus on objects relevant to that layer, so that shallow layers learn small objects and deep layers learn large objects. In FPN, almost every layer must learn objects of all sizes. However, for autonomous vehicle object detection, a significant portion of the objects in the dataset are small objects. In addition, each layer not only needs to focus on objects of its corresponding size, but also needs help from other layers to obtain more training samples. Therefore, the fusion factor is needed to control the priority of these two requirements and achieve a balance between them. determines to what degree deep layers in FPN participate in the learning of shallow layers by reweighting during gradient back propagation. If there are fewer objects in the deep layers or more objects in the shallow layers, the deep layers cannot help the shallow layers learn. Recent DETR-like models generate a set of predictions and use a one-to-one mapping strategy to find positive samples for training. Reducing the error in positive samples can significantly improve the performance of DETR-like models10. For the two subtasks involved in the detection task: classification and localization, we propose Dual Refinement Networks each to improve the performance in a targeted manner. Results

Experiments settings

The experimental platform’s operating system is Ubuntu 18.04. CUDA 11.3 is used for GPU acceleration. For fair comparisons, all experiments are implemented on PyTorch 1.12 and MMDetection 3.3.044. ResNet-5041 is the default backbone in this study if not specified. Most models adopt the 1x (12 epochs) training protocol in MMDetection44. We consider CO-DETR28 as our baseline. All hyperparameters and the optimization algorithm follow the settings in MMDetection44 if not specifically noted.

Datasets

The Berkeley Deep Drive 100k (BDD100K)9 is the largest open driving video dataset with 100K videos to evaluate progress of image recognition algorithms in autonomous driving. The dataset has geographic, environmental, and weather diversity. The road object recognition dataset we use has a total of 100,000 photos with an original size of 1280 720, and the dataset is randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in the ratio of 6:2:2. The road object recognition dataset includes ten classes: car, bus, person, bike, truck, motor, train, rider, traffic sign, and traffic light.

The KITTI dataset51, co-founded by the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) and the Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago (TTIC), is one of the commonly used datasets for autonomous driving scenarios internationally. KITTI contains real-world image data collected from various scenes such as urban areas, rural areas, and highways. In this paper, KITTI is randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 6:2:2. The object detection dataset includes six categories: Car, Van, Truck, Pedestrian, Cyclist, and Tram.

Ablation experiments

To investigate the effects of different components in the DDRN and the influence of multiple parameters, we performed numerous ablation experiments on BDD100K, which has a large data sample and is therefore more representative.

Components

To validate the contribution of DDRN components to detection performance, this paper designs component ablation experiments and evaluates the average precision metric. As shown in Table 4, the experimental results demonstrate that introducing FPN- increases AP from 28.8 to 29.4% (AP = +0.6%), proving its effectiveness in mitigating background misclassification in small object detection through gradient reweighting mechanisms; when CRN or LRN is independently integrated with FPN-, AP reaches 29.9% (AP = +1.1%) and 29.7% (AP = +0.9%) respectively, validating the independent efficacy of classification refinement and localization refinement modules; simultaneous integration of CRN and LRN further improves AP to 30% (AP = +1.2%), revealing synergistic gains from dual-branch optimization. Notably, increasing CRN/LRN stacking layers from 1 to 2 sustains AP improvement to 30.1% (AP = +1.3%), but performance regresses at 4 layers (AP = +1.0%), revealing nonlinear relationships between network depth and detection performance. This experiment comprehensively demonstrates FPN-’s feature enhancement capabilities and CRN/LRN’s fine-grained optimization for detection tasks, ultimately adopting the optimal configuration of 1-layer CRN and 2-layer LRN (AP = +1.4%) as the critical support for DDRN’s overall performance advancement.

Fusion factors

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed FPN- module, we conduct an ablation study on the BDD100K dataset to analyze the impact of different values. As shown in Table 5, when using the default setting ( = 1), the baseline AP is 29.3. Adjusting to values such as 1.25 and 0.75 leads to a decrease in performance, with AP dropping to 28.6 and 29, respectively. However, when is set to 0.5 and 0.25, the AP increases to 29.7 and 29.4, indicating that reducing the fusion factor can significantly improve detection performance on this dataset. To evaluate the efficiency and applicability of DDRN in autonomous vehicle object detection, we compared the results of different models in complex scenarios, as shown in Fig. 6. Compared to the ground truth in Fig. 6a, Dynamic R-CNN, CO-DETR, DiffusionDet, and DDQ misidentified a reflection of a car’s brake light as a traffic light; YOLOX missed a right turn sign; ViTDet misidentified an extra car; DINO misidentified both a traffic light and an extra car. Consistent with Fig. 5, DDRN optimized the detection performance of small objects in the background region. DDRN achieved detection of all five objects with high confidence scores of 99.9%, 99.6%, 92.5%, 90.7%, and 78.5%. We attribute this to DDRN’s use of FPN- to emphasize balanced learning between objects with different sizes, and the further enhancement of classification and localization effects using CRN and LRN.

Beyond the results shown in Fig. 6, the consistent state-of-the-art performance on the BDD100K (Table 1) and KITTI (Table 2) datasets further demonstrates DDRN’s strong generalization ability. Notably, DDRN achieves the highest small object detection performance () on both benchmarks: 14.6 on BDD100K (outperforming CO-DETR by 0.5) and 62.6 on KITTI (surpassing CO-DETR by 7.8). This robustness stems from two synergistic innovations: (1) The adaptive FPN fusion factor alone contributes 0.6 AP in ablation studies (Table 4), dynamically balancing scale-specific feature learning to handle diverse object scales; (2) The dual refinement network jointly optimizes classification and localization, delivering a total AP improvement of 1.4 when combined with . This architecture ensures consistent detection accuracy under varying environmental conditions and domain shifts, which is critical for real-world deployment where adverse weather, occlusions, and scale variations are inevitable.

Limitations of the study

This paper improves the performance of autonomous driving object detectors by addressing small object detection and environmental diversity. However, for those large objects that can be easily detected, the performance improvement of DDRN is quite limited and needs to be further improved in the future. Scenario mining from extensive autonomous driving datasets is crucial for the validation of self-driving systems53; therefore, the robustness of DDRN requires further evaluation across a broader set of datasets. Additionally, the modules added in this paper increase computational complexity, inevitably reducing inference speed, which is another direction for future improvement. Several interesting directions not discussed in this paper can be further explored. For example, the fusion factor can balance the degree to which deep layers contribute to shallow layer learning, which helps in learning objects with different sizes. Dual Refinement Networks further increase the potential for improving the performance of the detector. These features can be applied to other tasks, such as semantic segmentation, to improve the model’s performance on objects with different sizes.

Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the challenges faced by current autonomous vehicle object detectors: the difficulty of detecting small objects and the complexity and variability of traffic environments. To address these challenges, we proposed three components: FPN-, CRN, and LRN, and organized them into DDRN accordingly. FPN- balances feature learning from objects with different sizes through fusion factors, addressing the problem of background false positives in small object detection. CRN and LRN fuse FPN- features with predictions from state-of-the-art end-to-end detectors, further optimizing classification and localization, respectively. DDRN outperforms several advanced detectors on two public autonomous driving datasets, and extensive experiments demonstrate the efficiency, robustness, and applicability of our approach. In the future, we will extend DDRN to multimodal fusion technology to further enhance detection robustness; we will also apply DDRN to additional driving scenarios, such as those in the Argoverse 2 dataset54, which includes high-definition maps and geometric representations of objects, to explore potential improvements; additionally, improving DDRN through knowledge distillation techniques or lightweight design is a promising direction worth exploring.
