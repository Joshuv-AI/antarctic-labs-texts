Platelets induce endothelial cell mitochondrial dysfunction in myocardial infarction
Authors: Haoyu Sun, Florencia Schlamp, Matthew Muller, Yuhe Xia, Sarah Liberow, Nathaniel R. Smilowitz, Judith S. Hochman, Harmony R. Reynolds, Joshua A. Beckman, Tessa J. Barrett, Jeffrey S. Berger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coronary endothelial dysfunction plays a key role in the pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes. During myocardial infarction (MI), activated platelets release prothrombotic and proinflammatory factors, contributing to vascular injury and dysfunction. To investigate platelet-mediated endothelial dysfunction, endothelial cells (ECs) were treated with platelet-released factors from patients with MI and non-MI controls undergoing coronary angiography. RNA sequencing revealed that MI platelets induced EC mitochondrial dysfunction, confirmed by reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and disrupted mitochondrial networks. Integrating platelet transcriptomic data, we identified the C-C motif chemokine ligand 3 (CCL3) as significantly up-regulated in MI platelets and a key mediator of EC mitochondrial dysfunction. Blocking its receptor, CCR5, attenuated CCL3 effects. In an independent cohort of 261 patients with established cardiovascular disease, higher circulating CCL3 levels were associated with incident major adverse cardiovascular events. Together, these findings establish a mechanistic link between platelet activation and coronary endothelial dysfunction in MI.

INTRODUCTION

Myocardial infarction (MI), characterized by irreversible myocardial necrosis due to the interruption of blood flow in coronary arteries (1), remains the leading cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD) death worldwide (2). In the United States, nearly one million MIs occur annually, with incidence rates projected to rise (2). The most common cause of MI is the rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque (3). While advances in medical treatment and procedures, including primary percutaneous coronary intervention, have substantially reduced morbidity and mortality (4, 5), substantial unmet needs remain to prevent recurrent cardiovascular events and improve long-term outcomes (3, 6, 7).

The vascular endothelium, which serves as a dynamic interface between circulating blood and tissues, is vital in maintaining antithrombotic and anti-inflammatory homeostasis. Endothelial dysfunction, characterized by impaired vasodilation, increased permeability, and proinflammatory activation, contributes substantially to the initiation and progression of atherothrombosis. Coronary endothelial dysfunction has been identified as an independent predictor of acute cardiovascular events and is associated with poor patient outcomes (8–10). In MI, ischemia/reperfusion injury exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, contributing to impaired vasomotion, the no-reflow phenomenon, and increased myocardial damage (11, 12). Elucidating the mechanisms underlying coronary endothelial dysfunction is critical to improving MI prevention and treatment.

Platelets play a central role in atherothrombosis and tissue inflammation during MI. Platelets drive uncontrolled thrombosis that triggers acute myocardial ischemia and contributes to both cardiac and vascular dysfunction (13, 14). Furthermore, activated platelets release thrombotic and inflammatory mediators to the microenvironment, directly amplifying vascular inflammation and endothelial injury (15–18). However, the mechanisms linking platelets to coronary endothelial dysfunction remain incompletely understood.

Previous studies from our group have demonstrated that platelets act as potent effector cells in endothelial regulation. Platelets from patients with vascular dysfunction-associated diseases, including psoriasis, COVID-19, systemic lupus erythematosus, and HIV, induce inflammatory and procoagulant responses in endothelial cells (ECs). These disorders have been shown to drive platelet-mediated up-regulation of inflammatory markers such as interleukin-8, interleukin-1β, cyclooxygenase-2, and myeloid-related protein-8/14, which contribute to endothelial dysfunction and hypercoagulability (19–22). Given the pivotal role of platelet-endothelial interactions, this study aimed to investigate the effect of platelets on EC function in patients with MI.

RESULTS

Platelets from patients with MI induce EC mitochondrial dysfunction

To investigate the contribution of platelets to endothelial dysfunction during MI, human coronary artery ECs were treated with platelet releasate from patients with MI or control patients without MI, followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Fig. 1A, demographics in Table 1). Expression profiling identified 1831 differentially expressed genes in ECs treated with MI platelet releasate compared to controls (P < 0.05, 1025 up-regulated and 806 down-regulated; Fig. 1, B and C). Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) of differentially expressed transcripts revealed multiple EC canonical pathways enriched following MI platelet releasate treatment (fig. S1). Among these, mitochondrial dysfunction was the most significantly up-regulated (P = 1.7 × 10−8; Fig. 1D), with 58 genes significantly differentially expressed (Fig. 1E). Consistent with altered mitochondrial function, oxidative phosphorylation was the most down-regulated pathway (P = 7.2 × 10−8) following MI platelet releasate exposure (Fig. 1D).

To assess the functional consequence of the EC transcriptomic signature, EC mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed. Real-time live-cell imaging using MitoTracker Red found significantly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential in ECs treated with MI platelet releasate relative to controls (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2, A to C). Furthermore, ECs treated with MI-derived releasates exhibited significant mitochondria depolarization as assessed by EC 5,5,6,6′-tetrachloro- 1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimi-dazoylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) monomer/aggregate ratio (P = 0.0424; Fig. 2, D and E) and JC-1 aggregate intensity (P = 0.0021; Fig. 2F) consistent with MI-mediated EC mitochondrial dysfunction (23). Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP)-treated ECs served as a positive control of JC-1 staining shown in fig. S2.

Mitochondrial network morphology, a key indicator of mitochondrial function and cellular stress response, was quantified by measuring mitochondrial branch length and network area (Fig. 2, G and H) (24, 25). ECs treated with MI platelet releasate showed a significant reduction in mitochondrial network size (Fig. 2I) and increased mitochondrial fragmentation (Fig. 2J). These data demonstrate that platelet-derived factors released during MI contribute to EC mitochondrial dysfunction.

Besides the effects on mitochondrial function, we also evaluated the impact of platelet releasate on EC phenotype. MI platelet releasate induced significantly higher secretion of intercellular adhesion molecule–2 (ICAM-2) compared with control releasate (fig. S3A), indicating the MI releasate promotes EC activation and a shift toward a proinflammatory phenotype.

Platelet gene clusters correlate with EC mitochondrial dysfunction

To gain mechanistic insight into MI platelet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs, we analyzed platelet transcriptome data from patients with MI and controls within the cohort, identifying 1421 genes differentially expressed in MI platelets compared to controls (P < 0.05) (26). We then assessed the association between these platelet genes and the 58 differentially expressed mitochondrial genes identified in the EC transcriptome (Fig. 1E). Hierarchical clustering revealed three distinct platelet gene clusters (Fig. 3A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the platelet genes found that cluster 1 (482 platelet genes) was enriched in pathways associated with hemostasis, coagulation, actin filament organization, and platelet activation (Fig. 3B). Cluster 2 (376 genes) was enriched in transcripts associated with RNA and protein processing pathways (fig. S4A), and cluster 3 (563 genes) was linked to RNA catabolism and antigen processing and presentation (fig. S4B). These findings suggest that the alterations in the platelet transcriptome, particularly in platelet activation and aggregation-related pathways, are associated with EC mitochondrial dysfunction.

Platelet CCL3 is a driver of EC mitochondrial dysfunction during MI

To identify the platelet-derived factor contributing to EC mitochondrial dysfunction, we investigated differentially expressed genes encoding secreted proteins. In the MI-platelet transcriptome (26), we evaluated the top 50 candidates by cross-referencing the candidate list to the Protein Atlas secretome database (27). Five candidates coding for secreted proteins were identified: CCL3, LOXL3, MTRNR2L8, MTRNR2L10, and HPSE, with CCL3 as the most significantly up-regulated gene in the MI platelets compared to that in controls (P = 0.008; Fig. 3C). Consistent with the transcriptome, platelet releasate C-C motif chemokine ligand 3 (CCL3) levels were significantly higher in patients with MI (P = 0.0022; Fig. 3D), with platelet CCL3 and releasate CCL3 concentrations positively associated [correlation coefficient (r) = 0.7465, P = 0.0074; Fig. 3E]. In addition, circulating CCL3 levels were elevated in patients with MI (fig. S5) and associated with platelet CCL3 mRNA and releasate protein levels (r = 0.712, P = 0.039; and r = 0.638, P = 0.05, respectively; n = 5 in each group; Spearman correlation).

To investigate whether platelet-derived CCL3 contributes to EC mitochondrial dysfunction, we assessed the relationship between platelet CCL3 and the expression of EC mitochondrial genes (Fig. 1E). Both platelet CCL3 mRNA level and releasate CCL3 levels were significantly associated with up- and down-regulated mitochondrial genes (Fig. 3F), with consistent correlation directions matching the observed EC transcriptomic changes (fig. S6), suggesting that CCL3 plays a regulatory role in EC mitochondrial dysfunction. To further investigate this, ECs were treated with CCL3 (100 ng/ml), and transcriptomic changes were compared to those mediated by MI-derived platelet releasate. A significant overlap was observed in differentially regulated genes between ECs treated with CCL3 and ECs treated with MI-derived platelet releasate (r = 0.5217, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4A). Functionally, CCL3 exposure recapitulated MI platelet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to EC mitochondrial membrane depolarization, evidenced by a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4, B and C) and increased JC-1 monomer/aggregate ratio (P = 0.0491; Fig. 4, E and F). In addition to CCL3, it is possible that other platelet-derived chemokines (e.g., CCL4 or CCL5) or cofactors that are not differentially expressed act synergistically. We compared endothelial responses to non-MI platelet releasate alone versus releasate supplemented with exogenous CCL3. CCL3 exerted an additive effect, as ECs treated with CCL3 and control releasate exhibited greater mitochondrial membrane potential reduction than with control releasate alone (fig. S7). These findings indicate that CCL3 acts in an additive manner with other components in the platelet secretome. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that platelet-derived CCL3 is a critical contributor to mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs.

Next, we examined how CCL3-induced mitochondrial dysfunction affects EC function. CCL3 treatment significantly increased both general reactive oxygen species (ROS; measured by CM-H2DCFDA) and mitochondrial superoxide (measured by MitoSox) levels in ECs (fig. S8), consistent with oxidative stress downstream of mitochondrial injury. Furthermore, similar to MI releasate, CCL3 increased the secretion of ICAM-1, ICAM-2, and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule–1 (PECAM-1) from ECs (fig. S3, B to D). Together, these findings suggest that the CCL3-driven mitochondrial dysfunction is not an isolated event but is functionally linked to oxidative stress, endothelial activation, and inflammatory priming, which are key processes contributing to the vascular dysfunction during MI.

MI platelets induce EC mitochondrial dysfunction via the CCL3/CCR5 axis

CCR1 and CCR5 are the primary CCL3 EC receptors (Fig. 5A) (28, 29). To determine whether blocking CCL3 mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction, ECs were pretreated with CCR1 and CCR5 neutralizing antibodies before CCL3 exposure. As noted above, CCL3 induced EC mitochondrial membrane depolarization (Fig. 5, B to G). Blocking CCR5 or both CCR5 and CCR1 impaired CCL3-induced mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 5, B to G), whereas CCR1 inhibition alone had a less pronounced effect. To extend our findings, we examined EC mitochondrial morphology. Treatment with CCL3 led to a significant reduction in mitochondrial network size, as indicated by decreased mitochondrial network area (Fig. 5I), total branch length (fig. S9A) and perimeter (fig. S9B). Additionally, CCL3 induced the increased fragmentation of mitochondria, reflected by reduced branch length per mitochondrion (Fig. 5K), mean branch length (fig. S9C), and branch counts per mitochondrion (fig. S9D). Blocking CCR5 with neutralizing antibodies alleviated these CCL3-induced effects, whereas CCR1 inhibition alone did not and dual inhibition of CCR1 and CCR5 did not confer added benefit (Fig. 5, J and L; and fig. S9, A to D).

Live-cell imaging and JC-1 assay data indicate that CCR5 inhibition, but not CCR1 blockade, significantly restores CCL3-induced reductions of mitochondrial membrane potential in ECs. Therefore, we selected the CCR5 neutralizing antibody to further investigate the effect of blocking the CCL3 receptor after treatment of ECs with platelet releasate isolated from patients with MI and controls. As expected, MI platelet releasate reduced EC mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas CCR5 neutralizing antibody restored the mitochondrial membrane potential in MI platelet releasate–treated ECs, with no effect on the control-treated cells (Fig. 6, A and B; representative flow plots are shown in fig. To gain insight into the molecular mechanisms by which CCL3 induces mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs, we examined the expression of key regulators of mitochondrial dynamics and oxidative stress. CCL3 significantly increased the EC DNM1L (encoding dynamin-related protein 1 [DRP1]) and FIS1 expression, key mediators of mitochondrial fission, as well as NOX4, a mitochondrial-associated reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase (fig. S11, A to C). These effects were attenuated by CCR5 blockade, indicating that CCL3 modulates mitochondrial dysfunction primarily through CCR5-dependent activation of mitochondrial fission and oxidative stress pathways.

CCL3 correlates with cardiovascular events

To evaluate the clinical relevance of our findings, we investigated the association between circulating CCL3 and clinical outcomes in an independent cohort of female and male patients with established CVD (n = 261) and CVD-free female and male controls with multiple cardiovascular risk factors (n = 56; table S1). CCL3 levels were significantly increased in patients with CVD relative to the controls (P = 0.000094; Fig. 7A). This difference remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index, diabetes, and hypertension (adjusted P = 0.009). The patients with CVD (n = 261) were followed for adverse cardiac events for a median of 18 months. Among these patients, CCL3 levels were significantly higher in patients who experienced a major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE; defined as a composite of death, MI, and stroke) compared to those who did not (P = 0.0012, adjusted P = 0.0005; Fig. 7B). The cumulative incidence of MACE differed according to the median level of CCL3 (Fig. 7C). Patients with high CCL3 levels were more likely to have diabetes, coronary artery disease, or critical limb ischemia (characteristics are shown in table S2). After adjustment for age, sex, race, ethnicity, diabetes, coronary artery disease, prior stroke, and critical limb ischemia, a CCL3 value above the median was associated with a 2.4-fold increase in MACE (adjusted hazard ratio of 2.37, 95% confidence interval of 1.37 to 4.11, P = 0.002). The association between CCL3 and adverse events was consistent for the composite of death and MI and the individual endpoints of death and MI (Fig. 7D and fig. S12). Together, our findings demonstrate that platelet-derived CCL3 is increased in MI, significantly contributes to platelet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, and can be attenuated by blocking CCR5. Elevated circulating CCL3 is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events (Fig. 8).

DISCUSSION

During an MI, activated platelets adhere to the vascular wall, initiating thrombosis, EC activation, and inflammation, which collectively impair vascular function. Previous studies from our laboratory have demonstrated that activated platelets regulate endothelial activation and contribute to vascular dysfunction (19–22). In this study, we investigated the impact of platelets on EC transcriptional signatures to gain deeper insights into platelet-endothelium interactions in the setting of MI.

Our findings support a model in which platelets from patients with MI release CCL3, disrupting endothelial mitochondrial homeostasis. Specifically, exposure to MI platelets drives EC transcriptomic reprogramming, with up-regulated mitochondrial dysfunction and down-regulated oxidative phosphorylation pathways, accompanied by loss of membrane potential and mitochondrial fragmentation. Through platelet transcriptome analysis from patients with MI, we identify CCL3 as the top candidate associated with EC mitochondrial dysfunction. CCL3 emerges as a key mediator. In addition to its established chemotactic role (30), CCL3 induces E-selectin expression (31), increases microvascular permeability (32), and promotes cell apoptosis during ischemia/reperfusion injury (33) and cancer (34), processes that intersect with mitochondrial dysfunction. While leukocytes are a recognized source of CCL3, we confirmed the absence of leukocyte contamination (26), and prior ultrastructural studies show that platelets store and release CCL3 upon activation (35, 36). Together, these findings suggest a platelet-CCL3-endothelial axis in MI, wherein activated platelets release CCL3, contributing to EC mitochondrial dysfunction and amplification of vascular injury.

Our data reveal a significant association between platelet CCL3 mRNA expression and platelet releasate CCL3 levels with altered mitochondrial gene expression in ECs. Moreover, CCL3-treated ECs exhibited transcriptomic changes in mitochondrial genes and mitochondrial depolarization and fragmentation that closely mirrored changes induced by MI platelets. These findings identify CCL3 as a key contributor to the platelet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs during MI and uncover a previously unrecognized function of platelet-derived CCL3 in regulating endothelial mitochondrial function.

CCR1 and CCR5 are the primary receptors of CCL3 (30). Our data indicate that blocking CCR5 provides greater efficacy than CCR1 inhibition in rescuing the CCL3-induced reduction of mitochondrial membrane potential and disturbance of the mitochondrial network. Moreover, the dual inhibition of both receptors did not provide additional benefits beyond CCR5 inhibition alone. Mechanistically, our data identify a CCL3-CCR5 signaling axis that disrupts endothelial mitochondrial integrity by up-regulating the fission mediators DRP1 and fission, mitochondrial 1 (FIS1) and activating NOX4-dependent ROS production, linking platelet-derived CCL3 to oxidative stress-driven endothelial dysfunction in MI. We also evaluated additional regulators of mitochondrial homeostasis and redox balance, including NRF2-related genes (NFE2L2 and HMOX1) and SIRT1, but observed no significant changes (fig. S11, D to F). The protective effects of CCR5 blockade were further validated in MI platelet-treated ECs.

While the CCL3/CCR1 axis has been implicated in mediating inflammation (37, 38) and promoting tumor metastasis (39–41), emerging evidence suggests that the CCL3/CCR5 axis extends beyond immune regulation to influence mitochondrial function. CCL3 has been shown to regulate inducible nitric oxide (NO) synthase and cyclooxygenase 2 expression via CCR5 (42). Additionally, CCR5 has been implicated in white blood cell death promoted by α-fetoprotein during mitochondrial dysfunction (43). Notably, CCR5 expression, but not CCR1, is up-regulated during vascular injury, and CCR5 blockade attenuates NADPH oxidase-1–induced vascular inflammation and remodeling (44). The CCL3/CCR5 axis can activate the AMP-activated protein kinase, p38/mitogen-activated protein kinase, and nuclear factor κB signaling pathways (36, 42, 45), which regulate mitochondrial protein activity, mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial responses to oxidative stress and apoptosis (46–48). These pathways may serve as downstream mediators of the CCL3/CCR5 axis in regulating mitochondrial function in ECs. Our data suggest that CCR5 functions as a central integrator of multiple platelet-derived signals, with CCL3 acting as a key amplifier. The complete rescue of MI-induced mitochondrial dysfunction by CCR5 blockade, despite the likely presence of additional ligands such as CCL4 or CCL5, supports a model of convergent, synergistic signaling. The additive effect of exogenous CCL3 on control platelet releasate further underscores its capacity to potentiate other secretome components. These findings highlight CCR5-mediated synergy as a potential therapeutic target in platelet-driven endothelial injury. Together, these findings underscore the role of the CCL3/CCR5 axis in regulating mitochondrial function and indicate the therapeutic potential of CCR5 blockade in CVD.

Mitochondria are the primary source of ROS and play a key role in regulating NO signaling in ECs (49). Excess ROS production, along with diminished NO and endothelial NO synthase activity, is the critical factor underlying impaired endothelial function (50). Disturbed mitochondrial fission and fusion, as well as impaired energy metabolism, substantially contribute to endothelial dysfunction (51). Our functional assays demonstrate that CCL3-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs is linked to oxidative stress and endothelial activation, processes central to MI progression. CCL3 treatment increased general ROS and mitochondrial superoxide levels, consistent with oxidative stress downstream of mitochondrial injury. MI platelet releasate elevated ICAM-2 secretion, while CCL3 alone up-regulated ICAM-1, ICAM-2, and PECAM-1, indicating a proinflammatory, proadhesive phenotype that could facilitate leukocyte recruitment. These findings extend prior associations between CCL3 and CVD by identifying a platelet-driven, CCR5-dependent mitochondrial injury pathway in ECs, providing mechanistic insight into how MI-associated platelets promote vascular inflammation and dysfunction. Previous studies have shown elevated CCL3 levels in atherosclerotic arteries (52) and in patients with MI (53), as well as its correlation with risk of future acute coronary syndromes and short-term mortality (53, 54). Consistent with these findings, our data from an independent cohort of patients with CVDs reveal a strong association between circulating CCL3 levels and the risk of cardiovascular events. With the increase of CCL3, the risk of cardiovascular events significantly increased, supporting the potential of CCL3 as a biomarker for predicting clinical outcomes in patients with CVD.

Our research has several limitations. First, the study was conducted using samples from a cohort of female patients with MI, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to males. However, we validated the clinical relevance of our finding by demonstrating the association between circulating CCL3 and cardiovascular events in both male and female patients. Second, certain protein-level analyses were performed on a reduced subset of samples, selected on the basis of sufficient, high-quality platelet releasate, which may limit the generalizability of these specific findings. Third, aspirin use, while balanced between groups, was not formally adjusted for in our analysis. Given aspirin’s capacity to modulate platelet activation and mediator release, including chemokines such as CCL3, we cannot exclude the possibility that it contributed to variability in CCL3 levels or endothelial mitochondrial responses. Fourth, our investigation primarily focused on the effects of platelet-derived CCL3 in vitro using cultured ECs. Our EC–platelet releasate coculture system does not fully recapitulate the in vivo endothelial environment; future studies using minimally invasive approaches such as EC wire biopsy (55) or circulating EC isolation could validate these transcriptomic findings in patient-derived cells and enhance their translational relevance. In vivo studies and genetically modified animal models, such as CCL3 or CCR5 knockout mice, would also provide more comprehensive insights and stronger evidence. Last, while we focused on CCL3, other platelet-derived factors may also contribute to the effect of MI platelets on ECs. An additional consideration is that platelet-derived mitochondrial components, such as free mitochondria or extracellular vesicles containing mitochondrial cargo, may persist in platelet releasate despite the absence of intact platelets (56). Thus, while our data implicate CCL3 as a key mediator, we cannot exclude contributions from platelet-derived mitochondrial cargo, which may represent an additional pathway linking platelet activation to endothelial injury.

In conclusion, our study reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism by which platelet-derived CCL3 contributes to endothelial dysfunction in MI by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction in ECs. Elevated platelet CCL3 levels were strongly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and disturbed mitochondrial network in ECs. Blocking CCR5 restored mitochondrial function and morphology, highlighting its therapeutic potential. Clinically, circulating CCL3 levels were independently associated with increased risk of MACE, suggesting its value as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. These findings provide important insights into the role of platelet-derived factors in MI (Fig. 8).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort

Female patients aged more than 21 years old presenting with MI were recruited for the Women’s Heart Attack Research Program (platelet substudy: NCT03022552). Exclusion criteria included thrombocytopenia (platelet count of <100,000 platelets/ml), thrombocytosis (platelet count of >500,000 platelets/ml), anemia (hemoglobin of <9 mg/dl), use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs other than aspirin, inability to provide consent, cognitive impairment, or psychosis. Patients referred for coronary angiogram were classified into the MI group if they met the fourth universal definition of MI (57), while patients referred for coronary angiogram without MI were included as controls (26).

Plasma samples and clinical outcomes were collected from independent cohorts: a substudy of The Impact of Diabetes on rEvascularization (TIDE; NCT03085524) and the Platelet Activity in Vascular Surgery and Cardiovascular Events (PACE; NCT02106429) cohort. The circulating protein levels were measured by Olink proteomics. These two cohorts have been described previously (58, 59). For the TIDE cohort, male and female patients aged more than 18 years old with cardiovascular risk factors without CVD were recruited from 43 sites within the United States and represented controls. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained at each site. For the PACE cohort, male and female patients more than 21 years old with CVD were recruited from New York University Langone Health. As described previously, patients with CVD undergoing lower extremity revascularization were enrolled and followed for a median of 18 months. Plasma samples were collected prior to revascularization and stored in a −80°C freezer. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained for each participant. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of New York University Grossman School of Medicine, and the IRB approval number is i16-01104.

Generation of platelet releasate

From the cohort used for platelet RNA-seq in our previous study (26) (38 control subjects and 40 patients with MI), we obtained platelet releasate from a subset comprising 22 control subjects and 24 patients with MI. Blood samples were collected into tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate as an anticoagulant. After resting for 15 min, the samples were centrifuged at 200g for 10 min to isolate platelet-rich plasma (PRP). PRP added with acid citrate dextrose solution (v/v, 1:10; trisodium dihydrate citrate, 25 g/liter; citric acid monohydrate, 14 g/liter; and glucose, 20 g/liter) was centrifuged at 1000g for 10 min following 10 min rest. The platelet pellet was washed in Tyrode’s buffer with 1 μM prostaglandin E1, then resuspended at a concentration of 500,000/μl, and incubated for 30 min. The supernatant (platelet releasate) containing platelet-derived factor was collected by centrifuge platelets at 1000g for 3 min and stored at −80°C.

Human coronary artery EC culture and treatments

Primary ECs (Lonza, CC-2585) from a female donor were cultured in Microvascular Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2 MV medium) (Lonza, CC-3202) in 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. Cultured ECs between passages 5 and 8 were used. ECs were cultured in medium containing platelet releasate (v/v, 1:10) from patients with or without MI, human CCL3 (100 ng/ml; 270-LD-050, R&D Systems), mouse immunoglobulin G2b isotype control (20 μg/ml; BE0086, BioXCell), antihuman CCR1 antibody (20 μg/ml; MAB145-100, R&D Systems), and/or antihuman CCR5 antibody (20 μg/ml; MAB182-100, R&D Systems) at 37°C. For RNA-seq analysis, ECs were treated with platelet releasate for 6 hours. For all other functional assays, ECs were treated for 24 hours. The selection of CCL3 and CCR1/CCR5 neutralizing antibodies concentration was based on prior literature (summarized in tables S3 to S5) and tests with different doses (fig. S13).

RNA sequencing

After treatment, ECs were collected and lysed in QIAzol lysis reagent (QIAGEN), and total RNA was isolated with a Direct-zol RNA Microprep kit (R2062, Zymo Research). Following quality and quantity determination by Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies), same amount of RNA from each sample was used for library preparation using the Clontech SMART-Seq HT with Nxt HT kit (Takara Bio USA) and sequenced paired end on an Illumina NoVASeq 6000.

RNA-seq analysis

Quality control (QC) was assessed using FastQC v0.11.7 (60) and MultiQC v1.9 (61). Reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg38) using STAR v2.6.1d (62), and genomic features were assigned using Subread featureCounts v1.6.3.

All downstream analysis was performed in R v3.6.1. Normalization and differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v1.24 (63). Multiple hypothesis correction was performed using the default Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes annotated as pseudogenes were removed from downstream analyses and data visualization. GO term enrichment analysis was performed with the enrichGO function implemented in Bioconductor R package clusterProfiler v4.2.2 (64) using Bioconductor R package org.Hs.eg.db v3.14.0 and default parameters. IPA was performed using QIAGEN IPA software. A core analysis was created to identify the top canonical pathways by uploading the list of differentially expressed genes with base mean, log2 (fold change), P value, and P-adjusted value, with a cutoff of P value of <0.05.

Correlation of gene expression was done using the cor.test R function using Spearman correlation on log-transformed normalized counts. Hierarchical clustering on heatmaps were done on z-score–scaled log-transformed normalized counts using default Pearson correlation with R package pheatmap v1.0.12, and all other plotting was done using ggplot2 v3.5.1.

Mitochondrial membrane potential evaluation

Incucyte

ECs were seeded and cultured in 96-well plates for experiments. ECs were stained with MitoTracker Red FM (200 nM; M22425, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images of the cells and the mean fluorescent intensity in the red channel were recorded every 1 hour during the 24-hour incubation at 37°C by an Incucyte SX1 Live-Cell analysis system (Sartorius).

JC-1 assay

ECs were seeded and cultured in 24-well plates for experiments. After treatments, ECs were washed with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) once and incubated in serum-free EGM-2 MV medium containing 2 μM JC-1 dye (MitoProbe JC-1 Assay Kit; M34152, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C for 15 min. After washing with HBSS, ECs were collected and analyzed by a MACSQuant Analyzer 16 flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec). The JC-1 monomer/aggregate ratio was calculated on the basis of the mean fluorescence intensity (23).

Translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20) staining and mitochondrial network analysis

ECs were seeded and cultured in eight-well Nunc Lab-Tek II Chamber slides (154534PK, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for experiments. After treatments, ECs were washed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4°C for 15 min. ECs were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS at room temperature for 30 min. After blocking with 10% goat serum for 1 hour, the cells were incubated with Rabbit antihuman TOMM20 antibody [1:200 diluted in bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS (5 mg/ml); 42406, Cell Signaling Scientific] at 4°C overnight. The next day, cells were washed with PBS three times and incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody AF568 [1:2000 diluted in BSA/PBS (5 mg/ml); A11011, Invitrogen] at room temperature for 1 hour in the dark. After being stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for the nucleus, the slides were mounted. Images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800, Keyence). Ten to 15 fields of each well were randomly selected. The images were processed, and the mitochondrial network analysis of the ECs was performed using the Mitochondrial Analyzer plugins (24) of ImageJ software.

General and mitochondrial ROS measurements

ECs were seeded and cultured in 24-well plates for experiments. After treatments, ECs were washed with HBSS once and incubated in serum-free EGM-2 MV medium containing 2 μM CM-H2DCFDA dye (C6827, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or 1 μM MitoSOX Green (M36006, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C for 15 min. After washing with HBSS, ECs were collected and analyzed by a MACSQuant Analyzer 16 flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec).

CCL3 and EC activation markers measurements

Plasma was collected by centrifuge citrate blood sample at 2500g for 10 min. The concentration of CCL3 in plasma and platelet releasate samples was detected by LEGENDplex Human Proinflammatory Chemokine assay (B326082, BioLegend). The supernatant from ECs was collected after treatment, and EC activation markers were measured using a custom LEGENDplex assay panel (BioLegend).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

cDNA was synthesized from total RNA of treated ECs using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (1708841, Bio-Rad). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed with Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (4385612, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). mRNA levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase as the internal control. The following primer sequences were used: DNM1L, 5′-TTTGACACTTGTGGATTTGCCA-3′ (forward) and 5′-AGTGACAGCGAGGATAATGGA-3′ (reverse); FIS1, 5′-GATGACATCCGTAAAGGCATCG-3′ (forward) and 5′-AGAAGACGTAATCCCGCTGTT-3′ (reverse); and NOX4, 5′-CAGATGTTGGGGCTAGGATTG-3′ (forward) and 5′-GAGTGTTCGGCACATGGGTA-3′ (reverse).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were conducted in at least three independent biological replicates. All values were expressed as means ± SEM. An unpaired Student’s t test was used for comparison between two groups. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test or two-way ANOVA test was used to compare multiple groups. Spearman rank correlation was used for correlation analysis. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad software). Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were determined using log-rank tests. Cox proportional regression was used to estimate the hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval after the population was divided into groups on the basis of the median cut point for CCL3. Adjusted hazard estimates were obtained from regression models that adjusted for demographics and clinical risk factors including age, sex, race/ethnicity, diabetes, coronary artery disease, prior stroke, and critical limb ischemia.
