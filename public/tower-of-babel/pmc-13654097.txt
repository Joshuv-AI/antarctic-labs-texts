Test-bolus peak attenuation for predicting aortic enhancement in coronary computed tomography angiography: an exploratory subgroup analysis
Authors: Yi Ding, Shizhen Zhu, Pengfei Xia, Jun Cheng, Bin Song, Yuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdequate intravascular enhancement is essential for accurate coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) evaluation; however, conventional empirical contrast protocols often result in significant variability in enhancement because they do not account for individual physiological differences. Test-bolus techniques represent a potential approach for individualized contrast delivery; however, their precise quantitative relationship with final vascular enhancement requires further clarification. This study aimed to investigate the quantitative association between test-bolus peak attenuation and final aortic enhancement (FAE) in CCTA and to describe subgroup variations based on body mass index (BMI) and cardiac output (CO).MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 50 patients who underwent CCTA. Test-bolus peak attenuation and FAE were systematically recorded. To evaluate the quantitative association between test-bolus peak attenuation and FAE, univariate linear regression analysis was performed, followed by subgroup analyses stratified by BMI (normal, overweight, or obese) and CO (normal or high).ResultsTest-bolus peak attenuation was a significant positive predictor of FAE (β=0.924, P<0.001), accounting for 26.1% of the overall variance in FAE. Exploratory subgroup analyses revealed descriptive differences in the strength of this predictive trend across BMI categories. Notably, a stronger predictive trend was observed in obese patients (R2=0.812, P=0.037) than in normal weight (R2=0.310) and overweight (R2=0.247) patients. Conversely, while predictive efficacy remained significant in patients with normal CO (R2=0.288, P=0.001), it was attenuated and statistically non-significant in those with high CO (R2=0.240, P=0.090).ConclusionsTest-bolus peak attenuation demonstrated significant potential as a quantitative predictor of aortic enhancement in CCTA, suggesting that test-bolus peak attenuation may be useful in exploring individualized contrast protocols. The descriptive trends observed in the obese subgroup are strictly hypothesis-generating. Exploratory findings suggest that the predictive trend might vary according to BMI and CO. Larger studies are needed to confirm these descriptive observations.Keywords: Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), test bolus, aortic enhancement, body mass index (BMI), cardiac output (CO)

Introduction

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is a first-line non-invasive imaging modality for the evaluation of coronary artery disease (CAD) and has an exceptionally high negative predictive value. It not only enables the simultaneous qualitative and quantitative assessment of plaque characteristics but also provides important information for the risk stratification of cardiovascular events (1). Achieving adequate intravascular enhancement within the coronary lumen—typically ranging from 350 to 500 Hounsfield units (HU)—is an essential prerequisite for accurate evaluation (2). Suboptimal enhancement can limit the assessment of non-calcified plaques and may necessitate repeat scans, whereas excessive enhancement can exacerbate blooming artifacts, frequently leading to the overestimation of luminal stenosis.

Conventional CCTA protocols predominantly rely on fixed-dose or empirically determined weight-based contrast administration. However, such strategies largely overlook important individualized physiological variables, particularly body mass index (BMI) and cardiac output (CO). Physiologically, BMI profoundly influences the volume of contrast distribution, while CO determines the rate of contrast dilution and pulmonary transit. The failure to account for these factors frequently results in substantial interindividual variability in vascular enhancement, directly affecting image quality and diagnosis in some cases (3-5).

van den Boogert et al. (6) demonstrated that individualizing contrast administration protocols based on patient weight and tube voltage not only improves luminal attenuation but also effectively reduces the total contrast dose. Further, they found that compared to traditional bolus-tracking techniques, the test-bolus technique may offer advantages in guiding individualized contrast administration. Nevertheless, existing studies applying this technique often fail to incorporate patients’ real-time cardiac functional status, and standardized criteria for determining the relevant parameter settings remain lacking.

Preliminary evidence (6) suggests a correlation between the peak attenuation of a small-dose test bolus and luminal attenuation in the ascending aorta; however, the precise quantitative relationship between test-bolus parameters and subsequent vascular enhancement in routine clinical practice has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, using real-world clinical data, this study aimed to quantitatively assess the association between test-bolus parameters and final CCTA vascular enhancement. Further, this study sought to elucidate how patient-specific physiological profiles—specifically BMI and cardiac functional status—moderate this predictive relationship. Ultimately, the study findings may provide a robust quantitative rationale for optimizing individualized contrast administration protocols, thereby ensuring consistent and diagnostically adequate image quality while minimizing the issues associated with empirical dosing.

Therefore, this study aimed to address gaps in the current literature by: (I) quantifying the predictive value of test-bolus peak attenuation for final aortic enhancement (FAE) in a Chinese cohort; (II) exploring descriptive trends in this relationship across different BMI and CO subgroups; and (III) presenting exploratory subgroup findings to inform future research on individualized contrast administration. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0982/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with suspected CAD who underwent CCTA at Minhang Hospital, Fudan University, from September 2025 to February 2026. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) presence of normal sinus rhythm; (III) absence of hyperthyroidism; (IV) no history of allergy to iodinated contrast media; and (V) absence of severe hepatic or renal dysfunction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) severe coronary artery calcification; (II) incomplete clinical or imaging data; and/or (III) failure to comply with the prescribed contrast medium injection protocol. Ultimately, 50 patients were included in the final analysis. The detailed patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Flowchart of patient selection. CAD, coronary artery disease; CCTA, coronary computed tomography angiography.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Minhang Hospital. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

CCTA examination protocol

All CCTA examinations were performed using a third-generation dual-source computed tomography (CT) scanner (SOMATOM Definition Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany).

Patient preparation

Resting heart rate was routinely assessed before scanning. Patients with a resting heart rate >70 beats per minute (bpm) were administered oral beta-blockers (metoprolol, 25–50 mg) 30–60 minutes before scanning (n=34, 68%) to achieve a target heart rate ≤65 bpm. Contraindications to beta-blocker administration included resting bradycardia (<60 bpm), second- or third-degree atrioventricular block, and known beta-blocker allergy. Sublingual nitroglycerin was not administered before CCTA in any patient, as this is not part of Minhang Hospital’s routine protocol for CCTA. Patients were then positioned supine, and the scan range extended from 1 cm below the tracheal bifurcation (carina) to 2 cm below the diaphragm.

Image acquisition parameters

A retrospective electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated axial scanning mode was employed. Tube voltage was automatically modulated using CARE kV, and tube current was dynamically adjusted in real-time using an automated tube current modulation technique (CARE Dose4D; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Specific scan parameters included a detector collimation of 2 mm × 96 mm × 0.6 mm, a gantry rotation time of 250 ms, and a temporal resolution of 66 ms. Full cardiac cycle data were acquired using automated ECG-triggered acquisition.

Contrast injection protocol

A nonionic contrast medium (iomeprol, 400 mg I/mL) and a saline chaser were administered via the right antecubital vein using a dual-syringe power injector (MEDRAD Stellant; Bayer, Germany).

Test bolus

A dynamic single-slice scan was performed at the level of the main pulmonary artery (with a 1-s scan interval over 20–25 phases). An initial 20-mL bolus of contrast medium was injected at a fixed flow rate of 3.5 mL/s, immediately followed by a 20-mL saline chaser at the same rate. A region of interest (ROI) was placed in the ascending aorta to generate a time-density curve, from which the peak attenuation (HU) and time to peak attenuation were recorded. In routine clinical practice at Minhang Hospital, the test bolus is primarily used to qualitatively assess circulatory transit time and to generate the time-density curve for documentation. The main scan was triggered using bolus tracking (with a 60-HU threshold in the descending aorta) rather than the test-bolus timing, as this is the standard protocol at Minhang Hospital. The test-bolus peak attenuation values were extracted retrospectively from the stored time-density curves for research purposes. A circular ROI (200 mm2) was placed in the ascending aorta at the level of the main pulmonary artery, carefully avoiding vessel wall calcifications and plaques. Attenuation values were recorded as absolute HU without baseline subtraction. In cases of respiratory motion or beam-hardening artifacts, the ROI position was manually adjusted by the operator to ensure measurement accuracy.

Main scan

The total contrast volume was calculated using the following formula: Main scan contrast volume (mL) = [7 + estimated scan time (s)] × injection flow rate (mL/s). The injection flow rate was individualized based on the tube voltage automatically selected by CARE kV, with flow rates of 3.5 mL/s at 70 kV and 4.0 mL/s at 80 kV, followed by an increase of 0.5 mL/s for every subsequent 10-kV increment. Bolus tracking was performed with the monitoring ROI placed in the descending aorta and a trigger threshold of 60 HU. The main scan was automatically initiated once the attenuation threshold was reached. Immediately following the contrast injection, a 30-mL saline chaser was administered at the same flow rate. This contrast administration protocol was based on Minhang Hospital’s protocol, which was adapted from previous literature and Siemens vendor recommendations for CCTA contrast delivery (7). The constant “7” in the formula represents the baseline injection duration (in seconds), ensuring sustained vascular enhancement throughout the entire acquisition period.

Image reconstruction and post-processing

Images were reconstructed using Advanced Modeled Iterative Reconstruction (ADMIRE; strength level 4; Siemens Healthineers) with a slice thickness of 0.75 mm, an increment of 0.5 mm, and a Bv40 vascular kernel. The reconstructed datasets were transferred to a dedicated workstation (syngo.via; Siemens Healthineers) for post-processing, including volume rendering, maximum intensity projection, and curved planar reformation.

Measurement of aortic enhancement

Objective image measurements were independently performed by a radiologist with 10 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging who was blinded to the contrast injection protocols. The CT attenuation of the aortic root was measured on the axial images at the level of the left main coronary artery ostium. These images were derived from standard ECG-gated reconstructions used for routine coronary evaluation; depending on the patient’s heart rate and rhythm, the reconstruction phase was selected as the best diastolic phase (typically 70–80% of the R-R interval) for patients with regular and low heart rates, or the best systolic phase (typically 35–45% of the R-R interval) for those with higher or irregular heart rates.

A fixed ROI area of 200 mm2 was used, carefully avoiding vessel wall calcifications and non-calcified plaques. Measurements were repeated twice for each patient, and the average value was recorded as the FAE intensity. To assess the reproducibility of the objective image parameters, interobserver reliability for the FAE measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was calculated based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. An ICC value >0.75 indicated excellent reproducibility.

Measurement of cardiac functional parameters

The reconstructed multiphase CT datasets were transferred to the post-processing workstation (syngo.via), which automatically calculated and recorded the left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV). Cardiac functional parameters were calculated using the following formulas: stroke volume (SV) = LVEDV – LVESV; ejection fraction (EF) = (SV/LVEDV) ×100%. CO was calculated as CO = SV × heart rate, using the average heart rate recorded by ECG during the scan (8).

Study variables and grouping primary variables

The primary study variables were defined as follows—

Test-bolus peak attenuation: the maximum CT attenuation value (HU) derived from the test-bolus time-density curve;

FAE: the average CT attenuation value (HU) of the aorta measured according to the criteria in Section “Measurement of aortic enhancement”.

Time to peak attenuation was also recorded from the test-bolus time-density curve; however, preliminary correlation analysis revealed a weaker association with FAE (r=−0.284, P=0.046). Given its collinearity with CO and the focus of the study on peak attenuation as the primary predictor, time to peak attenuation was not included in the main regression models.

Based on established clinical reference ranges for CO (males: 2.82–8.82 L/min; females: 2.65–5.98 L/min), no patient in the cohort had low CO. Patients were thus dichotomized into normal CO (n=37) and high CO (n=13) groups.

Observation metric

Based on the optimal enhancement range required for routine clinical diagnosis (350–500 HU), the number of cases with aortic enhancement <350 HU was recorded to evaluate the clinical suitability of the routine contrast injection protocol.

Grouping criteria

The grouping criteria were as follows—

CO stratification: patients were categorized into normal CO and high CO groups based on established clinical reference ranges (males: 2.82–8.82 L/min; females: 2.65–5.98 L/min) (9);

CO stratification: patients were categorized into normal CO and high CO groups based on established clinical reference ranges (males: 2.82–8.82 L/min; females: 2.65–5.98 L/min) (9);

BMI stratification: according to the Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Overweight and Obesity in Chinese Adults, patients were classified into three groups: the normal weight group (BMI <24 kg/m2), the overweight group (24≤ BMI <28 kg/m2), and the obese group (BMI ≥28 kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on the results of the Shapiro-Wilk normality test; categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). Pearson correlation analysis was initially performed to evaluate associations between FAE and all candidate variables, including age, sex, BMI, CO, tube voltage, flow rate, contrast volume, test-bolus peak, and time to peak attenuation. Univariate linear regression analysis was subsequently performed to quantify the predictive relationship between test-bolus peak attenuation and FAE. Subgroup analyses stratified by BMI and CO were then conducted to evaluate whether this predictive relationship varied across these physiological profiles. Due to the retrospective and exploratory nature of this study and the use of consecutive temporal sampling, no formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed.

Exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by BMI

Exploratory subgroup regression analyses stratified by BMI demonstrated descriptive variations in the predictive trend across BMI categories (Table 2 and Figure 3B). Moderate correlations were observed in the normal-weight and overweight groups (R2=0.310 and 0.247, respectively), while the predictive efficacy was notably stronger in the obese group. In obese patients, test-bolus peak attenuation accounted for 81.2% of the variance in FAE, with a markedly steeper regression slope (β=3.300, P=0.037) than those observed in the normal-weight and overweight groups. A complete leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, alongside individual influence diagnostics for this subgroup, is provided in Table S2.

Exploratory subgroup analysis stratified by CO

In the exploratory analysis stratified by cardiac functional status, descriptive differences in the regression patterns were observed (Table 2 and Figure 3C). In patients with normal CO, test-bolus peak attenuation maintained significant predictive value (R2=0.288, β=1.017, P=0.001). In the high CO group, the regression slope was flattened (β=0.618) and did not reach statistical significance (P=0.090). As no formal interaction analysis was performed, this finding should be interpreted as an uncertain and potentially underpowered subgroup pattern rather than as definitive evidence of attenuated predictive efficacy.

Discussion

This study quantitatively demonstrated that test-bolus peak attenuation is a strong predictor of FAE in CCTA. Further, the exploratory analyses suggest that this predictive relationship may exhibit descriptive variations according to BMI and cardiac functional status. Rather than a “one-size-fits-all” correlation, the test bolus showed a strong correlation with the FAE in obese patients. Conversely, the findings for patients with high CO represented an uncertain and potentially underpowered subgroup pattern. Collectively, these findings support the potential use of the test bolus as a quantitative parameter rather than a qualitative visual assessment and provide a physiological rationale for further investigation of individualized contrast protocols in a Chinese population.

Consistent with previous studies (6,10), we found a positive correlation between test-bolus peak attenuation and FAE. van den Boogert et al. (6) reported that every 1-HU increase in the test-bolus peak attenuation resulted in an average increase of 0.58 HU in aortic attenuation. In our overall cohort, the regression coefficient was 0.924, which was slightly higher than that reported by van den Boogert et al., but of a similar magnitude. This discrepancy may stem from our use of a higher-concentration contrast medium (400 mg I/mL vs. the conventional 350 mg I/mL) and differences in the injection protocols. The physiological basis for using a test bolus to predict final enhancement is that the small-dose test bolus and the subsequent main bolus traverse the exact same circulatory pathway and undergo similar dilution and transit processes (11). Consequently, the peak attenuation may reflect the patient’s individualized hemodynamic status (e.g., transit time and central blood volume) (11)], thereby carrying important information for predicting the main-bolus enhancement (12,13).

Notably, an exploratory analysis revealed a trend toward a stronger correlation between test-bolus peak attenuation and FAE in the obese subgroup. However, given the very small sample size of the obese subgroup (n=5), these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. In the obese subgroup, the regression slope was 3.300, which was significantly higher than those of the normal-weight and overweight groups.

The “volume of distribution hypothesis” in contrast pharmacokinetics represents a possible explanation for this observation. Obese patients typically possess a larger central blood volume and contrast distribution volume, often accompanied by relatively reduced CO or altered circulatory patterns (14). In such a physiological state—characterized by a larger compartment and slower circulation—the intravascular dilution and transit of the contrast medium may remain relatively stable. The iodine bolus formed by the test injection may thus better preserve its signal characteristics, with less interference from rapid blood flow washout (15). Consequently, test-bolus peak attenuation may more precisely mirror the final enhancement of the main bolus.

This finding is consistent with the conclusions drawn from machine learning models by Lopes et al. (12), which identified test-bolus peak attenuation as the most critical feature for predicting suboptimal enhancement. Our study further quantified this relationship using traditional statistical methods. Because obese patients frequently require high tube voltage scanning, they may be particularly susceptible to marked iodine signal attenuation and thus face a high risk of suboptimal enhancement (16,17), making them the primary target population for individualized contrast protocols. Given the extremely small sample size of our study, the trends observed in the obese cohort are strictly hypothesis-generating. Future large-scale prospective studies are needed to rigorously evaluate whether these physiological observations are reproducible and whether they can ultimately inform individualized contrast dosing or algorithmic injection calculators.

Notably, the mean aortic attenuation in our cohort was 714 HU, substantially higher than the upper limit of the reported optimal range (500 HU), suggesting possible contrast overuse. No patient had enhancement below 350 HU; however, approximately 46 patients (92%) had enhancement exceeding 500 HU, which may increase blooming artifacts and contribute to the overestimation of stenosis. The widespread over-enhancement in our cohort may have been compounded by the combined use of a high-concentration contrast medium (400 mg I/mL) and a low tube voltage (70 kV in 92% of patients). Future individualized protocols should aim to minimize both under- and over-enhancement. Our subgroup-specific regression equations may provide a quantitative framework to achieve this balance.

Further, the descriptive variations observed across cardiac functional statuses warrant further investigation (4,10,18). In the normal CO group, the test-bolus peak demonstrated significant predictive value. Conversely, in the high CO group, the regression slope decreased to 0.618 and did not reach statistical significance (P=0.090). As no formal interaction analysis was performed to statistically compare the two strata, this result should be strictly interpreted as an uncertain and underpowered subgroup pattern. Although it is physiologically plausible that high CO states may accelerate contrast washout, the small sample size of this subgroup (n=13) severely limited the statistical power of the study. Consequently, any definitive mechanistic interpretation regarding the attenuation of the predictive signal remains speculative, and this descriptive pattern requires verification in larger, adequately powered cohorts (19).

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study without an a priori power calculation, the overall sample size was relatively small. Most notably, the obese group comprised only 5 patients. Linear regression based on such a limited sample heavily compromises the stability of the regression estimates. Although statistical significance was reached, the model is highly susceptible to overfitting and the influence of individual outliers, as evidenced by the extremely wide 95% CI (0.396–6.204) for the regression coefficient in this cohort. Consequently, while the predictive trend in obese patients is physiologically plausible, the current quantitative estimates should be considered statistically unstable and strictly hypothesis-generating, and require rigorous validation in future prospective, adequately powered multicenter studies. Further, we acknowledge the inherent statistical limitations of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis presented in our supplementary data (Table S2). With only four observations remaining in each iteration (n=4), the regression estimates are highly sensitive to minor data variations, and standard influence diagnostics have limited interpretability in such a small sample. These sensitivity analyses are thus provided solely for transparency regarding data leverage, rather than as evidence of model stability. Additionally, the absence of formal interaction testing (e.g., test-bolus peak × BMI group and test-bolus peak × CO group) means that the observed differences in predictive efficacy across subgroups should be interpreted as descriptive subgroup findings rather than evidence of statistically significant interactions.

Second, patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction (e.g., reduced EF) were not specifically included in this cohort; all patients had CO values within the normal or high range. Thus, the applicability of our findings to patients with low CO states remains unknown. Further, the absence of suboptimal enhancement (<350 HU) in our cohort limited our ability to evaluate the clinical utility of test-bolus peak attenuation for predicting binary endpoints (e.g., inadequate vs. adequate enhancement). The present findings thus primarily support its use for predicting continuous enhancement values.

Third, several technical factors may have influenced our measurements. The use of a fixed ROI area (200 mm2) for aortic attenuation measurements may not fully account for individual variations in aortic diameter. Beta-blockers were administered to a subset of patients (68%) for heart rate control; given their negative inotropic effects, they may have transiently influenced baseline hemodynamics and CO, and therefore represent a potential unmeasured confounder in the CO subgroup analyses. Additionally, nitroglycerin was not administered in this cohort, which may have affected coronary vasodilation and luminal diameter assessment, although this is unlikely to have influenced the primary outcome of aortic root attenuation.

Fourth, while estimated scan time was incorporated into the total contrast volume calculation, the potential interaction between scan time and final enhancement efficacy was not extensively analyzed. Although tube voltage showed a significant correlation with FAE (r=−0.422, P=0.002), it was not included in the main regression models due to its close association with BMI and the study’s focus on test-bolus peak attenuation as the primary predictive variable. Future studies with larger sample sizes may further elucidate the independent contribution of tube voltage.

Finally, this study did not evaluate diagnostic image quality endpoints such as signal-to-noise ratio, contrast-to-noise ratio, or the proportion of non-diagnostic segments, which are essential for establishing the clinical utility of test-bolus-based dose adjustment. Future prospective studies should incorporate these endpoints to validate the clinical applicability of our findings.

Conclusions

Test-bolus peak attenuation may serve as a useful predictor of FAE in CCTA, although its predictive performance may vary according to BMI and CO. The descriptive trends observed in the obese subgroup should be considered hypothesis-generating, and larger prospective cohorts are required to investigate their potential clinical utility.
