Oxidative stress-induced endothelial dysfunction drives mechanical and infectious lung injury: Therapeutic potential of kaempferol in males
Authors: Ziqi Shang, Zhiye Guo, Wen Tian, Feixiang Zhu, Tao Jiang, Weiyi Hu, Qingping Yao, Yunlong Huo, Yingxin Qi, Hanqin Wang, Kai Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanical ventilation (MV) remains an essential life-support strategy for patients with respiratory failure or critical illness. However, the mechanical stress generated during MV can lead to ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). By integrating population-level epidemiology with bulk microarray and single-cell transcriptomic profiling, we identified a convergent pathogenic program shared by MV- and sepsis-associated lung injury, characterized by persistent oxidative stress and widespread endothelial dysfunction. In the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), higher dietary intake of kaempferol, a plant-derived flavonol, was associated with improved pulmonary function in men. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) results showed that NADPH oxidase (NOX) in the plasma membrane is the direct target of kaempferol. AlphaFold Protein Structure Database and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) resolved NOX2 structure, combined with molecular docking, suggest a direct interaction between kaempferol and NOX2. We also found that MV led to elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induced endothelial dysfunction in various regions of the lung. Of note, kaempferol alleviated abnormal mechanical stress-induced ROS and endothelial barrier disruption by inactivating NOX2-Calcium-calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)- Extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) axis both in vivo and in vitro. Besides, kaempferol also improved survival rate and alleviated acute lung injury in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic mice via inhibiting the NOX2-CaMKII-ERK1/2 signaling pathway. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of kaempferol in attenuating MV-associated lung injury and alleviating pre-existing inflammatory damage to the lung.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a life-support treatment that helps or replaces breathing in patients with respiratory failure. It is used when oxygen levels are dangerously low or carbon dioxide levels are too high [1]. It is also required during general anesthesia to maintain safe breathing during surgery. MV is essential in intensive care units (ICUs) and operating rooms, saving lives in critical and surgical settings [1,2]. However, MV can induce lung injury and inflammation—a phenomenon known as ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) [3,4]. The alveolar-capillary barrier, composed of endothelial and epithelial cells, is disrupted during VILI. This damage increases permeability, allowing protein leakage into alveoli and promoting leukocyte infiltration, further driving inflammation [5,6]. To date, there is no specific pharmacological therapy for VILI [7]. Therefore, discovering the common molecular mechanisms of pre-existing lung injury and further damage caused by VILI will greatly benefit the prevention and treatment of endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and lung diseases.

Disruption of endothelial barrier integrity is a critical driver of vascular leakage in both VILI and sepsis-associated acute lung injury [5,[8], [9], [10]]. One of the key drivers of endothelial dysfunction is oxidative stress [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]], which arises from an imbalance between pro-oxidant forces and the antioxidant defense system. Abnormal mechanical stress-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation triggers downstream signaling cascades that exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and promote pathological vascular remodeling [8,[16], [17], [18]]. However, the molecular mechanisms through which MV-induced high cyclic stretch contributes to endothelial dysfunction and lung injury remain poorly understood, and the involvement of oxidative stress in this pathological cascade warrants further elucidation. Because oxidative stress is a central mechanism in VILI, the use of antioxidants may be a potential treatment for VILI. Previous studies have shown that antioxidant or redox-modulating agents, including 4-octyl itaconate [19], amifostine [20], superoxide dismutase (SOD) [21], N-acetylcysteine [22], and quercetin [8], can mitigate experimental VILI, suggesting that targeting oxidative stress is a rational therapeutic strategy in this setting.

Kaempferol, a natural flavonol within the flavonoid family, exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [23,24]. As a naturally occurring secondary metabolite, kaempferol is abundant in various fruits, vegetables, and herbal remedies, contributing to its dietary and therapeutic significance [24]. Numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated that kaempferol and its glycosides exhibit a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, encompassing antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anticancer, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, antidiabetic, anti-osteoporotic, estrogenic/antiestrogenic, anxiolytic, analgesic, and antiallergic effects [[24], [25], [26]]. Despite kaempferol's documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, its efficacy in VILI remains unexplored, with critical gaps in understanding its impact on oxidative damage and pulmonary barrier integrity.

Here, we found that dietary kaempferol intake shows a positive association with enhanced lung function parameters, particularly in men. Microarray data and single-cell transcriptomic analyses reveal that oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction serve as common pathological mechanisms underlying lung injury induced by both MV and CLP. Specifically, MV triggers NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) activation, leading to widespread oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. Notably, kaempferol emerges as a promising therapeutic agent by directly suppressing ROS production at its source. Furthermore, kaempferol effectively alleviates lung injury caused by mechanical stress or infection through inhibition of the NOX2-CaMKII-ERK1/2 signaling pathway, highlighting its potential as a dual-targeted intervention. As a naturally derived bioactive compound, kaempferol holds great potential for clinical translation in preventing VILI and other lung injuries.

Methods

Microarray data analysis

Transcriptomic datasets GSE226807 and GSE121550 were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database [27] (data acquisition via the R package GEOquery, v2.70.0) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in VILI and sepsis-induced lung injury models using the “limma” R package (v3.58.1). Functional characterization of these DEGs was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the “clusterProfiler” package (v4.10.1) to explore the molecular impact of MV and sepsis on lung function. Detailed procedures, group definitions, and analysis thresholds are provided in Supplementary Method 2.1.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

The Seurat (v4.4.0) was employed to analyze scRNA-seq datasets derived from lung injury models induced by MV and CLP. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, batch correction was performed by harmony (v1.2.0), and visualization was realized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). After cell type annotation based on established lung cell markers [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]], oxidative stress scores in endothelial cells were computed using AddModuleScore with the HALLMARK “REACTIVE_OXYGEN_SPECIES_PATHWAY” gene set. Detailed quality-control thresholds, clustering parameters, marker selection criteria, and gene-set retrieval procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 2.2.

Study population in NHANES

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics in the United States, collects demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related data for health assessment purposes. In this study, participants from the 2007-2010 NHANES surveys were considered as candidates, totaling 20,686 participants. After excluding samples with missing data on forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), those diagnosed with COPD, individuals aged 20 years or younger, and samples with missing data on demographics, diabetes, BMI, bilirubin, and energy intake, a total of 5587 samples were included. The specific process of participant selection is detailed in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Participants screening flow chart.

Participants screening flow chart.

Measurements of lung function parameters and PRISm definition

In this study, pulmonary function parameters such as FEV1 and FVC were obtained from the NHANES SPX dataset. The predicted values for each pulmonary function parameter were calculated using the NHANES III equations [35]. Cases of Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry (PRISm) were defined as those with an FEV1/FVC ratio ≥0.7 and an FEV1 percentage of predicted value < 80% [27].

Dietary kaempferol intake

The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Flavonoid Database provides comprehensive information on the dietary flavonoid content in various foods and beverages. Detailed data can be accessed at the following URL: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/Flav/Flav3.3.pdf. This database includes the flavonoid content (mg/100 g) of all foods and beverages listed in the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS), corresponding to the dietary data released by the What We Eat in America (WWEIA) survey and NHANES [36]. The dietary data from NHANES were collected through two non-consecutive 24-h dietary recall interviews.

Collection of baseline features

Baseline characteristics, including demographics, lifestyle factors, and health status, were extracted from the NHANES demographic, questionnaire, and laboratory modules. Standardized clinical criteria were applied to define smoking and alcohol status, as well as the diagnosis of chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Detailed operational definitions and diagnostic thresholds for smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia are provided in Supplementary Method 2.3.

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA)

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software (Version 145030503, Qiagen, Germany) leverages the Ingenuity Pathways Knowledge Database to interpret complex biological interactions, integrating curated data on genes, proteins, chemicals, and pathways for mechanistic analysis. It was used to identify functions and diseases related to kaempferol and analyze the top 30 molecules regulated by kaempferol. Furthermore, the distribution of these 30 molecules in different locations within the cell were presented by IPA.

Potential interaction between kaempferol and NOX2

The potential interaction between kaempferol and NOX2 was evaluated using the DeepMice platform, with protein structures retrieved from the AlphaFold database and the Protein Data Bank. Docking simulations were performed at predicted binding sites to assess binding orientations and affinities. The resulting molecular interactions were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL to identify key residues involved in the kaempferol-NOX2 complex. Detailed receptor or ligand preparation, and docking procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 2.4.

Animal studies

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Allocation of mice and samples into groups was random. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University and conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Decree No. 55, 2001, Ministry of Health, China).

Animal models

Male C57BL/6 mice were subjected to mechanical ventilation (MV) for 4 h at either low (9 mL/kg) or high (34 mL/kg) tidal volumes to establish the VILI model [8], with Kaempferol (15 mg/kg/day) administered as a 3-day pretreatment. For the sepsis-induced lung injury model, CLP was performed [12], and lung tissues were harvested 16 h post-surgery. All procedures were conducted under appropriate anesthesia and temperature control, following established protocols for acute lung injury induction. Detailed VILI and CLP protocols are described in Supplementary Method 2.5.

Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining

The levels of ROS in lung tissue were assessed using dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence staining. Mouse lungs were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned into 5 μm frozen slices. Human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs, immortalized) were seeded in confocal dishes and grown to approximately 70-80% confluence. After treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Both the fixed HAECs and the prepared lung sections were incubated with freshly prepared DHE staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by thorough washing with PBS. Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Fluoview 1000, Olympus), and fluorescence intensity was quantified in ImageJ using mean fluorescence intensity.

Electron spin resonance determination of superoxide production in lung tissues and HAECs

The lung tissues were freshly harvested and homogenized in lysis buffer containing 1:100 protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Millipore, USA), centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. Protein content of the supernatant was determined using a protein assay kit (Bio-Rad). For HAECs, cells were collected in cold modified Krebs/HEPES (KHB) buffer after treatment. The Electron spin resonance (ESR) was used to detect superoxide production. Detailed methods have been included in the Supplemental Materials.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from lung tissues using RIPA buffer and separated via 10% SDS-PAGE before being transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. Following three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents, and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software for relative quantification. Primary antibody information and detailed Western blot procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 2.6.

Immunofluorescence imaging

Frozen lung sections were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated overnight with primary antibodies. After incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI nuclear staining, slides were mounted and visualized using confocal microscopy (Olympus Fluoview 1000). Primary antibody information and detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 2.7.

Cell treatment

HAECs were obtained from QuiCell (Shanghai, China) and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM, QuiCell-Pri-8003) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). To investigate the effect of ROS on cellular function, HAECs were treated with H2O2. After seeding the HAECs onto a six-well plate, cells were allocated into three groups: the H2O2 group, in which cells were exposed to 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h; the kaempferol + H2O2 group, in which cells were pretreated with 10 μM kaempferol for 1 h followed by co-incubation with 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h; and the control group, in which cells received an equal volume of 0.1% DMSO for 6 h.

Cyclic stretch loading

Cells were subjected to cyclic stretch as previously described [8,[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Cyclic stretch was performed on human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) using the FX5000 T Strain Unit (FlexCell International, USA) to simulate cell deformation during high MV (H-MV) in vivo, with 20% stretch amplitude and 0.5 Hz frequency for 4 h. Then, HAECs were harvested for further examination.

Survival curve

For survival analysis, mice were monitored every 30 min starting at 6 h post-CLP surgery to document mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using Prism software.

Lung wet/dry weight ratio

The lung wet/dry weight ratio was measured to assess pulmonary edema, reflecting endothelial permeability and injury severity. After excision, lungs were weighed (wet weight), dried at 60 °C for 48 h, and re-weighed (dry weight) to determine the ratio.

Lung Evans Blue staining

To assess pulmonary endothelial permeability, an Evans Blue dye extravasation assay was conducted. A 2% Evans blue solution was prepared by dissolving 20 mg of Evans blue powder in 1 mL PBS. Mice were administered Evans blue (2%, 2 mL/kg) via tail vein injection and allowed to circulate for 0.5 h. Afterward, animals were euthanized and perfused with PBS through the heart until the effluent from the right atrium was clear, after which the lungs were collected and photographed to document Evans blue staining.

H&E staining and histological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (24 h), dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded. Sections (5 μm) were cut and stained with H&E. Histopathological evaluation was performed under bright-field microscopy, with representative images captured.

Statistical analysis

For the NHANES study, survey-weighted models were applied using R (v4.3.3) to account for complex sampling designs. Categorical and continuous variables were compared using Rao & Scott-adjusted Chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests [45,46], respectively, while multivariable linear and logistic regressions were constructed to evaluate the associations between kaempferol intake and lung function, adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates. Experimental data from animal and cell studies were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 and are presented as mean ± SD. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test or ANOVA (followed by Tukey's post hoc test) for normally distributed data, and Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests for non-normal data, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical analysis methods are described in Supplementary Method 2.9.

When compared to controls, DEG analysis revealed that 86.8% of MV-upregulated genes and 87.2% of MV-downregulated genes were consistently altered in the CLP group, indicating strong transcriptional overlap (Fig. 2A and B). Heatmap analysis of the top 15 upregulated and downregulated genes in the MV group revealed highly concordant expression patterns in CLP, with strong reproducibility across biological replicates, supporting the robustness of these transcriptional changes (Fig. 2C). GSEA based on DEGs in the MV group indicated a significant increase in ROS signaling pathway activity (NES = 2.04, Adjusted P < 0.001), suggesting oxidative stress as a core response mechanism (Fig. 2D). Notably, GSEA and GO analysis results for the CLP group exhibited highly similar enrichment profiles (Fig. 2F and G), indicating that the two models share common molecular mechanisms in terms of oxidative stress and endothelial homeostasis disruption.

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals impaired endothelial function and elevated oxidative stress in MV and CLP-driven lung injury

Building on the transcriptomic overlap identified in microarray, we turned to scRNA-seq to delineate cell-specific responses. We first analyzed self-sequenced high MV (H-MV) single-cell dataset. Lung tissues from 3 control mice and 3 H-MV-treated mice were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and subjected to scRNA-seq analysis. After rigorous quality control procedures, we retained 36,719 cells for downstream analysis, including 17,876 cells from control lungs and 18,843 cells from H-MV-treated lungs (Fig. 3A). Using UMAP for dimensionality reduction, we identified major lung-resident immune and structural cell populations, including Neutrophils (n = 4999), Alveolar macrophages (n = 3216), Interstitial macrophages (n = 676), Monocytes/Macrophages (n = 1655), Dendritic cells (DCs) (n = 1076), Basophils (n = 70), T cells (n = 4047), B cells (n = 8903), Natural killer cells (NK cells) (n = 1275), Alveolar type I cells (AT1) (n = 260), Alveolar type II cells (AT2) (n = 1701), Ciliated cells/Club cells (n = 107), Endothelial cells (n = 3413), Mesothelial cells (n = 53), Fibroblasts (n = 4147), Myofibroblasts (n = 631), Smooth muscle cells (SMC) (n = 432), Megakaryocytes (n = 58) (Fig. 3B). For the CLP-induced lung injury-related dataset GSE207651 (Fig. 3C), after quality assessment and filtering, we retained transcriptomic data from 17,090 cells, with 7103 cells derived from sham mouse lung tissue and 9987 cells from CLP-treated mouse lung tissue. Using the same UMAP dimensionality reduction approach, we identified 30 cell clusters, which were classified into 14 major cell types (Fig. 3D). Neutrophils (n = 2205), Alveolar macrophages (n = 833), Interstitial macrophages (n = 404), Monocytes (n = 1787), T cells (n = 1179), B cells (n = 227), NK cells (n = 634), AT1/Ciliated cells/Club cells (n = 233), AT2 (n = 179), Endothelial cells (n = 2112), Mesothelial cells (n = 355), Fibroblasts (n = 5764), Myofibroblasts (n = 263), SMC (n = 915). Associations between dietary intake of kaempferol and human lung functions

Kaempferol, as an antioxidant and active ingredient of traditional Chinese medicine, participates in a variety of biological functions and disease processes [[47], [48], [49]]. Some foods with high levels of kaempferol are shown in Fig. S1. Considering these established pharmacological effects, our primary objective was to assess the potential relationship between regular dietary kaempferol consumption and lung function parameters at the population level.

Table S1 shows the baseline characteristics of adults stratified by dietary kaempferol intake quartiles (Q1-Q4) in NHANES from 2007 to 2010. Significant trends were observed across quartiles: age increased progressively from Q1 (42.03 ± 14.50 years) to Q4 (43.65 ± 13.68 years; P = 0.002). Sex distribution varied markedly, with Q4 showing the highest proportion of males (58%) and lowest of females (42%; P < 0.001). Racial composition shifted toward Non-Hispanic White dominance in higher quartiles (Q4: 76%), while Mexican American and Hispanic/Other groups declined (P < 0.001). Educational attainment rose across quartiles, with 64% in Q4 achieving college-level education (P = 0.002). Alcohol consumption increased sharply, from 72% non-drinkers in Q1 to 86% drinkers in Q4 (P < 0.001), though smoking prevalence showed no clear pattern (p = 0.003 for group differences but no linear trend). Anthropometric trends included increased height from Q1 (167.46 ± 9.46 cm) to Q4 (171.40 ± 9.45 cm; P < 0.001), while BMI remained stable (P = 0.611). Blood biomarkers demonstrated upward trends: UA (uric acid), Albumin, and Bilirubin levels increased significantly across quartiles (P < 0.001 to P = 0.035), with HDL-C rising marginally (Q1: 51.08 ± 16.04 mg/dL to Q4: 53.41 ± 16.70 mg/dL; P = 0.028). Pulmonary function improved with higher quartiles, as evidenced by increased FVC, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC ratios (Except for the FEV1/FVC ratio p = 0.002, all p values are <0.001). Notably, PRISm prevalence decreased from 47% in Q1 to 34% in Q4 (P < 0.001), suggesting an inverse association between the quartile metric and PRISm risk. Hyperlipidemia remained highly prevalent (∼72%) across all groups (P = 0.922), with no significant differences in hypertension or diabetes prevalence.

FVC, FEV1, and PRISm were used as indicators reflecting impaired lung function [50,51]. We examined the association between the dietary kaempferol and lung functions using the NHANES database. Based on the quartiles of dietary kaempferol intake, we divided all participants into four groups and systematically evaluated the intrinsic association between kaempferol intake and FVC. In unadjusted analyses (Model 1), continuous kaempferol intake showed significant positive associations with FVC. This association remained significant across increasing intake quartiles, particularly in Q4. After sequential adjustment for demographic factors (Model 2: age, sex, race, education, FPIR) and lifestyle/clinical covariates (Model 3: BMI, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), both continuous intake and Q4 quartile associations maintained statistical significance. Given the well-documented gender disparities in dietary patterns and susceptibility to pulmonary diseases, we conducted stratified analyses by sex to explore potential differences in the association between kaempferol intake and FVC. This approach allowed us to examine whether the observed relationships varied between males and females. In addition to acting as an antioxidant, we also explored whether kaempferol could interact directly with NOX2. The structure of NOX2 (Fig. 6A–D) was acquired from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Mechanical ventilation promotes ROS generation and endothelial dysfunction in various lung regions

In the previous work, a mouse model of VILI was established using two ventilation strategies: low MV (L-MV) and H-MV [8]. The left lung was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin for histological analysis, while the right upper lung lobe was embedded in OCT compound, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and sectioned into 5 μm slices for DHE staining or immunofluorescence staining [8]. The remaining lung lobes were frozen at −80 °C for Western blot analysis [8]. Here, we established the same VILI model (Fig. 7A) while used the same lobe of the lung to explore the consistency of ROS generation and endothelial dysfunction in various lung regions. ROS levels were assessed using DHE staining, lung tissues from ventilated mice exhibited stronger red fluorescent signal from oxidized DHE compared to the control group (Fig. 7B and C). Consistent with the DHE staining, total superoxide production in lung tissues was quantified by ESR spectroscopy. Compared to the control group, ventilated mice in the L-MV and H-MV groups showed higher superoxide levels (Fig. 7D). Notably, the H-MV group exhibited the highest superoxide signal, confirming that increased tidal volumes significantly exacerbate oxidative stress in the lungs. The GSEA results demonstrated marked alterations in pathways associated with endothelial cell differentiation (Fig. 7E) and development (Fig. 7F) post-MV, suggesting impaired endothelial function. Discussion

Mechanical ventilation remains indispensable in critical care, but the harmful mechanical stress generated during ventilation could induce VILI. By integrating epidemiological observations, transcriptomic profiling, single-cell analyses, and mechanistic studies, we provide compelling evidence that ROS serves as a central driver of endothelial barrier disruption across diverse forms of lung injury. Notably, we further show for the first time that VILI is characterized by a marked increase of oxidative stress in various part of the lung. Consistent with these observations, we identify oxidative stress-driven endothelial dysfunction as a unifying pathogenic mechanism shared by both VILI and CLP-associated acute lung injury. Within this framework, kaempferol emerged as a candidate intervention that acts at the source of ROS, thereby preserving endothelial barrier integrity and mitigating downstream tissue damage.

Numerous flavonoid compounds have been shown to possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, among which kaempferol has attracted particular attention due to its broad spectrum of biological activities [57,58]. Previous studies have demonstrated the protective effects of kaempferol in various pulmonary diseases, including lung ischemia–reperfusion injury [59], LPS- and CLP-induced acute lung injury [60,61], and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [62]. Previous studies have reported associations between dietary flavonoid intake and respiratory health outcomes [63,64]. In our epidemiological analysis, dietary kaempferol intake was positively correlated with lung function in male subjects. This male-specific signaling motivated us to conduct subsequent mechanistic validation in male mice. The NOX family represents a major enzymatic source of ROS and is widely activated in various lung injury models and vascular diseases [8,11,12,52,65,66]. Different NOX isoforms exhibit cell-specific roles in distinct vascular pathologies [11]. We have proved that endothelial NOX2-induced ROS promotes endothelial dysfunction and lung injury under high cyclic stretch subjected to MV [8]. Therefore, inhibiting NOX2 and targeting ROS are potential therapeutic strategies for treating lung injury. This study proved that kaempferol mitigates VILI and pre-existing pulmonary damage by modulating MV-generated ROS, with a focus on the role of NOX2 in this mechanism.

Previous studies have shown that antioxidant or redox-modulating interventions, including 4-octyl itaconate [19], amifostine [20], superoxide dismutase [21], and N-acetylcysteine [22], can alleviate experimental VILI, supporting oxidative stress as a therapeutically relevant target in VILI. However, most of these studies mainly focused on isolated VILI model and did not address whether VILI and infection-associated lung injury share a common endothelial oxidative stress program. Here, we reveal a shared pathogenic pathway in MV- and sepsis-induced lung injury, driven by persistent oxidative stress and resulting in widespread endothelial dysfunction. Additionally, our study incorporates an extensive epidemiological analysis demonstrating an association between kaempferol and enhanced lung function, further substantiating its potential as a therapeutic agent in lung injury.

Regarding the sex-specific association between dietary kaempferol intake and lung function, we speculate that sex hormones may be a major driver of this heterogeneity. Under physiological conditions, females appear less susceptible to oxidative stress, potentially due to the antioxidative properties of estrogen and sex-dependent regulation of NOX activity [67]. In healthy young adults, in vivo oxidative stress biomarkers were reported to be significantly higher in men than in age-matched women [68]. From the perspective of ROS generation, sex differences may exist at the level of NOX isoform expression and activation [69]. For example, NOX2 expression was reported to be higher in isolated porcine coronary arteries from males, whereas females showed relatively higher NOX4 expression [70]. However, other work reported no significant sex difference in NOX2 expression in rat cerebral arteries, with sex-related effects observed primarily for NOX1 and NOX4 [71]. Although the existing literature does not provide consistent evidence that NOX2 expression is higher in males than in females, these findings collectively suggest that sex-dependent differences in NOX expression or activity may modulate the therapeutic effects of kaempferol. Estrogen levels also modulate ROS clearance, which may further contribute to sex-related differences in overall ROS level. Higher levels of estrogen are associated with increased expression of extracellular superoxide dismutase (ecSOD) and manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) in circulating monocytes, which may contribute to the clearance of ROS and partially mediate the vascular protective effect of estrogen [72].

Given the central role of endothelial barrier integrity in gas exchange, preserving the alveolar–capillary endothelium represents a rational therapeutic objective in VILI. In the mammalian lung, alveolar and capillary ECs form a tightly integrated structural barrier essential for efficient gas exchange [73]. MV with non-physiological parameters induces direct mechanical injury to these ECs, compromising barrier integrity [5]. Therefore, pharmacological interventions preserving endothelial integrity may offer a promising therapeutic avenue for VILI management. To date, studies on endothelial mechanotransduction in VILI have largely explored the role of mechanosensitive receptors, particularly ion channels and integrins, in disease pathogenesis [[74], [75], [76]]. However, the role of mechanical stress and infection-induced ROS in endothelial dysfunction and lung injury was not clarified clearly. Here, we demonstrate for the first time that kaempferol attenuates NOX2-driven ROS production, thereby alleviating endothelial dysfunction and lung injury by suppressing the CaMKII-ERK1/2 signaling pathway under high cyclic stretch. Notably, mechanical cues—including stretch, stiffness, circadian rhythms, fluid flow, intercellular signaling cascades, and cytoskeletal organization—can modulate drug-target interactions, offering critical insights for drug discovery and development [77]. This aspect remains underexplored in the current study, and future research should incorporate it to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, kaempferol is a well-recognized flavonol with intrinsic antioxidant and ROS-scavenging properties. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that at least part of its protective effects in our VILI and CLP models results from its general antioxidant activity rather than direct inhibition of NOX2. While molecular docking suggests a potential interaction between kaempferol and NOX2, these findings remain computational and lack direct experimental validation of NOX2 enzymatic inhibition. Second, our in vivo experiments employed a pretreatment design, which does not fully recapitulate the clinical scenario where mechanical ventilation is initiated emergently. Future studies should explore post-injury therapeutic dosing to establish an effective treatment window.

Collectively, kaempferol exerts robust protection in both VILI and CLP-induced lung injury, primarily via inhibition of NOX2-derived oxidative stress and associated signaling pathways. These data not only validate oxidative stress as a key driver of endothelial dysfunction but also position kaempferol as a promising candidate for treating pulmonary disorders involving redox imbalance in man.
