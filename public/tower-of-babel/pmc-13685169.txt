Toward targeted assessment of inspiratory muscle weakness before extubation: a pooled individual patient data analysis from multiple observational cohorts in critically ill ventilated patients
Authors: Clément Medrinal, Margaux Machefert, Claire Dubois, Guillaume Fossat, Guillaume Schnell, Guillaume Prieur, Yann Combret
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBedside neuromuscular tests are commonly proposed to assess readiness for extubation in critically ill patients. However, when used in unselected populations, their ability to predict clinically relevant outcomes such as extubation failure has been inconsistent. A more targeted use of these tests, restricted to patients in whom neuromuscular weakness is more likely to contribute to weaning failure, may improve their clinical relevance.MethodsWe conducted a pooled individual patient data analysis of multiple observational cohorts including adult ICU patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. The primary outcome was extubation failure, defined as reintubation or death within 48 h after extubation. Available neuromuscular assessments included maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), peak cough flow (PCF), and Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score. Prespecified thresholds were MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O, MEP ≤ 50 cmH₂O, PCF ≤ 60 L/min, and MRC < 48/60. Associations with extubation failure were assessed in the overall population and according to prior mechanical ventilation duration.ResultsAmong 494 included patients, 458 had an available extubation outcome and 72 (16%) experienced extubation failure. MIP was available in 230 patients, MEP in 124, PCF in 156, and MRC score in 252. In the overall population, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O showed only a borderline association with extubation failure (24% vs. 14%; OR 1.94, 95% CI 0.98 to 3.84; p = 0.06). In patients ventilated for more than 5 days, extubation failure occurred in 33% with MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O versus 14% with MIP > 30 cmH₂O. After adjustment, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O remained associated with extubation failure (adjusted OR 3.52, 95% CI 1.37 to 9.03; p = 0.009). No significant adjusted association was observed for MEP, PCF, or MRC.ConclusionsNeuromuscular tests may have limited prognostic value when applied broadly before extubation. Low MIP appears more informative after prolonged mechanical ventilation, supporting a targeted rather than systematic use of respiratory neuromuscular assessment before extubation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06305-1.

Introduction

Extubation is a critical step during an intensive care unit stay. Its success depends not only on the resolution of the initial cause of mechanical ventilation, but also on a combination of clinical, respiratory, neurological, and muscular factors [1, 2]. Extubation failure occurs in 14% to 18% of patients depending on the cohort [3–5] and may result from one or several combined mechanisms. This diversity of mechanisms explains why extubation failure remains difficult to predict and why a patient may successfully complete a spontaneous breathing trial while still being at high risk after removal of the endotracheal tube [1, 5].

Among the determinants of extubation failure, the patient’s neuromuscular status has a particular role and has been the focus of numerous studies [6–8]. Mechanical ventilation, sedation, sepsis, immobilization, and the use of neuromuscular blocking agents may impair both peripheral and respiratory muscles [9–13]. ICU-acquired weakness, clinically defined by a Medical Research Council (MRC) score below 48/60, has been associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, weaning difficulties, and extubation failure [7]. However, peripheral weakness and respiratory muscle weakness do not appear to be equivalent [14–17]. Indeed, the diaphragm, extra-diaphragmatic inspiratory muscles, and expiratory muscles may be affected independently or only partially alongside with global peripheral muscle strength [12, 18].

Although diaphragm assessment has been the most widely studied approach, respiratory muscle strength measurements using maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP and MEP), as well as peak cough flow (PCF), have been proposed to better characterize neuromuscular risk before extubation [8, 19–21]. Several studies have shown that low respiratory pressures are associated with adverse outcomes, including weaning failure, extubation failure, duration of mechanical ventilation, or mortality [7]. However, available cohorts often remain limited in size, with a small number of events. This constraint reduces statistical power, limits the possibility of adjustment, and makes it difficult to identify which measurement is truly useful in clinical practice.

Another important limitation of available studies is the lack of clinically contextualized implementation of these measurements. Neuromuscular tests are often evaluated as universal predictors, applied to all patients before extubation. However, the clinical meaning of a low MIP is probably not the same in a patient ventilated for 24 to 48 h and in a patient ventilated for several days [22, 23]. High MIP values may help rule out clinically relevant muscle weakness, but low MIP values may also reflect confounding factors other than true respiratory muscle weakness [24, 25]. We therefore hypothesized that respiratory neuromuscular measurements should not be interpreted uniformly in all patients, but according to the clinical context in which they are performed.

The primary objective was to identify the conditions under which MIP, MEP, PCF, and MRC score are associated with extubation failure.

Methods

Study design

We performed a pooled individual patient data analysis using six previously published source studies conducted in two French ICUs (Le Havre and Orléans). Recruitment periods across the source studies ranged from January 2014 to December 2024. The studies included prospective cohorts. Recruitment and sampling procedures were those predefined in each original study protocol and therefore differed across cohorts; these are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. No de novo patient sampling was performed for the present pooled analysis. All available individual patient records from the source datasets meeting the eligibility criteria of the present analysis were considered for inclusion. All cohorts had received approval from ethics committees in accordance with French legislation.

The included cohorts were published studies that shared the evaluation of adult mechanically ventilated ICU patients and the availability of at least one respiratory or peripheral neuromuscular measurement before extubation or during the ventilatory weaning process [19–21, 26–29]. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were those of the original cohorts. In general, patients were included if they were aged 18 years or older, admitted to the ICU, invasively mechanically ventilated for at least 24 h, and assessed before planned extubation or during the weaning process. Exclusion criteria included, depending on the cohort, decisions to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining treatments, death before extubation, unplanned extubation, tracheostomy before assessment, pre-existing neuromuscular disease with documented weakness, inability to perform the measurement, and technical limitations specific to each protocol. Patients without available information on extubation failure were excluded from the primary analysis. Patients with missing data for a given test were excluded only from the analysis corresponding to that test.

Neuromuscular measurements

MIP, MEP, and PCF measurements were performed after the weaning trial, while patients were awake and alert, breathing spontaneously with pressure-support ventilation of 7 cmH₂O and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 0 cmH₂O, and considered ready for planned extubation. MIP was measured using an electronic manometer connected to a micro-RPM® unidirectional valve. The procedure was explained to each patient, who was instructed and encouraged to generate a maximal inspiratory effort in accordance with respiratory muscle testing recommendations [31]. To maximize the likelihood of obtaining a truly maximal inspiratory pressure, the unidirectional valve allowed expiration while preventing inspiration, and inspiratory occlusion was maintained for 20 s according to the method described by Truwit and Marini [25].

PCF was recorded from the ventilator flow curve [21]. The participant was asked to inhale maximally before coughing. At least three measurements of MIP, MEP, and PCF were performed, and the best value was used for the analysis [30]. Peripheral muscle strength was assessed using the Medical Research Council sum score. In accordance with previous cohorts, predefined thresholds were MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O, MEP ≤ 50 cmH₂O, PCF ≤ 60 L/min, and MRC < 48/60.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was extubation failure, defined as reintubation or death within 48 h after extubation [31, 32]. Exploratory secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, ventilator-free days at day 28 when available, tracheostomy, and ICU mortality. Care and weaning protocols reflected standard ICU practice during each study period. Physicians responsible for patient care and ventilatory weaning were blinded to the results of neuromuscular tests. Decisions regarding the use of post-extubation respiratory support, including high-flow nasal cannula, non-invasive ventilation, or reintubation, were made on a case-by-case basis by the attending physician.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were described as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges or means with standard deviations for continuous variables, according to their distribution. For each analysis involving a neuromuscular measurement, only patients with available data for the corresponding test, duration of mechanical ventilation, and extubation failure outcome were included. The primary outcome was extubation failure, defined as reintubation or death within 48 h after extubation. All available neuromuscular tests were first explored for their association with extubation failure. The tests evaluated were maximal inspiratory pressure, maximal expiratory pressure, peak cough flow, and the Medical Research Council sum score. Predefined thresholds were MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O, MEP ≤ 50 cmH₂O, PCF ≤ 60 L/min, and MRC < 48/60. For each test, absolute risks of extubation failure were calculated according to test status. Results were reported as counts and percentages, absolute risk differences with 95% confidence intervals, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and p-values. Between-group comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test, as appropriate. Mechanical ventilation duration was explored using pragmatic thresholds of > 5, >7, and > 10 days. The > 5-day threshold was used for the main adjusted analysis because it was the earliest clinically actionable marker of prolonged ventilation, whereas the > 7-day and > 10-day thresholds were retained as sensitivity analyses to assess the consistency of the signal with increasing ventilatory exposure. The main adjusted analysis evaluated whether pre-extubation inspiratory muscle weakness, defined as MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O, was associated with extubation failure among patients ventilated for more than 5 days. Because the analysis was based on pooled individual data from several cohorts, the adjustment strategy was defined a priori and deliberately parsimonious. A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted with extubation failure as the dependent variable and MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O as the main independent variable. The model was adjusted for age, body mass index, SAPS II, and cohort of origin. Age, body mass index, and SAPS II were kept as continuous variables. Cohort of origin was included as a fixed effect to account for differences in source populations, measurement protocols, and inclusion criteria across cohorts. A penalized Firth logistic regression model including the same covariates was used as a sensitivity analysis to reduce small-sample bias and assess the robustness of the association. The same adjustment strategy was applied to MEP, PCF and MRC score among patients ventilated for more than 5 days. MIP was additionally analyzed as a continuous variable, expressed per 1-cmH2O decrease, using the same adjustment strategy. Effect modification by duration of mechanical ventilation was assessed using interaction terms between mechanical ventilation duration > 5 days and either continuous MIP or the predefined MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O threshold. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Secondary outcomes were analysed among patients ventilated for more than 5 days according to MIP status. Binary outcomes included ICU or hospital death, and tracheostomy. They were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. Continuous outcomes included ICU length of stay, total duration of mechanical ventilation, and ventilator-free days at day 28 when available. They were reported as medians, median differences with 95% confidence intervals, and p-values from the Mann-Whitney test.

The association between low MIP and extubation failure differed according to previous duration of mechanical ventilation. Among patients ventilated for 5 days or less, no association was observed. Among patients ventilated for more than 5 days, low MIP was associated with a higher rate of extubation failure, with failure occurring in 33% of patients with MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O and in 14% of patients with MIP > 30 cmH₂O (absolute difference + 19, 95% CI (5.7 to 32.8); p = 0.009). After adjustment, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O remained associated with extubation failure (adjusted OR 3.52, 95% CI (1.37 to 9.03); p = 0.009) (Fig. 2). This result was confirmed using Firth penalized logistic regression (adjusted OR 3.18, 95% CI (1.29 to 7.86); p = 0.012). By contrast, MEP ≤ 50 cmH₂O was not associated with extubation failure after the same adjustment strategy (adjusted OR 1.65, 95% CI 0.46 to 5.90; p = 0.43), nor was PCF ≤ 60 L/min (adjusted OR 1.49, 95% CI (0.45 to 4.94); p = 0.51), nor was MRC < 48/60 (adjusted OR 2.1, 95% CI (0.9 to 5.12); p = 0.10). Sensitivity analyses using later thresholds of mechanical ventilation duration showed a consistent gradient for MIP as presented in Table 3. This pattern was not observed for MEP, PCF or MRC score.

Additional analyses treating MIP as a continuous variable, including interaction analyses with mechanical ventilation duration, are presented in Supplementary Table S2. In exploratory secondary analyses, patients with MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O had a longer ICU length of stay, a longer duration of mechanical ventilation, and fewer ventilator-free days at day 28, whereas death and tracheostomy did not significantly differ according to MIP status (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

In this pooled multicohort individual patient data analysis, the main finding was that MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O was not uniformly associated with extubation failure. Its association depended on the prior duration of mechanical ventilation. Among patients ventilated for 5 days or less, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O was not associated with an increased risk of extubation failure. By contrast, among patients ventilated for more than 5 days, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O was associated with higher odds of extubation failure. Sensitivity analyses using the > 7-day and > 10-day thresholds showed a consistent gradient. The second important finding was the absence of a comparable signal for MEP and PCF. Thus, in this multicohort database, the main signal was driven by inspiratory weakness assessed by MIP, rather than by expiratory or cough-related measurements. The last important finding was that MIP also appeared to provide information among patients with peripheral weakness. In patients with MRC < 48/60, low MIP showed a clinically relevant but statistically imprecise association with extubation failure. This finding supports the concept that inspiratory weakness and peripheral weakness should not be considered interchangeable [12, 14, 16, 17]. MRC identifies a state of global peripheral weakness, whereas MIP specifically reflects the inspiratory reserve available at the time of extubation. These two measurements may therefore capture different dimensions of neuromuscular dysfunction and may be associated with different outcomes [33]. These results support a contextualized interpretation of neuromuscular tests [2, 6]. The clinical question may not be whether MIP predicts extubation failure in an entire heterogeneous population, but rather in which patients this measurement becomes informative [34]. A low MIP in a recently ventilated patient may be influenced by cooperation, immediate fatigue, or measurement variability [22, 24]. After several days of mechanical ventilation, the same value may reflect more clinically relevant inspiratory weakness, potentially related to ventilator-induced muscle dysfunction, systemic inflammation, immobilization, and treatments received during the ICU stay [35].

This approach is consistent with the principles of personalized medicine. Many ICU rehabilitation or inspiratory muscle training trials have applied interventions to broad and heterogeneous populations using a “one-size-fits-all” strategy [36, 37]. Several studies have shown improvements in physiological parameters, such as muscle strength or MIP, without a clear effect on major clinical outcomes such as mortality or total duration of mechanical ventilation [37]. One possible explanation is that these interventions are delivered to patients with very different risks, mechanisms of failure, and capacities to respond to treatment. Our results suggest that a simple measurement such as MIP may be more relevant when used to identify a clinical phenotype: patients ventilated for more than 5 days, in whom inspiratory weakness identifies an increased risk of extubation failure. From this perspective, neuromuscular testing should not be considered only as an isolated prognostic test [34]. It may become a stratification tool to identify patients in whom closer monitoring, preventive strategies against extubation failure, targeted respiratory muscle strengthening, or specific post-ICU follow-up could be considered. This hypothesis, however, requires prospective validation. Our study identifies the conditions under which MIP appears clinically informative. Although limited data are available on the time course of extra-diaphragmatic respiratory muscle deterioration, the 5-day threshold appears clinically relevant regarding the consequences of prolonged exposure to invasive mechanical ventilation [19, 35].

Several limitations should be discussed. First, this was a pooled analysis of available individual patient data from previously conducted cohorts, and the included studies were not selected through a systematic review process. Most cohorts were conducted within the same research network and over an extended time period, spanning from 2014 to 2024. During this period, mechanical ventilation, sedation, weaning, and particularly post-extubation respiratory support practices evolved substantially. Planned or rescue NIV and, more recently, high-flow nasal cannula were available and used according to the attending physician’s discretion, but their indication and timing were not consistently recorded across the source cohorts. Consequently, we could not distinguish prophylactic from rescue strategies or adjust the analyses for their use. This may be particularly relevant for rescue NIV, which could have modified the probability or timing of reintubation. Although cohort of origin was included as a fixed effect in the adjusted analyses to partly account for between-cohort differences, residual temporal and management-related heterogeneity cannot be excluded.

Second, neuromuscular tests were not available in all patients, largely because the original cohorts collected different sets of measurements according to their respective protocols. This resulted in test-specific nested populations, reduced the effective sample size for individual analyses, and limited direct comparisons between neuromuscular assessments.

Moreover, the selected cut-offs were based on widely used values available in the literature, but they may need to be reconsidered. Finally, our definition of extubation failure within 48 h represents a limitation. More recent studies have increasingly used longer time windows, often extending up to 7 days, thereby capturing delayed respiratory deterioration after extubation. However, these approaches generally assess a broader concept of ventilator weaning and separation from mechanical ventilation, whereas our outcome was intended to specifically capture early post-extubation failure. Consequently, our results should be interpreted within this narrower definition and may not be directly generalizable to studies using longer-term weaning outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this analysis provides new insight from a larger cohort of patients than previously published studies. Measurement procedures were based on common principles and standardized protocols, limiting measurement error as much as possible. Our analysis shows that the value of a low MIP is not constant across all clinical situations, but becomes more relevant after prolonged exposure to mechanical ventilation. It therefore proposes a more targeted way to use respiratory neuromuscular measurements before extubation, which are widely used in clinical practice, while avoiding dilution of their predictive value in overly heterogeneous populations.

Conclusion

In this pooled multicohort individual patient data analysis, MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O was not uniformly associated with extubation failure. After more than 5 days of mechanical ventilation, it was associated with a higher risk of extubation failure. Among patients with identified peripheral muscle weakness, MIP may also contribute to risk stratification. These results suggest that respiratory neuromuscular measurements should be interpreted according to clinical context, and that MIP may be particularly useful in patients exposed to prolonged mechanical ventilation.
