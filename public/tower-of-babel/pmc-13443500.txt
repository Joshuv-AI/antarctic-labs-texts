Cofactor binding interactions with reductive dehalogenase shape cobamide utilization in organohalide-respiring bacteria
Authors: Huijuan Jin, Jingjing Wang, Xiuying Li, Yiru Cui, Xiaocui Li, Huan He, Ke Shi, Siqi Huang, Tongyue Zhou, Frank E Löffler, Jun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelective utilization of certain cobamides, the vitamin B12-family enzyme cofactors, is a common physiological trait in organohalide respiration, an essential biogeochemical process relevant to global halogen cycling and the bioremediation of chlorinated contaminants. Variations in cobamide structure, particularly the lower base, can disrupt reductive dehalogenase (RDase) catalysis or even completely inactivate organohalide-respiring bacteria (OHRB). Yet, the molecular basis driving such stringent dependence on specific cobamides remains poorly understood. This study demonstrates, using competition-based in vivo assays, that RDase homologs exhibit substantially different cofactor-binding capabilities, directly mirroring the varying cobamide selectivities of their host OHRB. By combining structural modelling with molecular docking, we further identify candidate active-site features and amino acid residues predicted to contribute to cobamide binding and stability within the RDase scaffold. Collectively, our findings link cobamide-utilizing ability in OHRB to RDase architecture and establish an in silico approach for assessing cobamide preference at the enzyme level. Given the extensive diversity of naturally occurring lower bases, computational prediction of cobamide-enzyme compatibility offers a unique opportunity to guide targeted intervention and control of many host-associated and environmental microbiomes (e.g., methanogenic communities).Subject terms: Environmental microbiology, Element cycles

Introduction

Cobalamin (vitamin B12, Cbl) and its natural variants, collectively termed cobamides, serve as enzyme cofactors (aka coenzymes) to facilitate biochemically challenging reactions, such as methyl transfer, carbon skeleton rearrangement, and reductive dechlorination1–3. Cobamides play vital roles across diverse ecosystems, ranging from human gut microbiome to the open ocean, and are indispensable for climate-relevant processes including methanogenesis and CO2/H2 reductive acetogenesis (i.e., homoacetogenesis)4–7. The core structure common to these complex but structurally conserved biomolecules is a tetrapyrrole ring that chelates a nucleophilic cobalt ion (Fig. 1A). In the case of Cbl, 5’-deoxyadenosine is the upper axial ligand, and the pseudonucleotide loop carries a 5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole (DMB) lower base, which can coordinate with the cobalt center (i.e., base-on) or remain uncoordinated in the base-off configuration8. Cobamide structural variations arise primarily from the lower base moiety, which typically is a benzimidazole (Bza), nucleobase, phenol, or a related analog (Fig. 1A).

An intriguing phenomenon is that the lower base does not directly engage in enzyme catalysis but can interfere with cobamide-mediated microbial processes9. One well-documented case of lower base-induced metabolic disorders is organohalide respiration, a biogeochemically relevant process that transforms harmful organohalogens into less halogenated products with lower toxicity and/or reduced recalcitrance10–13. Access to a suitable cobamide, whether endogenously produced or salvaged from the environment, is crucial for organohalide-respiring bacteria (OHRB) to capture energy from the reductive dechlorination reactions. In Sulfurospirillum multivorans, substituting the native adenine lower base of norpseudo-B12 with a Bza analog resulted in diminished tetrachloroethene- (PCE-) dechlorinating activity14,15. The preferential use of DMB as the lower base has been reported in Dehalococcoides mccartyi (Dhc) strains, which lack the ability to synthesize a cobamide (i.e., are corrinoid auxotrophs) and are strictly dependent on exogenous Cbl to reduce the human carcinogen vinyl chloride (VC) to non-toxic ethene12,13,16. The use of 5-methoxybenzimidazolyl-cobamide ([5-OMeBza]Cba) but not Cbl caused suboptimal Dhc growth and 70–90% reduction in dechlorination rates12. A complete loss of ethene-forming activity was observed in Dhc cultures supplied with 5-hydroxybenzimidazolyl-cobamide ([5-OHBza]Cba) or those with a nucleobase lower base12,13. Contrary to Dhc’s strong preference for Cbl, the purinyl-cobamide-producing Desulfitobacterium hafniense is able to accommodate a diverse array of cobamides11,17. The attachment of various Bza analogs as an alternative lower base did not compromise Desulfitobacterium hafniense growth and dechlorination activity17. These distinct physiological responses highlight the need for an improved mechanistic understanding of cobamide utilization in relevant microbial processes, including organohalide respiration.

Central to organohalide respiratory chain is the reductive dehalogenase (RDase), which strictly depends on a cobamide cofactor to catalyze the cleavage of carbon-chlorine bonds18–21. The cobamide cofactor is deeply buried inside the substrate-binding pocket and accessible via a tunnel that extends from the RDase protein surface18–20. To date, only limited biochemical efforts have been made to investigate the impacts of lower base on organohalide respiration. Prior studies with Sulfurospirillum multivorans have suggested a role of lower base in RDase folding and maturation15,19. Non-selective utilization towards Cbl and other Bza-type cobamides was demonstrated in several recombinant Desulfitobacterium hafniense RDases17,22. By comparison, the 1,2-dichloroethane RDase of Desulfitobacterium dichloroeliminans preferred a 5-OMeBza lower base and exhibited no activity when heterologously expressed in a Cbl-producing host17. These findings demonstrate that lower base can modulate cobamide compatibility with RDases; however, the mechanisms underlying OHRB recognition and selection of certain cobamide(s), and the extent to which these physiological requirements can be attributed to RDase cofactor selectivity, are largely unknown. Interpreting the results of heterologous RDase production is complicated, as the cellular environments of the expression strain (e.g., E. coli) and native host OHRB can differ, and often hamper proper RDase assembly23.

Here, we established a blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE)-guided analytical workflow that integrates proteomics, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and computational modeling to characterize cofactor binding interactions between a RDase and cobamides carrying different lower bases (Fig. 1B). A stringent cobamide specificity has previously been shown in Dhc strains12,13,24, making their VC RDases ideal for examining cofactor selectivity. We cultivated respective Dhc strains harboring a VC RDase (BvcA, VcrA, or TceA), in the presence of a defined cobamide mixture. Following BN-PAGE separation, cobamides bound to each RDase were extracted from the resolved gel slices and identified. Dehalobacter restrictus (Dhb) strain PER-K23, another corrinoid auxotrophic OHRB expressing the PCE RDase PceA was subjected to the same analytical procedure and the cofactor profile was compared with those of the Dhc VC RDases. Molecular docking simulations assessed RDase-cobamide binding characteristics and spatial orientation of the lower base within the RDase active site. Our findings correlate RDase structural features with cobamide utilization strategies (i.e., specificity versus versatility) adopted by the host OHRB.

Results

Dhb does not show a cobamide preference as found in Dhc

Dhb strain PER-K23 culture that received Cbl dechlorinated the initial 60.1 ± 1.3 µmol PCE to stoichiometric amounts of cis-1,2-dichloroethene (cDCE) within 7 days at a rate of 253.8 ± 17.5 µM Cl- released d−1 (Fig. 2A). Other bza-type cobamides, including [5-OMeBza]Cba, [5-OHBza]Cba, 5-methylbenzimidazolyl-cobamide ([5-MeBza]Cba), and benzimidazolyl-cobamide ([Bza]Cba), also supported complete PCE-to-cDCE reductive dechlorination at similar rates (Fig. 2B–E). As shown in Fig. 2F, the control culture without cobamide addition produced only minor amounts (i.e., 4.4 ± 0.2 µmol) of trichloroethene (TCE). The discrepancy between PCE depletion and product formation suggests that a substantial fraction of the initial PCE loss resulted from abiotic processes rather than reductive dichlorination. A prolonged incubation of 4 weeks did not result in cDCE formation or further accumulation of TCE, indicating that carryover cobamide (i.e., introduced from the inoculum) was insufficient to sustain PCE-dechlorinating activity. Following complete PCE conversion to cDCE in cobamide-amended cultures, growth yields were not significantly different (P > 0.05), ranging from 1.1 ± 0.1 to 1.4 ± 0.5 × 108 cells per µmol Cl- released (Supplementary Table 1). Negligible changes in strain PER-K23 cell numbers were observed in control culture (Supplementary Table 1). These results indicate that, in contrast to Dhc’s strong favoring of Cbl, cobamides carrying DMB or a Bza analog as the lower base are functionally equivalent for Dhb.

Dhc and Dhb are capable of flexible uptake of different cobamides

The bacterial Btu system for cobamide uptake consists of the outer membrane receptor BtuB, the periplasmic binding BtuF, and the inner membrane transporters BtuC and BtuD25,26. A complete set of btu genes, except btuB, are present on the genomes of strain PER-K23 and sequenced Dhc strains13,16,27,28. The BtuFCD proteins are highly conserved among Dhc strains (i.e., 88.4–100% amino acid identity) but only share 36.5–46.9% identity with their homologs in strain PER-K23 (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine if cobamide specificity is due to Btu-mediated preferential uptake, we grew Dhc and Dhb cultures with an equimolar mixture of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba and compared the depletion of each cobamide in the medium supernatant. The lower base fragments, [DMB + H]+, [5-OMeBza + H]+ and [5-OHBza + H]+, were used as the signature ions for mass spectrometric analysis of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). The amount of each cobamide consumed (i.e., removed from the growth medium) was normalized to the corresponding value for Cbl. The calculated ratios represent the uptake efficiency of a cobamide relative to Cbl.

The cobamide mixture (18.4 nM each) enabled Dhc strain 195 to dechlorinate the initial 59.0 ± 0.7 µmol of TCE to VC and ethene at a rate of 222.2 ± 43.2 µM Cl- released d−1, comparable to the rate observed in replicate cultures supplemented with only Cbl (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Based on the changes in supernatant cobamide concentrations, Dhc strain 195 assimilated Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba in equal ratios of 1.00 ± 0.00 : 1.03 ± 0.02 : 1.00 ± 0.02 (Fig. 3A). The cobamide mixture also sustained Dhc strain BAV1 and Dhc strain GT cultures and cDCE-to-ethene reductive dechlorination occurred at rates of 198.6 ± 12.0 and 114.7 ± 9.8 µM Cl- released d−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3C–F). The remaining Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba in Dhc strain BAV1 culture supernatant were 9.2 ± 0.6, 9.1 ± 0.7 and 9.4 ± 0.9 nM, respectively, corresponding to uptake ratios of 1.00 ± 0.00 : 1.01 ± 0.01 : 0.98 ± 0.04 (Fig. 3B). Equal uptake of Cbl and its variants was also measured in Dhc strain GT cultures, and the supernatant Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba concentrations decreased to similar extents of 9.7 ± 0.5, 8.7 ± 0.5 and 9.1 ± 0.4 nM, respectively (Fig. 3C). By comparison, stable cobamide concentrations were measured in uninoculated medium over a 30-day incubation period (Supplementary Fig. 4). For strain PER-K23, robust dechlorination activity was maintained with the cobamide mixture (7.4 nM each) and reductive dechlorination of PCE to cDCE proceeded at a rate 247.0 ± 24.5 µM Cl- released d−1 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5). At the end of the incubation, 4.8 ± 0.1, 3.4 ± 0.1, and 2.5 ± 0.1 nM of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba, respectively, remained in the culture supernatants (Fig. 3D). The amount of [5-OHBza]Cba assimilated by strain PER-K23 was 23.1 ± 2.2% more than that of [5-OMeBza]Cba and nearly twice as much as that of Cbl (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate that Dhc and Dhb cells can assimilate different cobamides at nanomolar concentrations.

BN-PAGE separates catalytically-active RDase proteins

The simultaneous uptake of multiple cobamides creates a mixed intracellular pool, allowing cofactor competition for the assembly of a functional RDase. BN-PAGE using a 4–13% gradient gel (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 2) separated native Dhc and Dhb RDases from the dechlorinating biomass cultivated in the presence of a mixture of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba at 7.4 or 18.4 nM each. For Dhc strain BAV1 expressing BvcA, most cDCE-dechlorinating activity occurred in the gel slice representing the 146–242 kDa molecular weight (MW) region with a prominent ~180 kDa protein band (Fig. 4A). Dechlorination enzyme assays using this size fraction yielded 58.8 ± 6.0 nmol VC and 1.3 ± 0.2 nmol ethene, corresponding to 87.5 ± 1.1% of the total dechlorination activity recovered from all gel slices (Fig. 4A). Following the same procedure, the TceA and VcrA RDases expressed in Dhc strain 195 and strain GT, respectively, were also located in the gel slices corresponding to the MW range between 146 and 242 kDa (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). BN-PAGE separation of Dhb strain PER-K23 crude extract also showed PCE-dechlorinating activity in the gel slice representing the 146–242 kDa size range, which contained approximately 85.6 ± 0.6% of the total dechlorination activity (Fig. 4B). Other assayed gel slices only produced undetectable or negligible amounts of TCE and/or cDCE (Fig. 4B). The MWs of PceA, TceA, BvcA, and VcrA range from 45 to 65 kDa. In line with prior studies29–32, the association of reductive dechlorination activity with a much higher MW region indicates that these RDases are part of a larger protein complex.

Proteomic analysis was conducted on the gel slices showing the highest dechlorination activity. The Dhc VC RDases TceA, VcrA, and BvcA, and Dhb PCE RDase PceA were identified, and associated with these RDases were other components of the organohalide respiratory chain, such as Ni-dependent hydrogenases (Table 1). Although two distinct putative RDases were detected in each of the Dhc strain 195 and Dhb strain PER-K23 samples, their peptide abundances were 2–3 orders of magnitude lower than those of TceA and PceA. BvcA and VcrA were the only RDases present in respective gel slices generated from Dhc strain BAV1 and Dhc strain GT crude extracts. Other cobamide-dependent enzymes (e.g., ribonucleotide reductase) encoded in Dhc and Dhb genomes did not appear in any of the analyzed gel slices. Dhc and Dhb RDases exhibit distinct cofactor profiles

To identify which cobamides were utilized, we next extracted the cofactors from gel slices corresponding to VC or PCE RDase-containing bands. Since mature RDase proteins each harbor a single cobamide molecule (i.e., a 1:1 enzyme-to-cofactor ratio)33, the fraction of a specific cobamide in the extracted cofactor pool reflects the proportion of RDase proteins incorporating that cobamide. Analysis of the BvcA-containing gel slice detected Cbl (3.6 ± 0.3 pmol) and [5-OMeBza]Cba (1.1 ± 0.1 pmol) but not [5-OHBza]Cba, indicating that Cbl and [5-OMeBza]Cba accounted for 76.8 ± 0.3% and 23.2 ± 0.3% in the BvcA proteins, respectively (Fig. 5A). Cbl and [5-OMeBza]Cba were also the dominant cofactors in the TceA and VcrA proteins, accounting for 63.4–87.1% and 11.3–25.8% of the total recovered cobamides, respectively (Fig. 5B, C). [5-OHBza]Cba was detected in both the TceA- and VcrA-containing gel slices but made up only a minor fraction (1.6–10.8%) of the extracted cofactor pools (Fig. 5B, C). The BvcA, TceA, and VcrA proteins bound to Cbl were 2.5–7.7-fold more abundant than those incorporating [5-OMeBza]Cba, demonstrating Cbl as the preferred cofactor for the VC RDases. For the PCE RDase of strain PER-K23, a different cofactor composition of 12.5 ± 0.2 pmol Cbl, 18.0 ± 0.1 pmol [5-OMeBza]Cba, and 18.2 ± 0.6 pmol [5-OHBza]Cba was quantified (Fig. 5D). These results indicate that the PceA proteins incorporated [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba at similar percentages (~37%), whereas 25.7 ± 0.7% of the PceA proteins were bound to Cbl (Fig. 5D). No cobamides were detected in the gel lane loaded with a solution of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba mixture, indicating that only RDase-bound cobamides were retained during BN-PAGE separation.

Molecular docking simulation reveals cobamide-RDase binding interactions and stability

AlphaFold234 and AutoDock Vina35 were used for protein modeling and docking simulation of different cobamides to the Dhc VC RDases or the Dhb PCE RDase, each using its own grid space (Supplementary Table 3). The insertion of Cbl into BvcA resulted in a stable binding conformation with a calculated free energy ΔG of -17.5 kJ mol−1 (Fig. 6A and Table 2). Cbl is positioned at the interface adjacent to the carboxy-terminal region harboring two [4Fe-4S] clusters and forms seven hydrogen- (H-) bonds (2.1–3.2 Å bond distance) with the BvcA amino acid residues His18, Trp23, Gly286, Val300, Ala301, Thr312 and Arg317 (Fig. 6A and Table 2). The proximal [4Fe-4S] cluster is positioned 6.0 Å from the corrin ring and 8.1 Å from the cobalt center of Cbl (Fig. 6A). By comparison, docking [5-OHBza]Cba to BvcA forms fewer H-bonds and exhibits a positive ΔG of +15.5 kJ mol−1, indicating non-spontaneous and thermodynamically unfavorable interactions that hinder cofactor incorporation (Table 2). Surface hydrophobicity of the active site is expected to provide additional contacts to stabilize the cobamide cofactor within the RDase scaffold36,37. Compared with 5-OHBza, DMB is more hydrophobic owing to two methyl groups replacing a hydroxyl substituent, leading to a tighter fit into the lower base-anchoring hydrophobic cavity in BvcA (Fig. 6A). Similar hydrophobic interactions were also predicted for the TceA and VcrA, with the proximal [4Fe-4S] cluster positioned adjacent to the docked cobamide, indicating a conserved structural feature favoring Cbl as the cofactor for the VC RDases (Supplementary Fig. 9A–D). In contrast, nearly identical ΔG values between −44.4 and −46.9 kJ mol−1 were calculated for the PceA docked with different cobamides (Table 2). Cbl and [5-OHBza]Cba both establish H-bonds (1.9 to 2.8 Å) with the same set of the PceA amino acid residues, including Tyr337, Asn363, Gly366, Ser368, Leu386, Pro393, His395, and Lys459 (Fig. 6B, C, Table 2). In both models, the proximal [4Fe-4S] cluster lies 4.1–5.1 Å from the corrin ring and 6.8–6.9 Å from the cobalt center (Fig. 6B, C). Notably, within the PceA, the corrin ring is embedded in a hydrophobic region near the active site, while the lower base projects toward a solvent-exposed, more hydrophilic region (Fig. 6B, C). This orientation is consistent with a cofactor pose that does not sterically hinder substrate access, although a ternary RDase-cobamide-substrate configuration was not explicitly modeled in this study. This spatial orientation suggests that the cofactor binding of PceA is primarily governed by corrin ring interactions, with minimal contribution from the lower base.

Discussion

Organohalide respiration is a cobamide-dependent microbial metabolism that relies on RDase as the key enzyme to capture energy from reductive dechlorination reactions20,21,38. Our study reveals the intrinsic molecular interactions by which OHRB recognize and utilize specific cobamides. The Btu transport system does not account for the divergent cobamide-utilizing traits between Dhc and Dhb, as Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba, and [5-OHBza]Cba are all efficiently transported. Instead, cofactor profiles of the VC and PCE RDases directly reflect the physiological effectiveness of individual cobamides in sustaining Dhc and Dhb. Docking simulations further pinpoint active-site motifs involved in lower base orientation and cofactor stabilization that determine cobamide-RDase compatibility. Predictable cofactor-RDase interactions can serve as an indicator of cobamide requirement for OHRB growth and reductive dechlorination activity.

The uneven cobamide distribution and preferential Cbl incorporation in the Dhc VC RDases provide direct evidence that lower base structure strongly influences cofactor binding dynamics. DMB functions as a hydrophobic anchor that strengthens Cbl binding and stability within the Dhc VC RDases, whereas the poor anchoring of a hydrophilic lower base explains the previously reported inability of [5-OHBza]Cba to support VC reductive dechlorination12,13. By comparison, the Dhb PCE RDase accepts cobamides with diverse lower base structures, all of which sustain robust dechlorination activity. Effects of lower base structure on cofactor selectivity have been reported for other cobamide-dependent enzymes, including methyltransferases, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MCM)39,40, and methionine synthase (MetH)41,42, largely through in vitro analyses of purified or heterologously expressed proteins. Similar approaches have enabled the successful expression or reconstitution of several RDases from Dhb and other OHRB taxa30,31,43–47. For Dhc, however, such systems remain far more limited. Although VcrA has previously been reconstituted in vitro, the procedure involved recovery from inclusion bodies followed by denaturation, refolding, and cofactor reconstitution, raising uncertainty about how closely the recombinant RDase recapitulates the native folded state47. Another concern is that in vitro assays may not fully capture cobamide selectivity under physiological conditions, as cobamide-dependent growth is shaped not only by enzyme activity, but also by the integrated cellular processes governing cofactor acquisition and incorporation into target enzymes. In addition, cobamide preferences can also vary substantially among enzymes and organisms, potentially leading to discrepancies between in vitro activity and in vivo metabolic performance48. To address these limitations, we employed an alternative in vivo approach that directly examines cobamide incorporation into RDases within their native host strains. This strategy circumvents the limitations of heterologous expression and captures cobamide-enzyme interactions under physiologically relevant conditions. Beyond these insights, docking simulations hold promise for predicting RDase-cobamide compatibility by identifying cobamide variants most likely to support holoenzyme stability. Such predictions could ultimately facilitate heterologous RDase expression and sustain reductive dechlorination activity.

The observation that the VC RDases exhibit strict cobamide specificity, but their host Dhc strains cannot synthesize these cofactors presents a microbial ecology paradox. Cbl biosynthesis under anoxic conditions is restricted to a very narrow subset of cobamide-producing prokaryotes49,50. Dhc strains are strict anaerobes, and such physiological constraints likely impact their competitiveness in environments where Cbl is unavailable or scarce. One possible explanation is that VC RDases have evolved a strong preference for Cbl, potentially reflecting optimization of enzyme function with a specific cobamide rather than broader cobamide compatibility. This pattern should not be taken as evidence of restricted habitat breadth. Instead, it suggests that Dhc activity may be especially sensitive to the environmental availability of compatible cobamides. Under such conditions, Dhc may benefit from coexistence with certain cobamide-producers in anoxic environments where chlorinated compounds are available. Indeed, the ability to detoxify VC, a persistent groundwater contaminant, has only been identified in two OHRB taxa including Dhc, but not Dhb, suggesting that adaptation to Cbl may confer an ecological advantage. This ecological coupling reflects a co-evolutionary interdependence, wherein Dhc strains rely on Cbl supply from specific community partners. This metabolic interdependency parallels those observed in methanogenic archaea and homoacetogens, where the cross-feeding of a specific cobamide can shape community assembly and functionality51–55. In contrast to Dhc, Dhb expresses an RDase compatible with a broader range of Bza-type lower bases, indicating greater cofactor flexibility and, consequently, a higher capacity to remain active under variable cobamide conditions. Whether the Dhc VC RDases represent a more ancestral or specialized lineage among currently known RDase proteins remains unresolved; however, the cofactor preference highlights the critical role of cobamide availability as a key determinant of metabolic activity and functional distribution in OHR.

OHRB genomes often harbor a large set of non-identical RDase genes, of which only a few have been assigned a catalytic function and substrate range (http://RDaseDB.biozone.utoronto.ca)56–58. For example, Dhc strain BAV1 and Dhb strain PER-K23 possess 18 and 27 putative RDase genes, respectively, of which only bvcA (i.e., encoding BvcA) and pceA (i.e., encoding PceA), respectively, have been genetically and biochemically characterized27,59,60. Such extensive RDase gene inventories have previously been recognized as an adaptation to the great diversity of naturally occurring organohalogen compounds61–63. Our results indicate that cobamide specificity represents an additional dimension of functional differentiation among RDases. Emerging evidence points to the possibility that RDases and other cobamide-dependent enzymes from the same species may display distinct cobamide selectivity48. While growth media are generally supplemented with Cbl to satisfy microbial cofactor requirements, Cbl does not always guarantee optimal performance. Indeed, Cbl can inhibit the functionality of cobamide-dependent enzymes when mismatched with the native host’s cofactor preference14,42,64. These findings raise the possibility that the expression and activity of at least some putative RDases may require the presence of not only an appropriate chlorinated substrate but also a cobamide other than Cbl. From a bioremediation perspective, our results emphasize the importance of managing cobamide supply in contaminated environments. One promising alternative strategy is the amendment of free lower bases to guide microbial biosynthesis toward the cobamide variants compatible with a target RDase. Another approach involves engineering microbial consortia capable of producing the preferred lower base(s) and/or cobamide(s). Such interventions are promising to ensure that OHRB populations are supplied not only with adequate amounts (i.e., quantity) but also with the proper cobamide variants (i.e., quality) to maximize dechlorination rates and extents. The customization of cobamide availability is promising to fully exploit OHRB metabolic potentials.

The implications of cobamide specificity extend beyond environmental settings to host-associated ecosystems. A balanced cobamide supply is important to stabilize community structure and function, as many microbes are cobamide auxotrophs and access these cofactors through cross-feeding65. For example, supplementing Cbl to the mouse gut microbiota significantly reduced Bacteroidetes abundance while exerting minimal effects on other taxa, indicating that Cbl is not the preferred cobamide for Bacteroidetes66,67. This finding suggests that cofactor selectivity can shape community composition by influencing competition for available cobamides68. Therefore, perturbations to cobamide producers may restrict or eliminate the supply of preferred cofactors, with consequent effects on metabolic activity and the associated phenotypic traits. Together, these observations highlight that cobamide structural diversity and the alignment between cobamide supply and enzyme cofactor requirements represent a fundamental principle governing microbial cobamide utilization. This principle is especially relevant to organohalide respiration, in which the ability of keystone OHRB (e.g., Dhc) to perform reductive dechlorination depends on whether their RDases can function with cobamides carrying an appropriate lower base.

Methods

Chemicals

Polychlorinated ethenes (all ≥ 99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) or Acros Organics (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). VC and ethene (both ≥ 99.5%) were purchased from Dalian Special Gases (Dalian, Liaoning, China). Cbl in the cyano form (i.e., vitamin B12) and the lower base compounds 5-OMeBza, 5-methylbenzimidazole (5-MeBza) and Bza (all ≥ 98%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Other Bza-type cobamides were prepared via guided cobamide biosynthesis as described in the Supplementary Information. Titanium trichloride was purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Beijing Co., Ltd, and the titanium (III) citrate stock solution (100 mM) was prepared as described previously69.

Cultures and growth conditions

Dhb strain PER-K23 (DSM 9455) and Dhc strains were grown in 160-mL glass serum bottles containing 100 mL bicarbonate buffered (30 mM, pH 7.2), reduced mineral salt medium70, an N2/CO2 (80/20, v/v) headspace and Wolin vitamins without vitamin B1271. Acetate (5 mM) and H2 (10 mL, ca. 413.2 µmol) were provided as carbon source and electron donor, respectively. Electron acceptors were PCE (6 µL, ca. 59.0 µmol, 0.37 mM aqueous concentration) for Dhb strain PER-K23, TCE (6 µL, ca. 66.7 µmol, 0.51 mM aqueous concentration) for Dhc strain 195 (ATCC BAA-2100), and cDCE (5 µL, ca. 66.2 µmol, 0.59 mM aqueous concentration) for Dhc strain BAV1 (ATCC BAA-2099) and Dhc strain GT (ATCC BAA-2098). In addition, Dhb strain PER-K23 cultures were supplied with 0.5 g L−1 peptone (>99%, Macklin Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to meet its growth requirement for specific amino acids27. Triplicate bottles received an inoculum (1%, v/v) from a vitamin B12-amended, freshly grown Dhb or Dhc cultures and a single type of cobamide (7.4 nM [~10 µg L−1] for Dhb strain PER-K23 or 18.4 nM [~25 µg L−1] for Dhc), or a cobamide mixture containing equal molar concentrations of Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba (7.4 nM each for Dhb or 18.4 nM each for Dhc). Different initial cobamide concentrations were used for Dhc strains and Dhb strain PER-K23 based on the empirically optimized concentrations routinely used to support robust growth and complete dechlorination in each culture system63. All bottles were incubated statically with the stopper up at 30 °C in the dark. These experiments were performed in actively growing batch cultures, in which biomass and the abundance of assembled RDase proteins varied over the course of incubation. Accordingly, dechlorination rates are reported as culture-level rates (μM Cl- released d−1) calculated from the linear phase of the formation of a dechlorination product, rather than as biomass- or protein-normalized specific activities.

TaqMan-based quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay

One mL of strain PER-K23 culture suspension was collected and filtered through 0.22 μm filtration membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Genomic DNA was extracted from the filter using the TIANamp Soil DNA Kit (TIANGEN Inc., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified on a QuantStudioTM 3 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using primers Dhb881F 5′-CGACGCAACGCGAAGAA-3′ and Dhb1002R 5′-CGAAGGGCACTCCCATATCTC-3′ and probe Dhb905probe 5′-6FAM-AAGGCTTGACATCCAACT-MGB-3′72. Each qPCR reaction (25 µL) contained 12.5 µL of 2 × Premix TaqTM master mix (Takara Bio, Beijing, China), 200 nM each primer, 400 nM probe, 2 µL template DNA, 0.25 µL of 50 × ROX reference Dye II (Takara Bio) and nuclease-free water. The qPCR cycle conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s at 95 °C and 34 s at 60 °C. Standard curves were generated using independent dilutions of plasmid DNA containing a partial sequence of Dhb 16S rRNA gene (GenBank accession number: Y10164.1).

Cobamide biosynthesis and purification

Sporomusa sp. strain KB-1 was used to produce [5-OMeBza]Cba, [5-MeBza]Cba and [Bza]Cba via guided biosynthesis73. [5-OHBza]Cba was natively synthesized in Methanosarcina barkeri strain Fusaro (DSM 804)74. Strain KB-1 culture and strain Fusaro culture were grown in 2.2-L glass serum bottles containing 1.6 L bicarbonate buffered (30 mM, pH 7.2), reduced mineral salts medium, Wolin vitamins without vitamin B12 and a N2/CO2 (80/20, v/v) headspace70. Strain KB-1 culture was supplied with methanol (15 mM), betaine (50 mM) and a Bza-type compound (200 μM). Strain Fusaro culture received methanol (46 mM) as the sole substrate. After incubation at 30 °C for 7–10 days, cells were precipitated by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min and rinsed twice with sterile medium. Cobamides were extracted from the cell pellets following the KCN protocol12,24. The cobamide-containing fractions were manually collected using an Agilent 1260 II high performance liquid chromatography followed by purification using the Sep-Pak C18 cartridges (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) as described24. Absorbance of cobamide solution was measured at 361 nm using a UV-2600i spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Cobamide concentration was calculated using a molar extinction coefficient of 28,060 M−1 cm−1 as described previously64.

Cobamide analysis

The solubilized cobamides in culture supernatants were extracted as described63. For analysis of protein-bound cobamides, each RDase-containing gel slice was transferred to a 2-mL microcentrifuge tube containing 1 mL extraction solution (90% [v/v] methanol, 50 mM acetic acid and 20 mM KCN, pH 5.0) and incubated at 60 °C for 2 h in the dark11. After centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new 2-mL microcentrifuge tube and evaporated using an Eppendorf Vacufuge Plus vacuum concentrator (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) rotated at 1000 rpm and 45 °C for 2 h. The dried residue was re-suspended in 300 μL deionized water and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at room temperature to precipitate Coomassie Blue dye and insoluble gel debris. The extracted cobamides were analyzed using a VanquishTM ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system in tandem with a Q ExactiveTM Hybrid Quadrupole-OrbitrapTM mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as described63. Raw mass spectral data were processed using the XcaliburTM v3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TraceFinder v2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with default settings. The secondary adducts at m/z 147.093 ([DMB + H]+ ion), 149.072 ([5-OMeBza + H]+ ion) and 135.056 ([5-OHBza + H]+ ion) were selected to quantify Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). Cobamide concentrations were determined based on integrated peak area of the [lower base+H]+ ion in comparison to calibration curves generated using a series of standard solutions. The detection limits (5 μL injection volume) were 50 ng L−1 for Cbl and 100 ng L−1 for [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba (Supplementary Fig. 2). The quantification limits (5 μL injection volume) were 100 ng L−1 for Cbl and 200 ng L−1 for [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Preparation of crude protein extracts

Prior to transferring the bottles into an anoxic chamber (Coy Laboratory, Grass Lake, MI, USA), culture headspace was flushed with a gentle flow (~1 L min−1) of filter-sterilized N2 for 2 min to remove dechlorination product(s). Unless otherwise mentioned, the following procedures were performed inside an anoxic chamber. Approximately 700 mL of Dhb or Dhc culture suspensions was combined and dispensed into 500-mL O-ring screw-top centrifuge tubes, which were tightly closed and then transferred out of the anoxic chamber. Cells were precipitated by centrifugation at 14,400 × g for 40 min at 4 °C. The centrifuge tubes were transferred back to the anoxic chamber and the supernatants were decanted. Cell pellets were gently rinsed twice with 0.5 mL pH 7.0 Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM) and the re-suspended cells were transferred into 2-mL O-ring screw-top microfuge tubes containing 300 mg of 75-μm diameter glass beads and 0.5 mL pH 7.2 anoxic sample buffer containing 50 mM Bis-Tris, 50 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) glycerol, 0.001% (w/v) Ponceau S (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and 1% (w/v) digitonin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA)75. Cells were disrupted in 15 beating cycles using an Omni Bead Ruptor 24 homogenizer (Omni International, Kennesaw, GA, USA). Each cycle consisted of 30 s of bead beating at an intensity of 3.7 m s−1 and 2 min of ice incubation. Cell debris was precipitated by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatants were transferred to new 2.0-mL O-ring screw-top microcentrifuge tubes and protein concentrations were estimated using the Bradford assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard76. The crude protein extracts were amended with the NativePAGETM 5% G-250 sample additive (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to a final concentration of 0.25% (w/v) and stored at 4 °C.

Assembly and operation of BN-PAGE

The non-denaturing BN-PAGE can resolve respiratory enzyme complexes while retaining the native conformation of the individual components, including protein-ligand interactions31,77. The reagents and solutions used for gel preparation were listed in Table S2. The AB-3 mix consists of 7 mL of 30% (w/v) acrylamide/bis-acrylamide solution (29 : 1, w/v) (Sangon Biotech), 9.75 mL of 40% (w/v) acrylamide/bis-acrylamide solution (37.5 : 1, w/v) (Sangon Biotech) and 3.75 mL of deionized H2O. The 4 × native gel buffer (pH 7.0) was prepared in water and contained the protease inhibitor 6-aminohexanoic acid (200 mM) and imidazole-HCl. The 10% ammonium persulfate (APS) and tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED; Sinopharm, Beijing, China) solutions were used as the polymerizing reagents, and were not added to the gel solutions until the casting process was started. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, a Hoefer SG gradient maker (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), a magnetic stir bar, two vertical glass gel plates, a peristaltic pump, and other essential apparatuses (e.g., a lifter) were assembled to cast the gels. The gradient gel (4–13% acrylamide) was cast as follows. First, the 13% acrylamide solution was filled in the mixing chamber containing a magnetic stir bar, and the 4% acrylamide solution was gently poured into the reservoir chamber. Shortly before the tube clip and the connecting valve between the chambers were turned on, the 10% APS and TEMED solutions were added to both chambers and the stir bar was operated at ~400 rpm. The mixed gel was slowly cast into a SureCastTM Handcast glass-plate sandwich (18 × 24 cm, 1.0 mm thick; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) using a peristaltic pump. The top of the gradient gel was filled with 1 mL of water-saturated isobutanol, which was removed after the gel was completely polymerized at room temperature for 30 min. The glass-plate sandwich containing the prepared gradient gel was then transferred inside an anoxic chamber and stabilized at 30 °C for 12 h. The 3.5% stacking gel solution was freshly prepared on ice in the anoxic chamber and quickly pipetted on the top of the separating gel. A 5-well sample loading comb with a 100 μL load volume for each well was inserted into the stacking gel solution, which was incubated for at least 60 min to allow the gel to be fully polymerized.

Following cassette assembly and installation, separation of the RDase-containing protein complex was performed inside an anoxic chamber. The cathode chamber of the electrophoresis device was filled with 200 mL cathode buffer (50 mM tricine-HCl and 7.5 mM imidazole-HCl, pH 7.0) containing 0.02% (w/v) Coomassie Blue G-250 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the anode chamber was filled with 600 mL anode buffer (25 mM imidazole-HCl, pH 7.0). The ladder lane was loaded with 10 μL of 66–669 kDa protein standard (Real-Times Biotechnology, Beijing, China). An aliquot equivalent to ~30 μg crude proteins was loaded to each sample gel lane. The control lane was loaded with 40 μL of a solution containing Cbl, [5-OMeBza]Cba and [5-OHBza]Cba (1.5 nM each). Replicate lanes were prepared for gel staining to visualize protein bands and served as the references to cut gel slices for subsequent analyses. Gel chamber was maintained at approximately 10 °C using external cooling during the entire operation. The electrophoresis voltage was set at 100 V until the samples completely migrated into the gradient gel and then increased to 150 V, which was maintained for 60 min at a maximum current of 16 mA. The ladder and sample lanes were excised from the gel using a sterile scalpel and stained using the Fast Coomassie G-250 Staining protocol78. The molecular weight (MW) range of the proteins in a gel slice was estimated from a standard curve generated by interpolating the log MW of individual ladder proteins versus the ratio of the distance of gel slice’s upper and bottom edges to the Coomassie Blue dye front.

Dechlorination enzyme assay

The RDase-containing gel slices were identified as described31,79. Briefly, an unstained sample lane was aligned with the stained ladder lane and a stained replicate sample lane inside the anoxic chamber. Based on the visible protein bands in the stained sample lane, the unstained sample lane was excised into multiple gel slices using a sterile scalpel. Each gel slice was transferred to a 4-mL crimp-top glass vial filled with 1.0 mL assay buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.3), 2 mM titanium citrate and 2 mM methyl viologen (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA)31. Reductive dechlorination reactions were initialized by adding 0.39 μmol PCE (~0.1 mM final aqueous concentration), 0.44 μmol TCE (~0.4 mM final aqueous concentration) or 0.53 μmol cDCE (~0.5 mM final aqueous concentration) from a 1.2–2.4 mM stock solution. Vials receiving 40 μL (~18 μg) Dhc or Dhb crude proteins were used as positive controls. All vials were incubated at 30 °C in the anoxic chamber for 24 h prior to the analysis of dechlorination products.

Peptide extraction and mass spectrometric analysis

The gel slices showing the highest dechlorination activity were excised and suspended in 500 μL lysis buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM dithiothreitol, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). Samples were incubated at 100 °C (water bath) for 15 min and then cooled to ambient temperature. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was transferred into an ultrafiltration tube (Microcon units, 10 kD). Protein rinsing was repeatedly performed three times by adding 500 μL urea buffer (8 M in 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) and removing filtrate at 12,000 × g for 15 min. An aliquot of 1 mL iodoacetamide (50 mM) in urea buffer was added to block reduced cysteine residues and the treated samples were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in darkness. The obtained filter containing proteins was washed three times with 1 mL ammonium bicarbonate buffer (25 mM) for 10 min. Then 500 μL trypsin buffer (6 μg trypsin in 40 μL ammonium bicarbonate) was added and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. Finally, the peptides in filtrate were dried and dissolved in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The extracted peptides were analyzed using an Easy nLCTM 1200 nanoflow liquid chromatography in tandem with a Q-Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. For LC-MS analysis, samples (2 µL) were loaded onto a C18 precolumn (100 µm inner diameter × 20 mm length, 5 µm particle size; Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany) connected with an analytical C18 column (75 µm inner diameter × 150 mm length, 3 µm particle size; Dr. Maisch GmbH). Peptides were eluted at a flow rate of 0.3 μL min−1 using 0.1% formic acid (v/v) in water (eluent A) and 0.1% formic acid in 90 : 10 acetonitrile : water (v/v) (eluent B) as mobile phases. The column was initially equilibrated with 98% eluent A/ 2% eluent B, which was linearly changed to 65% eluent A/ 35% eluent B following sample injection over a 48-min time span. Then the gradient decreased immediately to 10% eluent A/ 90% eluent B over a 5-min time span followed by a 7-min hold before the column was re-equilibrated to initial conditions. Data were acquired in positive ion mode with a scan range of 300–1800 m/z, a mass resolution of 70,000, and an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 106. Normalized collision energy was set at 30 eV. Twenty MS/MS scans were obtained per full MS cycle with a secondary mass resolution of 17,500 and a target AGC value of 1 × 105. The raw data were analyzed using MaxQuant v1.6.1.080 with a precursor tolerance of 4.5 ppm and a MS/MS tolerance of 20 ppm. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was included as a fixed modification. Protein N-terminal acetylation and oxidation of methionine were included as variable modifications. The minimum 7 amino acids for a peptide, and at least 1 unique peptide was required for protein identification. False discovery rate (FDR) for peptide and protein identification was set to 1%. Eventually, proteins were identified by searching against the coding sequences derived from the genomes of Dhb strain PER-K23 (GenBank accession no. CP007033), Dhc strain BAV1 (NC_009455), Dhc strain 195 (NC_002936) and Dhc strain GT (CP001924).

Protein structure prediction and molecular docking

RDase amino acid sequences were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. The TAT signal peptides were predicted using SignalP 5.081 and removed prior to structure prediction. RDase structures were predicted using the AlphaFold2 (v2.3)34. Cbl and [5-OHBza]Cba were selected as ligand molecules in the subsequent docking experiments. The cofactor-binding site was predicted using the COACH and PrankWeb servers82,83. Cobamide structures presenting the “base-off” conformation were obtained from the RCSB PDB database, and geometry-optimized using Chem3D Pro to minimize spatial conformational energy. Protein pretreatments, including removal of water molecules, addition of hydrogen atoms, and calculation of Gasteiger charges, were carried out with AutoDock Tool 4.2.184. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina v2.2.135 with default parameters, except that the exhaustiveness was increased to 32 and each docking run was repeated 50 times to improve sampling robustness. The enzyme structures were kept rigid with no flexible residues, and docking grid dimensions are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Docking results are reported as binding affinity values in kJ mol−1 derived from compound-protein interactions. A stepwise docking strategy was employed, in which a cobamide was first docked into the modeled RDase structure, followed by the addition of two [4Fe-4S] clusters positioned based on a conserved binding motif, to complete the RDase-cobamide assembly and validate the predicted active-site configuration. Binding interactions, including polar bonds, bond distances, and hydrophobic interactions, were analyzed and visualized using BioVia Discovery Studio v4.5 (BIOVIA, San Diego, California, US) and PyMOL v3.0.3 (Schrodinger, LLC).

Analytical methods

Ethene and chlorinated ethenes were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatography equipped with a flame ionization detector and an Agilent DB-624 capillary column (0.32 mm inner diameter × 60 m length, 1.80 µm film thickness)85. Chloride release was calculated from the quantified dechlorination products (e.g., cDCE, VC, ethene) by summing the Cl- equivalents liberated at each reductive dechlorination step.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates. Data was presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs). All statistical analyses were performed with one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Origin 2021 (v9.8.0) was used for all statistical analyses, and Microsoft Excel (v16.54) was used for data-handling.
