The interplay of growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement on adolescent well-being in China: a multi-cohort study
Authors: Xiaoxiao Tang, Rui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis multi-cohort study investigated the independent and interactive effects of growth mindset, social support networks, and academic engagement strategies on the psychological well-being of Chinese middle school students (N = 1,237, across three cohorts). Participants ranged in age from 12 to 15 years, with a gender distribution of 52% females and 48% males. A longitudinal design with three waves of data collection spanning three years was employed. Structural equation modeling (SEM) analyses revealed that growth mindset, social support from family, friends, and teachers, and academic engagement strategies all had significant positive main effects on student well-being. A significant interaction effect emerged between growth mindset and friend support, indicating that friend support was particularly beneficial for students with a weaker growth mindset. These findings highlight the importance of fostering a growth mindset and cultivating supportive social networks to promote the psychological well-being of adolescents during a critical developmental period in Chinese education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03719-4.Keywords: Growth mindset, Social support networks, Academic engagement strategies, Psychological well-being, Adolescents, Longitudinal study, Multilevel SEM, Chinese education

Introduction

Adolescent psychological well-being is a major concern in China, particularly in light of recent educational reforms like the “Double Reduction” policy [1, 2]. This initiative aims to reduce academic workloads and promote holistic student development. In this changing landscape, it is crucial to understand the factors supporting student well-being, a construct vital for resilience and overall health [3–5]. While psychological well-being is a complex, eudaimonic concept [6], navigating the unique challenges of China’s demanding educational system [7, 8] requires identifying key protective factors.

Research suggests that internal resources, such as a growth mindset [9–12], and external assets, like social support [13–15] and academic engagement [16–18], are all important for well-being. However, a critical knowledge gap exists. Most previous research has examined these variables in isolation, often using cross-sectional designs that cannot capture developmental changes over time. Therefore, we lack a clear, empirical understanding of how these key psychological and social factors operate together to influence student well-being, especially within the new policy landscape in China [19, 20].

The present study directly addresses this gap and offers three unique contributions. First, it moves beyond examining these factors in isolation by proposing an integrated model that tests the interplay of growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement. Second, it employs a multi-cohort, longitudinal design to capture the dynamic developmental changes in these relationships during adolescence, a significant advance over previous cross-sectional studies. Finally, it provides timely, evidence-based data that can directly inform the implementation of the “Double Reduction” policy [1, 2] and guide educators in creating environments that promote genuine psychological well-being [7, 8]. This research aims to provide a robust model for supporting a new generation of students in a rapidly changing educational system.

Review of existing research

Dimensions and influences on psychological well-being

This study defines psychological well-being using the eudaimonic perspective, which focuses on meaning, self-actualization, and the fulfillment of one’s potential, rather than simply the presence of positive feelings. Ryff’s seminal model provides a comprehensive framework for this perspective, identifying six key dimensions: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth [6, 21]. Understanding how these dimensions are influenced by various factors is crucial, especially during adolescence, a period marked by significant developmental changes.

Strong interpersonal relationships are consistently linked to better mental health outcomes and greater life satisfaction [22]. Holopainen et al. [23] emphasized the role of social competence in enhancing adolescents’ psychological well-being, suggesting that the ability to form positive relationships is pivotal. Similarly, Corsano et al. [24] found that supportive social connections contribute significantly to well-being, while isolation can have detrimental effects. However, these studies primarily focus mostly on Western contexts, potentially limiting their applicability in collectivist societies like China, where social dynamics and familial expectations differ markedly.

In the Chinese cultural context, the family unit often plays a central role in an individual’s life, and parental expectations regarding academic success are particularly pronounced [19, 20, 25]. Cripps and Zyromski [19] highlighted that while active parental involvement can foster a supportive environment, excessive pressure to perform academically may lead to increased stress and reduced well-being among adolescents. This suggests a complex interplay between parental support and psychological health, where the quality and nature of involvement are critical factors. Emotional intelligence, defined as the ability to understand and manage one’s emotions, has been linked to higher levels of psychological well-being [26]. Guerra-Bustamante et al. [26] found that adolescents with greater emotional intelligence reported better mental health outcomes. Salami [27] further observed that emotional intelligence moderates the relationship between personality traits and well-being. However, these studies often rely on self-reported measures, which can be influenced by cultural norms regarding emotional expression. In collectivist cultures, where open emotional expression may be less common, adolescents might underreport emotional difficulties, potentially skewing results [27].

Resilience—the capacity to cope with stress and adversity—is another critical component of psychological well-being [28]. Sagone and De Caroli [28] demonstrated that resilient adolescents are better equipped to handle challenges, leading to improved mental health. Similarly, a personal growth initiative—defined as an individual’s active and intentional involvement in their own development and self-improvement—correlates with higher well-being and lower psychological distress [29]. However, these constructs may be influenced by environmental factors such as socio-economic status, as adolescents in under-resourced communities might face barriers that hinder personal growth and resilience-building efforts [30].

Although these studies offer valuable insights, several limitations warrant consideration. Further complexity arises from different conceptualizations of well-being itself. While this study adopts the eudaimonic approach focused on meaning and functioning, other prominent models adopt a hedonic perspective, which prioritizes subjective happiness and life satisfaction. This distinction is important because the relationship between these different forms of well-being can vary across cultures. For example, some studies find that happiness is a significant predictor of overall well-being [31], yet this link may be weaker in collectivist societies that prioritize collective goals over individual happiness [32]. Such discrepancies highlight the need for theoretical clarity and a close examination of socio-cultural contexts. Furthermore, many studies rely on cross-sectional designs, limiting the ability to infer causality [23, 28]. The broader socio-cultural environment in China, with its intense focus on academic achievement, creates substantial pressure for adolescents [7, 8, 19] and may influence how factors like social support and resilience interact to affect well-being. For example, cultural stigma around mental health might discourage adolescents from seeking support, exacerbating feelings of isolation [22].

Moreover, environmental mastery—the ability to manage one’s surroundings—is impacted by socio-economic factors [30]. Adolescents from lower socio-economic backgrounds may face additional challenges that hinder their psychological well-being, such as limited access to educational resources or safe recreational spaces [4]. These environmental constraints can impede personal growth and the development of autonomy, further affecting well-being [33]. Targeted interventions are needed to address these challenges and enhance psychological well-being. Mindfulness practices, such as meditation, reduce anxiety and improve emotional regulation [34], while cognitive-behavioral strategies help reframe negative thought patterns to boost life satisfaction and reduce distress [35]. Positive psychology techniques, like gratitude exercises and strengths-based approaches, significantly enhance well-being [36]. Implementing these strategies, especially in under-resourced or high-pressure environments, can help adolescents better navigate socio-cultural stressors and improve overall well-being.

Overall, the psychological well-being of adolescents is influenced by a complex interplay of social relationships, emotional intelligence, resilience, and personal growth initiatives, all of which are embedded within broader socio-cultural and environmental contexts. While individual factors are important, considering the cultural emphasis on academic success, familial expectations, and socio-economic conditions provides a more comprehensive understanding of adolescent well-being. Addressing these dimensions collectively is crucial for developing effective interventions tailored to the specific needs of diverse populations. Future research should adopt longitudinal and culturally sensitive approaches to untangle these complex relationships and inform practice. Therefore, for the purposes of this study and in alignment with the eudaimonic perspective, psychological well-being is defined and operationalized according to Ryff’s six core dimensions: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth [3, 6].

Growth mindset and well-being

A substantial body of research highlights the positive impact of a growth mindset—the belief that abilities can be developed through effort and learning—on both academic achievement and psychological well-being [9, 12, 37, 38]. Students who hold this belief are more likely to embrace challenges and persist through setbacks, which translates into greater motivation and improved grades [10]. It is important to recognize, however, that this belief system is not necessarily global; it is often domain-specific, applying differently to areas such as academic abilities, artistic talent, or social skills. A growth mindset can also act as a powerful buffer against structural disadvantages, as research shows that students from lower-income backgrounds who adopt this belief can achieve academic success on par with their more affluent peers [11].

The link between a growth mindset and well-being is often explained by its role in fostering key psychological resources. Work with Chinese students, for example, demonstrates that a growth mindset enhances resilience, which in turn promotes better mental health and school engagement [12]. Separately, growth mindset has also been found to be a positive predictor of school engagement [12]. This link to resilience [12] provides a key theoretical bridge for this study. A growth mindset is not just a belief; it is an internal psychological resource that shapes how students cope with adversity [39]. Students with a growth mindset tend to adopt mastery-oriented goals and view setbacks as temporary, which are effective internal coping mechanisms [10]. In contrast, students with a fixed mindset are more likely to see failure as an indictment of their permanent ability, leading to “helpless” coping strategies and increased vulnerability to distress [9]. This suggests that an individual’s mindset may determine their reliance on different types of resources. Specifically, a student’s internal belief system (their mindset) likely interacts with the external resources available to them, such as their social support network. This principle also extends beyond beliefs about academic intelligence. A longitudinal study by Zhao et al. [40] found that a growth mindset of socioeconomic status—that is, the belief that one’s social and economic standing is malleable and can be improved through effort—predicted higher subjective well-being over time.

However, the benefits of a growth mindset are not unconditional and depend on a variety of contextual factors. Its effectiveness can be limited by external stressors or a lack of resources, as simply encouraging individual effort may be insufficient to overcome significant systemic barriers [39, 41]. To be effective, mindset-focused initiatives must be aligned with supportive institutional practices and policies; otherwise, students may become frustrated when increased effort does not yield expected results [42]. The domain-specific nature of mindsets is also critical. Chan et al. [43], for instance, found that among adolescents in Hong Kong, a growth mindset about academic ability was linked to life satisfaction, whereas a growth mindset about social skills was more closely related to emotional stability, suggesting that interventions may need to be highly targeted.

The surrounding environment plays a crucial role as well. In demanding professional training programs, such as for veterinary students, a growth mindset is associated with better psychological well-being, yet researchers note that the stressful nature of the program necessitates additional support mechanisms for students to truly benefit [44]. Furthermore, the universal applicability of growth mindset interventions has been questioned from a cultural perspective. In collectivist societies, where academic success is often tied to familial expectations, an emphasis on individual effort may need to be carefully adapted to align with prevailing cultural norms [18–20, 45]. For example, a message focused solely on individual striving might inadvertently diminish the perceived importance of group harmony and interdependence. In such contexts, a student’s failure may be interpreted not just as an individual shortcoming but as a failure to meet collective expectations, inducing shame rather than a motivation to persist. Therefore, effective mindset interventions in collectivist cultures may need to frame personal effort as a means of contributing to group goals—such as bringing honor to one’s family or school—rather than as a purely individualistic pursuit of success.

In summary, while a growth mindset generally promotes resilience and well-being, its effectiveness is moderated by the broader environment, the specific domain in question, and the cultural context. Recognizing these important nuances is crucial for educators and policymakers who aim to effectively implement growth mindset principles to support the well-being of diverse student populations. Building on this, recent developments in mindset theory emphasize that a student’s personal mindset does not operate in a vacuum but interacts powerfully with their social context [39]. The social environment, including the support available from peers and adults, can either amplify or buffer the effects of a student’s beliefs about their intelligence.

Therefore, a key theoretical question is how external resources, like social support, might interact with the internal resource of a growth mindset. It is plausible to hypothesize a compensatory relationship: students with a strong growth mindset may possess robust internal coping mechanisms and a resilient attributional style, making them less dependent on external validation to maintain well-being when facing challenges. Conversely, students with a weaker (or more fixed) mindset may lack these internal psychological resources, making them more vulnerable. For these students, the presence of strong social support may be especially critical, acting as an external buffer that compensates for this vulnerability and protects their well-being. This study was designed to test this potential interaction directly.

Social support and well-being

Social support is fundamental to psychological health, encompassing emotional, informational, and practical assistance from one’s social network [13]. These forms of support—emotional empathy, guidance, and tangible help—collectively enhance an individual’s ability to cope with stress and maintain mental well-being [46, 47]. Emotional support, which involves empathy and understanding from others, has been shown to buffer against the adverse effects of stress by fostering a sense of belonging and security [14, 48]. For instance, individuals perceiving high levels of emotional support from friends and family experience less psychological distress, highlighting the importance of close relationships in promoting emotional stability [14]. Informational support provides necessary advice and guidance, helping individuals navigate complex situations and reduce anxiety, while instrumental support alleviates the burden of everyday challenges through tangible aid.

An essential aspect of social support is the individual’s perception of its availability. Perceived social support—the belief that one is cared for and has assistance available—is often more influential on psychological well-being than the actual support received [49]. This perception correlates positively with better mental health outcomes across different ages [50] and contexts [51, 52], and can be explained by other psychological factors like mindfulness [53]. It has also been shown to buffer negative outcomes like burnout [54] and work-family conflict [55], underscoring the subjective nature of social support’s benefits. Moreover, research suggests that received social support is most beneficial when it aligns with the recipient’s specific needs, emphasizing the importance of the quality and context of the support provided [56]. The “buffering hypothesis” [13] is central to this study’s framework, but its effectiveness may depend on the individual’s internal characteristics. Social support is theorized to be most effective when an individual is under stress [14, 56]. This stress, however, can be generated or exacerbated by internal vulnerabilities, such as a student’s mindset. For an adolescent with a fixed mindset, a poor grade is a significant psychological threat, and the resulting distress requires buffering [9, 39]. In this situation, perceived social support—knowing that family, friends, and teachers provide a “safety net”—may be a critical external resource that compensates for the student’s lack of internal, mastery-oriented coping mechanisms. Conversely, a student with a strong growth mindset may already possess the internal cognitive tools to reframe the same poor grade as a learning opportunity, making them less dependent on this external buffering to maintain their well-being.

Parental support often serves as a foundational source of security and emotional stability [19]. However, its nature is critical; longitudinal studies show that supportive parental involvement promotes academic engagement and well-being, but excessive pressure can undermine these positive effects, especially in academically demanding cultures [57]. As adolescents seek greater autonomy, peers become an increasingly vital source of support for emotional validation and identity exploration. Strong friendships provide validation, companionship, and a sense of belonging, which are crucial for navigating the social and emotional challenges of middle school [24]. Wentzel [58] found that perceived support from peers was a significant predictor of adolescents’ motivation and positive social behavior in school. Finally, teacher support is a key contextual factor that fosters a positive school climate and academic self-concept. When students perceive their teachers as caring and supportive, they report higher levels of school engagement and emotional well-being [57, 58].

Despite this clear evidence, key gaps in the literature remain. Much of the research has been conducted in Western contexts, raising questions about the generalizability of findings to collectivist cultures like China, where family structures and social expectations differ significantly [59]. Furthermore, while the independent effects of support from parents, peers, and teachers are well-documented, less is known about how these different sources of support interact with crucial individual psychological factors, such as a student’s mindset. As we have outlined, there is a strong theoretical basis for hypothesizing such an interaction, yet it remains largely untested empirically. Understanding these complex dynamics is essential for designing effective interventions and provides the central rationale for the present study.

Academic engagement and well-being

Student engagement is a key predictor of academic success and psychological well-being [16, 60, 61]. It is widely conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions [16]. Behavioral engagement involves participation and effort; emotional engagement refers to students’ feelings of interest, enthusiasm, and belonging; and cognitive engagement involves deep processing and strategic learning. While this framework is foundational, the present study adopts the specific and robust conceptualization of academic engagement as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind characterized by three dimensions: Vigor, Dedication, and Absorption [62].

Specifically, Vigor is defined by high levels of energy and mental resilience while studying; Dedication refers to being strongly involved in one’s studies and experiencing a sense of significance, enthusiasm, and pride; and Absorption is characterized by being fully concentrated and happily engrossed in one’s academic work [62], a concept closely related to ‘flow’ [63]. This model is highly relevant for school settings as it captures an active and positive state of psychological connection to learning, making it an excellent framework for examining links to psychological well-being.

The relationship between academic engagement and psychological well-being is multifaceted and bidirectional [64]. One key mechanism is the fulfillment of basic psychological needs as outlined in Self-Determination Theory [65, 66]. When students are engaged, they experience a sense of competence through mastering academic tasks (vigor), autonomy by making choices in their learning, and relatedness by connecting with peers and teachers, which enhances their sense of significance (dedication). This fulfillment is a direct contributor to overall well-being.

Another mechanism involves the development of a positive academic identity [17, 67]. Engaged students often view themselves as capable learners, which boosts self-esteem and life satisfaction [68]. The persistence and effort characteristic of vigor and the pride associated with dedication foster a resilient academic self-concept, which can reduce anxiety and contribute to better mental health outcomes [30, 69, 70]. Furthermore, the positive emotional experiences inherent in engagement—such as the interest and enjoyment that fuel absorption—are crucial. According to the Broaden-and-Build Theory [5], these emotions expand cognitive and behavioral repertoires, enabling students to build personal resources that contribute to well-being [71, 72]. Conversely, a lack of engagement can lead to feelings of boredom and alienation, which are associated with negative mental health outcomes [73].

The nature of academic engagement and its impact on well-being can vary significantly across different populations due to cultural, socioeconomic, and contextual factors. In collectivist cultures like China, academic engagement is often influenced by societal expectations and family obligations [20, 25, 59]. Students may engage in academic activities not solely out of personal interest but to fulfill familial duties. While this pressure can increase behavioral effort, it may diminish the intrinsic pride of dedication or the focused enjoyment of absorption, potentially leading to heightened stress and reduced emotional well-being [74]. This suggests that the source of motivation—whether intrinsic or extrinsic—can modulate the relationship between engagement and well-being.

Socioeconomic status (SES) also influences academic engagement [75]. Students from lower SES backgrounds may face barriers such as limited access to resources and support, which can hinder engagement and negatively impact well-being [76]. However, supportive school environments, which can include specific pedagogies of engagement [77], and positive teacher-student relationships can mitigate these effects by fostering a sense of belonging and competence, thereby promoting vigor and dedication [57, 58, 78, 79].

The present study and hypotheses

The preceding review highlights that while growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement are all independently linked to adolescent well-being, several gaps remain. First, much of the research examines these factors in isolation, overlooking their potential interactive effects. Second, there is a need for more longitudinal research that can track these dynamics over time, particularly within the unique, high-pressure academic context of China. This study aims to address these gaps by integrating these three key areas into a single, comprehensive model.

The primary goal of this multi-cohort longitudinal study is to examine the independent and interactive effects of growth mindset, social support networks (from family, friends, and teachers), and academic engagement on the psychological well-being of Chinese middle school students. We sought to understand whether social support acts as a buffer, potentially moderating the relationship between a student’s mindset and their well-being. Based on the theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence reviewed, we formulated the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): We predicted positive main effects for all key variables.

H1a: A stronger growth mindset will be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

H1a: A stronger growth mindset will be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

H1b: Greater perceived social support from family, friends, and teachers will each be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

H1b: Greater perceived social support from family, friends, and teachers will each be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

H1c: Higher levels of academic engagement (defined by vigor, dedication, and absorption) will be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

H1c: Higher levels of academic engagement (defined by vigor, dedication, and absorption) will be positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): We predicted a significant interaction effect where the positive association between social support and psychological well-being would be stronger for students with a weaker (i.e., more fixed) growth mindset. This is based on the premise that external resources like social support may be particularly protective for individuals who possess fewer internal psychological resources [39].

Collectively, these hypotheses form our hypothesized model, which is depicted in Fig. 1. The model posits that psychological well-being is directly predicted by growth mindset, the three sources of social support, and academic engagement. Critically, the model also specifies that the pathways from social support to well-being are moderated by an individual’s growth mindset.

Method

Participants

To capture the developmental shifts potentially influencing psychological well-being, this multi-cohort study employed a longitudinal design. Participants were recruited from five public middle schools situated in both urban and suburban areas of a major city in eastern China. The selection of schools was based on a stratified random sampling process to ensure representation of the socioeconomic diversity within the region. This stratification considered factors such as income levels and urban vs. suburban locations to enhance the representativeness of the sample for this specific metropolitan context.

Initially, schools were stratified into urban and suburban categories. Within each category, schools were further stratified based on income levels, identified through regional income data and school funding reports. This process ensured that schools from various socioeconomic backgrounds were included. From each stratum, schools were randomly selected to participate in the study. Within each selected school, a two-stage cluster sampling method was used to choose participants. In the first stage, classes within each grade (7, 8, and 9) were randomly selected. Specifically, four classes per grade were chosen through a simple random sampling technique using a random number generator. In the second stage, all students within the selected classes were invited to participate, ensuring a comprehensive and unbiased sample. This methodology minimized selection bias and enhanced the representativeness of the sample.

A total of 1,237 students participated in the study across three cohorts. These students (approximately 52% female, 48% male) entered the study in Grades 7, 8, and 9 respectively, with each cohort consisting of around 412 students. This approach allowed us to track the students’ psychological well-being and the hypothesized mediating factors over a three-year period, encompassing a critical developmental stage in Chinese education marked by increased academic pressure and heightened competition for entrance into high schools. Crucially, this three-year tracking was maintained for all cohorts, even as students in the older cohorts transitioned from their original middle schools into various public high schools within the same city. This ability to follow students across the critical academic and social transition to high school is a key feature of our longitudinal design. Inclusion criteria ensured a homogenous sample suitable for investigating the focal relationships. Students were required to be enrolled full-time in the participating schools and free from diagnosed learning disabilities or significant mental health concerns that could potentially confound the study’s findings.

Measures

Multidimensional Social Support Scale (MSSS)

The Multidimensional Social Support Scale (MSSS), developed by Zimet et al. [49], comprises 12 items designed to evaluate individuals’ perceptions of their social support networks, including friends, family, and significant others. Participants rate each item on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. The three-factor structure of the MSSS has been validated across various populations [80, 81].

For this study, the MSSS was translated into Chinese following a rigorous back-translation process. Initially, the scale was translated into Chinese by a bilingual expert, then back-translated into English by another independent bilingual expert to ensure accuracy and cultural relevance. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. To confirm the scale’s reliability and validity in the Chinese context, pilot testing was conducted with a sample of 50 Chinese middle school students. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the family, friends, and significant others subscales were 0.82, 0.85, and 0.80, respectively, indicating good internal consistency. Furthermore, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to verify the three-factor structure of the MSSS in the Chinese context. The CFA was performed using Mplus software, specifying three latent variables corresponding to the support from family, friends, and significant others. The model fit was evaluated using several fit indices: the chi-square statistic, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). The results of the CFA indicated a good fit for the three-factor model: χ²(51) = 72.45, p <.01, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.93, and RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI: 0.02 − 0.06). These indices suggest that the model adequately represents the data and confirms the validity of the MSSS for assessing social support among Chinese middle school students. The factor loadings for all items were significant and ranged from 0.68 to 0.85, indicating strong associations with their respective latent factors.

Growth mindset

The Growth Mindset Inventory, formulated by Dweck [9], assesses the extent to which individuals endorse a growth mindset versus a fixed mindset. The inventory contains eight items, including statements like, “You can learn new things, but you can’t really change your basic level of talent.” Respondents indicate their agreement with each statement on a 5-point Likert scale. For this study, the items were translated into Chinese using a back-translation method similar to the MSSS to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence. The translated version was reviewed by a panel of educational psychologists fluent in both languages. Pilot testing with 50 students indicated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the unidimensional structure of the inventory in the Chinese context, with fit indices indicating a good fit: χ²(20) = 28.63, p <.05, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.95, and RMSEA = 0.03 (90% CI: 0.01 − 0.05).

Academic engagement

To measure academic engagement, and in alignment with our conceptualization of the construct as Vigor, Dedication, and Absorption, we utilized the Chinese version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S) [62, 82]. This 17-item instrument is designed to capture these three distinct dimensions through its subscales, assessing vigor with six items measuring high levels of energy and mental resilience while studying (e.g., “When I’m doing my schoolwork, I feel bursting with energy”); dedication with five items reflecting a sense of significance, enthusiasm, and pride in one’s studies (e.g., “I am enthusiastic about my studies”); and absorption with six items measuring full concentration and happy engrossment in learning activities (e.g., “I am immersed in my studies”). Participants rated their responses for each item on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from never (0) to every day (6), with higher scores signifying a greater level of engagement. The Chinese version underwent a similar back-translation process, with both linguistic and cultural adaptations made to ensure the items were relevant and understandable for Chinese students. Pilot testing with 50 students revealed high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alphas of 0.84 for Vigor, 0.81 for Dedication, and 0.83 for Absorption. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated good fit for the three-factor model in the Chinese educational context: χ²(116) = 152.87, p <.01, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.92, and RMSEA = 0.05 (90% CI: 0.03 − 0.07).

Psychological well-being

Psychological well-being was assessed using a shortened 18-item version of the Psychological Well-Being Scale [3], which is designed to measure the six eudaimonic dimensions discussed in our literature review: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations, purpose in life, and self-acceptance. dimension is represented by three items, rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree). The translation process for this scale also employed the back-translation method to ensure cultural relevance and clarity. The translated items were reviewed by experts in psychology and linguistics, and pilot testing with 50 students demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alphas ranging from 0.76 to 0.83 across the six dimensions. The confirmatory factor analysis supported the six-factor structure of the scale in the Chinese context, affirming its validity and reliability for assessing psychological well-being among Chinese middle school students: χ²(120) = 165.32, p <.01, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, and RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI: 0.02 − 0.06).

Procedure

Ethical approval and consent

The research team ensured adherence to ethical standards by first obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shaoyang University. Following this, informed consent from parents and assent from students were secured before any participation. Parental consent forms were distributed and collected during parent-teacher meetings and via school communication platforms. These forms, translated into Mandarin, detailed the study’s objectives, procedures, and assurances of confidentiality. In addition to written consent from parents, students provided their assent through an interactive session where research assistants explained the study in age-appropriate language and addressed any questions. Cultural considerations were integral to this process; the research team held meetings to explain the study’s importance within the context of Chinese educational culture and considered the cultural importance of parental authority in decision-making, ensuring that parents felt fully informed and comfortable with their child’s participation. Confidentiality of the data was maintained by assigning unique identification codes to each participant and storing the data in securely password-protected files with restricted access.

Data collection protocol

Data collection was conducted in person for all participants over three annual waves. Trained research assistants administered the self-report measures to students in a group setting within their own classrooms during a standard 45-minute class period. This approach was chosen to ensure a familiar, comfortable, and standardized environment. During the sessions, students completed a paper-and-pencil questionnaire booklet containing all the study’s measures. The research assistants read a standardized script of instructions, clarified any student questions, and emphasized the confidentiality of their responses to encourage honest reporting. Baseline demographic information, including age, gender, and socioeconomic status, was collected during the first wave.

The data collection was carefully coordinated with school schedules to minimize disruptions and avoid periods of high academic stress, such as midterms and final exams. This strategic timing helped mitigate the impact of academic stress on students’ responses and ensured that they could participate without added pressure. The study also incorporated feedback from the initial assessment wave to refine and improve the administration process for subsequent waves.

Research assistant training

Research assistants responsible for administering the assessments underwent comprehensive training to ensure consistency and reliability. The training included detailed instructions on administering each instrument, managing group sessions, and addressing student queries. To maintain consistency across all schools and cohorts, a standardized protocol was developed and strictly followed, covering all aspects of the assessment process from setting up the environment to providing instructions. Research assistants were provided with a manual outlining these procedures and were supervised by senior researchers during the initial sessions to ensure adherence to the protocol.

Longitudinal follow-up and participant retention

Maintaining participant engagement over the three-year study period was a critical focus. For the waves of data collection that occurred after students had transitioned to high school, our research team coordinated with the administration of the students’ new schools to arrange similar classroom-based data collection sessions. To address attrition, several strategies were implemented. The research team conducted periodic check-ins with schools and provided regular updates to students and their parents about the study’s progress. Furthermore, small incentives such as gifts and certificates of participation were offered at each assessment wave, and personalized thank-you notes helped reinforce the value of their participation.

Data processing and utilization

After each of the three annual data collection waves, participant responses were linked using unique identification codes to create a longitudinal dataset. The data was then cleaned for errors, and missing data due to attrition were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation, a robust method for structural equation modeling (SEM) that provides unbiased estimates when data are missing at random (MAR).

We analyzed the final dataset using multilevel SEM to account for the nested structure of students within schools and the repeated measures over time. This approach allowed us to assess how within-person changes in growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement predicted changes in psychological well-being across the three years.

Data analysis

We used multilevel SEM with Mplus software [83] to analyze the data. This method is particularly suited to our nested data structure, where students are grouped within schools. Multilevel SEM accounts for the hierarchical nature of the data by allowing us to model relationships at both the individual (student) level and the group (school) level. This is important because students within the same school may share similarities due to common environments or institutional practices, which can lead to correlated errors if not properly addressed [84]. By using multilevel SEM, we obtain more accurate estimates and standard errors, reducing the risk of Type I or Type II errors that could arise from ignoring the nested structure.

The model specification involved detailed considerations for both latent variables and measurement models. Growth mindset was modeled as a latent variable with eight observed indicators from the Growth Mindset Inventory. Social support networks were represented by three latent variables corresponding to support from family, friends, and teachers, each measured by the relevant subscales of the Multidimensional Social Support Scale (MSSS). Academic engagement strategies were modeled as a latent variable with three subscales—Vigor, Dedication, and Absorption—from the Chinese version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S). Psychological well-being comprised six dimensions: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations, purpose in life, and self-acceptance, each represented by three observed indicators from the Psychological Well-Being Scale.

Prior to the main analysis, data were screened for outliers using standardized z-scores and boxplots. After review, data entry errors were corrected. Legitimate extreme values were retained to preserve the natural variability in the data [85], as sensitivity analyses (detailed in Supplementary Material A) confirmed they did not disproportionately influence the final model. As noted in sect. "Data processing and utilization", missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation [86]. This is a robust approach for SEM that uses all available data to produce unbiased estimates under the assumption of data missing at random (MAR) [87]. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic, as well as the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). We used standard cutoff criteria for good fit (CFI/TLI ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA ≤ 0.08) [88].

We tested the significance of direct and interaction effects within this framework. Bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) was used to obtain bias-corrected confidence intervals for all effects [89]. To explore significant interactions, we conducted simple slopes analyses at one standard deviation above and below the mean of the moderator [90]. To ensure the stability of our findings, a cross-validation was performed by splitting the sample; these results are available in the Supplementary Material B.

Results

Baseline equivalence and descriptive statistics

Prior to model testing, analyses were conducted to ensure equivalence across the three cohorts at baseline (Grade 7). One-way ANOVAs revealed no significant between-cohort differences in growth mindset (F(2, 1234) = 1.23, p =.29), social support from family (F(2, 1234) = 0.87, p =.42), social support from friends (F(2, 1234) = 0.98, p =.38), social support from teachers (F(2, 1234) = 1.54, p =.22), or psychological well-being (F(2, 1234) = 0.47, p =.62). These null findings suggest that the three cohorts were comparable on the study variables at the beginning of the investigation.

Descriptive statistics for the baseline measures are presented in Table 1. On average, students had a mean growth mindset score of 3.82 (SD = 0.54) on a scale ranging from 1 to 5. This suggests that students generally believe their intelligence and abilities can be developed through effort and learning. The distribution of growth mindset scores was slightly positively skewed (Skewness = 0.20) with a moderate kurtosis (−0.85), indicating a fairly normal distribution.

To provide a more detailed understanding, the distribution of responses for each scale was also examined. The growth mindset scores indicated that a majority of students (65%) scored above the midpoint, suggesting a prevalent belief in the malleability of intelligence. For social support, 60% of students reported high levels of family support, 55% reported high levels of friend support, and 45% reported high levels of teacher support, indicating variability in perceived support across different sources. Psychological well-being scores indicated that 70% of students were in the moderate to high well-being range, with only 5% falling into the low well-being category.

These baseline equivalence checks and descriptive statistics provided a foundation for further analyses examining the within-person and between-person relationships between growth mindset, social support networks, academic engagement strategies, and psychological well-being over the three-year study period.

Multilevel structural equation modeling analysis

To examine the relationships among growth mindset, social support, academic engagement strategies, and psychological well-being, we conducted a multilevel SEM analysis using Mplus software. This analysis utilized a two-level model to account for the hierarchical data structure, with students (Level 1) nested within schools (Level 2). The multilevel approach was necessary to adjust for the non-independence of observations within schools and to obtain accurate standard errors and parameter estimates. At Level 1 (within-student level), we modeled individual variations in the variables over time, capturing how changes in growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement strategies within students were associated with changes in their psychological well-being. At Level 2 (between-school level), we examined how differences between schools influenced the average levels of these variables and their interrelationships. This allowed us to disentangle within-student effects from between-school effects and understand how both individual and contextual factors contribute to psychological well-being.

Main effects

The multilevel SEM analysis yielded significant main effects for all focal variables, indicating that each variable independently predicted students’ psychological well-being at the within-student level. A robust positive association emerged between growth mindset and psychological well-being (γ = 0.18, 95% CI [0.12, 0.24], p <.001). This means that for every unit increase in growth mindset score, there was a corresponding 0.18 unit increase in psychological well-being score at the individual level. This suggests that students who endorse a growth mindset, believing their intelligence and abilities can be developed, experience greater well-being.

Similarly, social support from family, friends, and teachers all emerged as significant positive predictors of psychological well-being at the within-student level. Students who perceived greater support from each source exhibited higher well-being scores. The unstandardized regression coefficients were 0.12 (95% CI [0.04, 0.20], p <.01) for family support, 0.15 (95% CI [0.09, 0.21], p <.001) for friend support, and 0.10 (95% CI [0.00, 0.20], p <.05) for teacher support.

Students who reported utilizing more effective academic engagement strategies demonstrated significantly higher psychological well-being (γ = 0.21, 95% CI [0.17, 0.25], p <.001). This translates to a 0.21 unit increase in well-being score for each unit increase in student-reported engagement strategies at the within-student level.

At the between-school level, we found that the variance in psychological well-being between schools was not statistically significant (variance component = [value], p >.05), indicating that most of the variability in well-being was at the individual level. Therefore, the school-level predictors did not significantly influence psychological well-being in this sample (Table 2).

Interaction effects

The model tested for interactions between growth mindset and each source of social support. A significant two-way interaction emerged between growth mindset and social support from friends (γ = 0.04, p <.05). To unpack this interaction, simple slopes analysis was conducted at one standard deviation below the mean (M - SD) and one standard deviation above the mean (M + SD) of growth mindset.

The results, depicted in Fig. 2, showed that the positive association between friend support and well-being was strongest and significant for students with a weaker growth mindset (M − 1 SD; simple slope γ = 0.20, p <.01). For students with an average growth mindset (M), the slope remained positive and significant (γ = 0.14, p <.001). In contrast, for students with a stronger growth mindset (M + 1 SD), the slope was flatter and non-significant (γ = 0.08, p >.05).

Interaction plot of growth mindset x friend support on psychological well-being

To provide a fuller picture beyond these three arbitrary points, we also conducted a Johnson-Neyman (J-N) analysis. This technique identifies the specific “region of significance” for the effect of friend support across the entire continuum of the growth mindset moderator. The J-N analysis revealed that the positive effect of friend support on well-being was statistically significant for all students with a growth mindset score of 4.15 or lower (on the 5-point scale). For students with scores above this 4.15 threshold (representing a very strong growth mindset), the relationship was no longer significant.

These findings suggest that friend support is highly beneficial for students who hold a weaker (or more fixed) mindset. However, the J-N analysis clarifies that this supportive effect extends to students with average and even moderately strong growth mindsets, only diminishing for those at the very high end of the growth mindset scale. For these highly self-reliant students, who already possess strong internal beliefs in their ability to develop, friend support may play a less critical role in influencing well-being. No other significant interaction effects were detected in the model, suggesting that the independent effects of social support from family and teachers, as well as academic engagement strategies, on student well-being were not moderated by growth mindset.

As expected, baseline psychological well-being emerged as a strong positive predictor of subsequent well-being (γ = 0.42, p <.001), indicating stability in well-being scores over the three years of the study. Additionally, controlling for demographic factors revealed that older students reported slightly, but significantly, higher well-being scores (γ = 0.07, p <.05). Females also exhibited higher well-being compared to males (γ = 0.13, p <.001). Cohort differences in well-being were not statistically significant (p >.05).

The final multilevel SEM model demonstrated good fit to the data, with χ²(252) = 318.42, p <.001, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.92, and RMSEA = 0.05 (confidence interval: 0.04 − 0.06). These fit indices provide evidence that the model adequately captured the complex relationships between growth mindset, social support networks, academic engagement strategies, and psychological well-being in this study population.

In conclusion, the findings of this multi-cohort study provided evidence for the independent and interactive effects of growth mindset, social support networks, and academic engagement strategies on the psychological well-being of Chinese middle school students. Future research directions and potential implications for educational practices are discussed in the following sections.

Discussion

This multi-cohort longitudinal study investigated the interplay between growth mindset, social support networks, and academic engagement on the psychological well-being of Chinese middle school students. The findings offer valuable insights into how these factors independently and interactively influence student well-being over time. Our discussion is organized to first address the robust and consistent direct contributions of individual and social factors to well-being. Second, we will discuss the more nuanced, and potentially exploratory, finding regarding the interplay between a student’s mindset and their social support system.

The direct roles of mindset, support, and engagement in well-being

Consistent with our first research question, and representing the primary, most consistent finding of this study, our study confirmed the robust, direct, and positive main effects of several factors on well-being. We found a significant positive association between a growth mindset and well-being, aligning with Dweck’s theoretical framework [9]. A plausible reason for this is that a growth mindset may help students reframe academic challenges as learning opportunities rather than threats to their self-worth. This cognitive reframing is thought to foster resilience and reduce negative emotional responses, which is particularly adaptive in the high-pressure Chinese educational context [7, 12, 42, 91]. This finding reinforces that a student’s internal beliefs are a key asset for mental health.

Equally robust, our findings also underscore the vital roles that adults play in an adolescent’s life, as social support from both family and teachers independently predicted higher psychological well-being. A potential explanation is that these two sources of support serve distinct functions. Family support likely provides a secure base of unconditional acceptance, which is foundational for self-esteem [19], while teacher support may foster academic motivation and a sense of competence within the school environment [58]. In China, where family bonds and respect for teachers are culturally emphasized [8, 20], these stable social connections may be particularly crucial for adolescents’ emotional regulation and stress management.

Finally, academic engagement also emerged as a strong, independent predictor of well-being. This link is logical because active engagement in learning provides students with a sense of purpose and a pathway to mastery, which are core components of eudaimonic well-being [68, 71]. Experiencing progress and accomplishment through one’s own efforts can, in turn, boost self-esteem. In China’s education system, where academic success is highly valued [74], students who are actively engaged may be better equipped to meet academic demands and thus experience the psychological benefits of perceived competence.

Taken together, these main effects provide a clear and foundational model of support. They suggest that adolescent well-being is not reliant on a single factor, but is best supported by a combination of internal psychological resources (growth mindset), stable relational assets (family and teacher support), and positive behavioral pathways (academic engagement). From a practical standpoint, especially in light of the ‘Double Reduction’ policy [1, 2], these robust findings are arguably more significant than the single interaction. They highlight that holistic well-being rests on a ‘three-legged stool’: a positive mindset, supportive adult relationships, and a meaningful connection to learning.

The buffering effect of friendship: how mindset and social support interact

While the direct effects were the most robust findings of this study, our exploratory analysis of interactions provided one specific, nuanced insight. Notably, the hypothesized interactions between growth mindset and social support from family and teachers were not significant. The only significant interaction that emerged was between growth mindset and friend support, a finding that should be interpreted with caution as it may be context-specific. Our results showed that friend support was most beneficial for students with a weaker growth mindset. This suggests that for students who are more vulnerable to self-doubt, strong peer connections may act as a crucial external buffer against negative emotions, reinforcing their self-esteem at a developmental stage when peer influence is paramount [24, 92].

This finding aligns with a precise application of the buffering hypothesis [13]. While typically applied to external stressors, we extend its premise to an internal psychological vulnerability: a fixed mindset. In this context, friend support appears to be a protective factor that may weaken the potentially detrimental relationship between a fixed mindset and lower well-being. A likely reason for this specific effect is that in China’s collectivist culture, where social harmony and belonging are highly valued [20], the validation and encouragement from friends can be a primary source of self-worth for students struggling with their own sense of capability.

Conversely, students with a stronger growth mindset appeared less reliant on friend support for their well-being. This does not imply that friendships are unimportant for them, but rather suggests they may possess more developed internal resources that foster self-reliance. Their belief in personal growth and their ability to overcome challenges could reduce their dependence on external validation for emotional stability [9]. The mechanisms behind this self-reliance likely involve higher levels of intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy, enabling them to utilize effective coping strategies and problem-solving skills to handle difficulties independently [42, 69]. This suggests that a strong growth mindset helps students cultivate an internal locus of control, which is foundational to long-term mental health.

Conclusion

This multi-cohort longitudinal study aimed to investigate the complex interplay between growth mindset, social support networks, academic engagement strategies, and psychological well-being among Chinese middle school students. The findings revealed significant main effects of growth mindset, social support from family, friends, and teachers, and academic engagement strategies on students’ psychological well-being. Additionally, an important interaction effect between growth mindset and friend support highlighted the conditional nature of these relationships.

The results underscore the critical role of a growth mindset in fostering psychological well-being. Students who believe in the malleability of their intelligence and abilities are more likely to embrace challenges, persist in the face of setbacks, and view effort as a path to mastery. This belief system not only enhances academic outcomes but also promotes personal development and psychological health. The study also reaffirmed the importance of social support from family, friends, and teachers, which collectively contribute to students’ ability to cope with stress and maintain mental health. Academic engagement strategies, particularly self-regulated learning techniques, were found to significantly enhance psychological well-being, emphasizing the interconnectedness of academic and emotional development.

Implications

The findings of this study contribute to the theoretical understanding of psychological well-being by integrating concepts from growth mindset theory, social support frameworks, and academic engagement literature. This research highlights the multifaceted nature of psychological well-being and the importance of considering various individual and contextual factors that contribute to it. The robust associations between growth mindset, social support, and academic engagement underscore the need for comprehensive models that account for these interrelated factors. This study extends Dweck’s [9] growth mindset theory by demonstrating its significant impact on psychological well-being, not just academic performance. Moreover, it contributes to social support theory by detailing the unique and complementary roles of support from family, friends, and teachers in fostering adolescent well-being. The integration of academic engagement strategies further enriches our understanding of how educational practices can promote mental health alongside academic success.

The findings suggest several potential implications for educational settings. Given our findings, it may be valuable for schools to explore fostering a growth mindset among students by integrating growth mindset principles into the curriculum and teacher training programs. Interventions that teach students about brain plasticity and encourage a positive attitude towards challenges and failures could be particularly beneficial. Teachers might play a crucial role by providing constructive feedback that emphasizes effort and strategies over innate ability. Our results also point to the critical role of social support in students’ psychological well-being. Developing programs that strengthen family engagement, foster positive peer interactions, and build supportive teacher-student relationships might help create a more nurturing educational environment. Peer mentoring programs, family engagement workshops, and professional development for teachers on emotional support techniques represent potential strategies to be explored.

Encouraging the use of self-regulated learning strategies such as goal setting, time management, and self-evaluation may also be a promising avenue for enhancing students’ engagement and well-being. Schools could incorporate training on these strategies into regular classroom activities and offer workshops or seminars for students. Creating a school culture that values academic engagement and provides the necessary resources for students to develop these skills is likely important. The interaction effects found in this study highlight the potential importance of tailoring interventions to individual students’ needs. For students with a weaker growth mindset, enhancing peer support might be particularly effective. A possible application could involve assessing students’ mindsets and social support networks to identify those who might benefit most from targeted support. However, it is crucial to interpret these implications with caution. Our observational design highlights associations, not causation. Therefore, these practical ideas should be viewed as hypotheses for future research. We recommend that any potential interventions be tested through experimental or quasi-experimental studies to establish causal evidence before widespread implementation.

Limitations

Despite the valuable insights gained, this study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, our sampling strategy presents a key limitation. The sample was drawn exclusively from public middle schools in a single major city in eastern China. This specific urban and regional focus may limit the generalizability of the findings. The academic pressures, resource availability, and familial expectations characteristic of a large eastern city may differ substantially from those in rural, central, or western Chinese provinces. Therefore, we caution against generalizing these results to other regions or non-urban educational contexts. Future research should include more geographically and socioeconomically diverse samples to enhance generalizability.

Second, the reliance exclusively on self-reported measures for all variables raises the potential for common method variance (CMV), which may inflate the observed relationships between constructs. This single-source approach may introduce bias due to social desirability or inaccurate self-assessment. Although measures were taken to ensure anonymity and reduce response bias, the inherent limitations of self-report data remain. Therefore, future research should aim to triangulate these findings using multi-source data, such as teacher reports of academic engagement or parent reports on social support.

Third, while the longitudinal design allows for examining changes over time, causality cannot be definitively established. Experimental or quasi-experimental designs could provide stronger evidence of causality in future studies. Lastly, the study focused on a limited number of variables related to psychological well-being. Other factors, such as personality traits, school climate, and extracurricular activities, might also play significant roles and should be considered in future research.

Future research

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, we propose three systematic avenues for future research. First, replication in more diverse contexts is essential. Future studies should aim to include more diverse samples, particularly from rural schools or schools in central and western China, to test whether the robust main effects observed here are universal and whether the specific friend-support interaction is culturally or regionally specific.

Second, future work could benefit from mixed-methods approaches. While our quantitative data identified what relationships exist, qualitative interviews with students, parents, and teachers could help explain why they exist. For example, a mixed-methods study could explore why friend support, but not family or teacher support, appears to moderate the effect of growth mindset.

Finally, and most importantly, the potential implications of this study should be directly tested through intervention research. We recommend the design and evaluation of school-based intervention programs that aim to foster growth mindsets and strengthen social support networks simultaneously. Such studies, preferably using cluster-randomized controlled trials, would provide the causal evidence needed to translate these observational findings into effective educational policy.
