Slicing correspondences with high degree hypersurfaces
Ishan Banerjee

Abstract
. We approximately compute the correspondence degree (as defined by Lazarsfeld and Martin) between two unbalanced complete intersections. This is accomplished by showing that the procedure of taking a subvariety of a product Y × Y ′ Y\times Y^{\prime} and intersecting it with X × Y ′ X\times Y^{\prime} (for X X a sufficiently ample smooth divisor in Y Y ) induces a bijection between two sets of varieties. This may be of independent interest.



1.
Introduction

In the paper
[
4
]
, Lazarsfeld and Martin introduce the notion of the
correspondence degree
between two varieties. Given two varieties
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
of the same dimension, they define the correspondence degree between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
denoted,
\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})
by the formula:

\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})=\min\{\deg(W\to Y)\deg(W\to Y^{\prime})\},

where
W
is ranges over all irreducible subvarieties of
Y\times Y^{\prime}
of the same dimension as
Y
such that the projection maps from
W
to
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are dominant.

They prove the following Theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be very general hypersurfaces of degree
d,e\geq 2n+2
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}.
Then
\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})=\textrm{irr}(Y)\textrm{irr}(Y^{\prime})=(d-1)(e-1),
where
\textrm{irr}(Y),\textrm{irr}(Y^{\prime})
are the degrees of irrationality of
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
respectively.

They prove a bit more than this: the minimizing correspondence arises as a fiber product of the minimal coverings of projective space.

In the same paper they ask the following question:

Question 1.2
.

Let
M
be a smooth projective variety and
A
an ample divisor on
M
. Let
d,d^{\prime}\gg 0.

and
Y,Y^{\prime}
be very general hypersurfaces in
M
such that
Y\in|dA|,Y^{\prime}\in|d^{\prime}A|.
Can one find conditions on
M,A
such that
\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})\approx\textrm{irr}(Y)\textrm{irr}(Y^{\prime})?

We note that the results of
[
5
]
imply that
\textrm{irr}(Y)=d\textrm{mfd}(M,A)
, where
\textrm{mfd}(M,A)
refers to the
minimum fibering degree
of
M
with respect to
A
, a quantity defined and studied in the paper
[
5
]
.

In view of this, Question
1.2
becomes equivalent to showing that

\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})\approx\textrm{mfd}(M,A)^{2}dd^{\prime}.

We prove:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
be a smooth projective variety and
A
an ample divisor on
M
. Let
d\gg d^{\prime}\gg 0.
Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be smooth hypersurfaces in
M
such that
Y\in|dA|,Y^{\prime}\in|d^{\prime}A|.

Then

\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})\approx Kdd^{\prime}

for a fixed constant
K
depending only on
M
and
A
.

This can be seen as an answer to a corrected version of the above question of Lazarsfeld and Martin under the simplifying assumption that
d\gg d^{\prime}.

We can in fact prove much more:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n,r\geq 1
.
Let
d_{1}\gg d_{1}^{\prime}\gg\dots d_{r}\gg d_{r}^{\prime}\gg e,e^{\prime}\geq 0
.
Let
M,M^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n
. Let
H,H^{\prime}
be very ample divisorson
M,M^{\prime}
respectively. Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be complete intersections of codimension
r
in
M,M^{\prime}
, and let the multidegrees of
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
(with respect to
H
and
H^{\prime}
) be
(d_{1},d_{2}\dots,d_{r})
and
(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots d_{r}^{\prime})
respectively. Assume furthermore, that we can find a chain of smooth complete intersections
Y\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq Y_{2}\dots\subseteq Y_{r}=M
, and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq Y^{\prime}_{2}\dots\subseteq Y^{\prime}_{r}=M^{\prime}
where
Y_{i}
has multidegree
(d_{i+1},\dots d_{r})
and
Y_{i}^{\prime}
has multidegree
(d_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots d_{r}^{\prime})

Then for some constant
K
, only depending on
M,M^{\prime},A,A^{\prime}
,

\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})\approx d_{1}d_{2}\dots d_{r}d_{1}^{\prime}\dots d_{r}^{\prime}K.

Let us note that the constant
K
, obtained in Theorem
1.3
is
not
equal to
\textrm{mfd}(M,A)^{2}
for all choices of
(M,A)
, which is what it would be if
\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})\approx\textrm{irr}(Y)\textrm{irr}(Y^{\prime}).
In the particular case when
M={\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and
A
is a hyperplane section, our constant
K
does agree with
\textrm{mfd}(M,A)^{2}
(which is actually
1
in that particular situation).

An explicit example where these constants differ is given in Section
4
.

In fact, we can explain what this constant
K
is in explicit geometric terms and show that the minimizing correspondence arises as a fiber product of sorts.

Our degree assumptions in Theorem
1.4
and
1.3
should be seen as analogues of the
unbalanced
assumptions in
[
5
]
. Indeed, the main motivation of the authors in starting this project was to try to generalize the ideas of
[
5
]
to the context of correspondence degree, and to thereby solve several problems in this subject in the unbalanced situation. We essentially succeed in doing so and in the process we define several concepts that we think are of importance to understanding correspondences between hypersurfaces and complete intersections.

We can prove all of our results over an arbitrary algebraically closed field of characteristic 0. However, there are some serious issues regarding separability that arise when trying to generalize the results to positive characteristic, we will briefly remark on the difficulties that arise in doing this in the final section of this paper.

Our results also have the following advantage: We do not assume that our hypersurfaces
Y,Y^{\prime}
are very general, which appears to be the only setting in which results have been proven so far.

Let us remark on the
unbalanced
assumption appearing in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, i.e. the assumption that not only the numbers appearing in our multidegrees are large, but that they are all far away from each other in a particular way. This is a very strong assumption, but it is necessary as Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are
false
without this assumption. This can be seen with the following simple example: Let
d=d^{\prime}
,
Y=Y^{\prime}
. Then
\textrm{corr.deg}(Y,Y^{\prime})=1
, which is certainly not
\approx Kd^{2}
for large
d
. However, it is certainly possible that one could recover the statement of Theorem
1.3
say with just the assumption that
d,d^{\prime}\gg 0
, if we add the additional assumption that the pair
(Y,Y^{\prime})
is very general.

Remark 1.5
.

In this paper we will often say that two functions
f,g:{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{N}}
are approximately equal to each other, or
f(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\approx g(d_{1},\dots d_{n})
. By this we mean that as
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
goes to ìnfinity(́it will be clear what we mean by this in the usage) we have the limit
\frac{f(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})}{g(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})}\to 1.

We will also oftern say that a function is
O(1)
if it bounded by some constant independendent of the
d_{i},d^{\prime}_{i}
.

2.
The main definitions

We work over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. Varieties are assumed to be varieties over the field unless otherwise mentioned.

Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n
. Let
H,H^{\prime}
be very ample line bundles on
Y,Y^{\prime}
respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A
prime dominant k correspondence
between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
is an irreducible subvariety
W\subseteq Y\times Y^{\prime}
of dimension
n+k
surjecting onto both
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
.

An prime dominant
0
correspondence is just an irreducible variety
W\subseteq Y\times Y^{\prime}
dominating both factors and such that
\dim W=\dim Y=\dim Y^{\prime}
, precisely the kind of object that shows up when defining correspondence degree.

Associated to such a prime dominant
k
-correspondence
W
is a pair of numbers which we will call its degree. We define this as follows: Let
W\subseteq Y\times Y^{\prime}
be a prime dominant correspondence. Let
p:Y\times Y^{\prime}\to Y
and
p^{\prime}:Y\times Y^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
be the projection maps. Let
y\in Y,y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
be general points.
We then define the degree of
W
by the formula

\deg W=(p^{*}(y)\cdot p^{\prime*}((H^{\prime})^{k}),p^{\prime*}(y^{\prime})\cdot p^{*}((H)^{k})).

We now define a general dominant correspondence.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
a dominant
k
correspondence
W
between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
is an integral linear combination of prime dominant
k
correspondences with positive coefficients, i.e.
W
is a formal sum of the form
\sum_{i}a_{i}W_{i}
where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
W_{i}
are prime dominant
k
correspondences. We define the degree of
W
to be
\sum_{i}a_{i}\deg(W_{i}).

We define the set
{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(a,b)}(Y,Y^{\prime})
to be the set of dominant
k
correspondences
W
between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
such that
\deg W=(a_{0},b_{0})
where
a_{0}\leq a
and
b_{0}\leq b
.

Let
d,d^{\prime}>0
. Let
X,X^{\prime}
be smooth hypersurfaces in
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
respectively such that
X\in|dH|
and
|X^{\prime}|\in|d^{\prime}H^{\prime}|.

We will now need to define
uneven
dominant correspondences.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
Y,X^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n+1
and
n
respectively.
Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A
prime uneven dominant k correspondence
on
Y\times X^{\prime}
is an irreducible subvariety
W\subseteq Y\times X^{\prime}
of dimension
n+k+1
surjecting on to both factors
Y
and
X^{\prime}
under the respective projection maps.

Let
W
be an prime uneven dominant
k
correspondence between
Y
and
X^{\prime}
. Let
y\in Y,x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
be general points. We define the degree of
W
by the formula:

\deg W=(\#(\{y\}\times(H^{\prime})^{k}\cap p^{-1}(y)),\#(H^{k+1}\times\{y^{\prime}\}\cap p^{\prime-1}(y^{\prime}))),

we note that these intersection multiplicities are independent of the choice of
y,x^{\prime}
as long as they are general.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
An uneven dominant
k
- correspondence between
Y
and
X^{\prime}
is is a formal positive sum of prime dominant
k
correspondences, i.e.
W
is a formal sum of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}W_{i}
where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
W_{i}
are prime dominant
k
correspondences. We define the degree of
W
to be
\sum a_{i}\deg(W_{i}).

We define the set
{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(a,b)}(Y,X^{\prime})
to be the set of dominant correspondences
W
between
Y
and
X^{\prime}
such that
\deg W=(a_{0},b_{0})
where
a_{0}\leq a
and
b_{0}\leq b
.

We note that we use the same notation
{\mathrm{DomCor}}(\_,\_)
to denote the uneven and regular dominant correspondences- we hope this will not cause the reader any confusion. To understand whether we refer to uneven dominant correspondences in such a situation we just need to look at whether the varieties appearing in the parentheses have the same dimension or not. We will also often refer to both regular and uneven dominant correspondences as dominant correspondences without any adjectives, again we hope this does not cause any confusion.

Given a dominant correspondence
W
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we will say that
\deg W\geq(a,b)
if
\deg W=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
where
a^{\prime}\geq a
and
b^{\prime}\geq b
.

2.1.
The slicing operations
\Phi
and
\Psi

In this subsection we will define two functions between sets of dominant correspondences arising from slicing a correspondence with a hypersurface.

Let
Y,X^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n+1
and
n
respectively. Let
H,H^{\prime}
be very ample divisors on
Y,X^{\prime}
respectively. Let
d>0
and
X
be a smooth hypersurface in
Y
belonging to
|dH|.
Given an uneven dominant correspondence
W
between
Y
and
X^{\prime}
, one can do the following operation: we first form the intersection between
W
and
X\times X^{\prime}
.
Let
W_{1},\dots W_{r}
denote all the irreducible components of
W\cap X\times X^{\prime}
that dominate both
X
and
Y^{\prime}
. We then define
\Phi(W)=W_{1}\cup\dots\cup W_{r}
. If
\Phi(W)
is nonempty , then it is clearly a dominant correspondence between
X
and
X^{\prime}.

One immediately sees that given
e_{1},e_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, this construction
\Phi
defines a function

{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e_{1},e_{2})}(Y,Z)\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e_{1},de_{2})}(X,Z),

again assuming that the variety
\Phi(W)
is nonemepty. Note that the domain of the function
\Phi
is a set of uneven dominant correspondences, while the co-domain is a set of regular dominant correspondences.

Our main Theorems with regard to
\Phi
are as follows:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
d,e,e^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
d\gg e^{\prime}
(implicitly we also assume that
d\gg(H^{\prime})^{n}
).

Then the map
\Phi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{0}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{0}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(X,X^{\prime})
as defined above is a bijection.

Furthermore
\Phi
takes prime correspondences to prime correspondences.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
d\gg e^{\prime}
(implicitly we also assume that
d\gg|H^{\prime}|^{n}
). Let
k\geq 1

Then the map
\Phi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(X,X^{\prime})
as defined above is a bijection.

Furthermore
\Phi
takes prime correspondences to prime correspondences.

We note that we have singled out the case
k=0
into Theorem
2.5
. This is because we will very heavily use Theorem
2.5
in proving both Theorem
2.6
and several of our other Theorems and we thought it would be more sensible to make it a separate statement.

Let us now define another slicing operation, which we will denote by
\Psi
.

Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n+1
. Let
H,H^{\prime}
be very ample divisors on
Y,Y^{\prime}
respectively. Let
d^{\prime}>0
. Let
X^{\prime}
be a smooth hypersurface in
Y^{\prime}
belonging to
|d^{\prime}H^{\prime}|
. Let
W
be a dominant correspondence between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
. Then we can perform the following operation: we first form the intersection
W\cap(Y\times X^{\prime})
. We define
\Psi(W)
to be the union of all components of
W\cap(Y\times X^{\prime})
dominating both
Y
and
X^{\prime}
.The variety
\Psi(W)
, if nonemepty, is an uneven dominant correspondence between
Y
and
X^{\prime}

We note now that given
k,e,e^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and assuming that the varieties
\Psi(W)
are nonempty,
\Psi
actually defines a function

\Psi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k+1}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,Y^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(d^{\prime}e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime}).

Let us now state our main Theorems for this function
\Psi.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
e,e^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}

For
d^{\prime}\gg e
, the map

\Psi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{1}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,Y^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{0}_{\leq(d^{\prime}e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})

is a bijection.
Furthermore
\Psi
takes prime correspondences to prime correspondences.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
k,e,e^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
d^{\prime}\gg e
, the map

\Psi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k+1}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,Y^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(d^{\prime}e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})

is a bijection.
Furthermore
\Psi
takes irreducible correspondences to irreducible correspondences.

We single out the case
k=0
as its own Theorem for similar reasons to those for singling out Theorem
2.5
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
assuming that Theorems
2.5
,
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.8
are true.
We begin with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n,r\geq 1
.
Let
d_{1}\gg d_{1}^{\prime}\gg\dots d_{r}\gg d_{r}^{\prime}\gg e,e^{\prime}>0
.
Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n
. with very ample divisors
H,H^{\prime}
respectively. Let
X,X^{\prime}
be complete intersections of codimension
r
in
Y,Y^{\prime}
, and let the multidegrees of
X
and
X^{\prime}
be
(d_{1},d_{2}\dots,d_{r})
and
(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots d_{r}^{\prime}).
Assume furthermore that we can find a chain of smooth complete intersections
X\subseteq X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\dots\subseteq X_{r}=Y
, and
X^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq X^{\prime}_{2}\dots\subseteq X^{\prime}_{r}=Y^{\prime}
where
X_{i}
has multidegree
(d_{i+1},\dots d_{r})
and
X_{i}^{\prime}
has multidegree
(d_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots d_{r}^{\prime})

Then any dominant 0 correspondence
W
between
X
and
X^{\prime}
of degree
\leq(d_{1}^{\prime}\dots d_{r}^{\prime}e,d_{1}\dots d_{r}e)
is obtained from slicing an
r
correspondence of degree
\leq(e,e^{\prime})
between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
, more explicitly there is a dominant
r
correspondence
W_{r}
, of appropriate degree such that
(\Psi\circ\Phi)^{r}(W_{r})=W
.

As a special case, if
X,X^{\prime}
are curves, then any such
W
is obtained by slicing a divisor in
Y\times Y^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Consider the two flags of complete intersections
X\subseteq X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\dots\subseteq X_{r}=Y
, and
X^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq X^{\prime}_{2}\dots\subseteq X^{\prime}_{r}=Y^{\prime}
. We may iteratively apply Theorems
2.6
and
2.8
to lift
W
to a flag of correspondences
W\subseteq W_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq W_{r}
where
W_{i}
is an
i
correspondence between
X_{i}
and
X_{i}^{\prime}
of appropriate multidegree, where for each
i
the dominant correspondence
W_{i}
is obtained as
\Psi(\Phi(W_{i+1})).

The variety
W_{r}
is then our required correspondence, we obtain the required bounds on its degree from the bounds in Theorems
2.6
and
2.8
.
∎

We will also need a lower bound on the degrees of the correspondences
\Phi(W_{1}),\Psi(W_{2})
in terms of the degrees of
W_{1}
and
W_{2}.

Proposition 3.2
.

(1)

Let
W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,X^{\prime})
be such that
\deg W=(e,e^{\prime}).
Assume that
d\gg e^{\prime},
so that we are in the context where we can apply Theorem
2.6
.
Then
\deg\Phi(W)\geq(e,(d-1)e^{\prime})\approx(e,de^{\prime})
.

(2)

Let
W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,Y^{\prime})
be such that
\deg W=(e,e^{\prime}).
Assume that
d^{\prime}\gg e,
so that we are in the context where we can apply Theorem
2.6
.
Then
\deg\Psi(W)\geq((d^{\prime}-1)e,e^{\prime})\approx(d^{\prime}e,e^{\prime})

Proof.

We will only prove (1) since (2) is almost identical. We note that by Theorem
2.6
the map
\Phi
is injective and that
\deg\Phi(W)\leq(e,de^{\prime})
. If the degree of
\Phi(W)\leq(e,(d-1)e^{\prime})
then by the surjectivity part of Theorem
2.6
we may conclude that there is some
W^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,X^{\prime})
such that
\deg W^{\prime}\leq(e,e^{\prime}-1)
and
\Phi(W^{\prime})=\Phi(W).
But the injectivity of
\Phi
implies that
W^{\prime}=W,
which is a contradiction since they have different degrees.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let

K=\min\{ab|\deg W=(a,b),W\textrm{ is a dominant }r\textrm{ correspondence between }M\textrm{and }M^{\prime}\}.

Let
W_{0}
be a dominant
r-
correspondence between
M
and
M^{\prime}
realizing this minimum. Let
W_{1}=\Phi\circ\Psi(W),W_{2}=\Phi\circ\Psi(W_{1}),\dots W_{r}=\Phi\circ\Psi(W_{r-1}),
where the slicing operations
\Phi
and
\Psi
are with respect to the inclusions in the flags
Y\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq M
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq M^{\prime}.
As a consequence of Proposition
3.2
, the degree of
W_{r}
is approximately
(d_{1}^{\prime}\dots d_{r}^{\prime}a,d_{1}d_{2}\dots d_{r}b).
Thus to finish the proof of Theorem
1.4
it suffices to establish that there are no correspondences between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
of significantly smaller product degree.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
W
is a correspondence between
X
and
X^{\prime}
of degree
(a,b)
where

ab<d_{1}d_{2}\dots d_{r}d_{1}^{\prime}\dots d_{r}^{\prime}K.

Under our assumptions the above inequality implies that
d_{1}\gg\frac{b}{d_{1}}
.
Thus by Theorem
2.6
(and Proposition
3.2
) we may lift
W
to a dominant correspondence
W^{\prime}
between
X_{1}
and
X^{\prime}
of degree
(a,\frac{b}{d_{1}}).
We then note that
d_{1}^{\prime}\gg\frac{a}{d_{1}^{\prime}}
.
We then apply Theorem
2.8
to conclude that we may again lift
W^{\prime}
to obtain a dominant
1
correspondence between
X_{1}
and
X_{1}^{\prime}
of degree
(\frac{a}{d_{1}^{\prime}},\frac{b}{d_{1}}).
Repeating this procedure we eventually end up with a dominant correspondence of degree
(\frac{a}{d_{1}^{\prime}\dots d_{r}^{\prime}},\frac{b}{d_{1},\dots d_{r}})
between
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
. However the fact that

4.
Some applications and examples

4.1.
Product of two curves

In this subsection we will consider the case when
Y=Y^{\prime}=C_{1}\times C_{2}
where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are curves. We will assume the very ample divisor
H
on
Y
is of the form
k(C_{1}\times\{p\}+\{q\}\times C_{2})
for some
k>0
.

We note that in this situation there is a dominant
1
correspondence
W\subseteq Y\times Y
that is simply the preimage of the diagonal under the map
Y\times Y\to C_{1}\times C_{1}
. We note that the degree of
W
is
(k,k)
.

Let us note that
mfd(Y,H)
can also be computed; at least after making some assumptions on
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Let
f:Y{\dashrightarrow}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
be a rational function that does not factor through the projection to
C_{1}
. Then when we restrict
f
to
\{p\}\times C_{2}
it has degree at least
\textrm{irr}(C_{2}).
Thus the fibering degree of
f
with respect to
{\mathcal{L}}
is at least
k\times\textrm{irr}(C_{2}).
Consider a rational map
C_{1}{\dashrightarrow}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
realising the gonality. The composite of this map with the projection
Y\to C_{1}
is a rational map of fibering degree
k\times\textrm{irr}(C_{1})
. Assume that
\textrm{irr}(C_{1})<\textrm{irr}(C_{2}).
In this case the above shows that
mfd(Y,H)=k\textrm{irr}(C_{1}).

This gives us an explicit example of where the constant suggested by Lazarsfeld- Martin and our constant differ, ours is at most
k^{2},
whereas their constant is exactly
k^{2}\textrm{irr}(C_{1})^{2}
.

Let us remark on why this difference in constants occurs and how we came up with this example.

Let
Y
be a smooth projective surface and
{\mathbb{L}}
a very ample line bundle. An easy way to construct a dominant
1-
correspondence contained in
Y\times Y
is as follows: take a dominant rational function
f:Y{\dashrightarrow}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
that realises
mfd(Y,H)
. We then pullback the diagonal
\Delta\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
via the function
f\times f
to obtain a dominant
1-
correspondence
W
of degree
(mfd(Y,H),mfd(Y,H))
.

Morally speaking, the Lazarsfeld Martin constant is correct if the smallest degree 1 correspondence on
Y
is of this form.

However as we have just seen,this need not be the case. In the case of a product of two curves, the product structure allows us to naturally construct a dominant
1-
correspondence. More generally, let
Y
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n+1
with a dominant map to an variety
Z
of dimension
n
. Then we can pull back the diagonal
\Delta\subseteq Z\times Z
to get a dominant
1
correspondence in
Y\times Y
and in the case when
Y
is a product of curves, this gives us a smaller dominant correspondence.

4.2.
Covers of hypersurfaces

In this subsection we will discuss a curious applcation of out results to the setting of covers of hypersurfaces.
Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be smooth projective varieties of dimension
n+1
, let
H,H^{\prime}
be very ample divisors and let
d\gg d^{\prime}\gg e.
Let
X^{\prime}
be a smooth hypersurface in
Y^{\prime}
in the linear system
|d^{\prime}H^{\prime}|
.
A consequence of our results that we would like to emphasize is that under our unbalanced assumptions,
X^{\prime}
is not covered by
any
smooth projective hypersurface
X
in
|dH|
for
d\gg d^{\prime}
unless the covering degree is greater than
e
.

For example, suppose we consider the situation when
n=1
. Then in the above situation,
Y,Y^{\prime}
are surfaces and
X,X^{\prime}
are curves. There is no covering

of degree
\leq e.
This is quite surprising, as

is surely covered by some curves with the same genus as

and also our statements are for

smooth curves in the linear system
|d^{\prime}H^{\prime}|
and not just for the general curve. It is not clear to us if this consequence of our Theorem has a more elementary proof.

5.1.

Let us now review the definition of a Cayley Bacharach set as well as discuss briefly where these objects show up in the course of this paper.

Definition 5.1

Some important facts about Cayley-Bacharach sets that we will use in our proofs are as follows:

(1)

(2)

of degree

, Furthermore there is a unique curve of minimal degree containing

(this is Theorem 1.13 of

Let us sketch out how Cayley Bacharach sets and the above facts about them are used in this section.

Let

be a smooth projective variety of dimension

,

Let

W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,X^{\prime})
and let
V
be a general hyperplane in
|D|
. Then
V\cap W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(X,X^{\prime})
.

Proof.

This follows mutatis mutandis from the proof of the previous Proposition.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Assume Theorem
2.6
is true for
k-1
. Let
W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime}).

Then, for general
V\in|D|
,

\Phi(V\cap W)=V\cap\Phi(W).

Proof.

It suffices to establish this result when
W
is irreducible so we make that assumption. By Bertini’s irreduciblity theorem we may also assume that
V\cap W
is irreducible. Furthermore
\Phi(W)
is also irreducible by Theorem
2.6
and so by Bertini’s irreducibility theorem we may assume that
V\cap\Phi(W)
is also irreducible. Since both
\Phi(V\cap W)
and
V\cap\Phi(W)
are irreducible varieties of the same dimension it suffices to establish that
\Phi(V\cap W)\subseteq V\cap\Phi(W)
or equivalently
\Phi(V\cap W)\subseteq\Phi(W)
. Clearly
\Phi(V\cap W)\subseteq W\cap X\times X^{\prime}
. Now
W\cap X\times X^{\prime}=\Phi(W)\cup W_{1}\dots\cup W_{r}
where the
W_{i}
are components of
W\cap(X\times X^{\prime})
that do not dominate
X
. However,
\Phi(V\cap W)
is an irreducible variety that dominates
X
and thus it is forced to be contained in
\Phi(W)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
k\geq 1
.
Assume Theorem
2.8
is true for
k-1
. Let
W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,Y^{\prime})

Then for general
V\in|D|
,
\Psi(V\cap W)=V\cap\Psi(W).

Proof.

This follows mutatis mutandis from the proof of the preceding Proposition.
∎

\Phi
is injective even when
k\geq 1

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
d\gg e^{\prime}
, let
k\geq 0
. Then the map

\Phi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(X,X^{\prime})

is injective.

Proof.

Our proof will be by induction on
k
. If
k=0
, this follows by Theorem
2.5
. Let
W^{1},W^{2}\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,Z)
. Let us assume
\Phi(W^{1})=\Phi(W^{2})
. We must establish that
W^{1}=W^{2}.

It suffices to establish that for a general hyperplane
V\in|D|
that
V\cap W^{1}=V\cap W^{2}
. Now for a general hyperplane
V
, both
V\cap W^{1}
and
V\cap W^{2}
are irreducible dominant correspondences and furthermore
\Phi(V\cap W^{i})=V\cap\Phi(W^{i})
by Proposition
8.3
. However by induction,
\Phi
is injective on lower degree correspondences. Thus,
V\cap W^{1}=V\cap W^{2}
which is what we wanted to prove.
∎

Proposition 8.6
.

d^{\prime}\gg e
, let

Proposition 8.7
.

Let
d\gg e^{\prime}.
Let
k\geq 0
. Then the map

\Phi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,X^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(X,X^{\prime})

is surjective and that furthermore irreducible correspondences are sent to irreducible correspondences.

Proof.

Our proof will be by induction on
k
, the case
k=0
follows by Theorem
2.5
.
Assume the proposition is true for
k-1
.
Let
W_{X}\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(X,X^{\prime})
. We will establish that there exists
W\in{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}
such that
\Phi(W)=W_{X}.
We may assume without loss of generality that
W_{X}
is irreducible.
Let
(V_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}
be a general pencil in
|D|
. For general
t\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
V_{t}\cap W_{X}
is an irreducible dominant
k-1
correspondence.

Let
L\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be a Zariski open such that for
t\in L,

V_{t}\cap W_{X}
is an irreducible dominant correspondence. By the induction hypothesis, for each such
V_{t}\cap W_{X}
there is a unique dominant correspondence
W_{t}\subseteq V_{t}\cap Y\times X^{\prime}
such that
\Phi(W_{t})=V_{t}\cap W_{X}
.

Let
\mathrm{Hilb}^{\circ}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y\times X^{\prime})
denote the open subscheme of the Hilbert scheme of
Y\times X^{\prime}
parametrizing dominant
k
correspondences of degree
\leq(e,e^{\prime})
. Let
\mathrm{Hilb}^{\circ}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(X\times X^{\prime})
denote the open subscheme parametrizing dominant
k
correspondences of degree
\leq(e,de^{\prime})
. Note that neither of the above two schemes is necessarily connected.

Then, in light of Proposition
6.3
we have a stratification of
(\mathrm{Hilb}^{\circ}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y\times X^{\prime}))_{red}
by subvarieties
S_{\alpha}
such that on each stratum there is a map
\Phi:S_{\alpha}\to(\mathrm{Hilb}^{\circ}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(X\times X^{\prime}))_{red}
realizing
\Phi
on points.

Due to our induction hypothesis,
\Phi:\coprod S_{\alpha}\to\mathrm{Hilb}_{\leq(e,de^{\prime})}(Y\times Z)
is surjective and
\Phi
is injective on each
S_{\alpha}
. Therefore, one can find a fixed stratum
\alpha_{0}
such that the image of
S_{\alpha_{0}}
contains
W_{X}\cap V_{t}
for general
t\in L
.

This then implies that we have an open subset
L_{0}\subseteq L
lying in the image of
S_{\alpha}
, such that the map
\Phi
is invertible when restricted to the preimage of
L_{0}
(this uses the fact that a bijective morphism, in this case the pullback of
\Phi
to
L
, is birational).
We then may pullback the universal family along
\Phi^{-1}
to
L_{0}
, this gives us a family of generically irreducible varieties
\mathcal{W}\to L_{0}
.
We let
W
be the image of
\mathcal{W}
in
Y\times X^{\prime}
, it is an irreducible correspondence. We now claim that
\Phi(W)=W_{X}
. This will establish the result.

We note that by construction,
W\cap V_{t}
contains
W_{X}\cap V_{t}
and as a result,
W_{X}\subseteq W\cap X\times X^{\prime}
. Since
W_{X}
consists only of dominating correspondences, this implies that
W_{X}\subseteq\Phi(W)
as well.

We will now establish that
\Phi(W)\subseteq W_{X}
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\Phi(W)
contains another irreducible component
W_{X}^{\prime}
. This
W_{X}^{\prime}
would have to be a dominating correspondence dominating both
X
and
X^{\prime}
.
However, we note that for
t\in L_{0}
,
\Phi(W_{t})=V_{t}\cap W_{X}
and thus
W^{\prime}_{X}\cap V_{t}
can not contain a component dominating both
X
and
X^{\prime}.

But for general
x\in X
,
W_{X}^{\prime}\cap(\{x\}\times X^{\prime})
is a positive dimensional subvariety and must intersect
V_{t}
since since
V_{t}
is an ample divisor, Thus,
W^{\prime}_{X}\cap V_{t}
does in fact dominate
X
(and
X^{\prime}
by the same argument).

However this leads to the following contradiction: we may apply Proposition
6.1
to obtain a subvariety
U\subseteq W^{\prime}_{X}
, such that for general
t
,
U\cap V_{t}
consists of all components of
W_{X}^{\prime}\cap V_{t}
dominating
X
. But since
W^{\prime}_{X}
is irreducible and
U
is equidimensional with
W_{X}^{\prime}
, for general
t
, each component of
W_{X}^{\prime}\cap V_{t}
must be contained in
U
and must therefore dominate
X.
By the same argument, for general
t
, each component of
W_{X}^{\prime}\cap V_{t}
must dominate
X^{\prime}
as well. But this is a contradiction. Thus no such
W_{X}^{\prime}
can exist and
\Phi(W)=W_{X}
.

∎

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
d^{\prime}\gg e.
Let
k\geq 0
. Then the map

\Psi:{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k+1}_{\leq(e,e^{\prime})}(Y,Y^{\prime})\to{\mathrm{DomCor}}^{k}(Y,X^{\prime})

is surjective and takes irreducible correspondences to irreducible correspondences.

Proof.

This follows mutatis mutandis from the proof of the previous proposition.
∎

2.6
and

.

9.
Usage of smoothness

In this section we will comment a little on the smoothness hypotheses on
X
in Theorem
2.5
and more generally to our other Theorems. No generalisation to the case when
X
is arbitrary is possible (though possibly we can relax the statement to normal with canonical singularities)- This is illustrated by the following example brought to our attention by Olivier Martin- take a high degree map from
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. Let
X
be the image. Then if an analogue of Theorem
2.5
held in this situation, the birational map
X{\dashrightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
would extend (as a rational map) to all of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. However, this is not the case.

This immediately prompts the question of where in the proof are we using smoothness. Essentially the main usage of smoothness is in all the steps where we argue that some set of points on a variety is Cayley- Bacharach for
K_{X}.
For example the proof of
5.2
heavily uses smoothness of
X
. This may not be apparent at first glance, as most of the argument that explicitly requires smoothness appears in the results that we cite from other authors, who in turn cite other authors.
Hidden behind the lemmas we cite is Proposition 4.2 of
[
2
]
which involves studying the trace map on differentials coming from a correspondence. This requires that
X
is smooth, though it is conceivable that one could get some version of this result with weaker conditions on
X
.

10.
What goes wrong in characteristic
p
?

In this final section we will comment a little bit on what parts of our paper do not go through to characteristic
p
. Not only do our methods not work, we do not believe that the analogous results are themselves true in positive characteristic. The paper
[
6
]
shows that in positive characteristic result certain measures of irrationality of complete intersections behave a bit differently than in characteristic zero and it is not inconceivable that the same would be true for measures of association as well.

Primarily our main issue is in dealing with inseparable correspondences, in proving Theorem
5.4
we use the fact that given a dominant correspondence
W\subseteq Y\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and a general point
z\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, the set
W\cap X\times\{z\}
is a Cayley Bacharach set for
K_{X}
. This uses the fact that the map
W\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is separable, the proof of the above fact involves induced differentials. We do not know how to find an alternative construction in positive characteristic.

We did consider the idea of looking at only separable dominant correspondences. The problem there was that
\Phi
and
\Psi
do not take separable correspondences to separable correspondences and thus it hard to isolate just separable correspondences.
