Soft Electromechanical Elastomers Impervious to Instability Preprint: To appear in Journal of Applied Mechanics (DOI: 10.1115/1.4068630 )
Daniel Katusele, Carmel Majidi, Kaushik Dayal, Pradeep Sharma

Abstract
Soft dielectric elastomers that can exhibit extremely large deformations under the action of an electric field enable applications such as soft robotics, biomedical devices, energy harvesting among others. A key impediment in the use of dielectric elastomers is failure through instability mechanisms or dielectric breakdown. In this work, using a group-theory based approach, we provide a closed-form solution to the bifurcation problem of a paradigmatical elastomer actuator and discover an interesting result: at a critical electric field, the elastomer becomes impervious to Treloar-Kearsley instability. This limit is reached prior to the typical dielectric breakdown threshold. Our results thus establish a regime of electrical and mechanical loads where the dielectric elastomer is invulnerable to all common failure modes.

A compelling argument for the use of soft dielectric elastomers (DEs) is their ability to sustain large mechanical deformation under the action of an electrical field. Such a feature is a necessity for applications like biomimetic robotics or devices such as an adaptive eye lens. The facile deformability and the complex nonlinear material behavior that underlies its mechanics, also gives rise to instabilities such as wrinkling, creasing, pull-in instability, Treloar-Kearsley instability and others

Building on this solution, we find that an applied electric field of a certain critical strength completely suppresses the T-K instability.

4
Background on Singularity Theory

A bifurcation analysis of the dielectric employing singularity theory is presented below. This section is adapted from
chen2001singularity
with references from
golubitsky2012singularitiesI
and
golubitsky2012singularitiesII
.
Singularity theory is a mathematical tool that seeks to reduce a singular function to a simple normal form from which the properties of the bifurcation solution can be determined from a finite number of derivatives of the singular function.
We will be concerned with local bifurcation problems of the form

f(u,\kappa)=0

(14)

near a point
\displaystyle(u_{0},\kappa_{0})
, where
\displaystyle u
represents state variables and
\displaystyle\kappa
is the bifurcation parameter. Classically,
\displaystyle(u_{0},\kappa_{0})
is called a bifurcation point if the number of solutions changes as
\displaystyle\kappa
changes in the neighborhood of
\displaystyle\kappa_{0}
. We consider the principal branch
\displaystyle u
bifurcating near
\displaystyle u_{0}
when
\displaystyle\kappa
increases past
\displaystyle\kappa_{0}
.

An illustrative example of a bifurcation is the elastica problem of buckling of a column subjected to compressive forces
antman1995nonlinear
.
The principal branch is the unbuckled state while the buckled state is the bifurcated branch.
The T-K instability is another example of a bifurcation problem where a Mooney-Rivlin material stretched biaxially experience a symmetric stretch up to a bifurcation where the stretch becomes asymmetric leading to stable and unstable solutions
kearsley1986asymmetric
.
In this section will will explore the former to illustrate the singularity theory and the latter will be analysed in the subsequent sections.

The governing equation for the elastica problem is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle EIu^{\prime\prime}(s)+\kappa\sin u(s)=0,\quad 0<s<l,

(15)

where
\displaystyle u
is the angle between the undeformed rod and the tangent of the deformed rod,
\displaystyle s
the material coordinate,
\displaystyle E
the elastic modulus,
\displaystyle I
the moment of inertia,
\displaystyle\kappa
the compressive applied force, and
\displaystyle l
the length of the rod.
\displaystyle u
is a state variable and
\displaystyle\kappa
is the bifurcation parameter. The rod is hinged at its ends, with the boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(l)=0.

(16)

In linear beam theory, the assumption
\displaystyle|u(s)|\ll 1
leads to a linearized equation of the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle EIu^{\prime\prime}(s)+\kappa u(s)=0

(17)

with the non-trivial solution:

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(s)=C\cos\frac{n\pi s}{l},\text{ if and only if }\kappa=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}EI}{l^{2}}

(18)

where
\displaystyle n
is an integer. The lowest non-zero value
\displaystyle\kappa_{cr}=\pi^{2}EI/l^{2}
is the critical buckling load.

The solution to the linearized equation (
17
) gives some insight into the solution of (
15
), however, there are issues that cannot be addressed by the linearized analysis:

•

The necessary condition for bifurcation is the existence of a non-trivial solution near bifurcation point. However, this condition is not sufficient. Consider the nonlinear equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle x^{3}+\kappa x=0

(19)

where
\displaystyle x
is a real state variable and
\displaystyle\kappa
is a real bifurcation parameter. The equation (
19
) admits a trivial solution
\displaystyle x=0
for all values of
\displaystyle\kappa
. The linearlized equation
\displaystyle\kappa x=0
has non-trivial solution
\displaystyle x=\mathrm{const.}
for
\displaystyle\kappa=0
. The nonlinear equation, however, has no bifurcation solution branch at
\displaystyle\kappa=0
.

•

When a bifurcation branch exists, its qualitative behavior cannot be derived from the solution of the linearized equation. It is not possible to establish how many bifurcation branches there are and how these branches evolve as the bifurcation parameter varies. Consider, for illustration,

\displaystyle\displaystyle x^{3}-\kappa x=0

(20)

which has a trivial solution
\displaystyle x=0
for all
\displaystyle\kappa
. The linearized equation
\displaystyle\kappa x=0
has nontrivial solutions
\displaystyle x=\mathrm{const.}
near
\displaystyle\kappa=0
. It is, however, not possible to determine the number of bifurcation branches nor their evolution post-bifurcation. The nonlinear equation on the other hand admits solutions
\displaystyle x=0
,
\displaystyle x=\sqrt{\kappa}
and
\displaystyle x=-\sqrt{\kappa}
when
\displaystyle\kappa>0
as post-bifurcation branches.

Singularity theory is developed to address these issues in a systematic fashion.
It first employs the Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction to show that the solution to (
14
), which is defined in a function space in this case, is equivalent to that of an algebraic equation with one state variable.
Next, by solving the recognition problem, the solution to the algebraic equation can be shown to be equivalent to that of a polynomial that exhibit the same bifurcation type in the neighborhood of an origin.
Singularity theory can be expanded to bifurcation systems with symmetry. These equations presenting symmetry can be shown to be equivariant under certain group actions. Hence, the coupling of singularity theory and group theory provide tool for gaining insight into the persistence and/or the change of the symmetry that a bifurcation branch possesses near a bifurcation point
chen2001singularity
.

The early concepts of singularity theory were first proposed by R. Thom, then developed rigorously by J. Mather
mather1969stability
;
mather1970stability
, and later extended by V. I. Anorld
arnold1976local
;
arnold1981singularity
. Subsequently, the development of singularity theory was systematized, then combined with group theory by M. Golubitsky, I. Stewart, and D. G. Schaeffer
golubitsky2012singularitiesI
;
golubitsky2012singularitiesII
.

4.A
Liapunov-Schmidt reduction

Consider a smooth mapping
\displaystyle f\colon U\subset X\times K\to Y
where
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are Banach spaces (i.e., complete normed vector spaces),
\displaystyle U
is an open subset of
\displaystyle X
, and
\displaystyle K
is an open subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The equation of interest is written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(u,\kappa)=0

(21)

where
\displaystyle u
determines the state of the system (e.g., deformation, temperature),
\displaystyle\kappa
is a set of parameters (e.g., loads, geometry, material parameters), and
\displaystyle f
is a nonlinear differential operator.
\displaystyle f
is Fréchet-differentiable at
\displaystyle(u_{0},\kappa_{0})\in(U,K)
with respect to
\displaystyle u\in U
if there exists a bounded linear operator
\displaystyle D_{u}f(u_{0},\kappa_{0})\colon X\to Y
, referred to as the first-order Fréchet derivative, such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\|f(u_{0}+h,\kappa_{0})-f(u_{0},\kappa_{0})-hD_{u}f(u_{0},\kappa_{0})\|}{\|h\|}=0

(22)

where
\displaystyle\|h\|=\|u-u_{0}\|
and
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
is a norm induced by an inner product on a Hilbert space which will be discussed below.

4.B
Recognition problem

The singularity theory approach to bifurcation problems focuses on two issues.
First, the importance of higher order derivatives in the Taylor expansion of (
21
) in determining the qualitative behavior of the solution. In other words, to what extent is the qualitative behavior determined by low-order derivatives of (
21
)? The singularity theory term for this problem is
finite determinacy
.
Second, finding a polynomial equation as simple as possible whose solution is in one-to-one correspondence with the given equation near a bifurcation point.
This is termed as the
recognition problem
and the polynomial is referred to as the
normal form
which can be determined by a finite number of derivatives of the given equation.
The normal form will have the same qualitative behavior as the reduced bifurcation equation near the bifurcation.

Here, we focus on solving recognition problem for the reduced bifurcation problem in (
30
) of the form
\displaystyle g(x,\kappa)
.
We choose a 1-d space (
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
) for both the state variable and bifurcation parameter, and choose the bifurcation point to be at the origin
\displaystyle(0,0)
for convenience without loss of generality.

We define two smooth mappings
\displaystyle g,h\colon\mathcal{N}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as

if there exist functions
\displaystyle X(x,\kappa)
and
\displaystyle S(x,\kappa)
such that the relation

g(x,\kappa)=S(x,\kappa)h(X(x,\kappa),\kappa),

(51a)

holds near the origin and that the conditions

(51b)

are satisfied.
The most important consequence of equivalence is that the number of solutions of (
30

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(X,\kappa)=0.

(52)

To prove this, suppose that for a given
\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle g(x,\kappa)
has exactly
\displaystyle n
solutions of the form
\displaystyle x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{n}

From (

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(X,\kappa)=0~\text{if and only if}~X=X(x_{i},\kappa),i=1,\ldots,n.

\displaystyle X(x_{1},\kappa)<X(x_{2},\kappa)<\ldots<X(x_{n},\kappa)

The main idea behind the recognition problem is to explicitly characterize the smooth mappings which are strongly equivalent to
\displaystyle g
near the origin.
Much of singularity theory is devoted to finding the simplest normal form for a certain function for which a number of derivatives are given or computed at a certain bifurcation point. It is worth mentioning that for more complicated problems, with various degrees of sophistication, it may not be practical to derive the normal form from the derivatives.

In the next section, we will demonstrate, by using elementary calculus, how a normal form can be constructed and some conditions of equivalence between two functions are established through the examination of their tangent spaces.

In the example, we used elementary calculus to derive the defining and degeneracy conditions for the pitchfork bifurcation. Chief among these is the Taylor expansion of the smooth function
\displaystyle g
, which consist of a linear expansion of the partial derivatives with respect to both the state variable and the bifurcation parameter in the neighborhood of the origin. These partial derivatives of
\displaystyle g
can be grouped into three classes, namely, low-, intermediate- and high-order terms. These play an important role in solving the recognition problem. It is worth mentioning here that we have only considered a simple bifurcation problem. For more general bifurcation problems, sophisticated techniques from group theory and algebra might be needed, as described in
golubitsky2012singularitiesI
and
golubitsky2012singularitiesII
to solve the recognition problem. We will restrict our focus to the simple cases while explaining, briefly and without proof, some concepts necessary for understanding the procedure.

The low-order terms in the Taylor expansion are the monomials of the form
\displaystyle x^{p}\kappa^{q}
such that the corresponding partial derivatives
\displaystyle\partial^{p+q}g/\partial x^{p}\partial\kappa^{q}=0
in the defining conditions. In the example above, the low order terms will be
\displaystyle 1,x,\kappa
and
\displaystyle x^{2}
associated with the partial derivatives in (
62
). These terms are excluded from the Taylor expansion and the subsequent equivalence transformation in (
4.B.1
).

The higher order terms of the expansion are those that can be transformed out of the expression through strong equivalence as was done in (
4.B.1
). For our example, these are
\displaystyle\kappa^{2},\kappa x^{2},\kappa^{2}x,\kappa^{3},\ldots
. These can be identified through an analysis of the normal form and its perturbations employing the theorem stated above. These terms can be incorporated with other terms in the Taylor expansion after an appropriate redefinition of the coefficients. It is also worth mentioning that the higher-order terms do not correspond, in general, to monomials
\displaystyle x^{p}\kappa^{q}
of the Taylor expansion whose associated derivatives
\displaystyle\partial^{p+q}g/\partial x^{p}\partial\kappa^{q}
are not in neither the defining nor the degeneracy conditions. Some higher order monomials will be absorbed into other monomials through the equivalence transformation; thus they will not appear in the normal form even though their associated partial derivatives appear in either the defining or nondegeneracy conditions.

The monomial terms that are neither low-order nor higher-order terms are those that remain in the normal form. These are referred to as intermediate-order terms. After the given function is reduced to finite intermediate-order terms, it only remains to reduce their coefficients to constants to get the final expression of the normal form.

6
Concluding Remarks

There are two key failure modes for a dielectric elastomer: (i) dielectric breakdown, which occurs when the applied electrical voltage exceeds a critical threshold, and (ii) the triggering of mechanical instability. While the latter can sometimes be exploited for innovative designs, it is often undesirable. In this work, we derive closed-form solutions to the bifurcation problem governing the electromechanical loading of a dielectric disk. Specifically, we discover that in the presence of a through-thickness electric field, there exists a critical value beyond which an infinite mechanical force would be required to trigger Treloar-Kearsley instability. This allows us to determine the conditions necessary to design a dielectric elastomer configuration that is impervious to instability. In principle, our approach can be extended to any structural configuration, although numerical computations may be required for more complex shapes and boundary conditions.

The dielectric breakdown of DEs depends on the elastic modulus as well as the relative permittivity, defined as
\displaystyle(1+\chi)
as used in the electrical energy density in (
6
)
STARK1955Electric
.
A high-performance DE capable of large strain under electric field reported in
Li2022Dielectric
has a normalized dielectric breakdown strength
\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\text{b}}=0.6\sqrt{3}
, obtained from experimental results.
This breakdown strength, which is typical of high-performance DE, is well in excess of the
\displaystyle\hat{E}_{3}
values needed to suppress the T-K instability following the strategy proposed in this paper.
