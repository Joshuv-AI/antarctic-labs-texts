A systematic review of non-binary measures of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes and reactions, and associated outcomes
Authors: Karen Trister Grace, Erin K George, Elizabeth A Mosley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground“Unintended pregnancy” is a ubiquitous indicator of poor public health, but is highly problematic and limited. A conceptual evolution in pregnancy intention measurement and categorization has led to numerous, often hard-to-compare studies using a multitude of non-binary, multi-dimensional pregnancy measures. Herein we conducted a systematic review of research studying non-binary measures of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions, as predictors of public health outcomes.MethodsForty-three articles met inclusion criteria. We summarized existing pregnancy measures, cataloged the dimensions of pregnancy they measure, and tracked significant outcomes associated with these measures.ResultsWe documented numerous pregnancy-related measures including those on ambivalence, acceptability, timing, intention, emotional response, use of contraception, preparation for pregnancy, opinions/reactions of partners and others, perceived fecundity, readiness, denial, and self-efficacy to prevent pregnancy. The strongest associations with maternal and child health outcomes were observed for depression. Relationships with pregnancy complications and outcomes, postpartum care, breastfeeding, attachment, and child health are very unclear. Temporal confounding precludes many conclusions about psychosocial outcomes.ConclusionsAcknowledging multiple dimensions of and pathways to pregnancy is critical for researchers in public health, clinical care, and the social sciences. Research questions should be clear in their hypothesized relationships and pathways, and specific in their dimensions of interest. New paradigms are needed that move beyond pregnancy planning and intention.Keywords: Pregnancy intention, Unintended pregnancy, Reproductive health, Research measures, Health equity

Background

“Pregnancy intention” refers to a complex interplay of plans, perceptions, attitudes, and reactions leading up to and following a pregnancy. Researchers and clinicians have sought to measure pregnancy intention in a multitude of ways. “Unintended pregnancy” has historically been conceptualized as a poor public health indicator which should be targeted for prevention [1]. The rationale for measuring pregnancy intention has been purported associations between unintended pregnancies and poor outcomes [2–4].

It is commonly reported that in the US, 40–45% of pregnancies fall into the “unintended” category (that is, pregnancies that were mistimed or when the person did not want to be pregnant ever in the future) [5, 6]. However, many researchers argue this overestimates prevalence, and simplifies a highly complex experience [7, 8]. Evidence linking pregnancy intention to poor health outcomes is mixed and has been critiqued for not measuring confounding factors and offering a reductionist view of pregnancy which fails to capture its nuanced reality for many people [7, 9]. It is also asserted that the high prevalence of people who report being happy about pregnancies that are categorized as unintended indicates a need for new measures [10]. Reported pregnancy intention is vulnerable to perception of social acceptability, as less advantaged people may feel stigmatized if they describe a pregnancy as planned [11]. Additionally, the concept of unintended pregnancy is criticized as inherently racist and classist, which has been disproportionately problematized in relation to poor people and people of color [11–13], a phenomenon known as stratified reproduction [14, 15]. This framing inevitably risks coercive and eugenicist solutions [16, 17].

An alternative to these problematic conceptualizations of pregnancy intention centers Reproductive Justice and the Reproductive & Sexual Health Equity Framework (RSHEF). Reproductive Justice, a social movement and theoretical framework combining reproductive rights and social justice, and recognizing intersectional identities of race, class and gender (among others), reflects the right to not have children, the right to have children, and the right to safely parent children in supportive communities free of reproductive and bodily coercion including from structural factors [18]. These ideas are also reflected in the RSHEF [19], which provides principles to guide work in this area on equity and structural factors, centering marginalized communities, and addressing the impact of historical harms. Well-meaning researchers, providers, and policy makers may feel certain that some groups of people (usually poor, young, less educated, racially minoritized) would be helped by highly effective methods of contraception, but without the guiding RSHEF and principles of Reproductive Justice, harmful and coercive interventions and practices are likely.

Historically, pregnancy intention has been conceptualized and measured as a binary construct: pregnancy is either intended or unintended [5]. A 2017 systematic review of pregnancy intention outcomes noted the preponderance of studies that dichotomize intention and the limitations of oversimplifying different measures into a measurement of one construct [20]. Recently this paradigm has been expanded to encompass a multi-dimensional continuum. An example is the Unintended Pregnancy Risk Index, which incorporates pregnancy desire, likelihood, and contraception use from population-based data, to estimate annual risk of unintended pregnancy and evaluate trends over time [21]. Qualitative research adds depth to our understanding of these many dimensions. Participants in one study expressed pregnancy attitudes that researchers grouped as pro-pregnancy, ambivalent, and anti-pregnancy [22], and in another, pregnancy was conceptualized as a deliberate process, a pre-determined process, or a combination of the two [23]. Prior to the suspension of the Centers for Disease Control's Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) in 2025 [24], states collected population-based pregnancy data, historically measured with one question asking how the person felt about becoming pregnant, with responses dichotomized into intended and unintended [25]. In 2012, the option “I wasn’t sure what I wanted” was added, to incorporate ambivalence. Researchers found this change drew responses from all other previous question options (“I wanted to be pregnant sooner/ later/ never”) but did not draw responses from “I wanted to be pregnant then”, indicating the “sooner,” “later,” and “never” answer choices were multi-dimensional and heterogenous, and should be broken down further [25].

The conceptual evolution in pregnancy intention measurement and categorization has led to many hard-to-compare studies of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, reactions and outcomes, using a multitude of non-binary, multi-dimensional pregnancy measures [20]. The current science around pregnancy perceptions, attitudes and reactions leads to the question: which dimensions of pregnancy non-planning or un-intention are associated with poor outcomes? To answer this, we conducted a systematic review of research that used non-binary measures of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions as predictors of health outcomes, specifically conducted in US settings. We limited our review to the US due to the country’s unique political climate surrounding abortion access and stratified reproduction.

Methods

Following the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26], authors established article eligibility criteria, which included quantitative or mixed method studies conducted in US settings, published in any year, that included non-binary measures of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions. Studies were excluded if they were qualitative, not published in English, only looked at pregnancy perceptions, attitudes and reactions as dichotomous variables, or only looked at pregnancy wantedness/intention as an outcome variable. To synthesize results, studies were grouped by dimension being measured (e.g., timing). Keywords were chosen to conduct searches across four databases: CINAHL, Medline, Web of Science, and Global Health, including pregnancy intention or pregnancy ambivalence and instrument, measure, continuum, range, or assessment (("pregnancy intention" or "pregnan* ambivalen*") AND ( instrument* or measure* or continuum or range or changing or assessment)). All searches were conducted in September 2023.

A total of 786 articles was retrieved (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, 322 articles were imported using Rayyan [27] for independent blinded review by two reviewers. Abstract-only review was deemed insufficient to determine the levels of variables being measured, so all articles were included for a full text review. Discrepancies were discussed until agreement was reached. Forty-three articles met inclusion criteria. Variables of interest for data extraction included: whether studies were retrospective or prospective in design; how pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions were categorized and measured (utilizing any and all aspects of these dimensions that were measured in included studies); and all significant outcomes. Data were extracted and compiled in a Word table for summary and analysis, and measured dimensions were summarized graphically.

Results

Descriptive summary of included studies

The 43 studies that were included for analysis were published between 1998 [28] and 2023 [29–31] (Table 1). Over half of studies used retrospective measures (n = 26), and one study evaluated pregnancy intention in real-time, as participants were waiting for pregnancy test results [32]. The included articles were assessed for quality using the JBI Analytical Cross-Sectional Study checklist [33]. All articles met the quality benchmarks used in this tool.

Summary of measures

Studies used a wide variety of pregnancy-related measures, capturing an array of dimensions related to pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions. Most studies used categorical pregnancy variables, but 10 used continuous measures [41, 45, 46, 53, 59–61, 63, 65, 70]. Some measures categorize continuous measures such as the London Measure of Unplanned Pregnancy (LMUP) [42, 46, 63] and others [45]. The LMUP is a rigorously developed measure [71] which has been validated in multiple languages [72], and may be used as a continuous scale or a categorical variable [73]. Thirty-four studies incorporated concepts of ambivalence, an ambiguous category encompassing multiple concepts that do not all meet the strict definition of ambivalence (mixed feelings, which may be strong but contradictory [74]). These include indifference (not caring or having low levels of feelings on either side), uncertainty, changing feelings, and lack of agreement with a partner.

Ambivalence was also reflected in composite categories such as neutral/indifferent, positively ambivalent and negatively ambivalent [43], and mistimed/neutral and unwanted/neutral [32], or incorporated in continuous measures that, by design, allowed for ambivalence between extremes on the scale [32, 50, 53, 55, 59–61, 65].

In addition to ambivalence, studies captured 15 different pregnancy dimensions (Fig. 2). These included acceptability (e.g., “How okay would it be if you got pregnant right now?” [35, 50], anticipation, such as anticipating the financial impact of a baby [53], and perceived consequences [61, 65]. 14 studies asked about aspects of trying to get or not get pregnant. For example, if they were “definitely trying to get pregnant” [47], “not currently trying” [56], or “trying hard not to get pregnant” [60].

Seventeen studies used composite measures incorporating multiple dimensions either to create scales or multi-dimensional categories [28, 32, 35, 39–43, 45, 53, 59, 61, 63, 65, 66, 69, 70].

Also commonly captured was pregnancy timing, which was incorporated into measures in 30 studies, with questions such as ‘‘What did you think the best age would be for you to have your first child?’’ [55], and “I feel that my pregnancy happened at the right time, ok but not quite right time, or wrong time.” [42, 46].

Dimensions of “intention”, “planning” and “wanting/desire” capture similar but slightly distinct constructs. Intention, a cognitive action, was used in 7 studies, with measures such as “Just before I became pregnant, I intended to get pregnant/my intentions kept changing/I did not intend to get pregnant” [42, 45, 46, 63]. This is a subtle distinction from measures of planning, which implies a slightly more active step with more commitment than simply intending, and which was used in 11 studies. These asked when or if participants planned on having a baby [41, 56, 59, 64, 70], or simply “Was this pregnancy planned?” [47]. More commonly, 33 studies incorporated “wanting” into their measures. For example, the One Key Question measure includes an answer response “I never want to become pregnant” [65] and others ask for agreement with the phrase “I want this pregnancy at this time” [55].

Emotional response to pregnancy was captured in 14 studies. For example, one asked “How would you feel if you found out you were pregnant today?” [43]. Many studies measured level of happiness about a pregnancy [28, 39, 42, 43, 46, 50, 53–55, 59].

Some measures capture objective evidence of pregnancy planning and prevention. Use of contraception was incorporated into measures in 7 studies, as an objective indicator of trying to prevent pregnancy when it occurred. For example, Crissey incorporated contraception in composite categories such as “unintended pregnancy with birth control used” [40]. The LMUP incorporates frequency of contraceptive use as one component of a composite score and also asks about specific steps taken to prepare for a pregnancy, such as taking folic acid and quitting smoking [42, 45, 46, 63].

Opinions, reactions, or involvement of partners and others were captured in 9 studies. The LMUP asks about level of agreement with a partner [42, 45, 46, 63], and 2 measures ask about perception of partner’s pregnancy intention [62, 66]. One study inquired about “not wanting others to know about the pregnancy” [28] and one measured level of dread telling friends about the pregnancy [50].

Three studies incorporated perceived fecundity into their measures. One includes self or partner not thinking they can get pregnant in an unintended pregnancy risk level [69], and two ask about perceived likelihood of pregnancy [31, 59]

Several uncommon dimensions of pregnancy planning were captured in only one reviewed study. These included readiness to have a baby [55], and a composite variable of pregnancy denial, which incorporates prenatal care obstacles and not wanting to think about the pregnancy [28], and self-efficacy to prevent pregnancy [64].

Summary of outcomes associated with measured dimensions

Several health outcomes, psychosocial experiences, and substance use patterns were found to be associated with measured dimensions across studies. These experiences and outcomes occurred across preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum periods and had effects on both the pregnant person and neonate/child, though findings were at times conflicting or paradoxical (see Fig. 3 “Outcomes significantly associated with non-binary measures of pregnancy intention” for summary of outcomes).

Preconception

Seventeen studies examined outcomes during the preconception period, and significant associations were identified with outcomes of contraceptive use [35, 43, 53, 61, 64], preconception counseling [56], folic acid intake [38, 65, 67], physical activity [70], alcohol and smoking [67], and the impact of preconception counseling on prenatal care adequacy [68]. During the preconception period, contraception use was weakly to moderately associated with pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions. Contraception use and method effectiveness were associated with higher scores on a pregnancy non-intention scale (a combination of planning and anticipated problems and happiness; OR 1.14) [53], desire to avoid pregnancy (OR 1.63) [61], and lower LMUP scores (OR not reported) [63]. Lower pregnancy acceptability scores were strongly associated with use of highly effective contraception (OR 2.97) [35]. Ambivalence and trying to avoid pregnancy were also associated with consistent contraceptive use (OR 2.20–2.53) [39, 43]. Conversely, there were moderate associations found among those planning a pregnancy in the future and discontinuing contraception, compared to participants who did not intend to ever get pregnant (IRRs 1.42–3.41) [64]. Those who were not planning (using an adapted LMUP) and trying to avoid pregnancy had lower odds of having a positive pregnancy test, compared to participants who were uncertain about or planning to become pregnant [45]. Finally, adolescents were more likely to become pregnant if they were planning to become pregnant or had inconsistent attitudes and expectations [59], in bivariate analyses.

Strong associations were found with preconception counseling and folic acid intake. People feeling unsure about future pregnancy plans—compared to participants currently pregnant—experienced lower odds of receiving preconception counseling on folic acid, management of chronic medical and mental health conditions during pregnancy, and medication safety in pregnancy (ORs 0.27–0.44) [56]. The odds that participants took folic acid were higher among participants who wanted to become pregnant and lower among participants with higher desire to avoid pregnancy (ORs 1.57–3.36 and 0.14) [38, 65, 67, 69].

Trying to get pregnant now compared to not trying (OR not provided), and increases in pregnancy intention over time were weakly associated with increased levels of physical activity, compared to no changes in intention (adjusted β, 0.08) [41, 70] and moderately associated with a lower likelihood of consuming alcohol or binge drinking, and smoking (OR 0.54–0.72) [67] during the preconception period. Authors of another study found that intending pregnancy was moderately associated with the impact of preconception care on prenatal care adequacy (OR 1.22–1.63) [68].

Pregnancy

Thirteen studies examined pregnancy outcomes, and significant associations were identified with outcomes of early recognition of pregnancy [30, 49], prenatal care [28, 30, 44, 55, 58], antepartum depression [46, 55, 58], and pregnancy complications [30, 34, 51, 58]. During pregnancy, participants had lower odds that they would recognize pregnancy by 8 weeks if it was unwanted and initiate early prenatal care if pregnancy was mistimed (ORs 0.20–0.40) [30]. Pregnancy ambivalence, as well as unwantedness, unintendedness, mistiming, uncertainty, unhappiness, denial and lack of readiness for pregnancy were all associated with late prenatal care initiation and inadequate prenatal care, with mostly moderate strength (ORs 1.17–3.44) [28, 44, 55, 58]. Participants who wanted to be pregnant then or sooner were less likely to recognize pregnancy later than 6 weeks and to initiate prenatal care late (OR 0.3–0.8) [49] and less likely to delay prenatal care initiation (OR 0.49) [67]. The strongest associations were between pregnancy denial and inadequate and late prenatal care (OR 4.82–6.69) [28].

Strong evidence from one study supports an association with antepartum depression, while evidence was weak to moderate in other studies. Participants had higher odds of self-reporting depression if they were not ready to become pregnant (OR 2.21) [55] or were experiencing a mistimed, unwanted, unintended, or ambivalent pregnancy (ORs 3.2–9.23) [46, 58]. In another study, a composite measure of pregnancy context was not associated with low mental health-related quality of life scores [42].

Several studies showed weak relationships with pregnancy complications (ORs 1.04–1.50). Participants expressing pregnancy ambivalence experienced inadequate weight gain compared to those who wanted pregnancy then or sooner [58], though another study found no association with recommended weight gain [49]. Several studies found no significant associations with gestational diabetes [51, 55]. Preterm labor and birth were associated with unwanted pregnancy and ambivalence or uncertainty about pregnancy 69, 70], with lower odds among participants with mistimed pregnancy (the only evidence of strong association, OR 0.33) [30]. However, two studies found non-significant associations [49, 55]. The authors of another study about the relationship between intention and pregnancy complications did not find any significant associations among pregnancy planning, timing, wanting, emotional response, or readiness and spontaneous abortion, hypertension, preeclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, and operative delivery [55].

Two studies focused on pregnancy outcomes, which were defined as 1) continue pregnancy and parent, 2) continue pregnancy and opt for adoption, or 3) terminate the pregnancy through abortion. In one study, participants awaiting pregnancy test results who reported mistimed pregnancy or feeling unhappy about pregnancy were more likely to be uncertain about their pregnancy plan or planning abortion or adoption, in bivariate analysis [32]. However, another longitudinal study of pregnancy outcomes following emergency contraception use did not find significant differences in actual outcomes according to whether or not the person was trying to get pregnant or avoid pregnancy (ORs not reported) [60].

Postpartum

Ten studies examined postpartum care and outcomes, and significant associations were identified with outcomes of postpartum visit attendance [49, 58], postpartum contraceptive use [57], breastfeeding [49, 58], postpartum depression [32, 37, 48, 51, 58, 64] and maternal attachment [54]. For participants who experienced unwanted or mistimed pregnancies or felt ambivalent or uncertain about pregnancy, there was a weak association with being less likely to attend a postpartum visit compared to participants who wanted to be pregnant (ORs 0.6–0.9, aPR 1.28) [49, 58]. Postpartum contraception use was highest among participants who experienced unintended pregnancies while contracepting and lowest among participants who experienced a later than desired pregnancy or felt uncertain about pregnancy in bivariate analysis [57]. Lower rates of breastfeeding at 3 months were weakly associated with participants who experienced unwanted pregnancies or felt uncertain or ambivalent about pregnancy (0.5–0.9) [49, 58]. However, the relationship with breastfeeding was not significant in three studies [29, 30, 44].

Postpartum depression had moderate associations with pregnancy attitudes. In most studies, mistimed and unwanted pregnancies were moderately associated with incidence of postpartum depression (ORs 1.76–2.67) [30, 36, 49, 58, 66]. One study found strong associations with participants who never wanted pregnancy among some age groups (ORs 2.7–7.8), but no association among adolescents [52]. Ambivalence and uncertainty about pregnancy were weakly associated with postpartum depression in two studies (ORs 1.19–1.30) [36, 58]. Moderately strong evidence showed mild postpartum depression was more likely among participants experiencing mistimed versus wanted pregnancies, while severe postpartum depression was more likely among participants experiencing unwanted to wanted pregnancies (ORs 1.15–2.12) [66]. Participants experiencing any mistimed, unwanted, or uncertain feelings about pregnancy were also less likely to experience relief from postpartum depression (ORs 0.5-0.9), compared to participants with postpartum depression who wanted their pregnancies [49].

Finally, maternal attachment was examined in one study. Participants who had unwanted pregnancies had the lowest levels of postpartum maternal attachment, while participants with mistimed pregnancies had lower levels compared to those with wanted pregnancies, though results were described as marginally significant [54].

Neonatal and child outcomes

Six included studies focused on outcomes related to the health of the neonate or child, and significant associations were identified with low birthweight [30, 51], infant attachment [50], child health and development [40], and childhood injury [30]. Two studies found that participants experiencing unwanted or mistimed pregnancies, or who felt ambivalent about pregnancy, had higher odds of giving birth to a low birthweight infant, compared to those with wanted pregnancies, though this effect was weak to moderate (aOR 1.15–1.65) [30, 51]. Two other studies did not find significant associations between pregnancy wanting, planning, timing, emotional response or readiness and infant size or weight [49, 55]. Positive maternal motivation (a composite of acceptability, anticipation, emotional response, reactions of friends, and trying to get pregnant) was associated with greater infant attachment to their mothers, and negative maternal motivations were associated with lower infant attachment (R2 0.375, a weak association) [50]. Only one study examined the relationship with infant mortality [49], and paradoxically found that infants were less likely to die under the age of 1 if their mothers did not want their pregnancies, compared to mothers who wanted their pregnancies (OR 0.1). They caution, however, that, given the rarity of this outcome, the more appropriate interpretation may be that infants are at least as likely to die among unwanted versus wanted pregnancies.

Authors of one study found that mistimed pregnancies compared to wanted were significantly but weakly associated with less than excellent child health, as was unintended without the use of birth control compared to intended without the use of birth control (ORs 1.17–1.21) [40]. The same study found that unwanted pregnancies compared to wanted were significantly but weakly associated with child over or under activity, as was unintended while using birth control compared to intended without the use of birth control (ORs 1.29–1.46). Unintended births without the use of birth control compared to intended births without the use of birth control were also weakly associated with lower child development scores (OR 1.29). Another study did not find a significant association between pregnancy wantedness and limited child activity or having a pediatric care provider [30]. However, in the same study, the odds of a child experiencing a past-year injury were lower among participants with mistimed pregnancies, compared with wanted pregnancies, with moderate strength (ORs 0.45–0.52).

Psychosocial

Thirteen studies examined various aspects of psychosocial behavior and relationships in relation to dimensions of pregnancy intention, including the influence of partners. Significant associations were identified with the influence of partners [44, 62], marriage [25], intimate partner violence (IPV) [49, 58], substance and alcohol use [41, 55, 69], smoking [30, 37, 49, 58, 69], social support [46], and activity levels [69, 70]. In one study, participants who initially wanted pregnancy were more likely to change to unwanted if their partners did not want pregnancy, and the reverse scenario was also significant [44]. One study that examined the role of paternal influence in pregnancy intention found that mothers who did not want to become pregnant were less likely to delay prenatal care if fathers did want to become pregnant, compared to both parents not wanting pregnancy (OR 0.54) [62]. Participants were more likely to experience marriage dissolution (OR 2.2) and less likely to experience marriage formation (OR 0.50) if they had an unwanted pregnancy [48]. In two studies, moderately strong evidence showed participants experiencing an unwanted, mistimed, or ambivalent pregnancy were more likely to experience IPV than participants with wanted pregnancy (ORs 1.1–2.8) [49, 58].

Several studies examined the relationship between pregnancy intentions/wantedness and substance use. While planning, timing, wantedness, emotional response, and readiness were not significantly associated with drug use in one study [55], pregnancy ambivalence was weakly associated with lower alcohol use (OR 0.72) [41]. Participants intending pregnancies were also more likely to use alcohol during the preconception period, and while this association was strong (ORs 6.90–10.85), the comparison group was participants who were already pregnant [69]. Participants who reported drinking during pregnancy were moderately more likely to have mistimed or unwanted pregnancies, or not be ready for pregnancy (OR 2.09) [51, 55]. Wanting the pregnancy was not significantly associated with reduced alcohol intake during pregnancy [49]. Smoking was moderately more likely among participants not planning pregnancy or doing anything to prevent pregnancy (adjusted prevalence odds ratio (aPOR) 2.89) [69] or with mistimed, unwanted, or ambivalent pregnancies (ORs 2.03–2.08) [30, 51]. Weak associations were found for participants being more likely to quit smoking during pregnancy if they had a wanted pregnancy compared to participants who were experiencing ambivalent or unwanted pregnancies (ORs 0.6-0.86) [37, 49]. Mistimed pregnancy was also associated with quitting smoking during pregnancy in one study (OR 0.86) [37]. However, in three studies, pregnancy wanting, planning, trying, timing, emotional response or readiness were not associated with quitting smoking during preconception or prenatal periods [41, 55, 70]. In one study on postpartum smoking, participants who wanted their pregnancies were most likely to quit smoking after birth compared to participants with mistimed or unwanted pregnancies (ORs 0.6-0.9) [49].

Strong evidence supports an association between participants who planned to be pregnant reporting higher rates of social support during pregnancy compared to participants with unplanned or ambivalent pregnancies (ORs 4.0–13.1) [46]. Both leisure activity and physical activity were more likely among those who intended to be pregnant, compared to participants not planning but not doing anything to prevent pregnancy (aPOR 1.17–1.57) [69] or participants with no change in pregnancy wanting, planning, and trying [70].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes a robust body of literature covering non-binary and multi-factorial constructs that underlie and comprise perceptions, attitudes, and reactions to pregnancies as well as associated outcomes. Diverging from traditional and highly limited dichotomous measures of pregnancy intention, many studies now measure additional dimensions including pregnancy ambivalence, antecedent factors, intentions and planning, barriers and facilitators, and actions taken, as well as reactions to the pregnancy once it occurs. These complex factors intersect, overlap, and interact, with researchers working to understand how these factors influence the decision about how to proceed with the pregnancy and potential health outcomes associated with their reactions to this decision.

The findings on health sequelae are complex, varied, and difficult to compare, given the wide variety of predictors and dimensions of pregnancy being measured. In this systematic review, the strongest correlates with maternal-child health outcomes were the pregnancy dimensions of ambivalence, timing, planning and wanting and antepartum and postpartum depression. Some studies in our review found odds ratios as high as 7.75 (odds of postpartum depression among young adults with a never-wanted pregnancy) [52] and 9.23 (odds of antenatal depression among people with mistimed pregnancies) [46]. Among research conducted on binary measures of pregnancy intention, consistent but weaker associations were found in one systematic review/meta-analysis that included binary measures and global studies, which also found a significant association between unintended pregnancy and antenatal (aOR 1.59) and postpartum depression (aOR 1.51) [75]. Another study with similar methodology found unintended pregnancy increased the odds of antenatal (RE OR 2.24) and postpartum depression (RE OR 2.49) [76]. This consistency emphasizes the significant impact of mental health on perceptions, attitudes, and reactions surrounding pregnancy, and stronger associations in our review may reflect the greater depth of measures used to assess a wider variety of dimensions. This strong association may reflect that people with depression are less able to prioritize sexual and reproductive health, rather than that having an unintended pregnancy leads to depression.

Our review also found that the strongest associations between pregnancy dimensions and maternal-child health are among outcomes during the preconception and prenatal periods, including contraceptive use, likelihood of pregnancy, prenatal counseling, taking prenatal vitamins, alcohol and smoking, early recognition of pregnancy, prenatal care, and depression. Some of these associations are not surprising; common sense suggests someone planning a pregnancy would use less contraception and be more likely to get pregnant. Notably, ambivalence was sometimes associated with higher likelihood of pregnancy, compared to people who wanted to avoid pregnancy. This finding may be explained by a previous systematic review/meta-analysis, which looked specifically at pregnancy ambivalence and contraceptive non-use, and found a significant and moderate association (OR 2.41) [77]. Another systematic review/meta-analysis which included binary measures and global studies found no association between unintended pregnancy and preconception healthy food intake or physical activity [78]. Likewise, our review found that when people experienced an unwanted pregnancy or were unsure about a pregnancy, they were more likely to delay prenatal care (likely while taking time to accept the unexpected pregnancy or while deciding whether to have an abortion). This finding was consistent with other review/meta-analyses which included global studies and binary measures [79, 80].

However, the relationships between pregnancy intentions and other outcomes such as pregnancy complications, postpartum health behaviors, breastfeeding, attachment, and child health are less clear. Many studies had conflicting or paradoxical findings, and many had methodological limitations. For example, many studies had temporal confounding when measuring associations with psychosocial outcomes. In particular, IPV may result from the stress of an unwanted pregnancy, but unwanted pregnancy may also be a result of reproductive coercion, a type of IPV [81, 82]. And, similar to our understanding of the association between depression and pregnancy intention, higher rates of substance use and smoking in people with pregnancies categorized as unintended may simply reflect that people with substance use disorder are less able to prioritize sexual and reproductive health, rather than that having an unintended pregnancy leads to substance use. Additionally, the significant stigma associated with substance use especially in the context of pregnancy may lead to overreporting of pregnancies as unplanned.

Our review found small to medium effect sizes for most associations with smoking, alcohol, and substance use, consistent with existing literature. One systematic review/meta-analysis that included binary measures and global studies also found significant associations between unplanned pregnancy and prenatal smoking (OR 1.68) and alcohol use (OR 1.44) [83]. Another found a moderate association between unintended pregnancy and IPV (aOR 2.22), and weaker associations with preterm birth (aOR 1.21) and low birthweight (aOR 1.09), when compared to intended pregnancy [75]. Two others found small but significant associations in pooled data between unintended pregnancy and low birthweight ((RE OR 1.41) [20] and (OR 1.36) [84]). A scoping review which included binary measures and global studies found mixed but overall negative impact of pregnancy intention on child development outcomes, but noted the difficulty of comparing studies due to heterogenous methods and measures [85]. The use of multi-dimensional measures in our reviewed studies not only offers more depth to our understanding of these complex relationships, but also highlights the small to moderate strength of these associations.

Implications for research and practice

This review demonstrates that non-binary and multi-dimensional measures of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions provide more nuance to understanding the impact of pregnancy pathways on health outcomes than historically-used binary measures. The opportunity to consider multiple dimensions and pathways to pregnancy is of critical importance to researchers in public health and the social sciences. Research questions should be clear in their hypothesized relationships and pathways, and specific in their dimensions of interest. There is a need for further work identifying the phenotype of dimensions and pathways to pregnancy that are definitively and strongly associated with poor health outcomes in order to inform interventions. Understudied dimensions for future investigation include perceived readiness to have a baby, pregnancy denial, and self-efficacy to prevent pregnancy.

The variety of multi-dimensional measures we included in our review also demonstrates the need for a new conceptual framework to guide research that reflects the complex interplay of factors that lead to pregnancy, birth, and other health outcomes. One such example is the novel measure of “self-assessed pregnancy acceptability” after a pregnancy has been confirmed, which is being developed by researchers in partnership with pregnant people, clinicians, reproductive justice advocates, and measurement specialists [86]. Their framework posits three paths through pregnancy (parenting, abortion, adoption) each with two dimensions: personal attitudes (is that path the right option in this case?) and feasibility (is that path do-able right now?). A pregnancy is highly acceptable if they have positive attitudes and high feasibility for at least one path, while a pregnancy is completely unacceptable if all three paths are closed, either by negative personal attitudes or by low feasibility. All other situations are mixed acceptability. This novel framework offers multiple cognitive, affective, and logistical dimensions of pregnancy responses. It is currently being tested in a prospective validation survey to measure associations with maternal and infant outcomes [87].

This review also has important implications for clinical practice. While recognizing the reality that data systems may require simplified input fields, findings highlight the need to move beyond asking patients overly simplistic questions such as “Was this pregnancy planned?” When asking patients about pregnancy, providers should consider the Pregnancy Attitudes, Timing, and How (PATH) framework, which acknowledges multiple pregnancy dimensions and social acceptability barriers to disclosing pregnancy planning. PATH asks “Do you think you might like to have (more) children at some point?,” “When do you think that might be?,” and “How important is it to you to prevent pregnancy (until then)?” [88], and digital tools offer the ability to smoothly integrate PATH questions into clinical practice [89]. By approaching patients with these questions, providers can avoid inadvertent assumptions or judging a patient’s reproductive desires and can provide high quality healthcare to all who need it. Finally, our review found strong evidence for higher likelihood of depression for any kind of pregnancy besides those that were fully planned and intended—including ambivalent pregnancies. This highlights the importance of screening all patients for depression during and after pregnancy and providing access to the full range of sexual and reproductive health services including contraception and abortion care, for all people.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first systematic review to specifically examine non-binary measures of pregnancy intention and other dimensions. This review was limited by restricting eligibility to research conducted in the US. Including global findings may have revealed additional pregnancy dimensions with salience in a US context. Methodological heterogeneity makes the findings hard to compare and difficult to draw actionable conclusions from and introduces a level of subjectivity to our synthesis and interpretation. The absence of a formal meta-analysis restricts our ability to quantify pooled effects. Results are also inconsistent, and lead to questions about how to design interventions to improve health outcomes. The question of temporality between pregnancy dimensions and outcomes must be further explored to inform meaningful interventions. For example, it is unlikely that an intervention to prevent unplanned pregnancy would decrease substance use and possible that an intervention to prevent substance use may decrease unplanned pregnancy. However, it would be far more useful to design an intervention to support reproductive autonomy and healthy pregnancies over time among people with substance use. The most salient message to take from these varied findings is that the pathway from becoming pregnant to the resulting outcomes is complex, multifactorial, and difficult to capture in a single clinical encounter or a research measure, and given the lack of strong associations with outcomes, capturing it is of questionable benefit.

Conclusions

This systematic review of pregnancy perceptions, attitudes, and reactions demonstrates the highly varied landscape of existing binary and non-binary measures. While it is difficult to compare studies given this great diversity in measures, our review generally documented small to moderate effect sizes of existing measures on perinatal public health outcomes. Our review highlights the need for novel, rigorously-developed frameworks and measures that more accurately capture diverse experiences of pregnancy and can better predict maternal and child health experiences and outcomes. To date, the focus on unintended pregnancy has stigmatized non-hegemonic pregnancy (e.g., among lower income families), has not accurately reflected the experiences of people for whom pregnancy “just happens” [12], and has excluded abortion and adoption as acceptable outcomes for pregnancy. Future studies could apply the Reproductive Justice and the Reproductive & Sexual Health Equity frameworks [18, 19], which equally emphasize the human rights to contraception (when pregnancy prevention is desired), to have healthy pregnancies and parenting resources (when pregnancy continuation is desired regardless of premeditation), and to terminate pregnancies (when abortion is desired).
