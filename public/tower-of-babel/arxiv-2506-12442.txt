1.
Introduction

1.1.
Embeddings of recursive groups and Higman’s basic operations

This paper continues our recent research

[
21
,
19
,
23
]

on explicit embeddings of recursive groups into finitely presented groups.
According to Higman’s famous Theorem 1 in
[
9
]
, a finitely generated group can be embedded into a finitely presented group if and only if it is recursive.
According to Corollary on
p.
456 in
[
9
]
this theorem’s requirement is sufficient for the effectively enumerable
countably
generated groups: they also are embeddable into finitely presented groups, as soon as they are recursive. It is not hard to see that in this case the condition is no longer necessary though, see Example
4.2
below.

Higman’s result states
possibility
of such an embedding without giving an
explicit
algorithm for its construction. Moreover, even for the additive group of rational numbers
{\mdmathbb Q}
, which has a fairly uncomplicated recursive presentation, the question of its
explicit
embedding into a finitely represented group remained open until very recently.
That problem was directly stated, in particular, in
[
12
,
7
,
10
]
, and it was solved in
[
3
,
23
]
,
see also our report
[
18
]
of 2018.

[
23
]
intended to cover the embedding of the recursive group
{\mdmathbb Q}
into some finitely presented groups, but some wide generalizations of its methods are possible. Hence, continuing the cited research we recently started generalization of arguments of
[
23
]
to a common method that works not only for
{\mdmathbb Q}
but also for
arbitrary recursive group
, namely, for any group
G=\langle X\,|\,R\rangle
for which:

(1)

the set of generators
X
is an effectively enumerable finite or countable set;

(2)

the set of relations
R
is a recursively enumerable set.

Intuitively,
G
can be understood as a group given via
G=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\,|\,r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\rangle
, for which we possess an algorithm that outputs the relations
r_{i}
of
G
in whatever order (our algorithm recursively enumerates them).
More precisely, by the classic definition of recursive enumeration, we assume that we are given a bijection from
R
to a subset of the non-negative integers, and that subset can be built from the
zero
,
successor
, and
projection
functions by means of
composition
,
primitive recursion
, and
minimization
operations, see
[
6
,
26
]
.

In order to construct the desired explicit embedding, we over the past few years, have been systematically analyzing the main stages of the Higman’s construction
[
9
]
, trying to make them explicit, in case they are not already so.

A key tool by means of which Higman concatenates the logical concept of recursion to group-theoretical concepts is the notion of benign subgroup.
A subgroup
H
of a finitely generated group
G
is called a
benign
subgroup in
G
if the group
G
can be embedded into a finitely presented group
K
which has a finitely generated subgroup
L
such that
G\cap L=H
holds, see more details and references in Section
2.4
below.

For a given recursive group
G
the construction of
[
9
]
first outputs a specific set
\mathcal{X}
of functions from
{\mdmathbb Z}
to
{\mdmathbb Z}
with finite supports.
Then Higman introduces a respective subgroup
A_{\mathcal{X}}
inside the free group
F
of rank
3
, see definition in Section
2.3
below. The group
A_{\mathcal{X}}
turns out to be benign in
F
if and only if
\mathcal{X}
is recursively enumerable, see Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 in
[
9
]
.

A large part of
[
9
]
is dedicated to showing that if
A_{\mathcal{X}}
is benign in the free group
F=\langle a,b,c\rangle
of rank
3
, and if the set
\mathcal{Y}
is obtained from
\mathcal{X}
by one of nine specific
Higman operations
, see (
H
) in Section
2.2
below, then
A_{\mathcal{Y}}
also is benign in
F
.
To build an
explicit
embedding of
G
into a finitely presented group we, in this and in following papers, for each of nine Higman operations (
H
) plan to
explicitly
show the finitely presented group
K_{\mathcal{Y}}
with its finitely generated subgroup
L_{\mathcal{Y}}
admitting the equality
F\cap L_{\mathcal{Y}}=A_{\mathcal{Y}}
, as soon as
\mathcal{Y}
is obtained from
\mathcal{X}
by one of Higman operations.
Also, we plan to explicitly write the overgroup
K_{\mathcal{Y}}
by its generators and defining relations, and write the subgroup
L_{\mathcal{Y}}
by its generators, as soon as
K_{\mathcal{X}}
,
L_{\mathcal{X}}
are explicitly given in that format for the set
\mathcal{X}
, while
F\cap L_{\mathcal{X}}=A_{\mathcal{X}}
holds.

We understand that the above concise outline is not enough to give understanding of the position of our Theorem
1.1
in the framework of Higman’s construction in
[
9
]
.
Thus, in Appendix in Chapter
5
we present a more detailed depiction of the steps of
[
9
]
, to spot the fragment of
[
9
]
which Theorem
1.1
concerns to. Also notice Remark
5.1
and Remark
5.2
in which we stress some of the steps in
[
9
]
which cause extra problems, and which we try avoid via modifications of the construction.

1.2.
Motivation for Higman’s reversing operation
\rho
and Theorem
1.1

Higman’s operation

In the context outlined in previous section, the operation

Firstly

Secondly
, discussion of the operation

.
But

Yes, a reader with sound knowledge in free constructions could be able to write down the group assumed by Higman, but the efforts needed for that job are far from being trivial, and

is the first of operations (

) that very well stresses stress that fact.

The above motivation brings us to the the main objective of this paper: prove that, if for the given set
\mathcal{X}
of functions from
{\mdmathbb Z}
to
{\mdmathbb Z}
with finite supports, the respective subgroup

and

, and the new groups

can also be written down for this updated group

Let

be any subset of
{\mathcal{E}}

.

2.
Preliminary notation and references

2.1.
Integer-valued functions
f

Following
[
9
]
denote by
\mathcal{E}
the set of
all
functions
f:{\mdmathbb Z}\to{\mdmathbb Z}
with finite supports;
as usual the support of
f
is the set
{\rm sup}(f)=\big\{i\in{\mdmathbb Z}\;|\;f(i)\neq 0\big\}
.
When
f
for a certain fixed
m=1,2,\ldots
has the property that

f(i)=0
for
all

i<0
and
i\geq m
, then it is comfortable to record it as a sequence
f=(j_{0},\ldots,j_{m-1})
, where
f(i)=j_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,m-1
. For example, the function
f
which sends the integers
0,1,2
to
2,5,3
,  and all other integers to
0
, is recorded as
f=(2,5,3)
.
Clearly,
m
may not be uniquely defined for
f
​, and where necessary we may add extra zeros at the end of a sequence, e.g., the previous function can be also recorded as
f=(2,5,3,0,0)
.
See more on such functions in Section 2.2 in
[
21
]
.
Denote
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}\!=\big\{(j_{0},\ldots,j_{m-1})\;\mathrel{|}\;j_{i}\!\in\!{\mdmathbb Z},\;i\!=\!0,\ldots,m-1\big\}
for
m=1,2,\ldots

Clearly
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
, and by the agreement above
{\mathcal{E}}_{m^{\prime}}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
,
in case
m^{\prime}\leq m
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{E}
and
j\in{\mdmathbb Z}
we define the function
f_{j}^{+}
​ as follows:

f_{j}^{+}(i)=f(i)
for all
i\!\neq\!j
, and

f_{j}^{+}(j)=f(j)+\!1
.
When
f\in{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
, we define
f^{+}=f_{m-1}^{+}
, say, for the above
f\!=\!(2,5,3)\in{\mathcal{E}}_{3}
we have
f_{1}^{+}\!\!=(2,6,3)
and

f^{+}\!\!=(2,5,4)
.

2.2.
The Higman operations

Higman defines the following specific operations that transform the subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
to some new subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
:

(H)

\iota,\;\upsilon,\;\rho,\;\sigma,\;\tau,\;\theta,\;\zeta,\;\pi,\;\omega_{m},

m=1,2,\ldots
,
see Section 2 in
[
9
]
or Section 2.3 in
[
21
]
for details.

In particular, one of the Higman operations (
H
) is the
reversing
operation
\rho
defined as follows:
for any subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
the set

\mathcal{Y}=\rho\mathcal{X}
consists of all functions
f\in\mathcal{E}
for which there is a
g\in\mathcal{X}
such that
f(i)=g(-i)
for all
i\in{\mdmathbb Z}
. Intuition behind this definition is uncomplicated:
\rho
just reverses the “coordinates” of
f
symmetrically about the origin
0
.
Say, if
f
is the function sending
the integers
-1,0,1,2
to
3,2,9,8
,  and all other integers to
0
,
then
\rho f
is sending
-2,-1,0,1
to
8,9,2,3
,  and all other integers to
0
.

2.3.
Defining subgroups by sets of functions

Let
F=\langle a,b,c\rangle
be a free group of rank
3
. For any
i\in{\mdmathbb Z}
denote
b_{i}=b^{c^{i}}=c^{-i}b\,c^{i}
. Then for any function
f\!\in{\mathcal{E}}
one can define in

the following products:

(2.1)

b_{f}=\cdots b_{-1}^{f(-1)}b_{0}^{f(0)}b_{1}^{f(1)}\cdots\quad\text{and}\quad a_{f}=a^{b_{f}}=b^{-1}_{f}a\,b_{f}.

Say, for the above function

f
sending
-1,0,1,2
to
3,2,9,8
we have

b_{f}\!=\!b_{-1}^{3}b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{9}b_{2}^{8}
.
When
f
is in
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
, we can more comfortably record it as

f=(j_{0},\ldots,j_{m-1})
, see Section
2.1
, and then write

b_{f}=b_{0}^{j_{0}}\cdots b_{m-1}^{j_{m-1}}
.
Say, for
f\!=\!(2,5,3)

we may put

b_{f}\!=\!b_{(2,5,3)}\!=b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{5}b_{2}^{3}
and

a_{(2,5,3)}\!=a^{b_{(2,5,3)}}
.

For a set
\mathcal{X}
of functions from
{\mathcal{E}}
denote by
A_{\mathcal{X}}
the subgroup generated in
F
by all the conjugates
a_{f}=a^{b_{f}}
with
f\!\in\mathcal{X}
.
Say,
A_{{\mathcal{E}}_{2}}
is the subset generated in
F
by all words of type

a^{b_{(j_{0},\;j_{1})}}=b_{1}^{\!-j_{1}}b_{0}^{\!-j_{0}}\cdot a\cdot b_{0}^{j_{0}}b_{1}^{j_{1}}
for all possible integers
j_{0},j_{1}\in{\mdmathbb Z}
.

Applying the operation
\rho
to the above function
f
, we obtain from the elements

b_{f}=b_{\!-1}^{3}b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{9}b_{2}^{8}
,

a_{f}=a^{b_{\!-1}^{3}b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{9}b_{2}^{8}}
the following elements:

b_{\rho f}=b_{\!-2}^{8}b_{\!-1}^{9}b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{3}
,

a_{\rho f}=a^{b_{\!-2}^{8}b_{\!-1}^{9}b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{3}}
.

For some technical purposes we may use the above notation with
other
free generators also. Say, in the free group
\langle d,e\rangle

we may set

d_{i}=d^{e^{i}}
​,

d_{f}=\cdots d_{-1}^{f(-1)}d_{0}^{f(0)}d_{1}^{f(1)}\cdots

Or, we may take an isomorphic copy

of

the elements

2.5.

For background information on free products with amalgamation and on HNN-extensions we refer to

[
5
]
and
[

]
.
Since notation varies in the literature,
we fix them for this paper as follows.
Suppose that

and

are isomorphic subgroups of

, respectively, with an isomorphism

and

with amalgamation of

and

G*_{\varphi}H
.
When

and
H
are overgroups of the same subgroup

, and

, then we denote the corresponding amalgamated product as

.

If
G

and

\varphi:A\to B
, then the corresponding HNN-extension of the base group

by some stable letter

G*_{\varphi}t
.
In case when
A=B
and
\varphi
is the isomorphism just fixing
A
, we denote the corresponding HNN-extension by
G*_{A}t
.
We also use HNN-extensions

and

with more than one stable letters, see
[
20
]
for details.

Below we are going to use a series of facts about certain specific subgroups in free constructions

,

,

and

. We have collected them in Section 3 of
[
22
]
to refer to that section whenever needed.

3.1.
The
✻
-construction

Let
G,M,K_{1},\ldots,K_{r}
be a family of groups meeting the following conditions:

(3.1)

G\leq M\leq K_{1},\ldots,K_{r}
,  and
K_{i}\cap\,K_{j}=M
for any distinct
i,j=1,\ldots,r
.

Taking an arbitrary subgroup
L_{i}
in
K_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
,
we in Section 4 of

[
22
]
built an auxiliary technical “
✻
-construction” applying a nested combination of HNN-extensions and amalgamations:

(3.2)

\textstyle\textrm{\footnotesize✻}_{i=1}^{r}(K_{i},L_{i},t_{i})_{M}\!=\Big(\!\cdots\Big(\big((K_{1}*_{L_{1}}t_{1})*_{M}\!(K_{2}*_{L_{2}}t_{2})\big)*_{M}\!(K_{3}*_{L_{3}}t_{3})\Big)\cdots\!\Big)*_{M}\!(K_{r}*_{L_{r}}t_{r}).

Recall that
K_{i}*_{L_{i}}t_{i}
is the HNN-extension of the group
K_{i}
with the base subgroup
L_{i}
and with the stable letter
t_{i}
fixing
L_{i}
elementwise,
i=1,\ldots,r
.
Then (
3.2
) is the free product
of these HNN-extensions
K_{i}*_{L_{i}}t_{i}
, all amalgamated in their
common
subgroup
M
.

Figure 2.
Construction of the group
\textrm{\footnotesize✻}_{i=1}^{r}(K_{i},L_{i},t_{i})_{M}
.

In some extreme cases the
✻
-construction (
3.2
), in fact, coincides with certain well known “classic” constructions.

Example 3.1
.

When a certain group
G
and its subgroups
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
are fixed, then taking
K_{i}=M=G
and
L_{i}=A_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, we
have the HNN-extensions

, and then

\textrm{\footnotesize✻}_{i=1}^{r}(G,A_{i},t_{i})_{G}
is the amalgamation of all such

by their subgroup

.
In this case the
✻
-construction is the usual HNN-extension with multiple stable letters

:

G*_{A_{1},\ldots,\,A_{r}}\!(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}).

Example 3.2
.

If we again take a random group

, and put
K_{i}=M=G
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, and choose

, then
\textrm{\footnotesize✻}_{i=1}^{r}(G,A_{i},t_{i})_{G}

\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\rangle\cong F_{r}
of rank

G*\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\rangle=G*F_{r}.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the case when

for each
i=1,\ldots,r
. Then in

just fixes the whole

Factorizing the above with the relations

G\times\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\rangle\cong G\times{\mdmathbb Z}^{r}.

The following lemmas

Example 3.9

Take the sequence, say,

with the product

​​,

\big(b_{1}^{5}\big)^{d_{1}}
​ and

Inside the group

the isomorphism

(and, hence, conjugation by the element

fixed, and sends the elements

to their conjugates

Hence, also taking into account the action of

\begin{split}b_{0}^{d_{1}}&=\big(c^{-0}bc^{0}\big)^{e^{-1}de}\!\!=\big(b^{e^{-1}}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(cbc^{-1}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b_{-1}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b^{b}_{-1}\big)^{e}\\

from where, clearly,
\big(b_{0}^{2}\big)^{d_{1}}\!=b_{1}^{-1}b^{2}_{0}b_{1}
.
Similarly:

\begin{split}b_{1}^{d_{1}}&=\big(c^{-1}bc^{1}\big)^{e^{-1}de}\!\!=\big(c^{-1}b^{e^{-1}}c\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(c^{-1}cbc^{-1}c\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b_{0}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b^{b}_{0}\big)^{e}\\

The situation is a little different for the

\begin{split}b_{2}^{d_{1}}&=\big(c^{-2}bc^{2}\big)^{e^{-1}de}\!\!=\big(c^{-2}b^{e^{-1}}c^{2}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(c^{-2}cbc^{-1}c^{2}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b_{1}\big)^{de}\!\!=\big(b_{1}\big)^{e}\\

from where, again,
\big(b_{2}^{3}\big)^{d_{1}}\!=b^{3}_{2}

Finally, we have

, and then into

\begin{split}a_{f}^{d_{1}}&=\big(b_{2}^{\!-3}b_{1}^{\!-5}b_{0}^{\!-2}\;a\;b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{5}b_{2}^{3}\,\big)^{d_{1}}\!\!\\
&=\big(b_{2}^{\!-3}\big)^{d_{1}}\big(b_{1}^{\!-5}\big)^{d_{1}}\big(b_{0}^{\!-2}\big)^{d_{1}}\;(a)^{d_{1}}\;\big(b_{0}^{2}\big)^{d_{1}}\big(b_{1}^{5}\big)^{d_{1}}\big(b_{2}^{3}\big)^{d_{1}}\!\!\\

&=b_{2}^{\!-3}\big(b_{1}^{\!-1}b_{1}^{\!-5}\big)b_{0}^{\!-2}\;a\;b_{0}^{2}\big(b_{1}^{5}b_{1}^{1}\big)b_{2}^{3}\;=\;b_{2}^{\!-3}b_{1}^{\!-\,6}b_{0}^{\!-2}\;a\;b_{0}^{2}b_{1}^{6}b_{2}^{3}\\
&=a_{f_{1}^{+}}\end{split}

for the sequence

Next, taking, say,

5.6.
The group
K
and the “Higman Rope Trick”

For the free group
F
it is easy to pick such an isomorphic copy
F^{\prime}
of it, such that
F^{\prime}
intersects with
F
precisely in
\langle R\rangle^{F}
​ inside a larger common overgroup. For example, one can first embed
F
into the HNN-extension
F*_{\langle R\rangle^{F}}r
, and then choose
F^{\prime}
to be the conjugate
F^{r}
of
F
by the stable letter
r
.

Lemma 3.5 in
[
9
]
provides an alternative definition for benign subgroups. Applying it to
\langle R\rangle^{F}
​ one gets that it is benign in
F
if and only if the free product
H=F*_{\langle R\rangle^{F}}F^{\prime}
of the group
F
and of its copy
F^{\prime}
, amalgamated in
\langle R\rangle^{F}
​, is embeddable into some finitely presented group
K
. From the simple proof of Lemma 3.5 it is clear that as
K
one could choose the HNN-extension
K=K_{\mathcal{X}}*_{L_{\mathcal{X}}}s
of the above
K_{\mathcal{X}}
with the fixed subgroup
L_{\mathcal{X}}

(where
K_{\mathcal{X}}
and
L_{\mathcal{X}}
are those outputted at the end the previous step
5.5
). This extension is generated by
finitely
many generators of
K_{\mathcal{X}}
together with the stable letter
s
. Also,
K
is
finitely
defined by the finite set of defining relations of
K_{\mathcal{X}}
together with the finitely many relations telling that
s
fixes the finitely many generators of
L_{\mathcal{X}}
.

The embedding of
H
into
K
can trivially be continued to the embedding of
H
into the direct product
K\times G
, for which we in addition to finitely many relations of
K
may have
infinitely
many relations for
G
. Plus, the
finitely
many relations telling that the finitely many generators of
K
and
G
commute, of course.

The free group
F
has two isomorphic images
F
and
F^{\prime}=F^{r}
in the
first
direct factor of
K\!\times\!G
, and
F
has its surjective image
F/\langle R\rangle^{F}=G
as the
second
direct factor.

For any word
f\!\in F
we inside

can define
the identity map
(f^{r},1)\to(f^{r},1)
, and the map

(f,1)\to(f,\;\langle R\rangle^{F}\!f)
, with the coset
\langle R\rangle^{F}\!f
from

. Whenever the word
f
is a
relation
of
G
, we have
\langle R\rangle^{F}\!f=\langle R\rangle^{F}\!=1
in

, that is, these two maps
agree
on the amalgamated subgroup
\langle R\rangle^{F}
​, and they can therefore be continued on the whole
H
, to form an injective homomorphism
\alpha
from

into

.

Hence one can define the last HNN-extension

which turns out to be the desired finitely presented overgroup of

. This is shown by an elegant process often called the “Higman Rope Trick” that proves that all but
finitely
many of the relations of this HNN-extension are redundant.

Namely, the action of

on

\alpha
on
finitely
many generators of

and of its copy

.
Thus, the only remaining spot with infinitely many defining relations is

also see the helpful discussion
[

]
