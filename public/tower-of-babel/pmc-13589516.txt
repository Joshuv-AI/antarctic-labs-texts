Sex-Specific Predictive Value of Obesity-Related Indicators for Diabetic Kidney Disease in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Jing Mao, Shijun Gong, Jing Jin, Yajuan Tan, Ling Zeng, Quan Zhou, Fang Yu, Haifeng Zhou, Huilin Lu, Shenglian Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeObesity is a significant risk factor for diabetic kidney disease (DKD). However, the relationship between obesity-related indicators and DKD is controversial. The aim of this study was to investigate the gender-specific differences in predictive performance of eight obesity-related indicators concerning DKD risk among type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 1586 T2DM patients aged 18–80 years. The exposure variables included body mass index (BMI), triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), visceral adiposity index (VAI), and TyG-related composite indicators, which are TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist-hip ratio (TyG-WHpR), and TyG-waist-height ratio (TyG-WHtR). Point-biserial correlation analysis, multivariate logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, subgroup analyses, and interaction tests were used to explore the correlation between these indicators and DKD.ResultsThe prevalence of DKD in the studied T2DM patients was 37.3%. All eight obesity-related indicators were independently and positively associated with DKD. TyG-BMI had the largest AUC (0.6195) for predicting DKD in males, with a cut-off value of 53.3250. TyG-WHtR had the highest AUC (0.6213) for DKD in females, with a cut-off value of 1.0234. TyG-WHtR was more strongly associated with DKD in T2DM patients with systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, according to subgroup analysis (P for interaction = 0.0265).ConclusionThe optimal predictors of DKD in T2DM patients were gender-specific: TyG-BMI in males and TyG-WHtR in females. TyG-WHtR should be closely monitored in female T2DM patients (especially those with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg) to enable early identification and intervention for DKD risk. These gender-specific indices may provide preliminary evidence for early DKD risk stratification.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the microvascular complications of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). It has become the most common chronic kidney disease and the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) globally, accounting for more than 50% of all ESRD cases worldwide.1 The clinical diagnosis of DKD traditionally relies on renal puncture biopsy—the gold standard for confirming the disease. However, this procedure is not recommended as a routine test for T2DM patients due to its invasiveness, high cost, and limited necessity in most diagnostic scenarios. Notably, DKD often progresses asymptomatically in its early stages, meaning many T2DM patients may already have underlying renal impairment at the time of their diabetes diagnosis. It is therefore crucial to explore reliable predictors that enable early identification of DKD.2

Lipid metabolism disorders and hemodynamic abnormalities caused by hyperglycemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance (IR) are key contributors to DKD pathogenesis.3 However, clinical observations show that even T2DM patients with well-controlled blood glucose and blood pressure still face a risk of developing DKD, indicating gaps in current risk assessment frameworks.4 Given the rising prevalence of DKD, traditional obesity indicators are increasingly insufficient for predicting DKD progression. Therefore, it is particularly important to explore more indicators that can recognize the risk of DKD at an early stage. Research has demonstrated that obesity is positively associated with DKD and is an independent risk factor for the occurrence and development of DKD,5 and obesity may contribute to DKD pathogenesis through IR, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, fibrinolysis, and thrombosis.6,7

While obesity-related indicators offer advantages like ease of measurement and low cost—attributes that help reduce healthcare burdens—their predictive value for DKD remains controversial.8 For instance, Wan et al9 reported that the Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI) outperforms other indices (eg, neck circumference, visceral adiposity index (VAI), and waist circumference (WC)) in predicting DKD in a Shanghai-based cohort. Conversely. Wu et al10 found no consistent association between CVAI and DKD incidence through multiple sensitivity analyses. Li et al11 emphasized that the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index demonstrated stronger predictive power for DKD risk compared to traditional indicators such as WC and body mass index (BMI). Additionally, gender differences in adiposity and metabolic traits are well-documented. The BMI, which reflects overall obesity levels, may be more relevant for male populations, while the waist-height ratio (WHtR), which indicates central fat distribution, is more applicable for female populations.12 TyG-related indicators can integrate IR and obesity—two interdependent pathways driving DKD pathogenesis. TyG is a well-validated surrogate marker of IR. When combined with anthropometric traits (WC, BMI, WHtR), these composite indices capture the synergistic effect of metabolic dysfunction and abnormal fat distribution.

However, most prior studies either overlooked gender-specific differences in adiposity indices or failed to validate TyG-related composite indicators—including TyG-WC, TyG-BMI, TyG-waist-hip ratio (TyG-WHpR), and TyG-WHtR—leaving a critical gap in understanding optimal DKD predictors for men and women.13 BMI is a classic anthropometric measure of general adiposity. TyG serves as a reliable surrogate marker for IR, which plays a central role in renal injury and DKD progression. CVAI and VAI are established indicators of visceral adiposity, which is closely linked to chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction—key mechanisms driving the development and progression of DKD. TyG-related indicators integrate both adiposity and insulin resistance, thereby reflecting metabolic abnormalities more comprehensively than single indices. Each indicator reflects distinct aspects of abnormal fat distribution and metabolic disorders, making them rational and meaningful for evaluating DKD risk in patients with T2DM.

This study aimed to compare the predictive efficacy of eight obesity-related indicators for DKD risk in T2DM patients and to determine gender-specific optimal predictors and cut-off values, thereby providing evidence for early DKD detection and intervention.

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Participants Selection

The Metabolic Management Center (MMC) is a nationally standardized platform in China for the standardized diagnosis, treatment, and long-term follow-up of people with metabolic diseases.14 To ensure data reliability, the MMC database implements rigorous quality control (QC) procedures: All key variables (eg, anthropometrics, biochemical parameters, medication records) are required to be recorded at enrollment; records with missing critical data (≥10% of core variables) are automatically flagged and excluded from the analysis dataset. For the current study, the completeness rate of included variables was >98%. This study included 1665 diabetic patients, with all data obtained from the MMC database covering the period from 2020 to 2022. Exclusion criteria included non-T2DM (n = 23); age less than 18 years (n = 3); malignant tumors or alternative forms of kidney disease (eg, patients receiving dialysis, patients undergoing renal transplantation, nephrotic syndrome, and glomerulonephritis) (n = 10); clinically validated via nephrologist-confirmed diagnoses (electronic medical records); laboratory and imaging findings consistent with non-diabetic pathology; and missing fasting plasma glucose (FPG), WC, triglycerides (TG), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) data (n = 43).15,16 Finally, we incorporated 1586 patients with T2DM into the study.

Measurements of Variables

Trained researchers collected all variables using a standardized questionnaire provided by MMC. Basic demographic data (sex, age, work status), health behaviors (alcohol consumption, smoking status), diabetes duration, menopausal status, and medication records were collected.

All participants fasted overnight for 8 hours, and professional nurses collected venous blood samples to measure biochemical parameters. These parameters—including FPG, postprandial plasma glucose (PPG), total cholesterol (TC), TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), UACR, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)—were analyzed using an automated biochemical analyzer in the hospital’s central laboratory. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula.17 Subjects were timed from the first bite of the steamed bun meal, and PPG measured by venous blood samples collected 2 hours later was used to assess post-load glucose. All procedures and laboratory standards follow the 2020 version of the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of T2DM in China.18

Anthropometric measurements were performed with participants barefoot and wearing lightweight clothing. Height and weight were measured using a calibrated stadiometer (Model: Omron HNH-318, Japan). WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest using a non-elastic measuring tape, while hip circumference was measured at the most prominent point of the gluteal region.9,19 Blood pressure was measured twice using a digital sphygmomanometer (Model: Omron HBP-11200U, Japan) after participants rested for at least 5 minutes in a quiet room with a backrest, in line with the Chinese Hypertension Guidelines 2018.20

Definition

T2DM was diagnosed in accordance with the 2020 recommendations of the American Diabetes Association (ADA).24 The diagnosis required the presence of typical diabetic symptoms combined with any of the following: (1) FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) 2-hour PPG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L in an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); (3) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; or (4) random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L. For participants without typical symptoms, a confirmatory test on a different day was required.

DKD was defined based on the 2020 ADA diagnostic criteria,7 with two qualifying conditions: (1) eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; or (2) UACR ≥ 30 mg/g. Participants meeting either condition were classified into the DKD group.17

Statistical Analysis

The study population was categorized into two groups based on DKD and non-DKD. Continuous variables were displayed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (25th percentile, 75th percentile). For variables that followed a normal distribution, group disparities were assessed through analysis of variance. In contrast, the Kruskal–Wallis H-test was applied to analyze variables with a skewed distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and the chi-square test was utilized to examine differences across groups. EmpowerStats (version 5.2), SPSS (version 26.0), and R software (version 4.2.0) were used for all statistical analyses. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The linear associations between DKD and the eight obesity-related markers were assessed using point-biserial correlation analysis. Because the VAI showed significant right skewness (Shapiro–Wilk test, P < 0.001), we applied a natural logarithmic transformation (LogVAI) to normalize its distribution. This approach aligns with prior studies on adiposity indices and diabetic complications, ensuring valid statistical inference in multivariate logistic regression and subgroup analyses.25 Multiple logistic regression assessed independent associations between 8 obesity-related indicators and increased DKD. Four models were constructed in multivariate logistic regression: model 1 was unadjusted (no confounders included); model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), TC, LDL-C, smoking status, and alcohol consumption;3 model 3 added FPG, PPG, HbA1c, and duration of diabetes mellitus;6 and based on model 3, model 4 is fully adjusted for the use of medications (antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering drugs).26 To explore gender-specific predictive performance of obesity-related indicators, participants were stratified by gender first. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive efficacy of eight obesity-related indicators for DKD, with area under the curve (AUC) as the primary metric to determine optimal cut-off values via Youden’s index. We computed a 95% confidence interval (CI) for all AUC values to quantify result precision. Statistical comparisons of AUCs between indicators were performed using the DeLong test to identify significant differences in predictive performance. To further validate the stability of the predictive indicators’ association with DKD risk, we conducted subgroup analyses and interaction tests. The Bonferroni correction method was employed to address the potential risk of multiple-testing inflation in subgroup analyses.

Results

Participants’ Characteristics

This study contained data from 1586 T2DM patients, and the prevalence of DKD was 37.3% (n=592). DKD occurred in 232 females (39.2%) and 360 males (60.8%). The mean age of the participants was 52.3 ± 10.9 years. Comparisons between the DKD and non-DKD groups (Table 1) showed that the DKD group had significantly higher WC, BMI, TyG, SBP, DBP, TC, TG, FPG, PPG, HbA1c, UACR, TyG-WC, TyG-BMI, TyG-WHpR, TyG-WHtR, CVAI, and VAI (all P < 0.05). In contrast, the DKD group had a significantly lower eGFR (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of LDL-C, HDL-C, diabetes duration, alcohol consumption, glucose-lowering drug use, lipid-lowering drug use, or menopausal status (all P > 0.05). Discussion

This research showed that the prevalence of DKD in T2DM patients was 37.3%, with 60.8% of DKD patients being male and 39.2% being female. Our comparison of the eight obesity-related indicators revealed that the best predictor of DKD risk was TyG-BMI in men and TyG-WHtR in women. Importantly, these findings do not imply predictive superiority given the modest AUC values, but rather highlight the potential incremental value of combining metabolic and anthropometric parameters.

While existing research has confirmed that obesity-related indicators are associated with increased DKD risk, the optimal indicator for DKD risk screening remains controversial. Wang et al showed that in 5226 Chinese T2DM diabetes patients, body measurements like BMI, WHpR, WHtR, WC, and hip circumference were nonlinearly and positively correlated with DKD.27 Wan et al’s study, which involved 4658 diabetic patients in Shanghai, China, ages 33 to 90, revealed that CVAI had a stronger correlation with DKD than neck circumference (NC), WHpR, lipid deposition products (LAP), and VAI.9 Wu et al’s cohort study similarly showed that DKD development was independently correlated with VAI and CVAI.10 TyG has been reported to correlate more strongly with DKD risk than the traditional metrics of WC and BMI and is considered a better predictor of DKD.11 Liu et al evaluated 682 T2DM patients with an average age of 58 years in Wuhan, Hubei province. They discovered that the TyG index is a more reliable indicator of DKD in T2DM patients than HOMA-IR.28 Ou et al examined the association between DKD and obesity indicators in 1872 T2DM patients in Taiwan and found that related obesity indicators, such as BMI, WHpR, WHtR, LAP, VAI, CVAI, and TyG, were significantly correlated with DKD.15 However, these studies either failed to explore gender-specific differences or overlooked TyG-related obesity indicators. Our study fills this gap by systematically comparing eight indicators and identifying gender-specific optimal predictors.

Previous reports indicate that men are at a higher risk of developing DKD than women.29 Men aged 20–59 years have been identified as an important risk factor for the progression of DKD.30 Men have a higher incidence, prevalence, and mortality of DKD than women beginning at age 50. This may be because men are more likely than women to engage in harmful behaviors, including smoking and alcohol abuse. Furthermore, sex hormones play a crucial role in the development of diabetes and DKD.31 Women utilize more NO than men, reducing oxidative stress in the kidneys, which may slow the progression of diabetes-related chronic kidney disease (CKD).1,32

Research has demonstrated a high correlation between diabetes and TyG obesity-related indicators.33–36 This study found that TyG-BMI is the optimal predictor for DKD in men, while TyG-WHtR is the optimal predictor for women. Biologically, men have a higher average muscle content than women, tend to accumulate more visceral fat,37 and BMI (which reflects overall body mass) combined with TyG (a surrogate marker of IR) can better capture the synergistic effect of “overall obesity + IR” on renal damage. This explains why TyG-BMI is the optimal predictor in men. Adipose tissue distribution plays a significant role in T2DM comorbidity.38 Women (especially postmenopausal women) are more prone to central adiposity,39,40 and WHtR (which reflects central fat distribution) combined with TyG can more accurately assess the risk of “central obesity + IR”-induced DKD, making TyG-WHtR the optimal predictor in women.

We further analyzed subgroups of women who were or were not menopausal and found that TyG-WHtR was positively associated with DKD in both non-menopausal and menopausal women. For each SD increase in TyG-WHtR, there was a 1.5-fold increase in the risk of DKD in non-menopausal women and a 1.6-fold increase in the risk of DKD in women who were menopausal. Because postmenopausal women produce less estrogen and have higher levels of testosterone in their blood, they may have increased oxidative stress, central adiposity, and IR.41

TyG, a parameter that combines two metrics, TG and FPG, and which was used to assess IR, is significantly linked to DKD in patients with T2DM.42 TyG-BMI and TyG-WHtR are both indicators for assessing visceral obesity. Visceral obesity is tightly related to IR, and the majority of T2DM patients also have concomitant IR.43,44 Obesity and visceral adiposity promote insulin resistance, which triggers renal oxidative stress, endothelial injury, and chronic low-grade inflammation.45 These processes drive glomerular hyperfiltration, podocyte damage, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, ultimately accelerating the progression of DKD in patients with type 2 diabetes.46 The obesity-related indices examined in this study reflect adiposity distribution and metabolic dysfunction, which are closely linked to these renal pathogenic pathways. Furthermore, studies indicate that excess adiposity exerts direct nephropathological effects through multiple mechanisms. Altered renal hemodynamic parameters and dysregulated secretion of growth factors and adipocytokines collectively contribute to kidney dysfunction.47 These bioactive adipocytokines mediate vascular damage through three principal pathways: 1) compromising endothelial integrity, 2) triggering oxidative stress cascades and inflammatory responses, and 3) potentiating renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation.48 Concurrently, visceral adipocyte lipolysis elevates circulating free fatty acid concentrations, thereby upregulating proinflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6. Such inflammatory mediators induce glomerular endothelial impairment that manifests clinically as albuminuria exacerbation.49 The positive association between DKD and TyG obesity-related indicators can be partially explained by these mechanisms. Furthermore, our subgroup analysis revealed that the SBP ≥140 mmHg group had a stronger positive connection between TyG-WHtR and DKD. It may be due to the synergistic effect of hypertension and central obesity. Hypertension increases renal perfusion pressure and damages renal blood vessels, while central obesity exacerbates IR and inflammation. Together, they accelerate renal injury. This suggests that strict blood pressure control and TyG-WHtR monitoring are particularly important for female T2DM patients.

However, our study also included these limitations: First, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we could not rule out recall bias, nor could we establish a causal relationship between TyG-related obesity indicators and DKD. Second, as this study only included Chinese individuals with T2DM, care must be taken when extrapolating the findings to other racial groups.

Conclusion

This study found that eight obesity-related indicators were positively associated with the risk of DKD in Chinese patients with T2DM. TyG-BMI may be the optimal indicator for predicting DKD in men; TyG-WHtR may be the optimal indicator for predicting DKD in women, particularly those with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg.
