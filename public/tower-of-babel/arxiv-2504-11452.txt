The universal profile of the invariant factors of ( ℤ / m ​ ℤ ) × (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
Greg Martin, Reginald M. Simpson

Abstract




1.
Introduction

The
multiplicative group
of the positive integer
m
is the group of units
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, which encodes the structure of multiplication among the invertible residue classes modulo
m
. Many classical arithmetic functions can be defined in terms of this group, such as Euler’s totient function
\varphi(m)
(the order of the multiplicative group of
m
), the Carmichael lambda-function
\lambda(m)
(the maximum order of the elements of the multiplicative group of
m
), and the Dirichlet characters
\chi(m)
(arithmetic lifts of the irreducible representations of the multiplicative group of
m
).

As a finite abelian group, the multiplicative group of
m
can be uniquely decomposed into a direct product
of cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{\lambda_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{\lambda_{K}}
for some integer
K
, with orders
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{K}
such that
\lambda_{1}\mid\lambda_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\lambda_{K}
. This representation, which is called the
invariant factor decomposition
of the multiplicative group, is also connected to well-known arithmetic functions. In particular, the order
\lambda_{K}
of the largest cyclic group in the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is precisely
\lambda(m)
. Furthermore, it is not hard to show (see Lemma
2.7
below) that the length
K
of the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is essentially equal to
\omega(m)
, the number of distinct prime factors of
m
. The limiting distribution of various statistics of this family of multiplicative groups is therefore closely connected to classical number theory results such as the Hardy–Ramanujan theorem and the Erdős–Kac theorem.

In this article, we will demonstrate that the invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
are strongly restricted in their possible orders, at least for almost all numbers
m
(that is, on a subset of
\mathbb{N}
of natural density
1
). These special orders are defined by a specific sequence of sets
(\mathfrak{f}_{i})
, each one having either one or two elements, which we call the
sequence of universal factor order sets
. The first ten such sets are

(\mathfrak{f}_{i})=\bigl(\{2\},\{4,6\},\{12\},\{24,60\},\{120\},\{360,840\},\{2520\},\\
\{5040\},\{55440\},\{720720\},\ldots\bigr)

(1.1)

When we define this sequence precisely (Definition
1.4
below), we will see that the elements of each universal factor order set always divide the elements of its successor. In particular, we note that the first even number not found in any
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
is
8
.

Chang and the first author
[
3
]
showed that for almost all
m
, the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
has
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as an invariant factor (indeed, they gave an asymptotic formula for the counting function of the exceptions). Our first theorem is a vast generalization of this result, precisely classifying the orders of cyclic groups appearing in almost all multiplicative groups.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{1}(m),f_{2}(m),\ldots
be the distinct invariant factor orders of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
listed in increasing order. For any positive integer
D
, almost all numbers
m
satisfy
f_{i}(m)\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,D
. In other words, the set of numbers
m
for which
f_{i}(m)\notin\mathfrak{f}_{i}
has density
0
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, as does the set of numbers
m
for which the length of the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
has fewer than
D
distinct orders.

For a concrete implication of this theorem, we see that the set of positive integers whose multiplicative group has at least one invariant factor
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
has density
0
, while the set of positive integers whose multiplicative group has at least one invariant factor
\mathbb{Z}_{720720}
(indeed, for which
\mathbb{Z}_{720720}
is the tenth-smallest distinct invariant factor order!) has density
1
. We also see that almost all integers have one of
\mathbb{Z}_{360}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{840}
as an invariant factor (and no integer has both, since neither of
360
and
840
divides the other); we will see later in Theorem
1.24
that each of these two possibilities occurs on a set of density
\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem
1.1
already reveals a startling uniformity in the invariant factor decompositions within the family of multiplicative groups; but much more can be said. For instance, beyond asking simply which invariant factors arise, we can also ask about the multiplicities of these invariant factors:

Definition 1.2
.

Define
\mathfrak{I}(m)
to be the total number of invariant factors (counted with multiplicity) of the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. For any positive integer
d
, define
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
to be the number of times
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
appears as an invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

We can compute statistics of the distribution of these functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
. For example, we will show in Theorem
1.6
below that the average order (or expected value) of the number of invariant factors
\mathbb{Z}_{720720}
is

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{m\leq x}\mathfrak{I}(m;720720)\sim\frac{1}{48}\log\log x.

(1.2)

By comparison, the average order of the total number of invariant factors is the average order of
\omega(m)
(see Lemma
2.7
below), which is
\log\log x
.
Thus on average, a proportion
\frac{1}{48}
of the invariant factor decomposition of a multiplicative group consists of copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{720720}
. Section
1.2
contains many such expected-value results for the quantities
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
.

Remarkably, we can go even farther than examining the average orders of these counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
: we can establish their precise limiting distributions. In Section
1.3
, we show that normalized versions of the
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
have limiting distributions that follow Erdős–Kac-type laws—but with the very unusual property that while some universal factor orders
d
yield normal distributions, others yield skew-normal or even more complicated distributions.

1.1.
Definitions of important sequences

We need several definitions and pieces of notation to state these stronger theorems precisely. Before we can define the full sequence
\{\mathfrak{f}_{i}\}
of universal factor order sets, we must first define a particular ordering of the set of prime powers. (We remark that all of the definitions and notation in Section 1 are gathered in Appendix
A
for the reader’s ease of reference.)

Definition 1.3
.

We define the
total
\varphi
-sequence
to be the infinite sequence

(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=(2,3,4,5,8,7,9,16,11,13,17,32,19,27,25,23,29,\ldots)

which consists of every prime power, ordered by the values the Euler
\varphi
-function takes on them, with ties broken by their size.
More precisely, the total
\varphi
-sequence is ordered so that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(a)

If
i<j
then
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\leq\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
;

(b)

If
i<j
and
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
, then
\mathfrak{q}_{i}<\mathfrak{q}_{j}
.

We define a
(finite)
\varphi
-sequence
to be a finite contiguous section of the total
\varphi
-sequence, that is, a finite sequence of the form
(\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{j-1},\mathfrak{q}_{j})
for some positive integers
i\leq j
.

(We remark that all of the notation in Section 1 is gathered in Appendix
A
for the reader’s ease of reference.)

We can now present the definition of the sequence
\{\mathfrak{f}_{i}\}
whose first elements we saw in equation (
1.1
).
By definition, each set
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
is either a singleton or a doubleton.

Definition 1.4
.

We define the
universal factor order sets

(\mathfrak{f}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
to be

\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\begin{cases}\{\lcm[\mathfrak{q}_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i}]\},&\text{if }\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}),\\
\{\lcm[\mathfrak{q}_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i}],\lcm[\mathfrak{q}_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}]\},&\text{if }\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}).\end{cases}

We set
\mathfrak{F}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{f}_{i}
.
A positive integer
d
is said to be a
universal factor order
if
d\in\mathfrak{F}

and is otherwise a
rare factor order
.

It turns out that doubletons are never adjacent in the sequence
(\mathfrak{f}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
(see Lemma
2.10
below); we will take this fact for granted in the introduction for the sake of exposition.

It will also be helpful to define two often-used sequences of constants derived from the total
\varphi
-sequence:

Definition 1.5
.

The mu-sequence

(\mu_{i})=\bigg(\frac{1}{2},0,\frac{1}{4},0,\frac{1}{12},0,\frac{1}{24},\frac{1}{40},\frac{1}{60},\frac{1}{48},0,\frac{1}{144},0,\frac{1}{180},\frac{1}{220},\frac{3}{308},\ldots\bigg)

and the sigma-sequence

(\sigma_{i})=\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}},\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}},\sqrt{\frac{5}{18}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{5}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}},\sqrt{\frac{13}{96}},\sqrt{\frac{15}{128}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{9}},\sqrt{\frac{17}{162}},\ldots\bigg)

are defined, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, by

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}+\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})}-\frac{2}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})}},

where
\varphi
is Euler’s totient function.
We note that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}
is a telescoping series whose value is
1/\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{1})=1/\varphi(2)=1
.

1.2.
Expectations of invariant factor counting functions

In this section we are interested in the average orders (or expectations) of the counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
defined in Definition
1.2
.
We use of the general notation

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}f(m)

to denote the expectation of the function
f(m)
when
m
is chosen uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
We can describe the average orders of
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
for every universal factor order
d
in one succinct statement (which includes equation (
1.2
) as a special case):

Theorem 1.6
.

If
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\mu_{i}\log\log n+O_{d}\bigl((\log\log n)^{1/2}\bigr).

(We remark that all of the theorems in Section 1 are gathered in Appendix
B
for the reader’s ease of reference.)

In contrast, we can show that rare factor orders
d
indeed rarely occur as invariant factors of multiplicative groups. Theorem
1.1
implies, for example, that almost no multiplicative groups include
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
as an invariant factor; we can improve this statement by showing that
\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;8))=o(1)
, so that this family of groups contain vanishingly few invariant factors
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
on average as well. Even more strongly, we can show the same for all moments of
\mathfrak{I}(m;8)
, and even give a quantitative rate of decay of these expectations. The following theorem exhibits such a bound, indeed for all rare factor orders.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
d\notin\mathfrak{F}
, then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d)^{k})\ll_{d,r,k}(\log\log n)^{-r}.

We can compare these expectations to that of the total number
\mathfrak{I}(m)
of invariant factors, which is asymptotically
\log\log m
(see Lemma
2.7
below). The result is that on average, a proportion
\mu_{i}
of the invariant factors of multiplicative groups are equal to
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
whenever
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
. Moreover, since
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}=1
, these represent almost all of the invariant factors present. Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
therefore describe a “limiting histogram” of invariant factor orders among the family of multiplicative groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Figure
1
displays this limiting histogram; we will see in the next section that it describes the limiting distribution of invariant factors in a far stronger sense than merely “on average”.

Figure 1.
The universal profile of invariant factors for the multiplicative groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

We can see that some of the universal factor orders are missing from this histogram; indeed, it follows easily from Definitions
1.4
and
1.5
that
\mu_{i}
equals
0
precisely when
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
is a doubleton set. Thus Theorem
1.6
is true but somewhat misleading in these cases: since
\mu_{i}=0
, the “error term” is actually the main term (see Theorem
1.12
below). In other cases, the error term can be improved significantly and second-order main terms appear.

We now describe these refinements to Theorem
1.6
. The specifics turn out to depend on whether the adjacent sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1},\mathfrak{f}_{i},\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
are singletons or doubletons, so there are several cases (enumerated in Proposition
2.13
below) to consider. We will preface each case with a specific numerical example to help orient the reader. In our first example, the error term in the average order is improved dramatically:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
5040
is on average

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;5040))=\frac{1}{40}\log\log n+O(1).

(As a special case of the observations made before Figure
1
, we see that on average, the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{5040}
comprises
2.5\%
of the total number of invariant factors.) The general statement, which involves the notation
\mu_{i}
from Definition
1.5
, concerns the case where all the sets
f_{i}
involved are singletons.

Theorem 1.8
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\mu_{i}\log\log n+O_{d}(1).

In our next example, a secondary main term arises:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
2520
is on average

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;2520))=\frac{1}{24}\log\log n-\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5}{36\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

(1.3)

The general statement, of which this example is a special case, concerns the case where the set preceding
f_{i}
is a doubleton but the two other relevant sets are singletons:
The main term still involves
\mu_{i}
(as is consistent with Theorem
1.6
), but the secondary term involves the notation
\sigma_{i}
from Definition
1.5
.

Theorem 1.9
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\mu_{i}\log\log n-\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{i-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

We encourage the reader to begin forming the intuition that the presence of the doubleton
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}
to the left of the singleton
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
“steals” a few of the invariant factors from the sequence of copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. This intuition will be connected to Theorem
1.12
below and will also be significant in the next section, where we discuss limiting distributions of these counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
.

A similar outcome occurs when the relevant doubleton is on the other side:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
720720
and
2
are on average

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;720720))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{48}\log\log n-\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{15}{256\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;2))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\log n-\bigg(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

We remark that the first of these two formulas is a refinement of the example (
1.2
) we have already seen, while the second formula implies that
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is the most common invariant factor (indeed comprising nearly half of the invariant factors) on average.

These two examples are special cases of the following general theorem, concerning the case where the set following
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
is a doubleton but the two other relevant sets are singletons:

Theorem 1.10
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\mu_{i}\log\log n-\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{i+1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

The same statement holds when
i=1
(so that
d=2
).

Again it is beneficial to think that the presence of the doubleton
\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
to the right of the singleton
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
results in the loss of some of the copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

This phenomenon is exacerbated if there are doubletons on both sides:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
120
and
12
are on average

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;120))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\log\log n-\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{3}{16\pi}}+\sqrt{\frac{5}{36\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;12))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\log\log n-\bigg(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}+\sqrt{\frac{3}{16\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

(1.5)

These two examples are special cases of the following result, where (informally) doubletons on both sides of
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
each steal some of the copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
that would otherwise appear:

Theorem 1.11
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\mu_{i}\log\log n-\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

Taken together, Theorems
1.8
–
1.11
cover all of the “singleton cases”, namely the universal factor orders
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
where
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
.

The last refinement of Theorem
1.6
concerns a situation where the expected value is truly of size
(\log\log n)^{1/2}
rather than
\log\log n
:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
360
and
840
are on average

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;360))

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5}{36\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;840))

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5}{36\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

We can check that this example is consistent with Theorem
1.6
: we have
\mathfrak{f}_{6}=\{360,840\}
and
\mu_{6}=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{6})^{-1}-\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{7})^{-1}=\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{6}=0
. Therefore Theorem
1.6
, while correctly asserting that
\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;360))=O\bigl((\log\log n)^{1/2}\bigr)
, hides a more precise average order in this case (and similarly for
d=840
). The fact that
\mu_{6}=0
also explains why neither
d=360
nor
d=840
appears in the limiting histogram in Figure
1
, which depicts proportions of invariant factors after division by
\log\log n
.

It is also worth remarking that the doubleton
\mathfrak{f}_{6}=\{360,840\}
really can be seen here to be stealing some of the invariant factors from its neighbours
\mathfrak{f}_{7}=\{2520\}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{5}=\{120\}
: note that the two expressions in equation (
1.6
) are exactly the same size as negative secondary main terms in equations (
1.3
) and (
1.4
).

There is a corresponding general statement which covers all “doubleton cases”, namely the universal factor orders
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
where
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=2
:

Theorem 1.12
.

If
i
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathfrak{I}(m;d))=\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(1)\bigg)(\log\log n)^{1/2}.

As mentioned earlier, it follows easily from Definitions
1.4
and
1.5
that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=2
if and only if
\mu_{i}=0
; thus Theorem
1.12
is also consistent with, but more precise than, Theorem
1.6
in this case.

) of universal factor order sets as a flowchart where the singletons and doubletons play different roles, as in Figure

While all of the theorems in this section have concerned the
average
behaviour of the invariant factor order counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
, we have begun describing these phenomena as the
typical
behaviour. This shift in language is intentional: in the next section, we will see that the functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
have limiting distribution laws for every universal factor order
d
. Strengthening this section’s results in that way is quite analogous to strengthening the average order of the function
\omega(m)
to the Erdős–Kac theorem which gives its normalized limiting distribution. We will indeed prove theorems of Erdős–Kac type for each of these counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
, with one unexpected development: the limiting distributions that arise are not always normal distributions—they are sometimes skew-normal distributions or even more complicated ones.

1.3.
Limiting distributions of invariant factor counting functions

In this section we exhibit all of the limiting distributions of the counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
where
d\in\mathfrak{F}
is a universal factor order (as well as the trivial limiting distributions where
d\notin\mathfrak{F}
is a rare factor order). As with the theorems above concerning average orders, the theorems in this section depend on whether the adjacent sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1},\mathfrak{f}_{i},\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
are singletons or doubletons. Fortunately, the shape of the limiting distribution depends only upon the sizes of these adjacent sets, except in the special cases
i=1
(so that
d=2
) and
i=3
(so that
d=12
). We will again preface each case with a specific numerical example for the reader’s benefit.

For these theorems, some consistent notation for common probability distributions will be necessary.

Definition 1.13
.

We use the usual error function
\erf(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt
and define its relative
\eta(x)=\erf(ix)=i\erfi(x)
. We then define the standard normal probability density function

\phi(x)=\frac{e^{-{x^{2}}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

and the standard normal cumulative distribution function

\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\erf\biggl(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\biggr)+1\biggr).

The standard normal distribution has characteristic function
e^{-t^{2}/2}
.

Remark 1.14
.

(a)

To clarify a possible notational confusion: in this paper
\varphi
always denotes the Euler phi-function and
\varphi
-sequences,
while
\phi
and
\Phi
always denote the density function and the cumulative distribution function, respectively, of the standard normal distribution (or, when given a second argument, of the skew-normal distribution in Definition
1.17
below).

(b)

Typically the characteristic function of a generic random variable
X
is denoted by
\varphi_{X}
; however, to avoid further overloading this Greek letter, in this paper we denote such characteristic functions by
\chi_{X}
.

(c)

All the probability-related functions in this section are collected in one place in Appendix
A
for ease of reference.

We will use notation for probabilities themselves when sampling uniformly from an initial segment of the positive integers.
If
S(m)
is any assertion about its argument
m
, and
n
is any positive integer, we let
P_{n}(S(m))=\frac{1}{n}\#\{m\in\mathbb{N},\,m\leq n\colon S(m)
is true
\}
denote the probability that an integer
m
chosen uniformly from the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
satisfies
S(m)
.

With this notation in hand, we can now give a much stronger version of the example before Theorem
1.8
:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
5040
follows an Erdős–Kac law with mean
\frac{1}{40}\log\log n
and variance
\frac{1}{5}\log\log n
. More precisely, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;5040)-\frac{1}{40}\log\log n}{(\frac{1}{5}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi(x).

The general statement, which strengthens Theorem
1.8
, involves the notation
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
from Definition
1.5
and concerns the case where all the sets
f_{i}
involved are singletons.

Theorem 1.15
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;d)-\mu_{i}\log\log n}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi(x),

where
\Phi(x)
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.15
, where all relevant sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
are singletons, it turns out that the invariant factor order counting function
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
can be closely approximated by a single additive function of
m
(see equation (
2.4
) below). Thus the Erdős–Kac statement can be proved in a standard way in this case.

However, when some relevant sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
are doubletons, it turns out that
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
has a more complicated behaviour that involves multiple additive functions—it might, for example, be approximately the minimum of two additive functions of
m
. In such cases, while the limiting distributions of the individual additive functions follow Erdős–Kac laws, the joint distribution of the additive functions on which
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
depends will be either a nonsingular two-variable normal distribution or a singular four-variable normal distribution. As a consequence, the resulting distribution for
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
will be more complicated than a normal distributon.

To describe one such result, we need the following definitions.

Definition 1.16
.

Define Owen’s
T
-function

\mathbf{T}(h,a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}e^{-(1+t^{2})h^{2}/2}\frac{dt}{1+t^{2}}.

If
h
and
a
are nonnegative, then
\mathbf{T}(h,a)
is the probability that
X\geq h
and
0\leq Y\leq aX
, where
X
and
Y
are independent standard normal variables (see
[
12
]
). We note the obvious symmetries
\mathbf{T}(-h,a)=\mathbf{T}(h,a)
and
\mathbf{T}(h,-a)=-\mathbf{T}(h,a)
.

Definition 1.17
.

Define the skew-normal distribution with parameter
\alpha
(see
[
2
]
) by its probability density function

\phi(x;\alpha)=2\phi(x)\Phi(\alpha x)

and its cumulative distribution function

\Phi(x;\alpha)=\Phi(x)-2\mathbf{T}(x,\alpha).

This family of distributions includes the standard normal distribution since
\phi(x;0)=\phi(x)
and
\Phi(x;0)=\Phi(x)
. The mean of the distribution corresponding to
\phi(x;\alpha)
is
\alpha\sqrt{2/(\alpha^{2}+1)\pi}
, and its characteristic function is
e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigl(1+\eta\bigl(\alpha t/\sqrt{2(\alpha^{2}+1)}\bigr)\bigr)
.

This skew-normal distribution appears in our next example:

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
2520
follows a “skew-Erdős–Kac law”:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;2520)-\frac{1}{24}\log\log n}{\frac{1}{2}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi\bigg(x;-\sqrt{\frac{5}{13}}\bigg).

The characteristic function of this distribution is
e^{-t^{2}/2}\big(1-\eta(\frac{\sqrt{5}}{6}t)\big)
.

As with Theorem
1.9
, this example represents those universal factor orders
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
where the set preceding
f_{i}
is a doubleton but the two other relevant sets are singletons. The general theorem for this situation (and later ones) requires two more pieces of notation:

Definition 1.18
.

Given positive integers
i
,
k
, and
\ell
, define

\displaystyle\nu_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{i;k,\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{k}}+\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})^{\ell}}-\frac{2}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})}}.

Note that
\sigma_{i;1,1}
is the same as
\sigma_{i}
from Definition
1.5
.

Theorem 1.19
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;d)-\mu_{i}\log\log n}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi\biggl(x;-\frac{\nu_{i}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i;2,1}^{2}}}\biggr),

a skew-normal cumulative distribution function as in Definition
1.17
.
The characteristic function of this distribution is
e^{-t^{2}/2}\big(1-\eta(\frac{\nu_{i}}{2\sigma_{i}}t)\big)
.

While the normalization on the left-hand side uses the same mean
\mu_{i}\log\log n
and variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}\log\log n
as Theorem
1.15
, the presence of the doubleton set
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}
to the left of
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
“steals” some of the copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
away, resulting in a skew-Erdős–Kac law with negative mean instead of the standard Erdős–Kac law. Mathematically, the skew-normal distribution arises because
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
can be represented in terms of the limiting joint distribution of two additive functions, which in this case is a nonsingular bivariate normal distribution.

A similar phenomenon results from a doubleton set
\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
“stealing” some copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
from the right.

Example
.

The numbers of invariant factors of order
720720
and of order
2
each follow skew-Erdős–Kac laws:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;720720)-\frac{1}{48}\log\log n}{(\frac{13}{96}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\Phi\bigg(x;-\sqrt{\frac{45}{163}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;2)-\frac{1}{2}\log\log n}{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\Phi\bigg(x;-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigg).

The characteristic functions of these distributions are, respectively,
e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigl(1-\eta\bigl(\sqrt{\frac{45}{416}}t\bigr)\bigr)
and
e^{-t^{2}/2}\big(1-\eta(\frac{t}{\sqrt{8}})\big)
.

These examples demonstrate the following theorem, which is a refinement of Theorem
1.10
and exhibits a skew-normal distribution analogous to Theorem
1.19
.

Theorem 1.20
.

If
i>1
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;d)-\mu_{i}\log\log n}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi\biggl(x;-\frac{\nu_{i+1}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i;1,2}^{2}}}\biggr).

The characteristic function of this skew-normal distribution is

e^{-t^{2}/2}\big(1-\eta(\frac{\nu_{i+1}}{2\sigma_{i}}t)\big)
.
The same statement holds when
i=1
(so that
d=2
).

We now consider the situation where a singleton
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
is flanked by two doubletons
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
, each of which “steal” some copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. As mentioned before, the corresponding counting function
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
is essentially a linear combination of four additive functions, which leads to examining a (singular) tetravariate normal distribution. We call the resulting limiting distribution a “doubly skew-normal distribution”, although we note that it is different from the distributions described in
[
1
]
(their work combines two different skewing mechanisms, whereas our distribution is skewed twice in the same way). The precise distribution can be worked out, although we delay the complicated formulas for the sake of exposition.

Example
.

The number of invariant factors of order
120
has limiting cumulative distribution function

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;120)-\frac{1}{12}\log\log n}{(\frac{1}{3}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\mathbf{U}\bigg(4x;\frac{7}{\sqrt{6}},\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}},\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}\bigg),

where
\mathbf{U}(x;\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
is given in Definition
5.5
below.
The characteristic function of this distribution is

\displaystyle e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigg(1-\eta\bigg(\frac{3}{8}t\bigg)\bigg)\bigg(1-\eta\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5}{48}}t\bigg)\bigg).

Note that the characteristic function contains two factors involving the function
\eta
, in contrast to the characteristic functions in Theorems
1.19
and
1.20
that have only one such factor. The general result involves all the constants from Definitions
1.5
and
1.18
.

Theorem 1.21
.

If
i>3
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;d)-\mu_{i}\log\log n}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)\\
=\mathbf{U}\biggl(4x;2\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i;1,2}^{2}+\sigma_{i;2,1}^{2}+\sigma_{i;2,2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{i}},2\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i;1,2}^{2}-\sigma_{i;2,1}^{2}-\sigma_{i;2,2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{i}},2\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{i;1,2}^{2}+\sigma_{i;2,1}^{2}-\sigma_{i;2,2}^{2}}}{\sigma_{i}}\biggr),

where
\mathbf{U}(x;\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
is given in Definition
5.5
below.
The characteristic function of this distribution is

\displaystyle e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigg(1-\eta\bigg(\frac{\nu_{i}}{2\sigma_{i}}t\bigg)\bigg)\bigg(1-\eta\bigg(\frac{\nu_{i+1}}{2\sigma_{i}}t\bigg)\bigg).

Note that the case
i=3
is explicitly excluded from this theorem even though
\mathfrak{f}_{2}=\{4,6\}
,
\mathfrak{f}_{3}=\{12\}
, and
\mathfrak{f}_{4}=\{24,60\}
have the appropriate cardinalities. The reason is that the two coincidences that create the doubletons are
\varphi(3)=\varphi(4)=2
and
\varphi(5)=\varphi(8)=4
, and in this case there is the further coincidence that
4
and
8
are both powers of the same prime (a situation that never occurs again—see Proposition
2.13
(d)). It turns out that these nearby powers of
2
result in an additional dependence among the four random variables that breaks a helpful symmetry. Therefore the case
d=12
must be treated as its own special case.

Theorem 1.22
.

If
i=3
(so that
d=12
), then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;12)-\frac{1}{4}\log\log n}{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x),

where
F
is the singular four-variable normal distribution centred at the origin with covariance matrix

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&5/8&1/2&1/8\\
5/8&1&-1/8&1/4\\
1/2&-1/8&1&3/8\\
1/8&1/4&3/8&1/2\end{array}\right],

and
\mathbf{Mn}
represents the minimum of those four random variables (see Definition
5.1
).

There is not much else that can be said about this distribution, beyond the fact that it exists and its expectation can be computed (as was done implicitly in equation (
1.5
) in a previous example).

We have now described the limiting distributions of all of the most significant universal factor orders (the ones appearing in Figure
1
), which correspond to singleton sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
. The remaining universal factor orders, corresponding to doubleton sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
, are the interstitial elements that occur when transitioning from one more significant universal factor order to another (refer to Figure
2
). Unlike the more significant ones, whose distribution is on a
\log\log n
scale, these interstitial distributions are on a
\sqrt{\log\log n}
scale. The distribution is relatively simple but requires a bit more notation.

Definition 1.23
.

Using the notation in Definition
1.13
, we define a truncated normal probability density function

\phi_{+}(x)=\begin{cases}2\phi(x),&\text{if }x\geq 0,\\
0,&\text{if }x<0\end{cases}

and the corresponding truncated normal cumulative distribution function

\Phi_{+}(x)=\max\{\Phi(x)-\Phi(-x),0\}=\max\{\erf(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}),0\}.

This truncated normal normal distribution, which is the same as the distribution of
|X|
where
X
is a standard normal random variable, has characteristic function
e^{-t^{2}/2}(1+\eta(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}))
.

Example
.

The numbers of invariant factors of order
360
and of order
840
have the following distributions:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;360)}{(\frac{5}{18}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Phi(x),&\text{if }x\geq 0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;840)}{(\frac{5}{18}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Phi(x),&\text{if }x\geq 0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the sum of these counting functions has a truncated Erdős–Kac law:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\biggl(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;360)+\mathfrak{I}(m;840)}{(\frac{5}{18}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\biggr)=\Phi_{+}(x).

The corresponding general statement strengthens Theorem
1.12
:

Theorem 1.24
.

If
i
is an integer such that
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=2
, then for
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{I}(m;d)}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\begin{cases}\Phi(x),&\text{if }x\geq 0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\sum_{d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}}\mathfrak{I}(m;d)}{\sigma_{i}(\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\Phi_{+}(x).

Since
\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}
, each
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
appearing in Theorem
1.24
is positive on a set of integers
m
of density
\frac{1}{2}
. But note that these two sets are disjoint, since the two integers
d\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
do not divide each other. Therefore almost all multiplicative groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
have one of these two integers
d
among their invariant factor orders. (We believe the number of the exceptions up to
n
is asymptotic to some constant depending on
i
times
n/\sqrt{\log\log n}
.) These counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
are modeled by the absolute difference of two additive functions of similar size, and it is a coin toss as to which one will be larger for particular values of
m
.

The description of the limiting distribution laws for all the universal factor orders is now complete. By way of contrast, the rare factor orders are much simpler—we can easily strengthen Theorem
1.7
to the following distributional statement:

Theorem 1.25
.

If
d\notin\mathfrak{F}
, then almost all integers
m
satisfy
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)=0
.

For example, almost no multiplicative groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
have an invariant factor with order
8
.

As a side remark, we mention another application of our techniques, namely the determination of the limiting distribution of the multiplicity counting function of any fixed nontrivial abelian subgroup
G
of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

Definition 1.26
.

For any prime power
q
,
let
\mathfrak{P}(G,q)
be the number of elementary divisors of
G
of order
q
, so that
G\cong\prod_{q}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\mathfrak{P}(G,q)}
is an elementary decomposition.
Define

\mathfrak{S}(m;G)=\max\{a\in\mathbb{N}\colon G^{a}\leq(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\}.

Finally, define
\mu_{G}=\min\{1/\varphi(q)\mathfrak{P}(G;q)\colon\mathfrak{P}(G;q)>0\}
.

The following result, which is proved in the dissertation of second author
[
14
, Chapter 7]
, identifies the limiting distribution of
\mathfrak{S}(m;G)
,

Theorem 1.27
.

Let
G
be a nontrivial abelian finite group, and let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}
be the distinct elementary divisors of
G
that satisfy
\varphi(q_{i})\mathfrak{P}(G;q_{i})=\mu_{G}^{-1}
.
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{S}(m;G)-\mu_{G}\log\log n}{(\mu_{G}\log\log n)^{1/2}}\leq x\bigg)=\mathbf{Mn}_{H}(x),

where
H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=F\bigl(\mathfrak{P}(G,q_{1})^{1/2}x_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{P}(G,q_{N})^{1/2}x_{N}\bigr)
and
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is the cumulative distribution function of a multivariate normal random variable
X
whose characteristic function is

\chi_{X}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})=\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{(\varphi(q_{i})\varphi(q_{j}))^{1/2}}{\varphi(\lcm[q_{i},q_{j}])}t_{i}t_{j}\biggr).

Here
\mathbf{Mn}_{H}
represents the minimum of the
N
random variables comprising the components of
H
(see Definition
5.1
below).

1.4.
Overview of the proofs and outline of the paper

We highlight some key concepts and quantities that will be helpful to keep in mind throughout this paper. We begin with the class of additive functions that have been mentioned as important to the proofs of the results in Section
1.3
; these strongly additive functions turn out to be generalizations ot the standard distinct prime factor counting function
\omega(m)
.

Definition 1.28
.

For any set
S
of primes, we define
\omega(m;S)=\#\{p\mid m\colon p\in S\}
. We use a shorthand notation for the important special case

\omega(m;q,a)=\omega\bigl(m;\{p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}\bigr)=\#\{p\mid m\colon p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}.

Further, for any two sets
S
and
T
of primes, we define
\omega(m;S-T)=\omega(m;S)-\omega(m;T)
.

It follows easily, from classical techniques and the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, that
\omega(m;q,a)
follows an Erdős–Kac law with mean and variance asymptotically
\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\log\log m
when
(a,q)=1
.

We now introduce some notation related to the structure of the finite abelian groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. While the main results of this paper are related to the invatiant factor decomposition of such groups, it is helpful to approach the invariant factor decomposition via another canonical representation for finite abelian groups: every such group has a unique elementary divisor decomposition (or primary factor decomposition), that is, a representation as the direct product of cyclic groups of prime power order. Therefore we need notation that indicates how many times a particular prime power appears as an elementary divisor of a multiplicative group. It turns out that a more fundamental quantity groups together elementary divisors that are powers of the same prime:

Definition 1.29
.

Let
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
count the number of elementary divisors of the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
that are of the form
p^{\beta}
where
\beta\geq\alpha
. In particular,
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p)
is the number of
p
-groups in the elementary divisor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

It turns out (see Lemma
2.3
below) that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
is essentially equal to the quantity
\omega(m;p^{\alpha},1)
from Definition
1.28
. Therefore
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
also follows an Erdős–Kac law with asymptotic mean and variance
\frac{1}{\phi(p^{\alpha})}\log\log m
.

In particular, for most integers
m
we would expect
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
to be larger the smaller
\phi(p^{\alpha})
is and vice versa. This property of
m
is extremely important to our proofs throughout, and fortunately the property is indeed common; we are thus motivated to make the following definition.

Definition 1.30
.

Given a real number
y\geq 1
, an integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
is said to be
y
-typical
if for any two prime powers
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
:

(a)

If
\varphi(q_{1})<\varphi(q_{2})\leq y
, then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{1})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{2})
;

(b)

If
\varphi(q_{1})\leq y<\varphi(q_{2})
, then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{1})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{2})
.

In other words, a
y
-typical number is one where the counting functions
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
are “in the correct order” as
q
ranges over all prime powers with
\phi(q)\leq y
. The following proposition, proved in
Section
4.2
, justifies why this property is called
y
-typicality:

Proposition 1.31
.

For all real numbers
y\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
and for all integers
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

P_{n}(m\text{ is not $y$-typical})\ll_{r}\frac{y^{4r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}.

In particular, for any function
y=y(n)
satisfying
y=o\bigl((\log\log n)^{1/4}\bigr)
, almost all integers
m
are
y
-typical, in the sense that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}(m\text{ is $y(n)$-typical})=1.

We conclude this introduction by providing a guide to the remainder of the paper
and the proofs it contains. The majority of this paper is concerned with proving the results in Section
1.3
, since Theorem
1.1
and the theorems in Section
1.2
follow easily from them.

The argument starts in Section
2
with some elementary number theory to convert the problem of the invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
into a statement about the values of finitely many additive functions
\omega(m;q,1)
, provided that
m
is a
y
-typical integer for an appropriate
y
.
In Section
3
we apply the methods from Kubilius
[
8
]
on multivariate distributions
of additive functions to convert statements about additive functions into statements about probabilities.
In Section
4
, we prove probabalistic statements about
y
-typicality, including Proposition
1.31
, using a mixture of analytic and elementary arguments.

In Section
5
, we combine all the above results to finally prove the limiting distribution theorems in Section
1.3
, after which we show how they collectively imply Theorem
1.1
.
The arguments that derive the expectation theorems in Section
1.2
from the results in Section
1.3
are given in Section
6
.

In Appendix
A
we consolidate all the major notation of this paper (including all the notation that has appeared in this introduction). In Appendix
B
we gather all of the main theorems of this introduction together for ease of reference. Finally, Appendix
C
contains two lemmas related to sums of random variables and their characteristic functions and density functions.

2.
From the invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
to additive functions

In this section, we relate the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
to the values of the additive functions
\omega(m;q,1)
from Definition
1.28
, under the assumption that
m
is
y
-typical in the sense of Definition
1.30
. While readers familiar with invariant factor decompositions will find all the material in this section accessible (modulo the cumbersome notation), our later work will benefit from this careful translation from group theory to number theory.

2.1.
Relation between invariant factors and additive functions

We begin with lemmas that hold for all integers
m\geq 3
, without requiring any
y
-typicality assumption. Recall two pieces of notation from the introduction (Definitions
1.28
and
1.29
), namely
\omega(m;S)=\#\{p\mid m\colon p\in S\}
for any set
S
of primes, and
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
for the number of elementary divisors of the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
that are of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\beta}}
where
\beta\geq\alpha
. We will use a variant of this latter notation as well:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})
count the number of times
p^{\alpha}
appears as an elementary divisor of the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\sum_{\beta=\alpha}^{\infty}\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\beta})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})=\omega\bigl(m;\{q\text{ prime}\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel(q-1)\}\bigr)+E(m;p^{\alpha})

where

E(m;p^{\alpha})=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if $p^{\alpha}=2$ and $8\parallel m$},\\
1,&\text{if $p^{\alpha}=2$ and $(4\parallel m$ or $16\mid m)$},\\
1,&\text{if $p=2$ and $\alpha\geq 2$ and $2^{\alpha+2}\parallel m$},\\
1,&\text{if $p\neq 2$ and $p^{\alpha+1}\parallel m$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First suppose that
4\nmid m
. By the Chinese remainder theorem and the fact that multiplicative groups to odd prime power moduli (wihch have primitive roots) are cyclic, the elementary divisor decomposition of the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is

\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\parallel m}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\parallel m\\
p\neq 2\end{subarray}}(\mathbb{Z}_{p-1}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha-1}})\cong\prod_{p\mid m}\prod_{q^{\alpha}\parallel p-1}\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\alpha}}\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\parallel m\\
p\neq 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha-1}}.

For every prime power
p^{\alpha}
, the first factor contributes
\omega\bigl(m;\{q\text{ prime}\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel(q-1)\}\bigr)
to the quantity
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})
. If
p
is odd, the second factor contributes
1
if
p^{\alpha+1}\parallel m
and
0
otherwise, which is precisely
E(m;p^{\alpha})
in this case; while if
p=2
then the second factor contributes
0
which also equals
E(m;2^{\alpha})
since
4\nmid m
.

When
4\mid m
, there is one additional factor
(\mathbb{Z}/2^{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\alpha-2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
which does not affect
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})
for odd primes
p
; and one can check that in every case the definition of
E(m;2^{\alpha})
accounts correctly for this additional factor.
∎

From this formula for
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})
we can deduce a similar formula for
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})
, which will facilitate our computation of the invariant factors themselves.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\omega(m;p^{\alpha},1)+\bar{E}(m;p^{\alpha})

where

\bar{E}(m;p^{\alpha})=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if $p^{\alpha}=2$ and $8\mid m$},\\
1,&\text{if $p^{\alpha}=2$ and $4\parallel m$},\\
1,&\text{if $p=2$ and $\alpha\geq 2$ and $2^{\alpha+2}\mid m$},\\
1,&\text{if $p\neq 2$ and $p^{\alpha+1}\mid m$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular,
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;2)
for all prime powers
p^{\alpha}
.

Proof.

By Definition
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\sum_{\beta=\alpha}^{\infty}\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\beta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta=\alpha}^{\infty}\omega\bigl(m;\{q\text{ prime}\colon p^{\beta}\parallel(q-1)\}\bigr)+\sum_{\beta=\alpha}^{\infty}E(m;p^{\beta})

\displaystyle=\omega\bigl(m;\{q\text{ prime}\colon p^{\alpha}\mid(q-1)\}\bigr)+\sum_{\beta=\alpha}^{\infty}E(m;p^{\beta}).

(Note that all the above sums are convergent since
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\beta})
and
E(m;p^{\beta})
have only finitely many nonzero terms for any fixed
m
). Since
\omega\bigl(m;\{q\text{ prime}\colon p^{\alpha}\mid(q-1)\}\bigr)=\omega(m;q,1)
, we must simply check that the last sum equals
\bar{E}(m;p^{\alpha})
as defined in the statement of the lemma, which is straightforward. The last assertion follows from the individual inequalities
\omega(m;p^{\alpha},1)\leq\omega(m;2,1)
and
\bar{E}(m;p^{\alpha})\leq\bar{E}(m;2)
which are also easy to verify.
∎

The above statement is equivalent to the result given in
[
10
, Definitions 2.7 and 2.8 and Lemma 2.10]
, but stated more conveniently for our purposes. In
[
10
]
it was shown that (in our notation)
\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}^{\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{k})}
is the
p
-Sylow subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, and that the partition of
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{k})
that characterizes this finite
p
-group is the partition conjugate to
(\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{1}),\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{2}),\ldots)
.

The invariant factor orders of a finite abelian group can be obtained directly from the elementary divisors, in a way that is straightforward in practice but somewhat hard to notate as a general statement. The following example should illuminate the relevant ideas before we craft a general proof.

Example
.

Consider
m=1{,}616{,}615=5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 19
. Using the Chinese remainder theorem and the fact that odd prime powers possess primitive roots, we see that

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccccccc}(\mathbb{Z}/1616615\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\cong&(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\times&(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\times&(\mathbb{Z}/11\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\times&(\mathbb{Z}/13\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\times&(\mathbb{Z}/17\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\times&(\mathbb{Z}/19\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\\
&\cong&\mathbb{Z}_{4}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{6}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{10}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{12}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{16}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{18}&\\
&\cong&\mathbb{Z}_{4}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{4}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{16}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\\
&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{3}&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{3}&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{9}&\\
&&&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{5}&&&&&&&\\
\end{array}

where in the last step we again used the Chinese remainder theorem to rewrite each cyclic group on the second line as a product of the elementary divisors in the column below it. These elementary divisors have been grouped prime by prime, and reordering each such row so that the powers of each prime are in increasing order results in

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccccc}(\mathbb{Z}/1616615\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\cong&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{4}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{4}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{16}\\
&&&&&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{3}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{3}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{9}\\
&&&&&&&&&&&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{5}\\
&\cong&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{12}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{12}&\times&\mathbb{Z}_{720}\end{array}

after combining the groups in each column (another application of the Chinese remainder theorem). This last expression is the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/1616615\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

More generally, we can record the outcome of this method in terms of the following lemma:

Proposition 2.4
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 3
.
Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{M}
be a sequence of distinct prime powers containing all
the distinct elementary divisors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,
ordered so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
is decreasing in
i
, and set
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]
. Then

(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\bigg)\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_{M}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{M})}

(2.1)

is the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, after removing all factors in the product where the exponent equals
0
.

Remark 2.5
.

In light of the last assertion of Lemma
2.3
, we may always take
q_{1}=2
if we wish; in particular, doing so proves that the
d_{i}
, and hence the orders of the invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, are always even.

Proof.

Let
G
be the group defined on the right-hand side of equation (
2.1
). Define
\mathcal{E}
to be the set of all orders of elementary divisors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Since
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})}
by the definition of
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})
, it suffices to prove that
G\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})}
.

While
q_{i}
and
d_{i}
are defined only when
1\leq i\leq M
, we make the convention that the expression
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
equals
0
when
i>M
. Define
\mathcal{D}=\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})\}
, so that
M
is the largest element of
\mathcal{D}
. With this notation, we may rewrite

\displaystyle G\cong\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\bigg)\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_{M}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{M})}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\cong\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{p^{\alpha}\parallel d_{i}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

by the Chinese remainder theorem.

We claim that if
i\in\mathcal{D}
, then
p^{\alpha}\mid d_{i}
if and only if
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
.

•

First suppose that
p^{\alpha}\mid d_{i}
. By definition,
p^{\alpha}\mid q_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
, and therefore
q_{j}=p^{\beta}
for some
\beta\geq\alpha
. But then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\beta})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
as claimed.

•

Next suppose that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
. The assumption
i\in\mathcal{D}
implies that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})\geq 0
, and so there exists
\beta\geq\alpha
such that
p^{\beta}\in\mathcal{E}
; choose the smallest such
\beta
, so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\beta})
. Since
p^{\beta}\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
p^{\beta}=q_{j}
for some
j
, and then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\beta})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
implies that
j\leq i
. We conclude that
p^{\alpha}\mid p^{\beta}=q_{j}\mid d_{i}
as claimed.

Since
p^{\alpha}\parallel d_{i}
if and only if
p^{\alpha}\mid d_{i}
and
p^{\alpha+1}\nmid d_{i}
, it follows when
i\in\mathcal{D}
that
p^{\alpha}\parallel d_{i}
if and only if
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
. In particular, since
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
is equivalent to
\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})>0
, we see that
p^{\alpha}\parallel d_{i}
implies that
{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}
.

Therefore

\displaystyle G\cong\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{p^{\alpha}\parallel d_{i}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}\\
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{D}\\
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}

(2.2)

For a fixed prime power
p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}
, let
k
be the smallest integer such that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})
and let
\ell
be the largest integer such that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
. We claim that in fact
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})
and
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})
.

•

We first need to prove that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})
. Since
p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}
we know that
p^{\alpha}=q_{j}
for some
j
. But if
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})
, that would force
j<k
and thus
j
would be an integer smaller than
k
for which
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})
, contradicting the choice of
k
. Thus we indeed have
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})
.

•

We certainly have
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
by the choice of
\ell
, so we next need to prove that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
. If
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})=0
then
\ell=M
and so
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})=0
as well; so we may suppose that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})>0
. Let
\beta
be the largest integer such that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\beta})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
, which forces
p^{\beta}\in\mathcal{E}
, and thus
p^{\beta}=q_{j}
for some
j
. But if
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})<\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})
, that would force
\ell+1>j
and hence
\ell\geq j
, and then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
, contradicting the choice of
\ell
. Thus we indeed have
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{j})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})
.

From this discussion, we see that we have the telescoping sum

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})\end{subarray}}\bigl(\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{k})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})=\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha}),

and so the last expression in equation (
2.2
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle G\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;p^{\alpha+1})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\cong\prod_{p^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{P}(m;p^{\alpha})}

as required.
∎

Recall from Definition
1.2
that
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
denotes the multiplicity of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
in the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 3
.
Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}
be a sequence of distinct prime powers,
ordered so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
is decreasing in
i
, and set
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]
. Suppose that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
for every prime power
q\notin\{q_{1},\dots,q_{N}\}
. Then
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{N})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})
, and

\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})
for each
1\leq i\leq N-1
.

Furthermore, the sequence
d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
contains every invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
less than or equal to
d_{N}
.
If
d
is an invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
exceeding
d_{N}
, then
d_{N}\mid d
.

Proof.

As before, define
\mathcal{E}
to be the set of all orders of elementary divisors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, and set
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}\setminus\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}\}
and
M=N+\#\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. Write
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{q_{N+1},\dots,q_{N}\}
so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
is decreasing in
i
for all
1\leq i\leq M
(this is possible since
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
for all
q\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
by hypothesis), and extend the definition
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]
to all
i\leq M
. We may now apply Proposition
2.4
, obtaining

(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\bigg)\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_{N}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})}

as the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
(after removing all factors in the product where the exponent equals
0
). It follows immediately that
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i}+1)
for
1\leq i\leq N-1
; it also follows that
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{N})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N+1})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})
if
N<M
, while
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{N})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})
if
N=M
. The last assertions follow from the fact that
d_{1}\mid\cdots\mid d_{M}
and every invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is one of the
d_{j}
.
∎

A consequence of this lemma is a clean formula for the total number of invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
:

Lemma 2.7
.

For any integer
m\geq 3
,

\mathfrak{I}(m)=\begin{cases}\omega(m)+1,&\text{if }8\mid m,\\
\omega(m)-1,&\text{if }2\parallel m,\\
\omega(m),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular, this formula justifies the remark made after Theorem
1.7
that the average order of
\mathfrak{I}(m)
is
\log\log m+O(1)
.

Proof.

Set
q_{1}=2
and let
q_{2},\ldots,q_{M}
be the orders of the elementary divisors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
exceeding
2
, ordered so that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})
is decreasing in
i
(which is consistent with
q_{1}=2
thanks to the last assertion of Lemma
2.3
). If we set
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]
, then by Proposition
2.4
,

(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})}\bigg)\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_{M}}^{\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{M})}

is the invariant factor decomposition of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. In particular, the total number of invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is the telescoping sum

\mathfrak{I}(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})=\sum_{i-1}^{M-1}\bigl(\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})\bigr)+\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{M})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{1})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;2).

By Lemma
2.3
,

\mathfrak{Q}(m;2)=\omega(m;2,1)+\bar{E}(m;2)=\begin{cases}\omega(m)-1,&\text{if $2\mid m$},\\
\omega(m),&\text{if $2\nmid m$}\end{cases}\Biggr\}+\begin{cases}2,&\text{if $8\mid m$},\\
1,&\text{if $4\parallel m$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which is equivalent to the desired formula.
∎

2.2.
Properties of the invariant factor order sets

Having now expressed (via Lemmas
2.3
and
2.6
) the counting functions for invariant factors in terms of additive functions, we now study the structure of the invariant factor order sets. As these sets are defined in terms of the total
\varphi
-sequence, this section opens with some lemmas concerning this sequence, before culminating in Proposition
2.13
which classifies the short
\varphi
-sequences relevant to the various theorems in Sections
1.2
and
1.3
.

The first lemma appeared independently as
[
7
, Lemma 4.4]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
q_{1}<q_{2}
are prime powers satisfying
\varphi(q_{1})=\varphi(q_{2})
, then
q_{1}
is a prime.
Consequently, at most two distinct prime powers can have the same totient values, and any two such prime powers are relatively prime.

Proof.

We can immediately rule out
q_{2}
being prime, for if it were then
\varphi(q_{1})<q_{1}\leq q_{2}-1=\varphi(q_{2})
, contrary to assumption. Write
q_{1}=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}
and
q_{2}=p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
with
\alpha_{2}\geq 2
, so that

p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}(p_{1}-1)=\varphi(q_{1})=\varphi(q_{2})=p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}(p_{2}-1).

We can also rule out
p_{1}=p_{2}
since that would imply
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
and hence
q_{1}=q_{2}
, contrary to assumption. In particular,
(p_{1},p_{2})=1
, and thus
p_{2}\mid p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}\mid p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}(p_{1}-1)
implies
p_{2}\mid(p_{1}-1)
and hence
p_{2}<p_{1}
. If we had
\alpha_{1}\geq 2
, then the symmetric argument would show that
p_{1}\mid(p_{2}-1)
and thus
p_{1}<p_{2}
also, a contradiction; therefore
\alpha_{1}=1
and
q_{1}=p_{1}
is prime, establishing the first assertion of the lemma. The second assertion follows from the observation that for any three prime powers
r_{1},r_{2}<r_{3}

where
\varphi(r_{1})=\varphi(r_{2})=\varphi(r_{3})
, the first assertion implies that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are primes with the same totient, and hence
r_{1}=r_{2}
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

We have seen that pairs of prime powers with the same value under
\varphi
correspond precisely to solutions of the equations
p^{\prime}-1=p^{\alpha-1}(p-1)
in primes
p,p^{\prime}
for
\alpha\geq 2
. Equivalently, they correspond precisely to simultaneous prime values of the polynomials
n,n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
. When
\alpha\equiv 2{\text{\rm\ (mod~$6$)}}
it is easy to check that
n^{2}-n+1
divides
n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
, and therefore
n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
is never prime for
\alpha=8,14,20,\dots
.

In all other cases, however, Ljunggren
[
9
, Theorem 3]
proved that
n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
is irreducible, and we can check that
n,n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
is an “admissible” pair of polynomials (the residue class
n\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}
is always feasible); therefore Schinzel’s Hypothesis H implies that there should be infinitely many solutions for every other
\alpha\geq 2
. Contrary to early data, such solutions are rather rare: the primes
p^{\prime}
appearing in such solutions have relative density
0
, for the simple reason that at most
O(\sqrt{x})
integers up to
x
are values of any of the polynomials
n^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha-1}+1
. In other words, almost all of the
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
are singleton sets.

Lemma 2.10
.

No two doubleton sets are adjacent in the sequence
(\mathfrak{f}_{i})
of universal factor order sets.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Definition
1.4
and Lemma
2.8
.
∎

The next lemma will be used to show that powers of the same prime are rarely close to each other in the total
\varphi
-sequence.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any prime power
p^{\alpha}\geq 5
and any integer
\beta>\alpha
, there exists a prime
\ell
satisfying
\varphi(p^{\alpha})<\ell-1<\varphi(p^{\beta})
.

Proof.

We use Bertrand’s postulate in the form originally stated by Bertrand and proved by Chebyshev
[
15
]
: for any integer
n\geq 4
, there exists a prime in the interval
(n,2n-2)
. Taking
n=\varphi(p^{\alpha})+1
(which is at least
5
since the only integers
m
with
\varphi(m)<4
are
m=1,2,3,4,6
), we see that there is a prime
\ell
satisfying
\varphi(p^{\alpha})+1<\ell<2\varphi(p^{\alpha})
. In particular,
\varphi(p^{\alpha})<\ell-1<p^{\beta-\alpha}\varphi(p^{\alpha})=\varphi(p^{\beta})
as desired.
∎

We have seen that Theorems
1.8
–
1.11
that the expectations of the counting functions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
depend upon the size of three consecutive universal factor order sets
\mathfrak{f}_{i-1},\mathfrak{f}_{i},\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}
; the same is true of their normalized limiting distributions, as we saw in Theorems
1.15
and
1.19
–
1.21
. The next proposition will establish the fact that the cases in these quatrains of theorems exhaust all possibilities.

Recall the notion of a (finite)
\varphi
-sequence from Definition
1.3
.

Definition 2.12
.

A
two-totient
\varphi
-sequence
is a
\varphi
-sequence
\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{j}\}
satisfying:

•

\{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i}),\dots,\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})\}
has exactly two distinct elements;

•

If
k<i
then
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{k})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
;

•

If
k>j
then
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{k})>\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
.

In other words, for every two consecutive prime-power totients, there is a unique two-totient
\varphi
-sequence that is the inverse image of those two totient values under the Euler
\varphi
-function.

Proposition 2.13
.

Every two-totient
\varphi
-sequence is of one of four types:

(a)

\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}\}
with
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
.

In this case,
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, and
\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}
are relatively prime.

(b)

\{\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}\}
with
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
.

In this case,
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=1
, and
\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}
are pairwise relatively prime.

(c)

\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}\}
with
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+2})
.

In this case,
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, and
\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}
are pairwise relatively prime, except when
i=1
in which case
\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}\}=\{2,3,4\}
.

(d)

\{\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}\}
with
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+2})
.

In this case,
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i-1}=2
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i}=1
and
\#\mathfrak{f}_{i+1}=2
, and
\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}
are pairwise relatively prime, except when
i=3
in which case
\{\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1},\mathfrak{q}_{i+2}\}=\{3,4,5,8\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
, each of the two totient values has either one or two preimages, which immediately yields the four cases described. The cardinalities of the sets
\mathfrak{f}_{j}
follow directly from Definition
1.4
and the definition of a two-totient
\varphi
-sequence: for example, in the first case we automatically have
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
and
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+2})
.

The coprimality assertions follow from Lemma
2.11
: for example, in the first case
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
and
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
are consecutive prime-power totients, which means there cannot be a prime
\ell
with
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\ell)<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
, which by Lemma
2.11
means that
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}
cannot be powers of the same prime. These arguments hold whenever
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\geq 4
, and the remaining cases are easily checked by hand.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

The proof of Proposition

gives a bit of extra information, namely that
\mathfrak{q}_{i-1}
is prime in cases (b) and (d) and
\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}
is prime in cases (c) and (d).

2.3.
Invariant factors of
y
-typical numbers

Before summarizing the important properties of
y
-typical numbers for later use, we consider the following definitions of functions involving the prime powers with totient values up to some parameter.

Definition 2.15
.

We define

\mathfrak{V}(x)=\lcm\{p^{\alpha}\colon\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{W}(x)=\#\{p^{\alpha}\colon\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x\}.

As a reality check on the notation, we can verify from Definition
1.4
that
\mathfrak{f}_{\mathfrak{W}(x)}=\{\mathfrak{V}(x)\}
for all
x\geq 1
.

The two functions just defined are closely related to the classical prime-counting functions

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)=\log\bigl(\lcm\{p^{\alpha}\colon p^{\alpha}\leq x\}\bigr)\sim x

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\#\{p^{\alpha}\leq x\}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(x^{1/k})\sim\frac{x}{\log x};

(2.3)

we detour slightly to establish a brief lemma solidifying this intuition.

Lemma 2.16
.

For all
x\geq 3
, we have
0\leq\log\mathfrak{V}(x)-\psi(x)\ll\log x\log\log x
and
0\leq\mathfrak{W}(x)-\pi^{*}(x)\ll\log x
.

Proof.

We first observe that for any integer
\alpha\geq 1
, there is at most one prime
p
such that
\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x<p^{\alpha}
. Indeed, if
p^{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{p})=\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x<p^{\alpha}
then
p-1=p(1-\frac{1}{p})\leq p(1-\frac{1}{p})^{1/\alpha}\leq x^{1/\alpha}<p
, and therefore
p=\lfloor x^{1/\alpha}\rfloor+1
is forced.

Now, writing

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\log p\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\\
p^{\alpha}\leq x\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\\
p^{\alpha}\leq x\end{subarray}}\log p

\displaystyle\log\mathfrak{V}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\log p\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\\
\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\\
\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x\end{subarray}}\log p,

we immediately see that
\log\mathfrak{V}(x)\geq\psi(x)
. More precisely, each sum can be restricted to
\alpha\leq\frac{\log x}{\log 2}+1
(for otherwise
2^{\alpha-1}>x
), and thus

\displaystyle\log\mathfrak{V}(x)-\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\leq(\log x)/\log 2+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\\
\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x<p^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\log p

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\leq(\log x)/\log 2+1}\log\bigl(\lfloor x^{1/\alpha}\rfloor+1\bigr)\ll\sum_{\alpha\leq(\log x)/\log 2+1}\alpha\log x\ll\log x\log\log x.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(x)-\pi^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\leq(\log x)/\log 2+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\\
\varphi(p^{\alpha})\leq x<p^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\alpha\leq(\log x)/\log 2+1}1\ll\log x.\qed

The following proposition gives a condensed summary of the important properties of
y
-typical numbers, using the functions
\mathfrak{V}(x)
and
\mathfrak{W}(x)
just defined as well as the total
\varphi
-sequence
(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
and the universal factor order sets
(\mathfrak{f}_{i})
from Definitions
1.3
and
1.4
. Part (
g
) of the proposition relates the invariant factor counting function
\mathfrak{I}(m;d)
from Definition
1.2
to the additive function
\omega(m;S-T)
from Definition
1.28
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
y\geq 1
, and set
N=\mathfrak{W}(y)
, so that
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{N}
are the prime powers whose totients are less than or equal to
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})
. Fix a
y
-typical number
m
, and define
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
to be
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{N}
ordered so that for each
1\leq i\leq N-1
,

\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1}),\text{ and }\varphi(q_{i})\leq\varphi(q_{i+1})\text{ if }\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1}).

For each
1\leq i\leq N
, define
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]
. Then:

(a)

1=\varphi(q_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\varphi(q_{N})
.

(b)

Let
1\leq i\leq N-1
. If
\varphi(q_{i})<\varphi(q_{i+1})
then
d_{i}=\mathfrak{V}(\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i}))
and
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\{d_{i}\}
.

(c)

For
1\leq i\leq N-1
, we have
d_{i}\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
.

(d)

d_{N}=\mathfrak{V}(y)
and
\mathfrak{f}_{N}=\{d_{N}\}
.

(e)

If
d
is an invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
not exceeding
\mathfrak{V}(y)
, then
d=d_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq N
.

(f)

If
d
is an invariant factor of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
exceeding
\mathfrak{V}(y)
, then
\mathfrak{V}(y)\mid d
.

(g)

Define
Q_{i}=\{p\colon p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q_{i}$)}}\}
. Then for
1\leq i\leq N-1
,

\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})=\omega(m;Q_{i}-Q_{i+1})+\bar{E}(m;q_{i})-\bar{E}(m;q_{i+1}),

(2.4)

where
\bar{E}
is the function defined in Lemma
2.3
.
Furthermore,
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{N})\leq\omega(m;Q_{N})+\bar{E}(m;q_{N})
.

The reader should take care to distinguish the temporary ordering
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
, which depends on
m
, from the universal ordering
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{N}
from Definition
1.3
. Indeed, part of what is being proved in this proposition is that the two orderings are essentially the same (which is what
y
-typicality is designed to achieve), except that when
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
we only have the equality of unordered sets
\{q_{i},q_{i+1}\}=\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}\}
. Recall Definition
1.30
for
y
-typicality.

Proof.

Throughout this proof,
1\leq i\leq N-1
.

(a)

If
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})
, we are already given that
\phi(q_{i})\leq\varphi(q_{i+1})
. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})
, then
\phi(q_{i})>\varphi(q_{i+1})
would violate the assumption that
m
is
y
-typical; therefore
\phi(q_{i})\leq\varphi(q_{i+1})
in this case as well. This argument establishes the inequalities in part (
a
), which in turn implies that
q_{1}=2
(since no prime power has as small a totient value) and thus that
\varphi(q_{1})=1
.

(b)

By part (
a
) and the assumption
\varphi(q_{i})<\varphi(q_{i+1})
, there are exactly
i
prime powers with totient values less than or equal to
\varphi(q_{i})
, and therefore
\varphi(q_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i+1})
. (Note that we can’t conclude that
q_{i}=\mathfrak{q}_{i}
, since we might have
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
.) Then both
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\{\lcm[\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i}]\}=\{\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]\}=\{d_{i}\}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\mathfrak{f}_{\mathfrak{W}(\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i}))}=\{\mathfrak{V}(\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i}))\}
follow from Definition
1.4
.

(c)

The case
\varphi(q_{i})<\varphi(q_{i+1})
is already done by part (
b
). On the other hand, if
\varphi(q_{i})=\varphi(q_{i+1})
, then by part (
a
) and Lemma
2.8
,

\varphi(q_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\varphi(q_{i-1})<\varphi(q_{i})=\varphi(q_{i+1})<\varphi(q_{i+2}).

It follows that
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{i-1}\}=\{\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i-1}\}
and
\{q_{i},q_{i+1}\}=\{\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}\}
, and so
d_{i}=\lcm[q_{1},\dots,q_{i}]=\lcm[\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{i-1},q_{i}]\in\mathfrak{f}_{i}
by Definition
1.4
regardless of whether
q_{i}=\mathfrak{q}_{i}
or
q_{i}=\mathfrak{q}_{i+1}
.

(d)

Since
\varphi(q_{N})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})
by construction, the proof of part (
d
) is the same as the proof of part (
b
) (on noting that
\mathfrak{V}(\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N}))=\mathfrak{V}(y)
).

(e)

Extend the sequence
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
to a longer sequence
q_{1},\dots,q_{M}
that contains all the distinct elementary divisors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, ordered so that
\mathfrak{I}(m;q_{i})
is decreasing in
i
(this extension preserves the first
N
elements precisely because of part (b) of the definition of
y
-typicality), and extend the sequence
d_{i}
accordingly. Then all invariant factors of
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
are in the sequence
d_{1},\dots,d_{M}
by Proposition
2.4
. In particular, since each
d_{i}\mid d_{i+1}
and
d_{N}=\mathfrak{V}(y)
by part (
d
), all invariant factors up to
d_{N}
must be in
\{d_{1},\dots,d_{N}\}
as claimed.

(f)

Since
d_{N}=\mathfrak{V}(y)
by part (
d
), part (
f
) follows immediately from Lemma
2.6
, since the assumption that
m
is
y
-typical implies that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
for every prime power
q\notin\{q_{1},\dots,q_{N}\}
.

(g)

The equality
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})=\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i})-\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{i+1})
follows immediately from Lemma
2.6
, and thus by Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{i})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\omega(m;Q_{i})+\bar{E}(m;q_{i})\bigr)-\bigl(\omega(m;Q_{i+1})+\bar{E}(m;q_{i+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\omega(m;Q_{i})-\omega(m;Q_{i+1})+\bar{E}(m;q_{i})-\bar{E}(m;q_{i+1})

by Definition
1.28
. The assertions
\mathfrak{I}(m;d_{N})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{N})\leq\omega(m;Q_{N})+\bar{E}(m;q_{N})
similarly follow from Lemmas
2.6
and
2.3
. ∎

.

3.
From additive functions to probability distributions

In this section, we form a bridge between additive functions and probability distributions by using the techniques of Kubilius to explore the properties of the strongly additive functions
\omega(m;S-T)
, including
\omega(m;S)
and
\omega(m;q,a)
, from Definition
1.28
. Given that Section
2
connected invariant factors to these additive functions, the cumulative effect by the end of this section will be the ability to obtain multivariate limiting distributions for vectors whose components are the additive functions arising in the study of invariant factors. Proofs of our primary theorems, however, will still require further work in Section
5
to translate the multivariate distributions discussed in this section into univariate distributions for the invariant factors themselves.

3.1.
Concepts from Kubilius

Our arguments will make significant use of material from Kubilius
[
8
]
concerning joint distributions of multiple additive functions. For our purposes, it will be important to understand the dependence of error terms on the additive functions involved; accordingly, we include some slightly modified versions of Kubilius’s results in this section.

The following definitions encode information about the asymptotic means and variances of additive functions:

Definition 3.1
.

For any real-valued additive function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

\displaystyle A_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p},

\displaystyle C_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\max\{|f(p^{\alpha})|\colon p^{\alpha}\leq x\},

\displaystyle B_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{|f(p)|^{2}}{p}\bigg)^{1/2},

\displaystyle D_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq x}\frac{f(p^{\alpha})^{2}}{p^{\alpha}}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Definition 3.2
.

For any prime power
p^{\alpha}
, define

\rho(p^{\alpha})=\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigg).

Since the number of integers up to
n
that are divisible by an integer
d
is
\frac{n}{d}+O(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m\}

\displaystyle=\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\mid m\}-\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha+1}\mid m\}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{p^{\alpha}}+O(1)-\frac{n}{p^{\alpha+1}}+O(1)

\displaystyle=n\rho(p^{\alpha})+O(1).

We first give an adaptation of
[
8
, Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Uniformly for all additive functions
f
and all integers
n\geq 2
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))-\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})\ll\frac{C_{f}(n)}{\log n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))-A_{f}(n)\ll C_{f}(n).

Proof.

In this proof, all implicit constants are absolute.
We calculate that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}f(m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\parallel m}f(p^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\bigl(n\rho(p^{\alpha})+O(1)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f}(n)}{n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}1\biggr)=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f}(n)}{n}\pi^{*}(n)\biggr),

using the prime-power counting function
\pi^{*}
defined in equation (
2.3
); this establishes the first inequality since
\pi^{*}(n)\ll n/\log n
. Continuing,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f}(n)}{\log n}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq n}\biggl(\frac{f(p^{\alpha})}{p}-\frac{f(p^{\alpha})}{p^{2}}\biggr)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
\alpha\geq 2\end{subarray}}f(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f}(n)}{\log n}\biggr)

\displaystyle=A_{f}(n)+O\biggl(\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{C_{f}(n)}{p^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
\alpha\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{C_{f}(n)}{p^{\alpha}}+\frac{C_{f}(n)}{\log n}\biggr)

since
\rho(p^{\alpha})\leq\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}
. The first sum in the error term is
\ll C_{f}(n)
, while

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
\alpha\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{C_{f}(n)}{p^{\alpha}}\leq C_{f}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\sum_{\alpha=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}=C_{f}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p^{2}-p}\ll C_{f}(n)

as well. We conclude that

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))=A_{f}(n)+O\biggl(C_{f}(n)+C_{f}(n)+\frac{C_{f}(n)}{\log n}\biggr)=A_{f}(n)+O(C_{f}(n))

as claimed.
∎

We next provide an adaptation of
[
8
, Lemma 4.4 (see also the beginning of Chapter 3)]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Uniformly for all additive functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m))=\sum_{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\frac{f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})}{p^{\alpha}}+O\bigl(C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\bigr).

Proof.

We begin by writing

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m\leq n}f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\leq n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\parallel m}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})\sum_{q^{\beta}\parallel m}f_{2}(q^{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
q^{\beta}\leq n\end{subarray}}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m,\,q^{\beta}\parallel m\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m,\,q^{\beta}\parallel m\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m\}.

Since

\displaystyle\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}\parallel m,q^{\beta}\parallel m\}

\displaystyle=\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\mid m\}-\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha+1}q^{\beta}\mid m\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha}q^{\beta+1}\mid m\}+\#\{m\leq n\colon p^{\alpha+1}q^{\beta+1}\mid m\}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}}-\frac{n}{p^{\alpha+1}q^{\beta}}-\frac{n}{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta+1}}+\frac{n}{p^{\alpha+1}q^{\beta+1}}+O(1)

when
p\neq q
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\biggl(\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})\biggl(\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\biggl(\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\biggr)\biggr)-\sum_{p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\beta})

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{\alpha+1}}\biggr)\biggr).

(3.1)

We quickly estimate the error terms in this expression, since

\sum_{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})O\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\biggr)\ll\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)}{n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n}1\leq\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)}{n}\sum_{k\leq n}1=C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)

(we could save a factor of
(\log\log n)/\log n
, but other error terms make that unneeded) and

\displaystyle\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{\alpha+1}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p^{2}-p}\ll C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})

\displaystyle\ll C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha+\beta}\leq n\\
\alpha,\beta\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{\alpha+\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\beta=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{\alpha+\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\ll C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n).

Inserting these three estimates into equation (
3.1
) establishes the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Uniformly for all additive functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m))\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{2}(m))=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\sum_{q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\biggl(C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\biggr).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m))\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{2}(m))

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})\rho(p^{\alpha})+O\bigg(\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\bigg(\frac{C_{f_{2}}(n)}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\sum_{q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f_{2}}(n)}{\log n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}|f_{1}(p^{\alpha})|\rho(p^{\alpha})

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)}{\log n}\sum_{q^{\beta}\leq n}|f_{2}(p^{\alpha})|\rho(q^{\beta})+\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)}{\log^{2}n}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\sum_{q^{\beta}\leq n}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\biggl(\frac{C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)}{\log n}\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\rho(p^{\alpha})\biggr).

The lemma now follows since

\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\rho(p^{\alpha})\leq\sum_{p\leq n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}\rho(p^{\alpha})=\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p}\ll\log\log n.\qed

Lemma 3.6
.

Uniformly for all additive functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m))-\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m))\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{2}(m))=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\frac{f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})}{p^{\alpha}}+O\big(C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\big).

Proof.

Subtracting the formula in Lemma
3.5
from the formula in Lemma
3.4
yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m)f_{2}(m))

\displaystyle{}-\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{1}(m))\mathbf{E}_{n}(f_{2}(m))

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\frac{f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})}{p^{\alpha}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
q^{\beta}\leq n\\
p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}>n\end{subarray}}f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(q^{\beta})\rho(p^{\alpha})\rho(q^{\beta})+O\big(C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\leq n}\frac{f_{1}(p^{\alpha})f_{2}(p^{\alpha})}{p^{\alpha}}+O\biggl(C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}\leq n\\
q^{\beta}\leq n\\
p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}>n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{q^{\beta}}+C_{f_{1}}(n)C_{f_{2}}(n)\biggr).

But by
[
8
, equation (3.7)]
, the sum in the error term is bounded, which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

The main distributional results of Kubilius hold for a specific class of additive functions, which we now define.

Definition 3.7
.

The class
\mathcal{H}
is the set of real-valued additive functions
f(n)

for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{f}(n)=\infty
and for which
there exists an unbounded increasing function
r(n)=n^{o(1)}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{D_{f}(r)}/{D_{f}(n)}=1
.
For strongly additive functions, it is equivalent to stipulate that
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{f}(n)=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{f}(r)/B_{f}(n)=1
.

The following proposition gives the joint limiting distribution of a finite number of additive functions under the
same scaling.
Define the vector

\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)=\biggl(\frac{f_{1}(p)}{B_{f_{1}}(n)},\ldots,\frac{f_{s}(p)}{B_{f_{s}}(n)}\biggr).

(3.2)

Proposition 3.8

(Theorem 8.1 of
[
8
]
)
.

If
f_{1}(m),\ldots,f_{s}(m)\in\mathcal{H}
are strongly additive functions, then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\displaystyle F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{f_{1}(m)-A_{f_{1}}(n)}{B_{f_{1}}(n)}<x_{1},\,\ldots,\,\frac{f_{s}(m)-A_{f_{s}}(n)}{B_{f_{s}}(n)}<x_{s}\bigg)

is the cumulative distribution function of a random vector
\mathbf{Y}
;

(b)

There exists a nonnegative completely additive set
function
Q(\mathcal{E})
defined for all Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
not containing the origin
\mathbf{0}
,
a constant vector
\mathbf{\Gamma}
, and
a nonnegative quadratic form
\sigma(\mathbf{T})
in
s
variables
that satisfy:

(i)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)\in\mathcal{I}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}=Q(\mathcal{I})

for every interval of continuity
\mathcal{I}
of
Q(\mathcal{E})
with
\mathbf{0}\not\in\bar{\mathcal{I}}
;

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2}}{p(1+|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2})}\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)=-\mathbf{\Gamma}
;

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{n}(p))^{2}}{p}=\sigma(\mathbf{T}).

When these conditions hold, the characteristic function
\chi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{T})
of
Y
satisfies

\log\chi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{T})=i\mathbf{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{T}-\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{T})}{2}+\int_{|\mathbf{X}|>0}\bigg(e^{i\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{X}}-1-\frac{i\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{X}}{1+|\mathbf{X}|^{2}}\bigg)\,dQ.

(3.3)

Remark 3.9
.

In its full generality, the statement of Proposition
3.8
calls for some clarifications.
An
exceptional point
for a vector-valued random variable is any point at which any of the random vector’s component’s cumulative distribution function is discontinuous.
Since a cumulative distribution function can have exceptional points, in part (a) the limit defining
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
is only required to exist for nonexceptional points of
F
.

Also, in part (b)(i), an
interval of continuity

\mathcal{I}
for a function
Q
on the Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
is an
s
-dimensional rectangle with the property that
\lim_{n\to\infty}Q(\mathcal{A}_{n})=Q(\mathcal{I})
for any ascending chain of Borel sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq\cdots
where
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathcal{I}
,
and similarly that
\lim_{n\to\infty}Q(\mathcal{B}_{n})=Q(\mathcal{I})
for any descending chain of Borel sets
\mathcal{B}_{1}\supseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}\supseteq\cdots
where
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{I}
.

All this being said, our application of Proposition
3.8
(see
Proposition
3.17
below) will yield a multivariate normal distribution; in particular, there will be no exceptional points, and every interval will be an interval of continuity, in that situation.

[
8
]
; in the next section we apply these results to the additive functions relevant to our work.

3.2.
Application to the relevant additive functions

We now use the results of the previous section to establish multivariate Erdős–Kac theorems for vector-valued functions whose components are of the form
\omega(m;S-T)
.
We first set out some convenient notation for our analysis of the additive functions from Definition
1.28
and their associated quantities from Definition
3.1
.

Definition 3.10
.

Given a set
S
of primes, define its indicator function

1_{S}(p)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }p\in S,\\
0,&\text{if }p\notin S.\end{cases}

We write
A_{\omega}(x;S)
for
A_{\omega(m;S)}(x)
and similarly for the other quantities from Definition
3.1
, so that in particular

A_{\omega}(x;S)=B_{\omega}(x;S)^{2}=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1_{S}(p)}{p}

When
S=\{p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}
, we similarly write
A_{\omega}(x;q,a)
for
A_{\omega(m;q,a)}(x)
and so on.

Also, given two sets
S
and
T
, define their symmetric difference
S\sdif T=(S\setminus T)\cup(T\setminus S)
. We also write
A_{\omega}(x;S-T)
for
A_{\omega(m;S-T)}(x)
and so on, so that

A_{\omega}(x;S-T)=A_{\omega}(x;S)-A_{\omega}(x;T)\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\omega}(x;S-T)^{2}=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1_{S\sdif T}(p)}{p}.

(3.4)

Lemma 3.11
.

Uniformly for all sets
S
and
T
of prime numbers,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;S-T)\bigr)

\displaystyle=A_{\omega}(n;S-T)+O(1).

Proof.

Note that
0\leq C_{\omega}(n;S-T)\leq 1
for any sets
S
and
T
.
Thus by Lemma
3.3
,

\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;S-T)\bigr)=A_{\omega}(n;S-T)+O\bigl(C_{\omega}(n;S-T)^{2}\bigr)=A_{\omega}(n;S-T)+O(1),

where the implicit constant is absolute.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Uniformly for relatively prime integers
a
and
q\geq 2
and real numbers
x\geq 3
,

A_{\omega}(x;q,a)=B_{\omega}(x;q,a)^{2}=\frac{\log\log x}{\varphi(q)}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p(q,a)}+\frac{\log q}{\varphi(q)}\biggr),

where
p(q,a)
is the smallest prime congruent to
a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}
. In particular,

A_{\omega}(x;q,1)=B_{\omega}(x;q,1)^{2}=\frac{\log\log x}{\varphi(q)}+O\biggl(\frac{\log q}{\varphi(q)}\biggr).

Proof.

By definition,

A_{\omega}(x;q,a)=B_{\omega}(x;q,a)^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}=\frac{\log\log x}{\varphi(q)}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p(q,a)}+\frac{\log q}{\varphi(q)}\biggr),

where the last asymptotic formula appears in
[
13
, Remark 1 after Theorem 1]
. The second assertion follows since
p(q,1)\geq q+1>\varphi(q)
.
∎

Definition 3.13
.

The
Dirichlet density
of a set
S
of primes is

\mathfrak{d}(S)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log x}\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1_{S}(p)}{p}

provided the limit exists.
Note that for
0<\mu\leq 1
, we have
\mathfrak{d}(S)=\mu
if and only if
A_{\omega}(x;S)=B_{\omega}(x;S)^{2}\sim\mu\log\log x
. In particular, Dirichlet showed that
\mathfrak{d}\bigl(\{p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}\bigr)=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}
when
a
and
q\geq 2
are relatively prime.

The following lemma, used in the proof of Proposition
3.17
below, will help us practice the notation just introduced.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
,
T_{1}
, and
T_{2}
be sets of primes such that each
S_{i}\cap T_{j}
has a Dirichlet density. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log n}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{(1_{S_{1}}(p)-1_{T_{1}}(p))(1_{S_{2}}(p)-1_{T_{2}}(p))}{p}\\
=\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}(T_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(T_{1}\cap S_{2}).

Proof.

We have simply

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log\log n}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{(1_{S_{1}}(p)-1_{T_{1}}(p))(1_{S_{2}}(p)-1_{T_{2}}(p))}{p}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log n}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{S_{1}}(p)1_{S_{2}}(p)+1_{T_{1}}(p)1_{T_{2}}(p)-1_{S_{1}}(p)1_{T_{2}}(p)-1_{T_{1}}(p)1_{S_{2}}(p)}{p}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log n}\biggl(\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{S_{1}\cap S_{2}}(p)}{p}+\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{T_{1}\cap T_{2}}(p)}{p}+\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{S_{1}\cap T_{2}}(p)}{p}+\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{T_{1}\cap S_{2}}(p)}{p}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}(T_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(T_{1}\cap S_{2})

by the definition of Dirichlet density.
∎

As the results of Kubilius
apply primarily to additive functions in the class
\mathcal{H}
from Definition
3.7
, we need to understand when
\omega(m;S-T)
lies in
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
S
and
T
be sets of primes.

(a)

If
S\sdif T
has positive Dirichlet density, then
\omega(m;S-T)\in\mathcal{H}
.

(b)

If
S
has positive Dirichlet density, then
\omega(m;S)\in\mathcal{H}
.

(c)

For any relatively prime integers
a
and
q\geq 2
, we have
\omega(m;q,a)\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Certainly
\omega(m;S-T)
is a strongly additive function by definition. Moreover,

B_{\omega}(x;S-T)=\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1_{S\sdif T}(p)}{p}\bigg)^{1/2}\sim\bigl(\mathfrak{d}(S\sdif T)\log\log x\bigr)^{1/2}\to\infty.

Set
r=r(n)=n^{1/\log\log n}
, so that
r=n^{o(1)}
but still
r\to\infty
. Then

\bigg(\frac{B_{\omega}(r;S-T)}{B_{\omega}(n;S)}\bigg)^{2}\sim\frac{\mathfrak{d}(S\sdif T)\log\log r}{\mathfrak{d}(S\sdif T)\log\log n}=\frac{\log\log n-\log\log\log n}{\log\log n}\sim 1,

which completes the verification that
\omega(m;S-T)\in\mathcal{H}
as claimed in part (a). Part (b) follows from part (a) by taking
T=\emptyset
, and part (c) follows from part (b) by taking
S=\{p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}
.
∎

With these preliminary definitions and observations out of the way, we move on to statements about limiting distributions.
While the primary tool used in our proofs will concern multivariate limiting distributions, the univariate
distributions will also play a role. The limiting distribution for
\omega(m;S-T)
can be stated as follows:

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
S
and
T
be sets of primes such that
\mathfrak{d}(S\sdif T)>0
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\omega(m;S-T)-A_{\omega}(n;S-T)}{B_{\omega}(n;S-T)}<x\bigg)=\Phi(x),

(3.5)

the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable.

Proof.

By equation (
3.4
),

B_{\omega}(n;S-T)^{2}=\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1_{S\sdif T}(p)}{p}\sim\mathfrak{d}(S\sdif T)\log\log n

by Definition
3.13
of Dirichlet density. In particular, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, we see that
|\omega(p;S-T)|\leq 1<\varepsilon B_{\omega}(n;S-T)
once
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
\varepsilon
, and consequently

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{B_{\omega}(n;S-T)^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
|\omega(p,S-T)|>\varepsilon B_{\omega}(n;S-T)\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(p,S-T)^{2}}{p}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{B_{\omega}(n;S-T)^{2}}\cdot 0=0.

Since
\omega(m;S-T)
is strongly additive, the evaluation of the limiting cumulative distribution function (
3.5
) now follows from
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
(an analogue of Lindeberg’s condition from probability).
∎

The multivariate version of the above lemma is essential to later proofs:

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{s}
and
R_{1},\ldots,R_{s}
be sets of primes such that each
Q_{i}\sdif R_{i}
has a nonzero Dirichlet density,
and each
Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}
,
Q_{i}\cap R_{j}
, and
R_{i}\cap R_{j}
(including when
i=j
) has a Dirichlet density.
Then

F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\bigg(\frac{\omega(m;Q_{1}-R_{1})-(\mathfrak{d}(Q_{1})-\mathfrak{d}(R_{1}))\log\log n}{(\mathfrak{d}(Q_{1}\sdif R_{1})\log\log n)^{1/2}}<x_{1},\ldots\\
\ldots,\frac{\omega(m;Q_{s}-R_{s})-(\mathfrak{d}(Q_{s})-\mathfrak{d}(R_{s}))\log\log n}{(\mathfrak{d}(Q_{s}\sdif R_{s})\log\log n)^{1/2}}<x_{s}\bigg)

(3.6)

is the cumulative distribution function (with domain
\mathbb{R}^{s}
) of a random vector
\mathbf{X}
with characteristic function

\chi_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}))
,
where

\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap Q_{j})+\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\cap R_{j})-\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap R_{j})-\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\cap Q_{j})}{\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\sdif R_{i})^{1/2}\mathfrak{d}(Q_{j}\sdif R_{j})^{1/2}}t_{i}t_{j}.

(3.7)

Remark 3.18
.

The quadratic form
\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
can be written in the form
\mathbf{X}M\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}
, where
M
is the symmetric matrix whose
(i,j)
th entry is the fraction in equation (
3.7
).
Note that when
i=j
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap Q_{j})

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\cap R_{j})-\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap R_{j})-\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\cap Q_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i})+\mathfrak{d}(R_{i})-2\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap R_{i})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap R_{i})+\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\setminus R_{i})\bigr)+\bigl(\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\cap Q_{i})+\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\setminus Q_{i})\bigr)-2\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap R_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\setminus R_{i})+\mathfrak{d}(R_{i}\setminus Q_{i})=\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\sdif R_{i}),

and thus the diagonal entries of
M
always equal
1
.

Furthermore, note that the formula for
\chi_{\mathbf{X}}
implies that the limiting distribution is a multivariate normal distribution.
The matrix
M
is the covariance matrix of the distribution, and each entry in the matrix is the covariance of the random variables that are the vector components of
\mathbf{X}
.
While Kubilius
[
8
, pages 136–137]
gives a short discussion of this possibility as a special case of Proposition
3.8
, we provide a full proof here for the sake of clarity.

Remark 3.19
.

It is helpful to consider the special case
R_{1}=\cdots=R_{s}=\emptyset
, so that we are examining the distribution of
\omega(m;Q_{1}),\ldots,\omega(m;Q_{s})
since
\omega(m;S)=\omega(m;S-\emptyset)
.
In this situation, the function in equation (

) simplifies to

\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\cap Q_{j})}{\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i})^{1/2}\mathfrak{d}(Q_{j})^{1/2}}t_{i}t_{j}.

Proof of Proposition
3.17
.

We invoke Proposition
3.8
with the strongly additive functions
f_{i}(m)=\omega(m;Q_{i}-R_{i})
; note that these functions are indeed in the class
\mathcal{H}
by Lemma
3.15
. In this setting, equation (
3.2
) becomes

\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)=\bigg(\frac{1_{Q_{1}}(p)-1_{R_{1}}(p)}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{1}-R_{1})},\ldots,\frac{1_{Q_{s}}(p)-1_{R_{s}}(p)}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{s}-R_{s})}\bigg)

(3.8)

using the notation from Definition
3.10
.

We need to verify the conditions in Proposition
3.8
(b), which requires defining three objects; we use the quadratic form
\sigma(\mathbf{T})
from equation (
3.7
), we define
\mathbf{\Gamma}=\mathbf{0}
, and we define
Q(\mathcal{E})=0
for all Borel sets
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
whose closure does not contain the origin.

By equation (
3.4
), for each
1\leq i\leq s
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})^{2}}{\log\log n}=\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\sdif R_{i})>0.

(3.9)

If we define
K=\frac{1}{2}\min_{1\leq i\leq s}\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\sdif R_{i})
, then each
{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})^{2}}/\log\log n\geq K
once
n
is large enough, and therefore eventually

|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{1_{Q_{i}\sdif R_{i}}(p)}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{s}{K\log\log n}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log n}.

(3.10)

It follows that for any interval
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
whose closure does not contain the origin, no vector of the form
\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)
can lie in
\mathcal{I}
once
n
is large enough. Since
Q(\mathcal{I})=0
, we see that Proposition
3.8
(b)(i) is satisfied.

Furthermore, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\biggl|\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2}}{p(1+|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2})}\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2}}{p(1+|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2})}|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{3}}{p}\ll\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1/(\log\log n)^{3/2}}{p}\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log n)^{1/2}}

by equation (
3.10
). It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2}}{p(1+|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|^{2})}\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)=\mathbf{0}=-\mathbf{\Gamma},

which verifies Proposition
3.8
(b)(ii).

Finally, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, equation (
3.10
) implies that
|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|<\varepsilon
for all primes
p
once
n
is large enough.
Thus for any
\mathbf{T}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{n}(p))^{2}}{p}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{n}(p))^{2}}{p},

and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
|\mathbf{L}_{n}(p)|<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{n}(p))^{2}}{p}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{n}(p))^{2}}{p}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{1_{Q_{i}}(p)-1_{R_{i}}(p)}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})}t_{i}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{(1_{Q_{i}}(p)-1_{R_{i}}(p))(1_{Q_{j}}(p)-1_{R_{j}}(p))}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})B_{\omega}(n;Q_{j}-R_{j})}t_{i}t_{j}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{(\log\log n)^{1/2}}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{i}-R_{i})}\frac{(\log\log n)^{1/2}}{B_{\omega}(n;Q_{j}-R_{j})}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\frac{1}{\log\log n}\sum_{p\leq n}\bigg(\frac{(1_{Q_{i}}(p)-1_{R_{i}}(p))(1_{Q_{j}}(p)-1_{R_{j}}(p))}{p}\bigg)t_{i}t_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathfrak{d}(Q_{i}\sdif R_{i})^{-1/2}\mathfrak{d}(Q_{j}\sdif R_{j})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\bigl(\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}((T_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(S_{1}\cap T_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(T_{1}\cap S_{2})\bigr)t_{i}t_{j}

by equation (
3.9
) and Lemma
3.14
.
Note that the right-hand side is the limit of nonnegative quantities and is therefore itself nonnegative.
Since this expression is equal to the quadratic form
\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{s})
from equation (
3.7
), we conclude that Proposition
3.8
(b)(iii) holds as well.

We may now deduce that Proposition
3.8
(a) holds, that is, the function
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})
given in equation (
3.6
) is the cumulative distribution function of a random vector
\mathbf{X}
with characteristic function given in equation (
3.3
). However, since
\mathbf{\Gamma}=0
and
Q(\mathcal{E})=0
identically, equation (
3.3
) simplifies to
\log\chi_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
, which completes the proof of the proposition. (As each component of the random vector
X
has a normal distribution by Lemma
3.16
, there are no exceptional points, and so equation (
3.6
) holds for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
.)
∎

4.
Probability and
y
-typicality

In this section, we prove Proposition
1.31
, a quantitative bound that implies that
y
-typical numbers are indeed typical (that is, comprise a set of density
1
) for any particular
y
. Some technical work is necessary before we begin the proof itself.

4.1.
Background and technical lemmas

The main goal of this section is to establish Lemmas
4.5
and
4.8
, two technical results that are critical to the proof of Proposition
1.31
. We first deduce some estimates for central moments of additive functions from the work of Granville and Soundararajan
[
6
]
, which we then apply to various expectations involving the additive functions
\omega(m;q,1)
.
To avoid excessive nested parentheses, we use the convention that
\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))^{k}
is to be interpreted as
\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl((f(m))^{k}\bigr)
, the expectation of the
k
th power, as opposed to
\bigl(\mathbf{E}_{n}(f(m))\bigr)^{k}
, the
k
th power of the expectation.
When
a
and
b
are positive quantities, we note the general estimate

\mathbf{E}(a+b)^{k}\ll_{k}\mathbf{E}a^{k}+\mathbf{E}b^{k}

(4.1)

which follows from
(a+b)^{k}\leq(2\max\{a,b\})^{k}=2^{k}\max\{a^{k},b^{k}\}\ll_{k}a^{k}+b^{k}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f
be a real-valued strongly additive function with
|f(p)|\leq M
for all primes
p
,
and define

A_{f}(z)=\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{f(p)}{p}\quad\text{and}\quad B^{*}_{f}(z)^{2}=\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{f(p)^{2}}{p}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigg).

Let
S_{k}=2^{k/2}\Gamma(\frac{k+1}{2})/\sqrt{\pi}
.
Then uniformly for all even natural numbers
k\leq(B^{*}_{f}(z)/M)^{2/3}
,

\sum_{m\leq x}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr)^{k}=S_{k}xB^{*}_{f}(z)^{k}\biggl(1+O\bigg(\frac{k^{3}M^{2}}{B^{*}_{f}(z)^{2}}\bigg)\biggr)+O\biggl(M^{k}\biggl(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p}\biggr)^{k}\frac{\pi(z)^{k}}{k!}\biggr);

and uniformly for all odd natural numbers
k\leq(B^{*}_{g}(z)/M)^{2/3}
,

\sum_{m\leq x}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr)^{k}\ll S_{k}xB^{*}_{f}(z)^{k-1}k^{3/2}M+M^{k}\biggl(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p}\biggr)^{k}\frac{\pi(z)^{k}}{k!}.

Proof.

This lemma is a restatement, using notation consistent with the rest of this paper, of
[
6
, Proposition 4]
in the special case where
\mathcal{A}=[1,x]\cap\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathcal{P}
is the set of primes not exceeding
z
.
The standard sieve notation in that proposition simplifies greatly, as in this situation
h(d)=1
identically and
|r_{d}|<1
for all
d
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
be a real-valued strongly additive function with
|f(p)|\leq M
for all primes
p
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
z\leq n^{1/k}
is such that
B_{f}(z)^{2}\geq 2k^{3}M^{2}
, then

\sum_{m\leq n}\biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr|^{k}\ll_{M,k}nB^{*}_{f}(z)^{k},

and therefore

\mathbf{E}_{n}\biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr|^{k}\ll_{M,k}B_{f}(z)^{k}.

Proof.

We concentrate on the first claim, since the second claim follows directly from it and the inequality
B^{*}_{f}(z)\leq B_{f}(z)
.
Note that the hypotheses imply that
k\leq(B^{*}_{f}(z)/M)^{2/3}
since
B^{*}_{f}(z)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}B_{f}(z)^{2}
. When
k
is even, the lemma follows directly from Lemma
4.1
since
k^{3}M^{2}/B^{*}_{f}(z)^{2}\leq 1
and

\biggl(\pi(z)\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p}\biggr)^{k}\ll_{k}\biggl(\frac{z\log\log z}{\log z}\biggr)^{k}\ll_{k}n\ll_{M,k}nB^{*}_{f}(z)^{k}.

Then when
k
is odd, the lemma follows from the even case via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\biggl(\sum_{m\leq n}\biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr|^{k}\biggr)^{2}\leq\sum_{m\leq n}\biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr|^{k-1}\sum_{m\leq n}\biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p\leq z\end{subarray}}f(p)-A_{f}(z)\biggr|^{k+1}.\qed

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f
be a real-valued strongly additive function with
|f(p)|\leq M
for all primes
p
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
For all
n\geq 3
, if
B_{f}(n)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
then

\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m)-A_{f}(n)|^{k}\ll_{M,k}B_{f}(n)^{k}.

Proof.

Define
f(m;z)=\sum_{p\mid m,\,p\leq z}f(p)
.
By the estimate (
4.1
), we may write

\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m)-A_{f}(n)|^{k}\ll_{k}\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m)-f(m;n^{1/k})|^{k}\\
+\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m;n^{1/k})-A_{f}(n^{1/k})|^{k}+\mathbf{E}_{n}|A_{f}(n)-A_{f}(n^{1/k})|^{k}.

(4.2)

The first summand is bounded in terms of
M
and
k
because of the pointwise bound

f(m)-f(m;n^{1/k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
p>n^{1/k}\end{subarray}}f(p)\leq M(k-1),

while the third summand is also bounded since

\displaystyle A_{f}(n)-A_{f}(n^{1/k})=\sum_{n^{1/k}<p\leq n}\frac{f(p)}{p}

\displaystyle\ll M\sum_{n^{1/k}<p\leq n}\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle\ll_{M}\log\log n-\log\log n^{1/k}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{\log n^{1/k}}\biggr)\ll_{M}k.

(4.3)

Consequently, equation (
4.2
) becomes

\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m)-A_{f}(n)|^{k}\ll_{M,k}\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m;n^{1/k})-A_{f}(n^{1/k})|^{k}+1.

If
B_{f}(n^{1/k})^{2}\geq 2k^{3}M^{2}
, then Lemma
4.2
yields

\mathbf{E}_{n}|f(m)-A_{f}(n)|^{k}\ll_{M,k}B_{f}(n)^{2}+1\ll_{M,k}B_{f}(n)^{2}

as desired; in particular, this establishes the lemma when
B_{f}(n)^{2}
is sufficiently large in terms of
M
and
k
, since
B_{f}(n^{1/k})^{2}=B_{f}(n)^{2}+O(M^{2}k)
by a calculation analogous to equation (
4.3
).
But since the implicit constant in the lemma is allowed to depend on
M
and
k
, we can extend the range of validity down to
B_{f}(n)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

In all applications of Lemma
4.3
in this work, we will have
|f(p)|\leq 1
for all primes
p
; consequently, the dependence on
M
in Lemma
4.3
will be omitted entirely from this point onwards.

We now have the tools required to establish the first lemma required for the proof of Proposition
1.31
, which provides an upper bound on the likelihood of
\omega(m;q,1)
being far from its expected value.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
q
be a prime power and let
\delta
be a positive real number. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if
\log\log n\geq C\varphi(q)
, then for every integer
r\geq 1
,

P_{n}\bigl(|\omega(m;q,1)-\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n|>\delta\log\log n\bigr)\ll_{r}\frac{1}{\delta^{2r}(\varphi(q)\log\log n)^{r}}.

Proof.

First note that by Lemma
3.12
,

B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}=\frac{\log\log n+O(\log q)}{\varphi(q)}\asymp\frac{\log\log n}{\varphi(q)}

once
(\log\log n)/\log q
is sufficiently large, which can be guaranteed by the assumption
\log\log n\geq C\varphi(q)
.
By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle P_{n}\bigl(

\displaystyle|\omega(m;q,1)-\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n|>\delta\log\log n\bigr)

\displaystyle=P_{n}\bigl((\omega(m;q,1)-\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n)^{2r}>(\delta\log\log n)^{2r}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{n}(\omega(m;q,1)-\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n)^{2r}}{(\delta\log\log n)^{2r}}.

(4.4)

Since
\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n=A_{\omega}(n;q,1)+O(1)
by Lemma
3.12
, we see that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}_{n}(\omega(m;q,1)-\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\log n)^{2r}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)+O(1)\bigr)^{2r}}

\displaystyle\ll_{r}\mathbf{E}_{n}(\omega(m;q,1)-A_{\omega}(n;q,1))^{2r}+1

\displaystyle\ll_{r}B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2r}+1\ll_{r}\frac{(\log\log n)^{r}}{\varphi(q)^{r}}

by equation (
4.1
) and Lemma
4.3
and the assumption
\log\log n\geq C\varphi(q)
. Combining this esimate with the bound (
4.4
) completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We will need further results of a similar type, bounding probabilities that the
\omega(m;q,1)
are unexpectedly large. Part (c) of the next lemma will be useful in particular when the modulus
q
is substantial.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
0<\lambda\leq 1
and
a
be real numbers, let
q
be a prime power, and let
r\geq 1
and
n\geq 3
be integers.

(a)

If
\log q\ll\log\log n
and
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\lambda>1/\varphi(q)
, then

P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)\ll_{a,r}\frac{(\lambda-1/\varphi(q))^{-2r}}{(q\log\log n)^{r}}.

(b)

If
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
, then
P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)\ll_{a}(\log n)^{-\lambda\log 2}
.

(c)

If
\lambda\log\log n>1+a
, then

P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)\ll\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}+(\log q)^{2}}{q^{2}}.

Proof.

We first establish part (a). Since
\lambda>1/\varphi(q)
, we note that by Lemma
3.12
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\log\log n-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)-a

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}\log\log n-\biggl(\frac{\log\log n}{\varphi(q)}+O_{a}(1)\biggr)

\displaystyle\gg_{a}\Big(\lambda_{n}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\Big)\log\log n,

(4.5)

which is positive when
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
a
.
Hence by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)

\displaystyle{}+a>\lambda_{n}\log\log n\bigr)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)>\lambda_{n}\log\log n-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)-a)\bigr)

\displaystyle=P_{n}\bigl((\omega(m;q,1)-A_{\omega}(n;q,1))^{2r}>(\lambda_{n}\log\log n-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)-a))^{2r}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)\bigr)^{2r}}{(\lambda_{n}\log\log n-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)-a)^{2r}}\ll_{r}\frac{B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2r}}{(\lambda_{n}\log\log n-A_{\omega}(n;q,1)-a)^{2r}}

by Lemma
4.3
(valid since
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
). Moreover, since
\log q\ll\log\log n
, Lemma
3.12
implies that

\displaystyle B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}\ll\frac{\log\log n}{\varphi(q)}+\frac{\log q}{\varphi(q)}\ll\frac{\log\log n}{q},

since
\varphi(q)\geq\frac{q}{2}
when
q
is a prime power. Using this estimate and the lower bound (
4.5
), the upper bound (
4.5
) becomes

P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda_{n}\log\log n\bigr)\ll_{a,r}\frac{(\log\log n)^{r}/q^{r}}{(\lambda-1/\varphi(q))^{2r}(\log\log n)^{2r}},

which is the same as the bound stated in part (a). This proof required that
n
be sufficiently large in terms of
a
, but that assumption can be omitted since the implicit constant may depend on
a
.

For part (b), we first prove by induction on
\ell
that
\#\bigl\{m\leq n\colon\omega(m;q,1)=\ell\bigr\}\leq(\frac{1}{2})^{\ell-1}n
for all integers
n\geq 3
and
\ell\geq 1
. The base case
\ell=1
is trivial. When
\ell\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\#\bigl\{m\leq n\colon\omega(m;q,1)=\ell+1\bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\#\bigl\{m\leq\lfloor\tfrac{n}{p}\rfloor\colon\omega(m;q,1)=\ell\bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{\ell-1}}\biggl\lfloor\frac{n}{p}\biggr\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2^{\ell-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}=\frac{n}{2^{\ell-1}}B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}<\frac{n}{2^{\ell}}

since
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda_{n}\log\log n\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell>\lambda_{n}\log\log n-a}\#\bigl\{m\leq n\colon\omega(m;q,1)=\ell\bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell>\lambda_{n}\log\log n-a}\frac{n}{2^{\ell}}\ll 2^{-(\lambda_{n}\log\log n-a)}\ll_{a}\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\lambda_{n}\log 2}}

as claimed.

Finally, when
\lambda_{n}\log\log n>1+a
,

P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda_{n}\log\log n\bigr)\leq P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)>1\bigr)=P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)\geq 2\bigr)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
\omega(m;q,1)\geq 2\end{subarray}}1.

Then by Lemma
3.12
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
\omega(m;q,1)\geq 2\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<p_{2}\leq n\\
p_{1},p_{2}\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
p_{1}p_{2}\mid m\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},p_{2}\leq n\\
p_{1},p_{2}\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\frac{n}{p_{1}p_{2}}=\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq n\\
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\biggr)^{2}\ll\biggl(\frac{\log\log n}{\varphi(q)}+\frac{\log q}{\varphi(q)}\biggr)^{2},

which implies the estimate in part (c) since again
\varphi(q)\geq\frac{q}{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The estimates in part (b) are certainly not optimal, but the result suffices for our needs; the proof is effectively a simplified variant of a technique employed by Hardy and Ramanujan.
The proof for part (c) is a variant of a technique employed by Chang and the first author in
[
3
]
.

We can now establish the second lemma required for the proof of Proposition
1.31
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
0<\lambda\leq 1
and
a
and
r\geq 1
be real numbers, and let
n\geq 3
be an integer.
Define
L(\lambda)
to be the minimum positive value of
\lambda-1/\varphi(q)
as
q
ranges over prime powers.
If
\lambda\log\log n>1+a
and
L(\lambda)>(\log\log n)^{-1/2}
, then

P_{n}\bigl(\text{there exists a prime power $q$ with $1/\varphi(q)<\lambda$ and }\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)\\
\ll_{a,r}\frac{L(\lambda)^{-2r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}.

(4.7)

Proof.

For now we assume that
r\geq 2
is an integer.
By the union bound and Lemma
4.6
(iii), the probability on the left-hand side of equation (
4.7
) is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\\
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda\end{subarray}}P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr).

(4.8)

Letting
\nu
be a parameter, the contribution to this sum of the prime powers exceeding
\nu
is

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q>\nu\\
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda\end{subarray}}P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{q>\nu}\biggl(\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{q^{2}}+\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{\varphi(q)^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\nu}+\frac{(\log\nu)^{2}}{\nu}.

The contribution to the upper bound (
4.8
) of the remaining prime powers can be estimated by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq\nu\\
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda\\
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}\geq 1/2\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq\nu\\
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda\\
B_{\omega}(n;q,1)^{2}<1/2\end{subarray}}P_{n}\bigl(\omega(m;q,1)+a>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll_{a,r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq\nu\\
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{(\lambda-1/\varphi(q))^{-2r}}{(q\log\log n)^{r}}+\sum_{q\leq\nu}\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\lambda\log 2}}

\displaystyle\leq L(\lambda)^{-2r}\sum_{q}\frac{1}{(q\log\log n)^{r}}+\sum_{q\leq\nu}\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\lambda\log 2}}\ll\frac{L(\lambda)^{-2r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}+\frac{\nu}{(\log n)^{\lambda\log 2}}

by Lemma
4.6
(i)–(ii). If we set
\nu=(\log\log n)^{r+2}
, then these contributions total at most

\frac{L(\lambda)^{-2r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}+\frac{\nu}{(\log n)^{\lambda\log 2}}+\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\nu}+\frac{(\log\nu)^{2}}{\nu}\\
\ll\frac{L(\lambda)^{-2r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}+\frac{(\log\log n)^{r+2}}{(\log n)^{\lambda\log 2}}+\frac{1}{(\log\log n)^{r}}.

Since
L(\lambda)<\lambda\leq 1
, the first fraction on the right-hand side dominates the last fraction; moreover, since
\lambda>L(\lambda)>(\log\log n)^{-1/2}
, the first fraction also dominates the second fraction (with an implicit constant depending on
r
, which is acceptable).

We have therefore established the upper bound (
4.7
) for all integers
r\geq 2
; but the fact that the upper bound is a decreasing function of
r
then implies that the upper bound holds for all real numbers
r\geq 1
.
∎

With these technical results completed, the proof of Proposition
1.31
can begin.

4.2.
Proof that
y
-typical numbers are typical

Before starting, we observe that the condition that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{1})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{2})

is, by Lemma
2.3
, equivalent to

\omega(m;q_{1},1)+\bar{E}(m;q_{1})\leq\omega(m;q_{2},1)+\bar{E}(m;q_{2})
;
by the definition of
\bar{E}(m;q)
in that lemma, it follows that

\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{1})\leq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q_{2})\implies\omega(m;q_{1},1)\leq\omega(m;q_{2},1)+2.

(4.9)

We also introduce the new notation
\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
for the largest totient value less than
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
and

\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
for the smallest totient value greater than
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
. (So
\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})
if
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})\neq\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
while
\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-2})
if
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i-1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
, and analogously for
\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})
.) Define

\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{6}
and for
i\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\min\biggl\{\frac{1}{\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})},\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}-\frac{1}{\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}\biggr\}.

(4.10)

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
y\geq 1
be a real number, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
\log\log n\geq 6y^{2}
. Suppose that
m
is not
y
-typical. Then one of the following conditions must hold:

(a)

There exists
1\leq i\leq\mathfrak{W}(y)
such that
\displaystyle\biggl|\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i},1)-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}\log\log n\biggr|>\delta_{i}\log\log n
;

(b)

There exists a prime power
q
with
\varphi(q)>y
such that

\omega(m;q,1)+2>\biggl(\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}+\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}\biggr)\log\log n.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive, supposing that neither (a) nor (b) holds and proving that
m
is
y
-typical. Set
N=\mathfrak{W}(y)
. First note that if (a) does not hold, then for
2\leq i\leq N
,

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}\biggr)\log\log n\leq\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i},1)\leq\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}\biggr)\log\log n

(4.11)

If
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
then
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\leq\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
and
\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\leq\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
and thus

\displaystyle\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i},1)-\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{j},1)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}\biggr)-\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}\biggr)\biggr)\log\log n

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\biggl(\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})}+\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}\biggr)\log\log n

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}\log\log n>\frac{1}{3y^{2}}\log\log n\geq 2,

which implies that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{j})
by equation (
4.9
).
The special case
i=1
is similar: if (a) does not hold then
\omega(m;q_{2},1)\geq 1\log\log n-\frac{1}{6}\log\log n
and so

\displaystyle\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{1},1)-\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{j},1)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{5}{6}-\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi_{-}(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}\biggr)\biggr)\log\log n

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{5}{6}-\biggl(\frac{2}{3\cdot 2}+\frac{1}{3\cdot 1}\biggr)\biggr)\log\log n=\frac{1}{6}\log\log n\geq 2,

which again implies that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{1})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{j})
. Since
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{N}
are all the prime powers with totients not exceeding
y
, we have verified the first half of Definition
1.30
of
y
-typicality.

The first inequality in equation (
4.11
) implies that when (a) does not hold,

\displaystyle\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N},1)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}\biggr)\log\log n

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}+\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}\biggr)\log\log n>\biggl(\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}+\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}\biggr)\log\log n.

In particular, when (b) does not hold either, we see that
\omega(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N},1)>\omega(m;q,1)+2
for every prime power
q
whose totient value exceeds
y
, and thus
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
by equation (
4.9
). The exact same argument shows that if
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N-1})=\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})
then
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N-1})>\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
for all such prime powers.

On the other hand, the first half of the definition of
y
-typicality, which we have already established, implies that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N})
for all
1\leq i\leq N-2
, and also that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N-1})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{N})
if
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N-1})<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})
. In summary, we have shown that
\mathfrak{Q}(m;\mathfrak{q}_{i})\geq\mathfrak{Q}(m;q)
for all
1\leq i\leq N
and all
q
whose totient values exceed
y
, which verifies the second half of Definition
1.30
of
y
-typicality as well.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
1.31
, which asserts that for all real numbers
y\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
and for all integers
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

P_{n}(m\text{ is not $y$-typical})\ll_{r}\frac{y^{4r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}.

Proof of Proposition
1.31
.

We may assume that
\log\log n
is larger than
y^{4}
times some constant depending on
r
, for otherwise the statement is trivial. Set
N=\mathfrak{W}(y)
, define
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{6}
, and for
2\leq i\leq N
let
\delta_{i}
be defined as in equation (
4.10
). Further define

\lambda=\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}+\frac{2}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}

and

\mathcal{Q}_{j}=\bigl\{p\colon p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\mathfrak{q}_{j}$)}}\bigr\},\quad\text{so that }\mathfrak{d}(\mathcal{Q}_{j})=1/\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{j}).

(4.12)

Note that
1/\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})>\lambda>1/\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})
and that
\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})\leq y<\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})
, and so
\varphi(q)>y
is equivalent to
1/\varphi(q)<\lambda
for prime powers
q
. Therefore by Lemma
4.9
,

P_{n}(m\text{ is not $y$-typical})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{n}(|\omega(m;\mathcal{Q}_{i})-\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{-1}\log\log n|>\delta_{i}\log\log n)\\
+P_{n}\bigl(\text{there exists a prime power $q$ with $1/\varphi(q)<\lambda$ and }\omega(m;q,1)+2>\lambda\log\log n\bigr),

(4.13)

and it suffices to show that both terms on the right-hand side are
\ll_{r}{y^{4r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}
.

We may apply Lemma
4.5
to each term in the sum, since
\mathfrak{q}_{i}\leq y\ll\log\log n
. By the inequality
\delta_{i}\gg 1/\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle P_{n}(|\omega(m;\mathcal{Q}_{i})-\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{-1}\log\log n|>\delta_{i}\log\log n)

\displaystyle\ll_{r}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\delta_{i}^{2r}(\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})\log\log n)^{r}}

\displaystyle\ll_{r}\frac{1}{(\log\log n)^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{i})^{3r}\leq\frac{1}{(\log\log n)^{r}}\cdot 2\sum_{m\leq y}m^{3r}\ll_{r}\frac{y^{3r+1}}{(\log\log n)^{r}},

which is sufficient since
r\geq 1
.
On the other hand, we may apply Lemma
4.8
to the last term in equation (
4.13
), since
\lambda\log\log n\gg y^{-1}\log\log n>3
and

L(\lambda)=\lambda-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}=\frac{1}{3}\biggl(\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})}-\frac{1}{\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}\biggr)\geq\frac{1}{3\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N})\varphi(\mathfrak{q}_{N+1})}\gg y^{-2},

which implies that
L(\lambda)>(\log\log n)^{-1/2}

since we are assuming that
\log\log n
is at least a sufficiently large multiple of
y^{4}
.
Lemma
4.8
thus yields the bound

P_{n}\bigl(\text{there exists a prime power $q$ with $1/\varphi(q)<\lambda$ and }\omega(m;q,1)+2>\lambda\log\log n\bigr)\\
\ll_{r}\frac{L(\lambda)^{-2r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}}\ll_{r}\frac{y^{4r}}{(\log\log n)^{r}},

which completes the proof.
∎

5.1.
Lemmas for classifying the distributions

The key goal of this section is to find the distribution functions of maximum and minimum values of
correlated multivariate normal distributions. The first step is to provide notation for discussing
these maximums and minimums in terms of probability distributions:

Definition 5.1
.

For any
n
-dimensional probability distribution
F
, define two functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=F(x,\ldots,x),

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
\#S=k\end{subarray}}F(\vec{x}_{S}),

where for any
S\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
the vector
\vec{x}_{S}\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
has
i
th component equal to
x
if
i\in S

has
i
th component equal to
\infty
otherwise.

As these minimums and maximums are expressable as finite sums,
they behave nicely in terms of limits. In particular:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a random vector with multivariate distribution function
F
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=P(\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\leq x)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=P(\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\leq x)

are the distribution functions of the random variables
\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and
\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, respectively.
Furthermore, if there exists a sequence of multivariate probability distributions such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}=F
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{Mx}_{F_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x),

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{Mn}_{F_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x).

Proof.

Observe that as
F
is the multivariate distribution function of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,

\displaystyle P(\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\leq x)

\displaystyle=P(X_{1}\leq x,\ldots,X_{n}\leq x)

\displaystyle=F(x,\ldots,x)=\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x).

Similarly, as
F
is the multivariate distribution function of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and
by application of the inclusion-exclusion principle, it follows

\displaystyle P(\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\leq x)

\displaystyle=P((X_{1}\leq x)\cup\ldots\cup(X_{n}\leq x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
\#S=k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{k-1}P(X_{s}\leq x\colon s\in S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
\#S=k\end{subarray}}F(\vec{x}_{S})=\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x).

For the final statement, observe that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{Mx}_{F_{n}}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x,\ldots,x)=F(x,\ldots,x)=\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x).

Likewise,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{Mn}_{F_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
\#S=k\end{subarray}}F_{n}(\vec{x}_{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
\#S=k\end{subarray}}F(\vec{x}_{S})=\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x).\qed

We now characterize the bivariate distributions that will arise
in the proofs of the theorems in Section
1.3
.
The following proofs require descriptions of the characteristic functions and distribution functions of sums of independent normal variables and truncated normal variables. Most of these facts are routine within probability theory; two lemmas of this type are stated in Appendix
C
for clarity.

In the following proofs, we use the fact that sums of random variables whose joint distribution is a multivariate normal distribution
which correspond to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
are independent.
For example, if
X,Y
have a joint bivariate normal distribution, and
[1,1]
and
[1,-1]
are eigenvectors
of the covariance matrix, then the sums
X+Y
and
X-Y
are independent random variables.
This assertion can be inferred from
[
16
, pp. 27–28]
, which relates
the standard normal multivariate distribution to arbirary multivariate normal distributions. In doing so,
it demonstrates that these eigenvector-matching sums must be independent because they are the independent
random variables of the standard normal multivariate distribution after a change of basis.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
F(x,y)
is a normal bivariate distribution function with invertible covariance matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}1&a\\
a&1\end{bmatrix}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mx}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\Big(x;\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\Big)=\Phi(x)-2\mathbf{T}\biggl(x,\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1+a}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mn}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\Big(x;-\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\Big)=\Phi(x)+2\mathbf{T}\biggl(x,\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1+a}}\biggr)

with corresponding characteristic functions

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{Mx}_{F}}(t)=e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigg(1+\eta\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1-a}}{2}t\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{Mn}_{F}}(t)=e^{-t^{2}/2}\bigg(1-\eta\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1-a}}{2}t\Big)\bigg).

Proof.

Let
X,Y
be any random variables with a joint distribution of
F
, so that
F(x,y)=P(X\leq x,Y\leq y)
.
Since the matrix
M
is invertible, there exists a probability density function

f(\vec{x})=\frac{\exp-\frac{1}{2}\vec{x}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\vec{x}}{2\pi\sqrt{|M|}}

for this bivariate normal distribution
[
16
, Definition 3.2.1, p. 26]
.

Observe that
M
has eigenvectors
\vec{e}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1]
and
\vec{e}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,-1]
with respective eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}=(1+a)
,
\lambda_{2}=(1-a)
.
Thus
X+Y
and
X-Y
are independent random variables as discussed earlier.
Set
F_{k}(x)=P((X,Y)\cdot\vec{e}_{k}\leq x)
and
A_{k}(x)=\{\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\vec{y}\cdot\vec{e}_{k}\leq x\}
.
Then

\displaystyle F_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\iint_{A_{1}(x)}f(u\vec{e}_{2}+v\vec{e}_{1})\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u\vec{e}_{2}+v\vec{e}_{1})\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{u^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}+\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}))}{2\pi\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{1}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{2}}}\,du\,dv=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2\lambda_{1}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{1}}}\,dv=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\Big)

and similarly for
F_{2}(x)
.

Now, observe that
P(X+Y\leq x)=F_{1}(x/\sqrt{2})=\Phi(x/\sqrt{2(1+a)})
and

P(X-Y\leq x)=F_{2}(x/\sqrt{2})=\Phi(x/\sqrt{2(1-a)})
, and that

P(|X-Y|\leq x)=P(-x\leq X-Y\leq x)=\Phi_{+}\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2(1-a)}}\Big).

As
|X-Y|
and
X+Y
are independent random variables, we can find the probability density function
of
2\max\{X,Y\}=|X-Y|+X+Y
by application of Lemma
C.1
, obtaining

P(2\max\{X,Y\}\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{2};\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\Big).

Thus by a simple change of variables and Proposition
5.2
we obtain the desired result for the maximum.

For the minimum,
2\min\{X,Y\}=X+Y-|X-Y|
, which allows the application of Lemma
C.1

and gives the result that

P(2\min\{X,Y\}\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{2};-\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\Big),

which completes the proof after a final change of variables and Proposition
5.2
.
∎

,
with determinant
|M_{R}|=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}
. Note that
M_{R}
is invertible and diagonal by
definition.

Now, observe that
X-Y-Z+W=0
implies that
X-Y=Z-W
and therefore
|X-Y|-|Z-W|=0
.
Furthermore, since
X-Y
and
Z-W
have the same sign, it follows that
X-Y+Z-W

has the same sign, and thus
|X-Y|+|Z-W|=|X-Y+Z-W|
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\!\!\max\{X,Y,Z,W\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\max\{X,Y\},\max\{Z,W\}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{X+Y+|X-Y|,Z+W+|Z-W|\}/2

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(X+Y+Z+W+|X-Y|+|Z-W|+\big|X+Y-Z-W+|X-Y|-|Z-W|\big|\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(X+Y+Z+W+|X-Y+Z-W|+|X+Y-Z-W|\bigr).

Likewise,

\displaystyle\!\!\min\{X,Y,Z,W\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\min\{X,Y\},\min\{Z,W\}\}

\displaystyle=\min\{X+Y-|X-Y|,Z+W-|Z-W|\}/2

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(X+Y+Z+W-|X-Y|-|Z-W|-\big|X+Y-Z-W-|X-Y|+|Z-W|\big|\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(X+Y+Z+W-|X-Y+Z-W|-|X+Y-Z-W|\bigr).

As
\vec{e_{1}},\vec{e_{2}},\vec{e_{3}}
are eigenvectors of
M
and
X,Y,Z,W
is a multivariate
normal distribution,
it follows that
X+Y+Z+W
,
|X+Y-Z-W|
, and
|X-Y+Z-W|
are independent random variables, whose distributions we now determine.
For
k=1,2,3
, set
F_{k}(x)=P((X,Y,Z,W)\cdot\vec{e}_{k}\leq x)
and
A_{k}(x)=\{\vec{v}\in R\colon\vec{v}\cdot\vec{e_{k}}\leq x\}
.
Then for any permutation
(i,j,k)
of
(1,2,3)
,

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\iiint_{A_{k}(x)}f(\vec{u})\,dV

\displaystyle=\iiint_{A_{k}(x)}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\intercal}M_{R}^{-1}\vec{u})}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{3}|M_{R}|}}\,dV

\displaystyle=\iiint_{A_{k}(x)}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{u^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}+\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}+\frac{t^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}))}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{3}|M_{R}|}}\,du\,dv\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{u^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}+\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}+\frac{t^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}))}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{3}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}}\,du\,dv\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\lambda_{k}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{k}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2\lambda_{j}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{j}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2\lambda_{i}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{i}}}\,du\,dv\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\lambda_{k}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_{k}}}\,dt=\Phi\bigg(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\bigg).

Consequently,

\displaystyle P((X,Y,Z,W)\cdot\vec{e}_{k}\leq\frac{x}{2})

\displaystyle=F_{k}(\frac{x}{2})=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\Big)

\displaystyle P(|(X,Y,Z,W)\cdot\vec{e_{k}}|\leq\frac{x}{2})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{+}\Big(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\Big).

Thus by Lemma
C.2
,

4\max\{X,Y,Z,W\}=X+Y+Z+W+|X+Y-Z-W|+|X-Y+Z-W|.

Since
(X+Y+Z+W),|X+Y-Z-W|,|X-Y+Z-W|
are independent random variables with the distributions given above,

P(4\max\{X,Y,Z,W\}\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{4}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{4};\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}}}\Big)\\
-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{4};\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}}\Big)+\mathbf{S}(x;2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},2\sqrt{\lambda_{2}},2\sqrt{\lambda_{3}}).

The desired result is obtained by a simple change of variables and Proposition
5.2
.

Furthermore, by the same method, since

4\min\{X,Y,Z,W\}=X+Y+Z+W-|X+Y-Z-W|-|X-Y+Z-W|,

it follows that

P(4\min\{X,Y,Z,W\}\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{4}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{4};-\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}}}\Big)\\
-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{4};-\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}}\Big)+\mathbf{S}(x;2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},2\sqrt{\lambda_{2}},2\sqrt{\lambda_{3}}).\qed

Definition 6.1

Lemma 6.2

\bigl(U_{n}\omega(m)+V_{n}\bigr)^{s}\ll_{s}\bigl(U_{n}\omega(m)\bigr)^{s}+V_{n}^{s}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(f(m)^{s}

\displaystyle 1[m\text{ isn't $y$-typical}]\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll_{s}U_{n}^{s}\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m)^{s}1[m\text{ isn't $y$-typical}]\bigr)+V_{n}^{s}\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(1[m\text{ isn't $y$-typical}]\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll_{\rho,s,y}U_{n}^{s}\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m)^{s}1[m\text{ isn't $y$-typical}]\bigr)+\frac{V_{n}^{s}}{(\log\log n)^{\rho}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m)^{s}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\mathbf{E}_{n}\bigl(\omega(m)^{s}1[m\text{ isn't $y$-typical}]1[\omega(m)\geq 3s\log\log n]\bigr)

Totients and invariant factors

We define the

\varphi

\varphi

(a)

(b)

If

•

In other words, for every two consecutive prime-power totients, there is a unique two-totient
\varphi
-sequence that is the inverse image of those two totient values under the Euler
\varphi
-function.

We define the

.

\{5040\},\{55440\},\{720720\},\ldots\bigr).

Appendix C
Probability distributions

The two lemmas in this section are proved in the dissertation of second author
[
14
, Appendix B]
.

First, the sum of a random variable with a normal distribution and a truncated normal distribution
yields a skew normal distribution as described in Definition
1.17
.

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose that
X,Y
are independent random variables with
P(X\leq x)=\Phi(x/\sigma_{1})
and
P(Y\leq x)=\Phi_{+}(x/\sigma_{2})
, then

\displaystyle P(X+Y\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}},\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\Big),

\displaystyle P(X-Y\leq x)=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}},-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\Big),

with characteristic functions

\displaystyle\chi_{X+Y}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})t^{2}/2}\bigg(1+\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{2}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\chi_{X-Y}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})t^{2}/2}\bigg(1-\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{2}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\bigg).

We can similarly characterize sums of three independent random variables where one is has a normal distribution
and the other two have truncated normal distributions.

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
X,Y,Z
are independent random variables with distribution functions

\displaystyle P(X\leq x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\bigg(\frac{x}{\sigma_{1}}\bigg),

\displaystyle P(Y\leq x)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{+}\Big(\frac{x}{\sigma_{2}}\Big),

\displaystyle P(Z\leq x)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{+}\Big(\frac{x}{\sigma_{3}}\Big).

Let
\Sigma=\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{3}^{2}}
. Then
X+Y+Z
and
X-Y-Z
have distributions

\displaystyle P(X+Y+Z\leq x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma},\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{3}^{2}}}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma},\frac{\sigma_{3}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\Big)+\mathbf{S}(x;\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}),

\displaystyle P(X-Y-Z\leq x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma},-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{3}^{2}}}\Big)-2\mathbf{T}\Big(\frac{x}{\Sigma},-\frac{\sigma_{3}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\Big)+\mathbf{S}(x;\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}),

with characteristic functions

\displaystyle\chi_{X+Y+Z}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\Sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}}\biggl(1+\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{2}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\biggr)\biggl(1+\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{3}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\biggr),

\displaystyle\chi_{X-Y-Z}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\Sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}}\bigg(1-\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{2}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\biggr)\biggl(1-\eta\Big(\frac{\sigma_{3}t}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\biggr).
