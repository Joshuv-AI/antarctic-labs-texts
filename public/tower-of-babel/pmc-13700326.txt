“Emotionally, spiritually, physically depleting”: Muslim women’s experiences of pregnancy loss in the United States
Authors: Khadijat Adeleye, Oluwabunmi Ogungbe, Lucinda Canty, Kristen Leng, Favorite Iradukunda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy loss is a profound and traumatic experience. Cultural and religious factors often influence coping mechanisms following pregnancy loss. However, little is known about how Muslim women living in the United States navigate this challenging event within their unique cultural and spiritual context.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the lived experiences of Muslim women in the US who have experienced pregnancy loss.MethodsA qualitative interpretative phenomenological approach was employed. Ten Muslim women who had experienced pregnancy loss while living in the United States were interviewed. The interviews were audiotaped, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using principles of interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA).Results10 Muslim women who had experienced pregnancy loss, aged 29–49 years (mean age = 36.4; standard deviation ± 5.78). Four main themes emerged from the data: (1) Emotional and spiritual responses to pregnancy loss, (2) Relational support — spousal, family, and community responses to loss (3) Navigating healthcare insensitivity, and (4) Toward cultural and spiritually congruent care. Challenges in accessing culturally sensitive medical care were prevalent, with some women reporting feelings of isolation and misunderstanding in medical settings. The importance of family and community support was highlighted, as was the complex interplay between religious expectations and personal grief.ConclusionsThis study reveals the complex interplay between cultural, religious, and personal factors in shaping Muslim women’s experiences of pregnancy loss in the USA. Findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive healthcare practices and the importance of religious and community support in the healing process.ImplicationsThese findings can inform the development of culturally appropriate support services and guide healthcare providers in delivering more sensitive care to Muslim women experiencing pregnancy loss in the USA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02907-x.Keywords: Pregnancy loss, Muslim women, Cultural sensitivity, Religious beliefs, Healthcare experiences

Introduction

Pregnancy loss encompasses both miscarriages that occur before 20 weeks of gestation and losses that happen after this period, also known as stillbirths [1]. Globally, approximately 23 million miscarriages occur every year [2], affecting 10–20% of known pregnancies. About 1 in 100 pregnancies at 20 weeks or later is affected by stillbirth, and each year, approximately 24,000 babies are stillborn in the United States [3].

Muslims living in the United States represent an ethnically and racially diverse community, yet are bound by shared religious traditions that shape their health behaviors and healthcare experiences [4, 5]. Current estimates have the number of Muslim Americans at around 3.45 million [6], come from over 85 countries and belong to diverse ethnocultural and linguistic groups [7]. Despite their increasing presence, Muslim women often face unique challenges as immigrants, minorities, or socially disadvantaged individuals, which can significantly impact their experiences with healthcare, particularly in sensitive situations such as pregnancy loss.

For many Muslims, spirituality is recognised as a fundamental aspect of religion and a way of life [8], reflecting the profound importance placed on the connection between the individual and the divine. Islamic teachings view pregnancy as a blessing from Allah (God) and emphasize the sanctity of life from conception [9]. This emphasizes protection and reverence for life from the outset of conception through the various stages of pregnancy [10–12]. Specific Muslim communities have strong cultural expectations surrounding fertility and motherhood [13–15]. These expectations can lead to feelings of shame or failure when pregnancy loss occurs, as societal norms may view motherhood as a central aspect of a woman’s identity [16]. Understanding this lived experience is crucial for providing culturally sensitive care and support.

The impact of pregnancy loss is profound, with individuals experiencing a wide range of reactions, both immediate and long-term. These reactions, which are consistent across diverse cultures, can be categorized into psychological, behavioral, and physical domains [17–19]. Examples of common reactions include emotional reactions such as feelings of sadness, guilt, and anger [20–23], crying, withdrawal from social engagements [24], cognitive impact, which is often characterized by difficulties in concentrating on matters other than the loss, reduced self-esteem, and cognitive impairments [25–27] and physical effects, manifesting as disturbances in eating or sleeping patterns [28]. For Muslim women, the experience of pregnancy loss is further shaped by their religious beliefs, cultural traditions, and the societal norms surrounding pregnancy and loss [29, 30].

Many Muslim women who are immigrants or children of immigrants in the USA navigate a complex cultural landscape [31–33]. They often straddle two worlds: their Islamic heritage and the predominantly Western, secular American society [31, 32, 34]. This duality can create unique stressors, mainly when interacting with healthcare systems, where an understanding of Islamic practices and beliefs may be limited [35–38]. Additionally, Muslim women encounter barriers similar to those faced by other immigrant women [32, 39], such as language barriers, cultural misunderstandings, and healthcare practices that are often insensitive to their cultural practices [40, 41].

The intersection of factors such as minority religion, potential social disadvantage, and the integral role of Islam in daily life creates a unique landscape within which Muslim women in the USA experience pregnancy loss. Despite these unique challenges, there is a notable gap in research examining the experiences of pregnancy loss in this population. Much of the existing literature focuses on Muslim-majority countries or immigrant populations in Europe, potentially overlooking the unique context of Muslim women in the United States [42, 43]. Previous research has highlighted the importance of religious beliefs in shaping Muslim women’s responses to pregnancy loss [16, 44].

The objective of this study is to explore the lived experiences of Muslim women who have experienced pregnancy loss, with the aim of amplifying voices that are often underrepresented in maternal health disparity research. By capturing these perspectives, this research aims to inform more culturally and religiously sensitive practices, policies, and resources to support Muslim women navigating pregnancy loss.

Methodology

Research design

To explore how Muslim women living in the United States describe and make sense of their lived experiences following pregnancy loss. We employed interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) as an approach, process, and method [45, 46]. Researchers who engage in IPA acknowledge that experience is subjective and only accessible through interpretation [47]. IPA employs an ideographic approach, enabling researchers to hear participants’ voices and explore issues that are important to individuals.

Recruitment

Recruitment was done through a pregnancy loss support forum, Star Legacy, an independent platform [48], and Islamic organisations across the United States. For this study, miscarriage was defined as any pregnancy loss that occurred before 20th weeks of gestation, and a loss occurring after 24th weeks as still birth to accommodate early and late trimester pregnancy loss. Participants were eligible for the study if they were aged 18 years and older and had experienced one or more pregnancy losses (miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, and stillbirth) while living in the United States.

Participants

A purposive sampling of Muslim women with pregnancy loss experience was undertaken. A pre-determined and fixed sample of women was identified and contacted to be invited to take part in this research [46]. Potential participants were initially contacted via informational flyers distributed through relevant networks. Follow-up phone calls or emails were then conducted to confirm eligibility, provide further details about the study, and obtain informed consent. This process ensured that the participants met the inclusion criteria while allowing them to ask questions and make an informed decision about their participation. The identified homogenous group was ‘Muslim women,’ all of whom have experienced still birth, miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy, to provide insight into their pregnancy loss experience. We excluded Muslim women who had experienced pregnancy loss in another country, experienced neonatal loss in the 0–28 days after live birth, which is a part of infant mortality, were unable to provide informed consent, experienced severe emotional distress or mental health issues, or were unable to narrate their experiences.

Interview process

Relevant information was collected using in-depth, two serial one-to-one, semi-structured interviews with women who voiced subjective pregnancy loss experiences [49]. Given the topic’s sensitivity, the first author, a midwife, conducted all interviews as empathetic conversations. The interviews were audiotaped with participants’ consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim. The procedures for protecting confidentiality were carefully explained to all women.

Muslim women were interviewed individually and virtually in a quiet room to ensure privacy. The interview began with an open-ended general question, “Tell me a little about your reproductive history,” and continued with follow-up questions and probes. The complete interview schedule is provided as Supplementary Material. The interviews generally lasted 45–90 min between February to April 2024. Following the interview, each participant was thanked for their time, gift cards were presented as incentives, and they were referred to an emotional support group. Field notes were taken during and after each interview to document all important observations, including nonverbal behaviors.

Two to four weeks following the first interviews, a second interview was conducted to share the emerging themes with them and reflect on their meaning. The initial themes were shared with participants, and the interview lasted approximately 20–30 min. It allowed members to check and reflect on the emerging themes.

IPA analysis

Demographic data were extracted and documented in a Microsoft Excel file to describe participant characteristics. The primary author conducted all analyses in NVivo V.14 (QSR International, Doncaster, Australia)and followed IPA principles. IPA involves a case-by-case analysis of several accounts of how women make sense of their lived experiences with pregnancy loss, providing an in-depth understanding of the meaning of a shared experience.

Analysis was conducted by two authors (FI & KA).The first author (KA) is a practicing Muslim woman and a qualified midwife with clinical experience caring for women during and after pregnancy, including those who have experienced loss. The first author occupied an insider position regarding participants’ religious experiences. Interpretations were reviewed collaboratively with co-authors throughout the analytic process to surface alternative readings and to test whether emerging themes were grounded in participants’ accounts or shaped by the analyst’s own horizon of understanding. This allowed us to interrogate the data through rather different lenses, bringing complementary perspectives that enhanced both the depth and rigor of our interpretative work [46]. Throughout the analytical process, bracketing was employed to separate the researcher’s fore knowledge from the interpretive work. The researcher’s perspective is understood as a lens rather than a contaminant.

Trustworthiness of the data

Lincoln and Guba’s [50] Quality criteria were used to assess the study’s quality and scientific rigor. Credibility was achieved through data saturation in the interviews conducted and member checking. To enhance the data’s reliability, the researcher immersed themselves in the data by repeatedly reading the transcript and continually comparing key information. Further, the transferability of this study was enhanced by using direct quotations to represent the participants’ experiences and illustrate the theme.

Study findings

The demographic characteristics of the study participants reveal complex intersections that influence their experiences of pregnancy loss. The study participants were 10 Muslim women who had experienced pregnancy loss, aged 29–49 years (mean age = 36.4; standard deviation ± 5.78), bringing rich life experiences to our study with more complex emotional processing of pregnancy loss and intentional reproductive desire (Table 1). While all participants identified as Muslim, they represented diverse faith expressions and cultural backgrounds within Islam. This diversity encompasses variations in sectarian affiliation (Sunni and Shia), cultural heritage, levels of religious observance, and interpretations of Islamic teachings. Throughout our analysis, we recognize that Islam is not monolithic and that participants’ experiences of pregnancy loss were shaped by this intersection of their individual Muslim identity with their specific cultural contexts and personal relationships with faith. The themes that emerged reflect both commonalities across Muslim women’s experiences and the nuanced differences arising from this internal diversity within the Muslim community.

Participants’ sociodemographic distribution

The gestational age of pregnancy loss ranged from 12 to 24 weeks in the second trimester, when the pregnancy often becomes visible (Table 1); all were practicing Islam since birth and were currently married, suggesting that spousal and familial support may play an important role in the healing process. Several participants experienced multiple losses, which intensified their grief experiences and religious questioning. Most participants report frequent religious practices with strong religious integration that shapes their understanding and coping with loss. Their high educational attainment, ranging from a BSc to a PhD, and varied employment status, from not working to full-time employment, indicated a middle-to-upper socioeconomic status (Table 1). The participants’ locations across various U.S. states (East Coast, South, and Southwest) reveal important geographical considerations in their pregnancy loss experiences (Table 1).

Our interpretative phenomenological approach uncovered four themes, each capturing a distinct but interconnected dimension of participants’ experiences of pregnancy loss. Before presenting these themes, it is necessary to establish the relational and embodied landscape within which loss occurred [51]. For the women in this study, pregnancy was not a passive biological state but an active, intentional process through which maternal identity was being constructed day by day. This matters analytically because it means that what loss severed was not merely a pregnancy but an already-forming self. Each dominant theme was accompanied by subthemes and exemplificatory quotations (Fig. 1).

Participants’ lived experience following pregnancy loss

Theme 1. Emotional and spiritual responses to pregnancy loss

For the women in this study, pregnancy loss was not just a medical event. Instead, it was experienced as a rupture across multiple aspects of the self, emotional, embodied, and spiritual, in ways that cannot be separated from the participants’ identities as Muslim women. The depth and complexity of their reactions were influenced not only by grief but also by the theological and communal frameworks through which they understood their pregnancies. Many described feeling a deep bond with their unborn children through specific Islamic practices, such as reciting the Quran with intention, offering prayers of gratitude (Alhamdulillah), and mentally envisioning their children’s futures within a divinely ordered universe. When those pregnancies ended, the loss was therefore not just of a potential child but of a sacred relationship and spiritual project.

The initial discovery of pregnancy loss was overwhelmingly described in terms of shock and disorientation. For some participants, loss was confirmed suddenly at a routine appointment; for others, it unfolded over hours in emergency settings. Whatever its form, the experience consistently destabilized participants’ sense of reality. The initial shock of loss was described as overwhelming, with one participant stating, “Yeah, so devastated. I was feeling so devastated” (P4). Another participant shared, “I got mentally devastated, and I started just to take some medication right after my second loss” (P3). The repetition of the word “devastated” across accounts is notable; it captures an experience of inner annihilation that exceeds ordinary sadness. It is not merely that these women felt grief; they experienced a collapse of the self that the clinical category of “pregnancy loss” does not adequately name.

The sense of confusion was a significant emotional response to grief, especially in the immediate aftermath of their loss. A participant shared, “I didn’t know what I was experiencing… it was really hard processing it, especially when the doctor questioned whether I was even pregnant” (P5). The experience of not knowing what one is experiencing describes a particular kind of phenomenological disorientation. When the medical authority then questions whether the pregnancy was real, the participant is denied even the most fundamental reason for her grief. Another participant recalled, “Everything happened so quickly and unexpectedly. I was scared and confused, but I tried to stay calm” (P8). Her attempt to “stay calm” amid shock and confusion points to an Islamic orientation toward sabr, disciplined patience in the face of hardship, though for this participant, the effort of composure appears as much a survival strategy as a spiritual one.

Participant 4’s account introduces a further layer: “Every time I would bleed… there was anxiety, of course, grief too.” Grief recurring with each bleed, as if the body keeps rehearsing what the mind cannot fully process, suggests a form of traumatic repetition in which the physical and psychological become inextricably entangled. Participant 2 offered one of the richest accounts of the experience, describing her loss as “emotionally, spiritually, physically, and completely depleted on all levels.” (P2). The use of “depletion” suggests being drained or emptied captures how pregnancy loss consumes women across all life domains. The enumeration without hierarchy (emotional, spiritual, physical) conveys that these were simultaneous, interconnected forms of suffering rather than discrete categories, and then arrives at totality: “all levels.” Additionally, the inclusion of “spiritually” as a distinct category of suffering—separate from yet intertwined with emotional and physical pain—reveals that for Muslim women, pregnancy loss constitutes a religious crisis, not merely a life crisis.

Theme 2: Relational support — spousal, family, and community responses to loss

Participants consistently identified relational support as vital to their ability to grieve and recover. However, their accounts revealed a complex and sometimes conflicting landscape where the same relationships that offered comfort also required careful management. The forms that support took, and those it failed to take, were often influenced by Islamic cultural norms related to gender, communal obligation, and the expression of grief.

Subtheme 2.1: Spousal and family dynamics in shared grief

For most participants, the marital relationship formed what might be described as the innermost circle of grief, the space where loss could be most authentically and fully expressed. Participant 2 described her husband as “first and foremost, my primary support system,” which reflects not only the emotional centrality of the marital bond but also the gendered dimensions of grief disclosure in many Islamic cultural contexts. Another participant described how the experience of loss drew the couple into something resembling partnership: “I think there have also been some good things that have happened; it brought my husband and me so much closer together, like being on the same team and going through that process”(P3). The idiom “on the same team” reframes loss as shared navigation, a collaborative movement through adversity.

Several participants described their husbands as providing the form of presence that grief requires not problem-solving or theological consolation but simply being there. One participant described how her husband “would sit with me and talk it through, giving me comfort and helping me stay strong through it all” (P8); another recalled, “We would just cry… but having his support, just sitting with me, allowing me to cry it out, has been extremely helpful” (P7). The phrase “allowing me to cry it out” positions the husband’s role as one of permission and witness; he creates the conditions in which her grief can be expressed without judgment or containment.

This divergence was real for others. One participant reflected that she had failed to recognize her husband’s grief as grief. Participant 2 reflected with evident remorse: “He felt as though I didn’t recognize that that was his loss as well. He was very upset; he felt quite sad as well, but I didn’t recognize that in him.” The repeated phrase “didn’t recognize” is suggestive: it is not that she dismissed his grief, but that she could not see it. This reciprocal invisibility reveals how pregnancy loss can create not only shared sorrow but also mutual isolation within the same household. This account opens onto broader questions about the masculinity norms operating within this community and how Muslim men’s grief following pregnancy loss may remain structurally unsupported.

Subtheme 2.2: Islamic communal networks as support

Beyond the marital relationship, participants drew on various sources, including extended community and mosque networks, which provided a distinctly different kind of support, structured around practical care and ritual. The organized provision of food by community members was a recurring motif, described with a specificity that suggests its significance was not merely practical: “They all came to my house, and they just sent me a lot of food ” (P1). “The members of our community didn’t leave us alone. They were constantly checking in, and for three weeks afterwards, I didn’t have to worry about food’ (P8). This organized support was an important element of recovery, as reflected in another account: “People would bring cooked food, send food through Uber, and… sit with us. That helped tremendously” (P6). The image of uninterrupted community presence over three weeks, “they didn’t leave us alone,’’ casts the community as a protective surround, a buffer against the isolating experience of grief. Food itself is not merely sustenance in these accounts; it is the material form taken by communal acknowledgement, a tangible sign that loss has been witnessed and that the bereaved need not navigate daily life alone.

Furthermore, support from friends and other women who had experienced similar losses also provided comfort. One participant mentioned how friends shared their experiences with her, saying, “It was comforting to see and feel the support from my friends and family. It was nice to know that others had experienced something similar” (P4). However, one participant shared, “Only women were aware of my pregnancy loss. I didn’t feel comfortable disclosing it to men.” (P3). The gendered boundary around disclosure reflects a culturally specific modesty norm operating even within grief, which was not hidden. Still, Islamic and cultural protocols around gender, bodily privacy, and appropriate intimacy structured its circulation.

Religious and spiritual support were also crucial. One participant shared that the masjid (mosque) provided a space for peace and reflection: “Being in the masjid space always brings a sense of peace to emotions… It allows you to think and cry without being judged.” (P2) It implies that other spaces — clinical, social, perhaps even familial had been experienced as sites of potential judgment, where grief required justification or moderation. Another participant described how her ustadh (religious teacher) facilitated the funeral prayers, or janazah(funeral prayer), for her lost child: “He helped facilitate the Janazah (funeral) right after Jummah (Friday prayers) … That helped to alleviate some of our fear.” (P7) The faith leader’s role in facilitating the Janazah (funeral) reveals a powerful intersection of religious leadership, community ritual, and emotional processing.

However, community support was not uniformly available to all participants. For those who had immigrated to the United States without extended family networks nearby, geographical isolation compounded the experience of loss in acute ways: As one participant explained: “I was here in the US alone, just my husband and no family around… It was very tough and isolating.” (P8). The loneliness of miscarriage was compounded by the silence that often surrounded it in social life. The presence of a Muslim community varies considerably across the United States, and participants’ access to culturally and religiously congruent support reflected both their geographic location and their degree of community embeddedness. It is also worth noting that participants came from diverse cultural backgrounds, and while Islamic faith provided a broadly shared framework, the specific cultural expressions of mourning, community support, and gender norms differed meaningfully across these contexts.

Theme 3: Navigating healthcare insensitivity

All participants’ narratives reveal diverse experiences with healthcare professionals during and after pregnancy loss, highlighting both supportive and negative encounters. Across accounts, healthcare settings emerged not only as places where clinical care was delivered but as arenas in which participants’ grief, dignity, and religious identity were or were not recognized and respected. Negative experiences were both common and consequential, amplifying rather than alleviating the suffering of loss.

Subtheme 3.1: Negative healthcare experiences: insensitivity and emotional distress

Many participants reported negative interactions with healthcare professionals, marked by insensitivity and a lack of understanding of the emotional significance of pregnancy loss. A recurring theme was the feeling of being disregarded or treated as “just another case” rather than being met with empathy and care. A feeling of depersonalization in clinical encounters that left grief unacknowledged at precisely the moment it was most acute. Participant 2 reflected that for healthcare staff, the loss “was just like any other procedure or just. I don’t think they perceived it as a loss or human loss.” The use of the word “human loss” carries weight: it implies that the clinical procedure had stripped the event of its humanity, rendering it a biological transaction. For Muslim women for whom miscarriage carried both divine and communal significance, this reduction was doubly erasing.

Participant 8 described waiting five to six hours in an emergency room to be told clinically that her baby had not survived, emphasizing the disproportion between the gravity of what she was experiencing and the urgency with which the system responded: “And I had to go to the ER and wait for about 5 to 6 hrs., just for somebody to tell me that my child is not alive.” (P8) The temporal dimension here “five to six hours’’, time, in the context of loss, is charged with meaning: the waiting prolongs the liminal state between hope and devastation, forcing the participant to sit with uncertainty about her child’s fate.

Some participants experienced active challenges to the validity of their experiences. Participant 5 recounted that the emergency room doctors “questioned whether I was even pregnant beforehand, as if we were making all the 12 weeks up.” The phrase “as if we were making all the 12 weeks up” reveals the depth of the affront: not merely a clinical misunderstanding but a perceived accusation of fabrication. Twelve weeks of pregnancy, twelve weeks of embodied experience of bonding, of spiritual investment, were implicitly dismissed as potentially invented. The use of “we” rather than “I” is also notable: the participant implicitly includes her partner, thereby invalidating the wound as a shared one and challenging the couple’s joint reality.

Subtheme 3.2: Communication failures and bodily autonomy

A distinct dimension of negative healthcare experience concerned failures of communication about the physical dimensions of recovery, with lasting emotional consequences. Participant 3 described a particularly painful instance: “One thing that no one told me, which is kind of crazy because it’s a hospital, but no one told me that I would start lactating, so I had to deal with that, which was painful when there’s no baby to feed, right? It’s physically painful. It’s also emotionally painful.” The phrase “kind of crazy because it’s a hospital” marks this as a breach that should not be possible within the institution specifically designated for bodily care. Her lactation becomes involuntary in the absence of an infant, making the loss materially inescapable grief could not be kept at the level of thought or feeling but was written into physical sensation. The body, in other words, continued to live out a pregnancy that had ended, and did so without preparation or support.

Participants also reported encounters in which their bodily autonomy was directly violated. Participant 4 described a nurse who demanded, with an aggression that the participant clearly experienced as transgressive: “Show me your blood… pull down your pants. I want to see your blood.” The threefold repetition of the demand, combined with the command to disrobe, is experienced by the participant as extraordinary: “I told her, okay, I mean, this is very weird.” For Muslim women, for whom modesty (Haya) carries deep religious significance and for whom bodily exposure is governed by precise ethical protocols, such an encounter constitutes both a clinical and a religious violation.

Subtheme 3.3: The absence of spiritual and culturally congruent care

Several participants noted that while their physical medical needs were eventually addressed, their emotional and spiritual needs were either overlooked or handled through generic, culturally misaligned responses. She stated, “Yeah, I think everyone was very good; they’re medical professionals… They’re not mental health professionals, but as much as they could be, they were very compassionate and understanding. They had a printed-out piece of paper for the referral, and they just handed me lists of therapists and support groups. Most of them were too far away from where I lived. This was pre-pandemic.”(P3). Being handed “a printed-out piece of paper” listing therapists and support groups, most of which were too far to access. In a moment of acute bereavement, the participant received a document, impersonal, mass-produced, pre-existing, rather than an individualized human response.

More specifically, participant 7’s experience illustrates how well-intentioned provision can enact cultural erasure. She described receiving a small basket from the hospital that contained, among other items, a pendant featuring a small cross. She noted with measured restraint: “Weirdly enough, though, as a Muslim, they gave me a Christian book inside of this box… It had a small cross pendant. Of course, I didn’t make a fuss about it. I didn’t see anything. I just felt that clearly.” The phrase “I didn’t make a fuss about it” reveals the labour of restraint required from Muslim patients navigating a healthcare system that implicitly assumes Christian normativity. The participant suppressed her response, “I didn’t see anything,” an act of social self-effacement that nonetheless left a lasting impression: “I just felt that clearly.” The word “clearly” is quietly devastating despite her silence; the participant experienced the institution’s religious presumption with perfect clarity. She goes on to articulate the specific need left unmet: “I needed a Muslim OB-GYN, someone who could advise me from an Islamic point of view without having to outsource.” The word “outsource” is precise—it names the gap in which Islamic spiritual guidance, rather than being integrated into care, must be sought separately. This was not an experience unique to one location: participants across multiple states, including those with substantial Muslim populations, described the absence of culturally congruent spiritual care as a consistent and unmet need.

Participant 6’s account similarly captures the inadequacy of spiritually inattentive care: “They didn’t do anything about spiritual care. They told me there was a psychologist if I wanted to talk with them, but I didn’t.” The refusal is not petulance; it signals a recognition that a psychologist, operating within a secular therapeutic framework, would not address the specifically theological dimensions of her grief. The availability of psychological support, while valuable, does not substitute for religiously congruent spiritual care, a distinction that, in these accounts, healthcare providers appear to appreciate only rarely.

Theme 4.4: Toward culturally and spiritually congruent care

Despite the negative healthcare experiences, participants’ stories included instances of care that truly acknowledged and met them where they were — recognizing the emotional, relational, and religious aspects of their loss and responding appropriately. These moments of compassionate acknowledgment were felt as transformative precisely because they contrasted with the dominant institutional culture of clinical detachment.

Empathic care from staff was experienced as fundamentally restorative. One participant described the environment at a second clinic where she experienced her second miscarriage: “Everybody and the doctor were so sweet, caring, and nice. The day after that, I had a D&C. My husband took the embryo to the lab; they were so sweet; they tried to be empathetic and supportive” (P1). The repetition of “sweet” suggests something warmer than professionalism, a quality of care that exceeded clinical competence and reached toward the person experiencing the loss. Participant 3 described her overall experience as “very positive, both with the staff and the institution,” noting that “the nurses were very comforting and understanding.” After the complexity and pain, such simplicity of expression signals a care that did not demand translation or justification.

Religious accommodation emerged as a particularly meaningful form of respect in positive encounters. For instance, a participant described how the healthcare staff went out of their way to accommodate her religious preferences, which made her feel respected and cared for: “It was known that I had a loss and that the baby wouldn’t make it, and so, you know, we requested that all the nurses be female. That way, I could remove my hijab(veil) and be a bit more comfortable.” (P7). The ability to “remove my hijab” within a female-only environment appears to have preserved the participant’s religious identity and dignity during medical care and helped to alleviate some of the emotional distress associated with the loss.

Participant 2 described a particularly resonant encounter with a male physician who disclosed that he and his wife had experienced pregnancy loss: “The doctor who was there when I had the second loss was a male who was kind. He expressed that he and his wife had gone through pregnancy loss as well. Moreover, he was very supportive in providing the information.” The physician’s self-disclosure functions here as an act of empathetic interaction that created an environment of care, enabling participants to feel more emotionally secure, even in the face of profound loss. For this participant, who had previously experienced a profound absence of recognition for her grief, this acknowledgement of shared vulnerability was not a distraction from professional care but its highest expression.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that the defining features of positive healthcare experiences were not specialist knowledge or expanded resources, though both matter, but rather the willingness of providers to recognise pregnancy loss as a genuinely human event: one that has emotional, relational, spiritual, and cultural dimensions that cannot be adequately addressed within a purely biomedical framework. For Muslim women specifically, this requires at minimum an awareness of the Islamic practices and beliefs that shape how pregnancy is experienced, how loss is mourned, and what forms of support and accommodation carry religious significance. The aspiration articulated by Participant 7, a Muslim OB-GYN capable of advising “from an Islamic point of view,” reflects not an unreasonable sectarian demand but a call for the kind of integrated, identity-affirming care that all patients deserve and that Muslim women, in these accounts, too rarely receive.

Discussion of findings

The findings of this phenomenological study indicate that demographic factors (parity, gestational age of pregnancy) and experience of pregnancy loss have a considerable impact on Muslim women’s reproductive identity. Participants in this study characterized pregnancy as a profound life and spiritual transition that reshapes women’s identity and their roles within the family and community. For Muslim women, pregnancy is a spiritual journey, as pregnancy is seen as an act of worship (Ibadah) [52].

The emotional responses described by Muslim women in this study largely align with those reported in the broader literature on pregnancy loss from various cultural backgrounds [20–22, 53]. Most women without living children have significantly higher levels of grief than women who already have children [54]. However, the depth of devastation and isolation reported by Muslim women might be intensified by the cultural and religious significance placed on motherhood in Islamic societies [55]. Hence, maternal healthcare providers must adopt a more holistic approach that recognizes and respects the spiritual, emotional, and cultural dimensions of pregnancy for Muslim women.

Participants’ uniformly married status suggests the presence of spousal support, though this entails specific cultural and religious expectations around mourning and recovery. Spousal support and faith have positive influences on coping with the psychological impact of pregnancy loss among Muslim women [56, 57]. Finding solace and support from spouses is consistent with previous studies, which indicate that spousal support is vital for women coping with pregnancy loss [58]. Hence, this suggests the need for family-centered approaches in supporting women through pregnancy loss, with particular attention to cultural and religious factors.

Some participants experienced culturally insensitive comments or actions from healthcare providers. This aligns with studies on racial disparities in healthcare, where women of color often report feeling disrespected or misunderstood [59–61]. This oversight highlights a systemic issue in healthcare systems that may not be adequately prepared to support patients from diverse racial and religious backgrounds. Hospitals and clinics should develop clear protocols for accommodating patients’ diverse needs, including those related to religion, race, and culture.

We found the importance of religious accommodation in healthcare settings for Muslim women related to modesty, gender segregation in healthcare settings, and Islamic perspectives on grief and loss. This highlights the intersection of religious practices and healthcare needs, demonstrating how culturally sensitive care can positively impact emotional well-being [62, 63]. This aligns with research suggesting culturally competent care is crucial for minority populations [41, 64, 65]. The emphasis on modesty and shared religious understanding in choosing a therapist highlights the need for culturally sensitive mental health services for Muslim women in the USA [34, 66].

The robust community support described by participants, including food preparation and regular check-ins, reflects the Islamic concept of ummah (community) [67, 68]. This finding is consistent with studies on other tight-knit religious communities [69]. For example, [70] found that Muslim immigrant women in the US often relied on their religious community for support during infertility treatments. The organized nature of this support in the current study suggests a well-established system within Muslim communities for caring for those in need, reflecting the hadith: “The believers in their mutual kindness, compassion, and sympathy are just like one body. When one of the limbs suffers, the whole body responds to it with wakefulness and fever” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6011) [71]. Hence, community-based interventions [72, 73] could be developed to harness this existing support structure, potentially improving outcomes for Muslim women experiencing pregnancy loss.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s primary strength lies in its focus on pregnancy loss, a topic often overlooked in research and social discourse. This, in turn, addresses a critical gap in our understanding. The study’s emphasis on an underrepresented population highlights unique perspectives and experiences often overlooked in mainstream research, thereby contributing to a more inclusive body of knowledge. Methodologically, triangulation through two separate interviews with each participant adds depth and reliability to the data collected, allowing for a more nuanced exploration of participants’ experiences over time.

A key limitation of this study is the participants’ diverse geographic distribution across states, which introduces variability in available resources and healthcare systems. This diversity makes it challenging to draw conclusions about experiences specific to states or regions. Additionally, the study population is racially and culturally diverse, which is valuable for broadening perspectives but complicates the identification of patterns or experiences that might be unique to specific racial or cultural groups. However, the unifying factor of a shared faith belief among participants provides a common thread, potentially influencing experiences and coping mechanisms related to pregnancy loss. This shared-faith aspect provides a unique lens for interpreting the findings, but also limits the generalizability of the results to populations with different belief systems. These factors collectively underscore the need for more targeted future research on a specific group.

Clinical implication

Our findings may offer vital suggestions for providing compassionate, sensitive care to Muslim families experiencing pregnancy loss. Mental health support should incorporate Islamic perspectives, recognizing how faith influences the grieving process and provides comfort during loss. Healthcare providers must create an environment of gentle understanding where families feel safe expressing their emotional and spiritual needs. This includes respecting modesty requirements, gender preferences for care providers, and religious practices with genuine empathy.

Family-centered care should be delivered with deep sensitivity, acknowledging that pregnancy loss affects not only the mother but also the entire family unit and community. Healthcare providers should approach each family with cultural humility, recognizing that their presence during this vulnerable time requires professional expertise and compassionate awareness. The critical role of extended family and the Muslim community in the healing process should be supported with sensitivity, allowing families to draw strength from their faith traditions and support systems in authentic and comforting ways.

Healthcare provider training must go beyond basic cultural competency to foster a genuine understanding of how to provide gentle, attentive care that honors both the universal human experience of loss and the religious and cultural needs of Muslim families. By creating an environment of tender support and cultural sensitivity, healthcare providers can help families navigate their grief journey while feeling understood, respected, and cared for with genuine psychological outcomes.

Conclusion

For some Muslim women, pregnancy is not only a physical experience but also a spiritual act of worship, where motherhood is seen as a divine responsibility. Pregnancy loss, therefore, represents both the loss of a child and a disruption of a spiritual journey, leading to profound emotional and spiritual impacts. The community support suggests that healthcare providers should integrate these elements into their care practices to better address the emotional and spiritual dimensions of pregnancy and loss. This study calls for a more holistic, culturally competent framework to support Muslim women through the challenges of pregnancy and miscarriage.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Belmont Report, which emphasizes respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. The Institutional Review Board granted ethical approval for the study at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. All participants signed a consent form indicating their willingness to participate. At the beginning of each interview, the study's aim was explained, and each woman was assured that her information would be kept confidential. Participants were aware of their right to withdraw without providing a reason. To ensure privacy, all recorded materials were stored in a locked cabinet in the researcher’s office at school, and all electronic data were saved on a password-protected computer, accessible only to the researcher. To ensure anonymity, pseudonyms were used in place of the participants’ names, and any information provided by participants was password-protected and encrypted. Participant identifiers were kept separate from the audio recordings, and transcripts will not be disclosed in any publication or presentation of findings. Transcripts will be kept on file for 5 years, per the UMASS Amherst IRB policy, and then shredded and disposed of to protect confidentiality. The discussion of the experience of pregnancy loss was anticipated to be distressful for some women. Participants were invited to stop and resume the interview later if they felt stressed or uncomfortable. Information about pregnancy loss support groups available in US communities was also provided to participants in case they required additional support.
