Optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system connects enteric neurons dynamics and gut homeostasis
Authors: Gitali Naim, Hadar Romano-Zadaka, Sivan Amidror, David Jessula Levy, Adva Cohen, Carmel Sochen, Hadar Gilberg, Nairouz Farah, Vladislav Rudenko, Yasmin Yarden, Mengyang Feng, Rotem Tsentsarevsky, Ziv Brodie, Yasmin Reich, Ariel Simon, Einat Toister, Irit Shoval, Leah Armon, Maya Schiller, Yossi Mandel, Moshe Biton, Nissan Yissachar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The enteric nervous system (ENS) senses microbiota-derived signals and orchestrates mucosal immunity and epithelial barrier functions. However, mechanistic dissections of intestinal neuro-immune-microbiota communications remain challenging. Here, we present an optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system that enables real-time, whole-tissue stimulation of defined ENS lineages, and detailed analysis of their functional impact. We demonstrate that optogenetic activation of enteric cholinergic neurons rapidly modulates intestinal physiology. Interestingly, distinct neuronal firing patterns differentially modulate neuro-immunological gene expression and epithelial barrier integrity. Furthermore, diverse enteric neuronal lineages exert distinct regulatory roles. While cholinergic activation enhances gene-sets associated with type-2 immunity, tachykininergic neurons modulate distinct mucosal defense programs. Intriguingly, luminal introduction of the immunomodulatory bacterium Thomasclavelia ramosa remodeled cholinergic-induced neuro-immunological transcription. These findings suggest that microbial and neuronal signals are locally integrated to fine-tune gut immunity and barrier defense. Collectively, we provide a powerful platform for systematic discovery and mechanistic exploration of functional neuroimmune connections, and their potential modulation by microbes, drugs or metabolites.

Introduction

Homeostatic functions of organs and tissues critically rely on multidirectional and dynamic interactions between diverse cellular systems. This is especially evident in the gut, where intricate communications between neuronal, immunological, and epithelial compartments - along with the gut microbiota - are integrated to maintain intestinal function1,2. Interestingly, neurons of the enteric nervous system (ENS) emerge as a cellular hub that senses microbiota-derived signals and orchestrates epithelial barrier functions and immunological outcomes. The ENS is a dense and complex neural network embedded within the gut wall, organized into two main ganglionated plexuses: the myenteric plexus, which regulates smooth muscle contraction and intestinal motility, and the submucosal plexus, which governs epithelial secretion, blood flow, and absorption3,4. Enteric neurons comprise diverse subtypes with distinct molecular identities, as revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing5–8, that perform specialized physiological functions.

Recent studies underscore the essential role of the ENS in mediating neuro-immune interactions within the gut. For example, cholinergic enteric neurons control ILC2 activity and type 2 immunity by neuromedin U (NMU) neuropeptide secretion9–12. ENS-derived interleukin-6 (IL-6) control regulatory T cell (Treg) development and immunological tolerance in the colon13,14. Reciprocal signaling between enteric neurons and tissue-resident macrophages is critical for maintaining gut motility and orchestrating protective responses to luminal disturbances15,16. Moreover, the ENS convey stress signals to regulate gut inflammation17, and ENS-derived IL-18 supports epithelial antimicrobial defense and maintains barrier integrity18,19. Further insights have recently emerged from chemogenetic approaches that target specific enteric neuron populations: nitrergic neurons influence Th17-like cells, cholinergic neurons regulate neutrophil activity, and Trpv1⁺ nociceptors exert broad effects on the gut Treg niche20. Remarkably, ENS activity can directly impact microbiome composition itself21, underscoring a bidirectional relationship between host neuronal activity and microbial ecology. Together, these findings establish the ENS as a key integrator of intestinal and systemic immune homeostasis.

Despite significant advances, systematic investigation of neuro-immune and neuro-epithelial interactions remains hindered by methodological constraints. In vitro systems fail to replicate the complex spatial organization and cellular heterogeneity of the gut, while in vivo models offer limited control over experimental variables and reduced throughput, restricting their utility for detailed mechanistic investigations. Dissecting local ENS-specific signaling in vivo remains challenging, as current models inevitably involve central nervous system (CNS) input and nonspecific activation of peripheral nerves, obscuring ENS-intrinsic effects. Additionally, the functional impacts of ENS firing dynamics remains poorly defined. Temporal coding is a well-established mechanism in the CNS, where neuronal firing frequency modulates transcriptional and functional outputs22–24. Whether similar mechanisms operate in the ENS remains largely unexplored. Chemogenetic tools, though valuable, lack the fine temporal control required to interrogate these dynamics with regional specificity. Finally, the inability to precisely manipulate luminal content in vivo limits our understanding of how microbiota, dietary components, metabolites, or drugs influence specific neuro-immune pathways. Together, these challenges underscore an unmet need for experimental systems that combine spatial, temporal, and compositional control with tissue-level complexity to unravel the principal mechanisms underlying enteric neuro-immune regulation.

To address these challenges, we develop an optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system that enables precise, real-time activation of specific ENS subtypes in multiplexed whole-tissue gut cultures, facilitating controlled and comparative analyses across replicates or experimental conditions. This ex vivo platform couples Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2)-based neuronal stimulation with tightly controlled experimental conditions, preserving the structural and cellular complexity of native tissue while enabling defined manipulation of luminal inputs. Using this system, we identify neuronal subtype- and frequency-specific activity patterns that distinctly modulate immunological transcriptional responses and epithelial barrier integrity. Intriguingly, our results reveal that complex combinatorial signals from gut microbes and enteric neurons are locally integrated to fine-tune intestinal immunity and barrier function. These findings establish a robust platform for the systematic discovery of neuroimmune circuit dynamics and their modulation by microbial cues, advancing our capacity to uncover functional neuroimmune interactions in the gut.

Results

Development of an optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system

To unravel mechanisms underlying enteric neuro-immune communications, we devised an optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system that facilitates precise stimulation of specific enteric neuronal lineages in multiplexed culture of whole intestinal fragments (Fig. 1a). For this purpose, we modified the gut organ culture device we previously developed13,25. The new device is opaque, to prevent light transfer between multiple gut fragments cultured in individual channels and is sealed by a 3D-printed lid that incorporates LED light diodes and O2/CO2 input ports (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a, Supplementary data 1). LED light/dark cycles, frequency and duration in each channel are controlled by an Arduino open-source electronic platform and custom-made user interface software developed in-house (Supplementary Fig. 1b-c, Supplementary Data 2). The gut culture device supports parallel culture of 6 intestinal fragments, each connected to input and output ports. Computer-controlled syringe pumps maintain luminal flow (to facilitate the coordinated introduction of microbes or compounds) and continuously replenish the extra-intestinal culture medium (to support cultured tissues viability). We next generated transgenic mice that express the light-sensitive ion channel channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in specific subsets of the ENS. We first focused on enteric cholinergic neurons, that control ILC2 activity and type-2 immunity9–12. For this purpose, we crossed Ai32 mice (homozygous for a loxP-flanked STOP cassette followed by channelrhodopsin-2/EYFP fusion gene) with ChAT-IRES-Cre knock-in mice, to generate ChAT-ChR2 homozygotes transgenic mice that express the ChR2-EYFP fusion protein in enteric cholinergic (ChAT + ) neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Whole-mount immunostaining of colonic tissues confirmed robust ChR2-EYFP expression in the ENS, with clear co-localization observed with both the pan-neuronal marker βIII-tubulin (Fig. 1b) and the cholinergic marker choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) (Fig. 1c), validating specific targeting of cholinergic neurons. EYFP expression in enteric neurons was further validated by tissue fractionation (myenteric plexus, lamina propria and intestinal epithelium), dissociation and flow cytometry (within CD9highCD45negSca1neg enteric muscularis myenteric plexus (MMP) neurons14) (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). In addition, immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections revealed ChR2-EYFP fusion protein expression localized to the ENS (Supplementary Fig. 3d–e).

We next analyzed colonic responses to optogenetic activation of enteric neurons, across a range of physiologically relevant frequencies. Electrophysiological recordings of colonic ENS activity performed in previous studies identified homeostatic innervation at 2 Hz26,27. Firing frequencies were significantly elevated under inflammatory conditions (i.e., infection or experimental colitis models28–30). Accordingly, ChAT-ChR2 colonic tissues were connected to the gut culture device13,25 and illuminated with cycles of blue light (460 nm) for neuronal activation, or yellow light (590 nm) as an internal control (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Light was delivered for 30 min, in 60 cycles of (10 sec of 1 ms pulses delivered at either 2 or 10 Hz, followed by a 20 sec break31). After an additional 1.5 h culture in the dark (total of 2 h culture), tissues were collected and analyzed. Whole mount staining revealed increased nuclear localization of the neuronal transcription factor and activation marker cFos in the myenteric plexus of tissues illuminated by blue light (at both 2 and 10 Hz), compared with yellow light controls (Fig. 1d–e, Supplementary Fig. 4a). To exclude the possibility of non-specific neuronal activation by blue light alone, ChAT-ChR2 heterozygote tissues were dissected from Cre-positive and Cre-negative littermates (that do and do not express ChR2, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 2a), and connected to the gut culture device. Here again, blue light illumination significantly increased cFos nuclear localization in ChATCre-Positive-ChR2 tissues (compared with ChATCre-Negative-ChR2 tissues; Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Functionally, live imaging of light-stimulated ChAT-ChR2 colonic tissues showed intracellular calcium influx in myenteric neurons in response to blue light illumination, compared with yellow light background control (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f; Supplementary Movie 1). Finally, to assess the physiological impact of optogenetic activation of cholinergic excitatory motor neurons, we measured intestinal motility following ex vivo stimulation of ChAT-ChR2 heterozygote tissues (dissected from Cre-positive and Cre-negative littermates), illuminated by blue light. Cultured intestinal tissues were infused with luminal FITC-dextran, and motility was quantified using time-lapse fluorescence imaging and automated image analysis using custom-made software developed in-house. Notably, optogenetic stimulation significantly enhanced gut motility (unpaired t test p = 1.25*10−149) (Fig. 1g-h, Supplementary Fig. 4g-h, Supplementary Data 3, and Supplementary Movie 2), further validating the platform’s ability to capture dynamic physiological responses in cultured tissues. Together, these results demonstrate that precise optogenetic stimulations of intestinal organ cultures trigger a measurable activation of enteric neurons.

Frequency-dependent activation of enteric cholinergic neurons drives divergent neuro-immunological gene programs

We next aimed to determine whether on-chip neuronal activation modifies intestinal gene expression, and whether distinct firing patterns differentially shape the intestinal transcriptome. ChAT-ChR2 colonic tissues were illuminated ex vivo with cycles of blue or yellow light (30 min stimulation; tissues collected after 2 h). Interestingly, optogenetic activation of cholinergic neurons rapidly altered colonic transcription, as determined by whole-tissue RNA sequencing (RNAseq) (Fig. 2a). Comparison of 2/10 Hz activation patterns revealed both frequency-dependent and frequency-independent transcriptional programs. For example, the cholinergic neuropeptide Nmu was induced by all activation patterns, in addition to other genes highly expressed by enteric cholinergic neurons6, such as the neuropeptide CGRP-β (Calcb), and the choline transporter Slc5a7 (mediates acetylcholine synthesis) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 4–5). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant induction in neuronal processes, including synaptic transmission and neurotransmitter secretion in both 2 Hz and 10 Hz (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 5a–b, Supplementary Data 12). In contrast, neuronal transcripts as the nitrergic inhibitory neuron marker Nos1, the serotonin receptor 2 A Htr2a, and the adrenergic receptors alpha 2a and 2b (Adra2a/b) were induced only by 2 Hz stimulation. Increased illumination frequency to 10 Hz enhanced the expression of neuronal genes (i.e., Syn1 and Stx1a) and pathways related to neural excitation and neurotransmitter release (Fig. 2a–b, Supplementary Fig. 5a–b). Remarkably, we identified frequency-dependent expression of immunological programs. 10 Hz (but not 2 Hz) stimulation of cholinergic enteric neurons induced ILC2 and type-2 immunity programs, including Il5, Il6, Il33, and Cxcl1 while suppressing genes and pathways related to barrier defense (i.e., Nlrp10, Il18, Retnlb, Lyz2, Cldn4) lymphocytes activity (i.e., Cd5, Cd79a, Cd28) and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 2a-b, Supplementary Fig. 5a-b, Supplementary Data 4, 5, 12). Analysis of Il5 and Il6 expression revealed that some responses were sustained at 4 hr, while others displayed a rapid but transient induction, suggesting diverse temporal dynamics downstream of cholinergic neuronal activation (Fig. 2d–e). To confirm that optogenetically induced transcriptional changes were reflected at the protein level, we analyzed IL-6, as a representative early-response gene, and confirmed its upregulation also at the protein level, supporting the functional relevance of the observed RNA changes (Fig. 2f).

Finally, to underscore the specificity of blue-light-induced gene expression, we confirmed that genes induced by blue light were not significantly upregulated in tissues exposed to yellow light, compared with those maintained in darkness (Supplementary Fig. 5c, Supplementary Data 7). Taken together, these findings reveal that distinct firing patterns of enteric cholinergic neurons differentially control neuro-immunological transcriptional programs.

Short-term ENS Stimulation Does Not Disrupt Microbial Composition

Gut microbiota influences on the central and peripheral nervous systems are well established, with recent studies showing that microbial alterations can affect neural function3,5,13,14,32,33. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that perturbations of gut-innervating neurons may impact microbiota composition21. We therefore investigated whether the rapid transcriptional changes induced by 10 Hz activation of cholinergic enteric neurons might also trigger dysbiosis, potentially confounding the interpretation of the immune phenotypes observed. To this end, luminal contents from ChAT-ChR2 colonic tissues were collected following 10 Hz blue or yellow light stimulation in the ex vivo culture system. After 2 hr, microbial composition was profiled using 16S rDNA sequencing (paired littermates were used to minimize cage-related variability). Our analysis revealed no evidence of broad microbial dysbiosis following neuronal activation. We detected no changes in within-sample (alpha) or between-sample (beta) diversity, indicating that the microbial communities in blue light–stimulated tissues closely resembled those in yellow light controls (Supplementary Fig. 6a-b). Moreover, differential abundance analysis revealed only modest shifts in two bacterial taxa (Supplementary Fig. 6c, Supplementary Data 8), likely reflecting natural inter-individual variability rather than neuronally induced effects. Taken together, these findings suggest that the rapid, frequency-dependent modulation of colonic neuro-immunological transcription by enteric cholinergic neurons reflects a direct neuro-immune regulatory mechanism, rather than a secondary effect of short-term microbiota changes.

Distinct ENS lineages differentially modulate colonic neuro-immunological transcription

To determine whether colonic transcription is differentially modulated by distinct neuronal ENS lineages, we examined tachykinin (Tac1)-expressing excitatory neurons (the Tac1-encoded neuropeptide Substance P controls Tregs development13,14 and regulates tissue protection34). We generated Tac1-ChR2 heterozygotes transgenic mice where expression of Cre recombinase results in transgene positive and negative littermates (that do and do not express ChR2, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 2b). Immunofluorescence staining of Tac1Cre-PosChR2 tissue sections revealed ChR2-EYFP fusion protein expression in the ENS (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Tac1-ChR2 colonic organ cultures were illuminated by blue light (for 30 min, in 60 cycles of (10 sec of 1 ms pulses delivered at either 2 or 10 Hz, followed by a 20 sec break31)). Tissues were collected at 2 hr, and the transgene negative tissues served as an internal control (Fig. 3a). cFos nuclear localization in the myenteric plexus was significantly increased in the Tac1Cre-PosChR2 transgene positive tissues (compared with Tac1Cre-NegChR2 tissues; Fig. 3b-c, Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast to cholinergic neurons, 10 Hz optogenetic stimulation of Tac1-ChR2 neurons induced transcription of type-1 immune responses and barrier defense programs (Fig. 3d-e, Supplementary Fig. 7b, Supplementary Data 9, 12). These include Ifng (interferon-γ), the ENS-derived cytokine Il18 (Ifng-inducing factor, controls barrier immunity18,19), the IL-18-dependant anti-microbial peptide Retnlb19,35, and the antimicrobial peptides Reg3b and Reg3g. Additional epithelial barrier defense factors included the antimicrobial and IFN-γ-induced chemokine Cxcl10, the pathogenic Th17 cells inducer Saa1 (serum amyloid A36), and the reactive oxygen species-producing enzymes Nos2, Duox2 and Duoxa2 (Fig. 3d). In agreement, pathways related to interferon signaling and defense response to symbiont were highly enriched (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 7b, Supplementary Data 12). Distinct ENS lineage and firing frequency differentially regulate epithelial barrier integrity

Emerging evidence suggest that the ENS coordinates epithelial integrity and barrier protection18,19,37. To determine whether on-chip activation of distinct neuronal subtypes and activation patterns affects epithelial tight junction (TJ) integrity, homozygote ChAT-ChR2 and heterozygote Tac1-ChR2 colons were optogenetically activated at 2/10 Hz. Interestingly, cholinergic activation of ChAT-ChR2 colons at 10 Hz, but not 2 Hz, rapidly compromised TJ integrity, as indicated by a significant decrease in epithelial ZO-1 and E-cadherin protein staining (Fig. 3f–h, Supplementary Fig. 8a). In contrast, optogenetic activation of Tac1+ neurons suppressed TJ protein expression in both 2 Hz and 10 Hz frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 8b-d).

To rule out epithelial cell death as the cause of reduced TJ expression, we performed TUNEL assay on optogenetically stimulated tissue sections (Supplementary Fig. 8e). TUNEL-positive cells were readily detected in positive control colons (collected 24 hr after 10 Gy whole-body irradiation), confirming assay sensitivity. In contrast, we observed no significant accumulation of TUNEL-positive cells in tissues stimulated at 10 Hz relative to yellow-light controls or freshly dissected samples, indicating that the stimulation protocol does not induce epithelial apoptosis or necrosis (Supplementary Fig. 8e). These findings suggest that the reduction in E-cadherin and ZO-1 expression reflects regulated TJ remodeling in response to ENS activation, rather than epithelial damage.

Finally, to determine whether the observed TJ disruption functionally impacts gut permeability, we combined on-chip optogenetic activation with ex vivo intestinal permeability assay (X-IPA) previously developed in our lab38 (Fig. 3i). This integration enabled a direct approach to measure gut permeability changes in response to defined enteric neuronal activation. ChAT-ChR2 heterozygous tissues were dissected from Cre-positive and Cre-negative littermates and infused with FITC-dextran (4 kDa) ex vivo. Notably, 10 Hz blue light stimulation, but not 2 Hz, significantly increased extra-intestinal FITC-dextran flux in ChATCre-Positive-ChR2 tissues compared with ChATCre-Negative-ChR2 tissues, indicating enhanced gut permeability and functional barrier disruption (Fig. 3j). Thus, distinct ENS lineages differentially control epithelial barrier integrity and gut permeability in an innervation frequency-dependent manner.

Gut microbes modulate ENS-driven transcriptional and barrier responses

Under physiological conditions, intestinal immunocytes and enterocytes are simultaneously exposed to numerous microbial and neuronal-derived signals. Potentially, cellular integration of combinatorial signals may drive diverse synergistic or antagonistic outcomes. Yet intestinal responses to defined combinations of microbial and neuronal signals are mostly unknown and are extremely challenging to investigate using existing methodologies. In contrast, the optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system facilitates precise luminal infusion of gut microbes in parallel to optogenetic-mediated ENS stimulation, thus accelerating combinatorial neuro-immune-microbiota research at the whole-tissue level.

We examined colonic responses to the human symbiont Thomasclavelia ramosa (formally Clostridium ramosum), a potent Treg-inducing microbe39 which we have previously found to modulate ENS activity to promote anti-inflammatory immune responses1,13,14. Cultured ChAT-ChR2 colons were illuminated with blue or yellow light (10 Hz, pattern described above), in parallel to luminal infusion of T. ramosa or Finegoldia magna (formally Peptostreoptococcus magnus; that does not induce Tregs development39) as an internal control (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, despite vigorous optogenetic neuronal stimulation, Treg-inducing T. ramosa reduced cFos nuclear localization intensity in myenteric neurons (Fig. 4b-c, Supplementary Fig. 9a). Likewise, luminal infusion of T. ramosa reduced optogenetic-induced cFos nuclear localization intensity in myenteric neurons of Tac1-ChR2 colons, suggesting a conserved modulatory effect of T. ramosa across distinct ENS populations (Supplementary Fig. 9b–c). Interestingly, T. ramosa differentially modulated optogenetics-induced colonic transcription of ChAT-ChR2 colons (Fig. 4d-e, Supplementary Fig. 9d, Supplementary Data 10–12). For example, optogenetics-mediated induction of cholinergic neuropeptides (e.g., Nmu, Calcb, and Scg3) and the suppression of barrier defense (i.e., Nlrp10, Lyz2, Cldn4) and lymphocytes activity (i.e., Cd5, Cd79a, Cd28) related genes were effectively eliminated in optogenetics-stimulated tissues infused with T. ramosa (Fig. 4d). In contrast, luminal T. ramosa did not affect optogenetics-mediated induction of type-2 cytokines (as Il5, Il6, or Cxcl1) (Fig. 4d). Further, combinatorial optogenetic stimulation and luminal T. ramosa infusion remodeled neuro-immunological responses induced by optogenetic stimulation alone, mainly affecting genes and pathways related to cytokines production, binding, and cell-cell contact (Fig. 4d-e, Supplementary Fig. 9d, Supplementary Data 10–12). Finally, luminal infusion of T. ramosa (but not F. magna) ameliorated epithelial TJ disruptions induced by 10 Hz cholinergic innervation, suggesting a potential role for gut microbes in regulating enteric neuro-epithelial connections (Fig. 4f–g, Supplementary Fig. 8e, Supplementary Fig. 9e-f). Discussion

The ENS is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of gut immunity, integrating microbial cues to shape mucosal responses. However, current methodologies offer limited access to the fine-scale dynamics of ENS-mediated control, impeding mechanistic insights. In this study, we introduce an optogenetics-integrated gut organ culture system that enables precise, real-time stimulation of defined ENS subtypes across intact intestinal tissues. By combining spatial and temporal neuronal control with direct luminal manipulation, this platform permits systematic interrogation of how neuronal and microbial signals converge to influence host physiology. The ex vivo design, devoid of CNS or systemic inputs, offers high-resolution insight into local ENS-driven pathways.

Using this system, we dissected the acute effects of cholinergic ENS activation, previously implicated in ILC2 regulation and type-2 immunity9–12. Optogenetic stimulation rapidly induced neuroimmune gene programs and neurotransmission pathways, revealing an immediate onset of ENS-driven immune modulation. Notably, these responses were strongly dependent on neuronal firing frequency. High-frequency (10 Hz), but not low-frequency (2 Hz), stimulation selectively triggered type-2 immune transcripts while suppressing epithelial barrier defense and lymphocyte activation genes. Activity-dependent gene transcription is a fundamental feature of neuronal signaling in the CNS, where distinct firing patterns - whether tonic, phasic, bursting, or irregular - encode information and activate specific molecular cascades that reshape transcriptional profiles, cellular function, and circuit plasticity22–24. Our findings extend this paradigm to the ENS, identifying firing frequency as an instructive signal that governs discrete transcriptional and functional outcomes. These effects are likely mediated by frequency-dependent release of neurotransmitters, neuropeptides, and cytokines, positioning ENS temporal dynamics as a critical regulator of immune and epithelial responses in the gut.

The mechanisms governing immune-related firing dynamics in the ENS remain largely elusive. ENS neurons are known to adapt their activity in response to physiological cues: for instance, in the context of gut motility, mechanosensitive enteric neurons adjust their firing patterns in response to tissue stretch, initiating reflexes that coordinate smooth muscle contraction40. Whether analogous mechanisms govern neuroimmune communication is unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that microbial composition can modulate ENS excitability13,41,42, raising the possibility that dysbiosis alters firing patterns, reshapes neuronal gene expression, and disrupts the release of neuromodulatory signals - ultimately driving immune imbalance and barrier dysfunction. This hypothesis warrants direct investigation using tools such as real-time calcium imaging with genetically encoded activity reporters (e.g., GCaMP6) or electrophysiological recordings, in response to defined microbial perturbations.

Beyond firing dynamics, our findings underscore the role of neuronal lineage identity in shaping immune outcomes. Optogenetic stimulation of Tac1⁺ (tachykininergic) neurons elicited a transcriptional program distinct from cholinergic neurons, marked by robust induction of type-1 immune mediators and barrier defense genes. Prior studies showed that ENS-derived IL-18 regulates goblet cell secretion of antimicrobial proteins critical for mucosal defense18,19. Here, we demonstrate that high-frequency activation of Tac1⁺ neurons (but not cholinergic neurons) rapidly initiate IL-18–related transcription. We further show that epithelial barrier integrity and gut permeability are modulated by ENS activity in a lineage- and frequency-dependent manner. Of note, while our observations are most likely driven by ENS neuronal firing, we cannot entirely exclude contributions from rare ChAT⁺ or Tac1⁺ non-neuronal cells, such as tuft or enterochromaffin cells. Together, our findings support and extend the concept of neuroimmune cell units (NICUs)43–46, previously described in the CNS and peripheral nervous system, by adding a dynamic layer: distinct ENS subtypes and activity patterns rapidly and selectively coordinate immune programs suited to physiological context.

Critically, our system also revealed that microbial signals reshape ENS-driven transcription. Luminal infusion of the Treg-inducing bacterium T. ramosa markedly attenuated cholinergic activation and neuropeptide expression and reversed ENS-mediated suppression of barrier-related genes and tight junction components. These findings align with and extend earlier work by us and others demonstrating that T. ramosa engages in bidirectional crosstalk with enteric neurons and colonic Treg cells13,14. By modulating neuronal activation, density, and IL-6 production, T. ramosa tunes Treg differentiation and promotes mucosal tolerance. Our current results build on this framework, showing that defined microbial signals can acutely modulate ENS activity and its transcriptional output, highlighting both the regulatory role and therapeutic promise of targeted microbes in shaping neuroimmune circuits.

The notion that ENS lineage and firing dynamics serve as a biologically instructive variable carries important implications for both basic and translational research. While chemogenetic and pharmacological tools have advanced our understanding of ENS function, their utility may be enhanced by approaches that account for the temporal dynamics of neuronal activity. In this context, in vivo optogenetics31,47,48 and the optogenetics-integrated gut culture system presented here offer complementary platforms for high-resolution dissection of frequency-dependent responses in intact intestinal tissues. Clinically, frequency-tuned neuromodulation of specific neuronal subsets offers a promising avenue to restore barrier integrity and mitigate inflammation, whether through bioelectronic devices49,50 or microbial interventions that recalibrate ENS activity.

While our study establishes a robust framework for dissecting ENS-regulated immunity, several limitations should be noted. The ex vivo system preserves tissue viability and architecture for up to 24 hr, limiting studies of chronic immune adaptation or long-term host-microbiota interactions. The lack of systemic circulation precludes assessment of endocrine or neurohumoral feedback, and although CNS exclusion enhances mechanistic clarity, it restricts insight into gut–brain axis signaling. We therefore view this platform as a powerful complement to existing models, providing mechanistic insights under defined conditions that can guide and sharpen subsequent validation in complex in vivo and clinical settings.

In conclusion, this study introduces a versatile tool that overcomes key barriers in gut neuroscience and immunology. We show that neuronal identity, firing dynamics, and microbial inputs converge to orchestrate distinct epithelial and immune responses, establishing a mechanistic basis for microbiota-based and neuromodulatory therapies. Moving forward, this platform may support finer-resolution mapping of ENS-to-target cell interactions and integration of patient-derived microbial communities to model disease-relevant contexts. These insights position the ENS as both a dynamic integrator of gut homeostasis and a tractable target for therapeutic intervention.

Mice

Transgenic mice (based on C57BL/6 mouse background) were obtained from Jackson Lab and maintained in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice facility at Bar-Ilan University (Israel). Mice were handled following protocols approved by the Bar-Ilan University ethics committee (ethics approval number BIU-BIU-IL-2203-131-1). Both males and females mice were used in all experiments. Mice expressing channelrhodopsin-2/EYFP [RCL- ChR2(H134R)/EYFP] fusion protein in the Rosa26 locus downstream of a floxed-STOP cassette (Ai32 mice; RRID: IMSR_JAX:024109) were crossed with either ChAT-Cre (RRID: IMSR_JAX:006410) or Tac1-Cre (RRID: IMSR_JAX:021877) mice to generate neuron-specific optogenetic reporter lines. Breeding strategies are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. Briefly: (1) To generate ChAT-ChR2 homozygous mice (ChAT-Cre + /+; ChR2-EYFP + /+), ChAT-Cre homozygous mice were crossed with ChR2 homozygous mice to obtain double heterozygotes. These were further intercrossed to produce ChAT-Cre +/+; ChR2 +/+ offspring, in which all enteric cholinergic (ChAT⁺) neurons express ChR2-EYFP. (2) To obtain ChAT-ChR2 heterozygous mice (ChAT-Cre + /-; ChR2-EYFP + /+), ChAT-Cre +/-; ChR2 +/+ mice were bred to ChR2 +/+ partners, yielding litters in which the offspring either expressed ChR2 in ChAT⁺ neurons (ChAT-Cre +/- ChR2 + /+) or not (ChAT-Cre -/- ChR2 + /+), serving as littermate controls. (3) Tac1-ChR2 heterozygous mice (Tac1-Cre + /-; ChR2-EYFP + /+) were generated by crossing Tac1-Cre +/-; ChR2 +/+ mice with ChR2 +/+ mice to obtain Tac1-Cre +/-; ChR2 +/+ and Tac1-Cre -/-; ChR2 +/+ littermates. For all experiments, tissues were harvested from littermates with identical ChR2 status, differing only in Cre expression.

Optogenetics-integrated Gut Organ Culture System

The gut culture device was modified to support on-chip optogenetic stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Fabrication of the gut organ culture device for gut organ culture experiments13 included carbon black (Holland-Moran Cas#1333-86-4) in PDMS (184 ®SYLGARD#761036) mixture to fabricate opaque gut culture devices. We developed a three-layer culture device (Supplementary Fig. 1a): (1) a standard organ culture device at the bottom, containing 6 intestinal organ cultures; (2) a middle section adapter for the flow of filtered and humidified medical-grade 95% O2 / 5% CO2 gas mixture; and (3) a top section containing blue or yellow LED diodes controlled by the Arduino UNO platform, to control LED illumination patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1b). All experiments were conducted in the dark.

Gut organ culture experiments were performed as previously described13,25. Briefly, intact whole colons were dissected sterilely from 14-day-old transgenic mouse littermates reared under SPF conditions. The solid lumen content was gently flushed, and the gut fragment was threaded and fixed over the luminal input and output ports of the gut organ culture device, using sterile surgical thread. The culture device was placed in a custom-made incubator that maintained a temperature of 37 °C, and tissue was maintained half-soaked in sterile, serum-free culture medium (Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium without phenol-red (IMDM, GIBCO) supplemented with 20% KnockOut serum replacement (GIBCO), 2% B-27 and 1% of N-2 supplements (GIBCO), 1% L-glutamine, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% HEPES) using a syringe pump (medium flow rate: 1 ml/hour). Tissues were infused with sterile culture medium without phenol-red into the gut lumen using a syringe pump, with or without purified bacterial cultures (T. ramosa or F. magna, at 107 CFU/mL) (luminal flow rate: 30 µl/hour). The gas outlet in the device lid enabled the flow of a humidified and filtered, medical-grade 95% O2 / 5% CO2 gas mixture into the device. Experiments were terminated 2 hr after stimulation unless otherwise indicated. For selected analyses, including ELISA and selected RT–PCR assays, tissues were collected 4 hr post-stimulation, as noted in the corresponding figure legends. After culture, tissues were subjected to further analysis (including RNAseq, immunofluorescence staining, and imaging).

Stimulation of optogenetic neurons using the gut culture device

Tissues were illuminated using blue or yellow LED diodes (5 mm, 3.5 V ~ 4 V) (Supplementary Fig. 1e) connected to the Arduino UNO platform and placed above the gut organ cultures. The illumination pattern was controlled by custom-made software (Supplementary Fig. 1c-d). Tissues were illuminated for 30 minutes, in 60 cycles of (10 seconds of 1 ms pulses delivered at either 2 or 10 Hz, followed by a 20-second break)31. Following optogenetic stimulation, tissues were maintained in culture for 1.5 hr and collected 2 hr post-stimulation. For 4-hr experiments, tissues were similarly illuminated for 30 minutes, then cultured for an additional 3.5 hr prior to collection.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Following organ culture, intestinal tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C until further processing. Tissue segments (5 mg each) were homogenized in 450 µL of cold PBS (1×) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, P8340; 1:100 dilution). Homogenization was carried out at 4 °C using a Precellys Evolution bead-based homogenizer (1.4 mm and 2.8 mm beads) at 5000 rpm for 1 minute (two 15-second cycles separated by a 30-second pause). Lysates were centrifuged twice at 20,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected as total protein lysates for ELISA. IL-6 levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, ELK1157) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytokine concentrations were normalized to total protein content, determined by Bradford assay, and reported as pg IL-6 per mg of total protein.

Ex vivo intestinal permeability assay (X-IPA) combined with ex vivo optogenetics

For intestinal permeability assay using the gut culture system (as described at38), ChAT-ChR2 heterozygous tissues were dissected from Cre-positive and Cre-negative littermates, and connected to the optogenetics-integrated gut culture device. FITC-dextran (4 kDa; 0.5 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 60842-46-8) was resuspended in sterile culture medium lacking phenol red and infused using a syringe pump into the gut cultures lumen. Tissues were illuminated with blue at 10 Hz for 30 minutes using the temporal pattern described above. Experiments were terminated 2 hr following stimulation. At the endpoint, the concentration of FITC-dextran in the extraintestinal medium – a direct measurement of gut permeability38 - was assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity using a fluorometer (excitation: 485 nm; emission: 520 nm).

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

Whole mount staining of the myenteric plexus

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (SIGMA, 252549) for 1 hour at 4 °C, washed with Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (GIBCO, 14190-094) and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 hr at 4 °C. Blocking solution containing 10% donkey serum (Merck, D9663), 5% Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Millipore,821006), and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Millipore,1086031000) was added for 1 hour at room temperature (RT). Primary antibodies anti-βIII-tubulin (Abcam ab41489), cFos (Cell Signaling Technology, 2250), and HuC/D (Santa Cruz SC-515624) were diluted in blocking solution (1:100) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For ChAT staining, primary antibodies against ChAT (Abcam, clone EPR16590) were diluted 1:1000 in blocking solution and incubated with the samples for 48 hr at 4 °C. Following primary antibodies incubation, tissues were washed 3 times with PBS-T (0.1% Tween in PBS) and incubated overnight with secondary antibodies Cy3-AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (Jackson Lab, #711-165-152) and Cy5 (Abcam, ab97147) diluted in blocking solution (1:200). After washing 3 times with PBS-T, tissues were stained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS, Mercury #1246530100) for 15 minutes at RT. Finally, tissues were mounted on slides with Fluoroshield mounting medium (SIGMA, F6182), and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope Leica Stellaris 5 (Leica Microsystems).

Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections

For tissue section staining, intestinal tissues were frozen in OCT (Labotal, CAT- 14020108926) at −80 °C. 7μm-thick sections of fresh-frozen tissues were sliced using a cryostat (CM1950). The sections were fixed with 100% cold acetone for 20 minutes, washed with PBS, and blocked (10% donkey serum, 0.1% Triton in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies for ZO-1 (Invitrogen cat: 40–2200) 1:100 and E-cadherin (BioLegend cat:147302) 1:200 were diluted in blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, slides were washed 3 times with PBS-T (0.1% tween in PBS) and incubated with secondary antibodies Cy5 (Jackson cat: 712-175-153) 1:250 and Cy3 (Jackson cat: 711-165-152) 1:500 diluted in PBS-T (0.1% tween) for 1 hour at room temperature. After washing 3 times with PBS-T, tissues were stained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS) for 15 minutes at RT. Finally, tissues were mounted on slides with Fluoroshield mounting medium (SIGMA, F6182). Tissue sections were visualized using a confocal fluorescence microscope Leica Stellaris 5 (Leica Microsystems), processed and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Image analysis

Quantification of cFos nuclear localization in whole mount images was performed using custom ImageJ macros, which were tailored for ChAT-ChR2 or Tac1-ChR2 tissues, respectively.

The ChAT-ChR2 tissue macro employed a multi-step process. First, the DAPI channel was segmented after being smoothed via Gaussian blur (sigma=2), and touching nuclei were separated with the watershed function. YFP channel (expressed in cholinergic enteric neurons) segmentation was done after applying the smoothing (Gaussian blur; sigma=5) and fill holes functions. To verify the segmentation of the nuclei, the DAPI mask was eroded by 1. Then, YFP positive nuclei were identified by using the image Calculator function (And) to keep only overlapping areas of both binary masks. To measure the cytoplasmic cFos MFI, the nuclei outlines were enlarged, a new binary mask was generated, with the original DAPI mask being subtracted via the image calculator function (subtract). Finally, the cFos MFI was quantified for both the nuclear and cytoplasmic regions by applying the analyze Particles function on the cFos channel. The ratio of nuclear to cytoplasmic MFI was presented to account for variations in cFos expression between cells.

The Tac1-ChR2 tissue macro works in a similar fashion. Following manual verification of proper spatial localization in the tissue, via presence of HuC/D staining, the macro quantifies the cFos MFI within all nuclei present in each field of view.

In both analyses, the measured cFos MFI was normalized to the mean of the control MFI values to account for potential inter-experimental variations in staining intensity. Additionally, manual verification of identified nuclei was conducted to minimize inclusion of false positives. To quantify ZO-1 and E-cadherin signal intensity in intestinal tissue, an ImageJ (FIJI) macro was used following the workflow below: First, to delineate the full area of the intestinal tissue, the DAPI and E-cadherin channels were merged, followed by Gaussian filtering and segmentation using the Huang automatic thresholding algorithm. Subsequently, based on the resulting tissue segmentation, the mean fluorescence intensity of E-cadherin and ZO-1 was measured across the defined tissue area.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Tissue sections were analyzed for TUNEL assay staining using a TUNEL Kit provided by Abcam (cat. no. ab66110), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. In short, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and labeled with BrdU by TdT enzyme for 1 hour at 37 °C, followed by BrdU-Red antibody staining for 30 minutes at room temperature and 7-AAD staining for nuclei visualization. Apoptotic nuclei were visualized using a ZEISS LSM 900 Airyscan 2 confocal microscope and analyzed using Zen software (ZEN Blue edition 3.3).

The large intestine was fractionated into three different layers: ENS, lamina propria, and intestinal epithelial cells, as detailed below:

ENS isolation

Colons were opened lengthwise, and the muscularis myenteric plexus was completely dissected under a binocular microscope using watchmaker forceps (Zhang and Hu, 2013) and placed in a 1.5 mL tube containing 0.1 mg/mL Liberase TL (54101135001, Sigma) in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, 02-018-1 A, Biological Industries) to digest for 0.5 hr at 37 °C, and mechanically disrupted by mild shaking. The tissue was further digested with 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA solution at 37 °C for 10 minutes. After trituration using a P1000 pipette, single-cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 µm filter and cleaned of debris by centrifugation through 1 mL fetal bovine serum (110 × g for 10 minutes). These cells were then centrifuged and counted for staining and flow cytometry.

IEC and LP isolation

Intestinal pieces were incubated in fresh, pre-warmed extraction medium (5% DTT (Fisher, BP172-5), 0.5 M EDTA (Bio-Lab 009012230100), FBS (SIGMA, F9665), and RPMI (SIGMA, R7509)) for 10 minutes at 37 °C in a urine cup with a stir bar (per tissue, stirred at 500 rpm). The extraction medium containing the IEC layer was collected and stained for flow cytometry. For isolation of LP lymphocytes, the intestinal pieces were washed in RPMI (37 °C) and rolled over paper towels to remove mucus. The tissue was cut into small pieces using scissors and incubated in pre-warmed digestion medium (FBS, Dispase (Gibco cat. no 1705-041), Collagenase (Gibco cat. no 1701-015), and RPMI) for 30 minutes at 37 °C (per tissue, stirred at 500 rpm). The medium was filtered using a 40 µm cell strainer into a 50 mL tube with 2 mL FBS. It was then rinsed with an additional 20–25 mL of cold RPMI and 1 mL of FBS. Following centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 minutes the cellular pellet was resuspended in 1 mL, and filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer. This solution was then centrifuged and counted for staining and flow cytometry.

Extracellular staining and flow cytometry

Purified single-cell suspensions were stained using antibodies against mouse CD45 (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-117-498), SCA1 (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-102-832), and CD9 (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-102-612) for 15 minutes at 4 °C. Cells were washed using RPMI and fixed (1% formalin in DMEM, overnight at 4 °C). Cells were analyzed by BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer and data were processed with FlowJo software.

Calcium imaging

To monitor neuronal calcium dynamics, colonic tissues were dissected from ChAT-ChR2 mice and stained with the synthetic calcium indicator Rhod-2 AM (R1245MP, Invitrogen). Dye solution was prepared by mixing 8 µl DMSO (D2650, Sigma-Aldrich), 2 µl Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 90 µl physiological saline with one vial of 50 µg Rhod-2, followed by agitation for 30 min at room temperature. Tissues were incubated in gut culture medium supplemented with 20 µl of the dye solution for 45 min at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Following incubation, tissues were transferred to a Slicescope 6000 (Scientifica, UK) equipped with an EXI-Blue CCD camera (QIMAGING) and continuously illuminated with yellow light for background illumination. Optogenetic stimulation was delivered using 50 ms blue light pulses (164.5 µW/mm²) via a Polygon 4000 digital micromirror device (Mightex) integrated into the microscope optical path. Image sequences were acquired at 10 Hz (100 ms/frame) for a total of 1 min per recording.

Fluorescence changes were quantified offline. ΔF/F was calculated by subtracting each frame from the average of the baseline (pre-stimulus) frames and dividing by this baseline average (defined from the first 100 frames under yellow light). Cellular responses were determined by averaging pixel intensity values within manually defined regions of interest (ROIs).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Colon tissue fragments (3 mm, proximal colon) were stored in RNAlater (Qiagen) overnight at 4 °C, and homogenized using a bead beater. Then, bulk RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Universal Mini QIAGEN kit. Libraries were prepared using a modified SMART-Seq2 protocol. In brief, 1 ng of purified RNA was taken for reverse transcription with Maxima Reverse Transcriptase (Life Technologies) and whole-transcription amplification (WTA) with KAPA HotStart HIFI 2× ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems) for 20 cycles. WTA products were purified with Ampure XP beads (Beckman Coulter), quantified with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher), and assessed with a high-sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent). RNA-seq libraries were constructed from purified WTA products using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina). To increase the depth of sequencing, paired-end reads were sequenced on four lanes of an Illumina NextSeq 500/550 three times by The Crown Genomics Institute of the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science.

Poly-A/T stretches and Illumina adapters were trimmed from the reads using cutadapt51 (v2.7); resulting reads shorter than 30 bp were discarded. Reads were mapped to the M. musculus reference genome GRCm39 using STAR52 (v2.7.3a), supplied with gene annotations downloaded from Ensembl (https://www.ensembl.org/index.html) (with the EndToEnd option and outFilterMismatchNoverLmax set to 0.04). Reads with the same UMI were removed with the PICARD MarkDuplicate tool using the BARCODE_TAG parameter. Expression levels for each gene were quantified using htseq-count53 (v0.11.2), using the gtf mentioned above. The pipeline was run using snakemake54. The output was ~4 million reads per sample per Illumina NextSeq run. All three counts tables from the 3 independent sequencing runs were merged together, for a final sequencing depth of ~10–12 million reads per sample. The initial processing described above was done by The Mantoux Bioinformatics Institute of the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science.

All gene reads were converted from Ensembl ID to gene symbol (in case of multiple Ensembl ID that were assigned to the same gene, the sum of total counts was calculated and assigned to the corresponding gene symbol). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq255 (v1.36.0) R/bioconductor package. Significant differentially expressed genes were selected using threshold values of p-value <= 0.05 and abs(log₂FC) >= 0.58 (Fold Change (FC) > = 1.5). Heatmaps were generated for data visualization using ComplexHeatmap56 (v2.12.1) R package. Briefly, variance stabilization transformation was applied, and normalized expression for each gene in each sample was normalized to the average of the control group, and z-score were calculated and color-coded for generating the heatmaps. For comparison of ENS-expressed genes within optogenetic-activated tissues, genes within enteric neuron classes7 that exhibited significant up- or down-regulation in response to optogenetic stimulation were identified and extracted. Principal component analysis was done using the stats (v4.2.1) R package, and a 3-D PCA plot was generated using the plotly57(v4.10.1) R package. Statistical analysis of permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using pairwiseAdonis package in R with pairwise.adonis() function with default parameters. A scatter plot was generated using ggplot258 (v3.4.2) R package. For pathway enrichment analysis, differentially expressed genes were analyzed using Metascape59. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis60 (GSEA v4.3.2) was performed for all the genes ranked (-log10(p-value)/sign(log₂FC)), with M5 ontology gene sets (v2023.1). Relevant pathways from GSEA results with q-value (FDR) < = 0.05 were plotted using ggplot2 or complexHeatmap. Non-significant pathways (FDR q > 0.05) were assigned as NES = 0. To evaluate the potential effect of yellow light illumination on colonic gene expression RNASeq of whole tissue samples following yellow light illumination at 10 Hz was performed and compared with tissue that was not illuminated at all (darkness). Samples (n = 3 per condition) were used for library construction (using TruSeq stranded mRNA library prep kit) and sequenced at the Bar-Ilan University Scientific Equipment Center using the Illumina NextSeq platform (NextSeq 500 High Output v2 kit (#FC-404-2005)). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 as described above, and a volcano plot was generated using ggplot2.

Analysis of gut motility ex vivo

To model gut motility within the gut organ culture system13, intact intestinal fragments were infused with FITC-dextran (4 kDa, 0.5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) into the gut lumen, allowing real-time visualization of intestinal motility. ChATCre-Positive-ChR2 tissues (compared with ChATCre-Negative-ChR2 tissues) were illuminated with blue light using a Dino-Lite fluorescence video microscope (AM4115T-GRFBY) and time-lapse fluorescence images were captured (1 image per second), enabling quantitative motion analysis.

Segmentation process

All image processing and quantification steps described below were implemented using Python (v3.10) with a custom analysis pipeline built on open-source libraries. To analyze gut motility, time-lapse fluorescence images were first subjected to a segmentation procedure designed to isolate the gut from the background. Initially, a region of interest (ROI) was manually selected by the user on the first frame using a graphical interface implemented with the Tkinter library ensuring consistent coverage of the gut across all subsequent frames. Fluorescence images were then split into channels using OpenCV (v4.10.0), and the green fluorescence channel—selected for its high contrast—underwent Gaussian blur filtering using OpenCV’s GaussianBlur function to reduce noise artifacts. Next, Otsu’s adaptive thresholding was applied via OpenCV’s cv2.threshold method to transform the blurred image into a binary mask, separating the bright fluorescent areas (i.e., gut) from the darker background. To enhance segmentation quality, morphological operations including dilation (cv2.dilate) and closing (cv2.morphologyEx) were performed using elliptical kernels, connecting fragmented regions and removing minor artifacts. Finally, contours of sufficient size ( ≥ 10,000 pixels) were extracted using cv2.findContours, retaining only the principal gut structure for subsequent motion tracking. These binary masks formed the basis for all downstream analyses of gut motility.

Motion quantification

Two primary metrics—Centroid Distance and Percent Change—were used to characterize gut motility from the time-lapse fluorescence datasets. For Centroid Distance, the gut’s centroid was identified in each frame (based on the segmented binary mask), and frame-to-frame displacement of the centroid was calculated via the Euclidean distance formula from the math and NumPy61 (v1.26.2) libraries. This approach highlighted overall positional shifts of the gut over time.

In parallel, Percent Change captured dynamic structural fluctuations by tracking black-to-white and white-to-black pixel transitions within the segmented region. Each binary mask was analyzed to quantify the number of transitioning pixels between consecutive frames, reflecting the extent of local morphological changes. Together, these parameters enabled a detailed examination of both global displacement and fine-scale motility patterns in the gut.

Statistical analysis

To compare gut motility patterns between the experimental groups, a two-sample unpaired t-test was performed on each parameter using ggpubr (v0.6.0) package in R. Specifically, centroid distance values from Cre− versus Cre+ groups were tested to assess differences in overall displacement. In addition, the percent change metric—which reflects local pixel transitions—was similarly analyzed between Cre− and Cre+ cohorts. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05, allowing for identification of group-level differences in gut motility. Data visualization was carried out using ggplot2.

16S rRNA sequencing

To evaluate potential differences in the colonic microbial composition induced by optogenetic stimulation of ChAT-ChR2 homozygous mice, we dissected colon tissues that were connected to the gut culture device, and illuminated at 10 Hz with either blue light or yellow light (at 2 h post-illumination). DNA was extracted from colonic content using the PureLink™ Microbiome DNA Purification Kit, as described by the manufacturer (A29790, Invitrogen). DNA concentrations were measured by nanodrop, and 20 ng of DNA was used for 16S library preparation. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by HyLabs Ltd (Israel), using MiSeq platform (Illumina). Raw paired-end FASTQ files containing 16S rRNA gene sequences were processed using the QIIME262 pipeline (v2023.7.0). Sequences were imported into QIIME2 and were denoised using the DADA2 algorithm. For quality trimming, forward reads were truncated at 221 bp, reverse reads at 241 bp, and 13 bp were removed from the left of both forward and reverse reads. ASVs were aligned using the MAFFT program, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed with the FastTree algorithm. ASVs with fewer than 20 reads in total were removed. Taxonomic assignment was performed with the classify-sklearn naïve Bayes classifier in QIIME2, using the GreenGenes2 (v2022.10) reference database.

Alpha and beta diversity were analyzed to assess within- and between-sample microbial variation. Read count data were first aggregated at the species level, and each sample was normalized to relative abundances by dividing all feature counts by the total number of reads in that sample.

For alpha diversity, the Shannon diversity index was computed using the diversity() function from the vegan63(v2.6.4) R package. Group differences between light exposure conditions were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test. Boxplots with individual sample points were generated using ggplot2, with custom color schemes for visual distinction between groups.

For beta diversity, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices were computed using the vegdist() function (vegan). Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed using the cmdscale() function to reduce the multidimensional distance data into two principal axes. PCoA plots were generated with ggplot2, with points colored by light exposure condition. Group-level differences in community composition were tested using PERMANOVA (adonis2() function, 999 permutations), with Light as the grouping variable. The resulting p-value was reported directly on the PCoA plot.

Differential abundance analysis was conducted in R using the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0). Significant taxa at the species level were identified based on a p-adjusted (Benjamini–Hochberg) value ≤ 0.1 and a abs(log₂FC) ≥ 0.58 (corresponding to a fold change of ≥ 1.5). In the DESeq2 analysis, the design formula used was ~ Littermates + Light to remove potential batch effects associated with Littermates.

Statistics & reproducibility

Sample sizes were chosen based on standard practices in the field and our prior experience with similar experimental systems, ensuring sufficient power to detect reproducible biological effects. No data were excluded from the analyses. Unless otherwise noted, each data point represents an individual mouse or independent sample, and all experiments were reproduced in at least three independent biological replicates. The investigators were blinded to allocation during image analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6), as detailed in the figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with post hoc multiple-comparisons testing was applied. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Exact P-values, test types, and replicate numbers (n) are reported in the figure legends. RNA-sequencing and 16S rRNA microbiome sequencing were each performed with independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3 per group). RT-qPCR and ELISA assays were validated across at least two independent experiments (and mostly more than three independent experiments). Immunofluorescence and barrier integrity (FITC-dextran permeability) assays were performed in multiple animals per group, with reproducible outcomes across independent experiments. Motility assays and calcium imaging were repeated in separate biological replicates to confirm consistency.
