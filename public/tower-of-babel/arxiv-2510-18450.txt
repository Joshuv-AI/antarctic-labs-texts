The Momentum Light Ray Transform
Sombuddha Bhattacharyya ∗, Tuhin Mondal ∗, Suman Kumar Sahoo †

Abstract
. In this article we study Momentum Light Ray Transform (MLRT) on symmetric tensor fields. MLRT is an integral transform in time-space domain ( ( t , x ) ∈ ℝ 1 + n (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n} ), which integrates a scalar function or a tensor field along the light rays with a polynomial type weight. We explore necessary and sufficient conditions for injectivity of MLRT, over general order tensors on space dimension ≥ 2 \geq 2 , from full and restricted measurements. Furthermore, we develop an inversion algorithm for MLRTs in the restricted measurement setting. To prove the results, we use tools from tensor tomography, geometry, and analysis.

Introduction and main results

The study of integral geometric inverse problems dates back to the seminal work of J. Radon
[
34
]
, who introduced the
Radon transform
that integrates a function over hyperplanes in

The central questions of kernel description, inversion, and range characterization are well-studied for these classical transforms
[
38
,
31
]
. In contrast, the
Light Ray Transform (LRT)
, which integrates a function along light rays in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, has received less attention.
The light rays (unit speed) are lines parallel to the direction
(1,\omega)
for a unit vector
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
39
,
35
]
to the best of our knowledge.
In this article, we study
Momentum Light Ray Transform (MLRT)

MLRT as well generalizes the

The light ray transform arises naturally in the study of wave propagation. Solutions of the wave equation, in homogeneous medium, carry singularities that travel at the speed of light along straight paths, often referred to as light rays. To analyze or reconstruct information about the underlying medium, one is therefore led to consider integrals of functions or tensor fields along these trajectories, which is precisely the definition of the light ray transform. This approach was first introduced in the works of Stefanov, Ramm, and Sjöstrand
[
39
,
35
]
, where injectivity of LRT for functions was established.
Subsequently, the works
[
22
,
21
]
extended the LRT to vector fields and symmetric two tensor fields, establishing corresponding injectivity results. The work
[
10
]
studied the LRT for symmetric
m
tensor fields in stationary and static Lorentzian geometries, reducing tensor tomography via LRT to the spatial X-ray transform. A support theorem for the LRT acting on functions was proved in
[
41
]
, while
[
33
]
established a support theorem for the LRT on vector fields. A microlocal analysis of the LRT, viewed as a Fourier integral operator, was developed in
[
26
]
, which established recovery of spacelike singularities in the absence of conjugate points. The use of microlocal analysis within the framework of integral geometry dates back to
[
12
,
11
]
. For further results on parametric constructions and their connections to inverse problems in wave equations and cosmology, see
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
More recently, the study of the LRT has been extended to pseudo-Riemannian settings in
[
15
,
3
]
, highlighting its versatility and deep connections with both geometry and inverse problems.

The momentum ray transform (MRT) was introduced in the works
[
37
,
38
]
, where the kernel of the MRT acting on compactly supported symmetric tensor field distributions was described. A support theorem result for MRT was established in the work
[
1
]
on analytic manifolds. Uniqueness, explicit inversion formulas, and range characterizations of the MRT for Schwartz-class symmetric tensor fields were obtained in
[
23
,
24
]
, while analogous results for partial MRT were studied in
[
28
,
29
]
. More recently, unique continuation properties (UCP) for the MRT were established in
[
2
,
14
]
. In addition,
[
14
]
introduced a fractional version of the MRT for functions and established a UCP in that setting, which was subsequently extended to symmetric tensor fields in
[
19
]
.
A normal operator approach to study MRT was initiated in
[
2
]
, and this perspective was subsequently employed in
[
17
,
18
]
to derive inversion formulas in terms of the MRT’s normal operator.

The study of ray transforms has been strongly motivated by applications in medical imaging, geophysics, and other branches of science. Moreover, ray transforms have emerged as essential tools in addressing Calderón-type problems for second-order PDEs, such as the Schrödinger and Maxwell equations, see
[
42
,
9
,
20
,
43
]
. More recently, MRTs have also been utilized to study inverse problems related to biharmonic and polyharmonic operators, see
[
4
,
36
,
6
,
5
]
. We refer the survey
[
25
]
for more results related to MRT. This naturally suggests that the momentum light ray transforms (MLRTs) may provide a framework for solving inverse problems associated with higher-order wave equations of the form
\square^{2}+\text{(lower order terms)}
, see
[
16
,
7
]
, just as the LRT has played a central role in studying inverse problems for wave equations due to singularity propagation along light rays (null bicharacteristics).
A general result in this direction appears in
[
40
,
30
]
, where the authors investigated inverse problems for real principal type operators (such as the wave and Tricomi operators) and introduced a null bicharacteristics transform for the unknowns and the latter transform was further studied in
[

in the setting of double fibration transform introduced in

2.
Preliminary results

In this section, we collect several preliminary results related to the Light Ray Transform (LRT). Our analysis in this section follows the existing literature, though we highlight and discuss proofs of certain results that involve either mild generalizations or adaptations of previously known arguments.
We begin with the proof of the injectivity of the LRT for functions with
c>0
. Recall that this result was established in
[
39
,
35
]
for the case
c=1
, utilizing the Fourier Slice theorem.
To this end, we recall the definition of partial Fourier transform in the hyperplane
(c,\omega)^{\perp}
as

(\mathcal{F}_{(c,\omega)^{\perp}}g)(\zeta)=\int_{(c,\omega)^{\perp}}g(l)e^{-\mathrm{i}l\cdot\zeta}dH(l),

where
\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1
,
dH
denotes the
n
dimensional Lebesgue measure on
(c,\omega)^{\perp}
.

Lemma 2.1

(Fourier slice theorem,
[
35
]
)
.

For every
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
and each fixed
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

\hat{f}(\zeta)=\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(c,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{0,0}f(-,\omega))(\zeta),\quad\text{whenever}\quad\zeta\perp(c,\omega).

This is a restricted version of the classical Fourier slice theorem. As a corollary one can prove the uniqueness result for functions. Given that,
\tilde{L}^{0,0}f((t,x),\omega)=0
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
and
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\epsilon)
, using Lemma
2.1
, we deduce
\hat{f}(\zeta)=0
for
\zeta\perp(c,\omega)
where
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\epsilon)
. Now varying
\omega
in
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\epsilon)
one can show that the
\{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:\zeta\perp(c,\omega),\,\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\epsilon)\}
has non-empty interior in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
,
n\geq 2
. On the other hand, as
f
is a compactly supported function, the Paley-Wiener theorem implies
\hat{f}
is real analytic. As a result,
\hat{f}=0
everywhere. This completes the proof.

Our next result shows that the highest order MLRT determines all the lower order MLRTs.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
f^{(m)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{m})
, one has

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\omega}\;,\;\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p}\tilde{L}^{m,k}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p}\frac{k!}{(k-p)!}\tilde{L}^{m,k-p}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega),\quad\mbox{for $0\leq p\leq k$}.

Proof.

For
p\leq k
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, the following identity holds

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\omega},\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p}L^{m,k}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\langle\tilde{\omega},\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\langle\tilde{\omega},\nabla_{t,x}\rangle f_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}^{(m)}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})\,ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\langle\tilde{\omega}\;,\;\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\frac{d}{ds}\left[f_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}^{(m)}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\langle\tilde{\omega}\;,\;\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p-1}k\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k-1}f_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}^{(m)}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})\,ds.

We get last equality using integration by parts and the fact that components of
f^{(m)}
are compactly supported. As
p\leq k
, we can continue this process for
k
-times. After performing this process for
p
times, we get

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\omega}\;,\;\nabla_{t,x}\rangle^{p}L^{m,k}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\frac{k!}{(k-p)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k-p}f_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}^{(m)}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p}\frac{k!}{(k-p)!}L^{m,k-p}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega).

This completes the proof.
∎

Now we discuss an important aspect of symmetric tensors, which resembles the trace-free decomposition. This particular decomposition will be useful in the rest of this article. Before we demonstrate the decomposition, let’s define the following operator
J:S^{m}\to S^{m-2},
by

[Jf^{(m)}]_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m-2}}=-c^{2}f^{(m)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}00}+\sum_{p=1}^{n}f^{(m)}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m-2}pp}.

We now state a decomposition result using
J
. A somewhat similar decomposition result can be found in
[

,
31
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that,
f^{(m)}\in S^{m}
for
m\geq 2
. Then,
f^{(m)}
can be decomposed in the following way

f^{(m)}=A^{(m)}+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)},\text{ where }JA^{(m)}=0.

(6)

The proof of this decomposition result is based on the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
f^{(m-2)}\in S^{m-2}
for
m\geq 2
, then the following relations hold.

1.

Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}=D_{m}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}Jf^{(m-2)}+C_{m}f^{(m-2)},

where
D_{m}=\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\binom{m-2}{2},
and
C_{m}=\binom{m}{2}^{-1}(n+m-1)
.

2.

i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}=0\implies f^{(m-2)}=0.

Proof.

For fixed indices
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m-2}=0,\cdots,n
,

\displaystyle\Big[Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\Big]_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}

\displaystyle=-c^{2}\Big[i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\Big]_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}00}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\Big]_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}kk}

\displaystyle=-c^{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\underbrace{\Big[g_{i_{1}i_{2}}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{3}\dots i_{m-2}00}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-1}i_{m}}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}\Big]}_{i_{m-1}=i_{m}=0}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\underbrace{\Big[g_{i_{1}i_{2}}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{3}\dots i_{m-2}kk}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-1}i_{m}}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}\Big]}_{i_{m-1}=i_{m}=k}.

The last term in the first bracket is equal to

-c^{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}g_{00}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}=\binom{m}{2}^{-1}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}.

A similar calculation shows that the last term in each of the other brackets is also equal to

\binom{m}{2}^{-1}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}
.
Therefore, these terms contribute a total of

(n+1)\binom{m}{2}^{-1}f^{(m-2)}
.
Next, we carefully handle the remaining terms in each bracket.
First, consider those terms where
i_{m-1}=i_{m}=0
, so that they remain with
f^{(m-2)}
.
In the first bracket, their contribution is

-c^{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[g_{i_{1}i_{2}}(f^{(m-2)}_{00})_{i_{3}\dots i_{m-2}}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-3}i_{m-2}}(f^{(m-2)}_{00})_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-4}}\Big].

For the other brackets, the same reasoning yields

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[g_{i_{1}i_{2}}(f^{(m-2)}_{kk})_{i_{3}\dots i_{m-2}}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-3}i_{m-2}}(f^{(m-2)}_{kk})_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-4}}\Big].

Together, these two expressions contribute

\binom{m-2}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\big[i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}Jf^{(m-2)}\big]_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}.

Now we are left with the following terms:

\displaystyle-c^{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[g_{i_{1}0}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{2}\dots i_{m-2}0}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-2}0}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-3}0}\Big]+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[g_{i_{1}k}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{2}\dots i_{m-2}k}+\cdots+g_{i_{m-2}k}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-3}k}\Big].

Rewriting, we obtain

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[-c^{2}g_{i_{1}0}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{2}\dots i_{m-2}0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{i_{1}k}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{2}\dots i_{m-2}k}\Big]+\cdots

\displaystyle+\binom{m}{2}^{-1}\Big[-c^{2}g_{i_{m-2}0}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-3}0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{i_{m-2}k}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-3}k}\Big].

By the definition of
g
, in each bracket exactly one term survives, with coefficient
+1
. Hence, the total contribution is

(m-2)\binom{m}{2}^{-1}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-2}}.

Putting everything together, we obtain

Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}=\binom{m-2}{2}\binom{m}{2}^{-1}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}Jf^{(m-2)}+\binom{m}{2}^{-1}(m+n-1)\,f^{(m-2)}.

This completes the proof of the first part.

For the second part, we prove the claim by considering particular choices of indices.
First, let all indices vanish, i.e.
i_{1},\dots,i_{m-2}=0
. Then

\big[i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\big]_{0\cdots 0}=0\;\;\implies\;\;f^{(m-2)}_{0\cdots 0}=0.

Next, allow exactly one index to be nonzero. Without loss of generality, let
i_{1}\neq 0
and all others vanish. Then

\big[i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\big]_{i_{1}0\cdots 0}=0\;\;\implies\;\;f^{(m-2)}_{i_{1}0\cdots 0}=0.

By the symmetry of
f^{(m-2)}
, every component with exactly one nonzero index must vanish. Proceeding inductively, increasing the number of nonzero indices at each step, the same argument shows that all corresponding components vanish. Hence, every component of
f^{(m-2)}
is zero, completing the proof.
∎

With this lemma at hand, we are now ready to present the proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

What follows is intended not merely as a formal proof of the decomposition, but rather as a constructive procedure that explicitly demonstrates how it can be achieved. With this perspective in mind, let us begin with
the assumption that the decomposition (
2.3
) can be done. So we can rewrite
f^{(m)}
in the following form

f^{(m)}=A^{(m)}+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}\Big[\underbrace{A^{(m-2)}+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}A^{(m-4)}+\cdots+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}A^{(m-2k+1)}+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}^{k-1}f^{(m-2k)}}_{B^{(m-2)}}\Big],\text{ where }\,k=[\frac{m}{2}].

We now utilize the condition
JA^{(m)}=0
, which implies
Jf^{(m)}=Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}B^{(m-2)}.
This along with the commutator relation stated in Lemma
2.4
implies

\displaystyle Jf^{(m)}=

\displaystyle Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}B^{(m-2)}=

\displaystyle D_{m}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}JB^{(m-2)}+C_{m}B^{(m-2)}.

Let us consider
C_{m}A^{(m-2)}=Jf^{(m)}
. Note that, for symmetric tensor fields of rank 2 and 3, we are done at this step. Now for
m\geq 4
from above equation we obtain

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle D_{m}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}J\Big[\underbrace{A^{(m-2)}+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}A^{(m-4)}+\cdots+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}^{k-1}f^{(m-2k)}}_{B^{(m-2)}}\Big]+C_{m}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}\Big[\underbrace{A^{(m-4)}+\cdots+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}^{k-2}f^{(m-2k)}}_{B^{(m-4)}}\Big].

Next using the second relation of Lemma
2.4

i.e.,i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}=0\implies f^{(m-2)}=0
from above we conclude that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle D_{m}\,JA^{(m-2)}+D_{m}Ji_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}{B^{(m-4)}}+C_{m}B^{(m-4)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{m}JA^{(m-2)}+D^{\prime}_{m}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}JB^{(m-4)}+C_{m}^{\prime}B^{(m-4)}.

where
D_{m}^{\prime}
and
C_{m}^{\prime}
are some constant depends on
m
and
n
. Next we choose
A^{(m-4)}
in such way that
D_{m}JA^{(m-2)}+C_{m}^{\prime}A^{(m-4)}=0
. Again notice that, at this point we are done for symmetric tensor fields of rank 4 and 5. We shall proceed for
m\geq 6
. This gives

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle D_{m}^{\prime}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}JB^{(m-4)}+C_{m}^{\prime}i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}\Big[\underbrace{A^{(m-6)}+\cdots+i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}^{k-3}f^{(m-2k)}}_{B^{(m-6)}}\Big].

We now iterate this process and apply Lemma

to obtain

until we end up with either

or

depending on whether

, for

and
f^{(0)}
or

in (

), we reach our desired decomposition (

). The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

One might ask how this decomposition differs from the usual trace-free decomposition of a symmetric tensor field, to be specific, whether

3.
Injectivity of MLRT

This section is divided into three parts. First part contains injectivity of MLRT when the direction vectors
\omega
are taken from
(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
. The second part demonstrates the injectivity of MLRT provided the direction vectors vary in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Finally, the third part studies MLRT when the direction vectors are in an open neighborhood of a fixed direction in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For the first case, we prove that the highest moment MLRT of any symmetric tensor field is injective when there is no restriction on the direction vectors. In the second part, MLRT is no longer injective for higher order symmetric tensors. We determine a necessary and sufficient condition for MLRT to be injective when the direction vectors are in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For the last part, we prove that the kernel for MLRT coincides with the kernel in the second case for dimension
n\geq 3
, though for
n=2
they are different.

3.1.
Direction vector in
(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})

Here, we study injectivity results of MLRT for symmetric
m
tensor-fields when the direction vector
\omega
varies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. This allows us to differentiate MLRT with respect to
\omega
. Similar ideas for MRT can be found in
[

]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
,
n\geq 2
,
f^{(m)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{m})
and fix an index
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then the following equality holds

mL^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{p}=\partial_{\omega_{p}}L^{m,k}f^{(m)}-\partial_{x_{p}}L^{m,k+1}f^{(m)}\quad\text{for}\quad k=0,1,\dots,m-1,

(7)

where
(f^{(m)})_{p}
is a symmetric tensor field of rank
(m-1)
defined as follows

((f^{(m)})_{p})_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-1}}=f^{(m)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m-1}p},\quad\mbox{for}\quad i_{1},\cdots,i_{m-1}=0,\cdots,n.

Proof.

For any
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, we have that
\partial_{\omega_{p}}(x+s\omega)=s\,\partial_{x_{p}}(x+s\omega)
. Using this we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega_{p}}L^{m,k}f^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\omega_{p}}\left(\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\,f_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}^{(m)}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{j}}^{p}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\Big((f^{(m)})_{p}\Big)_{i_{1}\dots i_{j-1}i_{j+1}\dots i_{m}}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k+1}\partial_{x_{p}}f^{(m)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds

\displaystyle=mL^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{p}+\partial_{x_{p}}L^{m,k+1}f^{(m)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

For
m=0
, the result is classical, see
[
39
,
35
]
. For
m=1
by assumption, we have that
L^{1,1}f^{(1)}=0
. This along with Lemma
2.2
implies that
L^{1,k}f^{(1)}=0
for

. Next using Lemma
3.1
we obtain

for

. This along with injectivity of the LRT over scalar functions implies
(f^{(1)})_{p}=0
for
1\leq p\leq n
. Inserting this into

entails

, which further gives
(f^{(1)})_{0}=0
. As a result, we have

.

We next argue by induction and assume that the result is true for

. Then for

, by (
7
), we obtain
L^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{p}=0
for

, and

. By induction we have that
(f^{(m)})_{p}=0
for
1\leq p\leq n
. Using the symmetry of

, we have that each element of

is zero except

. This can be achieved using the fact that

and

∎

3.2.
Direction vector restricted on the sphere:

In this subsection, we study the injectivity of MLRT when the direction vector
\omega
varies on the sphere. We see that, the kernel description of MLRT of symmetric tensor fields changes significantly from the case where

. We start with the observation that, symmetric tensor products of a symmetric tensor fields with the Minkowski metric tensor (

) always lie in the kernel. The following lemma describes it properly.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

and

. Then the following equality holds

\tilde{L}^{m,k}\Big(i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}\Big)=0\quad\mbox{for any}\quad f^{(m-2)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{m-2}).

Proof.

Consider
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\begin{split}\tilde{L}^{m,k}(i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)})((t,x),\omega)&=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}(i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds\\
&=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{m}}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{\pi(1)}\cdots i_{\pi(m-2)}}(g_{\frac{1}{c}})_{i_{\pi(m-1)}i_{\pi(m)}}ds\\
&=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{m}}(g_{\frac{1}{c}})_{i_{\pi(m-1)}i_{\pi(m)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}f^{(m-2)}_{i_{\pi(1)}\cdots i_{\pi(m-2)}}ds.\end{split}

(8)

For any fixed

,

will be in

. Without loss of generality assume that,

This gives

Recall that

, this implies

Therefore,

\tilde{L}^{m,k}(i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)})((t,x),\omega)=0.

∎

The above lemma provides proof of the converse parts of both Theorem

Let

\partial^{1}_{\tau}\hat{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(0,\cdot)
, we obtain
\partial_{\tau}\hat{A}(0,x)=0
, for all

. So our statement is true for

.
Now assume that,
\partial^{l}_{\tau}\hat{A}(0,x)

=0
for
l\leq(n_{1}-1)
and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then from (
18
) we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\widehat{\tilde{L}^{m,k}A}(\tau,x,\omega)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}\,e^{\mathrm{i}sc\tau}\,\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\hat{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(0,x+s\omega)\,\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\cdots\tilde{\omega}_{i_{m}}ds.

Using (
11
)– (
17
) for
\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\hat{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(0,\cdot)
, the above integral identity entails
\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\hat{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(0,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus by induction we have
\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\hat{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(0,x)

=0
for all
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
A
is compactly supported in
t
variable, by Paley-Wiener theorem
\hat{A}(\tau,x)
is analytic in
\tau
and
\partial^{n_{1}}_{\tau}\hat{A}(0,x)=0

\hat{A}(\tau,x)=0
for every

. Thus
A(t,x)=0
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. Hence
f^{(m)}=i_{g_{\frac{1}{c}}}f^{(m-2)}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Direction vector near a fixed vector on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
:

So far, we have proved injectivity results for MLRT of symmetric
m
-tensor fields where the direction vectors lie in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
or on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Now, we restrict our focus to the case when the direction vectors come from an open neighborhood in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
n\geq 3
of a fixed unit vector
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Given
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\delta>0
, recall the set

B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)=\{\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:|\omega-\omega_{0}|<\delta\}.

Definition 3.3
.

[
32
]

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set with a smooth boundary denoted by
\Gamma
, and let
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function on
\Gamma
. The tangential gradient of
f
on the surface
\Gamma
is defined by

\nabla_{\Gamma}f\coloneqq\left[\nabla F-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nu}\right)\nu\right]\Big|_{\Gamma},

(19)

where
F
is a smooth extension of
f
onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\nu
is the normal vector to
\Gamma
. This definition is independent of the choice of the extension
F
.

Remark 3.4
.

Since in our situation the embedded sub-manifold is
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the tangential gradient is given by

\nabla_{S}f=\nabla_{\omega}F-(\omega\cdot\nabla_{\omega}F)\omega,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

Note that these tangent vectors are not linearly independent as they satisfy the following relation
\omega\cdot\nabla_{S}=0
.

Let us start the discussion for symmetric 2-tensor fields.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
f^{(2)}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{2})
and
n\geq 2
. If

\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0
, for all
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta),\,(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\,\text{and }\,k=0,1,2,

then the following identity holds

\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i}=-c\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0},\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,n,

(20)

where
(f^{(2)})_{i}=(f^{(2)}_{0i},\dots,f^{(2)}_{ni})
, the i-th column of the associated matrix.

Proof.

We start with taking tangential gradient (in the
\omega
variable) of
\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),-)
over
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
. So the definition of tangential gradient entails

\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=\Big[\nabla_{\omega}L^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)-(\omega\cdot\nabla_{\omega}L^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega))\omega\Big],

(21)

where
L^{m,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),-)
plays the role of extension of
\tilde{L}^{(m,k)}f^{(2)}((t,x),-)
onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\nabla_{\omega}
denotes gradient in
\omega
variables. Thus the
i
-th component of the tangential gradient is

\displaystyle(\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega))_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\omega_{i}}L^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)-\omega_{i}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\partial_{\omega_{j}}L^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)\Big]

\displaystyle=2L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i}((t,x),\omega)+\partial_{x_{i}}L^{2,k+1}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)

\displaystyle-\omega_{i}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}[2L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{j}((t,x),\omega)+\partial_{x_{j}}L^{2,k+1}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)]\Big].

We next observe that

(\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega))=0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\partial_{t}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla_{x}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0,

since
\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0
, for all
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
, and
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\,\text{and }\,k=0,1,2
. This along with preceding equation entails

\displaystyle\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i}((t,x),\omega)=\omega_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{j}((t,x),\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}\sum_{i_{1},j=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\tilde{\omega}_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}f^{(2)}_{i_{1}j}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds-c\,\omega_{i}\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{k}f^{(2)}_{i_{1}0}((t,x)+s\tilde{\omega})ds

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)-c\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}((t,x),\omega)=-c\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}((t,x),\omega).

To obtain the last relation we have again used the fact that
\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0
for
k=0,1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The above lemma provides a relation between MLRT of any column and MLRT of the
0
-th column of the associated matrix. We exploit this relation in the upcoming results. Moreover, we see that a similar relation holds for symmetric
m
-tensors as well.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f^{(2)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{2})
and assume that

\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}((t,x),\omega)=0,\quad\text{for all }\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta),\,(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\,\text{and }k=0,1,2,

then the following identity holds

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}=c(1-n)\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0},\quad\text{for }k=0,1.

(22)

Proof.

From the given hypothesis and Lemma
3.5
, we have

\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})i=-c\,\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0},\quad\text{for $k=0,1$}\quad\mbox{and $1\leq i\leq n$}.

(23)

We now apply the tangential gradient over
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
to both sides of this relation. To do so, we compute the tangential gradient of the left-hand side and the right-hand side separately, and then compare the results.
We now apply the tangential gradient on the L.H.S of (
23
) and deduce that

\displaystyle(\nabla_{S}(\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i})_{p}

\displaystyle=\Big[\partial_{\omega_{p}}(L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i})-\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\partial_{\omega_{j}}(L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle=\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ip}+\partial_{x_{p}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{i})-\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}[\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}+\partial_{x_{j}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{i})]

\displaystyle=\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ip}-\omega_{i}\partial_{x_{p}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{0})-\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}[\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}-\omega_{i}\partial_{x_{j}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{0})].

Furthermore, choosing
p=i
in above and taking sum over
i=1,\cdots,n
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla_{S}(\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i})_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}.

(24)

Similarly applying
\nabla_{S}
on the R.H.S of (
23
) we obtain

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(\nabla_{S}\,(c\,\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}))_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\Big[\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\partial_{\omega_{j}}(\omega_{i}L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0})-\partial_{\omega_{p}}(\omega_{i}L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\Big[\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}[\delta_{i}^{j}L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\omega_{i}\partial_{\omega_{j}}(L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0})\Big]

\displaystyle-\Big[\delta_{i}^{p}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\omega_{i}\partial_{\omega_{p}}(L^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\Big[\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\delta_{j}^{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}-\delta_{i}^{p}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{0j}-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{0p}

\displaystyle+\omega_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{0})-\omega_{i}\partial_{x_{p}}(\tilde{L}^{1,k+1}(f^{(2)})_{0})\Big].

Next choosing
p=i
and summing over

we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla_{S}\,(c\,\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}))_{i}=c(1-n)\,\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}.

(25)

We next equate (
24
) and (
25
) and conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}=c(1-n)\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0},\quad\text{ for }k=0,1.

(26)

This completes the proof.
∎

Step 2.
The case of

In this case we first assume that

\displaystyle c^{2}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{00}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}=c(1-n)\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0},\quad\text{for }k=0,1.

\displaystyle 0=\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-c^{2}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{00}+2c\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\,\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}.

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\,\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{ij}-c^{2}\tilde{L}^{0,k}(f^{(2)})_{00}=-2c\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{0}).

(f^{(2)})_{i}=0

.

We now drop the assumption

Step 3.
The case of

We argue by induction on

\tilde{L}^{m,k}f^{(m)}((t,x),\omega)=0

4.
Inversion formula for MLRT of symmetric tensor fields

In the proofs of Theorems
2
and
3
, we discussed the injectivity of symmetric tensor fields and observed that a vector field and the trace part of a symmetric tensor field (of rank
\geq 2
) can be recovered from the MLRTs. In this section, we provide an algorithmic way to reconstruct vector fields and trace free higher order symmetric tensor fields from its momentum light ray transforms.

4.1.
Reconstruction of vector fields:

We now proceed to derive the Fourier slice theorem for LRT [
\tilde{L}^{1,0}
] and MLRT [
\tilde{L}^{1,1}
]. For
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
, the Fourier transform of
f
at
\zeta
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}f(\zeta)=\hat{f}(\zeta)
. The Fourier transform of
f
on a hyperplane through the origin, say
(1,\omega)^{\perp}
, is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}f
. To this end, we denote
\tilde{\omega}=(1,\omega)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(
Fourier slice theorem for vector fields
):
For any
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, every
f^{(1)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{1})
, must satisfy

\displaystyle(1,\omega)\cdot\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)=

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\omega,\zeta):=\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{1,0}f^{(1)}(-,\omega))(\zeta),\quad\mbox{for any }\zeta\perp(1,\omega);

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{j}(\tilde{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\zeta})\widehat{f^{(1)}_{j}}(\zeta)=

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(\omega,\zeta):=2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{1,1}f^{(1)}(-,\omega))(\zeta),\quad\mbox{for any }\zeta\perp(1,\omega).

(33)

Proof.

The identity (
32
) is nothing but restricted version of the classical Fourier Slice Theorem for vector fields. The following steps demonstrate the proof of (
32
). For a fixed
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\displaystyle(1,\omega)\cdot\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}}f^{(1)}_{i_{1}}(t,x)e^{-\mathrm{i}(t,x)\cdot\zeta}\,dtdx

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\int_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{(1)}_{i_{1}}(l+r(1,\omega)dr\Big]e^{-\mathrm{i}l\cdot\zeta}dl

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}\Big(\tilde{L}^{1,0}f^{(1)}(-,\omega)\Big)(\zeta),\quad\text{ for}\,\zeta\perp(1,\omega).

In order to obtain the relation (
33
), we observe that, for any
\zeta\perp(1,\omega)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(\omega,\zeta)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{1,1}f^{(1)}(-,\omega))(\zeta)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\int_{\tilde{\omega}^{\perp}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}sf^{(1)}_{i_{1}}(l+s\tilde{\omega})e^{-\mathrm{i}l\cdot\zeta}\,dsdl

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}}(t,x)\cdot\tilde{\omega}f^{(1)}_{i_{1}}(t,x)e^{-\mathrm{i}(t,x)\cdot\zeta}\,dtdx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{n}\tilde{\omega}_{i_{1}}(\tilde{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\zeta})\widehat{f^{(1)}_{j}}(\zeta).

This completes the proof for (
33
).
∎

These two equalities [(
32
), (
33
)] play an important role in the reconstruction of
f^{(1)}
. We see that,
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
can be recovered in some open set in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
even if we have partial data of MLRTs of
f^{(1)}
.
Before establishing Theorem
4
, we first examine the corresponding result for vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. Subsequently, we will identify the necessary modifications required for vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. Recall that,
H_{n}
is defined as follows

H_{n}:=\{\zeta=(\zeta_{0},\zeta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:|\zeta_{0}|<|\zeta^{\prime}|,\quad\zeta\perp(1,\omega)\quad\text{for some }\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)\}.

Let us present an algorithmic way to calculate
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
on
H_{n}
from
\tilde{L}^{1,k}f^{(1)}((t,x),\omega)
in order to complete the reconstruction.

Reconstruction Algorithm for
m=1
and
n\geq 3
:

Step 1:

Given
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
, and
\zeta\in H_{n}
such that
\zeta\perp(1,\omega)
, calculate

\Phi_{1}(\omega,\zeta):=\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{1,0}f^{(1)}(-,\omega))(\zeta),\qquad\Phi_{2}(\omega,\zeta):=2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\tilde{L}^{1,1}f^{(1)}(-,\omega))(\zeta),

where
\mathcal{F}_{(1,\omega)^{\perp}}(\cdot)
denotes the partial Fourier transform on the hyperplane
(1,\omega)^{\perp}
.

Step 2:

Given
\zeta=(\zeta_{0},\zeta^{\prime})\in(1,\omega)^{\perp}
, find a rotation matrix
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

R\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{|\zeta^{\prime}|}\right)=e_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R(\omega)=(b_{1},b_{2},0,\cdots,0)=\mathrm{w}_{1}.

Construct the
(1+n)\times(1+n)
matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}
.

Step 3:

Set
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta-|\omega-\omega_{0}|\right)
. For any
\varphi_{i}\in(-\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
, set
\mathrm{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
for
i=1,\cdots,n
as in (
35
) for
n=3
and (
39
) for
n>3
.
Note that
\mathrm{w}_{i}
satisfies
(M\zeta)\perp(1,\mathrm{w}_{i})
, and
\{\mathrm{w}_{i}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
is linearly independent.

Step 4:

Use Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to find a
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
A
orthonormalizing

\{(1,\mathrm{w}_{i}):i=1,\cdots,n\}
.

Step 5:

Compute the vector
\Phi_{3}(\zeta)
and the scalar
\Phi_{4}(\omega,\zeta)

\displaystyle\Phi_{3}(\zeta):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\Phi_{1}(R^{-1}\mathrm{w}_{j},\zeta)[A^{T}A]_{kj}(1,\mathrm{w}_{k})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{4}(\omega,\zeta):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{j}[M^{-1}\Phi_{3}(\zeta)]_{i}.

(34)

Step 6:

Finally, calculate

\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}[\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{4}]\zeta-M^{-1}\Phi_{3}
, for all
\zeta\in H_{n}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that
f^{(1)}

\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+3};S^{1})
,
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\delta>0
small enough. If
\tilde{L}^{1,k}f^{(1)}((t,x),\omega)
is known for
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
,
\omega\in B_{3}(\omega_{0},\delta)
and
k=0,1
, then
\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)
is known for
\zeta\in H_{3}
.

Proof.

Choose
\zeta=(\zeta_{0},\zeta^{{}^{\prime}})\in H_{3}
, where
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3})
.
That is,
\zeta\perp(1,\omega)
, for some
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
satisfying
|\omega-\omega_{0}|<\delta
for some fixed
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\delta>0
.
Choose
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\eta\perp\{\omega,\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}\}
.
Take
R
to be the rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which takes
\eta\to e_{3}
and
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}\to|\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}|e_{2}
, where
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
If
R\omega=b=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
then the condition
\eta\perp\omega
implies that
b_{3}=0
. Again, the condition
\zeta\perp(1,\omega)
implies
\zeta_{0}+|\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}|b_{2}=0
.
For
i=1,2,3
, let us choose

\mathrm{w}_{i}=(b_{1}\cos\varphi_{i},b_{2},b_{1}\sin\varphi_{i}),\quad\mbox{where }\varphi_{1}=0,\quad\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}\in(-\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}).

(35)

After the rotation, (
32
) can be written as

(1,p)\cdot g(\xi)=\Phi_{1}(R^{-1}p,M^{-1}\xi),\quad\mbox{where }M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi=M\zeta=(\zeta_{0},|\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}|e_{2}),\quad

(36)

with
g(\xi)=M\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)
and
p\in B_{3}(b,\delta)
such that
(1,p)\perp\xi
.

Notice that,
(1,\mathrm{w}_{i})
is close to
(1,b)=(1,R\omega)
and
\omega
is close to
\omega_{0}
, which implies
(1,\mathrm{w}_{i})
is close to
(1,R\omega_{0})
. Therefore, by the choice of
\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
, we can choose
p=\mathrm{w}_{i}
in the (
32
).
Denote
\xi^{\perp}
by
V
. Note that
\{(1,\mathrm{w}_{i}):i=1,2,3\}
is contained in
V
using the fact that
\zeta_{0}+b_{2}|\zeta^{\prime}|=0
, and they are linearly independent, so they form a basis for
V
.
Assume that,
\{\mu_{i}:i=1,2,3\}
is any orthonormal basis for
V
.
Let
A=[a_{ij}]_{4\times 4}
be the basis change matrix fixes
\xi
and changes the basis of
V
from
\{(1,\mathrm{w}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{3}
to
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
. That is, we have

A\xi=\xi,\quad\mu_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}(1,\mathrm{w}_{j}),\quad\mbox{for }i=1,2,3.

(37)

Hence,
g(\xi)
can be re-written as

g(\xi)=c_{0}\xi+\sum_{i=1}^{3}c_{i}\mu_{i},\quad\mbox{where}\quad c_{i}=g(\xi)\cdot\mu_{i}.

So, (
36
) implies

c_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}(g(\xi)\cdot(1,\mathrm{w}_{j}))=\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}\Phi_{1}(R^{-1}\mathrm{w}_{j},M^{-1}\xi).

Using this form of
c_{i}
in the expression of
g(\xi)
, we get

\displaystyle M\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)=

\displaystyle g(\xi)=c_{0}\xi+\sum_{j,k}\Phi_{1}(R^{-1}\mathrm{w}_{j},M^{-1}\xi)[A^{T}A]_{kj}(1,\mathrm{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\implies\quad\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)=

\displaystyle c_{0}\zeta+M^{-1}\Phi_{3},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\Phi_{3}=\sum_{j,k}\Phi_{1}(R^{-1}\mathrm{w}_{j},\zeta)[A^{T}A]_{kj}(1,\mathrm{w}_{k}).

Now substituting form of
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
in (
33
), we see

c_{0}(\omega,\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}[\Phi_{2}(\omega,\zeta)-\Phi_{4}(\omega,\zeta)],\quad\mbox{where}\quad\Phi_{4}=\sum_{p,i}\tilde{\omega}_{p}\tilde{\omega}_{i}\partial_{p}[M^{-1}\Phi_{3}]_{i}.

Therefore, varying
\zeta\in H_{3}
we have

\widehat{f^{(1)}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}[\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{4}]\zeta-M^{-1}\Phi_{3},\quad\forall\zeta\in H_{3}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The complete recovery of the vector field
f^{(1)}
proceeds through the following sequence of steps. Since the components of the vector field
f^{(1)}
are compactly supported, the Paley-Wiener theorem implies that the components of
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
are analytic. Proposition
4.2
ensures that
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
is known in the open set
H_{3}
(contains space-like vectors). By analytic continuation, this determines
\widehat{f^{(1)}}
uniquely on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. Applying the inverse Fourier transform then yields the reconstruction of
f^{(1)}
.

Remark 4.3
.

We want to highlight the fact that, in the proof, there might be several choices for
\{\mu_{i}:i=0,1,2,3\}
(orthonormal basis for
V=\xi^{\perp}
). For instance, Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of
\{(1,\omega_{i}):i=0,1,2\}
provides us with a choice.

Now we see the reconstruction of vector fields defined on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.

.

4.2.
Reconstruction of symmetric
m
-tensor fields:

At the heart of this sub-section lies proof of the Theorem
5
. We approach the proof using the method of induction on rank of the tensor field,
m\,(\geq 2)
. Trace free decomposition of a symmetric 2-tensor field
f^{(2)}
allows us to write

f^{(2)}=A^{(2)}+i_{g}f^{(0)},

where
g=\diag(-1,1,\dots,1)
;
A^{(2)}
is symmetric 2-tensor satisfying
j_{g}A^{(2)}=-A^{(2)}_{00}+\sum_{p=1}^{n}A^{(2)}_{pp}=0
and
f^{(0)}
is a complex valued function. We have already seen,
\tilde{L}^{2,k}(i_{g}f^{(0)})=0
.
So, we cannot recover the trace part using MLRTs. Recovery can be done for trace-free part only. We use the tangential derivative to get MLRTs of a 1-tensor field [ restricting one index in the 2-tensor field ].

Proposition 4.5
.

For
n\geq 3
, assume that
f^{(2)}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};S^{2})
satisfies
j_{g}f^{(2)}=0
. For a fixed
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\delta>0
, if
\tilde{L}^{2,k}f^{(2)}

((t,x),\omega)
is known for
k=0,1,2
,
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
,
\omega\in B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
then we can recover
\widehat{f^{(2)}}
in the open set
H_{n}
.

Proof.

Let’s calculate tangential gradient of
\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}(-,\omega)
on
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
. The
i
-th component of the tangential gradient of
\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}
can be written as

(\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)})_{i}=2\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{i}+\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{2,1}f^{(2)}-\omega_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}[2\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{j}+\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{2,1}f^{(2)}],\text{ [see Lemma \ref{lemma2.7}]}.

We rewrite the above equation in the following way

\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{i}=-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}-\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)},

(40)

where
\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{2,1}f^{(2)}-\omega_{i}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{2,1}f^{(2)}-(\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)})_{i}\Big]+\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}
, contains known terms only. In the notation
\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)}
, the superscript
(0)
indicates that we start with zero moment, while
(i)
specifies that we are considering the
i
-th component of the tangential gradient.
Again take tangential gradient over
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)
in both sides of (
40
). Considering the
i
-th component, we get the following

\displaystyle\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\omega_{i}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ij}+\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{i}-\omega_{i}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{i0}+\omega_{i}^{2}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{0j}

\displaystyle-\omega_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\omega_{i}^{2}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\omega_{i}^{2}\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}-(\nabla_{S}\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})_{i}.

Note that, in the above deduction, we have used the result
\partial_{\omega_{i}}L^{1,0}f^{(2)}=L^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\partial_{x_{i}}L^{1,1}f^{(2)}
.
Taking sum over
i
on both sides in the above equation, we get

\displaystyle(1-n)\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}(\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})]_{i}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ij}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{i}-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{i}.

Now we shall use (
40
) in the above equation to get

\displaystyle(1-n)\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}(\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ij}+\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}[-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}]

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}[-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{1,1}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}].

That is

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ij}+(n-1)\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}(\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})]_{i}-\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}+

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}.

The condition
j_{g}f^{(2)}=0
gives us
(f^{(2)})_{00}=\sum_{p=1}^{n}(f^{(2)})_{pp}
. Notice that,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{ij}=\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}-2\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}+\tilde{L}^{0,0}(f^{(2)})_{00}.

Using these two equalities, from the above equation we get

\displaystyle 2\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}-\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}+(n-1)\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}(\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})]_{i}-\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}+\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}

\displaystyle\implies\tilde{L}^{1,0}(f^{(2)})_{0}=

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}^{(0)},

where
\Psi_{2}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{(n+1)}[\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}(\Psi_{1}^{(0),(i)})]_{i}-\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}+\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\Psi_{1}^{(1),(i)}+\tilde{L}^{2,0}f^{(2)}]
, contains known terms only. In the notation
\Psi_{2}^{(0)}
, the superscript
(0)
denotes that we are working with zero moment (
\tilde{L}^{2,0}\,)
.

So, the knowledge of LRT of symmetric
2
-tensors is now reduced to the LRT of a vector field.
Similarly, if we proceed with the 1st moment,
\tilde{L}^{2,1}
, then we end up with MLRT of the
0
-th column of
f^{(2)}
.
The relation (
40
) helps to get
\tilde{L}^{1,k}(f^{(2)})_{i}
for
k=0,1
and for any
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Therefore, using the reconstruction of vector fields Theorem
4
, we get the Fourier transform of these vector fields,
(f^{(2)})_{i}
for
i=0,1,\dots,n
in some open set in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.
As we have started with compactly supported components of
f^{(2)}
,
\widehat{f^{(2)}}
can be extended to
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
uniquely, using analytic continuation.
Therefore, we can recover
f^{(2)}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We use the method of induction on
m\geq 2
, the rank of symmetric tensor fields. For

4.5

We start with

m=2
in Proposition
4.5

i

\tilde{L}^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{i}=-\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{0}+\Psi_{1}^{(m),(k),(i)},\text{ for }k=0,\dots,(m-1),

(41)

where

\Psi_{1}^{(m),(k),(i)}=\frac{1}{m}\Big[\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{L}^{m,k+1}f^{(m)}-\omega_{i}\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{L}^{m,k+1}f^{(m)}-(\nabla_{S}\tilde{L}^{m,k}f^{(m)})_{i}\Big]+\omega_{i}\tilde{L}^{m,k}f^{(m)}.

Again taking tangential gradient over
B_{n}(\omega_{0},\delta)

\displaystyle(m-1)\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{L}^{m-2,k}(f^{(m)})_{ii}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\tilde{L}^{m-2,k}(f^{(m)})_{ij}\Big]

\displaystyle+\Big[\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\Psi_{1}^{(m),(k),(i)}-\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\Psi_{1}^{(m),(k),(i)}\Big]

\displaystyle=(1-n)\tilde{L}^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{0}+\sum_{i}[\nabla_{S}\Psi_{1}^{(m),(k),(i)}]_{i}.

Now using the condition

\displaystyle\tilde{L}^{m-1,k}(f^{(m)})_{0}
