Criticality as a Universal Thermodynamic Requirement for Perfect Intrinsic Superconducting Diodes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Pavan Hosur

Abstract
Superconducting diodes promise dissipation-less rectification, yet intrinsic platforms invariably have very low efficiencies. We reveal a fundamental thermodynamic origin of this behavior that is independent of microscopic details. Denoting ϵ = I c − / I c + \epsilon=I_{c}^{-}/I_{c}^{+} , where I c ± I_{c}^{\pm} are critical current magnitudes in opposite directions with I c + > I c − I_{c}^{+}>I_{c}^{-} by convention, we show that ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 is impossible without fine-tuning, while ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 can occur but only upon tuning to a critical point within the superconducting state. Away from such internal instabilities, using general Landau theory, we derive a lower bound on ϵ \epsilon that limits intrinsic diode performance. We illustrate these ideas in a minimal superconductor-Ising model, where the strong nonreciprocity can be seen explicitly. In particular, if the internal transition is continuous, we show that the scaling of ϵ \epsilon near the transition is locked to known critical exponents.



Recent years have seen an explosive resurgence of interest in superconducting diodes (SDs)
(
Ma et al. 2025
;
Nadeem et al. 2023
)
. SDs are defined
by unequal critical current magnitudes
I_{c}^{\pm}
in opposite
directions. They offer the enticing prospect of rectification and nonreciprocal signal processing without dissipation, and are natural building blocks for superconducting logic, memory, and neuromorphic elements. As superconducting electronics moves beyond SQUIDs and conventional Josephson-junction circuits, understanding which material platforms can host highly efficient, scalable SDs has become an important design problem.

Assuming without loss of generality that
I_{c}^{+}>I_{c}^{-}
, the quality of the SD can be quantified via the diode asymmetry

\epsilon=\frac{I_{c}^{-}}{I_{c}^{+}}\in[0,1],

(1)

so that perfect diode behavior corresponds to
\epsilon=0
. The diode coefficient

\eta=(1-\epsilon)/(1+\epsilon)
,
so
\epsilon\ll 1
is equivalent to
\eta\approx 1
. Most intrinsic platforms report

\eta\sim 10^{-2}
, and only a few experiments have achieved
|\eta|\gtrsim 0.3

(
Bauriedl et al. 2022
;
Pal et al. 2022
;
Tanaka et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Castellani et al. 2025
;
Ingla-Aynés et al. 2024
;
Chiles et al. 2023
)
.
Achieving
\epsilon\ll 1
or
\eta\approx 1
in a controlled and reproducible manner therefore remains a central goal of the field.

A SD response generically appears when breaking time-reversal, inversion, and any spatial symmetry that exchanges “left” and “right” are broken. While creating an SD is relatively easy – indeed, extraneous factors such as irregular sample geometry, stray vortices, or local inhomogeneity can produce an observable effect – a far subtler questions remains: when can an intrinsic superconducting phase exhibit a large SD effect rather than merely a nonzero one? Existing proposals typically rely on specific microscopic mechanisms such as Rashba spin-orbit coupling, Zeeman fields, altermagnetism, finite-momentum pairing, and engineered junction geometries, and the enormous parameter space obscures which ingredients truly matter. Meanwhile, extrinsic factors such as sample geometry, inhomogeneity, or mesoscopic Josephson physics can induce large efficiencies, complicating interpretation. These challenges motivate a complementary, mechanism-agnostic viewpoint: are there general constraints on
\epsilon
that hold independently of microscopic details?

In this work, we identify a fundamental connection between critical phenomena and the efficiency of intrinsic SDs, i.e., SDs not relying on Josephson junctions or proximity effects. Our main result is that general thermodynamic principles impose stringent lower bounds on
\epsilon
in bulk superconductors, and achieving
\epsilon\ll 1
requires tuning to a phase transition
within
the superconducting state. Moreover,
\epsilon
can vanish only asymptotically as one approaches the critical point; exact
\epsilon=0
without fine-tuning would require a free-energy discontinuity (sometimes dubbed a zeroth-order phase transition) that cannot occur in conventional solids. When the internal phase transition is continuous, the scaling of
\epsilon
near is locked to conventional critical exponents.

These results explain why invariably
\eta\ll 1
or
\epsilon\sim O(1)
in existing intrinsic SDs. This is significant because scalable superconducting electronics will likely need alternatives to Josephson junctions, which rely on magnetic fluxes, are challenging to miniaturize, and typically have lower
T_{c}
than the constituent superconductors. They also suggest that unusually low
\epsilon
may itself serve as a sensitive diagnostic of nearby internal structure in the superconducting phase.

Constraints on
\epsilon
:
Intrinsic SDs are described by a Cooper-pair momentum (
q
)-dependent condensation energy density
F(q)
that yields superconductivity when negative. The supercurrent density is

j(q)=-2e\,\partial_{A}F(q-2eA)\big|_{A=0}=2e\partial_{q}F(q)

(2)

whose largest positive and negative magnitudes are the critical current densities
j_{c}^{\pm}
:

j_{c}^{\pm}=\max_{q}\left[\pm j(q)\right]

(3)

and
\epsilon=j_{c}^{-}/j_{c}^{+}
. For a quasi-1D system, this is equivalent to
\epsilon=I_{c}^{-}/I_{c}^{+}
.

First, we show that
\epsilon
can vanish only asymptotically; exactly
\epsilon=0
is impossible. To see this, suppose
j_{c}^{-}=0
and
j_{c}^{+}\neq 0
. This implies
j(q)\geq 0

\forall q
and
j(q)>0
strictly for at least some subinterval within the superconducting regime defined by
q\in(q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+})
, where
F(q)<0
. For simplicity, we assume a single contiguous range of
q
where superconductivity exists. Systems with reentrant superconductivity can generically have multiple disconnected ranges, and the arguments in this paper extend trivially to such cases.

Now, we must have
F(q_{c}^{+})=F(q_{c}^{-})=0
for
F(q)
to be continuous and vanish outside the superconducting regime of
q
. On the other hand,
j(q)>0
on s finite range within
(q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+})
implies

F(q_{c}^{+})-F(q_{c}^{-})=\frac{1}{2e}\int_{q_{c}^{-}}^{q_{c}^{+}}\frac{dq}{2\pi}\,j(q)>0

(4)

which contradicts
F(q_{c}^{\pm})=0
. Thus,
\epsilon=0
– equivalently
j(q)\geq 0~\forall q
– forces
F(q)
to have a discontinuity; see Fig
1
(left). Such
zeroth order
phase transitions have indeed been found or proposed in some systems that violate conventional thermodynamics via nonlocal interactions
(
Maslov 2004
)
, absence of ergodicity
(
Hou 2021
)
, or involvement of gravity
(
Altamirano et al. 2014a
;
Altamirano et al. 2013
;
Altamirano et al. 2014b
;
Wei et al. 2016
;
Frassino et al. 2014
;
Zou et al. 2014
;
Jahani Poshteh et al. 2013
;
Hennigar and Mann 2015
;
Gunasekaran et al. 2012
;
Kubizňák and Simovic 2016
;
Dehyadegari et al. 2017
;
Dehyadegari and Sheykhi 2018
)
. In intrinsic superconductors that respect locality and have extensive energy and entropy,
F(q)
must be continuous, so such phase transitions and hence
\epsilon=0
are impossible.

F(q)

Next, we ask, ”can one obtain a perfect SD as a limit
\epsilon\to 0
?” This limit is permitted by standard thermodynamics as it allows
j(q)
to have vanishingly small negative values which can cancel the positive contributions in (
4
) and ensure
F(q_{c}^{\pm})=0
.
However, we prove that it necessarily implies non-analyticity of
F(q)
within the superconducting regime, indicating an internal phase transition. The proof is easiest by contradiction and builds on the argument above for the impossibility of
\epsilon=0
.

Consider the family of analytic functions parametrized by
\lambda
,
F_{\lambda}(q)
, for
q\in\left(q_{c}^{-}(\lambda),q_{c}^{+}(\lambda)\right)
. By (
2
),
j(q)
must be analytic as well and generically produce a diode asymmetry
\epsilon(\lambda)
. Now, suppose
\epsilon(\lambda)\to 0
as
\lambda
tends to some value
\lambda_{0}
. Then, the negative segments of
j_{\lambda}(q)
vanish in the limit, i.e.,
j_{\lambda_{0}}(q)=0
for finite segments of
q
. By the identity theorem for analytic functions, such a function cannot be analytic unless
j_{\lambda_{0}}(q)=0
identically
\forall q
. Thus,
j_{\lambda_{0}}(q)
and hence,
F_{\lambda_{0}}(q)
must have non-analyticities in the interval
q\in\left(q_{c}^{-}(\lambda_{0}),q_{c}^{+}(\lambda_{0})\right)
, ass illustrated in Fig.
1
(right). Physically, this means phase transitions occur within the superconducting regime between different superconducting states.

A natural follow-up question is, ”how small can
\epsilon
be if one is close to – but not exactly at – the relevant phase transition?” To answer this, we adopt the standard Landau prescription of modeling
F(q)
as a finite-degree polynomial in
q
over the interval
q\in(q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+})
. Polynomial functions obey strong rigidity constraints: a polynomial that is small over a finite subinterval cannot be arbitrarily large elsewhere on that interval. This idea is solidified in Appendix
A
using the Remez inequality. The upshot is a lower bound on
\epsilon
:

\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{T_{n}\!\left(\frac{1+\theta}{1-\theta}\right)},

(5)

where
T_{n}(x)=\cosh[n\cosh^{-1}(x)]
is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree
n
in the range
x>1
, and

\theta\equiv\frac{\text{range of }q\text{ over which }j(q)>0}{\text{total superconducting range }(q_{c}^{+}-q_{c}^{-})}\in(0,1).

(6)

For example, if
F(q)
has a single minimum at
q=q_{0}\in(q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+})
where
j(q_{0})=0
, then
\theta=(q_{0}-q_{c}^{-})/(q_{c}^{+}-q_{c}^{-})
. Conversely, (
5
) implies a bound on
n
:

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\cosh^{-1}(1/\epsilon)}{\cosh^{-1}(1+2\theta)}

(7)

\displaystyle\gtrsim C(\theta)\log(2/\epsilon)\qquad\text{ for }\epsilon\ll 1.

where
C(\theta)
is an
O(1)
function of
\theta
. Thus, achieving effect with
\epsilon\ll 1
requires a polynomial of parametrically large degree that grows logarithmically with
1/\epsilon
. In particular,
\epsilon\to 0
demands
n\to\infty
. Physically, this corresponds to a Landau theory capable of describing a large number – up to
\sim n/2
– of competing superconducting phases with distinct Cooper-pair momenta, each associated with a local minimum of
F(q)
.

We stress that the above structures are agnostic to the microscopic details. They do not assume a particular band structure or pairing symmetry, and apply to any superconducting state – including topological, multiband and nodal ones – whose long-wavelength dynamics is controlled by a single gauge-invariant phase mode with a well-defined free energy
F(q)
.

Ising-coupled superconductor:
To make this structure concrete, we consider a simple, exactly solvable toy model that realizes the required non-analyticity. Specifically, consider a superconductor coupled to an Ising variable
\sigma=\pm 1
, with free energy

F_{\sigma}(q)=-F_{0}+\frac{K_{\sigma}}{2}\,q^{2}-\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}q.

(8)

where
K_{\sigma}>0
are left/right stiffnesses and
\gamma_{\sigma}>0
are linear magnetoelectric couplings. Minimizing over
\sigma
at each

q
selects the lower branch and produces a piecewise condensation free energy

\displaystyle F(q)=\begin{cases}-\!F_{0}+\dfrac{K_{-}}{2}q^{2}+\gamma_{-}q,&q<0,\\[4.0pt]
-\!F_{0}+\dfrac{K_{+}}{2}q^{2}-\gamma_{+}q,&q>0,\end{cases}

(9)

with a cusp at
q=0
reflecting the switch of the internal Ising configuration. We assume the cusp is at
q=0
for simplicity; in general, it can occur at non-zero
q
and momentum must be measured relative that point. Superconductivity exists when
F(q)<0
or
q\in(q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+})
where
q_{c}^{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{K_{\pm}}\left(\gamma_{\pm}+\sqrt{\gamma_{\pm}^{2}+2K_{\pm}F_{0}}\right)
, and Cooper pairs in the two superconducting phases carry momenta
q_{*}^{\pm}=\pm\gamma_{\pm}/K_{\pm}
where
F(q)
has local minima. The corresponding supercurrent,

j(q)=2e\,\partial_{q}F(q)=\begin{cases}2e(K_{-}q+\gamma_{-}),&q_{c}^{-}<q<0,\\[3.0pt]
2e(K_{+}q-\gamma_{+}),&0<q<q_{c}^{+},\end{cases}

(10)

vanishes at
q_{*}^{\pm}
and has a discontinuity of
2e(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})
at
q=0
. The model clearly has a
q
-dependent transition, which is first (second) order if
\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-}\neq 0\quad(=0)
as reflected by the behavior of
j(q)
at
q=0
. However, this transition is an incidental property of the exactly solvable model. The key feature that enables a perfect SD with
\epsilon\to 0
is the presence of competing phases and hence, the possibility of a transition between them.

The conditions for a perfect SD are determined by the critical currents
j_{c}^{\pm}=\max[\pm j(0^{\mp}),\pm j(q_{c}^{\pm})]

j_{c}^{\pm}=2e\max\left(\gamma_{\mp},\sqrt{\gamma_{\pm}^{2}+2K_{\pm}F_{0}}\right),

(11)

Clearly, (
11
) shows that
\epsilon=j_{c}^{-}/j_{c}^{+}\to 0
requires
\gamma_{-}
,
\gamma_{+}
and
K_{-}
to all vanish, so the ‘-’ branch becomes essentially
q
-independent, while the right branch retains a finite stiffness
K_{+}
. In other words, the unfavorable branch becomes parametrically soft, allowing its critical current to vanish while the opposite branch remains stable.

More generally,
\epsilon\to 0
requires
F(q)
to become nearly flat over a large interval of
q
while a small interval retains a non-zero positive slope. Since a flat region can easily be nudged into a shape with a shallow local minimum corresponding to a metastable phase, a vanishing
\epsilon
can be viewed as reversing the nudge, i.e., the loss of metastability.

The findings of Refs.
(
Yuan and Fu 2022
;
Shaffer et al. 2024
;
Chakraborty and Black-Schaffer 2025
)
that near-perfect diode behavior can occur when superconducting states with distinct Cooper-pair momenta compete provide concrete realizations of the mechanism identified here. In each case, tuning parameters can bring the system close to a regime where one current direction approaches an instability of a metastable superconducting branch, causing the corresponding critical current to become parametrically small while the opposite-direction critical current remains finite. Among these realizations, the Rashba-Zeeman setting of Ref.
Yuan and Fu 2022
is currently the most experimentally relevant, since such effects are common in superconducting heterostructures and thin films, although experiments are far from the asymptotic
\epsilon\to 0
regime.

Critical scaling:

At a continuous internal phase transition within the superconducting state, the diode ratio
\epsilon
may vanish asymptotically upon tuning a control parameter
r
to a critical value
r_{c}
. We define the nonreciprocity exponent
x
via

\epsilon\propto|r-r_{c}|^{x}.

(12)

Importantly, the existence of an internal transition is necessary but not sufficient for
\epsilon\to 0
; the scaling behavior discussed here applies only along tuning directions in parameter space where the unfavorable-direction critical current is driven to zero while the opposite-direction critical current remains finite.

Within the general mechanism identified in this work, the
x
is controlled by the slowest vanishing scale associated with the loss of analyticity of
F(q)
. Equivalently, it is set by the softest mode governing the internal restructuring of the superconducting state that suppresses
I_{c}^{-}
. As a result,
x
is not an independent critical exponent but is determined by conventional critical exponents characterizing the internal transition.

This structure is illustrated by the Ising-coupled superconductor discussed above, where strong nonreciprocity arises when one superconducting branch becomes progressively softer as the transition is approached. In that model, if criticality is reached as

\gamma_{\pm}\sim|r-r_{c}|^{a_{\pm}},\qquad K_{-}\sim|r-r_{c}|^{b},

(13)

with
a_{\pm},b>0
, then the diode ratio scales as

x=\min(a_{+},a_{-},b/2),

(14)

reflecting the slowest vanishing scale that controls the suppression of
I_{c}^{-}
. While this example admits an interpretation in terms of metastability loss, the scaling form itself does not rely on this specific mechanism and applies generically to continuous internal transitions that asymmetrically suppress the critical currents.

Experimental implications:

Our results inform the interpretation of existing and near-term experiments on intrinsic SDs. Intrinsic platforms studied to date typically exhibit
\epsilon=O(1)
, consistent with the absence of internal phase transitions within the superconducting state. In this context, observation of a small diode ratio
\epsilon=I_{c}^{-}/I_{c}^{+}
in a nominally intrinsic platform does not, by itself, imply an intrinsic origin.

Instead, a small
\epsilon
must arise either from extrinsic effects, such as inhomogeneity, geometry, or junction/proximity physics, or from an internal transition between competing superconducting phases. The latter possibility requires tuning to an exotic internal criticality between two superconducting phases and is therefore expected to be rare. In the absence of independent evidence for such an internal transition, a small observed
\epsilon
is more naturally attributed to extrinsic mechanisms.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Remez–Chebyshev bound

In this Appendix we derive the quantitative bound on the diode asymmetry

\epsilon=j_{c}^{-}/j_{c}^{+}
quoted in the main text for finite-order Landau
theories. We assume that within a Landau expansion truncated at order

n
, the supercurrent

j(q)=\partial_{q}F(q)

(15)

is a real polynomial of degree
\leq n
on the superconducting momentum
window

I=[q_{c}^{-},q_{c}^{+}].

(16)

Using the definition of
\theta
as in the main text,

\theta\equiv\frac{\text{length of region where }j(q)>0}{|I|}\in(0,1).

(17)

let
I_{+}\subset I
denote the set where
j(q)>0
and
I_{-}\subset I
the
set where
j(q)<0
. These intervals need not be contiguous. Then
|I_{+}|=\theta|I|
and
|I_{-}|=(1-\theta)|I|
.

The critical currents are

j_{c}^{+}=\sup_{q\in I}j(q),\qquad j_{c}^{-}=\sup_{q\in I}[-j(q)],

(18)

and
\epsilon=j_{c}^{-}/j_{c}^{+}
. The basic strategy is to show that for given
j_{c}^{-}
and
n
,
j_{c}^{+}
cannot be to large; it is bounded above by a finite function of
n
, yielding a lower bound on
\epsilon
.

To ensure superconductivity is absent for
q\to\pm\infty
, we must have
j(q)<0
for
q
slightly to the right of
q_{c}^{-}
and
j(q)>0
as
q
approaches
q_{c}^{+}
from the left. Thus, we introduce a rescaled variable

x=a(q-q_{c}^{-})-1

(19)

which maps the leftmost point of
I_{-}
maps to
-1
. Furthermore, we chose
a
such that
I_{-}
maps to an interval
E
of length 2. Under (
19
), the full interval
I
then maps to
[-1,1+2\theta]
.

Next, define the normalized polynomial

p(x)=\frac{j(q(x))}{j_{c}^{-}}

(20)

By construction,
p(x)
has degree
\leq n
and satisfies

|p(x)|\leq 1,\qquad x\in E,

(21)

since on
I_{-}
we have
-j(q)\leq j_{c}^{-}
.

The Remez inequality states that if a real polynomial
p(x)

of degree
\leq n
satisfies
|p(x)|\leq 1
over an interval

E\subset[-1,1+2\theta]
of length
\geq 2
, then

\sup_{x\in[-1,1+2\theta]}|p(x)|\leq T_{n}(1+2\theta),

(22)

where
T_{n}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind,

T_{n}(\cos\phi)=\cos(n\phi)
. Undoing the normalization and reverting to
q

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|=\frac{\sup_{q\in I}|j(q)|}{j_{c}^{-}}=\frac{j_{c}^{+}}{j_{c}^{-}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon},

(23)

and therefore

\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{T_{n}(1+2\theta)}

(24)
