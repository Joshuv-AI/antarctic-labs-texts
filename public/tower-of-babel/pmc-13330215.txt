Enhancing adult learning through problem-based learning and knowledge scaffolding: a case study from postgraduate immunology education
Authors: V. Abeysuriya, J. Gamage, W. A. D. Jayawardana, V. Abeysuriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe integration of Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Knowledge Scaffolding in postgraduate immunology education has been shown to enhance the quality of adult learning. This process supports the development of problem-solving skills in learners through critical thinking, self-direction, and practical application of knowledge. This study aims to improves the postgraduate immunology teaching to strengthen lifelong learning capacities by incorporating PBL and knowledge scaffolding.MethodologyIn this study, PBL was implemented with thirty postgraduate students enrolled in the Immunity to Infectious Diseases course. Students were divided into three small groups and engaged in collaborative problem-solving by analyzing immunology research papers and constructing domain-specific mind maps. These were later integrated into a comprehensive class-wide mind map, with data collected through structured feedback forms.ResultsOut of the 30 feedback forms distributed to participants, 29 (97%) were returned and used for analysis. The majority reported that small-group activities encouraged idea sharing (87%), class-wide integration boosted confidence (83%), and the exercise enhanced problem-solving skills (80%). Moreover, students also valued teacher guidance (90%) and recommended continuation of the activity (93%). Further the facilitator’s notes confirmed higher engagement compared with lecture-based sessions.ConclusionThe findings suggest PBL supported by scaffolding promotes confidence, collaboration, and self-directed learning in postgraduate immunology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09488-x.

Introduction

Adult learning in higher education increasingly emphasizes not only the acquisition of disciplinary knowledge but also the development of transferable skills such as problem-solving, collaboration, and lifelong learning. Traditional lecture-based teaching methods, while efficient for content delivery, often limit opportunities for learners to actively engage with knowledge, challenge assumptions, and apply concepts to real-world contexts [1, 2]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in postgraduate biomedical education, where learners are expected to integrate rapidly advancing scientific knowledge with professional responsibilities [3].

PBL is a student-centered pedagogy widely adopted in medical and health sciences education to foster critical thinking and deep understanding [4]. In PBL, learners in small collaborative groups tackle authentic, open-ended problems (e.g., diagnosing immune disorders or evaluating vaccine data) through a structured process: encountering the problem, identifying knowledge gaps and learning objectives, independently researching, synthesizing findings, justifying solutions, and reflecting [5]. Originating at McMaster University in the 1960 s, PBL now underpins curricula at institutions like Harvard Medical School and Maastricht University, yielding superior clinical reasoning, knowledge retention, and teamwork versus lectures [6]. Unlike passive learning, PBL promotes experiential engagement and professional relevance, aligning with adult learning theories [7].

In parallel, knowledge scaffolding, a structured strategy that supports learners as they move from novice to independent expertise provides a useful pedagogical complement. Scaffolding allows instructors to guide learners initially and gradually reduce support as competence increases [8]. Within immunology education, scaffolding may include providing conceptual frameworks, curated research readings, or structured group tasks that help learners make sense of highly complex and interconnected biological systems.

These approaches powerfully enhance modern postgraduate education by building critical thinking, problem-solving, and self-directed learning which is a key for adult learners motivated by real-world relevance [9]. PBL drives collaborative solution-building and practical application, while scaffolding equips learners to manage cognitive demands through systematic strategies. Together, they cultivate lifelong learning and deeper understanding of essential concepts [10].

The COVID-19 pandemic has had profound and lasting effects on higher education systems worldwide. The adult education was also affected by this pandemic. As educational institutions and universities scrambled through this period to adapt to the new normal, adult education programs had to roll on quickly to online and hybrid learning platforms. Also in this period, adult education programs had to navigate unprecedented challenges in access, delivery, and engagement [11].

The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the importance of innovative approaches such as PBL and scaffolding [12–15]. Immunology became central to public health discourse, and postgraduate learners were expected to critically interpret emerging research on host–pathogen interactions, vaccine responses, and immune dysregulation [16]. However, evidence suggests that traditional postgraduate curricula often do not adequately equip learners with the skills required to navigate evolving knowledge landscapes [11].

In this context, we designed and implemented an educational intervention within a postgraduate immunology course that combined PBL and knowledge scaffolding. Students engaged in small-group creation of domain-specific mind maps and subsequently integrated these into a comprehensive conceptual framework on immunity to COVID-19. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of this intervention on learners’ confidence, collaboration, and lifelong learning competencies evaluated via post-intervention surveys and mind maps.

Methods

Study design

This was a post educational intervention study within a postgraduate immunology module implementing a novel PBL and knowledge scaffolding approach and evaluating its immediate effect via structured student feedback. The intervention aimed to shift learning from lecture-based delivery to an active, student-centered approach where students collaboratively constructed mind maps to solve an authentic problem. This mind mapping exercise operationalized PBL principles encountering a complex problem, identifying learning needs, independently analyzing resources, and synthesizing group solutions through presentation and class-wide integration. Similar approaches have been shown to enhance engagement and critical thinking in higher education [4, 17–22].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in the Immunity to Infectious Diseases module of the Master of Cellular and Molecular Immunology program, University of Colombo, Sri Lanka. Thirty postgraduate students (2023 intake) participated. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

A structured feedback questionnaire was specifically developed for this study based on the learning objectives of the PBL–scaffolding intervention. The instrument consisted of eight items measured on a 4-point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) which is administered immediately after two steps completion assessing idea sharing, confidence, problem-solving, teacher guidance, usefulness for assessment, and guided independent study [24]. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 revision) [25]. Ethical clearance for the educational intervention and feedback collection was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Biochemistry, Molecular Biology and Biotechnology (IBMBB), University of Colombo, Sri Lanka. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all students prior to data collection. Anonymity and confidentiality were assured throughout the process.

Observational findings

Facilitator notes indicated that scaffolding (progressing from group work to integration of the whole class) helped learners gradually build confidence. Students engaged in critical evaluation of research articles, collaborative negotiation of meaning, and consolidation into structured visual representations.

Discussion

This study shows that postgraduate immunology students perceived combining PBL with knowledge scaffolding as highly effective for developing collaboration, confidence, creative problem-solving, self-directed learning, supporting its feasibility for curriculum enhancement (Tables 2 and 3) and facilitator’s observations. These outcomes suggest that replacing traditional lecture-based delivery with structured, student-centered strategies may lead to more meaningful learning experiences in postgraduate biomedical education.

PBL and active engagement

PBL has consistently been associated with the promotion of active learning and collaborative engagement. In this study, the majority of students agreed that both small-group activities and class-wide integration helped them express ideas more confidently and engage in problem-solving. This aligns with earlier findings that PBL fosters teamwork, enhances communication, and improves motivation for self-directed learning [4, 18]. By situating learners within authentic problems (in this case, immunity to COVID-19) the intervention encouraged students to bridge theoretical knowledge with practical application, which is a hallmark of effective adult learning environments.

Knowledge scaffolding and conceptual understanding

The scaffolding approach, where students first created domain-specific mind maps and then integrated them into a class-wide framework, appeared to enhance conceptual clarity and learner confidence. Scaffolding has been described as an essential strategy to guide learners from novice understanding towards independent expertise [7]. Within this intervention, scaffolding provided structured support in the early phases of knowledge construction, which was gradually reduced as students took responsibility for integrating and presenting their ideas. Such approaches have been emphasized in medical education research as critical for deep learning and retention [8].

Relevance to adult learning theory

A key finding of this study was that students recognized the broader value of the activity beyond immediate course outcomes and many students indicated that the approach was useful for assessments, guided independent study, and future professional development. This reflects the principles of adult learning theory, which emphasize that adult learners are motivated when learning is relevant, problem-centered, and transferable to professional contexts [11]. The collaborative setting also provided opportunities for peer learning, inclusivity, adaptability and competencies essential for lifelong learning.

Alignment with broader outcomes

The intervention appeared to foster lifelong learning attributes such as teamwork, communication, and adaptability. These findings align with prior studies showing that PBL enhances transferable competencies in health sciences education [26, 27].

Contribution to the literature

Our findings are consistent with meta-analyses showing that PBL improves teamwork, communication, and competency development compared with traditional methods [28, 29]. Importantly, this study extends such evidence into postgraduate immunology, a field where complex concepts often present barriers to learning. The use of mind maps as a scaffolding tool further complements prior research demonstrating that visual learning strategies support the organization and retrieval of complex biomedical knowledge [17]. The combination of PBL small-group analysis and class-wide visual integration provided complementary knowledge construction approaches, as evidenced by consistently high satisfaction across diverse competency items (Table 3).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it incorporated both quantitative survey data providing a deeper insight of the intervention’s effectiveness. Second, it was situated in a postgraduate context, where learners often face competing professional and academic demands; the positive response suggests that PBL and scaffolding can be integrated even in such settings.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. The modest sample size restricts generalizability, and the reliance on self-reported feedback may introduce bias. Moreover, the study did not include objective measures of learning outcomes, such as pre- and post-intervention assessments or performance data, which could strengthen the evidence. One limitation of this study is the design of the questionnaire, where some items were phrased in a manner that may have guided participants toward positive responses. Terms such as “increased my confidence” and “helped me solve problems” may have introduced response bias, potentially influencing how participants evaluated the learning experience. No comparison was performed between intervention and non-intervention student cohorts using test scores or other academic assessments; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as perception-based rather than performance-based outcomes. Future studies should address these limitations by employing mixed-methods designs, including longitudinal follow-up to evaluate the sustained impact of such interventions on professional practice.

Implications for postgraduate education

The results suggest that embedding PBL and scaffolding into postgraduate biomedical curricula is both feasible and effective. For educators, these strategies offer practical ways to foster deeper engagement, critical thinking, and lifelong learning skills. For institutions, the findings highlight the importance of investing in pedagogical innovation, especially in fields like immunology where learners must navigate rapidly evolving scientific knowledge.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that integrating PBL with knowledge scaffolding provides an effective approach to postgraduate immunology education. The intervention not only strengthened learners’ confidence, collaboration, and problem-solving abilities but also supported the development of transferable skills essential for lifelong learning. By progressing from small-group tasks to a class-wide synthesis, the activity enabled students to engage more deeply with complex immunological concepts and to construct shared frameworks of understanding.

The findings suggest that moving beyond traditional lectures towards active, collaborative frameworks enhances both disciplinary learning and the acquisition of professional competencies such as teamwork, communication, and adaptability. These attributes are critical for postgraduate biomedical learners who must navigate evolving scientific knowledge while balancing academic and professional responsibilities.

Although limited by sample size and reliance on self-reported data, the study provides evidence that combining PBL with structured scaffolding can serve as a scalable and sustainable model for postgraduate education. Future research should include objective measures of learning outcomes, longitudinal follow-up, and exploration of scalability across other biomedical disciplines. By embedding such innovations into curricula, higher education institutions can cultivate resilient, self-directed learners who are better prepared for the challenges of modern scientific and clinical practice.
