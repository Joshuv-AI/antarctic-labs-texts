Comparative gene expression analysis of nestin-expressing hair follicle-derived cells
Authors: Mohammad Saied Salehi, Nahid Ashjazadeh, Yasaman Mohammadi, Nasrin Ghorbani, Amin Ashayerizadeh, Mohammad Reza Jafarzadeh Shirazi, Robert M. Hoffman, Sareh Pandamooz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since the discovery of nestin-expressing hair follicle-derived cells (NHFCs), they have attracted significant attention for regenerative medicine including neural-tissue engineering. The present study examines the gene expression profiles of NHFCs, focusing on neurotrophic factors and neural differentiation markers, and compares them to those of other well-characterized adult composite stem/progenitor cell systems, including bone marrow-derived mesenchymal cells, dental pulp-derived cells, as well as cortical astrocytes and hypothalamic neurons. The expression of key neurotrophic factors such as Bdnf, Ngf, Vegf, Ntf3, and Gdnf as well as neural-differentiation markers such as Nes, Sox10, Dcx, Tubb3, and Gfap are compared. The present study shows that NHFCs express significantly higher levels of Bdnf, Ngf, and Vegf compared to most of other evaluated cell types, indicating their potential neuroprotective properties. Additionally, NHFCs at passage 4 exhibit lower expression of Nes than the other cell types, suggesting a predisposition to differentiate into mature neuronal lineages. Elevated expression of Tubb3 in NHFCs further indicates the potential for neuronal maturation. The simultaneous expression of both neuronal and glial markers in NHFCs indicates their unique differentiation potential. The present study highlights the distinct molecular signature of NHFCs, suggesting their potential as a valuable resource for neuroregenerative therapies and other regenerative medicine applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56299-7.

Introduction

Neurological disorders, ranging from neurodegenerative diseases to traumatic injuries of the central nervous system (CNS), represent significant challenges in modern medicine due to their often irreversible nature and limited treatment options1. Recent advancements in regenerative medicine have highlighted the potential of composite stem/progenitor cell populations-based therapies for neural repair and functional recovery2. Composite stem/progenitor cells derived from various tissue sources exhibit diverse capacities for neuronal differentiation, secretion of neurotrophic factors, and modulation of the CNS microenvironment, making them promising candidates for therapeutic intervention2.

Since the discovery of nestin-expressing hair follicle-derived cells (NHFCs)3, also termed hair follicle-associated pluripotent (HAP) stem cells, they have shown properties that support neurogenesis and neural repair mechanisms4,5. NHFCs exhibit several advantageous characteristics for cell therapy. They are highly abundant and can be obtained with minimal invasiveness, reducing the need for extensive surgical procedures. Furthermore, the straightforward isolation process and non-invasive harvesting techniques make NHFCs an ideal candidate for repeated or large-scale collection. These attributes enhance their practicality and feasibility for therapeutic applications, positioning NHFCs as a valuable resource for regenerative medicine6,7.

NHFCs possess the ability to differentiate into neuronal and glial lineages under appropriate conditions, offering a renewable source of cells for replenishing damaged or lost neural tissue8–10. Importantly, NHFCs have been shown to secrete high levels of key neurotrophic factors, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (Bdnf), nerve growth factor (Ngf), neurotrophin-3 (Ntf3), glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (Gdnf), and vascular endothelial growth factor (Vegf)11,12. These factors play crucial roles in promoting neuronal survival, enhancing synaptic plasticity, and fostering neurogenesis, thereby facilitating recovery processes in neurological diseases and injuries11,12. The expression profile of neurotrophic factors in NHFCs underscores their potential to create a supportive microenvironment that nurtures neuronal health and function.

In parallel, NHFCs exhibit distinct patterns of neural differentiation markers such as nestin (Nes), SRY-box transcription factor 10 (Sox10), doublecortin (Dcx), tubulin beta 3 class III (Tubb3), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (Gfap)8. These markers serve as indicators of cellular maturity and lineage commitment towards neural phenotypes, thereby influencing the functional outcomes of NHFC-based therapies. Understanding the interplay between neurotrophic factor secretion and neural differentiation in NHFCs is crucial for optimizing their application in targeted cell therapies for diverse neurological disorders. NHFCs have also been shown to differentiate into blood vessels13, beating heart cells14 and tyrosine-hydroxylase-expressing dopamine neurons15. NHFCs have been shown to repair peripheral nerves10 and the spinal cord16. Also, NHFCs can enhance wound healing17 and NHFCs have repaired intracerebral hemorrhage18.

The present study compares the gene expression profiles of NHFCs with bone marrow-derived mesenchymal cells (BM-MCs), dental pulp-derived cells (DPCs), primary rat cortical astrocytes, and hypothalamic neurons, focusing on the expression levels of neurotrophic factors and neural differentiation markers to delineate the unique molecular signatures that define these populations. BM-MCs and DPCs were selected because they represent two of the most extensively studied and well-characterized adult composite stem/progenitor cell populations, widely used as reference models in regenerative medicine due to their accessibility, multipotency, and established neurotrophic potential. Astrocytes and hypothalamic neurons were included as representative mature neural cell types, since NHFCs have demonstrated the ability to differentiate into both neuronal and glial lineages, making their inclusion biologically relevant for comparative analysis.

In the present study, we specifically refer to these cultures as hair follicle-derived cells, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal cells, and dental pulp-derived cells rather than “stem cells” to more accurately represent their biological nature. Although the cultures were derived from tissue regions known to harbor multipotent stem and progenitor cells—such as from the hair follicle bulge, bone marrow stroma, and dental pulp—they likely contain a mixture of stem cells and their differentiated progeny and are therefore referred to as composite cell-culture system containing both stem cells and their differentiated progeny. Therefore, the terms NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs are used throughout the manuscript to reflect these heterogeneous but stem cell-containing populations rather than implying a homogeneous stem cell culture19–23.

Results

NHFCs isolation, migration, and nestin expression

Hair follicles were isolated from the whisker pads of Sprague–Dawley rats, and the whisker hair bulges were explanted onto collagen-coated cell culture plates. NHFCs began to migrate outward from the bulge region within 2–3 days post-explantation, followed by extensive proliferation (Fig. 1A-C). To confirm the identity of the migrated cells, immunofluorescence staining was performed on passage one cultures. The cells showed robust nestin expression, indicating that the migrated population contained nestin-positive stem/progenitor cells derived from the hair follicle bulge (Fig. 1D-F)5,10.

Immunophenotyping

Flow cytometry analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression of cell surface markers CD34, CD44, CD45, and CD90 on NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs at passage 4. NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs were negative for CD34, a hematopoietic stem cell marker, confirming the absence of hematopoietic lineage cells. All three cell types showed high expression of CD44, a marker associated with stem cell identity and migration. NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs were negative for CD45, a common leukocyte marker, confirming the absence of hematopoietic cells. High expression of CD90 was observed in NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs, consistent with the mesenchymal stem cell phenotype (Figs. 2A and 3A, and 4A).

Differentiation potential

The adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential of NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs were assessed to confirm their multipotency. After 21 days of culture in specific differentiation media, cells were stained with Oil Red O and Alizarin Red S to visualize adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation, respectively. All three stem cell types showed positive staining with Oil Red O, indicating successful differentiation into adipocytes. NHFCs had a comparable degree of lipid droplet formation (Fig. 2B) to BM-MCs (Fig. 3B) and DPCs (Fig. 4B), confirming their adipogenic potential. Alizarin Red S staining demonstrated mineralized matrix formation in NHFCs (Fig. 2C), BM-MCs (Fig. 3C), and DPCs (Fig. 4C), indicating successful differentiation into osteoblasts. The extent of mineralization in NHFCs was similar to that observed in BM-MCs and DPCs, confirming their osteogenic potential.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the purity of cultured cortical astrocytes using markers GFAP and S100β. Cultured astrocytes at passage 4 were positive for GFAP and S100β, confirming their astrocytic identity and purity. The cells exhibited strong fluorescence signals for both markers, indicating a high degree of purity in the astrocyte cultures (Fig. 5).

Comparative gene expression analysis

The relative expression levels of major neurotrophic factors and neural differentiation markers, including Bdnf, Ngf, Ntf3, Gdnf, Vegf, Nes, Sox10, Dcx, Tubb3, and Gfap, were evaluated in rat NHFCs compared with BM-MCs, DPCs, cortical astrocytes, and hypothalamic neurons (H32 cell line). Expression levels were normalized to NHFCs. The complete cycle threshold (Ct) dataset for all analyzed genes among all cell types is provided in the Supplementary Materials 1.

In our experiments, we evaluated the expression stability of three commonly used housekeeping genes—glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh), ribosomal protein L13A (Rpl13a) and hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyl transferase-1 (Hprt1)—among all analyzed cell types. None of these genes demonstrated uniform stability among all cell populations. Therefore, to improve normalization robustness and minimize variability, a multi-gene normalization strategy was applied. Specifically, the mean Ct value of the three reference genes (Gapdh, Rpl13a, and Hprt1) was used as the internal reference for all samples.

Differential expression of these housekeeping genes among the cell types was observed and is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Given the well-established functional roles of Gapdh, Rpl13a and Hprt1 in cellular metabolism, protein synthesis and nucleotide turnover, these differences likely reflect intrinsic biological variability among the analyzed cell populations rather than purely technical variation.

Neurotrophic factor expression

Bdnf: Bdnf expression in NHFCs was approximately 1.9-fold higher than in DPCs (P < 0.01); 77-fold higher than in cortical astrocytes (P < 0.001); and 4.4-fold higher than in H32 cells (P < 0.001). Bdnf expression in NHFCs was comparable to that in BM-MCs (P = 0.76; Fig. 6).

Ngf: NHFCs expressed 7-fold higher Ngf mRNA than astrocytes (P < 0.001); and 20-fold higher than H32 cells (P < 0.001); whereas Ngf expression in NHFCs was 0.35-fold of BM-MCs (P < 0.001) and 0.5-fold of DPCs (P < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Ntf3: Ntf3 mRNA levels in NHFCs were 0.57-fold of BM-MCs (P < 0.01); 0.18-fold of DPCs (P < 0.001); and 0.02-fold of cortical astrocytes (P < 0.001), indicating much lower expression in NHFCs. In contrast, NHFCs expressed approximately 140-fold higher Ntf3 levels than H32 cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Gdnf: Gdnf expression in NHFCs was comparable to that in BM-MCs (P = 0.99) and DPCs (P = 0.57) but was 4.8-fold higher than in cortical astrocytes (P < 0.001) and 27-fold higher than in H32 cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Vegf: NHFCs exhibited the highest Vegf mRNA expression among the examined cell types. Vegf expression in NHFCs was approximately 2.7-fold higher than in BM-MCs (P < 0.001); 3.3-fold higher than in DPCs (P < 0.001); 5.4-fold higher than in astrocytes (P < 0.001); and 4-fold higher than in H32 cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Neural differentiation marker expression

Nes: NHFCs exhibited lower Nes mRNA expression compared to the other analyzed cell types. Nes expression in NHFCs was approximately 0.72-fold that of BM-MCs (P = 0.04) and 0.38-fold that of DPCs (P < 0.001). With regard to differentiated cells, Nes expression in NHFCs was 0.03-fold that of cortical astrocytes (P < 0.001), and 0.22-fold that of H32 cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 7).

Sox10: Sox10 expression in NHFCs was comparable to that in BM-MCs (P = 0.1) and DPCs (P = 0.68). With regard to differentiated cells, astrocytes exhibited approximately 5,800-fold higher Sox10 expression than NHFCs (P < 0.001), while Sox10 transcripts were undetectable in H32 cells (Fig. 7).

Dcx: Dcx expression in NHFCs was 0.08-fold that of BM-MCs (P < 0.001); 0.1-fold that of DPCs (P < 0.001); and 0.02-fold that of cortical astrocytes (P < 0.001). NHFCs exhibited approximately 41-fold higher Dcx expression than H32 cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 7).

Tubb3: NHFCs expressed Tubb3 mRNA approximately 2.1-fold higher than BM-MCs (P < 0.001) and 1.4-fold higher than DPCs (P = 0.02). Compared with H32 cells, HFSCs showed about 28.5-fold higher Tubb3 expression (P < 0.001). However, Tubb3 expression in NHFCs was comparable to that in cortical astrocytes (P = 0.62; Fig. 7).

Gfap: Gfap mRNA levels in NHFCs were statistically comparable to DPCs (P = 0.58) and H32 cells. Cortical astrocytes expressed more than 480,000-fold higher Gfap levels than NHFCs (P < 0.001) and BM-MCs exhibited 2.1-fold higher Gfap expression than NHFCs (P < 0.01; Fig. 7).

Discussion

The field of adult stem cells was significantly advanced with the discovery of NHFCs in the bulge area of hair follicles3. NHFCs have been shown to differentiate to neurons, glial cell, adipocytes5,10, cardiovascular cells14, blood vessels13 and dopamine producing neurons15. Specifically, NHFCs have shown promise for regeneration in models of central nervous system disorders such as cerebral ischemia and stroke18,24–26, vascular dementia and Alzheimer’s disease27–30, spinal cord injury16,31–33, peripheral nerve injury10,34,35 and traumatic brain injury36. Therefore, NHFC should be developed clinically for these applications.

One of the significant findings of the present study is the expression of neurotrophic factors in NHFCs, particularly Bdnf, which plays a critical role in neural survival, plasticity, and recovery following CNS injury37. NHFCs in the present study had significantly higher levels of Bdnf compared to other stem cell types, such as BM-MCs and DPCs. Bdnf has been well-established as a neuroprotective agent, facilitating neurogenesis and the repair of damaged neural circuits38. This result makes Bdnf secretion by NHFCs a key factor contributing to their therapeutic potential in neuroregeneration. Moreover, Ngf, another crucial neurotrophic factor, was also expressed at higher levels in NHFCs compared to other cells, such as DPCs, astrocytes, and hypothalamic neurons. Ngf is essential in the development and maintenance of neurons, with a particularly important neuroprotective role in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease39. Ngf enhances neurogenesis, neuronal differentiation, and survival40, making NHFCs an attractive candidate for therapeutic strategies targeting cognitive deficits and neurodegenerative conditions.

In addition to Bdnf and Ngf, Vegf which is an essential protein for angiogenesis and neuroprotection, was found to be expressed at significantly higher levels in NHFCs compared to DPCs and astrocytes. Vegf is involved in CNS homeostasis, particularly under conditions of ischemia or neurodegeneration, where it not only promotes neuronal survival but also aids in maintaining the blood-brain barrier and promoting synaptic plasticity41, thereby indicating the potential of NHFCs to promote neurovascular regeneration in addition to their direct effects on neuronal repair.

Another significant finding was the differential expression of neural differentiation markers in NHFCs, including Nes, Sox10, and Tubb3. NHFCs exhibited lower Nes expression compared to other stem cell populations, such as BM-MCs and DPCs, suggesting that NHFSCs may have greater potential to differentiate into mature neural lineages rather than remaining in an undifferentiated state42,43. The present study also demonstrates high expression of Tubb3 in NHFCs, indicating their maturation towards neuronal phenotypes. The present results are consistent with previous studies showing that Tubb3 expression is crucial for neuronal differentiation and the formation of neuronal structures, such as neurites and synapses44,45. Higher levels of Tubb3 in NHFCs further suggest that these cells could play an essential role in functional neural regeneration by promoting the formation of mature neurons capable of integrating into existing neural circuits. The ability of NHFCs to differentiate into neurons was originally discovered by Amoh et al. 20055.

NHFCs had higher Sox10 expression compared to BM-MCs and DPCs, indicating their potential to differentiate into glial cells, particularly Schwann cells, which play a critical role in myelination in the peripheral nervous system46,47. The ability of NHFCs to differentiate into Schwann cells was also reported earlier10 The higher Sox10 expression suggests that NHFCs have more potential to differentiate to glial lineages, which is important for nerve regeneration and myelination in peripheral nerve injuries. The simultaneous higher expression of Sox10 and Tubb3 in NHFCs suggests that NHFCs possess an ability to differentiate into both neuronal and glial lineages which makes NHFCs particularly promising for neuroregenerative therapies, as they can contribute to both axonal regeneration and myelination in the peripheral nervous system, as well as neuronal replacement in the central nervous system. The ability of NHFCs to undergo this differentiation process suggests their potential for treating mixed neurological injuries where both neuronal and glial regeneration are necessary for optimal recovery.

Conclusion

The present study shows that NHFCs possess distinct molecular signatures compared to other stem cell populations, particularly in their ability to secrete neurotrophic factors and differentiate into neural lineages indicating their potential for regenerative medicine for treating neurological disorders. Further pre-clinical studies are needed on the mechanisms of the neuroprotective and neurogenic properties of NHFCs followed by clinical applications to promote recovery from CNS injuries and treating neurodegenerative diseases6. The abundance of NHFCs, their accessibility, ease of isolation, ability to differentiate into neuronal and glial cells, as well as ability to preserve their differentiation potential during cryopreservation48 demonstrate NHFCs have promise for regenerative medicine; It is thus possible for individuals to establish a personal cryopreserved bank of their own NHFCs for future use.

Limitations and future directions

One limitation of this study is that none of the evaluated housekeeping genes exhibited completely stable expression across all analyzed cell types. To address this, a multi-gene normalization strategy based on the mean Ct of Gapdh, Rpl13a, and Hprt1 was applied to improve normalization robustness. However, residual variability in reference gene expression may still influence relative expression estimates. Future studies may further improve normalization accuracy by validating additional reference genes or applying advanced stability algorithms.

Notably, the housekeeping genes evaluated in the present study—Gapdh, Rpl13a, and Hprt1—are not merely technical controls but play essential roles in fundamental cellular processes. Gapdh is involved in glycolysis and also participates in transcriptional regulation and apoptotic signaling; Rpl13a is required for protein synthesis; and Hprt1 is essential for nucleotide metabolism and salvage pathways. Therefore, differences in their expression among various cell types may reflect intrinsic biological variation related to metabolism, survival, and regenerative capacity, rather than purely technical variability. While these genes were used here for normalization purposes, their differential expression may itself represent a biologically relevant feature that warrants further investigation.

Another limitation of the present study relates to the number of statistical comparisons performed in the context of relatively small sample sizes (n = 3 per group). Although one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied, some P values were close to the conventional significance threshold (P = 0.05), raising the possibility that certain findings may reflect random variation. Future studies with larger sample sizes and additional validation approaches will help to strengthen the robustness of these findings.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.SUMS.AEC.1401.041; 2023) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used.

Isolation and culture of NHFCs

NHFCs were isolated from the whisker pads of three 4–5-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 100–150 g, as previously described49. Hair follicles were dissected from the whisker pads, and the hair bulges were isolated. The hair bulges were then explanted onto collagen-coated cell culture plates. The cultures were maintained in alpha MEM (GIBCO, #11900073) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, #A5256701), 5% chick embryo extract, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Seven days post-explantation, the NHFCs which migrated from the bulge area of the hair follicles were passaged and maintained in culture. Cells used for the present study were at passage 4, which corresponds to 40 days after the initial isolation.

Isolation and culture of BM-MCs

BM-MCs were isolated from the femurs and tibias of three 4–5-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 100–150 g, which were different from the rats used for the isolation of NHFCs. The femurs and tibias were dissected, cleaned, and both ends of the bones were cut. Bone marrow was then extracted by flushing the medullary cavity with cell-culture medium. The collected bone marrow was minced and digested with trypsin for 30 min at 37 °C. After digestion, the cells were plated onto collagen-coated cell culture flasks and cultured in alpha MEM, supplemented with 10% FBS, 5% chick embryo extract, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin50. Cells were maintained in culture and used for the study at passage 4, which corresponds to 40 days after the initial isolation.

Isolation and culture of DPCs

DPCs were isolated from the dental pulp of three 4-5week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 100–150 g, distinct from the rats used for the isolation of NHFCs and BM-MCs. After dissection of the maxilla and thorough cleaning, the incisors were carefully sectioned to isolate the dental pulp. The dental pulp were then minced and digested with trypsin for 30 min at 37 °C. Following digestion, the cells were plated onto collagen-coated cell culture flasks and cultured in alpha MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 5% chick embryo extract, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin50. The cells were maintained in culture and used for the study at passage 4, which corresponds to 40 days after the initial isolation.

Isolation and culture of cortical astrocytes

Cortical astrocytes were isolated from the cerebral cortices of three male Sprague-Dawley rats aged between postnatal days 1 and 3. The cerebral cortices were dissociated, minced, and digested with trypsin for 20 min at 37 °C. Following digestion, the cells were plated onto poly-D-lysine-coated cell culture flasks and cultured in high-glucose DMEM (GIBCO, #12800082) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, 1% non-essential amino acids (Pan Biotech, #P08-32100), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After 10–12 days, when the cortical cells reached approximately 90% confluence, the cultures were shaken at 240 rpm for 6 h to detach loosely adherent microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells50,51. Astrocytes were maintained in culture and used for the study at passage 4, which corresponds to 40 days after the initial isolation.

Culture of hypothalamic neurons

The immortalized rat hypothalamic cell line (H32 cells) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (GIBCO, #32500035) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and used for the present study at passage 15–20.

Flow cytometry analysis for immunophenotyping

To evaluate the expression of cell surface markers and assess the purity of the cultures, flow cytometry analysis was performed on NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs at passage 4. Flasks containing passage 4 cells were detached using trypsin, centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS. The cells were then blocked with PBS supplemented with 10% FBS for 20 min at room temperature to minimize nonspecific antibody binding. After blocking, the cell suspension was adjusted to a final volume of 100 µl per tube, and 5 µl of monoclonal antibody was added to each tube, including CD90-PerCP anti-rat (BioLegend, Cat. No. 202515), CD44H-FITC anti-rat (BioLegend, Cat. No. 203906), CD34-PE anti-rat (BioLegend, Cat. No. 553142), and CD45-FITC anti-rat (BioLegend, Cat. No. 550616). The samples were gently mixed and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed with 500 µl of PBS, centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in 250 µl of PBS for analysis. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, USA) flow cytometer.

Assessment of differentiation potential

To confirm the multipotency of the cultured stem cells, the adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential of NHFCs, BM-MCs, and DPCs were assessed. After 21 days of subculture in specific differentiation media, the cells were stained with Oil Red O for adipogenic differentiation and Alizarin Red for osteogenic differentiation52.

Immunofluorescence analysis of hair follicle-migrated cells

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to confirm nestin expression in hair follicle–migrated cells. Passage 1 cells were seeded in a 4-well chambered cell culture slide and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with mouse anti-nestin antibody (1:50, Abcam, #ab6142). The following day, cells were washed and incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher, #A-11001) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA). Slides were mounted and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence analysis of astrocyte purity

Immunofluorescence was conducted to assess the purity of cultured astrocytes. Cortical astrocytes at passage 4 were seeded in a 4-well chambered cell culture slide for immunostaining against GFAP and S100β. Astrocytes were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were exposed overnight at 4 °C to rabbit anti-GFAP monoclonal antibody (1:500, Cell Signaling, #12389s) and mouse anti-S100β monoclonal antibody (1:500, Sigma-Aldrich, #S2532). The next day, the cells were washed and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse IgG AlexaFluor488 (1:1000, ThermoFisher, #A-11001) or goat anti-rabbit IgG AlexaFluor488 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab150085). DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) was used for nuclear staining.

Comparative gene expression analysis

qRT-PCR was performed based on standard procedures53 to evaluate the relative expression of target genes in NHFCs compared to other cell types. Total RNA was extracted from all five cell types using TRIzol, and RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcriptase kit (Cat. No: 22701; AddBio, Korea). Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Real-time PCR was conducted using RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green (Cat. No: A325402; Ampliqon, Denmark) on an ABI StepOne Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems).

In the present experiments, we evaluated the expression stability of three commonly used housekeeping genes—Gapdh, Rpl13a, Hprt1—for all analyzed cell types. As no single reference gene exhibited stable expression in all groups, a multi-gene normalization approach was applied. Specifically, the mean Ct value of the three reference genes (Gapdh, Rpl13a, and Hprt1) was used for normalization. Relative changes in target gene expression were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with the averaged Ct of the selected housekeeping genes serving as the internal reference.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Relative gene expression data were log₁₀-transformed prior to analysis. Differences among groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, in which each cell type was compared directly with NHFCs as the reference group. Results are presented as geometric means with individual biological replicates shown. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
