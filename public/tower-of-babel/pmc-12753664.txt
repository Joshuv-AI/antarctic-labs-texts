Multi-omics framework integrating genetics microbiome and immunity for understanding motor neuron degeneration pathogenesis
Authors: Shuyu Wang, Ting Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) remains a devastating neurodegenerative disorder with poorly understood pathogenesis. We conducted Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses integrating cardiovascular factors, gut microbiota (GM) composition, and immune cell phenotypes with transcriptomic profiling to establish causal relationships with ALS susceptibility. MR revealed causal associations between elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, systolic blood pressure, and increased ALS risk. GM analysis identified protective Alistipes species effects and detrimental Bacteroides associations. Multiple immune cell subsets demonstrated significant disease associations, particularly CD3 + T cell populations and CD62L+ monocytes. Colocalization studies identified 53 genes with shared genetic architecture, enabling differential expression analysis across datasets. Predictive modeling developed a five-gene biomarker panel achieving 96% training accuracy and 93% external validation. Quantitative PCR confirmed differential expression patterns for biomarkers in patient cohorts. This multi-omics investigation establishes ALS as a complex disorder with actionable cardiovascular and microbiome therapeutic targets, providing applications for risk stratification and prevention.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Microbiology

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive motor neuron degeneration, leading to muscle weakness, paralysis, and typically death within 3–5 years of diagnosis1. Despite considerable research efforts, the underlying causes of ALS remain elusive and involve complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental factors2.

Accumulating clinical evidence suggests associations between metabolic dysfunction and ALS susceptibility. Cardiovascular risk factors, including elevated blood pressure, disturbed glucose homeostasis, and aberrant lipid profiles, have gained attention as potential contributors to neurodegeneration. However, their causal role remains disputed. Emerging evidence indicates that gut-brain axis dysfunction and immune system perturbations constitute important pathogenic mechanisms in ALS3. Intestinal microbiome imbalances appear to modulate neuroinflammatory processes through alterations in microbial metabolite production and disruption of gut barrier integrity4. Moreover, aberrant immune cell profiles, particularly among T-cell and macrophage populations, demonstrate significant associations with disease progression patterns and clinical trajectories5.

Mendelian randomization (MR) provides a robust framework for establishing causality by using genetic variants as natural experiments, thereby avoiding confounding and reverse causation inherent in observational studies6. Contemporary large-scale genomic investigations have mapped genetic determinants of gut microbial communities and immune cell characteristics, creating opportunities for rigorous causal assessment7.

We aimed to determine whether blood pressure regulation, glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis, gut microbiota (GM) composition, and immune cell populations causally influence ALS development using comprehensive MR analyses. Colocalization studies identified genes underlying these relationships, and transcriptomic profiling revealed expression patterns of relevant genes. Predictive modeling facilitated development of classification algorithms and the construction of a clinical nomogram for ALS risk stratification.

Results

GWAS and GEO dataset collection

The comprehensive MR analysis integrated genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from a substantial cohort comprising 20,806 ALS patients and 59,804 control participants of European ancestry with extensive genetic datasets encompassing cardiovascular-metabolic factors, GM composition across 412 distinct taxonomic units, and immune cell phenotypes spanning 731 individual traits (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Mendelian randomization analysis

The comprehensive MR analysis revealed significant causal associations between multiple exposure factors and ALS susceptibility. Among traditional cardiovascular risk factors, several circulating lipids demonstrated notable relationships with ALS risk. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) demonstrated a significant positive association with ALS risk (OR = 1.109, 95% CI = 1.075–1.143, P < 0.001), Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) similarly increased ALS risk (OR = 1.096, 95% CI = 1.056–1.137, P < 0.001), whereas apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I) showed a nonsignificant protective trend (OR = 0.982, 95% CI = 0.944–1.022, P = 0.380). Systolic blood pressure (SBP) demonstrated a significant positive association with ALS risk (OR = 1.084, 95% CI = 1.045–1.124, P < 0.001), whereas diastolic blood pressure (DBP) showed similar trends (OR = 1.077, 95% CI = 1.039–1.117, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 3).

Forest plots depicting significant causal associations (P < Bonferroni-corrected threshold) between (A) cardiovascular-metabolic factors, B gut microbiota composition, and C immune cell phenotypes with ALS risk. GM analysis revealed particularly striking associations with ALS susceptibility. Multiple bacterial taxa showed significant causal relationships, with several Bacteroides species demonstrating protective effects. Bacteroides clarus (OR = 1.084, 95% CI = 1.053–1.116, P < 0.001) and Bacteroides vulgatus (OR = 1.127, 95% CI = 1.060–1.199, P < 0.001) increased ALS risk, whereas Alistipes species consistently showed protective associations. Alistipes onderdonkii (OR = 0.883, 95% CI = 0.828–0.942, P < 0.001) and Alistipes putredinis (OR = 0.873, 95% CI = 0.812–0.938, P < 0.001) significantly reduced ALS risk (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 4).

Immune cell phenotype analysis demonstrated extensive associations with ALS. Multiple T-cell subsets, particularly CD4+ populations, showed significant relationships with disease susceptibility. CD3 expression on various T-cell subsets consistently correlated with ALS risk, with most associations showing protective effects (OR range: 0.954–0.972). Monocyte and dendritic cell phenotypes also displayed notable associations, with CD62L+ monocyte percentages showing protective effects (OR = 0.967, 95% CI = 0.950–0.984, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 5). Complete results, including all tested exposures and sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables 3–5 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis focused on exposures demonstrating significant MR associations with ALS. Following identification of significant MR associations, colocalization analysis was conducted to determine whether exposure traits and ALS shared causal variants within specific genomic regions. This analysis identified 53 genes with posterior probability 4 (PP4) values exceeding 0.50, indicating strong evidence for shared genetic architecture between exposure factors and ALS susceptibility at these loci. These colocalized genes provided the foundation for subsequent differential expression analysis, representing biologically plausible candidates for mediating the observed causal associations (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Differential expression analysis and differentially expressed associated gene identification

Differential expression analysis of the 53 colocalized genes between control and ALS groups identified significant expression changes for all examined genes (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). The genes displayed diverse expression patterns, with 20 genes demonstrating highly significant differential expression (P < 0.001). ARHGAP8 exhibited the highest statistical significance (P = 2.3 × 10⁻⁸) coupled with substantial upregulation (log₂fold change = 2.87) in ALS samples, indicating robust and biologically meaningful dysregulation. Although certain genes demonstrated larger absolute fold changes, ARHGAP8’s combination of exceptional statistical significance and consistent directional change across samples, subsequently confirmed through predictive modeling as the highest-ranked feature gene and validated by quantitative PCR (qPCR) (2.156-fold upregulation, P = 0.002), substantiates its biological relevance in ALS pathogenesis. Additional genes with highly significant differential expression included ADCY8 (P = 4.1 × 10⁻⁷, log₂FC = 2.34), BPTF (P = 8.7 × 10⁻⁷, log₂FC = 2.01), and DACH1 (P = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵, log₂FC = 1.89), all showing marked expression differences (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 6).

Notable patterns emerged among the differentially expressed genes. DOCK6 exhibited significantly reduced expression in ALS samples compared with controls, while IGFBP5 and PDE3A showed elevated expression in disease samples. These expression patterns aligned with subsequent MR validation results, providing initial evidence for the biological relevance of these genes in ALS pathogenesis (Fig. 2B).

Chromosomal mapping revealed widespread genomic distribution of differentially expressed genes, with notable concentrations on chromosomes 1, 2, 6, 11, and 17. This broad chromosomal distribution indicated that ALS-associated genetic influences operate through multiple genomic regions rather than single chromosomal clusters (Fig. 2C).

Correlation analysis among differentially expressed genes revealed predominantly positive correlations, suggesting coordinated expression changes in ALS. The strongest correlations occurred between genes involved in similar biological processes, particularly those related to cellular signaling and metabolic regulation (Fig. 2D).

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune cell composition analysis revealed significant differences between control and ALS samples across multiple cell types. The analysis identified 22 distinct immune cell populations with varying abundance patterns between the groups. ALS samples demonstrated notably altered immune profiles compared with controls, with several cell types showing statistically significant changes (Fig. 3A).

Memory B cells showed significant elevation in ALS samples (P < 0.001), whereas naive B cells remained relatively unchanged. Among T-cell populations, CD8 + T cells exhibited reduced abundance in ALS samples, whereas CD4+ memory resting T cells showed increased proportions. Natural killer (NK) cells populations displayed mixed patterns, with activated NK cells reduced in ALS samples while resting NK cells remained stable (Fig. 3B).

Macrophage populations demonstrated particularly notable changes in ALS samples. M0 macrophages significantly increased in ALS samples (P < 0.05), whereas M2 macrophages showed reduced abundance. This shift in macrophage polarization suggested altered inflammatory responses in ALS pathogenesis. Neutrophil infiltration also increased significantly in ALS samples (P < 0.05), indicating enhanced innate immune activation (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 7).

Correlation analysis between differentially expressed genes and immune cell populations revealed predominantly negative correlations, suggesting that gene expression changes in ALS may suppress certain immune cell functions. ARHGAP8, ADCY8, and other highly differentially expressed genes showed strong negative correlations with multiple immune cell types, particularly T-cell subsets and NK cells (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table 8).

Predictive modeling selection and nomogram construction

Four predictive modeling algorithms were evaluated for predicting ALS based on differentially expressed gene profiles: random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), generalized linear model (GLM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB). Performance comparison revealed that RF achieved the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.962, 95% CI: 0.928–0.996), followed by SVM (AUC = 0.955, 95% CI: 0.918–0.992), XGB (AUC = 0.925, 95% CI: 0.881–0.969), and GLM (AUC = 0.827, 95% CI: 0.764–0.890) (Fig. 4A, B).

Although the confidence intervals for the RF and SVM models exhibited marginal overlap (RF lower bound: 0.928; SVM upper bound: 0.992), RF demonstrated superior performance across multiple evaluation metrics beyond AUC. Specifically, RF exhibited lower root mean square error (RMSE = 0.187 versus SVM RMSE = 0.213), more favorable residual distribution patterns (Fig. 4C), and greater stability in reverse cumulative distribution analysis. Furthermore, RF demonstrated robustness to feature reduction, maintaining 92.4% accuracy with only five features compared to 88.7% for SVM, while providing interpretable feature importance rankings (Fig. 4D) that offered additional advantages for clinical translation. These complementary performance indicators collectively supported RF as the optimal model.

A nomogram was constructed based of the five feature genes to provide quantitative ALS risk assessment. Each gene contributed specific point values based on expression levels, with total scores corresponding to disease probability estimates. The nomogram demonstrated excellent calibration between predicted and actual probabilities, with calibration curves closely following the ideal line (Fig. 4E).

Decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram, showing superior net benefit compared with treating all patients or no patients across a wide range of threshold probabilities. The model maintained positive net benefit for threshold probabilities between 0.1 and 0.8, indicating robust clinical applicability (Fig. 4F).

External validation using the independent GSE87385 dataset (n = 12: 4 ALS cases, 8 controls) assessed both individual gene and composite model performance. Individual gene analysis demonstrated that DACH1 achieved the highest discriminative accuracy as a single predictor (AUC = 0.969, 95% CI: 0.893–1.000), followed by PDE3A (AUC = 0.875, 95% CI: 0.734–1.000) and IGFBP5 (AUC = 0.781, 95% CI: 0.595–0.967) (Fig. 4G). The composite RF model integrating all five feature genes achieved an AUC of 0.932 (95% CI: 0.820–1.000) (Fig. 4H), confirming robust predictive performance and model generalizability for clinical risk assessment.

Feature gene expression quantitative trait loci and protein quantitative trait loci mendelian randomization analysis

External validation through MR analysis utilizing cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTL) and cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTL) data was conducted for three feature genes: DOCK6, IGFBP5, and PDE3A. These genes satisfied the quality criteria for instrumental variable selection (P < 5×10⁻⁸, F-statistic > 10), with available genetic instruments identified in the eQTLGen Consortium and deCODE genetics databases. ARHGAP8 and DACH1 were excluded from this analysis owing to insufficient cis-acting genetic instruments in publicly available resources. These analyses assessed causal effects of gene and protein expression levels on ALS susceptibility, providing independent validation of our findings.

DOCK6 demonstrated significant protective effects against ALS across multiple MR methods. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) analysis revealed an OR of 0.874 (95% CI: 0.851–0.898, P < 0.001), indicating that increased DOCK6 expression substantially reduces ALS risk. Weighted median and simple mode methods yielded consistent results (OR = 0.874, 95% CI: 0.811–0.943, P < 0.001 and OR = 0.870, 95% CI: 0.853–0.887, P < 0.001, respectively), confirming the robustness of this protective association (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

IGFBP5 analysis revealed strong evidence for increased ALS risk with elevated expression levels. The primary IVW method showed an OR of 1.348 (95% CI: 1.328–1.369, P < 0.001), indicating that higher IGFBP5 expression significantly increases ALS susceptibility. Alternative MR methods provided consistent evidence (weighted median OR = 1.347, 95% CI: 1.319–1.375, P < 0.001), supporting the causal nature of this relationship (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

PDE3A demonstrated a more modest but statistically significant association with ALS risk. The IVW analysis yielded an OR of 1.099 (95% CI: 1.042–1.159, P < 0.001), suggesting that increased PDE3A expression promotes ALS development. However, sensitivity analyses using MR-Egger showed non-significant results, indicating some uncertainty in this association compared to DOCK6 and IGFBP5 (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

MR analysis demonstrated significant associations between genetically predicted expression levels of DOCK6, IGFBP5, and PDE3A and ALS risk. DOCK6 exhibited protective effects (OR = 0.874, 95% CI: 0.851–0.898, P < 0.001), whereas IGFBP5 (OR = 1.348, 95% CI: 1.328–1.369, P < 0.001) and PDE3A (OR = 1.099, 95% CI: 1.042–1.159, P < 0.001) conferred increased disease risk (Supplementary Table 9). These causal estimates aligned with the directional changes observed in differential expression analysis, thereby supporting the biological relevance of these genes in ALS pathogenesis.

Quantitative real-time PCR validation

Quantitative PCR validation confirmed differential expression patterns for all five feature genes between ALS patients and healthy controls. DOCK6 was significantly downregulated in ALS samples (0.634-fold vs controls; P = 0.003) (Fig. 5A). This finding corroborated our MR analysis showing DOCK6’s protective effects against ALS development.

Genes associated with increased ALS risk showed consistent upregulation in patient samples. IGFBP5 exhibited the most pronounced elevation, with 2.847-fold higher expression in ALS patients compared to controls (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). ARHGAP8 expression increased 2.156-fold (P = 0.002), whereas DACH1 showed 1.742-fold elevation (P = 0.015) (Fig. 5D, E). These expression changes aligned with differential expression analysis from transcriptomic datasets, providing experimental validation of computational predictions.

Discussion

This comprehensive multi-omics investigation represents the first systematic exploration of causal relationships between multiple environmental and biological exposures and ALS susceptibility through integrated MR, transcriptomic analysis, predictive modeling, and experimental validation. Our findings reveal novel insights into ALS pathogenesis while providing a methodological framework for identifying actionable therapeutic targets in complex neurodegenerative diseases.

Our MR analyses revealed evidence of causal associations between diverse risk factors and ALS development, extending beyond traditional genetic determinants. The identification of significant relationships between cardiovascular metabolic factors including elevated LDL-C, ApoB, and blood pressure and increased ALS risk aligns with emerging epidemiological observations but provides crucial genetic evidence for causality8. These findings challenge conventional thinking about ALS as purely a motor neuron disease and support a broader systemic pathogenesis model.

Recent studies have reinforced the importance of lipid metabolism in ALS. A 2023 two-sample MR study demonstrated positive associations between preclinically elevated lipids and ALS risk, with LDL-C serving as a mediating factor in the pathway from polyunsaturated fatty acids to ALS9. Our results corroborate these findings while extending them to include blood pressure regulation, suggesting that cardiovascular risk factors may represent modifiable targets for ALS prevention strategies.

The GM findings present particularly striking implications for understanding ALS pathogenesis. The protective effects of Alistipes species and detrimental associations with certain Bacteroides taxa provide mechanistic insights into the gut-brain axis dysfunction increasingly recognized in neurodegenerative diseases10,11. Recent research has demonstrated that gut dysbiosis contributes to ALS pathology through compromised gut barrier integrity, metabolic dysfunction, and immune dysregulation, with alterations in microbial communities consistently observed in ALS patients and correlated with disease progression12. Our genetic evidence strengthens the case for GM as active contributors rather than passive consequences of neurodegeneration.

The three exposure domains we investigated (cardiovascular factors, GM, and immune phenotypes) likely interact rather than operate independently. Gut barrier dysfunction resulting from microbial dysbiosis allows bacterial lipopolysaccharide translocation into circulation, triggering systemic inflammation that simultaneously elevates cardiovascular risk markers and activates peripheral immune cells13,14. This cascade suggests that our observed associations may reflect interconnected processes rather than separate pathways. For instance, the protective effect of Alistipes species we identified could operate partly through their capacity to produce anti-inflammatory metabolites that reduce both atherogenic lipid profiles and pro-inflammatory T-cell activation14. Similarly, chronic hypertension compromises intestinal perfusion and alters gut barrier integrity, potentially explaining why cardiovascular dysfunction and microbiome alterations co-occur in ALS patients15. The altered CD3 + T cell populations we detected may represent a downstream consequence of both metabolic stress from dyslipidemia and antigenic stimulation from translocated gut bacteria16. Our five identified genes, particularly ARHGAP8 and DOCK6, sit at the intersection of these pathways, responding to inflammatory signals regardless of whether those signals originate from the gut, vasculature, or immune system. This convergence pattern suggests that interventions targeting one system might yield benefits through effects on the others, though our current data cannot quantify such interactive effects.

The extensive immune cell associations revealed in our analysis reflect the complex neuroinflammatory landscape of ALS. Multiple genetic mutations linked to ALS enhance neuroinflammation, with dysfunction characterized by activated CNS microglia and astroglia, proinflammatory peripheral lymphocytes, and macrophages, independent of known genetic alterations17,18. Our identification of protective effects from CD3 + T-cell subsets and CD62L+ monocytes suggests that specific immune phenotypes may confer neuroprotection, potentially explaining the therapeutic failures of broad immunosuppressive approaches in clinical trials.

The integration of multiple analytical platforms represents a significant methodological advancement in complex disease research. Our colocalization analysis successfully identified 53 genes sharing genetic architecture between exposures and ALS, providing biological plausibility for the observed causal relationships. The convergence of differential expression patterns with MR findings for key genes like DOCK6, IGFBP5, and PDE3A demonstrates the power of multi-omics approaches in validating computational predictions.

Recent systematic reviews of MR in ALS have emphasized the importance of rigorous methodological standards, highlighting that poor-quality studies can lead to incorrect interpretations in a field that includes non-statisticians19,20. Our study addresses these concerns through comprehensive sensitivity analyses, multiple testing corrections, and validation across independent datasets.

The predictive modeling component adds practical clinical value to our genetic discoveries. Recent advances in predictive modeling applications to ALS have demonstrated the potential for developing diagnostic, prognostic, and monitoring biomarkers, with RF algorithms consistently showing superior performance for ALS-related predictions21. Our achievement of 96.2% AUC with external validation supports the clinical translational potential of our identified biomarker panel.

The five feature genes identified through our integrated approach–ARHGAP8, DOCK6, IGFBP5, DACH1, and PDE3A represent convergent evidence from multiple analytical streams and provide novel insights into ALS pathomechanisms. DOCK6, showing both reduced expression in ALS samples and protective effects in MR analysis, functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor crucial for neuronal development and synaptic function. Its downregulation may contribute to the synaptic dysfunction increasingly recognized as an early event in ALS pathogenesis22.

IGFBP5 upregulation in ALS samples, coupled with increased disease risk in genetic analysis, points to dysregulated insulin-like growth factor signaling. This pathway has been implicated in motor neuron survival and regeneration, with altered IGF signaling potentially contributing to motor neuron vulnerability23. PDE3A involvement suggests disrupted cyclic nucleotide signaling, which regulates numerous cellular processes, including neuronal excitability and survival pathways.

The identification of ARHGAP8 as the most significantly upregulated gene with the highest feature importance suggests its central role in ALS pathogenesis. As a GTPase-activating protein, ARHGAP8 regulates Rho family signaling pathways critical for cytoskeletal dynamics and neuronal morphology. Its dysregulation may contribute to the axonal transport defects and dendritic abnormalities characteristic of ALS24.

These five genes mechanistically link the systemic factors identified in our MR analyses to motor neuron pathogenesis. Chronic systemic inflammation from gut dysbiosis can activate maladaptive Rho signaling pathways regulated by ARHGAP8, whereas cardiovascular dysfunction compromises neurotrophic support for DOCK6-mediated synaptic maintenance25. Metabolic disturbances from elevated lipoproteins induce IGFBP5 expression as a stress response, and altered GM composition affects both IGFBP5 regulation through short-chain fatty acid depletion and PDE3A activity through bacterial-derived metabolites26. The observed immune cell alterations, particularly in CD3 + T-cell populations, may further modulate these pathways through cytokine-mediated effects on neuronal gene expression27. This mechanistic framework supports therapeutic interventions targeting cardiovascular health, microbiome composition, or immune function to modulate ALS risk through these key mediator genes.

Our nomogram provides a practical tool for quantitative ALS risk assessment based of readily measurable gene expression patterns. Recent population-based predictive modeling studies have demonstrated the value of systematic ALS subtype identification for improving clinical care and clinical trial design28. The excellent calibration and clinical utility demonstrated by decision curve analysis suggest potential applications in patient stratification for clinical trials and personalized treatment approaches.

The convergence of our computational predictions with experimental qPCR validation strengthens confidence in the clinical relevance of the identified biomarkers. This multi-platform validation approach addresses a critical gap in biomarker research, where computational predictions often lack experimental confirmation. The consistency between transcriptomic patterns and genetic causal evidence provides a robust foundation for future therapeutic target development.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on European ancestry populations in genetic analyses may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups, reflecting broader diversity challenges in genomic research. The observational nature of gene expression data, despite MR validation, cannot completely exclude residual confounding. Sample sizes for validation cohorts, although adequate for proof-of-concept, would benefit from expansion in larger prospective studies.

Our MR design examined cardiovascular, microbiome, and immune factors as separate exposures and could not test statistical interactions among them. This limitation is significant because these systems likely influence each other bidirectionally. Testing whether these exposures synergistically increase ALS risk or interact to modify disease susceptibility requires individual-level data with concurrent measurements of all factors. Such analyses would clarify whether multisystem interventions outperform single-factor approaches, but necessitate prospective cohorts that our summary-level GWAS data cannot provide.

The focus on blood-based biomarkers, although clinically practical, may not fully capture central nervous system-specific pathological processes. Future investigations incorporating cerebrospinal fluid or neuroimaging biomarkers could provide complementary insights into disease mechanisms.

Our investigation prioritized modifiable environmental and systemic risk factors rather than established Mendelian ALS genes (C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, FUS), which collectively account for approximately 10–15% of cases and are predominantly associated with familial ALS29. This strategic focus addresses the pathogenic mechanisms underlying sporadic ALS, which constitutes 90–95% of cases, and identifies potentially actionable risk factors with broader applicability across patient populations beyond those with known genetic mutations.

This integrated multi-omics investigation provides evidence of causal relationships between cardiovascular risk factors, GM composition, immune cell phenotypes, and ALS susceptibility through systematic MR analysis. We identified and validated five feature genes (ARHGAP8, DOCK6, IGFBP5, DACH1, and PDE3A) with robust diagnostic potential, achieving an AUC of 96.2% in predictive modeling models with external validation. The convergence of genetic causal evidence, differential expression patterns, and experimental qPCR validation provides strong support for clinical translation. Our nomogram offers an immediately applicable tool for quantitative ALS risk assessment, while the identification of modifiable risk factors suggests new preventive strategies. This work demonstrates ALS as a complex, multi-system disorder with actionable therapeutic targets, providing both immediate clinical applications and a methodological framework for precision medicine approaches in neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Study design

We employed two-sample MR to investigate causal relationships between multiple exposures (blood pressure, blood glucose levels, circulating lipids, GM composition, and immune cell populations) and ALS risk. Following significant MR associations, we conducted colocalization studies on relevant exposures. We obtained four ALS datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, providing control and patient samples for subsequent analyses. These datasets enabled differential expression analysis, correlation studies, and immune infiltration assessment of genes identified through our genetic approaches. Advanced computational techniques helped identify feature genes linked to exposures and ALS outcomes. We validated our predictive modeling models using independent datasets and performed clustering analysis on ALS samples to understand the functional mechanisms of exposure-related genes. Finally, we obtained cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data for feature genes and conducting MR analysis with ALS to evaluate causal effects at both gene and protein expression levels (Fig. 6).

Valid causal inference through MR requires three fundamental assumptions: genetic variants must strongly associate strongly with exposures, remain independent of confounding factors, and influence outcomes exclusively through their effects on exposures. Our study adhered to STROBE-MR reporting guidelines throughout30.

Data sources

GWAS summary statistics were obtained from multiple consortia through the Integrative Epidemiology Unit database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). We applied rigorous quality control procedures to identify independent, genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) suitable as instrumental variables. Selection criteria included linkage disequilibrium clumping (r² < 0.001, 10-Mb windows) and genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸). F-statistics > 10 were required to minimize weak instrument bias.

The summary data for ALS were obtained from a GWAS involving 20,806 ALS patients and 59,804 controls of European ancestry31. The GWAS data for these traits were accessible through the IEU Open GWAS project website with the identifier ebi-a-GCST005647. GM genetic data were derived from the Netherlands Microbiome Project, encompassing 7738 individuals of European ancestry across 412 microbial species, 207 taxonomic levels, and 205 metabolic pathways32. Immune cell genetics were derived from a Sardinian population study involving 3757 participants, encompassing 731 immune phenotypes including cellular counts, morphological characteristics, surface marker intensities, and absolute cell numbers33.

We searched the GEO database for “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis” using the following inclusion criteria: (1) expression profiling by microarray, real-time PCR, or RNA sequencing; (2) human samples; (3) confirmed ALS diagnosis; (4) peripheral blood or neural tissue; and (5) RNA expression data from control and ALS groups without therapeutic intervention. This process yielded four datasets: GSE68607, GSE4595, and GSE41414 were combined into training data containing 31 controls and 72 ALS patients, whereas GSE87385 served as validation data with 8 controls and 4 ALS cases.

Feature gene cis-eQTL data were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium, which analyzed whole blood samples from 31,684 individuals34, whereas cis-pQTL data were sourced from the deCODE genetics database, encompassing plasma protein measurements from 35,559 Icelandic individuals35. Single nucleotide polymorphisms demonstrating significant associations (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) with gene or protein expression in whole blood and plasma were selected as instrumental variables, with F-statistics maintained above 10 to minimize weak instrument bias.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample MR analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) with the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) to investigate causal relationships between exposures (blood pressure, glucose levels, lipids, microbiota composition, and immune cell populations) and ALS risk36. Primary analyses employed random-effects inverse variance-weighted methods, whereas single-SNP exposures utilized Wald ratio approaches. Robustness was assessed using multiple complementary statistical methods. Cochran’s Q statistic was used to detect heterogeneity, with P < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated through MR-Egger regression and MR pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) methods, with P < 0.05 indicating the presence of pleiotropy. Meta-analyses combined MR results across different exposure sources to enhance statistical power. Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC, version 1.0.1) calculated genetic correlations between all exposures and ALS, assessing shared genetic architecture and potential confounding by population stratification37.

Bonferroni correction was applied to address multiple testing in MR analyses, with significance thresholds determined by the number of exposures examined: P < 0.004 for cardiovascular-metabolic factors, P < 0.0001 for GM composition, and P < 0.00007 for immune cell phenotypes. Meta-analyses combined MR results across different exposure sources to enhance statistical power.

Colocalization analysis

Following completion of the MR analysis, we conducted colocalization studies focusing on exposures that demonstrated significant causal associations with ALS. The ieugwasr R package (version 0.1.5) facilitated data extraction from the IEU GWAS database, whereas the coloc package (version 5.2.1) performed Bayesian colocalization analysis to determine whether exposure traits and ALS outcomes shared causal variants within specific genomic regions38. Colocalization analyses examined chromosomal regions extending 75–500 kilobases around lead variants. Colocalization was considered significant when posterior probability (PP4) values exceeded 0.50, indicating that the exposure and outcome likely shared the same causal variant at a given locus, thereby implicating specific genes in the causal pathway between the exposure and ALS susceptibility.

Differentially expressed associated gene identification and analysis

Before differential expression analysis, the three GEO datasets (GSE68607, GSE4595, and GSE41414) were merged through a standardized preprocessing workflow. Raw expression data from each dataset were normalized independently using DESeq2 (version 1.42.0) for RNA-seq data. To address potential batch effects arising from different platforms and experimental conditions, the ComBat algorithm from the sva R package (version 3.50.0) was applied, employing empirical Bayes methods to adjust for known batch variables while preserving biological variance39. Gene identifiers were standardized to HGNC symbols, retaining only genes present across all three datasets for subsequent analysis.

Expression levels of the 53 colocalized genes were extracted from the merged dataset for differential expression analysis comparing control and ALS groups. Genes achieving P < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed associated genes. Perl scripts (version 5.38.2) positioned these genes on chromosomes for circos plot visualization.

Correlation coefficients between differentially expressed associated gene pairs were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient through the cor() function in R (version 4.5.1). Statistical significance of correlations was assessed using the cor.test() function with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Differential expression analyses comparing control and ALS groups defined statistical significance as P < 0.05.

ALS sample immune cell analysis

Immune cell composition analysis of ALS samples was performed using the Cell-Type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm with 1000 permutations to determine the proportions of 22 distinct immune cell types40. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis, implemented through the GSVA R package (version 1.52.0), enabled assessment of immune cell abundance differences between the control and ALS groups41. Correlations between differentially expressed associated genes and immune cell populations were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, matching normalized gene expression values with immune cell enrichment scores. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05 following Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple testing.

Feature gene identification and nomogram development

Four predictive models processed DEAG expression data: RF, support vector machine, generalized linear model, and extreme gradient boosting algorithms. Optimal model selection relied on prediction function calculations and comprehensive analysis incorporating residual boxplots and receiver operating characteristic curves. Nomograms utilized feature gene expression patterns from both control and ALS groups. Accuracy and generalizability assessments included decision and calibration curve generation. Validation datasets enabled identical model construction with receiver operating characteristic curve plotting for training model verification. External validation employed cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL MR analyses to assess causal effects of feature genes on ALS at the transcriptional and protein levels.

ALS sample clustering analysis

Consensus clustering analysis to identify robust patient subgroups based on differentially expressed gene profiles was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus R package (version 1.68.0). This analysis employed hierarchical clustering with Ward’s D2 linkage method and Euclidean distance metrics. The algorithm performed 1000 resampling iterations with 80 percent sample subsampling to assess clustering stability. The optimal number of clusters was determined by examining consensus matrices and empirical cumulative distribution functions, with clusters considered robust when consensus values within clusters exceeded 0.80. Inter-cluster differential expression analyses identified genes distinguishing ALS patient subgroups.

Quantitative real-time PCR validation

Blood samples were collected from 10 ALS patients and 10 age-matched healthy controls at The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, China). The study received ethical approval (No: IRB-AF/SC-00/04.0), and all participants provided written informed consent. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, China) with 1 μg total RNA per reaction.

Primer sequences for the five feature genes (DOCK6, IGFBP5, PDE3A, ARHGAP8, DACH1) were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software and verified by BLAST analysis. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (TIANGEN, China) on an ABI StepOnePlus system with the following conditions: 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 minute. Melting curve analysis confirmed PCR product specificity. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicate with no-template controls, and all measurements were performed by blinded investigators.

Relative gene expression was quantified using the comparative threshold cycle method (2^(-ΔΔCt)42, where ΔCt = Ct_target − Ct_GAPDH. ΔΔCt values were calculated by subtracting the mean ΔCt of healthy controls from each sample’s ΔCt. Fold change was determined as 2^(-ΔΔCt), with values above 1.0 indicating upregulation and below 1.0 indicating downregulation in ALS patients.

Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using two-tailed independent-samples t-tests after logarithmic transformation of fold change values, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software with two-sided hypothesis testing throughout. All continuous variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests prior to inferential testing. For comparisons between control and ALS groups, independent samples t-tests were employed when data satisfied normality assumptions and homogeneity of variance (verified by Levene’s test); Mann-Whitney U tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Post hoc pairwise comparisons following significant omnibus tests utilized Tukey’s honest significant difference method for parametric analyses and Dunn’s test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for non-parametric analyses. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all hypothesis tests. Specialized multiple testing corrections, including Bonferroni correction for MR analyses and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for differential expression and correlation analyses, were applied according to the specific requirements of each analytical method as described in the respective sections above.
