Effect of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine on HPV type replacement and sexual behaviors in the post-vaccine era: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiahe Zhang, Yan Chen, Siji Chen, Chunting Hua, Hao Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination has been implemented for more than 15 years to prevent HPV infection and associated precancerous lesions. However, limited data are available regarding the long-term impacts of HPV vaccination, particularly concerning cross-protection and type replacement. Using data from the 2007–2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we employed the survey-weighted Student’s t-tests, chi-square tests, and logistic regression models to examine the association between vaccine effectiveness and timing of vaccination. Among 9040 females aged 18 years and over, vaccination showed great effectiveness against both vaccine-targeted and non-vaccine-targeted high-risk HPV (hrHPV) among those vaccinated before sexual debut. After full adjustment, participants vaccinated after sex had a 48% increased risk of hrHPV infection (OR (95% CI) = 1.48 (1.16, 1.88)) compared to unvaccinated participants, and their sexual behaviors were more active as well. The most prevalent HPV subtypes were HPV 16/51/52/66, and the prevalence of subtypes 18/35/52/66/68 differed significantly across racial groups. Despite the prevalence of most hrHPV subtypes declined continuously or fluctuated over time, it is noteworthy that the infection rate of HPV 66 increased by 59% compared to the unvaccinated (95% CI: 0.18–0.92). Continuous surveillance is warranted for hrHPV types antigenically distant from vaccine strains, as early type replacement may be emerging. Given the notable difference in effectiveness based on vaccination timing, public-health education should emphasize both the benefits and limitations of HPV vaccination in the context of sexual health.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection (STI) worldwide.1 Persistent HPV infection is the primary causative agent of nearly all cervical cancers, as well as other anogenital cancers and anogenital warts, which may manifest years or decades after infection.2,3 Based on their carcinogenic potential, HPV genotypes are categorized as high-risk HPV (hrHPV) and low-risk HPV (lrHPV). Anogenital warts are predominantly caused by lrHPV 6/11, while approximately 70% of cervical cancers are attributable to hrHPV 16/18.4,5 The quadrivalent HPV vaccine was licensed since 2006 in the United States as the first formulation for primary prevention. Routine immunization is recommended for female adolescents at 11–12 years, with catch-up vaccination for individuals up to age 26. Moreover, females can choose to get vaccinated up to the age of 45.6 The bivalent and nonavalent vaccines were approved by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2009 and 2014 in succession. Due to the long latency between HPV infection and the onset of cervical cancer, clinical trials usually used vaccine-type-specific infections or precancerous cervical lesions as surrogate endpoints to evaluate vaccine efficacy.7 Clinical trials have demonstrated that HPV vaccines elicit high immunogenicity, with more than 99% of females showing antibody response after completing three-dose vaccination, and the levels of antibody generated for vaccine-type HPV were many times greater than natural infection attained.8–10

The real-world studies on vaccine effectiveness are the most valid part of evaluating the post-license vaccination. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a major program of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which can assess the prevalence of HPV nationally in the United States. This program contains vaginal HPV tests before and after HPV vaccination introduction.11 The prevalence of vaccine-type HPV was first observed decline among young-female injectors,12,13 and data have shown a reduction of more than 10% between the pre-vaccine (2003–2006) and post-vaccine (2015–2018) eras.12–14

Although the effectiveness of HPV vaccine has already been proved both in the lab and in the real world, the study on individual HPV subtypes, including vaccine-targeted types and nonvaccine-targeted types, still leaves a vacancy. A study using NHANES data from 2007 to 2012 found that vaccinated females aged 20–26 years had a higher prevalence of nonvaccine-type hrHPV.15 However, another study based on data from 2007 to 2016 verified cross-protection existed despite being confined to females vaccinated before their sexual debut aged 18–22 years.16 The efficacy of cross-protection needs further examination and analysis to confirm. Previous studies mainly focused on a specific type of vaccine or confined subjects to adolescents or females under 35 years. To our knowledge, we are the first study to contain all three licensed HPV vaccines and expanded to adult females across all ages to survey the initial impact of the vaccination program. Recent research in the Netherlands studied changes in the prevalence of HPV domestically and indicated possible type replacement.17 Exploring the prevalence of nonvaccine-type HPV allows for the investigation of possible type replacement and cross-protection. Hence, we utilized NHANES data from 2007 to 2016—the first decade following vaccine introduction – to examine type-specific HPV prevalence and assess the real-world effectiveness of the vaccination program among U.S. females. This work aims to evaluate the early population-level impact of HPV vaccination and provide insights to inform clinical and public health strategies.

Materials and methods

Data source

NHANES is an ongoing, repeat-cycle, cross-sectional national survey of the US non-institutionalized civilian population. The survey was administered by the NCHS using a stratified multistage probability sampling method.18 Information pertaining to demographics, socioeconomic status, and health was collected from household interviews. Physical examinations and laboratory tests were conducted in mobile examination centers (MECs).

Study population

This study recruited the most recent publicly available data from five NHANES survey cycles, 2007–2016, since laboratory testing for vaginal HPV DNA has not been publicly available since 2018. A total of 25,516 female participants were enrolled for the first step, and the exclusion of participants without valid laboratory testing for vaginal HPV DNA (inadequate and missing data, n = 16,168) and participants without HPV vaccination data (refused, do not know, and missing data, n = 308). 9,040 participants were ultimately included in our study and divided into hrHPV test positive group (n = 2,113) and hrHPV test negative (n = 6,927) (Figure 1). Covariate assessment

Potential covariates that may be associated with HPV tests were analyzed in the multivariable-adjusted models. Demographic covariates in this study comprised age (years), race (White non-Hispanic/Black non-Hispanic/Mexican American/others), country of birth (US/non-US), family income-to-poverty ratio (>3.5, 1.3–3.5, <1.3), health insurance (covered/not covered), marital status (married or living with partner/never married, separated, divorced, widowed/not asked <20 years). Education was assessed by combining education level in youth aged 6–19 and adults ≥20 years (less than high school/high school, GED or equivalent/above high school).

Sexual covariates are composed of first sex age (≤15, 16–18, ≥19 years), lifetime sex partner (0 or 1, 2, 3, ≥4 partners), and last annual sex partner (0, 1, 2, ≥3 partners). Condom use in the last year was also included and defined as consistent (never had sex without a condom in the last year) and inconsistent or never used (always, more than/about/less than half of the time had sex without a condom in the last year).

HPV test (positive/negative) was ascertained by a vaginal swab sample from which DNA extracted was evaluated by the Roche Linear Array HPV Genotyping test. The temporal relation between HPV vaccination and the initiation of sexual behavior was categorized as non-vaccinated, vaccinated before sex, and vaccinated after sex by subtracting self-reported age at first sexual behavior by age at first HPV vaccination. It was considered vaccinated before sex if the number >0; otherwise, it was defined as vaccinated after sex. Participants without any sexual behavior were also defined as vaccinated before sex.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses complied with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines. The analysis considered the complex multistage cluster survey design and applied an adequate NHANES sample weight.

In descriptive analysis, continuous variates were presented as means with standard error (SE), and categorical variates were presented as percentages. The differences between groups separated by the hrHPV test were assessed using either a weighted Student’s t-test for continuous variates or a weighted chi-square test for categorical variates. A weighted Chi-square test was used to evaluate the differences among 16 HPV subtypes in infection grouped by vaccination situation. Three distinct multivariable regression models were employed to investigate the association between vaccination timing and HPV tests, accounting for NHANES complex sampling weights. In Model 1, no covariates were adjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and race. Model 3 was adjusted for age, race, education level, country of birth, marital status, family income to poverty level, and health insurance. As for sensitivity analysis, sexual behavior characteristics were redefined from continuous variates to refined categorical variates to evaluate their stability. We performed subgroup analysis by stratified multivariable logistic regression models and employed sexual behavior characteristics as stratified factors. An interaction term was introduced using the likelihood ratio test to assess the heterogeneity of connections among subgroups, and these stratified factors were also regarded as prespecified possible effect modifiers. The two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Empower software (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA; www.empowerstats.com.) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software; www.graphpad.com).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Participants are nationally representative and recruited from the NHANES database from 2007 to 2016 in the US. We included 9040 females classified by of vaginal hrHPV test (negative/positive). Among hrHPV-negative participants, 650 (8.64%) had been vaccinated, compared with 330 (16.14%) of hrHPV-positive participants. 37.8% of participants were non-Hispanic white, 22.0% were non-Hispanic black, 17.0% were Mexican Americans and 23.2% were of other races. Compared to uninfected participants, those who are positive are more likely to be younger, to be non-Hispanic black and Mexican American, to be at an inferior education level, to be in the middle- and low-income class, to be uninsured, to be unmarried or living with a partner (all p < .0001) and to be born in the US (p = .0009). Regarding sexual behavior, hrHPV positive participants initiated sexual activity at a significantly younger age (OR (95% CI) = 1.44 (1.27, 1.62) for ≤15 years, OR (95% CI) = 1.23 (1.08, 1.41) for 16–18 years), had a greater number of lifetime partners (OR (95% CI) = 8.30 (5.95, 11.58) for having ≥2 sex partners) and were less likely to report consistent condom use (OR (95% CI) = 1.24 (1.04, 1.46)) (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table S1). Ethnic distribution of HPV infection

Our study encompassed 16 HPV subtypes: 14 hrHPV and 2 lrHP types included in the vaccine. Across all racial groups, HPV 16/51/52/66 were the most prevalent subtypes. The prevalences of HPV 18/35/52/66/68 differed statistically significant among different races, which was particularly higher in non-Hispanic black participants than other races. The lowest prevalence of HPV 18/52/68 infection was detected in Mexican Americans, while the prevalences of HPV 58 and HPV 66 ranked the lowest in non-Hispanic whites and other races, respectively. Epidemic trend of HPV infection

To assess temporal trends, we extracted data from cycles of 2007–2008, 2011–2012, and 2015–2016, which corresponded to the first, the middle, and the last two years in this decade. Those timings also corresponded to the introduction of bivalent, quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines. (Figures 4 and 6) For non-Hispanic whites, HPV 16 remained the most prevalent subtype. The proportion of HPV 66 has risen sequentially to become the third most common type by 2015–2016. As for non-Hispanic black participants, the proportion of HPV 16/18 infection had declined apparently, while HPV 51/52 have increased by cycles and replaced HPV 16 as the two most prevalent subtypes. Mexican Americans showed continued decreases in HPV 16/18 alongside a pronounced rise in HPV 39/52/66 after 10-year vaccination. For other races, the most common subtypes shifted from HPV 16 (13.76%) in the first cycle, to HPV 51 (13.22%) in the middle cycle, and to HPV 39 (15.89%) in the last cycle, with overall declines in HPV 16/56/58 and increases in HPV 39/51/52. The association between vaccine effectiveness and sexual behavior characteristics

Given the significant influence of sexual behavior on vaccine effectiveness, we conducted subgroup analyses to examine sexual behavior characteristics across the three vaccination-timing groups and to assess the robustness of the association among various sexual characteristics. As shown in Table 2, the association between vaccination timing and effectiveness was consistent across all strata of sexual behavior characteristics (all p for interaction <.05). We found that the vaccinated-after-sex group accounted for the highest proportion of initiating sex ≤15 years (42.37%) and reported inconsistent or never used condoms in the last year (80.61%), indicating higher sexual activity and sexual risk. In addition, 95.95% of this group had more than 4 lifetime sex partners, far more than that in the unvaccinated group (69.59%) and the vaccinated-before-sex group (78.57%). Cross-protection against nonvaccine-type hrHPV

As shown in Figure 8(a), the prevalence of HPV 16 and HPV 16 + HPV 18 decreased dramatically yearly. Except for a slight rise for HPV 33, the prevalences of other hrHPV subtypes generally presented a decline with fluctuation during this decade, suggesting possible cross-protective effects. To further determine the prevalence ratio of each subtype, we compared its proportion between unvaccinated and vaccinated participants (Figure 8(b)). To minimize interference by prior infection, only participants vaccinated before sex were enrolled in the cross-protection analysis. The proportion of HPV 16/18/31 infection significantly dropped after vaccination. Notably, the vaccinated group took a 59% higher proportion of HPV 66 prevalence than the non-vaccinated (95% CI: 0.18–0.92), which is considered to be associated with type replacement. Discussion

We selected a representatively long-term period – the first decade in a post-vaccine era – to evaluate the efficacy of the HPV vaccination program among adult females of all ages with decreased selection bias. NHANES has been collecting female genital HPV tests since the pre-vaccine era, allowing monitoring national HPV prevalence in the United States to be possible.19 With ten-year vaccination coverage expansion, the predominant infected subtype transformed to be HPV 16/66/52 for non-Hispanic white participants, HPV 52/51/66 for non-Hispanic black participants, HPV 66/51/52/39 for Mexican Americans, and HPV 39/52/51 for other races. Concurrently with the decline in vaccine-targeted subtypes, a fluctuating decrease was observed in the prevalence of nearly all other hrHPV types. Although bivalent and quadrivalent cross-protection has been described, our findings extend this evidence to all three licensed formulations.20,21 However, a notable increase in the prevalence of HPV 66 was detected among vaccinated participants. A previous survey in the Netherlands found an increased prevalence trend among subtypes with significant phylogenetic distance to the vaccine types.17 Most critically, our study found difference in vaccine effectiveness based on vaccination timing relative to sexual debut. Vaccination before sexual debut was associated with a 47% reduction in hrHPV infection risk, whereas vaccination after debut was associated with a 48% increase in risk, as well as raised risk for vaccine-type hrHPV, nonvaccine-type hrHPV, and lrHPV. Further subgroup analysis confirmed the same influence of sexual behaviors on vaccine efficacy.

In this cross-sectional study of 9040 participants, we observed a negative association between hrHPV infection and age, education level, income, first sex age, lifetime sexual partner, and frequency of last annual condom use. Non-Hispanic Black people and Mexican Americans were more likely to be found with hrHPV-infected. Our findings suggest that HPV infection can be impacted and intensified by social conditions and disparities. The syndemic theory noticed that societal disadvantages like low income and uninsured, especially nonwhite people would contribute to exacerbating the disease burden of women.22,23 Prior study has reported that black women have much higher STI rates than non-Hispanic whites.24 In our study, we found non-Hispanic Black females had the maximum ratios of early sexual initiation and the largest number of sexual partners, indicating more active and higher-risk sexual behaviors. Non-Hispanic White females were detected to have the highest ratio of inconsistent or never condom use, a pattern that may partly reflect fertility planning rather than higher sexual activity. A similar trend has been reported that black adults had a higher frequency, more partners, and an earlier age of initiation for sex than White and Asian adults before.25,26

From 2007 to 2016, the first decade after the HPV vaccine introduction, the prevalence of HPV 39/51/52/66 presented declines with fluctuation. However, their relative proportion among all infections increased by 2016. However, it was also found that black females reported fewer high-risk behaviors, indicating that prior disease control programs may neglect broader prospectives or the unbalanced distribution of which did not reach the satisfaction of the partial population.27,28 Persistent infection with hrHPV was the primary causative agent in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer.29 The heterogeneity of HPV subtype prevalence among different regions and races has been confirmed previously.30,31 In our study, the prevalence of HPV 16/51/52/66 presented consistently high during 2007–2016 in the US. The prevalence of HPV 18/35/52/58/66/68 showed significant racial disparities, of which non-Hispanic Black participants consistently took the highest infection rates. Combined with the transition of prevalent subtypes ratio for each race, the conspicuous decline in HPV 16/18 could be detected for non-Hispanic Black participants and Mexican Americans over the decade, during which the quadrivalent vaccine targeting HPV 16/18 was predominantly used. This result suggested that the HPV vaccine may have a delightful role for high-burden populations, improving vaccine coverage of high-risk populations can be an efficient way to cut down the infection ratio. The prevalences of these subtypes were conspicuously higher than vaccine-targeted subtypes (HPV 16/18). This phenomenon can be explained as an indicator of type replacement. As a result of vaccination, several specific subtypes are eliminated to some extent, and some other subtypes not vaccine-targeted come to be occupied. A cross-sectional study in the Netherlands observed increasing trends for HPV 39/52/56 and also suggested the possibility of type replacement.32 The population of the Netherlands is characterized by 79% being made up of Dutch, with nearly 10% immigrants from Western countries who are physically white and can be perceived ethnically as Dutch. Only 11.7% of the population is from non-Western countries.33 The difference in race may indirectly affect the efficacy of the HPV vaccine and the popularity of subtypes. Another study in Finnish investigated ecological diversity in non-vaccine subtypes for 8 years since vaccination and found it expanded over time, indicating a shift of niche occupation to non-vaccine subtypes.34 Such a phenomenon may be facilitated by superinfection exclusion, where prior infection with one HPV type limits subsequent infection by others.35 The competition of HPV DNA and neutralizing antibody was detected for HPV 16 and HPV 52 previously.36 Another alternative explanation may be that sexuality is becoming more open with the times. A study in Finnish concluded that there was no evidence for type replacement, and higher levels of HPV 51/52 were found in more promiscuous vaccinated females.37,38

In the analysis of whole participants, unexpectedly, participants from 2007 to 2016 who received the vaccine had about twice the odds of hrHPV infection (95% CI: 1.68–2.47) compared to unvaccinated. Vaccination history was categorized as either before or after the sexual debut of the participant based on whether the age at which the first vaccine dose was received was less than the age at which the participant first engaged in sexual activity. Participants who had not engaged in any sexual activity at the time of the interview were also considered vaccinated before sex. After stratification analysis by vaccination timing, we found that protective efficacy in vaccine-before-sex group was valid for both hrHPV and non-vaccine type hrHPV. On the contrary, participants who were vaccinated after sexual debut showed an apparent rise in infection risk (OR (95% CI) = 1.48 (1.16, 1.88)). The resembled impact was detected for vaccine-type hrHPV, non-vaccine-type hrHPV, and lrHPV. These results align with the previous opinion highlighted that HPV immunization should be administered prior to the sexual debut and thus before exposure to HPV infection for maximum effectiveness.39 Egemen et al.40 found that vaccination before sex had an 82% decline in HPV16/18 prevalence compared to vaccination after sex. Some observational studies also confirmed greater reductions in cervical cancer incidence with earlier vaccination.41,42 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommends routine HPV vaccination at 11–12 years.43 The US government’s Vaccines for Children Program offers free vaccinations to children from low-income households and those otherwise at risk.44 However, real-world cross-sectional surveys indicated that HPV vaccine initiation was relevant low, with only 68.1% of adolescents receiving at least one dose of vaccine in 2018 and 71.5% of that in 2019.45,46 These coverage rates fell well short of the US Department of Health and Human Service goal of 80% teenage HPV vaccination coverage.47 Furthermore, vaccination is often initiated and completed later than recommended. A study on data from the National Immunization Survey of Teens found that vaccination imitation was higher in elder adolescent females aged 13–17 than those at recommended ages 11–12.48

The low vaccination rate is partly on account of concerns about public concerns regarding vaccine safety like the possibility of incurring autoimmune reactions. But a recent comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis by Ferrari et al.49 which included millions of participants, found no association between HPV vaccination and an increased risk of autoimmune diseases, providing strong reassurance about its safety profile. Another main concern about vaccination comes to be worries about promoting sexual debut and risk-compensation, behavioral disinhibition, or perceived endorsement of sexual activity after vaccination50 though several studies have found no such association.51–53 In our subgroup analysis, we found that participants vaccinated after sexual debut were characterized by the highest proportion (42.37%) of individuals who initiated sex at age 15 or younger (p < .001), the highest proportion of individuals with multiple sexual partners (≥4) at 95.95% which far exceeded the other two groups (p < .001) and the highest proportion (80.61%) of individuals who inconsistently or never use condom during sexual activities (p = .011). This suggests that females who are sexually active or have risky sexual behaviors were more likely to seek vaccination, often after HPV exposure, resulting in reduced vaccine effectiveness. Additionally, the vaccinated-before-debut group had the highest proportion with multiple recent partners (44.83%, ≥3 partners in the past year), indicating possible risk compensation post-vaccination. We suppose it may be due to transformed risk perception; whereby more active sexual behavior is observed after vaccination. A large-scale follow-up study reported that the use of condoms with a casual partner showed no difference. By contrast, vaccinated females were less likely to use condoms with their regular partners.54 Previous studies noted that young married women may underestimate the necessity of HPV vaccine.55 The estimated the HPV prevalence for females with only one sexual partner up until the age of 44 reached up to 58%, indicating that HPV transmission can occur even with one lifetime partner.56 Our data is based on the first decade since the HPV vaccine was introduced globally; these results may be attributed to inadequate education and professional instruction from healthcare providers. The adoption of vaccination is somehow based on of relationship status and perceived sexual risk.57 Some people amplify the effect of the HPV vaccine, hoping it will reverse infection status or get rid of HPV infection despite having risky sexual activities. Improving provider-patient education is essential to correct misconceptions and emphasize continued prevention. Our findings on type replacement and the increased risk for late vaccinators underscore the ongoing challenge of managing HPV-related disease. Even with effective prophylactic vaccines, persistent infection with high-risk HPV types remains a key oncogenic promoter.29 Therefore, appropriate management strategies, including regular screening and timely treatment of precancerous lesions, are crucial complements to vaccination programs. As highlighted by Zhang et al.58 understanding the molecular mechanisms of HPV persistence is vital for developing tailored therapeutic approaches, such as immunotherapies or targeted antiviral agents, to address existing infections and prevent progression to cancer, especially in individuals for whom vaccination is no longer preventive.

The cross-protection was detected among participants vaccinated before sexual debut (48% lower prevalence of non-vaccine type hrHPV than non-vaccinated participants). We found an apparent decline in the prevalence of HPV 16 and HPV16 + HPV 18 by years, and nearly all subtypes of hrHPV had a decline in the prevalence after a ten-year vaccination program. Previous studies have found cross-protection against HPV31/33/45 for quadrivalent HPV vaccine59–61 and HPV 31/35/45/52 for bivalent HPV vaccine.21,62,63 Attributed to the expanded use of the HPV vaccine, the proportion of prevalence of HPV 16/18/31 showed a statistically significant decrease; thus, the dominant infected subtype turned out to be HPV 66. Thereby, the infection rate of HPV 66 presented a significant rise among vaccinated participants. No statistically significant difference was detected among the rest of the subtypes. The L1 protein of HPV 66 and HPV 16 had significant structural and antigenic differences, which affects the specificity of targeted neutralizing antibodies, suggesting that a vaccine based on HPV 16/18 would not provide cross-protection against infections with HPV66.64 The infection of HPV 66 seems to be emerged as a new issue, suggesting that vaccine developers and healthcare providers may need to pay more attention to it in the following research. Given that the questionnaire collected data on HPV immunization did not distinguish between bivalent, quadrivalent, and nonavalent HPV vaccines, further evaluation on cross-protection for respective subtypes seems impractical. Further research to investigate cross-protection for the HPV vaccine is warranted.

There are several limitations in this study. As a cross-sectional study, we cannot to obtain the exact causal relationship. Besides, attributed to the design of the NHNAES survey, data on vaginal HPV DNA tests for participants aged under 18 years was not released which limits generalizability to adolescents. Thus, a subsequent large-scale cohort study seems essential to confirm the present results. Vaccination and sexual behavior were self-reported that may be susceptible to recalling bias.65,66 As historical reports, the vaccination coverage recorded in NHANES was higher than that from National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) but lower than that from provider-verified records in the National Immunization Survey-Teen.67 The error of measurement for sexual activity could be regarded as non-differential because the proportion of asexual participants by vaccination status was found to resemble. Although we distinguished the prevalence of HPV by race and confirmed its effect, we were limited to examining or adjusting for geographical differences because the geographical data in NHAENS was restricted.68 Besides, the NHANES database does not include baseline HPV infection status at the age of vaccination, so our analysis was limited to participants vaccinated prior to sexual debut to clear the influence of baseline infection as much as possible. The sample size for cross-protection may affect the stability of the results. In addition, the result of the Thinprep Cytologic Test was not collected in NHANES, so we don’t have access to assess the impact of the HPV vaccine on cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and cervical cancer.

Conclusions

HPV vaccine shows substantial effectiveness against vaccine-type HPV in females. However, for non-vaccine-targeted subtypes, although absolute prevalence showed fluctuating declines, their relative proportion among all infections increased, suggesting early indicators of type replacement with more distantly related subtypes as HPV 66. Despite the disease burden of non-vaccine subtypes we detected is lower than that of vaccine-targeted subtypes,69 some of the remaining emerging subtypes are carcinogenic as well. Thus, we still need to be vigilant about the prevalence of these HPV subtypes, and the prevalence monitoring at the type-specific level remains indispensable.

Females vaccinated before sexual debut had far more robust protection than those vaccinated after sex. Possible sex permissiveness was detected after vaccination. Advocating the importance of vaccination before sexual debut for more significant protective effects is a vital part that should be highlighted in education. Meanwhile, policy and advocacy efforts ought to address misconceptions by clearly communicating the vaccine’s limitations and reinforcing the continued importance of safe sexual practices.
