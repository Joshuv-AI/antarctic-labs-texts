Experimental Evidence of High‐Dimensional Elementome Shifts Due to Coexistence and Its Effects on Growth Rates
Authors: M Fernández‐Martínez, A Martín, E Rodríguez‐Penedo, S Poblador, E Romero, F Sabater, R Grau‐Andrés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTElementomes (i.e., the concentration of bioelements of an organism) have been shown to be related to organism traits and function, and to change across space and time mostly due to changes in environmental conditions, but also due to coexistence amongst species. While there is vast observational evidence for that, experimental evidence remains elusive. Here we set up a diversity experiment, based on 20 aquatic microcosms where we grew one, two, three or four moss species together, to test whether coexistence amongst species is an important driver of high‐dimensional elementome shifts in mixed communities. Our results provide experimental evidence showing that species tend to shift their elemental composition in response to coexistence, and that the magnitude of this shift increases with the elemental diversity of the community. Overall, our results provide the first experimental evidence of high‐dimensional shifts in elementomes due to coexistence, supporting the biogeochemical niche hypothesis.Keywords: biogeochemical niche, competition, diversity, elemental composition, interaction, phosphorus, richness

Introduction

The elemental composition of organisms, that is, the elementome (Peñuelas et al. 2019), has been shown to be a good predictor of functional traits and, therefore, the ecological niche that species occupy (Sardans et al. 2023). This is why the biogeochemical niche, defined as the n‐dimensional elemental space occupied by a species (Peñuelas et al. 2008), has been proposed as a fundamental component of species' ecological strategies. As such, recent work on different plant taxa showed that elementomes are phylogenetically conserved and that they are tightly associated with suites of morphological and functional traits (Fernández‐Martínez, Corbera, et al. 2021; Sardans et al. 2021). Further work with vascular plants showed that productivity is better predicted by elementomes and elemental diversity than by commonly measured functional traits or diversity indices (Furey and Tilman 2023; Yan et al. 2026). By reflecting both physiological constraints and evolutionary histories, species‐specific biogeochemical niches, and thus also individual elementomes, can improve our understanding of how species interact with their environment and with each other, providing a mechanistic basis for coexistence, competitive dynamics, and ecosystem functioning.

Elementomes change over time: during evolutionary timescales, ontogeny (Ouyang et al. 2024), in response to shifting environmental conditions (Jonard et al. 2014; Peñuelas et al. 2020) and due to competition (Fernández‐Martínez, Preece, et al. 2021; Guiz et al. 2018). The magnitude of these changes depends on the degree of biochemical homeostasis of the organism. For example, plants typically have high plasticity in their elementomes, while animals tend to be more homeostatic (Sterner and Elser 2002). Competition is a central mechanism structuring plant communities, and shifts in nutrient acquisition and allocation strategies are key to competitive ability and coexistence (Violle et al. 2012). Crucially, competition is likely to cause elementome shifts, as plants may alter uptake, storage and allocation of bioelements in response to neighbours (i.e., potentially benefiting from increased niche complementarity; (Ashton et al. 2010)). However, current evidence for shifts in high‐dimensional elementomes due to species coexistence is entirely based on observational studies (Fernández‐Martínez, Preece, et al. 2021; Peñuelas et al. 2019; Sardans et al. 2021), besides some experimental evidence based on low‐dimensional elementomes, including only C, N and P (Guiz et al. 2016, 2018). While observational studies provide valuable insights, the high variability imposed by the contrasting environmental conditions makes it difficult to disentangle elementome differences emerging from biotic interactions from those underlying environmental heterogeneity. This limitation also hampers the reliable estimation of species' biogeochemical niches from field data alone. By controlling environmental factors, experimental approaches allow us to isolate the role of coexistence in driving elementome shifts.

Given the tight relationship between elementomes and organism traits, the concept of elemental diversity was recently developed as a new way to assess functional diversity (Fernández‐Martínez 2022) in order to study biodiversity‐ecosystem functioning relationships, a central question in ecology with direct implications for predicting ecosystem responses to global change (Cardinale et al. 2011). Elemental diversity, understood as the diversity of elementomes within a community, has been suggested as an alternative to overcome some limitations of the functional trait approach (Fernández‐Martínez 2025): traditional functional traits lack comparability amongst distant taxa, their selection is subjected to potential biases, and elemental diversity is likely to reflect hidden diversity otherwise missed (i.e., traits seldom measured but reflected by elementomes, such as herbivore deterrence denoted by high Si concentrations). Two main mechanisms have been proposed to explain why biodiversity may increase productivity: selection and complementarity (Loreau and Hector 2001). Selection effects relate to the higher probability of having a highly productive species in more diverse communities. Complementarity effects, instead, occur when coexisting species differ in their ecological niches and thus partition resources more efficiently. Consequently, species may grow better in mixed communities compared to monocultures (i.e., overyielding; (Cardinale et al. 2007)). In elemental terms, complementarity is expressed as differences in species' biogeochemical niches, that is, distinct elementomes reflecting divergent nutrient acquisition and allocation strategies (Fernández‐Martínez 2022). If such differences exist, species should respond differently to environmental conditions, because their distinct elemental requirements should determine how limiting factors (e.g., nutrients, temperature) affect their growth rates (Peñuelas et al. 2019). Therefore, elemental diversity is a promising new approach to better characterise the role of complementarity in driving biodiversity‐ecosystem functioning relationships. Observational data has recently shown that elemental diversity explains ecosystem productivity better than species richness and diversity (Yan et al. 2026). However, no experimental study has yet tested its validity.

The main aim of this study was to investigate the elemental shifts in coexisting plant species compared to species living in monoculture. To do so, we set up a diversity experiment based on 20 aquatic microcosms where we grew one, two, three or four moss species together, while keeping the environmental conditions mostly constant. We first tested the response of moss growth rates to the elemental composition of mosses, their nutrient uptake, taxonomic biodiversity (measured by species richness and Shannon's diversity), and elemental diversity (the diversity in the elemental composition of specimens, measured using different indices described for functional and elemental diversity). We specifically investigated whether our experimental species presented overyielding (i.e., whether species growing in diverse communities are more productive than when growing in monoculture) by estimating the effect of diversity on their growth rates. Finally, we analysed the changes in the elemental composition of the moss samples living in monoculture compared to those living in mixed communities to investigate whether and how their elementomes shift due to competition. Addressing these research questions will provide novel experimental evidence of the validity of the biochemical niche hypothesis to explore underlying mechanisms driving biodiversity‐ecosystem functioning relationships.

Methods

Experimental Design

Experimental Setup

We set up an experiment based on 20 flow‐through microcosms filled with freshwater in a closed circuit (Figure S1). Each microcosm was part of an independent circuit of 1240 mL with a constant water flow of 10 mL min−1. Hence, the water residence time was 124 min. The experimental microcosm was an 840 mL transparent methacrylate tube placed vertically. The water flowed from the bottom to the top of the microcosm, and then onto a 400 mL aeration chamber where atmospheric air was injected into the experimental water using an air pump. The water was then pumped back to the microcosm's bottom. The entire circuit was driven by a peristaltic pump, and all tubing was made of silicone. The 20 microcosms received light 14 h per day. The light source consisted of nine full‐spectrum LED tubes (Valoya L14, spectrum NS12) placed horizontally at a distance of 10 cm from the microcosms. The incident light intensity estimated at the surface of the microcosm was around 90–100 μmol m−2 s−1, and the water temperature fluctuated slightly between 23 and 26 degrees celsius during most of the experiment. The experiment ran for 42 consecutive days.

Water Chemistry

The experimental solution consisted of low‐salinity water with added nitrogen (as nitrate‐ammonium, Flourish Nitrogen) and phosphate (as potassium phosphate, Flourish Phosphorus). We fertilised the initial solution aiming to reach a concentration of 15 mg L−1 of nitrate and 100 μg L−1 of phosphate, resulting in measured concentrations of 13 mg L−1 and 83 μg L−1, respectively. These concentrations are well within the range of natural conditions in most unpolluted springs where the chosen species occur (Fernández‐Martínez et al. 2019). As a result of the N and P addition, the concentration of K was also increased because both N and P fertilisers contained K as well. We analysed three samples of the initial water solution and one water sample for each microcosm at the end of the experiment. The chemical composition of the water samples was analysed by ICP‐OES/MS (i.e., Na, K, Ca, Mg, P, Fe, Mn, Zn, B), high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, i.e., Cl−, SO4 2− and NO3 −) and colorimetry (i.e., PO4 3−, following Murphy and Riley (1962)). We calculated the change in water chemistry for each chemical compound as the final minus initial concentration divided by the initial concentration, multiplied by 100 (see Equation 1).

Species and Diversity Treatment

We selected five hygrophytic moss species for the experiment: Cratoneuron filicinum (Hedw.) Spruce, Fissidens grandifrons Brid., Fontinalis antipyretica Hedw., Leptodictyum riparium (Hedw.) Warnst and Rhynchostegium riparioides (Hedw.) Cardot. All five species are known for coexisting in Mediterranean springs (Fernández‐Martínez et al. 2019; Fernández‐Martínez, Preece, et al. 2021). The diversity treatment consisted of growing these species in monoculture and in communities of two, three and four species together. In total, we had five replicates per species richness level. We placed, in each microcosm, a total of 2 g of fresh weight of the selected community (2 g divided between all species in the microcosms). Hence, monocultures consisted of 2 g of the same species, while for mixed communities, the 2 g were divided between the species present. Out of all the possible species combinations, we chose one amongst those that kept the number of samples per species as similar as possible across species (Table S2). Due to an error in sample identification, the experiment did not include R. riparioides as a monoculture. Hence, our experiment did not include all species‐combinations possible per species richness level, which means that in our results, richness effects could be partly confounded with species‐composition and neighbour‐identity effects. Given that mosses often grow in colonies, and are thus dominated by facilitative interactions (Cornelissen et al. 2007; Elumeeva et al. 2011), our experiment assumes that intraspecific competition is not playing a relevant role with regard to diversity‐functioning relationships. Fresh weight was calculated by soaking mosses in water and then gently drying them by pressing them against laboratory paper for 5 s, leaving no external water. This process was repeated three times for each moss sample (i.e., each species in each microcosm, N = 50). The averages of the three repeated measurements were used to calculate the fresh weights of both the initial and final samples. We used moss fresh weight instead of dry weight to have comparable measurements from the beginning to the end of the experiment, as initial samples had to be in optimal conditions and not rehydrated. We then calculated the sample growth rate as the final minus the initial weight divided by the initial weight. We calculated the growth rate at the microcosm level by performing the same calculation based on the total moss mass of the community.

Moss Elemental Composition

We analysed the elemental composition of all moss samples at the end of the experiment. For monospecific microcosms, we separated the sample into two equal portions in order to have an estimation of elemental diversity (and distance) as well. To do so, we first dried the samples in an oven at 60°C for 48 h and then ground them to a powder using a ball mill (MM400, Retsch, Germany). Elemental concentrations of C and N and the isotopic ratios δ 13C and δ 15N were measured by an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS). The instrument consisted of a Flash EA1112 and TC/EA coupled to a Delta C IRMS through a ConFlo III interface (Thermo Finnigan, Thermo Electron Corporation, Bremen, Germany). Concentrations of C, N, P, K, Mg, Na, Ca, S, Fe, Zn, Mn and B were determined using inductively coupled plasma—optical emission/mass spectrophotometry (ICP‐OES/MS) (ELAN 600 and Optima 8300, Perkin Elmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Samples were predigested overnight at 90°C in a 2:1 nitric acid: hydrogen peroxide solution. All analyses were carried out at the Scientific and Technical Centres of the University of Barcelona.

Diversity Indices

We used a total of 11 diversity indices to assess the effect of biodiversity on microcosm biomass production. We first selected traditional taxonomy‐based metrics, species richness and Shannon's diversity index (H). We also included metrics originally designed for functional trait analyses to estimate elemental diversity: functional dispersion (FDis), functional divergence (Fdiv) and Rao's quadratic entropy index (RaoQ) (Villéger et al. 2008). As adapted here, FDiv measures how abundance (moss biomass) is distributed towards the extremes of the occupied elemental trait space; FDis calculates the average distance of each species to the centroid of all species in a multidimensional elemental space, weighted by moss abundance and RaoQ measures the multi‐dimensional divergence in elemental space. Finally, we selected metrics specifically formulated for elemental analysis: the elemental dissimilarity index (ED) and the proportional elemental variability (PVe) (Fernández‐Martínez 2022, 2025). Elemental dissimilarity is an abundance‐weighted measure based on Euclidean distances between species, and PVe is the average of all proportional variability estimates for each element across species. We calculated ED, PVe, FDis, FDiv and RaoQ both with and without standardising the concentrations of bioelements (i.e., hereafter, these acronyms followed by the suffix ‘.std’ indicate the standardised version of the index). The non‐standardised ED was calculated based on the natural logarithm of elemental concentrations. The correlation amongst all elemental diversity indices is shown in Figure S2.

Statistical Analyses

We first examined differences in growth rate per species at the sample level (N = 50). To do so, we performed linear mixed‐effects models using the glmmTMB function from the glmmTMB R package (Brooks et al. 2017). We used the sample‐level logged growth rate as the response variable, species as the predictor, and the microcosm as the random factor. To further explore whether some species presented overyielding when living in mixtures compared to monocultures, we used mixed effects models in which the response variable was the sample‐level growth rate (logged) and the predictor variables were: (i) an interaction between species identity and a species or elemental diversity metric (see below); (ii) the moss elemental concentration of C, N, P, K, Mg, Na, Ca, S, Fe, Zn, Mn and B and (iii) the average percent removal of the chemical compounds that were mostly removed from the water solution at the end of the experiment (Table S1), this is, B, Zn, P, Mg, K and SO4 2−. The microcosm was again included as the random factor in the models. The interaction between diversity and species identity should indicate whether different species show overyielding (positive coefficients of diversity) or not. The elemental composition of the mosses was used to control for the effect that the elemental composition of mosses can have on moss growth, while the removal of chemical elements from water was included to control for nutrient uptake by the community, as larger growth may require larger uptake rates.

We explored these models for 11 different diversity metrics: species richness, Shannon's diversity, PVe, ED, ED.std., FDis, FDis.std., FDiv, FDiv.std., RaoQ and RaoQ.std. For each diversity metric, we performed a best‐subsets model selection using the dredge function in the MuMIn R package (Barton 2018). We forced the model selection to always retain the interaction between species identity and the specific diversity metric, while keeping ≤ 5 additional predictors. We retained the best model, based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), per diversity metric, and compared them using BIC to investigate which diversity metric better explained biodiversity effects on moss performance.

We then investigated the biodiversity effects at the community level (N = 20). To do so, we performed linear models in which the response variable was the community growth rate, and the predictor variables were the diversity indices and moss concentrations of C, N, P, K, Mg, Na, Ca, S, Fe, Zn, Mn and B. Following the previous analyses, we performed a best‐subsets model selection procedure, based on the minimum BIC, for each family of models belonging to each of the diversity metrics. Due to the reduced number of replicates, we restricted the number of variables included in the models to three. We then retained the best model per diversity index and compared them based on the BIC to investigate which diversity index explained the growth of the community best. We used the visreg R package (Breheny and Burchett 2017) to plot partial residual plots in order to visualise the relationships emerging from the models.

We also investigated whether higher nutrient uptake from water was related to higher concentrations in moss tissues at the community level. We did this using linear models where the response variables were the differences in concentration of a given element in water before and after the experiment, and the predictor variables were the average elemental compositions of the same element in the community.

Finally, we investigated whether mosses shifted their elemental composition with increasing diversity (i.e., when living in mixtures compared to monocultures). To do so, we first calculated the elementome Euclidean distances between all pairs of samples in the experiment using the usedist package in R (Bittinger 2020). For each species, we selected all the comparisons between samples at richness Level 1 (monoculture) and samples from mixtures (2, 3 and 4 species). Due to a misidentification during the setup of the experiment, R. riparioides was not included in the monoculture, so only comparisons between richness Level 2 and the rest (3 and 4) were possible for this species. Hence, this set of results for R. riparioides should be interpreted with caution. Cross‐species comparisons were removed from the matrix. We then fitted mixed‐effects models in which the response variable was the logged Euclidean distance between samples of the same species and the predictor variable was one of the 11 diversity indices studied here. The highest value of diversity per pairwise comparison was selected as the diversity predictor in the model (hence, the comparison was always between monoculture and a higher level of diversity). We also included the microcosm identity of both samples being compared as a random factor, as well as the species identity of the samples. We fitted this model for the 11 indices of diversity mentioned above and compared their BIC to investigate which diversity metric explained elemental shifts better. In order to visualise the trajectories in the elemental shifts of the samples, we performed a principal component analyses (PCA) using mixOmics R package (González et al. 2012; Rohart et al. 2017) based on the logged elemental composition (C, N, P, K, Mg, Na, Ca, S, Fe, Zn, Mn and B) of all 50 moss samples. For each species, we then calculated the average PCA scores for the first three PCA axes per level of species richness and plotted them in two‐dimensional spaces.

Results

Growth Rates and Their Controls

Our results indicate that L. riparium and C. filicinum presented higher growth rates than F. antipyretica , F. grandifrons and R. riparioides (Figure 1). Growth rates of L. riparium and C. filicinum , however, were clearly affected by the diversity of the microcosm community: while L. riparium presented evidence of overyielding (faster growth when growing in mixed communities), C. filicinum growth rates decreased with increasing diversity (Figure 2a). The growth rates of the other three species ( F. antipyretica , F. grandifrons and R. riparioides ) were insensitive to the diversity of the microcosm community. The diversity effect on species‐specific growth rates was best captured by species richness and by elemental divergence (FDiv, Figure S3a), but very closely followed by elemental dissimilarity using standardised elemental concentrations (ED.std., Figure S4a), showing an increase of only 3.2 units in BIC (Table S3). Results and their interpretation remained unchanged when refitting the models excluding monospecific microcosms, for which we lack replication at the microcosm level (Tables S4, S5 and S6).

The average percentage of nutrients removed from the water was not a significant predictor of growth rates, either at the individual or the community level. Elemental Shifts Due to Coexistence

Principal component analyses (PCA) based on the elemental composition of mosses showed a clear separation amongst all species (Figures 5a,b and S5). Averaged PCA axis position per species and richness treatment showed that the elemental composition of mosses followed clear trajectories from monocultures (species richness = 1) to richness Treatment 4 (four species), differing amongst species (Figure 5c,d). In general, C. filicinum , L. riparium , F. grandifrons and R. riparioides tended to decrease PC1 with increasing diversity in the community. These changes represent lower concentrations of P, Mg and B, and higher concentrations of Fe and Mn. For PC2, however, trajectories differed more amongst species. While F. antipyretica , F. grandifrons and R. riparioides tended to decrease in PC2, C. filicinum and L. riparium tended to increase in PC2 scores, suggesting an increase in Mn, P and Ca but a decrease in Na, K, S and Fe. For PC3 scores, all species but F. antipyretica followed an increasing trajectory, linked to increasing mostly Fe and P but decreasing Na, Ca and B. Furthermore, our analyses of within‐species pairwise elemental distances between monocultures and mixed communities revealed a strong relationship between elemental diversity in the community and the elemental distance with respect to monocultures. In this case, elemental diversity estimated by the RaoQ.std index was the best predictor of diversity, explaining differences in elemental distances between mixtures and monocultures (Figure 5e). Functional dispersion (FDiv.std) and ED.std followed RaoQ.std as best performers, with less than 3 units of difference in BIC compared to RaoQ.std (Table S7).

Discussion

Our results show varying responses of growth rates to species or elemental diversity, as well as significant effects of moss elemental concentration on moss growth rates. However, we did not find a general increase in productivity at the community level due to increased diversity, probably due to the absence of environmental variability, precluding complementarity effects from playing a role. On the other hand, we provide experimental evidence showing that species tend to shift their elemental composition as a response to, most likely, interspecific competition, and that the magnitude of this shift increases with the elemental diversity of the community.

Diversity and Nutrient Effects on Growth Rates

We found that growth rates were significantly associated with the diversity in the microcosm community for two out of the five moss species we examined. In particular, L. riparium had higher growth rates in more diverse communities, potentially indicating a stronger effect of intraspecific competition compared to the other species. This pattern is consistent with ecological complementarity theory, which predicts that species benefit from coexistence with others due to reduced niche overlap (Cardinale et al. 2007). Conversely, C. filicinum grew better in monocultures than in mixed communities, and growth of F. antipyretica , F. grandifrons and R. riparioides were unaffected. These results indicate that complementarity effects on community growth rates were inconsistent and often minor in our study system. This is unexpected, especially given the distinct species biogeochemical niches we observed (Figure 5a,b), which should be associated with strong complementarity effects (Fernández‐Martínez, Preece, et al. 2021). One possible explanation is that, besides L. riparium , intraspecific competition in our study was minimal. That could entail a shift from intraspecific facilitative effects in monospecific microcosms to competition amongst species in mixed communities. Another possibility is that there may have been an early depletion of essential elements in the microcosm water due to moss absorption, thus limiting growth during most of the experiment and leading to negligible differences in growth rates amongst communities. For example, N was almost completely depleted in the microcosm water by the end of the experiment (Table S1), which could indicate early depletion. This is consistent with our finding that the two species with less apparent growth limitations (i.e., those with the highest growth rates, namely L. riparium and C. filicinum ) also exhibited significant responses to the diversity treatments. Related to this, the observed lack of direct relationship between diversity and growth rates at the community level could also be explained in part by the very limited environmental variability in our study system. We hypothesise that the main reason for this lack of a direct relationship between diversity and growth rates is the inability of complementarity effects to take place in the absence of environmental variability. Alternatively, this result could emerge because the bigger ecological niche covered by more diverse communities is linked to different species optima across different axes of variability (e.g., some species may function better at lower/higher nutrient availability or at cooler/warmer temperatures), so the more optimal resource use of highly diverse communities should be maximised where there is a wide range of environmental variability.

Species‐specific responses of growth to diversity were best described by species richness and by the elementome‐based diversity metrics such as functional dispersion (FDiv) and standardised elementome dissimilarity (ED.std) (Table S3). These results indicate that the drivers underlying diversity effects on growth are equally well captured by taxonomic and elemental diversity metrics. The good performance of elemental diversity indices suggests that elemental niche partitioning could underlie these patterns, that is, species with different elementomes may coexist better. However, these findings do not support the theory that elementome‐based diversity metrics are better predictors of biodiversity‐ecosystem functioning relationships than traditional diversity metrics (Fernández‐Martínez 2022). Future experiments including larger functional and elemental diversity gradients may help disentangling the effect of species richness from that of elemental diversity.

Surprisingly, moss samples with higher tissue P and N grew more slowly, despite the well‐established positive effects of these two macronutrients on plant growth (Elser et al. 2007; Wright et al. 2004). One possible explanation is that rapid biomass production diluted tissue nutrient concentrations, thus producing a negative correlation between growth rate and some nutrients (Ouyang et al. 2024; Sterner and Elser 2002). Further analyses support this hypothesis, as fast‐growing mosses generally showed lower N and P concentrations (Figure 2b and Figure S4) but higher contents overall (Figure S6). Another non‐mutually exclusive possibility is that mosses could have taken up nutrients in excess of immediate growth needs and allocated them to non‐metabolic pools, which has been observed in plants, especially when another element limits growth (Ågren 2008; Moeneclaey et al. 2022). In contrast, B concentration was positively associated with growth rates, which could be attributed to the crucial roles of B in maintaining cellular membrane integrity and facilitating metabolic processes (Brown et al. 2002; Lilay et al. 2024). At the community level, growth rates were positively related to Zn, supporting similar findings in grasslands (Radujković et al. 2021), and negatively to C. Zn is essential for enzyme activity and photosynthetic proteins (Lilay et al. 2024), while high C concentrations may indicate greater investment in structural tissues (e.g., cell walls, or even secondary compounds that could act as allelopathic), linked to conservative strategies and slower growth (Grau‐Andrés et al. 2022; Wright et al. 2004). As for the relationship between specific element concentrations in moss tissue and their uptake from water, we found that higher removal from water was related to lower concentrations in moss for several elements (e.g., P, Mn; Figure 4). One likely explanation is that the moss samples that absorbed the most of a given nutrient were also those with a greater initial deficit of that nutrient. Therefore, samples with low concentrations of some elements could have absorbed the most of that element from the water.

Interspecific Competition as a Driver of Elementome Shifts

Our results showed that different moss species occupied distinct regions in elemental space (Figure 5a,b), in support of the biogeochemical niche hypothesis (Peñuelas et al. 2008; Sardans et al. 2021). Such strong elemental differentiation suggests that there was relatively low overlap in biogeochemical niches amongst our species, which should enhance positive effects of complementarity. For each species, elementomes were altered in response to increased diversity level, indicating that there was plasticity in elemental composition in response to coexistence, that is, mosses alter nutrient uptake or allocation depending on the competitive environment. This could reflect resource partitioning: when competitors are present, species reduce concentrations of certain elements while increasing others, possibly due to altered uptake dynamics. Furthermore, PCA of the two main axes of variation showed that four out of five species elementomes tended to converge with increasing diversity. This result was unexpected and contradicts biogeochemical niche theory, which predicts greater divergence in species elementomes under coexistence (Fernández‐Martínez 2022; Peñuelas et al. 2019). One possible explanation is that competition‐induced depletion of elements in mixed assemblages may drive species in that community towards elemental convergence (Meinzer 2003). The depletion of elements, thus, could have forced the elemental composition of the species to move towards a common region in elemental space. In line with this, PCA showed that species elementomes responded to diversity following broadly similar trajectories, especially along PC1 and PC3. This suggests that responses to coexistence are to some extent coordinated amongst species, which may underpin convergence of strategies. Finally, we found that the elemental distance from monoculture to mixture was strongly related to diversity, further indicating coexistence effects on elementome shifts. Elementome shifts with diversity were better captured by elemental diversity metrics (standardised RaoQ, FDiv and ED) than by taxonomic metrics such as Shannon or species richness. This finding suggests a pivotal role of elemental niche partitioning in driving shifts in elementomes within mixed communities. Hence, we overall found that elemental niches shift in response to coexistence, but these shifts did not translate into enhanced individual or community productivity, potentially, due to the absence of environmental variability.

Conclusions

We provide the first experimental evidence of shifts in high‐dimensional plant elementomes due to coexistence. Our results partially support the biochemical niche hypothesis (Peñuelas et al. 2008) and align with ecological theory positing that elementome shifts are important consequences of plant competitive dynamics and community assembly (Fernández‐Martínez 2022, 2025). Given that elementomes can be tightly linked to functional traits and may thus drive ecosystem functioning, our finding that elemental diversity indices outperform taxonomic indices in explaining elementome shifts could lead to improved predictions of biodiversity–ecosystem functioning relationships, supporting recent research based on observations at the ecosystem scale (Yan et al. 2026). However, the clear associations we found between elementome shifts and diversity were not apparent for growth. Thus, further research is needed to confirm our predictions regarding elemental diversity and ecosystem functioning. This may be due to limitations of our experiment (e.g., short duration, lack of environmental variation, and low content of certain elements), which may have hindered growth. Further experiments including larger diversity gradients and controlled variability of environmental conditions are thus needed to validate the present results and to further test whether elemental diversity offers a quantifiable advantage over traditional metrics of diversity.
