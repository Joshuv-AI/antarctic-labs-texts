Multiphase horizontal migration of BTEX in aquifers during in-situ oil shale exploitation
Authors: Li Qingyu, Wu Hao, Li Jiqing, Hu Shuya, Xiu Zongming, Ma Rachel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the multiphase horizontal migration characteristics of benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX) in aquifers during in-situ oil shale pyrolysis. Through two-dimensional hydrodynamic dispersion experiments and numerical simulations, the main parameters, including the dispersion coefficient and residual saturation of the nonaqueous phase liquid (NAPL) phase were determined. The results indicated that in the same media, the dispersion coefficients and residual saturations of BTEX compounds differ. The longitudinal dispersivities decreased in the order of benzene (0.114 m) > ethylbenzene (0.053 m) > toluene (0.050 m) > xylene (0.046 m), whereas the transverse dispersivities decreased in the order of toluene (3.90 × 10⁻⁷m) > xylene (3.85 × 10⁻⁷m) > ethylbenzene (2.96 × 10⁻⁷m) > benzene (1.35 × 10⁻⁷ m). The calibrated residual saturation of the NAPL phase was highest for benzene (0.17), followed by toluene (0.15), ethylbenzene (0.14), and xylene (0.12). Numerical simulations were conducted to quantify interphase partitioning, revealing different equilibrium volume fraction ratios (gas: aqueous: NAPL) at the injection point and observation points. This study provides critical parameters and a numerical model for simulating BTEX multiphase migration, which can aid in predicting contamination plume evolution, assessing risks, and designing effective groundwater protection and remediation strategies during oil shale exploitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48144-8.

Introduction

Oil shale, which is an important alternative energy source, has received much attention1–4. In-situ pyrolysis of oil shale is a technique in which solid organic matter (kerogen) is converted to liquid shale oil and gas by heating it directly underground and the products can be collected at the surface through boreholes5–7.

However, the release of organic matter and increased thermal stress can cause fractures in the surrounding rock formations and aquifers, creating a network of water-conducting fractures that establish hydraulic connections with aquifers8–12. These fractures provide favorable pathways for pollutant transport13–16. The benzene series (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX)) produced during the high-temperature pyrolysis process may cause groundwater pollution17–19.

BTEX compounds, which are known for their toxicity, bioaccumulative potential, volatility, and carcinogenic properties20,21, are light nonaqueous phase liquids (LNAPLs) because their density is lower than that of water. In porous media, BTEX exists in multiple phases, including the non-aqueous phase liquid (NAPL) phase, dissolved phase, and vapor phase, resulting in complex migration mechanisms22–25. The dissolved phase can diffuse extensively under hydrodynamic forces, whereas the free phase, affected by capillary and viscous forces, can be adsorbed onto surfaces within porous aquifer media, altering porosity, permeability, and groundwater flow velocity. This phase can continuously release pollutants over extended periods26. Hence, understanding the characteristics of the multiphase migration of BTEX in aquifers is important for ensuring environmental protection.

Over the past decades, extensive research has been conducted on multiphase transport and partitioning of LNAPLs in subsurface environments. Sookhak Lari et al.27,29,31 investigated gasoline multiphase partitioning in the vadose zone, provided a critical review of natural source zone depletion (NSZD) supporting modeling approaches, and examined the feasibility of developing digital twins for NSZD. Lekmine et al.28 further evaluated the reliability of equilibrium dissolution assumptions for residual gasoline, while Lenhard et al.30 evaluated analytical models for predicting subsurface LNAPL distributions.

In the context of geological engineering, Shores et al.32 investigated the transport rates of BTEX in different media under hydraulic fracturing conditions, while Valsala and Govindarajan33,34 employed numerical simulations to examine the effects of colloids, microbes, and geological fractures on BTEX migration. Recently, Sookhak Lari et al.35 presented a comprehensive roadmap for understanding key knowledge gaps in NSZD, emphasizing the need for integrated experimental and modeling approaches to address challenges in measurement, simulation, and prediction of NSZD rates. They identified the lack of reliable, component-specific transport parameters for complex contaminant mixtures as a critical barrier to advancing NSZD as a management strategy—a gap that the present study directly addresses.

In this study, a conceptual model of multiphase BTEX migration was first developed based on governing physical processes. This conceptual model was then implemented as a numerical model in COMSOL Multiphysics. Parallel laboratory experiments were conducted in a two-dimensional sand tank to observe the migration of both NAPL-phase and dissolved-phase BTEX under controlled conditions. The experimental data, including areas of NAPL plumes and dissolved-phase concentration breakthrough curves, were used to calculate key model parameters that could not be determined a priori, particularly the residual saturation of the NAPL phase for each compound. Finally, the calibrated numerical model was used to simulate the multiphase transport processes and to quantify the phase partitioning behavior of BTEX compounds in the aquifer. The integration of physical and mathematical simulation methods enables a comprehensive analysis of the processes and mechanisms underlying BTEX contamination in aquifers.

Methods

Experimental design and materials

Experimental materials

The target contaminants used in the experiment are benzene (C₆H₆), toluene (C₇H₈), ethylbenzene (C₆H₅C₂H₅), and xylene (C₈H₁₀), which can be detected in oil shale residues. To isolate the multiphase migration behavior of each compound and avoid competitive dissolution and sorption effects, each BTEX compound was prepared and injected separately. Quartz sand washed with deionized water was employed as the aquifer medium to enable visual monitoring of BTEX migration.

Experimental installation

The primary experimental setup is a self-designed organic glass sand tank with dimensions of 80 cm × 60 cm × 15 cm(Fig. 1a.). Two evenly perforated partition plates are installed at 10 cm intervals on each side, which divide the sand tank into three sections, namely, 1, 2, and 3. Section 2, which encompasses the partition plates and tank walls, serves as the observation area for pollutant migration. Sections 1 and 3 on both sides are used to regulate water levels and flow rates. At a depth of 10 cm from the tank bottom, the intake and discharge ports are positioned on opposite sides and are controlled by peristaltic pumps and valves. The sampling ports are strategically arranged along the front panel. The entire sand tank assembly is vertically mounted36,37(Fig. 1b.).

Experimental process

Hydrodynamic dispersion experiment

During sand filling, the sample sand was evenly spread into the tank and compacted every 2 cm to ensure a uniform density and complete contact between the emplaced sand and the tank walls. The sand tank was saturated with water to a height of 30 cm from the bottom, creating a saturated zone (0–30 cm depth) and an unsaturated zone (30–60 cm depth). A steady-state flow field was established by adjusting the peristaltic pump at the left inlet and the valve at the right outlet, with a constant flow rate of 2 mL/min maintained throughout the experiments. Because BTEX compounds are highly volatile, the opening of the top of the tank was wrapped with plastic film to decrease volatilization effects on the experimental results.

For each experimental run, one of the four BTEX compounds was stained with Sudan IV for visual observation, introduced as a finite-duration pulse over 10 min (total volume 20 mL) using a peristaltic pump(Fig. 1b.). The injection tube was inserted to a depth of 30 cm, releasing the compound directly into the top of the saturated zone. The infiltration process of dyed NAPL-phase BTEX was recorded through time-lapse photography. Sampling was conducted every 5 min after benzene compounds were added, and the TOC content was monitored in real time. When the dyed BTEX approached the sampling port, the interval was adjusted to 3 min. Sampling ceased when the dyed BTEX passed through the tank and exited into the right water tank (sampling intervals determined through repeated experiments). After each experiment, the sand tank was completely emptied, cleaned, and repacked with fresh quartz sand to ensure identical initial conditions for the next compound.

Quantification of volatilization losses

Volatilization losses were assessed using benzene, the most volatile BTEX compound, in separate experiments under sealed (plastic film cover) and open conditions. For each test, 100 mL of benzene was injected into the sand tank following the same procedure as the main experiments. After 2 h, the sand was sectioned and sampled at 12 evenly distributed locations, and residual benzene was extracted with methanol and analyzed by GC-MS. The plastic film reduced volatilization losses by approximately 85% compared to the open system, from 23.1% to 3.5% of the injected mass. Losses for toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene were estimated by scaling the measured benzene loss according to the ratio of their vapor pressures (Text S1).

Data analysis and parameter calculation

Data processing

The external standard method was used to quantitatively analyze BTEX38,39. The pretreated samples were analyzed via gas chromatography mass spectroscopy (GC-MS) (Agilent 5975 C). The column used was a TG-WAX column (30 m×0.25 mm×0.25 μm), with an injector temperature of 250 °C and a line temperature of 220 °C. The following program was used: column temperature of 40 °C (4 min) at 5 °C/min, 100 °C (0 min) at 25 °C/min and to 250 °C (2 min); flow rate of 0.8 mL/min; split ratio of 40:1; and helium carrier gas. The recovery rates ranged from 78% to 108%, with detection and quantification limits for the four BTEX compounds ranging from 0.010 to 0.238 µg/g and 0.86 to1.81 µg/g, respectively.

Because the benzene series compounds dyed with Sudan Red IV dye exhibit distinct chromatic differences from quartz sand, their migration distances can be determined through visual observation. During the experiments, digital cameras were used to document the migration distribution and area of the NAPL phase relative to BTEX at different times, and migration process diagrams of the NAPL phase in the aquifers were created.

Numerical model establishment

Constitutive model

On the basis of laboratory-scale conceptual modeling from indoor sand tank tests and incorporating multiphase migration and transformation theories of organic pollutants, a numerical model was developed. The numerical model was implemented using COMSOL Multiphysics (version 6.1). To simulate the multiphase transport and interphase partitioning of BTEX compounds in porous media, the “Porous Media and Subsurface Flow” module was coupled with the “Transport of Diluted Species” module from the physics interface. Considering that benzene compounds undergo dissolution and volatilization after entering aquifers, occurring in porous media as NAPL, liquid, and gaseous phases, the model describes their migration and transformation processes through the seepage continuity equation, multiphase flow mass control equation and solute transport Eqs. 24,43,44.

Boundary conditions and mesh configuration

On the basis of two-dimensional laboratory experiments in sand tanks, the sand tank is modeled as two vertically arranged rectangular chambers, designated rectangular chambers represent a saturated aquifer and a nonsaturated aquifer. The two-dimensional computational domain was discretized using a structured quadrilateral mesh consisting of 12,480 elements. Local refinement was applied in critical regions: the maximum element size was reduced to 0.001 m near the injection point and 0.002 m across the water table, while elements along the expected migration path were maintained at 0.003–0.004 m. Elsewhere, a coarser resolution with a maximum element size of 0.0052 m was used, with a global minimum element size of 6.0 × 10–5 m.

Prior to BTEX release, both chambers established stable Darcy flow fields, and the initial liquid phase saturation is determined by the pore water content. Before pollutant injection, the initial slug phase saturation and liquid-phase benzene compound concentration were set to zero. The saturated aquifer was simulated as a mass flux boundary, whereas the lower boundary served as a no-flux boundary. The left boundary represents groundwater inflow from the aquifer, and the right boundary functions as an outflow boundary. A time-dependent solver with adaptive time stepping was used. The injection was simulated as a 10 min mass flux at the injection point, after which the flux was set to 0.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of dissolved-phase BTEX transport

The temporal variations in the concentrations of the four BTEX compounds at monitoring points A and B within the sand tank are shown in Figure 2.

Fig. 2Temporal variation of dissolved-phase BTEX concentration at observation points A and B.

Temporal variation of dissolved-phase BTEX concentration at observation points A and B.

As shown in Fig. 2, the benzene concentrations at locations A and B ranged from 0 to 57.181 mg/L and 0–0.482 mg/L, respectively, and the peak concentrations occurred approximately 56 and 53 min, respectively, after BTEX injection. The benzene concentrations observed at both monitoring points initially increased but then decreased. After 77 min, the concentration at point A had decreased to 4.199 mg/L, and at point B, it had decreased to 0.043 mg/L, after which both concentrations stabilized.

The maximum concentration of toluene monitored at location A was 18.092 mg/L, which occurred 56 min after release. At location B, the peak concentration was 0.189 mg/L, which was detected 50 min after release. Although the benzene and toluene concentration ranges at A differed significantly, their temporal trends were similar, and their concentrations peaked at approximately 56 min and then declined, approaching stabilization by 70 min. This is attributed to the stronger adsorption capacity of toluene. The toluene concentration monitored at location B also initially increased but then decreased.

Ethylbenzene was detected at relatively low concentrations at both monitoring points, with maximum concentrations of 3.121 mg/L at A and 0.067 mg/L at B, both of which were recorded at 56 min. The lower detection rate of ethylbenzene at point B is likely attributable to its lower migration capacity in the aquifer media.The maximum concentration of xylene monitored at location A was 6.953 mg/L, which occurred at approximately 53 min. At location B, the peak concentration was 0.171 mg/L, which was observed at approximately 59 min.

The concentrations of all four BTEX components first gradually increased but then decreased, with peak concentrations occurring between 53 and 56 min. At the same initial release concentrations, the monitored concentration ranges at point A, in descending order, were benzene (1.162–57.181 mg/L), toluene (0.114–18.092 mg/L), xylene (0.194–6.953 mg/L), and ethylbenzene (0.078–3.121 mg/L). Furthermore, when the migration time of the four BTEX components in the aquifer reached approximately 70 min, a tailing phenomenon was observed. This tailing phenomenon is likely attributed to the slow desorption of BTEX from the sand surface and/or the continued dissolution of residual NAPL trapped in pore spaces, which sustains aqueous-phase concentrations even after the main contaminant plume has passed.

On the basis of the monitoring data from point B in the aquifer, the concentrations of all four BTEX compounds were determined to be less than 1 mg/L, indicating limited lateral migration capacity. These sequential detections clearly reveal distinct migration rates among the four BTEX components.

Using the analytical solution (Eq. 1), apparent dispersion coefficients for the four BTEX compounds were calculated for each compound (Table 1). The results indicated that the longitudinal dispersivities of the BTEX compounds in the coarse sand medium ranged from 0.046 to 0.114 m, with variations observed among the different compounds within the same homogeneous medium.

Conventional hydrogeological theory treats dispersivity as an intrinsic property of the porous medium, independent of the solute type. This assumption holds for conservative tracers such as NaCl, which do not interact with the solid matrix. However, the apparent dispersivities obtained for BTEX compounds showed a clear inverse relationship with adsorption coefficients. This discovery demonstrates that the transport behavior of organic contaminants is not governed solely by the physical properties of the aquifer medium. Instead, compound-specific physicochemical interactions significantly influence how contaminants spread through porous media. This observation has important implications for practical risk assessment.

Characteristics of NAPL-phase BTEX transport and diffusion in aquifers

During the experiments, a digital camera was used to record the distribution and migration of the NAPL-phase BTEX at various time intervals, and a schematic of the NAPL-phase migration process in the aquifer was constructed (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6).

Fig. 3Spreading and Distribution of the Benzene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

Spreading and Distribution of the Benzene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

As shown in Fig. 3, after the release of benzene into the aquifer, it initially diffused in an elliptical pattern before it transitioned to a largely horizontal migration trajectory. The NAPL phase gradually spread along the direction of groundwater flow, resulting in the continuous expansion of the contaminant plume. After approximately 10 min, a symmetrical fin-shaped plume had formed, migrating 11.5 cm away. After approximately 20 min, the plume continued to extend in both the direction of groundwater flow and the opposite direction, although the rate of expansion had noticeably decelerated. After 35 min, the contaminant plume largely stabilized, with a final horizontal migration distance of 26.5 cm and a vertical migration distance of 9.0 cm.

After the toluene solution was injected, it migrated 8.7 cm vertically and 18 cm horizontally within the initial 0–3 min, indicating a largely horizontal migration pattern (Fig. 4). As migration progressed, the area of the contaminant plume continued to expand, but its morphology significantly changed. From 5 to 60 min, the vertical migration distance remained nearly constant, whereas the lateral migration distance increased markedly from 19 cm to 37 cm. From 60 to 120 min, the plume dimensions stabilized with no significant further expansion.

Fig. 4Spreading and Distribution of the Toluene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

Spreading and Distribution of the Toluene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

As shown in Fig. 5, after the ethylbenzene solution was injected into the sand tank, its migration was significantly affected by both infiltration pressure and gravity. Within the first minute, the solution migrated approximately 7 cm both horizontally and vertically, after which the solution began to diffuse elliptically in all directions. After 3 min, the horizontal migration distance was 13.5 cm, whereas the vertical distance was 8 cm, indicating a largely horizontal migration pattern. This trend gradually became more obvious. After 20 min, the horizontal migration distance of ethylbenzene reached 21 cm, which was substantially greater than the vertical distance of 8.8 cm. The vertical expansion of the contaminant plume subsequently ceased. After 120 min, the horizontal migration distance was approximately four times the vertical distance, and the plume exhibited a symmetrical, fin-shaped distribution within the aquifer medium.

Fig. 5Spreading and Distribution of the Ethylbenzene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

Spreading and Distribution of the Ethylbenzene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

Fig. 6Spreading and Distribution of the Xylene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

Spreading and Distribution of the Xylene NAPL Phase in the Aquifer.

The migration and distribution characteristics of the xylene NAPL phase in the aquifer were similar to those of ethylbenzene (Fig. 6). Within the first 5 min after it entered the aquifer, the xylene NAPL phase spread elliptically in all directions. After 5 min, the contaminant plume migrated 12 cm horizontally and 8.5 cm vertically, resulting in a relatively smooth and rounded leading edge. After 60 min, the horizontal migration distance was approximately 4.5 times greater than the vertical distance. The migration rate subsequently decreased, and after 120 min, a relatively stable contaminant plume had formed.

The expansion of the NAPL plume during different stages was controlled by different physical mechanisms. During the initial stage (0–20 min), the NAPL migrated under the combined influence of injection pressure, gravity, and capillary forces, rapidly filling available pore spaces. As the injected NAPL entered the saturated zone, gravity drove vertical redistribution, while capillary forces controlled pore-scale invasion. After the injection ceased (after 10 min), the mobile NAPL continued to migrate under gravity and the ambient groundwater flow field, but at a decreasing rate as the NAPL pressure dissipated and residual trapping accumulated. The NAPL phase migrates via multiphase flow, described by Darcy’s law extended to multiple fluid phases, with relative permeabilities and capillary pressures controlling the flow.NAPL The gradual stabilization of the plume after 30–40 min reflects the transition from mobile to residual NAPL, as capillary forces trap the NAPL in pore throats and dead-end pores, leaving the remaining free phase immobile under ambient flow conditions.

Under the condition of hydrodynamic dispersion, an LNAPL plume would typically be expected to assume a fin-shaped configuration aligned with the groundwater flow direction. However, the experimentally observed NAPL plume was not exclusively distributed along the flow direction; a symmetrical plume segment also developed opposite to the flow direction. This bidirectional spreading is a behavior following NAPL release onto or near the water table. During injection, the accumulating NAPL forms a local mound at the release point, creating an additional hydraulic gradient within the NAPL phase that drives flow in all directions, including upgradient. As this mound dissipates after injection ceases, the upgradient driving force diminishes, and subsequent NAPL migration becomes dominated by the ambient groundwater gradient.

Development and validation of a numerical model against the experimental results

The transport of BTEX compounds in aquifers entails a multiphase process involving the transformation of components between the liquid, NAPL, and vapor phases, representing a complex, coupled multiphysics problem. The numerical model was implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics (version 6.1) as described in Sect. 2.4. The model was calibrated against measured NAPL plume areas (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6) by adjusting only the residual saturation for each compound. The excellent agreement (R² > 0.95, Fig. 7) confirms that the model captures the essential migration processes.

The calibrated parameters for the four BTEX compounds are presented in Table 2. The differences in residual saturation among the four compounds (Table 2) can be attributed to their distinct interfacial tensions with water. Benzene has the highest interfacial tension and the highest residual saturation (0.17), while xylene has the lowest interfacial tension and the lowest residual saturation (0.12). This trend is consistent with capillary theory, where higher interfacial tension promotes greater residual trapping. Additionally, the experiments represent a process of NAPL displacing water, which influences the residual saturation values.

The transport and diffusion of BTEX in porous aquifer media comprise a complex multiphase partitioning process, whose interphase interactions are difficult to characterize through direct observation. Numerical simulations can effectively capture this multiphase partitioning behavior. After calibration, the distributions of benzene, toluene, xylene, and ethylbenzene NAPL phases among water and gas were quantified via the use of the experimental model (Figs. 8 and 9).

The injection point serves as the initial entry location of BTEX into the aquifer, reflecting the partitioning characteristics among the different phases after it was added. Monitoring point A provides comprehensive BTEX data, reflecting the complete transport process at this location. Accordingly, injection point and monitoring point A were selected to analyze the three-phase partitioning behavior.

As shown in Fig. 8, the volume fractions of the gaseous, aqueous, and NAPL phases of BTEX at the injection point significantly vary over time. With respect to benzene, the NAPL fraction increased rapidly within the first 5 min, as the benzene NAPL phase displaced both water and air in the porous media. Between 5 and 40 min, this displacement continued, as indicated by a further increase in the NAPL fraction and a decrease in the air and water fractions, albeit at a noticeably reduced rate. After 60 min, the NAPL fraction at the injection point stabilized substantially. The air fraction slowly increased, whereas the water fraction gradually decreased, indicating that the maximum adsorption capacity of the porous media for free-phase benzene had been reached. This adsorption altered the porosity of the media, consequently reducing the water volume fraction. After 140 min, the partitioning ratio among the gas, aqueous, and NAPL phases was 47:8:45.

After 5 min, the NAPL fraction remained largely stable, with only a slight increase, whereas the water fraction exhibited a similar trend. After 140 min, the gas: aqueous: NAPL partitioning ratios for both toluene and ethylbenzene were 54:17:29, and that for xylene was 55:18:27.

Compared with those of toluene, xylene, and ethylbenzene, the aqueous phase fraction of benzene exhibited distinct trends. When benzene was used the contaminant, the aqueous phase fraction mirrored the trend in the gas phase, with both revealing an inverse relationship with the NAPL phase fraction. These results indicate that after injection, all four contaminants (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene) formed an NAPL phase that gradually occupied the pore space and displaced both water and gas. Benzene, which is more soluble, migrates with the aqueous phase, whereas toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene generally remain in situ and cause NAPL contamination.

To evaluate the reliability of the results and to analyze the potential effects of volatilization, adsorption, and other losses, a mass balance analysis was performed for each BTEX compound (Text S3).

Conclusion

On the basis of hydrodynamic dispersion theory for porous media and the essential characteristics of BTEX transport and transformation, in this study, a laboratory sand-tank experiment was designed to simulate the migration and transformation processes of BTEX compounds. The main findings are as follows:

BTEX compounds exhibit compound-specific apparent dispersivities that deviate significantly from the intrinsic dispersivity of the porous medium. The longitudinal dispersivity decreased in the order of benzene (0.114 m) > ethylbenzene (0.053 m) > toluene (0.050 m) > xylene (0.046 m), showed an inverse relationship with adsorption coefficients, demonstrating that organic contaminant transport is governed by both physical and chemical processes. This implies that using conservative tracer-derived dispersivity values in risk assessments may lead to substantial errors, highlighting the need for compound-specific parameters in predicting organic contaminant fate and transport.The sand-tank experiment was simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics, and the NAPL-phase migration parameters of the four BTEX compounds were fitted and calibrated against the experimental data. The resulting residual saturations of the NAPL phase were 0.17 for benzene, 0.15 for toluene, 0.12 for ethylbenzene, and 0.14 for xylene. These are important parameters for simulating the migration of contaminants in porous media.The partitioning characteristics of water, gas, and organic phases in porous media after the injection of the four BTEX compounds were investigated. After injection, BTEX compounds displaced water and gas in the porous media, establishing new three-phase equilibria. At the injection point, the gas:aqueous:NAPL partitioning ratios were 47:8:45 for benzene, 54:17:29 for toluene and ethylbenzene, and 55:18:27 for xylene. At observation point A, the ratios were 65:10:25 for benzene, 64:19:17 for toluene and ethylbenzene, and 60:24:16 for xylene. These differences reflect the compounds’ distinct solubility and volatility, which control their interphase partitioning and transport behavior.

The methodology—combining visual tracking of NAPL plumes, dissolved-phase monitoring, and numerical model calibration—provides a framework that can be extended to other contaminants and more complex mixtures in future studies. While this study focused on individual BTEX compounds, the parameters obtained provide essential baseline data for future investigations of BTEX mixtures and actual shale oil. Future studies should extend this work to multi-component systems to address this limitation.
