Non‐Destructive Analysis of Phenolic and Flavonoid Contents in Medicinal Plant Powders Using Hyperspectral Imaging and Variable Selection
Authors: Rahul Joshi, Sushma Kholiya, Himanshu Pandey, Mahipal Singh, Ameeta Tiwari, Jinsu Lim, Ramaraj Sathasivam, Mohammad Akbar Faqeerzada, Sang Un Park, Byoung‐Kwan Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhenolic and flavonoid contents in medicinal plants are essential to their growth and development and provide numerous health benefits, yet their quantification using traditional wet chemistry is labor‐intensive and time‐consuming. This study utilized the combination of two benchtop hyperspectral imaging (HSI) systems, namely short‐wave infrared (SWIR) and visible near‐infrared, for the non‐destructive quantitative estimation of the phenolic and flavonoid contents present in 12 powder samples of medicinal plants. For quantitative analysis, partial least squares regression (PLSR) prediction models used several spectral preprocessing techniques. To further enhance performance, variable importance in projection (VIP) selected the most informative spectral variables and combined them with PLSR to improve predictions. The optimized VIP‐PLSR models further improved the predictive performance, yielding coefficients of determination (R 2) of 0.985 and 0.996 and even lower standard root mean square error of prediction values of 0.165 and 1.150 mg/g for the respective compounds. Additionally, chemical imaging provided a clear spatial visualization of both compounds within the samples. These results underscore the potential of SWIR‐HSI combined with chemometrics as an effective tool for screening and quality control in medicinal plant powders.

Introduction

Medicinal plants have been ingrained in human civilisation for thousands of years and used in daily life, either in their raw or extracted forms. Since ancient times, people in developing nations have utilised medicinal plants. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), traditional healthcare systems are used by 70%–80% of the population in developing nations, including Africa and India [1]. Medicinal plants are a rich source of essential phytochemical compounds, which play an essential role during their growth and development process. The two main groups of phytochemicals are (1) primary metabolites, which include proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates, and (2) secondary metabolites, which include phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Plants thrive and develop because of their primary metabolites, but secondary metabolites are crucial for protecting plants against risks from outside influences, such as insects and environmental toxins [2]. Phenolic compounds and flavonoids are regarded as an important family of biomolecules with substantial therapeutic properties for humans. A phenolic (C6H5OH) ring, carboxylic acid (‐COOH), and hydroxyl groups (‐OH) make up the fundamental structural elements of phenolic compounds, which include gallic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, protocatechuic acid, and coumaric acid. Additionally, polyphenols with at least two phenolic rings are known as flavonoids. They are further divided into subclasses, which include isoflavones, flavonols, flavonones, flavones, and flavanolols [3, 4, 5, 6]. Both phenolic compounds and flavonoids have significant pharmacological activities, including antitumor, antimicrobial, anti‐adhesive, and anti‐inflammatory properties [7, 8, 9, 10]. Phenolic and flavonoid contents are often extracted using the Folin–Ciocalteu technique [11] and the aluminium chloride colourimetric test [12]. These techniques frequently need many pretreatment steps and are time‐consuming and destructive. On the other hand, standard chromatography‐based techniques, such as liquid chromatography−mass spectrometry [13] and high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [14] based procedures are also frequently employed for the accurate and efficient analysis of these essential compounds in plants. However, these techniques also have a number of drawbacks, including the fact that they are expensive and hazardous by nature, and there is a need for specialised operators while taking measurements.

Spectroscopic techniques, such as Fourier‐transform infrared (FT−IR) [15], FT‐near IR (FT−NIR) [16, 17], and Raman [18], are well‐established analytical methods for the assessment of plant phytochemical compounds with higher performance. These spectroscopic methods offer a wealth of chemical information about the scanned material. However, they are point‐based scanning methods and only look at a tiny portion of a specimen; they are unable to give spatial information that is crucial for plant product quality and quantity applications. Furthermore, when working with a larger number of samples, point‐scan measurements are time‐consuming and entirely ineffective. One crucial way to address the aforementioned shortcomings of these spectroscopic approaches is through hyperspectral imaging (HSI). HSI has recently garnered prominence as an innovative and promising method that can quickly sense spectral and spatial information for the quantitative examination of agricultural products [19, 20]. A hyperspectral image is a three‐dimensional (3D) cube that is typically referred to as a hypercube that contains spectral data for each individual pixel. In comparison to single‐machine vision technology or spectroscopy analysis technology, HSI can offer more thorough or comprehensive information, including the internal structure characteristics, morphological information, and chemical composition of the sample. Additionally, because multiple samples may be evaluated simultaneously, the HSI technique is quicker than point‐based spectroscopic techniques. The visible–NIR (Vis‐NIR) and short‐wave infrared (SWIR) spectral bands, which together make up the HSI system, are the most often used. Several studies have been performed using the HSI technique for the examination of food and agricultural products, such as the determination of the moisture content in bell peppers [21], fake eggs [22], meat quality [23], soil quality measurement [24] and pungent constituents in dried ginger [25]. Although HSI holds significant promise for applications in food and agricultural sciences, building robust calibration models from HSI data presents considerable challenges. The main difficulty lies in interpreting and analyzing massive datasets composed of complex spectral information and numerous variables. Addressing these challenges requires a strategic selection of spectral variables, effective preprocessing, and advanced chemometric analysis.

This study focuses on the non‐destructive quantitative assessment of phenolic and flavonoid compounds in powdered medicinal plant samples collected from different regions. To accomplish this, HSI data from both the Vis/NIR and SWIR ranges will be combined. The concentrations of these compounds will be predicted using partial least squares regression (PLSR), with variable importance in projection (VIP) applied to identify the most informative spectral features. In addition, chemical imaging will be used to map the spatial distribution of phenolic and flavonoids within the powdered samples.

Materials and Methods

Samples for Experiment

Fresh plant materials were sourced from various regions across Uttarakhand, India, encompassing eleven distinct plant species, as illustrated in the schematic image shown in Figure 1. In addition, detailed information about these plant species, including their names, is provided in Table 1. Following collection, the plant samples were cleaned and shade‐dried. Prior to crushing, all species samples were additionally oven‐dried at 70°C for 1 day to minimize moisture effects. The dried plant material for each species was then ground into powder. Though we didn't examine the particle size quantitatively, all the plant species samples were prepared using the same crushing procedure to ensure a relatively consistent particle size distribution. The samples underwent further drying for several hours before experimental measurements commenced. Selection of the plant parts for each species was guided by their availability and both traditional and contemporary uses referenced in existing literature. Rather than individual plants of different varieties, different species of plants were chosen in order to capture diversity among both phenolic and flavonoid chemical compounds. In addition, this diversity would allow us to provide a more comprehensive understanding of chemical variability.

Instruments

In our study, to examine the medicinal plant powder samples, two different laboratory‐based HSI instruments, namely, Vis/NIR and SWIR, were used. The key components of the Vis/NIR HSI system included an objective lens (focal length of 28 mm f/1.4), an imaging spectrograph (Headwall Photonics, Fitchburg, MA, USA), and an EMCCD detector (Mega Luca R; ANDOR Technology, USA). The Vis/NIR camera generated images with 128 spectral bands, each with a wavelength between 400 and 1000 nm. The detector size was 1004 × 1002 pixels, and the frame rate was 12.4 Hz. The SWIR system consisted of an objective lens with a 25 mm f/1.4 focal length and a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector (Model: Xeva‐2.5320; Xenics, Belgium). With a total pixel count of 320256 and a frame rate of 100 Hz, the SWIR camera recorded images with 275 bands ranging from 1000 to 2500 nm. Both systems consisted of six 100 W tungsten lamps for light sources, fiber optics for sample illumination, a translation stage for sample movement, and a DC motor for speed control of the translation stage. Using Microsoft (MS) Visual Basic (version 6.0) available in Microsoft Windows, the control application for the HSI systems was developed. Figure 2 depicts a schematic diagram for our laboratory‐based HSI system for HSI data collection.

Schematic illustration of the laboratory‐based pushbroom hyperspectral imaging system, featuring a motorized linear translation stage for line scanning utilized during data collection.

Medicinal plant powders with varying concentrations of phenolic and flavonoids rested on a black square plate covered with black tape to minimise light reflection. The powders filled a 3 × 3 cube, 50 mm in depth, and a thin steel rod levelled the surface without applying compression to maintain uniformity. Each sample was then moved on a conveyor unit for line‐by‐line scanning with both HSI systems. Visual Basic software controlled key acquisition parameters, including conveyor speed, field of view, motor speed, and exposure time. The scanning process generated hyperspectral images in the form of a hypercube, containing two spatial dimensions (x, y) and one spectral dimension (λ), which enabled the extraction of chemical and physical attributes of the samples.

HSI Correction and Spectral Data Preprocessing

The dark current produced by the camera resulted in noise being present in the captured HSI images. Both dark and white reference images were gathered in order to rectify the HSI images. The images were first calibrated by covering the lens using an opaque metal cap and shutting off the light source to produce a black image (0% reflectance) and using a white ceramic Teflon sheet to obtain a white image (100% reflectance). The following formula was then used to compute the relative reflectance: (1) I=Io−DW−D where the original image is represented by I0, the dark image by D, the white image by W, and the corrected image by I. In addition, the HSI images underwent a region‐of‐interest (ROI) step to extract the spectral data for additional analysis. Prior to the additional analysis, the ROI pixel selection was manually calculated, and the pixel spectra extracted from the medicinal plant powder samples at each phenolic and flavonoid concentration were averaged.

Following the ROI extraction process, the reference HPLC data and spectral data were used to perform the model construction processes. The classification and regression processes were carried out using the best model, and the resulting chemical images were produced for chemical visualisation. The extracted spectral data from both HSI systems were affected by undesired noise effects such as scattering, baseline shift, and overlapping peaks. Prior to model development, the extracted spectra were subjected to several preprocessing techniques, including normalisation (mean, maximum and range normalisation), standard normal variate (SNV), multiplicative scatter correction (MSC), and Savitzky–Golay filtering with first and second derivatives. These methods were applied to minimise variations resulting from the heterogeneous nature of powder samples. Specifically, MSC corrects scattering effects caused by particle size and surface irregularities, normalisation compensates for intensity fluctuations, and derivative‐based filtering enhances spectral resolution by resolving overlapping peaks. These steps have a significant influence on the classification and regression model analysis. The article by Rinnan et al. contains a full description of the preprocessing steps [26]. The preprocessed spectral data of powder samples were later utilised for generating quantitative prediction models using PLSR using MATLAB version 7.0.4 (MathWorks, Inc., MA, USA) software.

Regression Analysis Model Development

In this study, the quantitative examination of phenolic and flavonoid compounds was performed using a robust machine learning algorithm known as PLSR. It is one of the widely used techniques to perform prediction analysis. It works on the principle of deriving the relationships between the dependent variable Y (also known as the reference data) and independent variables X (also known as the spectral data). PLSR is a technique that uses a linear model to describe the relationship between the two data matrices of X and Y. For more details, please refer to the article by Wold et al. [27]. This study employed PLSR because of the excellent performance of this technique for the quantitative prediction of phytochemicals, such as phenolic compounds, in our previously published reports [15, 16].

A total of 480 spectral samples were collected from 40 ROIs across 12 powdered medicinal plants. The dataset was divided into two subsets: 300 spectra for calibration and 180 spectra for independent prediction. This 70:30 split provided sufficient data for model development while allowing an unbiased evaluation of model performance using the prediction set. Cross‐validation was implemented within the calibration dataset to determine the optimal latent variable (LV) structure, whereas the prediction dataset was exclusively utilised for the final external evaluation of the model, thereby enhancing the reliability and rigour of the model. The same calibration and validation subsets were used for both the Vis/NIR and SWIR hyperspectral datasets to ensure consistency when developing the PLSR model.

Variable Importance in Projection

HSI data typically contain a very large number of spectral variables, which can increase computational load and reduce model efficiency due to long processing times. To address this limitation and enhance predictive performance, a variable selection approach was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the data while retaining the most informative features. In this study, the VIP method, first introduced by Wold and colleagues in 1993 [28], was employed as the waveband selection strategy. VIP is a widely used technique in chemometrics that ranks variables according to their contribution to the predictive performance of a PLSR model. The mathematical foundations of VIP are thoroughly described in the work of Farrés et al. [29], and therefore are not repeated in detail here. A critical step in applying this method involves determining an appropriate VIP threshold value, which defines the cut‐off for selecting relevant variables. Based on preliminary trials, a threshold of 0.85 was adopted in this study. All variables with VIP scores below this value were excluded from further analysis. The variables retained above this threshold were subsequently used for developing PLSR models aimed at the quantitative prediction of phenolic and flavonoid contents in powdered medicinal plant samples.

Model Performance Parameters

The estimation of a model relies on the identification of appropriate statistical parameters that enhance the predictive capacity of the model. Several performance assessment parameters, such as correlation coefficients (Rc2, and Rp2), bias and standard root mean square errors of calibration and prediction (RMSEC and RMSEP) were calculated in this study to assess the effectiveness of the PLSR prediction model. Analysis of Relative Total Flavonoid Content

The total flavonoid content (TFC) was analysed according to the protocol described by Kim et al. [30]. The TFC was calculated in terms of milligrams of quercetin equivalent per gram of dry weight (QE mg/g DW). All the analyses were performed in triplicate.

Extraction and HPLC Analysis of Individual Phenolic Compounds

The extraction and analysis of phenolic compounds were performed by using the protocol described by Lee et al. [31]. A total of 3 mL of 80% methanol (v/v) was added to 100 mg of each sample. Then the mixture was vigorously vortexed and sonicated for 1 h at room temperature. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, the supernatant was taken and filter‐sterilized using a 0.45 µm PTFE syringe filter. The instrument was coupled with an Agilent 1260 Infinity II series and an Optimapak C18 column (250 mm x 4.6 mm, 5 µm, RStech, Daejeon, Korea). Mobile phases were composed of 0.2% acetic acid (v/v) and pure methanol and eluted at 1.0 mL/min. The column temperature, wavelength, and injection volume were set to 30°C, 280 nm, and 10 µL, respectively. A detailed gradient program was performed according to the protocol described by Lee et al. [31]. Retention time comparison and spike tests were conducted to identify and quantify individual phenolic compounds. All the analyses were performed in triplicate, and the results were represented as mg/g DW. Further, due to the limited availability of HPLC reference standards, only a specific group of phenolic compounds could be quantified. Therefore, the phenolic values used for subsequent prediction model development were calculated as the sum of the identified phenolic compounds quantified by HPLC and do not represent total phenolic content (TPC).

Spectral Interpretation for SWIR and Vis/NIR HSI

Figure 7a,b presents the average SWIR and Vis/NIR HSI spectra of medicinal plant powder samples. The spectra were acquired in the wavelength ranges of 950–2000 nm (SWIR) and 400–1000 nm (Vis/NIR), corresponding to variations in phenolic and flavonoid contents as determined by reference chemical analysis. Due to limited useful spectral information outside these regions, only the relevant wavelength ranges were selected for further analysis. The spectral regions sensitive for the determination of phenolic, compounds in the SWIR spectra were observed from 1000–1200, 1410–1520, and 1950–2000 nm and are associated with C‐H second overtone, C‐H combination (CH2 and aromatic), C‐H stretching from first overtone, C = O stretching from second overtone, O–H bending /C‐O stretching combination, C–H bending from second overtone, and C‐H stretching/C–C [32, 33]. However, the flavonoid compounds found in the powder samples exhibit the spectral fingerprints that were detected at around 1120, 1320, 1215 and 1490 nm [34, 35] due to the second overtone of C−H stretching vibrations, and first overtone of O‐H stretching. The sensitive spectral areas for the identification of both chemical components are highlighted in dashed rectangular boxes in Figure 7a.

On the other hand, in the case of Vis/NIR HSI data, the dashed rectangular areas with different colors shown in Figure 7b from 440 to 480, 520 to 570 and 760 to 970 nm [36, 37, 38] represent the important spectral waveband regions sensitive to both the chemical compounds present in the medicinal plants powders. They might experience chemical composition changes during the drying process, which could lead to the generation of complex reflectance spectra in the SWIR and Vis/NIR regions. Because of this, performing the quantitative prediction of both phenolic and flavonoids just by visual observation of both spectra proved to be ineffective. Hence, in order to extract critical information needed for a suitable estimation in a more accurate and effective way, the application of proper chemometric analytic techniques is essential.

PLSR Model Results for Prediction of Phenolic and Flavonoid Contents Using SWIR, and Vis/NIR HSI Techniques

PLSR models were first developed using SWIR HSI data in the wavelength range of 950–2000 nm. To enhance the model's quantitative prediction performance, various preprocessing techniques were tested. For phenolic contents, the MSC method provided the best results. The enhanced model performance can be attributed to the effectiveness of MSC in correcting multiplicative scattering effects caused by variations in particle size and surface irregularities inherent to medicinal powder samples. By minimizing noise and light scattering distortions, this preprocessing step significantly improved model accuracy. The model achieved a strong correlation between measured and predicted values, with R 2 = 0.989 for the calibration dataset and R2 = 0.982 for the prediction dataset. The corresponding RMSEP values were very low, 0.133 mg/g and 0.171 mg/g, respectively, with a bias of 0.018. The model utilized nine LVs, which represent new, uncorrelated variables that summarise the most relevant spectral information. The prediction results for phenolic contents are illustrated in Figure 8a. It is important to note that the phenolic values used to develop the PLSR model were derived from HPLC quantification of individual phenolic compounds (Table 3) and therefore do not represent TPC. While for flavonoid contents, the best‐performing model was obtained using maximum normalization preprocessing. This improvement results from preprocessing techniques that normalize spectral data and reduce intensity variations and baseline shifts, thereby improving spectral consistency and facilitating accurate identification of chemical features. This approach further enhanced model performance, achieving R 2 = 0.992 for calibration and R 2 = 0.994 for prediction datasets. The RMSE values were 1.711 and 1.567 mg/g, with a bias of 0.077, also using nine LVs, as shown in Figure 8b. The comprehensive results of the quantitative prediction analysis for the SWIR‐HSI models are summarized in Table 4. Overall, the SWIR‐based PLSR models exhibited exceptional predictive capability for the non‐destructive estimation of phytochemical constituents in powdered medicinal plant samples

To determine the optimal HSI approach, the PLSR model prediction performance of Vis/NIR HSI data (400–1000 nm) was compared with the SWIR HSI results described above. For phenolic contents, the developed PLSR models achieved R 2 = 0.945 (calibration) and R 2 = 0.967 (prediction), with RMSE = 0.298 and 0.236 mg/g, respectively, using range normalization preprocessing, and nine LVs with a bias of −0.393. The prediction analysis results for both chemicals are shown in Figure 9a,b, and summarized in Table 4. Comparing both spectral ranges shows that Vis/NIR HSI data produced higher prediction errors and lower correlation coefficients than the SWIR models. These results indicate that the Vis/NIR range is less effective for quantitative prediction of flavonoid and phenolic compounds due to its limited sensitivity to specific chemical absorption features. Overall, the study clearly shows that SWIR HSI data outperform Vis/NIR HSI data for the quantitative analysis of both phenolic and flavonoid contents in powdered medicinal plants. The SWIR models consistently provided higher correlation coefficients, lower prediction errors, and better robustness. This confirms that the SWIR spectral range contains more specific and informative absorption features than the Vis/NIR range, making it the preferred choice for precise and non‐destructive phytochemical quantification in powdered medicinal plants.

VIP as Variable Selection

To enhance the performance of the PLSR models and identify the most informative wavelengths for predicting phenolic and flavonoid contents, VIP was employed as a variable selection method. This approach reduces spectral dimensionality by eliminating less relevant wavelengths while retaining those most influential for prediction. The VIP procedure was applied to the SWIR HSI dataset. Multiple VIP threshold values were systematically evaluated, and a cut‐off of 0.85 was selected as optimal based on the minimization of the root mean square error of cross‐validation (RMSECV). Although a threshold of VIP > 1 is commonly used, a lower threshold was adopted in this study to prevent the loss of subtle yet informative spectral wavelengths that contributed to improved predictive performance. This data‐driven selection also ensures a robust balance between model simplicity and prediction performance. Using this criterion, the number of wavelengths was reduced from 201 to 135 for phenolic and 143 for flavonoids. Separate PLSR models (VIP‐PLSR) were then developed using the selected wavelengths. The resulting models showed excellent performance, comparable to or better than the full‐spectrum PLSR models.

For phenolic content, the VIP‐PLSR model, using raw preprocessing, achieved high correlation coefficients (R 2) of 0.981 and 0.985 for the calibration and prediction sets, respectively. The corresponding RMSE values were low (0.141 mg/g and 0.165 mg/g), with a bias of 0.055. The model utilized 10 LVs, as shown in Figure 11b. It should be noted that the VIP‐PLSR model was constructed using phenolic values obtained from HPLC quantification of individual phenolic compounds (Table 3). Therefore, the predicted phenolic values clearly represent the sum of the identified phenolic compounds and should not be interpreted as TPC.

On the other hand, for flavonoid content, the model performed best with maximum normalization preprocessing. This resulted in an even stronger performance, with R 2 values of 0.995 and 0.996. The RMSE values were also very low at 1.298 mg/g and 1.150 mg/g, with a bias of 0.023, and using 10 LVs as shown in Figure 11e. The strong performance of the VIP‐PLSR models with a reduced set of variables confirms that the VIP method is highly effective for selecting the most informative wavelengths. Additionally, the beta coefficient plots generated by the VIP‐PLSR models (Figure 11c,f) exhibited spectral patterns consistent with those identified in earlier analyses, indicating that the selected wavelengths are highly sensitive to variations in l phenolic and flavonoid contents. This further confirms that the VIP method successfully isolates the most relevant spectral information for predicting these phytochemicals. The VIP‐PLSR models demonstrated improved prediction accuracy, with higher R2 and lower RMSEP values. Visualisation of Phenolic and Flavonoid Contents in Medicinal Plants Powders

In the past, HSI has demonstrated a great deal of promise for determining the chemical distribution in measured products. This assertion is supported by published studies in which researchers employed HSI to carry out various tasks, including the determination of moisture content in meat [39], food product adulteration [40] and active pharmaceutical ingredient distribution in pharmaceutical samples [41].

In this study, chemical images were generated using PLSR to perform chemical visualization of phenolic and flavonoid contents in the medicinal plant powder samples. To generate the chemical images, the beta coefficient values obtained from the best preprocessing spectra using the PLSR model with SWIR HSI were multiplied by the spectrum of each pixel of the original hyperspectral image of each powder sample. The color bar scale shown in Figure 12a represents the concentration distribution of phenolic contents ranging from 0.019 to 3.94 mg/g. The resulting image's colour shifts from blue to red with an increase in phenolic concentration. In a similar way, a chemical image was also generated for the chemical distribution of flavonoid contents, which ranged from 0.06 to 61.58 mg/g, as shown in the color bar scale in Figure 12b. Additionally, the generated image illustrates that a higher concentration of flavonoids causes the color to shift from blue to intense red. These results clearly demonstrate the capability of SWIR‐HSI for non‐destructive prediction and spatial visualization of phytochemical contents in medicinal plant powders.

Chemical visualization of (a) phenolic, and (b) flavonoid contents in 12 medicinal plant powder samples representing 11 different species.

Earlier research, such as the work by Hssaini et al., which utilized mid‐infrared spectroscopy combined with PLSR to predict phenolic acids and flavonoids in figures, reported lower R 2 values of 0.86 and 0.84 [42], respectively. Similarly, Kusumiyati and Asikin applied near‐infrared spectroscopy for these compounds’ quantification in horticultural produce, achieving validation R 2 values of just 0.78 and 0.72 [43]. Compared to these studies, the present investigation demonstrates higher predictive accuracy and lower RMSE values.

In previous research, our group achieved remarkably high R 2 (0.999) for phenolic compound prediction in powdered Arabidopsis plants under different stress scenarios using a combination of IR and NIR spectroscopy [16], and in moringa powder using FT‐IR spectroscopy [15]. However, these approaches and the other studies above relied on point scan measurements, which restrict throughput and are less suited for rapid, large‐scale screening in industrial contexts. Moreover, previous works often incorporated numerous, varied spectral bands in model development, complicating implementation and increasing computational demands.

The current study overcomes these limitations by introducing waveband selection, enhancing model accuracy while reducing spectral redundancy, and by generating PLS‐based chemical images to visualise compound distribution. Although our previous work also employed SWIR and Vis/NIR HSI for phenolic content estimation in Arabidopsis under stress conditions [44], the current study significantly expands the application scope. Specifically, it focuses on commercially relevant medicinal plant powders sourced from eleven different medicinal plant species in India and evaluates both phenolic and flavonoid contents. The diversity of the samples introduces real‐world chemical variability, enhancing the robustness and applicability of the models.

Overall, this approach demonstrates strong potential for rapid, reliable, and non‐destructive analysis of phytochemical composition in medicinal plant powders, with direct applications in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical industries. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset and integrating advanced deep learning techniques, such as 1D and 3D convolutional neural networks, to further improve predictive performance.

Conclusions

With the increasing worldwide demand for medicinal plants and their valuable phytochemicals, there is an ongoing necessity for efficient, non‐invasive analytical methods. This investigation concentrated on accurately quantifying the phenolic and flavonoid contents of medicinal plant powder samples by integrating chemometrics and HSI. The analytical results demonstrated that SWIR–PLSR models consistently provided better predictive outcomes than Vis/NIR‐PLSR, as indicated by higher R 2 values (0.982 and 0.994), reduced RMSEP (0.171 and 1.567 mg/g), and lower bias (0.018 and 0.077). Further improvement was achieved by integrating SWIR‐based PLSR with VIP for variable selection, resulting in even greater R 2 (0.985 and 0.996), along with further decreased RMSEP (0.165 and 1.150 mg/g) and minimal bias (0.055 and 0.023) compared to full‐spectrum SWIR approaches. In addition, chemical imaging enabled clear visualisation of the spatial distribution of phenolic and flavonoid contents within the powder samples. Overall, this study demonstrates that HSI combined with chemometric modelling and targeted wavelength selection is an effective, rapid, and non‐destructive approach for the reliable quantification of phenolic and flavonoid contents in medicinal plant powders.
