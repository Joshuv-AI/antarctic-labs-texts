An FGF21-based fusion protein ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction and promotes recovery post spinal cord injury
Authors: Xuehai Chen, Yibo Ying, Yuchao Zhang, Jiali Lv, Minghao Jiang, Yujun Mo, Zhiyi Wu, Huixin Lin, Zhiyang Huang, Dawei Jiang, Yanming Zuo, Xiaokun Li, Zhouguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dysfunction and defective repair are central drivers of secondary injury following spinal cord injury (SCI), yet effective strategies to restore mitochondrial quality control remain limited. Here, we develop an engineered long-acting fibroblast growth factor 21 fusion protein (SFGF21) by incorporating a linker peptide to enhance molecular targeting and oxidative stability. In a mouse model of SCI, sustained delivery of SFGF21 specifically potentiated dynamic crosstalk between mitochondria and lysosomes, thereby activating the BNIP3/BNIP3L pathway-mediated mitophagic flux and clearing dysfunctional mitochondria in injured neurons. This process restored mitochondrial homeostasis, suppressed lipid peroxidation, and alleviated oxidative stress in injured neurons. Loss-of-function experiments confirmed that both FGFR1 and BNIP3 are required for SFGF21-induced mitophagy and neuroprotection. Together, our findings establish engineered modulation of organelle crosstalk as a therapeutic strategy to activate mitophagy and support neural repair after SCI, highlighting mitochondria-targeted protein engineering as a potential therapeutic path for spinal cord repair.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, SFGF21, Ferroptosis, Mitophagy, Mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is one of the most devastating neurological conditions, often leading to permanent motor, sensory, and autonomic impairments. It imposes a substantial and lasting burden on patients, their families, and healthcare systems worldwide [1]. The growing public health challenge is underscored by its rising incidence manifested by ca. 234,000 new cases are reported annually in China alone [2]. Current standard-of-care interventions, including surgical decompression, corticosteroid administration, and intensive rehabilitation, primarily aim to stabilize the lesion site and mitigate secondary damage. While these approaches may prevent further neurological deterioration, their capacity to promote meaningful axonal regeneration or restore damaged neural circuits remains severely limited [3]. Consequently, achieving functional recovery after SCI persists as a major unmet medical need, underscoring the urgent demand for novel therapeutic strategies targeting both neuroprotection and neural repair.

The limited success of existing therapies is largely attributed to the complex cascade of secondary injury, which unfolds over hours to weeks following the initial mechanical insult and serves as a critical determinant of functional outcomes [4]. Historically, research has focused on neuronal apoptosis, necrosis, and neuroinflammation as core drivers of cell death [5,6]. However, emerging evidence has established that ferroptosis—a form of iron-dependent, non-apoptotic programmed cell death driven by catastrophic lipid peroxidation—plays a key role in neuronal loss post-SCI [[7], [8], [9]]. Importantly, ferroptosis in this context does not occur in isolation but is orchestrated by maladaptive inter-organellar crosstalk, forming a self-amplifying pathological axis. Damaged mitochondria, a hallmark of secondary injury, act as prolific sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and serve as the initial “engine” for lipid peroxidation [10,11]. Concurrently, SCI disrupts lysosomal integrity, triggering aberrant ferritin degradation and excessive release of ferrous iron (Fe2+); via the Fenton reaction, Fe2+ acts as the critical “fuel” for the ferroptotic cascade [12]. Lysosomal dysfunction also impairs mitophagy—the selective autophagic clearance of defective mitochondria [13]. This failure establishes a vicious cycle: sustained ROS accumulation from persistently damaged mitochondria further oxidizes cellular components, while Fe2+ leaked from dysfunctional lysosomes cataly the amplification of ROS production. This “lysosome-mitochondria-ferroptosis” axis forms a feed-forward loop that relentlessly drives neurons toward iron-dependent demise. Thus, therapeutic strategies targeting only a single downstream component are inherently limited, as they fail to restore the fundamental inter-organellar communication and homeostasis required for long-term cellular survival. Effective interventions must simultaneously target multiple nodes within this pathological axis to reset the dysregulated organelle network.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a pleiotropic hormone with well-characterized roles in systemic metabolism and cellular stress resistance, represents a promising multi-activity candidate [14,15]. Beyond its canonical signaling via cell-surface FGFR/β-Klotho complexes, FGF21 exhibits intracellular properties relevant to the identified pathology [16,17]. Notably, FGF21 has been characterized as a ferroptosis inhibitor, capable of upregulating Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity and suppressing lethal lipid peroxidation [18]. Additionally, independent studies demonstrate that FGF21 can restore lysosomal function and reactivate autophagic flux by promoting nuclear translocation of the master regulator Transcription Factor EB (TFEB) [19]. These attributes position FGF21 as a unique agent with the potential to modulate both the lysosomal and ferroptotic arms of the pathological axis. However, therapeutic limitation exists in that endogenous FGF21 lacks an intrinsic mechanism for specific subcellular localization. Crucially, it lacks the ability to selectively target mitochondria—the primary source of ROS and a critical trigger for ferroptosis. This inability to spatially engage the mitochondrial component of the axis significantly constrains its capacity to directly disrupt the iron-ROS cycle and orchestrate a coordinated organellar response.

To overcome this fundamental targeting limitation and enable precision intervention, we pursued a rational protein engineering strategy. We hypothesized that endowing FGF21 with mitochondrial-homing capability would enable its protective functions to be deployed precisely at the nucleation site of the pathological cascade. To this end, we conjugated FGF21 with the mitochondria-targeting peptide SS-31. SS-31 (D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH2) is a cell-permeable tetrapeptide that selectively accumulates in the inner mitochondrial membrane, driven by both the negative mitochondrial membrane potential and its high affinity for cardiolipin [20,21]. It not only acts as a robust delivery vehicle but also possesses intrinsic properties to stabilize mitochondrial electron transport and suppress oxidative stress [22]. Using bioorthogonal copper-free click chemistry, we synthesized a novel fusion protein termed SFGF21. This engineered construct is designed to exert bifunctional therapeutic effects: the SS-31 moiety serves as a “molecular guidance system” to deliver the cargo directly to neuronal mitochondria, while the FGF21 module locally mediates lysosome-restorative and anti-ferroptotic actions. We propose that this spatial colocalization is not merely additive but synergistic, enabling SFGF21 to strengthen mitochondria-lysosome contact sites, catalyze the clearance of damaged mitochondria via enhanced BNIP3/BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy, and thereby dismantle the iron-ROS amplification loop. In essence, SFGF21 is conceptualized as a molecular tool to reprogram the dysfunctional “lysosome-mitochondria-ferroptosis” axis from within.

In this study, we report on the design, synthesis, and comprehensive evaluation of SFGF21 in a murine model of contusive SCI. We demonstrate that SFGF21 successfully localizes mitochondria in injured spinal neurons. Mechanistically, it reinstates lysosomal acidification and proteolytic activity, promotes robust mitophagic flux, and reprograms mitochondrial metabolism to alleviate peroxidative stress. This coordinated multi-organellar action translates to potent suppression of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, significantly preserving neuronal viability and reducing lesion volume. Consequently, SFGF21-treated mice exhibit markedly superior and sustained locomotor function recovery compared to controls. Our work establishes a protein engineering paradigm for directly manipulating inter-organellar crosstalk for therapeutic gain. By demonstrating that a single engineered molecule can coherently reset a complex pathological axis, we bridge a critical gap between subcellular biology and translational neurology, offering a new blueprint for treating SCI and other disorders driven by organelle dysfunction.

Results

FGF21 prevents the progression of neuronal ferroptosis following SCI

To investigate the molecular events underlying secondary injury following SCI, we performed transcriptomic sequencing and subsequent gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The results revealed significant differences in mRNA of ferroptosis in the Sham group and SCI group (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this transcriptional signature, we observed a marked increase in protein levels of 4-Hydroxynonenal (4HNE) and NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) (Fig. 1B–D), alongside abnormal accumulation of lipid droplets within neurons (Fig. 1E–F), collectively providing robust evidence of ferroptotic activity in the SCI. To investigate the effects of FGF21 on lysosomal function after SCI, we performed GSEA analysis on the samples of SCI group and FGF21 group. The results revealed a significant alteration in the expression profiles of lysosome-related genes between the two groups, indicating that FGF21 treatment substantially modulates lysosomal pathways after SCI (Fig. 1G). Consistently, Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that FGF21 treatment significantly reduced the levels of 4HNE and NOX4 in neurons, while concurrently upregulating the expression of lysosomal markers cathepsin D (CTSD) and Lysosomal associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1). These molecular changes were accompanied by a marked reduction in lipid droplet accumulation in neuronal cells (Fig. 1H–K). Western blot analysis further confirmed that FGF21 treatment not only restored the SCI-induced elevation of 4HNE and NOX4, but also significantly increased the protein levels of LAMP1 and CTSD (Fig. 1L–P). Collectively, these results indicated that FGF21 improves lysosomal function and inhibits ferroptosis after SCI, ultimately preserving neuronal lipid homeostasis. To further confirm in vivo results and identify action mechanism, we turned to cellular models of ferroptosis. Treatment with FGF21 effectively attenuated ROS levels while preserving lysosomal function (Suppl Fig. S1A–C). Given that ferroptosis is characterized by lethal lipid peroxidation, we assessed peroxidized lipid accumulation using the C11-BODIPY probe. FGF21 treatment significantly mitigated FINO2-induced lipid peroxidation (Suppl Fig. S1D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis showed that FGF21 reduced the enrichment of 4HNE and NOX4 on lysosomes (Suppl Fig. S1E–J). Consistently, western blot analysis demonstrated that FGF21 not only reversed the FINO2-induced upregulation of 4HNE and NOX4 but also significantly enhanced the protein levels of LAMP1 and CTSD (Suppl Fig. S1K–O). Collectively, these results establish that FGF21 confers protection against ferroptosis, at least in part, by stabilizing lysosomal function.

Design and Characteristics of SFGF21

The SFGF21 conjugate was synthesized via a two-step procedure: FGF21 was first coupled to DBCO-PEG-NHS, followed by copper-free click chemistry with N3-SS31 (Fig. 2A–C). Following dialysis (2000Da) to remove unreacted small molecules, successful conjugation was confirmed. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed successful conjugation by the absence of key azide (–N3) absorption band of SS31 at approximately 2100 cm−1 in the SFGF21 spectrum, indicating its participation in the click reaction. Meanwhile, the retention of SS31-specific vibrational modes within the 1600–1000 cm−1 range further supported the formation of the conjugate (Fig. 2D). Consistent with these findings, SDS-PAGE analysis stained with Coomassie blue revealed a higher molecular weight for SFGF21 relative to native FGF21, confirming the formation of a covalent complex. The final product was further characterized by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting (Fig. 2E). Based on the BCA protein concentration assay before and after dialysis, the modification efficiency was estimated to be 86.79% (Table 3). To establish optimal working concentrations, we performed CCK-8 assays, revealing that 12.5 μM FINO2 and 10 μg/mL SFGF21 were suitable for subsequent assays (Suppl Fig. S2). Calcein-AM/PI staining confirmed that SFGF21 at 10 μg/mL exhibited no significant cytotoxicity in PC12 cells (Fig. 2F–G). Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy analysis revealed that the protein structures of FGF21 and sFGF21 are altered (Fig. 2H).

SFGF21 prevents neuronal ferroptosis by maintaining lysosomal function and lipid homeostasis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the differential therapeutic effects of SFGF21 and FGF21 following SCI, we performed RNA-sequencing analysis. Transcriptome Gene Ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that SFGF21 exhibits significantly greater neuroprotective potential compared with FGF21 (Fig. 3A). Further GSEA analysis indicated that SFGF21 possesses superior regulatory capacity in inhibiting ferroptosis and associated oxidative stress (Fig. 3B–C). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that SFGF21 significantly inhibited the expression of 4HNE and NOX4, attenuated lipid droplet accumulation, and upregulated Lamp1 and CTSD (Fig. 3D–G). These findings were corroborated by western blot analysis, which revealed lower protein levels of 4HNE and NOX4, alongside elevated LAMP1 and CTSD expression, in the SFGF21-treated group, indicating an enhanced capacity to alleviate oxidative damage and preserve lysosomal homeostasis (Suppl Fig. S3A–E). At the cellular level, flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining consistently demonstrated that SFGF21 surpasses FGF21 in reducing ROS production and protecting lysosomal function (Suppl Fig. S4A–E). Given that ferroptosis is fundamentally driven by lipid peroxidation, we employed the C11-BODIPY586/596 probe to assess peroxidized lipid accumulation. SFGF21 treatment resulted in markedly greater suppression of lipid peroxidation than FGF21 (Suppl Fig. S4F). Immunofluorescence imaging further revealed that SFGF21 more effectively reduced lysosomal enrichment of 4HNE and NOX4 (Suppl Fig. S4E–L), reinforcing its protective role against lipid peroxidation-mediated injury. Moreover, Western blot confirmed that SFGF21 treatment led to lower 4HNE and NOX4 expression and higher LAMP1 and CTSD levels relative to FGF21 treatment (Suppl Fig. S4M–Q). Further, we investigated the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling pathway, a central regulator of lipid metabolism. While FGF21 only modestly increased the phosphorylation of AMPK and Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase 1 (ACC1), SFGF21 markedly enhanced their phosphorylation, highlighting its pivotal role in restoring lipid metabolic homeostasis (Suppl Fig. S5A–F). To assess the functional consequences of this enhanced AMPK activation, we examined Perilipin 2 (PLIN2), a key lipid droplet-associated protein, in neurons surrounding the injury site. SCI induced prominent PLIN2 expression, which was partially reduced by FGF21 treatment and markedly diminished by SFGF21 treatment, indicating superior restoration of neuronal lipid homeostasis (Suppl Fig. S5G). Collectively, both in vivo and in vitro evidence demonstrate that SFGF21 exerts superior neuroprotective effects compared with FGF21 after SCI. These enhanced effects are achieved through coordinated improvement of lysosomal function, suppression of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, activation of AMPK/ACC1 signaling, and restoration of neuronal lipid homeostasis.

SFGF21 promotes mitochondrial–lysosomal crosstalk

To assess the mitochondrial targeting capability of SFGF21 and its role in reducing oxidative stress, we measured mitochondrial superoxide levels using the mitochondrial superoxide probes (MitoSOX) probe. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that SFGF21 exhibited significantly greater capacity to scavenge mitochondrial peroxides than FGF21 (Fig. 4A; Suppl Fig. 7A). To further investigate how SFGF21 modulates mitochondrial function and its interplay with lysosomes, we performed fluorescence colocalization analysis to visualize the spatial relationship between mitochondria and lysosomes. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that SFGF21 showed markedly higher accumulation in mitochondria than FGF21. Moreover, SFGF21 more effectively promoted the colocalization between lysosomes and mitochondria (Fig. 4B–E), indicating enhanced inter-organellar crosstalk. To determine whether receptor positioning mediates SFGF21's coordination of mitochondrial and lysosomal function, we next examined the subcellular localization of FGF21 receptors, FGFR1 and FGFR2, relative to these organelles. Immunofluorescence colocalization with organelle markers revealed distinct distribution patterns: FGFR1 was localized not only to the cell membrane but also exhibited clear signals in mitochondria and lysosomes in the SFGF21-treated group. In contrast, FGFR2 remained largely restricted to the cell membrane (Fig. 4F, Suppl Fig. S6, Suppl Fig. S7B–C), suggesting that SFGF21 may exert its organelle-directed effects primarily through FGFR1. Given that an acidic lysosomal environment is essential for degradative activity and cellular homeostasis, while stable mitochondrial membrane potential is critical for Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) production and normal mitochondrial function, we next assessed these parameters following SCI. Consistent with previous reports [23,24], SCI induced lysosomal alkalinization and mitochondrial membrane depolarization. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that SFGF21 more effectively restored lysosomal acidity and stabilized ΔΨm compared to FGF21 (Fig. 5G and H, Suppl Fig. S7E), highlighting its superior capacity to maintain organelle function after injury. Considering these improvements in organelle function and ROS clearance, we next examined whether SFGF21 promotes the removal of damaged mitochondria. Immunofluorescence analysis of the autophagy marker LC3B revealed that SFGF21 markedly enhanced its colocalization with mitochondria, indicating a stronger capacity to induce mitophagy compared to FGF21 (Fig. 5I, Suppl Fig. S7D). To confirm this hypothesis, we employed siRNA-mediated FGFR1 knockdown. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that upon FGFR1 inhibition, SFGF21 failed to effectively reduce either total or mitochondrial ROS levels (Fig. 5A–B). Consistently, JC-1 staining indicated that SFGF21 could no longer restore mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in the absence of FGFR1 (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence colocalization of 4HNE/LysoTracker and NOX4/LysoTracker demonstrated that FGFR1 knockdown abrogated SFGF21's ability to suppress abnormal accumulation of lipid peroxidation markers on lysosomes (Fig. 5D–E). Western blot analysis confirmed that, following FGFR1 inhibition, SFGF21 failed to restore lysosomal-associated proteins (LAMP1 and CTSD) or ameliorate oxidative stress and ferroptosis markers (NOX4 and 4HNE) (Fig. 5F–J). Collectively, these findings indicate that SFGF21 exerts its mitochondrial-targeting and ROS-scavenging effects, at least in part via FGFR1-facilitated mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk. In summary, these results demonstrate that SFGF21 possesses superior mitochondrial-targeting capability, efficiently reduces mitochondrial ROS, and coordinates organelle function. This enhanced efficacy is likely mediated through FGFR1 and involves facilitated mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk and subsequent mitophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria.

SFGF21 promotes mitophagy via BNIP3/BNIP3L pathway

First, we performed GO enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes. Differentially expressed genes were identified using a fold change >2 and p-value <0.05 as thresholds. Volcano plot analysis revealed that, compared with the SCI group, the SFGF21 group exhibited 1868 upregulated and 960 downregulated genes (Fig. 6A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of biological processes and cellular components related to the “mitochondrial inner membrane,” “mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I,” “mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I assembly,” and “mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV” in the SFGF21 group relative to the SCI group (Fig. 6B). GSEA further revealed significant enrichment of mitophagy-related genes in the SFGF21 group compared to the SCI group (Fig. 6C). To investigate the mechanisms of mitophagy activation, we systematically evaluated both the PTEN-induced putative kinase 1(PINK1)/Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (Parkin)-dependent and receptor-mediated pathways. Since Western blot analysis revealed no significant changes in PINK1 or Parkin expression across the SFGF21, FGF21, and SCI groups at both the cellular and tissue levels (Suppl Fig. S8A–F), we focused on the receptor-mediated pathway. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that SFGF21 significantly enhanced the colocalization of BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3) with LC3B in vivo (Fig. 6D–E). Consistently, Western blot analysis confirmed that SFGF21 activated the BNIP3/BCL2 interacting protein 3 like (BNIP3L)-mediated mitophagy pathway, promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria, reducing intracellular ROS accumulation, restoring GPX4 expression, and ultimately suppressing ferroptosis (Fig. 6F–k). These effects were further confirmed using cellular models (Fig. 6L–N).

Given our earlier finding that FGFR1 mediates SFGF21-induced mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk, we next investigated whether FGFR1 acts as an upstream regulator of BNIP3/BNIP3L. Western blot analysis revealed that siRNA-mediated silencing of FGFR1 significantly attenuated SFGF21-induced upregulation of BNIP3 and BNIP3L (Suppl Fig. S9), indicating that FGFR1 is required for SFGF21-induced activation of the BNIP3/BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy pathway.

To elucidate the critical role of BNIP3 in SFGF21-mediated neuroprotection, we established a BNIP3 knockdown model in PC12 cells using a lentiviral vector. Western blot analysis confirmed a substantial reduction in BNIP3 protein levels in cells transfected with the pCLenti-U6-shRNA(BNIP3)-CMV-Puro-WPRE lentivirus (Suppl Fig. 10). Functional assays revealed that, following BNIP3 knockdown, SFGF21 failed to effectively reduce either total ROS or mitochondrial superoxide levels, as assessed by flow cytometry with ROS and MitoSOX probes (Fig. 7A–B). Consistently, JC-1 staining revealed that SFGF21 could no longer restore ΔΨm in the absence of BNIP3 (Fig. 7C). Immunofluorescence colocalization analysis revealed that BNIP3 deficiency abrogated SFGF21's ability to suppress abnormal accumulation of 4HNE and NOX4 on lysosomes (Fig. 7D–G). Moreover, SFGF21 treatment failed to decrease mitochondrial p62 or increase mitochondrial LC3B in BNIP3-deficient cells, demonstrating that BNIP3 is essential for mitophagy induction (Fig. 7H–K). Western blot analysis further confirmed that, in the absence of BNIP3, SFGF21 could neither restore lysosome-associated protein expression (LAMP1 and CTSD) nor ameliorate oxidative stress and ferroptosis markers (NOX4 and 4HNE) (Fig. 7L–R). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SFGF21 suppresses oxidative stress and ferroptosis by promoting BNIP3/BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy and the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria. This protective effect requires FGFR1-dependent activation of BNIP3/BNIP3L, as knockdown of either FGFR1 or BNIP3 abolishes SFGF21's neuroprotective actions, underscoring the FGFR1–BNIP3/BNIP3L axis as a central mediator of therapeutic efficacy after SCI.

SFGF21 promotes axonal regeneration and preserves axonal structural stability

The efficacy of SCI repair is evaluated using two key indicators: axonal regeneration and effective neuroprotection. To compare the neurorestorative capacities of different treatment methods, Neurofilament 200 (NF200) and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) antibodies were used to label axonal structures and glial scar regions, respectively, within the injury border zone. Compared to SCI group and FGF21 group, the SFGF21 group exhibited a significantly reduced lesion area and the greatest capacity for axonal penetration through the glial scar, indicative of enhanced axonal regeneration (Fig. 8A–C). Axonal stability is another critical indicator of functional integrity. Acetylated tubulin (ACE Tubulin) is a well-established marker of stable microtubule assemblies, and its expression levels correlate strongly with structural persistence. In contrast, tyrosinated tubulin (TYR Tubulin) is associated with dynamically unstable microtubules, reflecting their inherent structural lability. Following SCI, TYR Tubulin expression is markedly elevated in most axons, accompanied by microtubule depolymerization and instability (Fig. 8D–F). In contrast, SFGF21 treatment significantly upregulates ACE Tubulin, indicating its efficacy in promoting stable microtubule assembly and thereby preserving axonal structural integrity.

SFGF21 promoted the restoration of spinal cord tissue architecture and the recovery of behavioral function in SCI mice

Firstly, we evaluated the spinal cord tissue structure and neuronal survival in each group using H&E and Nissl staining. Among all groups, SFGF21 treatment exhibited the most pronounced protective effects, as evidenced by reduced tissue damage and greater preservation of neuronal architecture. A higher number of neurons was observed within the lesion core area in the SFGF21-treated group (Fig. 9A–B). Next, Electrophysiological assessment using electromyography (EMG) revealed that while only weak signals were detected in the SCI group, both FGF21 and SFGF21 treatments significantly improved signal transmission. SFGF21-treated mice exhibited stronger evoked responses than those receiving FGF21 alone (Fig. 9C). To further assess motor function recovery, we conducted behavioral assessments using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) and the footprint test. Mice treated with SFGF21 demonstrated occasional plantar stepping and significantly improved hindlimb coordination relative to control groups, whereas mice in other groups exhibited only limited flexion and extension of the ankle joint (Fig. 9D–E). High-resolution video-based motion analysis further revealed coordinated movements at the ankle, knee, and hip joints in SFGF21-treated mice, accompanied by marked improvements in hindlimb weight support and gait cycle rhythm (Fig. 9F–G). Together, these data demonstrate that SFGF21 treatment confers robust neuroprotection and promotes functional recovery after SCI.

Discussion

SCI remains as one of the most devastating neurological conditions, yet effective strategies to promote neural repair and functional recovery are still lacking. The present study demonstrates that the SFGF21 exerts potential neuroprotective effects in a murine model of SCI by targeting the FGFR1–BNIP3/BNIP3L axis to enhance mitophagy and suppress ferroptosis. Our findings not only elucidate a novel mechanism underlying SFGF21's therapeutic potential but also establish the FGFR1–BNIP3/BNIP3L axis as a critical mediator of organelle crosstalk and neuronal survival after SCI.

Our results substantiate the foundational hypothesis by providing multimodal evidence for the superior efficacy of SFGF21 over native FGF21. Lipid droplets (LDs), as dynamic organelles involved in lipid storage and metabolism, normally serve as buffers against oxidative stress [25]. However, pathological LD accumulation following SCI exacerbates neuronal ferroptosis [26,27]. Lysosomes, the primary degradative organelles responsible for clearing excess lipids through lipophagy [28], are particularly vulnerable to injury-induced oxidative stress, as elevated intracellular ROS compromises lysosomal integrity [29]. Quantitative assessments in vivo and in vitro consistently demonstrated that SFGF21 suppresses canonical ferroptosis markers more potently, mitigates pathological lipid droplet accumulation, and enhances lysosomal integrity, as indicated by increased LAMP1 and CTSD expression. Unbiased transcriptomic profiling via GSEA further validated these observations at a systems level, revealing that SFGF21 induces a transcriptional program more strongly associated with ferroptosis inhibition, oxidative stress reduction, and neuroprotection. These collective findings directly address the need for interventions capable of targeting the multifaceted organelle dysfunction in SCI, and to further confirm that rational protein engineering successfully augmented the therapeutic potential of FGF21, establishing a firm empirical basis for mechanistic inquiry.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, this study further delineates the causal pathway by which SFGF21 reprograms the maladaptive “lysosome-mitochondria-ferroptosis” axis. The principal innovation resides in achieving spatially coordinated subcellular intervention. By leveraging the mitochondrial-targeting peptide SS-31, SFGF21 localizes precisely to the epicenter of ROS generation. This site-specific delivery fosters synergistic action: it enables local delivery of FGF21's lysosome-restorative functions while concurrently enhancing physical and functional interactions between mitochondria and lysosomes. A major pivotal discovery was the identification of the BNIP3/BNIP3L pathway, rather than the PINK1/Parkin pathway, as the primary mediator of SFGF21-induced mitophagy [30,31]. Genetic ablation of BNIP3 unequivocally abolished the beneficial effects of SFGF21 on ROS scavenging, organelle functional recovery, and ferroptosis suppression. Such signaling pathway comprises mitochondrial targeting, enhanced organelle contact, BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, and subsequent clearance of damaged mitochondria coherently models how SFGF21 disrupts the self-amplifying pathological loop. It represents a paradigm shift from merely ameliorating downstream symptoms to actively resetting the dysfunctional subcellular network by reinstating homeostatic inter-organelle communication.

The molecular and cellular benefits conferred by SFGF21 ultimately translated to significant anatomical and functional restoration. Treatment resulted in superior tissue preservation, promoted axonal regeneration across the inhibitory glial scar, and enhanced axonal structural stability. These histological improvements led to substantial and sustained recovery of locomotor function, as rigorously quantified through kinematic gait analysis, electrophysiological assessments, and standardized behavioral scoring. These outcomes directly meet a major unmet clinical challenge, the scarcity of therapies that foster genuine neurological repair and functional restitution after SCI. Consequently, this study validates the “lysosome-mitochondria-ferroptosis” axis not merely as a descriptive pathological framework, but as a powerful therapeutic target, thereby offering a novel tool for designing neuroprotective strategies.

To broaden the interpretive scope and foster interdisciplinary dialogue, we propose two complementary perspectives. From a “systems bioenergetics standpoint” the profound impact of SFGF21 on lipid metabolism and mitochondrial quality control suggests its role as a regulator of post-injury neuronal metabolic reprogramming. Spinal cord injury induces a bioenergetic crisis [32]; SFGF21, by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria—sources of metabolic inefficiency and ROS—and restoring lipid homeostasis, may facilitate a critical shift from a pathological, peroxidative metabolic state toward a sustainable bioenergetic profile supportive of neuronal survival and repair. This aligns with the established systemic metabolic functions of FGF21 and suggest its engineered variant as a candidate for investigating other disorders characterized by metabolic-stress-induced neurodegeneration. Furthermore, from a “nanomedicine perspective”, the SFGF21 construct exemplifies a proteinaceous nanotherapeutic. The SS-31 component serves as a biologically derived, organelle-specific targeting ligand, while the PEG linker modulates pharmacokinetic properties. This biomimetic design strategy, which utilizes endogenous peptides for subcellular targeting, may offer advantages over synthetic nanocarriers, including reduced immunogenicity and enhanced biocompatibility. Such hypothesis positions our platform at the convergence of protein engineering and advanced drug delivery, proposing a generalizable platform for developing precision therapeutics targeting organelle dysfunction across a spectrum of diseases.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size for transcriptomic analysis was relatively small which may limit the statistical power for detecting differentially expressed genes. Second, the lack of publicly available transcriptomic datasets on FGF21-treated SCI precluded external validation of our sequencing data. Although key transcriptional findings were validated at the protein level, future studies with larger cohorts and independent datasets are warranted to confirm the robustness of our conclusions.

In summary, by combining mitochondrial-targeting capabilities with the bioassays of FGF21, the construct effectively restores mitochondrial quality control and lysosomal homeostasis. Mechanistic assays reveal its operation via FGFR1-dependent mitochondria-lysosome crosstalk and BNIP3-dependent mitophagy, and functional outcomes affirm its translational promise. By deploying a targeted protein engineering strategy to disrupt a defined pathological axis, we provide not only a promising candidate for SCI treatment, but also a conceptual and methodological framework for addressing complex disorders rooted in dysfunctional inter-organelle communication. Future studies focusing on the proposed bioenergetic and nanotherapeutic aspects are expected to further enhance the scientific relevance and translational applicability of such FGF21-based fusion protein. Future mechanistic assays should investigate additional pathways to act synergistically with BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, including other autophagy or mitochondrial homeostasis.

Animals and models

C57BL/6 mice (aged 8 weeks, weighing 20–22 g) were obtained from the Animal Center of Hangzhou Medical College. All animals were housed for 7 days under specific pathogen-free conditions with a controlled environment (temperature 23–25 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle, 50–60% humidity) with free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University and were conducted in strict accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Ethics Approval No. wydw2025-0232).

The establishment of the spinal cord injury model was conducted in accordance with the method previously reported [33]. Mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium prior to surgery. After confirming adequate anesthesia, the T9–T10 spinal segments were exposed by removing the spinous processes and laminae. A contusive spinal cord injury was then induced at the T9 level using a Rewald impactor under standardized parameters (impact velocity: 1.5 m/s; displacement: 0.8 mm). Following surgery, manual bladder expression was performed twice daily (morning and evening) until reflexive urination was restored.

Transcriptome sequencing

RNA isolation and library preparation

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Sham, (2) SCI, (3) FGF21, and (4) SFGF21. Spinal cord tissues were harvest at 7 days post-injury. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analyses were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

RNA sequencing and differential gene expression analysis

RNA sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads, yielding an average of 64.3 million raw reads per sample. Raw FASTQ data were processed using fastp (v0.20.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, yielding an average of 52.8 million clean reads per sample. The cleaned reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified with HTSeq-count to obtain read counts and calculate FPKM values. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R (v3.2.0) to assess biological reproducibility among samples.

Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package. Significant DEGs were identified using a Q-value < 0.05 and an absolute fold change > 2 as thresholds. Hierarchical clustering was conducted in R (v3.2.0) to visualize gene expression patterns across experimental groups and samples. Additionally, the top 30 DEGs were visualized using radar plots generated with the R package ggradar, highlighting the distinct expression profiles of upregulated and downregulated genes.

To explore the biological significance of the identified genes, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted in R (v3.2.0) using a hypergeometric distribution algorithm to identify significantly overrepresented biological processes and pathways. Significant enrichment results were visualized using bar, chord, and bubble plots. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed with GSEA software (v4.2), in which genes were ranked according to their differential expression between groups, and predefined gene sets were assessed for enrichment at the top or bottom of the ranked list to identify functionally relevant pathways.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from samples using standard protocols. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 8%-12% SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and washed 3 times with TBST (10 min each). The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following an additional three washes, the immunoreactive bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen spinal cord sections were thawed at room temperature for 30 min and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove the optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. The sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. After three washes with PBST (10 min each), the sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, with all images collected at the lesion interface. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected from each experimental group for analysis. Quantification of positive cells and fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software, and statistical graphs were generated with Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Synthesis and characterization of SFGF21

FGF21 was conjugated with DBCO-PEG-NHS via amidation, followed by copper-free click chemistry with N3-PEG-CH2CH2CO-D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH2 (azide-modified SS31) to yield SS31-DBCO-FGF21 (SFGF21). All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood to maintain sterility. Briefly, 1 mg FGF21 and 0.24 mg DBCO-PEG-NHS were dissolved in 1 mL PBS, homogenized, and stirred at 4 °C for 48 h to generate PEGylated FGF21. Subsequently, 0.0496 mg azide-modified SS31 dissolved in 200 μL PBS, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 48 h at 4 °C. The resulting SS31-DBCO-PEG-FGF21 conjugate (designated SFGF21) was purified by dialysis (20 kDA MWCO) with three buffer exchanges over 24 h, sterilized via a 0.22 μm syringe filter (Millipore, USA), and lyophilized to yield SFGF21 powder. Successful conjugation was confirmed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (NICOLET iS20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) over 500–4000 cm−1. A small portion of the synthesized SFGF21 was stored at −80 °C as the undialyzed control, while the rest was dialyzed against a 20-kDa membrane for 72 h. The protein concentrations of both dialyzed and undialyzed samples were measured by BCA assay, and the modification efficiency was calculated accordingly. All procedures were performed in 3 times.

FGF21 and SFGF21 injection

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Sham, (2) SCI, (3) FGF21, and (4) SFGF21. FGF21 and SFGF21 were freshly dissolved in sterile physiological saline at a concentration of 100 μg/mL immediately before administration. The drug concentration was based on the concentration used in our previous study [34]. Intrathecal injections were performed following a previously established protocol [35]. Briefly, mice under isoflurane anesthesia were placed in the prone position, and the lumbar skin was disinfected. An insulin syringe was inserted between the L5 and L6 vertebrae, with correct placement confirmed by a tail-flick reflex. A 10 μL solution was infused slowly over 30 s, and mice were monitored until full recovery. Treatment was initiated 30 min after SCI induction and administered once daily for 7 consecutive days. Spinal cord tissues were collected at two endpoints: 7 and 30 days post-injury.

Cell culture

PC12 cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

CCK-8

Cells were digested to a single-cell suspension and uniformly seeded into 96-well plates, followed by 24 h of incubation under standard culture conditions. The culture medium was then removed, and cells were treated with the appropriate drug solution for an additional 24 h. A CCK-8 working solution was prepared by diluting CCK-8 reagent in RPMI 1640 medium at a 1:9 ratio. After removing the drug-containing medium, 100 μL of the CCK-8 working solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Changes in absorbance were used to assess cell viability and proliferation. Each experimental condition was tested in triplicate, and at least three independent experiments were performed.

Cell group and administration

The PC12 cell line was randomly divided into 4 groups: 1) Control, 2) FINO2, 3) FINO2+FGF21,4) FINO2+SFGF21. The optimal working concentrations of FINO2 and SFGF21 were determined using the CCK-8 assay.

Probe staining

A mitochondrial probe powder (50 μg) was dissolved in 93 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 1 mM stock solution, which was subsequently diluted 1:10,000 with assay buffer to obtain the working solution. Cells were incubated with the working solution in a 37 °C humidified incubator for 30 min, washed three times with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2si).

The lysosomal probe stock solution was diluted 1:1000 in culture medium to prepare the working solution. Cells were incubated with this solution at 37 °C for 30 min, washed three times with sterile DPBS, and immediately imaged using a confocal microscope.

Calcein AM/propidium iodide (PI) stock solution was diluted 1:1000 in pre-warmed culture medium to prepare the working solution. Cells were loaded with the dye for 30 min at 37 °C, washed three times with DPBS, and visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

The ROS probe stock solution was diluted 1:1000 in HBSS or culture medium to obtain the working solution. Cells were incubated with the working solution at 37 °C for 30 min, protected from light, washed 3 times with sterile DPBS, and analyzed by confocal microscopy.

A MitoSox probe powder (9 μg) was dissolved in 10 μL of N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) to prepare a 1 mM stock solution, which was subsequently diluted 1:1000 with RPMI medium 1640 to generate the working solution.

A BODIPY 493/503 probe power (5 mg) was dissolved in 3.8152 mL of DMSO to prepare a 5 mM stock solution, which was subsequently diluted 1:1000 with PBS to generate the working solution. Slides were incubated with the working solution for 20 min, washed three times with PBS, and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2si).

Cell flow assay

Cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 3 min at room temperature. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the cells were resuspended in sterile DPBS containing the respective fluorescent probes: ROS probes, lysosomal probes (LysoTracker) and MitoSOX. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 45 min with gentle agitation every 15 min to ensure uniform staining. After incubation, cells were washed three times with sterile DPBS. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) with FITC or PE channels. Data were processed and analyzed using FLOWJO software.

siRNA-mediated knockdown of FGFR1

For siRNA-mediated silencing of FGFR1, cells were seeded on a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL and cultured for 24 h. Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) with Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After transfection, the medium was replaced with normal culture medium containing serum, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. SiRNA Negative Control was used as a negative control. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blot.

Construction of BNIP3−/− cells

PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight to reach 30-50% confluence prior to transduction. Cells were then transfected with pCLenti-U6-shRNA (BNIP3)-CMV-Puro-WPRE virus, while the negative control cells were transfected with pCLenti-U6-shRNA (NC)-CMV-Puro-WPRE virus. The shRNA targeting sequence for BNIP3 was 5′-AGATACCAACAGAGCTGAAAT-3′, and the negative control shRNA sequence was 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′. All viral constructs were obtained from Shanghai OBiO Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For transduction, cells were incubated with virus at an MOI of 10 in medium containing 5 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After 12 h, the medium was replaced, and cells were cultured for an additional 60 h. At 72 h post-transduction, cells were harvested for protein extraction. Knockdown efficiency was validated using Western blot. Three independent experiments were performed, and BNIP3 levels were quantified and normalized to controls to confirm significant knockdown.

Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining

Proteins were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 30 min following the manufacturer's protocol. The gel was then incubated in deionized water overnight to destain the background and achieve clear visualization of protein bands. Images were captured using a digital imaging system.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Nissl staining

HE staining

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde to preserve structural integrity, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 4 μm sections were prepared using a microtome. H&E staining was performed following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, sections were rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin and eosin to visualize nuclei and cytoplasm, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological alterations were examined and documented under a light microscope.

Nissl staining

Tissue sections (4 μm) were prepared as described above. Nissl staining was performed following the manufacturer's protocol. Sections were rehydrated and incubated in cresyl violet solution at 37 °C for 1 h. After rinsing with distilled water, slides were differentiated in graded alcohols, dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and cover slipped with mounting medium. Images were captured using a light microscope.

Analysis of the functional recovery of mice

Locomotor function in mice was assessed using a plastic runway (50 cm × 5 cm × 20 cm). Each mouse was recorded with an iPhone at 60 frames per second, capturing at least 6 consecutive gait cycles. Video acquisition focused on the central 30 cm segment, where mice exhibited continuous walking, dragging, or stepping over approximately 6–10 strides. If hindlimb movement was absent, gait rhythm was inferred from forelimb motion. Each mice underwent a minimum of three independent trials to ensure reliability. Videos were selected for analysis based on two criteria: (1) uninterrupted forward movement within the recording zone, and (2) absence of head turning or directional changes. Selected videos were processed with DeepLabCut [36] for automated joint tracking, and resulting behavioral data were analyzed in MATLAB to generate bar plots of hindlimb kinematics. Gait cycle parameters—including swing angle, joint excursions at the hip, knee, and ankle, and horizontal hindlimb displacement—were quantified following established protocols [37,38]. To ensure objectivity, data collection and analysis were performed by separate experimenters. DeepLabCut enabled quantitative evaluation of locomotor activity on a transparent surface.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0. All data met the assumption of normality (P > 0.05) and were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student's t-test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied. BMS scores were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
