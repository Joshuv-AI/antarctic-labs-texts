Validation of the 9th Edition of the Tumor‐Node‐Metastasis Staging System for Non‐Metastatic Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma: A Multicenter Retrospective Analysis From High‐Incidence Regions
Authors: Yongquan Wei, Danxue Qin, Baoqing Yang, Bin Deng, Qianxian Luo, Lihong Huang, Jiazhang Wei, Shaojie Zhang, Weiming Deng, Guiping Lan, Min Li, Shenhong Qu, Jingjin Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate and compare the prognostic value of the 8th and 9th editions of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging system in patients with non‐metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC).MethodsClinical data from 470 patients with NPC treated between November 2019 and June 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were restaged according to the 8th and 9th editions of the AJCC/UICC staging system. OS was the primary endpoint; locoregional recurrence–free survival (LRFS), distant metastasis–free survival (DMFS), and disease‐free survival (DFS) were secondary endpoints. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using global and pairwise log‐rank tests. Prognostic discriminative ability was evaluated using the concordance index (C‐index) and Akaike information criterion (AIC).ResultsSeventeen patients (3.6%) classified as T3 by TNM‐8 were reclassified as T4 by TNM‐9. Thirty‐one patients (6.6%) were reclassified to N3 because of advanced iENE. TNM‐9 improved discrimination for DMFS across N categories (log‐rank p < 0.001 vs. p = 0.016 for TNM‐8) and for OS (p < 0.001 vs. p = 0.042). T categories discriminated LRFS in both editions (p = 0.012 for each), whereas OS differences were not significant (TNM‐8 p = 0.139; TNM‐9 p = 0.137). TNM‐9 overall stage discriminated OS (p = 0.002), unlike TNM‐8 (p = 0.121). Both editions discriminated DFS (both p < 0.001), but TNM‐9 provided better pairwise separation between early and advanced overall stages. The 9th edition exhibited enhanced predictive performance compared with the 8th edition, as evidenced by higher C‐index values and lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) values for both DFS and OS prediction across overall stages.ConclusionThe 9th edition of the AJCC/UICC staging system demonstrated superior prognostic discriminative ability compared with the 8th edition, enabling more accurate risk stratification and supporting more informed clinical decision‐making for patients with NPC.Keywords: 9th edition, AJCC/UICC staging, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, prognosis, survival analysis

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor originating from the epithelial tissues of the nasopharynx and is characterized by a unique geographical distribution, with high prevalence in Southern China, Southeast Asia, and North Africa [1, 2]. Unlike other head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, NPC is closely associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection and has a high tendency for local invasion and cervical lymph node metastasis [3]. Accurate tumor staging is essential for treatment planning, prognostic evaluation, and clinical research. The tumor‐node‐metastasis (TNM) staging system, jointly issued by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC), is the most widely used standard for tumor staging worldwide. With advances in diagnostic and therapeutic technologies and the accumulation of clinical data, staging systems require continuous updates to meet evolving clinical practice needs.

The 8th edition of the AJCC/UICC staging system, released in 2017, introduced significant revisions compared with the 7th edition. These changes included the removal of masticator space involvement as a criterion for T4 classification and the introduction of the lower neck region concept, defined as lymph nodes below the caudal border of the cricoid cartilage in the N classification [4, 5]. Multiple studies have confirmed that the 8th edition has better prognostic value than the 7th edition [6]. Nevertheless, discrimination remained suboptimal for Stage I versus II and for T2 versus T3 disease, while imbalanced distributions—particularly the concentration of patients in T3 and N1 categories—could further reduce statistical power for adjacent‐stage comparisons [7, 8]. Additionally, the 8th edition M classification is relatively simplified, failing to adequately reflect the impact of different metastatic patterns on prognosis and lacking a detailed classification based on the number and location of metastases [9].

In 2024, the AJCC/UICC released the 9th edition staging system, further optimizing the staging criteria for NPC. The major revisions include the addition of advanced extranodal extension as a criterion for the N classification, refining the M classification, expanding Stage I into IA (T1‐2N0M0) and IB (T1‐2N1M0), downgrading stages III and IVA in the 8th edition to stages II and III in the 9th edition, respectively, and reserving Stage IV exclusively for patients with distant metastasis (M1), which is further divided into IVA (M1a) and IVB (M1b) [8]. These revisions aim to provide a more accurate reflection of tumor biological behavior and patient prognosis.

However, since the 9th edition was released recently, validation studies from high‐incidence regions of NPC in China remain lacking. The applicability and prognostic value of the 9th edition staging system in different regions and treatment modalities require further validation. Therefore, this study aimed to validate the prognostic value of the newly released 9th edition AJCC/UICC staging system and compare its performance with the 8th edition by retrospectively analyzing the clinical data of 470 patients with NPC treated at three high‐incidence centers, providing evidence‐based support for the clinical application of the 9th edition staging system.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study included patients with NPC who received radical treatment at the Department of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery, People's Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, and Ward 3, Department of Oncology, The Tenth Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, and Ward 2, Department of Oncology, The Wuzhou Red Cross Hospital, between November 2019 and June 2022. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed NPC; (2) treatment‐naïve patients without prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or surgery; (3) received radical intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) with or without concurrent chemotherapy and/or induction/adjuvant chemotherapy; (4) complete pre‐treatment imaging data (including nasopharyngeal and neck magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], chest computed tomography [CT], abdominal ultrasound or CT, whole‐body bone scan or positron emission tomography [PET]‐CT); and (5) complete clinical and follow‐up data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concurrent malignancies, (2) distant metastasis before treatment (8th edition Stage IVB), (3) incomplete radical treatment due to severe comorbidities, and (4) loss to follow‐up or follow‐up of < 3 months. A total of 470 eligible patients were included. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: KYSY2024002), and the requirement for informed consent was waived. Imaging Assessment

All patients underwent pretreatment nasopharyngeal and neck‐enhanced MRI. MRI examinations were performed using 1.5 T or 3.0 T MRI scanners, with scanning range from the skull base to below the clavicle. Scanning sequences included axial T1‐weighted imaging (T1WI), axial and coronal T2‐weighted imaging (T2WI), axial diffusion‐weighted imaging, and axial and coronal enhanced T1WI. Gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd‐DTPA) was used as a contrast agent for enhanced scanning.

Imaging data were independently reviewed in a blinded manner by two radiologists with over 10 years of experience in head and neck tumor diagnosis. Without knowledge of clinical information and outcomes, they assessed the extent of primary tumor invasion and lymph node metastasis status (including location, size, number, and iENE). In cases where the two assessments differed, the final decision for this study was made by consensus. Each patient was staged according to both the 8th and 9th editions of the AJCC/UICC NPC TNM staging criteria.

Diagnostic criteria for lymph node metastasis were as previously described [2]: (1) retropharyngeal lymph nodes with short‐axis diameter ≥ 5 mm or cervical lymph nodes with short‐axis diameter ≥ 10 mm; (2) central necrosis or ring enhancement in lymph nodes; (3) extranodal extension; (4) clustering of three or more lymph nodes with short‐axis diameter ≥ 8 mm. Advanced extranodal extension was defined as imaging evidence of lymph node capsular disruption with involvement of adjacent muscles, skin, and/or neurovascular bundles.

Staging Criteria

All patients were staged according to both the 8th edition (released in 2017) and 9th edition (released in 2024) of the AJCC/UICC NPC TNM staging systems. The main differences between the two editions are as follows:

T category: The 9th edition refines the T3 and T4 definitions by adding unequivocal cranial nerve involvement and inferior orbital fissure involvement as explicit criteria for T4 classification.

N category: The 9th edition added “advanced extranodal extension involving adjacent muscles, skin, and/or neurovascular bundles” as a criterion for N3 classification.

M category: The 9th edition subdivided M1 into M1a (≤ 3 metastatic lesions involving ≥ 1 organ/site) and M1b (> 3 metastatic lesions involving ≥ 1 organ/site).

Overall stages: The 9th edition combined stages I and II of the 8th edition and redefined them as Stage IA (T1‐2N0M0) and IB (T1‐2N1M0), downgraded the 8th edition Stage III (T1‐2N2M0, T3N0‐2M0) to Stage II, downgraded the 8th edition Stage IVA (T4 or N3, M0) to Stage III, and Stage IV was restricted to patients with distant metastasis, further divided into stages IVA (M1a) and IVB (M1b).

Treatment

All the patients underwent radical IMRT. Radiotherapy was delivered using 6 MV X‐rays, with prescription doses of 68–72 Gy to the gross tumor volume of the nasopharynx (GTVnx) in 30–33 fractions; 66–70 Gy to the gross tumor volume of involved lymph nodes (GTVnd); 60–66 Gy to the high‐risk clinical target volume; and 50–56 Gy to the low‐risk clinical target volume. Based on staging and general condition, patients with Stage II and higher disease received concurrent chemotherapy; patients with locally advanced disease (8th edition stages III–IVA) received induction chemotherapy and/or adjuvant chemotherapy as clinically indicated.

Follow‐Up

After treatment completion, the patients were regularly followed up according to the following schedule: every 3 months during the first year, every 6 months during the second and third years, and annually thereafter. Follow‐up examinations included physical examination, nasopharyngoscopy, nasopharyngeal and neck MRI, chest CT or radiography, abdominal ultrasonography or CT, and plasma EBV DNA detection. Whole‐body bone scan or PET‐CT was performed when necessary.

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from treatment completion to death from any cause. Secondary endpoints were LRFS (time to local or regional recurrence), DMFS (time to distant metastasis), and DFS (time to any recurrence, distant metastasis, or death). The follow‐up cutoff date is June 30, 2025.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software version 4.3.3 (https://www.r‐project.org/). Continuous variables are presented as median (range) or mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the groups were compared using the log‐rank test.

To compare the prognostic predictive ability of the 8th and 9th editions, the following metrics were used: (1) receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC). ROC curves for predicting OS, LRFS, DMFS, and DFS were plotted for both editions, and AUC values were calculated and compared using DeLong's test. (2) The concordance index (C‐index). Harrell's C‐index was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of the staging system for survival outcomes. (3) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which was used to compare the goodness of fit of the prognostic models constructed using the two staging systems. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.01.

Comparison of Prognosis by T Category

LRFS

The 3‐year LRFS rates for TNM‐8 T1–T4 were 94.09%, 93.10%, 86.97%, and 81.39%, respectively (global log‐rank p = 0.012; Figure 2A). Corresponding TNM‐9 rates were 94.09%, 93.10%, 87.44%, and 81.54% (p = 0.012; Figure 2B). The overall log‐rank test for LRFS revealed significant differences across T categories in both the 8th and 9th edition staging systems (both p = 0.012). However, post hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrated that only the difference between T2 and T4 reached statistical significance in both editions (p = 0.005 for both). The comparison between T1 and T4 showed a trend toward significance (p = 0.014 for 8th; p = 0.015 for 9th). No other pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (all p > 0.05).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for locoregional recurrence–free survival (LRFS; A, B) and overall survival (OS; C, D) by T category in the 8th (A, C) and 9th (B, D) editions.

OS

The 3‐year OS rates for TNM‐8 T1–T4 were 96.51%, 90.76%, 88.89%, and 88.16%, respectively (global log‐rank p = 0.139; Figure 2C). Corresponding TNM‐9 rates were 96.51%, 90.76%, 88.95%, and 88.17% (p = 0.137; Figure 2D). The overall log‐rank tests for OS were not statistically significant for either the 8th (p = 0.139) or 9th (p = 0.137) edition staging systems.

Comparison of Prognosis by N Category

DMFS

The 3‐year DMFS rates for TNM‐8 N0–N3 were 96.97%, 89.61%, 81.33%, and 79.49%, respectively (global log‐rank p = 0.016; Figure 3A). Corresponding TNM‐9 rates were 96.97%, 95.75%, 84.96%, and 69.97% (p < 0.001; Figure 3B). Pairwise comparisons in the 8th edition showed that N0 differed from N3 (p = 0.011) and N2 (p = 0.027), and N1 differed from N3 (p = 0.022), while no significant differences were observed between N0 and N1 (p = 0.210), N1 and N2 (p = 0.071), or N2 and N3 (p = 0.338). In the 9th edition, however, N3 was significantly distinguished from N0, N1, and N2 (all p < 0.001), with an additional significant difference between N1 and N2 (p = 0.014).

OS

The 3‐year OS rates for the 8th edition N categories were 93.94% for N0, 91.14% for N1, 92.66% for N2, and 83.84% for N3 (p = 0.042, Figure 3C). The 3‐year OS rates for the 9th edition N categories were 93.94% for N0, 94.59% for N1, 94.42% for N2, and 80.77% for N3 (p < 0.001, Figure 3D). The 8th edition showed only a marginal difference between N2 and N3 (p = 0.010), with no other pairwise comparisons reaching significance (all p > 0.09). In the 9th edition, N3 was significantly different from N2 (p < 0.001).

Comparison of Prognosis by Overall Stages

DFS

The 3‐year DFS rates for the 8th edition overall stages were 100.00% for Stage I, 87.50% for Stage II, 76.87% for Stage III, and 66.44% for Stage IV (p < 0.001, Figure 4A). The 3‐year DFS rates for the 9th edition overall stages were: 100.00% for Stage IA, 89.47% for Stage IB, 80.43% for Stage II, and 62.98% for Stage III (p < 0.001, Figure 4B). The overall Log‐rank test revealed significant differences in DFS across overall stages for both staging systems (both p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons further demonstrated that for the 9th edition, DFS was significantly higher in Stage IA compared to Stage III (p = 0.0042), and in Stage IB compared to Stage III (p = 0.0011). However, no significant differences were observed between Stage IA and IB (p = 0.185) or between Stage IB and II (p = 0.147) under the 9th edition. For the 8th edition, significant DFS differences were only observed between Stage II versus IV (p = 0.002) and Stage III versus IV (p = 0.005).

OS

The 3‐year OS rates for the 8th edition overall stages were 100.00% for Stage I, 93.75% for Stage II, 92.16% for Stage III, and 86.30% for Stage IV (p = 0.121, Figure 4C). The 3‐year OS rates for the 9th edition overall stages were 100.00% for Stage IA, 97.37% for Stage IB, 93.62% for Stage II, and 84.53% for Stage III (p = 0.002, Figure 4D). The 8th edition staging system did not show a significant overall difference (p = 0.121), with only a marginal pairwise difference between Stage III and IV (p = 0.048). In contrast, the 9th edition demonstrated a significant overall OS difference (p < 0.001), which was primarily driven by the distinction between Stage II and Stage III (p = 0.002).

Comparison of Prognostic Predictive Ability Between Staging Systems

To validate the 9th edition staging system, we plotted ROC curves between the 8th and 9th editions (Figure 5A–D). The 9th edition consistently outperformed the 8th edition across all endpoints. For LRFS with T category, C‐index improved from 0.6205 to 0.6220 (AIC: 657.02 to 656.91) (Figure 5A). For DMFS with N category, C‐index increased markedly from 0.5909 to 0.6759 (AIC: 970.42–943.85) (Figure 5B). For clinical stage, C‐index for DFS improved from 0.5984 to 0.6367 (AIC: 1482.49–1465.17) (Figure 5C), and for OS from 0.5889 to 0.6411 (AIC: 618.75–609.09) (Figure 5D). These findings confirm that the 9th edition offers superior discriminative ability and model fit.

Discussion

The TNM staging system for NPC is crucial not only for guiding clinical treatment decisions and determining patient prognosis but also for evaluating treatment efficacy. Therefore, establishing an accurate and effective staging system is essential. Since the first version of the NPC TNM staging system was released by AJCC/UICC in 1977, it has undergone multiple revisions [10]. This study provides external validation of the 9th edition staging system. Our findings revealed that TNM‐9 improved N‐category and overall‐stage discrimination for the clinically relevant primary endpoint of OS and for N‐specific DMFS. By contrast, the T‐category revision reclassified 17 patients but yielded only marginal changes in LRFS and OS performance, highlighting that not every anatomical clarification necessarily produces a large prognostic gain in a modest cohort.

Previous studies have identified certain limitations of the 8th edition of the NPC TNM staging system. Pan et al. [4] and Tang et al. [5] reported that survival curves for different N categories in the 8th edition showed inadequate discrimination, suggesting the need for improvement, which aligns with our findings. The 9th edition introduced important revisions to the N classification: while retaining the original criteria of lymph node size (> 6 cm) and location (below the caudal border of the cricoid cartilage), it added “advanced radiologic extranodal extension involving adjacent muscles, skin, and/or neurovascular bundles” as a criterion for N3 classification. The clinical significance of this adjustment lies in the identification of patients with high‐risk locally advanced disease. Previous studies have shown that extranodal extension is an independent adverse prognostic factor for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [11]. Jiang et al. [2] demonstrated that MRI‐detected extranodal extension was significantly associated with distant metastasis‐free survival and OS in patients with NPC. Additionally, King et al. [12] confirmed that patients with extranodal extensions involving adjacent structures had a significantly worse prognosis than those with intact capsules. In our cohort, 31 patients were upstaged to N3, and the 3‐year DMFS of TNM‐9 N3 was 69.97%, lower than the 79.49% for TNM‐8 N3. The revised N classification also increased the DMFS C‐index from 0.591 to 0.676. Because imaging interpretation can vary, reproducibility is important; the observed kappa of 0.78 supports substantial agreement, although structured imaging criteria, reader training, and external multi‐institutional calibration remain necessary.

Another significant change in the 9th edition staging system was the reclassification of the overall stages. The 8th edition divided nonmetastatic NPC into stages I–IVA, whereas the 9th edition reclassified them as stages IA, IB, II, and III, reflecting a more rational risk stratification approach. Specifically, the 9th edition combined the stages I (T1N0M0) and II (T1N1M0, T2N0‐1M0) of the 8th edition into a new Stage I (IA: T1‐2N0M0; IB: T1‐2N1M0), downgraded Stage III (T1‐2N2M0, T3N0‐2M0) to Stage II, downgraded Stage IVA (T4 or N3, M0) to Stage III, and reserved Stage IV exclusively for patients with distant metastasis (M1). The theoretical basis for this adjustment is that, with the widespread application of IMRT and the optimization of comprehensive treatment regimens, the prognosis of patients with locally advanced NPC has significantly improved [13, 14]. Moreover, the survival outcomes of patients with original Stage IVA are now markedly better than those of patients with distant metastasis, making it no longer appropriate to group these populations within the same stage.

In our study, the 9th edition clinical stage showed statistically significant differences between stages in predicting 3‐year OS, whereas the 8th edition showed no statistically significant differences between stages. This finding suggests that the 9th edition staging system can better discriminate between patient groups with different prognostic risks. Notably, only 8 patients (1.7%) were classified as Stage I of the 8th edition, compared to 48 patients (10.2%) in Stage II, and this imbalance in sample size may have affected the statistical power of the 8th edition staging. After applying the 9th edition staging, 16 patients (3.4%) had Stage IA disease and 38 patients (8.1%) had Stage IB disease, resulting in a more balanced distribution of patients with early‐stage disease and improving the clinical utility of the staging system.

TNM‐8 has also been criticized for limited prognostic separation between T2 and T3 disease and for imbalanced T‐category distributions. TNM‐9 clarifies unequivocal cranial nerve and inferior orbital fissure involvement, leading to reclassification of 17 patients from T3 to T4 in our cohort. Nevertheless, LRFS and OS performance changed little, suggesting that T3 disease remains biologically and anatomically heterogeneous. Patients with limited, early skull‐base invasion may have a more favorable prognosis than those with extensive skull‐base or intracranial extension. In future practice, this subgroup could be identified using standardized MRI parameters, including precise measurements of skull‐base invasion depth, the number of involved anatomical subsites, and the absence of high‐risk features such as intracranial extension, inferior orbital fissure involvement, or extensive soft tissue infiltration.

The subdivision of M1 into M1a (≤ 3 metastatic lesions) and M1b (> 3 metastatic lesions) in the 9th edition also has important clinical implications. In recent years, the concept of “oligometastasis” has gained widespread attention in oncology [15]. You et al. [16] showed that patients with NPC and oligometastases can achieve long‐term survival after local treatment. Additionally, Chen et al. [17] confirmed that patients with oligometastases have significantly better prognoses than those with extensive metastases and can benefit from radical local treatment. Because all source cohorts for this study were assembled for non‐metastatic MRI staging validation, de novo M1 patients were not available and should not be added retrospectively without comparable imaging review. Future M1 revisions should be tested in prospective multicenter cohorts with standardized whole‐body staging and should evaluate lesion number together with involved organ/site, number of organs, total metastatic tumor volume, plasma EBV DNA, and response to initial systemic therapy.

The performance analyses clarify where TNM‐9 adds value. The largest gains were observed for N‐category prediction of DMFS and OS and for overall‐stage prediction of DFS and OS. These findings are consistent with those of a recent validation study by Pan et al. [8], which confirmed the superiority of the 9th edition staging system in a large Asian population. The improvements in the 9th edition of the staging system have important clinical implications. First, a more precise risk stratification helps guide individualized treatment strategy formulation. According to current clinical guidelines, early‐stage patients with NPC (9th edition Stage IA) may receive radiotherapy alone or with reduced chemotherapy, while locally advanced patients (9th edition Stages II–III) require concurrent chemoradiotherapy ± induction or adjuvant chemotherapy [18]. The 9th edition combines stages I and II of the 8th edition into a new Stage I (IA and IB), emphasizing the good prognosis and similar treatment strategies for these patients. Meanwhile, downgrading original Stage IVA (T4 or N3, M0) to Stage III avoids the excessive pessimistic expectations associated with the term “Stage IV” for patients and clinicians. Second, the inclusion of advanced extranodal extension as a new criterion for N3 suggests that these patients may require more aggressive systemic treatment strategies, including induction chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy [19, 20].

Notably, although the 9th edition staging system outperformed the 8th edition on multiple metrics, the difference in predictive ability between the two was not extremely significant. This finding suggests that the traditional TNM staging systems may approach the upper limit of their prognostic predictive capacity. With the rapid development of molecular biology and radiomics research, future staging systems may need to integrate non‐anatomical factors, such as EBV DNA load, molecular markers, and radiomic features [21, 22, 23]. The prognostic stratification model incorporating EBV DNA proposed by Chen et al. [24] has shown superior predictive value compared with TNM staging alone. Additionally, the MRI radiomics‐based prediction model developed by Kou et al. [25] provides a new approach for individualized prognostic assessment in NPC. The introduction of the term “unequivocal” in the 9th edition of T staging to describe bone invasion and cranial nerve involvement emphasizes the importance of imaging evidence, laying the foundation for future integration of radiomics parameters.

This study had certain limitations. First, as a retrospective study, selection bias affecting the baseline variables cannot be completely avoided, despite the relatively large sample size. Second, this study only included nonmetastatic patients who received radical treatment, excluding patients with distant metastasis (8th edition Stage IVB); therefore, we were unable to validate the prognostic differences between M1a and M1b in the 9th edition. Third, although the median follow‐up time of 48 months in this study demonstrated that the 9th edition staging system has a good discriminative ability for the prognosis of patients with NPC, long‐term survival data need to be accumulated.

In conclusion, compared with the 8th edition, the 9th edition staging system demonstrated superior prognostic stratification ability and predictive accuracy in both the N category and clinical stage. The 9th edition better reflects the prognostic characteristics of patients with contemporary NPC by combining early stages, downgrading locally advanced stages, adding advanced extranodal extension criteria, and subdividing distant metastatic stages. Future external validation in multicenter prospective cohorts incorporating biological markers such as EBV DNA is recommended.
