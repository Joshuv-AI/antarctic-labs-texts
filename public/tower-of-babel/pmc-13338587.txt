Beyond plagiarism: emerging challenges in research integrity and editorial responsibility
Authors: Yong-Gun Kim, Shin-Young Park, Jae-Kook Cha, Dae-Young Kang, Hwan Tae Ahn, Jun-Beom Park
Published: 2026

Confidence in the scientific literature rests on the expectation that research findings are reported accurately, assessed fairly, and communicated transparently. Researchers, clinicians, journals, and ultimately patients depend on credible published evidence. Maintaining research integrity is therefore essential to scientific progress. In recent years, however, publication ethics concerns have become more varied and now extend well beyond plagiarism. Textual plagiarism has traditionally received substantial attention because it is visible and can be screened with similarity-detection software. Routine screening has improved journals’ ability to identify overlapping text. However, editorial experience indicates that integrity concerns encountered during manuscript handling often involve more than text similarity.

Among these concerns, simultaneous submission remains a persistent problem. Submitting the same or closely related manuscripts to multiple journals duplicates peer-review work and imposes unnecessary demands on editors and reviewers. More importantly, it can lead to duplicate publication and undermine confidence in editorial decision-making. Redundant publication raises similar concerns. Republishing substantially identical findings can distort the literature, inflate authors’ publication records, and bias evidence synthesis. Because clinical recommendations in periodontology and implant dentistry increasingly depend on systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the accuracy of the published record is particularly important. Authorship-related questions have also become more prominent. Contemporary dental research often involves collaboration among clinicians, basic scientists, statisticians, and industry partners. Although multidisciplinary work can strengthen scientific quality, it may also create uncertainty about individual responsibilities. Gift authorship, ghost authorship, and limited transparency about author contributions therefore remain important concerns.

Authorship should signify more than academic recognition. Authors are expected to contribute substantially to the work, approve the final version of the manuscript, and take responsibility for the reliability of the reported findings. They are also responsible for ensuring originality, obtaining appropriate ethical approval, disclosing conflicts of interest transparently, and avoiding duplicate or concurrent submissions. Accountability therefore remains an integral component of responsible authorship.

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) raises another set of editorial considerations. AI-assisted technologies can support language refinement and improve accessibility for investigators for whom English is not a first language. At the same time, these tools may produce inaccurate information or nonexistent references. Because AI systems cannot accept responsibility for scientific content, disclosure and careful human review are essential.

Publication-integrity concerns also include image manipulation, citation distortion, and interference with the peer-review process. These practices are often difficult to detect through conventional text-similarity analysis because they involve data presentation, citation practices, or editorial processes rather than text duplication alone. For this reason, editorial offices are increasingly adopting more comprehensive approaches to integrity assessment. Ethical problems, however, do not always reflect intentional wrongdoing. Inadequate familiarity with editorial policies, differences in research environments, and insufficient training in the responsible conduct of research may all contribute to inappropriate practices. Accordingly, education and mentorship should be treated as essential components of research integrity, alongside detection and corrective measures.

The role of journal editors has also expanded. In addition to assessing scientific merit, editors are expected to oversee fair editorial procedures, address ethical concerns, communicate with authors and institutions, and implement corrections or retractions when warranted. Protecting the accuracy and reliability of the scientific record has become a core responsibility of editorial leadership. Sustaining integrity in scientific communication requires contributions from all stakeholders.

Authors must report their findings honestly and transparently, reviewers should provide objective and confidential assessments, institutions should promote responsible research practices, and journals must maintain clear and consistent editorial policies. Ethical publication should be regarded not as an administrative obligation, but as an integral aspect of scientific professionalism.

As scientific communication evolves, the scope of publication ethics will continue to expand. Although plagiarism remains an important issue, challenges such as duplicate submission, authorship accountability, responsible use of AI, and manipulation of the publication process now require sustained attention. Preserving confidence in the scientific literature requires a shared commitment among authors, reviewers, editors, institutions, and publishers.

The Journal of Periodontal & Implant Science remains committed to promoting ethical publishing through transparent editorial practices, careful verification of author contributions, and adherence to internationally recognized standards. Safeguarding publication integrity is a collective responsibility and is essential to ensuring that scientific progress remains credible and trustworthy.
