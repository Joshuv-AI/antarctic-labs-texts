Conventional osteosarcoma of the ilium masquerading as a brown tumor in primary hyperparathyroidism: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Anqi Tang, Mengyu Wan, Zhijian Wu, Yajing Zhang, Xiaoli Lan, Xun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The coexistence of primary osteosarcoma in the setting of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is exceedingly rare, and overlapping features between reactive brown tumors and malignancies pose a severe risk of misdiagnosis and diagnostic omission. We report the case of a 65-year-old female who presented with a left hip lesion. Although her clinical manifestations, imaging features, and initial pathology all favored a PHPT-induced brown tumor, a second pathology examination confirmed the coexistence of osteosarcoma within the pelvic lesion. In patients with PHPT, reactive giant cell hyperplasia and cystic degeneration can easily mask the malignant components of osteosarcoma. When encountering “red flags”, such as a solitary, extensive bone destruction that is disproportionate to parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels, clinicians must remain vigilant for a concurrent malignant bone tumor and promptly perform expanded sampling to prevent missed diagnoses. Multimodal imaging evaluations and, when necessary, repeated or expanded biopsies are also critical to avoiding misdiagnosis.

Introduction

The coexistence of primary osteosarcoma in the setting of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is exceedingly rare, and overlapping features between reactive brown tumors and malignancies pose a severe risk of misdiagnosis and diagnostic omission.

Case description

A 65-year-old female presented with a 2-week history of left hip pain. A CT scan from the referring hospital revealed osteolytic destruction in the left iliac-acetabular region. A subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI at our institution demonstrated a cystic and solid mass measuring approximately 5.6 × 3.0 × 4.2 cm in the left ilium and acetabulum (Figures 1A–C). The lesion exhibited internal septations and significant peripheral enhancement, which initially suggested a tumor or a tumor-like lesion. Given the patient’s history of brain tumor surgery (angioleiomyoma), an 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed for a systemic evaluation (Figures 1D–F). The PET/CT revealed significantly increased FDG uptake in the solid component of the left hip lesion (SUVmax 9.6), accompanied by expansile thinning of the bone cortex and residual bony ridges. Additionally, a mildly hypermetabolic nodule (0.7 × 0.6 cm, SUVmax 1.9) was incidentally detected at the posterior-inferior aspect of the right thyroid lobe (Figures 2A–C). No explicit signs of malignancy were observed elsewhere in the body, and the remaining skeleton showed no abnormal metabolism.

Given the patient’s concurrent hypercalcemia (2.90 mmol/L; reference range: 2.03–2.54) and hypophosphatemia (0.89 mmol/L; reference range: 0.96–1.62), the hypermetabolic cervical nodule was highly suspected to be a hyperfunctioning parathyroid adenoma. Further testing revealed an elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) level (326.4 pg/ml; reference range: 15.0–68.3). A 99mTc-MIBI scan localized an abnormal focus of radiotracer accumulation in the right parathyroid gland (Figures 2D–F). Consequently, the diagnosis of PHPT was established.

Under these circumstances, the primary diagnosis for the left iliac lesion was a brown tumor secondary to osteoclast overactivation induced by PHPT. The patient subsequently underwent an open curettage and biopsy of the pelvic lesion. Intraoperatively, a pale yellow, clear fluid exudate was observed from the cavity, along with a substantial amount of neoplastic soft tissue. Histopathologically, the specimen showed a giant cell-rich fibro-osseous lesion, which favored the diagnosis of a brown tumor (Figure 3A). However, suspicious pathological mitotic figures were noted microscopically, precluding the definitive exclusion of a malignancy. Due to the equivocal pathological diagnosis and the patient’s persistent postoperative bone pain, a secondary procedure was performed 15 days later for further debridement and expanded tissue sampling. Microscopic examination of the expanded biopsy specimens demonstrated highly atypical tumor cells and neoplastic osteogenesis, accompanied by hemorrhage and cystic changes (Figures 3B, C). A definitive pathological diagnosis of conventional osteosarcoma (malignant fibrous histiocytoma-like osteosarcoma) was ultimately established, which was concordant with a pathology consultation from another medical center. The patient subsequently underwent a parathyroidectomy, and pathological biopsy (Figure 3D) confirmed that the highly avid parathyroid focus incidentally found on PET/CT was indeed a parathyroid adenoma. Postoperatively, the patient’s PTH, serum calcium, and phosphorus levels gradually normalized after the parathyroidectomy, and her hip pain was partially resolved following the second procedure. Comprehensive staging evaluation, including the initial 18F-FDG PET/CT, confirmed the absence of distant metastases to the lungs or other skeletal sites. Given that the patient’s overall general condition precluded tolerance to aggressive systemic chemotherapy or further radical pelvic surgery, and in accordance with the preferences of the patient and her family, local radiotherapy was implemented. A follow-up pelvic MRI performed two months postoperatively (prior to radiotherapy) revealed patchy enhancement within the surgical bed and surrounding soft-tissue edema (Figure 4). An initial intralesional curettage for conventional osteosarcoma is generally associated with an increased risk of local recurrence. Consequently, close and high-frequency radiological surveillance remains mandatory to monitor her long-term oncological outcome.

Discussion

PHPT can promote bone resorption through persistently elevated PTH levels, leading to osteoporosis and even the formation of brown tumors (1). The incidence of brown tumors in patients with PHPT is only approximately 3-4.5% (2), and these lesions frequently mimic giant cell tumors of bone or primary bone tumors both radiologically and histopathologically. In this case, the initial imaging manifestations and biopsy results were highly consistent with the characteristics of a brown tumor, creating a highly deceptive diagnostic pitfall. This indicates that the brown tumor-like reactive giant cell components induced in the context of PHPT can easily mask the core malignant elements of osteosarcoma, such as neoplastic osteogenesis (3). Furthermore, limited or localized biopsies have significant shortcomings when evaluating bone lesions characterized by prominent cystic changes, hemorrhage, and tissue heterogeneity. This is particularly critical in pelvic osteosarcomas, which are frequently massive and highly heterogeneous. In our case, the initial sampling captured the superficial reactive zones, where giant-cell-rich areas completely masked the true biological nature of the underlying malignancy.

From a radiological perspective, the lesion was highly deceptive as it lacked classical osteosarcoma “red flags,” such as a prominent periosteal reaction or obvious neoplastic osteogenesis. The predominantly osteolytic destruction, accompanied by a soft tissue mass and elevated metabolism, perfectly mimicked the expected features of a brown tumor, creating a severe diagnostic trap. Consequently, the true “red flags” prompting the repeat biopsy were not subtle malignant imaging signs, but rather the profound clinicoradiological discordance. Specifically, there was a discrepancy between the extent and severity of the bone lesion and the patient’s endocrine parameters. The patient’s PTH level (326.4 pg/ml) was only mildly to moderately elevated, and there were no other obvious metabolic bone lesions or ectopic calcifications; instead, the initial presentation was a solitary, prominent osteolytic lesion. According to previous literature, typical brown tumors are generally observed in patients with severe, long-standing disease and extremely high PTH levels (usually >1000 pg/ml) (4–9), though in rare instances, PTH can be around 500 pg/ml (10). This contradiction suggested, to some extent, that the osteolytic lesion was not solely driven by high PTH levels, necessitating the consideration of a coexisting primary bone tumor.

Whether a pathophysiological link exists between PHPT and osteosarcoma remains controversial. Classic toxicological animal studies have demonstrated that long-term exposure to high doses of PTH analogs increases the risk of osteosarcoma in rats (11, 12). In vitro studies also suggest that PTH may influence osteosarcoma cell proliferation. Because osteosarcoma cells overexpress parathyroid hormone receptor 1 on their surface, PTH can activate relevant signaling pathways to maintain the proliferative capacity, invasiveness, and dedifferentiated state of osteosarcoma cells (13, 14). However, several small-scale human cohort studies have yet to confirm a significant association between human PHPT and osteosarcoma (15, 16). Nevertheless, the hyperparathyroid state and the resulting active bone remodeling could theoretically create a favorable microenvironment for the development of malignant bone tumors. This hypothesis requires further validation through future large-sample studies.

Notably, the FDG metabolic level cannot serve as a reliable criterion for distinguishing between a brown tumor and a primary malignant bone tumor. Previous literature reports that due to the abundance of giant cells and active bone metabolism, the SUVmax of brown tumors is generally elevated and highly variable, typically ranging from 6.3 to 18 (4, 5, 17–19), and can even reach 26.4 (20). The SUVmax in this case was 9.6, which falls entirely within the plausible metabolic range of a brown tumor. In this scenario, the core value of PET/CT lay in systemic evaluation and the incidental discovery of the suspicious parathyroid lesion, which indicated the possibility of PHPT. Concurrently, it was instrumental in evaluating the systemic impact of PHPT on both skeletal and extraskeletal tissues. When confronted with a clinical presentation where the extent and severity of a lesion are inconsistent with typical disease progression patterns and laboratory results, a comprehensive analysis must be conducted to consider the possibility of comorbidities.

Additionally, other pelvic osteolytic lesions warrant careful differentiation. Giant cell tumor of bone and aneurysmal bone cyst can mimic the giant-cell-rich or multi-cystic features of this case, but they lack severe cellular anaplasia and malignant osteogenesis. Bone metastases often present as aggressive pelvic destruction in older adults, but typically feature a known primary tumor history and lack parathyroid abnormalities. Lastly, bone tuberculosis can cause extensive bone destruction and cystic changes, but is generally characterized by cold abscesses, systemic infectious symptoms, without neoplastic osteoid matrix.

Conclusion

The coexistence of PHPT and osteosarcoma is rarely reported in clinical practice. Given that the management strategies for these two conditions are fundamentally different, a misdiagnosis, missed diagnosis, or delayed diagnosis of this coexistence can severely misguide treatment decisions, potentially leading to catastrophic clinical consequences. This case highlights that in patients with PHPT, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion, even when initial imaging and biopsies align with a brown tumor. In such clinical scenarios, multimodal imaging facilitates a comprehensive evaluation of the disease state, wherein enhanced CT/MRI assesses local lesions, 18F-FDG PET/CT screens for distant metastases, and 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy localizes the parathyroid adenoma. When a large, solitary osteolytic lesion presents alongside only a mild-to-moderate elevation in PTH, and the disease extent mismatches the typical progression of PHPT, specialized clinical evaluations must be integrated to rule out a concurrent primary bone tumor, thereby preventing the missed diagnosis of a severe malignancy. When encountering such clinical discordance, it is strongly recommended to initiate early multidisciplinary team reviews and rigorous radiological follow-ups. Furthermore, performing image-guided, multi-site core needle biopsies is advised to prevent the catastrophic diagnostic omission of hidden malignancies and secure optimal oncological outcomes.
