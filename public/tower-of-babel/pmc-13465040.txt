The effect of educational intervention based on the theory of planned behavior on promoting dairy consumption among high school students in Rudbar-e-Jonoob, Iran
Authors: Maryam Amiresmaili, Mohammad Mehdi Fadakar Davarani, Seyed Vahid Ahmadi Tabatabaei, Mohabbat Mohseni, Tania Dehesh, Fatemeh Rezabeigi Davarani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConsumption of dairy products is not sufficient in adolescents which may lead to some chronic diseases in adulthood. The present study aimed to determine the effects of an educational intervention on dairy consumption by adolscents based on the theory of planned behavior (TPB).MethodsThis quasi-experimental study was performed on 179 female students of high schools in 2022. Participants were randomly assigned into two groups of intervention and control. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire consisted of 4 parts and 52 questions. These parts including demographic characteristics, knowledge, questions related to the TPB, and dairy consumption. Intervention was applied to the intervention group based on the TPB for one month. Seventeen types of interventions (10 educational and 7 health-promotion oriented).Two months after the termination of the intervention, data were recollected from the groups and analyzed using descriptive statistics, paired and independent t-tests, Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney U, Chi-square, Pearson correlation coefficient, and Linear regression by SPSS version 21.ResultsThe mean age of the intervention and control groups were 13.56 ± 0.97 and 14.31 ± 1.31 years, respectively. Twenty-four percent of the variance in predicting dairy consumption was attributed to knowledge and Constructs of the TPB. Perceived behavioral control (PBC) and behavioral intention were significant predictors of dairy consumption (p<0.0001).After intervention, all TPB constructs differed significantly between the intervention and control groups (p<0.05); however, within-group analysis indicated that only Attitude (p=0.003) and dairy consumption (p=0.04) improved significantly in the intervention group after intervention.ConclusionTheory-based educational interventions improved attitudes and increased dairy consumption in the intervention group. Therefore, it is suggested that health educators use interventions based on this theory to increase dairy consumption.

Introduction

Dairy products play an important role in preventing diseases such as osteoporosis, coronary artery diseases (CAD), stroke, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, prostate and colorectal cancers, and tooth decay [1]. Dairy consumption has a great effect on bone mineral density (BMD) due to the presence of minerals such as calcium and magnesium, which involve in the formation of solid bone mass [2].

According to the studies, half of the adult peak bone mass (PBM) occurs in adolescence (about 40 to 60%). The relationship of calcium intake from dairy consumption with bone mineral content (BMC) and BMD has been studied and it was revealed that dairy consumption increases the skeletal mass of younger people [3].

The efficiency of calcium absorption increases during puberty. Maximum balance of the amount of calcium in this period is achieved by calcium intake between 1200 and 1500 mg (3 servings of dairy products per day) [4].

Adolescence is one of the most important periods of human life, because habits, especially nutritional habits, are formed in this period and evolve gradually [5]. Studies have shown that nutritional requirements of adolescents are greatly increased during this period [6–8].

Studies have shown that dairy consumption in the world [2, 3, 9, 10] and also in Iran [11, 12], is lower than the recommended values ​​in this age group.

According to various studies, the prevalence of osteoporosis in women is higher than of men [10]; this is due to reasons such as false beliefs about dairy consumption by women [3, 13], lower dairy consumption in females than males [14, 15] and also due to the subsequent physiological experiences of women such as pregnancy and breastfeeding. Girls up to 18 years old get 92% of their bone mass. Therefore, adolescence is a critical period to prevent osteoporosis. Adolescent mothers who have not reached the PBM, are more likely to experience more complications in their skeletal system during pregnancy [16, 17].

Since access to a large number of adolescents through schools is possible, school setting can be used to carry out any interventions to promote health behaviors. In formal learning environments, teachers and school staff can transfer knowledge, create a positive attitude, change behavior, and ultimately change the health of the community. In addition, schoolchildren can transfer health-related messages from school to home [18, 19].

Less dairy consumption among adolescents shows the need for designing some interventions to promote dairy consumption. The use of theories can be effective in designing such interventions [20, 21].

Educational programs that are systematically designed, theory-based, age-appropriate, and skill-oriented play a crucial role in enhancing nutritional awareness and fostering positive attitudes. They also facilitate the adoption of healthy behaviors, including adequate dairy consumption [22]. Interventions that actively engage students, correct misconceptions, and strengthen self-efficacy are more likely to result in sustainable behavioral changes. Therefore, educational programs that follow a structured framework and are grounded in behavioral theories can more effectively promote positive attitudes, increase perceived behavioral control, and ultimately improve healthy dietary behaviors among students [23]. Consequently, integrating structured educational interventions into school-based programs can serve as an effective strategy to improve dairy consumption among adolescents and should be considered by policymakers and health program planners [24].

To guide the design of such interventions, behavioral theories can provide a structured framework. One of these theories is the TPB, which provides a framework for identifying effective factors and interventions to improve the behavior. This theory was introduced by Fishbein and Ajzen in 1987. In this theory, intention is predictor of the intended behavior, and also the long-range product of the constructs of attitude, subjective norms, and PBC [25].

Attitude is the overall evaluation of a behaviour by the individual, and it considers the outcomes of performing the behavior. Subjective norms are a person’s belifes about the extent to which significant individuals think the person should perform or not perform the behavior. PBC is defined as individuals’ perception of the ease or difficulty in performing the behavior that reflects past experiences about behavior [25, 26].

Many studies have supported prediction of health-related behaviors using TPB [20, 27]. The efficacy of related interventions in promoting dairy consumption using this theory has been approved in various studies. Interventions that are mostly educational are effective in increasing the score of the model constructs [12, 27].

A review of the literature indicates that previous studies on dairy consumption have been subject to several limitations, including the use of small and convenience samples, a focus on specific educational levels, and a unidimensional assessment largely limited to milk consumption [12, 20]. In response to these limitations, the present study was conducted to examine consumption patterns of all dairy products through the simultaneous application of educational and non-educational interventions grounded in the TPB.

This study was carried out in 2022 among first-year female high school students in Rudbar-e Jonoob County with the aim of reducing the knowledge–behavior gap and strengthening perceived behavioral control. It is expected that the findings of this study will provide a more practical and generalizable basis for the design of effective health-promoting interventions.

Materilas and methods

Study design and participants

This quasi-experimental study was performed on 179 female students of high school who were randomly assigned into two groups of intervention (n = 89), and control (n = 90) in Rudbar-e-Jonoob county, in the southeast of Iran, in 2022. Necessary permits were obtained from Kerman University of Medical Sciences to submit to the Department of Education of Rudbar-e-Jonoob county. After coordination with the local education authorities, four schools (two boarding and two day schools) were first selected using simple random sampling. Two schools were then assigned to the intervention group and two to the control group (one boarding and one-day school in each group).

Within each school, participants were selected using stratified proportional sampling to ensure representative selection from each grade based on the student population in each school and class. In the intervention group, the boarding school contributed 18 students from grade 1, 17 from grade 2, and 18 from grade 3, while the day school contributed 11 students from grade 1, 13 from grade 2, and 12 from grade 3. In the control group, the boarding school contributed 27 students from grade 1, 21 from grade 2, and 22 from grade 3, and the day school contributed 8 students from grade 1, 7 from grade 2, and 5 from grade 3.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a researcher-made questionnaire [28], consisted of 4 parts and 52 questions. The first part includes demographic characteristics (age, place of residence, Living status with parents, Parents’ occupation, parents’ education level, housing status, and family income).

Content validity was assessed qualitatively through an expert panel review. Due to the exploratory and applied nature of the questionnaire development, quantitative indices such as the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were not formally calculated. Instead, expert judgments were used to refine item relevance, clarity, and wording prior to finalization of the instrument.

The second part is related to knowledge (9 items), the thired part is based on the constructs of TPB including attitude (10 items), subjective norms (10 items), PBC (5 items), and behavioral intention (4 items), and the last part is related to dairy consumption (14 items) (daily and weekly).

Knowledge was scored based on a 3-point scale (No/I do not know = 0, and yes = 1). The scores of TPB constructs were graded based on a 5-point Likert scale (very low = 1, low = 2, moderate = 3, high = 4, and very high = 5). The scores of attitude ranged from 10 to 50; subjective norms, 10 to 50; PBC, 5 to 25; and behavioral intention, 4 to 20. Reverse scoring was used for negative questions. Dairy consumption was scored based on a 4-point Likert scale (not at all = 0, one serving = 1, two servings = 2, and three servings and more = 3).

The initial questionnaire was developed by studying books, theses, articles, scientific publications, existing questionnaire, and based on a thorough consultation with professors and experts.

The face validity was evaluated with qualitative approaches. To ensure face validity, the questionnaire was given to 10 students who provided feedback on the clarity, transparency, appearance, and understandable of the items. based on the suggestions of the students difficult and incomprehensible items were modified.

Content validity of the questionnaire was determined through valid scientific resources and expert panel’s model (8 experts in health education and promotion, epidemiology, and nutrition). Comments were received regarding grammar, appropriate words, placement of items in the appropriate place, and clarity. Then their corrective comments were applied in the questionnaire, then, its content validity was confirmed.

After applying the experts opinion, the questionnaire was also given to 10 students and their opinions on the content of the questionnaire were considered in the final version of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire’s reliability was assessed by internal consistency and stability. Internal consistency calculated using Cronbach’s alpha to determine the absolute agreement between individual. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.86 for the whole questionnaire, 0.71 for knowledge, 0.76 for attitude, 0.88 for subjective norms, 0.77 for perceived behavioral control, 0.79 for behavioral intention, and 0.83 for behavior.

Besides, stability was evaluated by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For this purpose, the test-retest method was used with two weeks’ interval involving 29 students. ICC of 0.75 was obtained.

Procedure

To observe ethical principles, prior to the implementation of the interventions, participants were provided with adequate information about the study objectives, and written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians due to the participants being under 16 years of age. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information and informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

After completing the questionnaire, all questions were assessed by the researcher. Then, data analysis was performed, and various health education and promotion interventions based on the TPB were designed and conducted in the intervention group for one month in their free time. The intervention program was organized with the coordination of the two schools (intervention group) managers and teachers in each class A total of 17 intervention components were implemented for each class. These included lectures (five sessions of 30 min each, with 11–18 participants), question-and-answer sessions (five sessions of 20 min each, with 11–18 participants), and group discussions (12 sessions of 45 min each, with 6–8 participants).

Role-playing activities were conducted in the form of theatrical performances, including two performances of approximately 15 min in the boarding school with an average of 25 participants, and one 15-minute performance in the day school with 36 participants. Educational video clips were also presented in five sessions of 20 min each, with 11–18 participants per session.

Training sessions were held for teachers (two sessions per school, each lasting 25 min, with 12 participants in the boarding school and 8 participants in the day school). In addition, educational sessions for parents were conducted in five sessions of 30 min each, with 7 to 25 participants per session. A separate educational session was also held for education authorities (one session lasting approximately one hour with seven participants).

Furthermore, orientation and educational sessions were organized for school cafeteria owners (two sessions of 20 min each). Educational text messages were sent via a messaging system (three educational messages per day for one week).

Peer education was also implemented by activating peer groups during students’ free time. In this process, one to three students from each intervention class were trained as peer educators, resulting in a total of 39 trained students through a 10-minute instructional session.

Two months after the interventions, the questionnaire was recompleted by both groups. To observe ethical principles, after the interventions in the intervention group, educational pamphlets regarding the importance of dairy consumption were also distributed in the control group. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using tests Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney, paired t-test, independent t-test, Chi-square, Pearson correlation coefficient, Linear regression, and descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, standard deviation and mean) by SPSS version 21. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. thereby, parametric or nonparametric statistical tests were also identified. Statistical analyses were conducted in two ways of in-group and inter-group. Statistical significant level was considered at p = 0.05.

Results

In both groups, the level of education of fathers (intervention group: 59.5%, control group: 86.6%) and mothers (intervention group: 70.7%, control group: 94.4%) was mostly at elementary. In terms of economic status, the majority of participants in both groups were Low income (intervention group: 67.8%, and control group: 75.3%).

The results of Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests showed that the difference in place of residence, living status with parents, and Parents’ occupation status between intervention and control groups was not significant (p > 0.05). Table 2 shows the frequency distribution of the variables.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of interventions based on the TPB in increasing dairy consumption among the female students of high school in the southeast of Iran.

Knowledge

In this study, knowledge had the least effect on dairy consumption, and it was not a significant predictor of dairy consumption.

Rouhani et al., study showed that the average score of knowledge in female High-School Students was at a good level. But, there was no significant correlation between knowledge and dairy consumption behavior [29].

Although it is now clear that knowledge may not be able to create, modify, or change behavior without considering other effective factors, its effective and facilitating role in changing behavior cannot be denied.

Based on the results of the present study, mean score of knowledge significantly increased in the intervention group after the interventions.

The study by Chughtai et al. showed that nutrition education interventions were effective in improving the knowledge of students regarding milk consumption patterns [30]. Other similar studies reported the increase of average of knowledge of school students about nutrition behaviors after educational intervention [31, 32]. The results of the study by Keikha et al., showed a significant difference between health literacy and nutritional performance of students in the intervention and control groups after the intervention [33].

Attitude

In the current study, attitude was not a significant predictor of dairy consumption.

In the study by Rezabeigi et al., attitude was not a significant predictor of intention and nutrition behavior in the study group [34]. Contrary to the results of this study, in the study by Grønhøj et al. attitudes had a positive impact on behavioural intention to eat healthily amongst Danish adolescents [35]. Given that multiple factors may influence Improving eating behavior, changing attitudes alone is unlikely to have an impact on this behavior.

In this study, among the constructs of the TPB, only the attitude increased significantly after the educational intervention.

In the study by Khani Jeihooni et al. the mean area of attitude after the intervention showed a more significant increase compared to other areas [36]. The studies by Gheysvandi et al., [12], Gholipour et al., [27] and Chughtai et al., [30] on milk and dairy consumption are consistent with the results of this study. Other studies also showed the effect of educational intervention on attitude towards nutrition-related behaviors among school students [19, 37].

These findings indicate the effect of TPB-based interventions on the promotion of attitudes in the most studies. When people have a positive attitude towards the outcomes of a behavior (dairy consumption), they are encouraged to perform that behavior.

Subjective norms

In this study, subjective norms was not a significant predictor of dairy consumption.

In a study in Kerman, subjective norms were not a significant predictor of nutritional intention and behavior in health volunteers [34]. According to the results of a review article, subjective norms have been identified as the weakest predictor of intention and behavior [38].

In this study, the mean scores of subjective norms increased in the intervention group after the educational intervention. however, this increase was not significant.

Similar to this study, the study of Tabatabaei et al., on the effect of TPB-based educational intervention on the physical activity of the Health Center staff showed no significant difference in the subjective norms [39]. Another study by Mohammadimanesh et al., also reported no difference in the students’ subjective norms of breakfast consumption after the intervention [19]. Contrary to the results of the current research, in studies conducted by Gheysvandi et al., and Gholipour-Baboli et al., educational programs based on the TPB led to a significant increase in the scores of subjective norm among girl students [12, 27].

In the study by Lacroix et al. which aimed to examine the salient beliefs underlying fluid milk and cheese consumption among adults. Family and friends, health professionals and advisors, and communications domain were cited as major influences affecting consumption milk and cheese [40].

Since subjective norms reflect the social support of significant others such as parents, teachers, and peers, the lack of improvement in this construct following the intervention may be partly attributed to insufficient engagement of these groups. For instance, parental dissatisfaction with the educational sessions, limited cooperation of teachers with educational authorities in delivering the intervention content, and inadequate involvement of peer groups in implementing the planned activities may have reduced the effectiveness of the intervention. In addition, contextual barriers in the study area, including low household income and limited access to dairy products, likely constrained students’ ability to act upon social encouragement.

Perceived behavioral control

In the present study, PBC was the strongest predictor for dairy consumption.

In the study by Rezabeigi et al., PBC was a strong predictor for intention and nutritional behavior in health volunteers [34]. In the study by Mazloomy Mahmoodabad PBC was the most important predictor variable to lose Weight in Adolescents with Overweight and Obesity [41]. in the study by Grønhøj et al. PBC was the most important factor in predicting behavioral intention to eat healthily amongst Danish adolescents [35].

In this study, the mean scores of PBC increased in the intervention group after the educational intervention. however, this increase was not significant.

These results are not consistent with several studies [12, 27, 36, 42]. These results indicate that the impact of education on PBC may vary in different societies.

PBC refers to a person’s belief about access to or lack of access to dairy products and barriers of dairy consumption.

In the study by Lacroix et al. major barriers to milk and cheese consumption included high price, reduced confidence in the product, health status, problems linked to supply, and habits and cultural values [40] .

In the study area, low household income and limited access to nutritious foods likely contributed to the lack of significant improvement in PBC following the intervention. Despite follow-up efforts and coordination with relevant authorities to increase access to milk in schools, distribution remained irregular or insufficient. Given the low socioeconomic status of most students, economic constraints acted as a major barrier to dairy consumption. These findings suggest that educational interventions alone are insufficient and should be combined with structural and economic support to achieve meaningful changes in PBC and related health behaviors.

In the study by Lacroix et al., price reduction, product improvement, supply increase and variation, favorable food/ drink combinations, and access were among the most commonly cited facilitators for milk and cheese consumption expressed by participants [40].

Behavioral intention

Based on the results of the present study, behavioral intention was a significant predictor of dairy consumption.

Dehdari et al., showed in their study, behavioral intention alone was predicted 30% of the variation in daily fruit consumption among girl students who are living in dormitories [43].

In this study, the mean scores of behavioral intention increased in the intervention group after the educational intervention. however, this increase was not significant.

Similarly, Kothe et al., also reported no difference in the behavioral intention of the participants towards fruits and vegetables consumption in the intervention group [44]. However, these results are not consistent with several studies [12, 27, 36].

Lack of significant difference in this construct is inconsistent with TPB assumptions. Intention has been introduced as the immediate determinant factor of behaviour Therefore, the stronger the intention to perform the behavior, the more likely the behavior to be performed [25].

Dairy consumption

In this study, there was a significant difference in the mean score of dairy consumption (behavior) in the intervention group after the interventions.

Along with the results of this study, other studies have also indicated that after the educational intervention, based on TPB, milk and dairy product consumption in female students of the intervention group improved compared with the control group [12, 27]. The findings of the present study indicated that despite the absence of significant changes in behavioral intention, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms, dairy consumption behavior improved significantly. The intervention led to significant improvements in knowledge and attitudes, which appear to have been sufficient to bring about behavioral [45, 46]. It is also noteworthy that the presence of a ceiling effect in the constructs of behavioral intention and PBC at baseline, such that the relatively high initial levels of these constructs limited the ability to detect statistically significant change, even though those levels were sufficient to support the occurrence of the behavior [47]. Although Behavioral Intention and PBC did not demonstrate statistically significant within-group improvement, both constructs remained significant predictors of dairy consumption.

In the present study, 24% of the variance in dairy consumption was explained by knowledge and the constructs of the TPB, indicating a moderate yet meaningful explanatory power of the model in the studied population. Among these constructs, PBC and behavioral intention emerged as significant predictors of dairy consumption.

The intervention successfully improved students’ knowledge and attitudes toward dairy consumption, which was reflected in positive changes in their dietary behavior. Educational strategies such as lectures, group discussions, role-playing activities, and school-based educational materials appeared to be effective in increasing awareness and fostering more favorable attitudes toward dairy intake.

Despite improvements in knowledge and attitudes, no significant change was observed in behavioral intention or perceived behavioral control. This finding suggests that increasing awareness alone may be insufficient to influence behavioral intention, particularly when individuals perceive limited control over their behavior. Within the framework of the TPB, PBC reflects both available resources and environmental constraints that may hinder individuals’ ability to translate motivation into action.

In the present study, contextual barriers such as low household income and limited access to dairy products in the study area played a substantial role in weakening perceived behavioral control. Despite educational efforts and follow-up with relevant authorities, structural challenges—such as irregular distribution or lack of access to milk—persisted. These findings highlight the necessity of integrating educational interventions with structural and economic support in order to achieve sustainable improvements in health-related behaviors, particularly in socioeconomically deprived settings.

Although our interventions improved attitudes and behavior, they could not fully overcome structural and economic barriers, as factors such as culture, economic development, and IT infrastructure critically shape the relationships proposed by the TPB. Consequently, behavioral intention and perceived control remained strongly influenced by the broader socio-economic context [48].

Rudbar-e-Jonoob city is one of the less developed and deprived areas located in the southeast of Kerman. The existence of multiple barriers in less developed communities including low income, low education level, lack of access can affect behavioral intention. In areas of economic and social deprivation, educational intervention alone is not enough to change behavior. Intention requires a combination of factors, including financial and physical access, cultural and nutritional beliefs, and family participation.

Limitations

This study was conducted on students from one of the deprived and less developed regions of Iran, suggesting caution in generalizing the results. In addition, future research should incorporate qualitative approaches to gain deeper insights into the barriers to dairy consumption in disadvantaged areas. Dairy consumption was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. To address this limitation, more objective dietary assessment methods—such as three-day food records, direct observation in school cafeterias, or analysis of purchase receipts—can be used to complement self-reported data and reduce potential reporting bias.

In general, smaller group sizes are associated with higher educational quality. However, due to scheduling constraints and the reluctance of many teachers to allocate additional class time—particularly because the educational sessions coincided regular school class hours related to the dairy curriculum—it was not feasible to conduct a larger number of smaller-group sessions. Consequently, most intervention sessions were conducted with relatively larger groups (up to 18 participants), although students’ free time was used whenever possible.

For future studies, stronger coordination and negotiation with school authorities and teachers are recommended to facilitate the allocation of more dedicated time for educational interventions, which may allow for smaller group sizes and improved instructional effectiveness.

In addition, it was not possible to systematically monitor the activities of peer groups in terms of knowledge transfer to their classmates. Future research could benefit from tracking peer educators’ activities and evaluating their role in disseminating educational content among peers, thereby making more effective use of the peer education approach.

The relatively short two-month follow-up period limits conclusions regarding the sustainability of behavior change. Accordingly, future studies are recommended to include a follow-up period of at least three months.

Conclusion

In the present study, 24% of the variance in dairy consumption was explained by knowledge and the constructs of the TPB. The mean scores of knowledge, attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, behavioral intention, and dairy consumption increased in the intervention group after the educational intervention. This increase in knowledge, attitude and dairy consumption was statistically significant. Although dairy consumption increased significantly, it did not reach the desired level.

Educational interventions alone are not enough to create or change a health-related behavior and significantly increase the factors influencing it.

It is necessary to identify other factors affecting behavior and to implement interventions to support individuals and remove barriers. Therefore, the findings of this study can be considered by decision-makers.

In the present study, PBC and behavioral intention were significant predictors of dairy consumption. therefore, it is recommended to plan interventions according to this factors to improve dairy consumption.
