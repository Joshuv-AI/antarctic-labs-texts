Central limit theorem for stochastic nonlinear wave equation with pure-jump Lévy white noise
Raluca M. Balan and Guangqu Zheng

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the random field solution to the stochastic nonlinear wave equation (SNLW) with constant initial conditions and multiplicative noise σ ⁡ ( u ) ​ L ˙ \sigma(u)\dot{L} , where the nonlinearity is encoded in a Lipschitz function σ : ℝ → ℝ \sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} and L ˙ \dot{L} denotes a pure-jump Lévy white noise on ℝ + × ℝ \mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R} with finite variance. Combining tools from Itô calculus and Malliavin calculus, we are able to establish the Malliavin differentiability of the solution with sharp moment bounds for the Malliavin derivatives. As an easy consequence, we obtain the spatial ergodicity of the solution to SNLW that leads to a law of large number result for the spatial integrals of the solution over [ − R , R ] [-R,R] as R → ∞ R\to\infty . One of the main results of this paper is the obtention of the corresponding Gaussian fluctuation with rate of convergence in Wasserstein distance. To achieve this goal, we adapt the discrete Malliavin-Stein bound from Peccati, Solé, Taqqu, and Utzet ( Ann. Probab. , 2010), and further combine it with the aforementioned moment bounds of Malliavin derivatives and Itô tools. Our work substantially improves our previous results ( Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. , 2024) on the linear equation that heavily relied on the explicit chaos expansion of the solution. In current work, we also establish a functional version, an almost sure version of the central limit theorems, and the (quantitative) asymptotic independence of spatial integrals from the solution. The asymptotic independence result is established based on an observation of L. Pimentel ( Ann. Probab. , 2022) and a further adaptation of Tudor’s generalization ( Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. , 2025) to the Poisson setting.

Introduction

The study of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs)
is an active research area,
which has been growing tremendously over the past four decades,
providing the rigorous framework for modeling random phenomena
that evolve in space and time.
Among various approaches in the literature,
the random field approach initiated by Walsh in his Saint-Flour notes
[
49
]

has been very influential, being embraced quickly by the scientific community.
1
1

1

A
random field solution is a collection
of real-valued random variables indexed by space-time.

Walsh’s random field approach was built on Itô’s theory of stochastic integration,
and was further pushed forward by Dalang in
[
17
]
. As a result,
the random field approach also bears the name of Dalang-Walsh’s approach.
Two families of equations that have been extensively studied using this approach are
the
stochastic heat equations
(SHEs) and the
stochastic wave equations

(SWEs).

Pragmatically speaking,
an SPDE often describes a complex system with two main inputs
(i.e., the initial condition and the noise).
We will not discuss about the initial condition here,
but we will make a few comments about the noise within the random field approach.
The classical example is the stochastic (nonlinear) wave equation posed on

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(t,x)=\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{W}(t,x),

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is deterministic, Lipschitz continuous,
and
\dot{W}
stands for the space-time Gaussian white noise on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
.
This is the running example in Walsh’s notes
[
49
]
.
The space-time Gaussian white noise
\dot{W}
is a generalization of the Brownian motion,
and one can develop the corresponding Itô integration theory to study
the above SWE (
1.1
). In fact, the above SWE admits a unique random field solution
only in spatial dimension one, and the same comment applies
to SHE (with
\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}
replaced by the heat operator

\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}
). When the spatial dimension is strictly bigger than one,
the above SPDEs fall into the realm of singular SPDEs and thus do not
admit a random-field solution.
In a seminal work
[
17
]
,
Dalang introduced the
Gaussian noises that are white in time and spatially homogeneous
such that the spectral measure of spatial correlation function satisfies
certain mild integrability assumption (known as the Dalang’s condition nowadays).
In Dalang’s generalized framework, one retains the martingale features
necessary for Itô’s theory due to the noises being white-in-time.
Another popular example of Gaussian noises
is the fractional Gaussian noise, which is a generalization of the fractional Brownian motion.
In this case, due to the absence of martingale structures (when the Hurst index in the
temporal direction
is different from
1/2
), one has to bring in other techniques to develop a solution theory.
For example, when
\sigma(u)=u
(i.e., we consider a linear equation),
one can apply the Malliavin calculus (particularly Wiener chaos expansion)
to establish a solution theory; see, e.g.,
[
24
,
7
]
.
For the general theory of Malliavin calculus, we refer interested readers
to Nualart’s monograph
[
33
]
;
see also
[
34
]
that presents
a pedagogical introduction to Malliavin calculus in both Gaussian and Poisson settings.

In this paper, we consider another family of driving noises,
the space-time Lévy noises that generalize the classical Lévy processes;
see
[
2
]
for the corresponding
Itô integration theory and its applications to SPDEs.
An in-depth analysis of various solution properties of such
SPDEs driven by finite-variance Lévy noise is possible using a discrete version of Malliavin calculus, with respect to the underlying Poisson random measure of the noise. More details about Malliavin calculus in the Poisson setting can be found in
[
27
,
29
,
30
,
40
]
.

Among many studies of various solution properties of SPDEs, a problem that comes persistently
in the recent literature is related to the asymptotic behaviour of the spatial integral of the solution, when the integration region tends to cover the whole space.
Such a problem was initiated by Huang, Nualart, and Viitasaari in their paper
[
25
]
,
which established the Gaussian fluctuation of the spatial averages of the SHE with
space-time Gaussian white noise. Since then, there has been a rapidly growing literature
on the limit theorems for these spatial statistics. This research theme also includes
SWEs and spatially homogeneous Gaussian noises and/or fractional Gaussian noises.
The following sample of relevant references will convince the reader of the order of magnitude of this growing area:
[
26
,
19
,
11
,
5
,
14
,
15
,
37
,
38
,
35
,
39
,
36
,
43
,
21
,
22
]
. In a nutshell, by combining Stein’s method for normal approximation with Malliavian calculus techniques, one can prove that with appropriate normalization, the spatial integral converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution, and can provide precise estimates for the rate of this central convergence, yielding a so-called
qualitative central limit theorem
(QCLT). From this, without a significant amount of effort, one can
also usually derive a
functional central limit theorem
(FCLT) for the spatial integral process.

In a recent article
[
9
]
, we initiated a similar program
for the stochastic wave equation with pure-jump Lévy white noise
\dot{L}
.
More precisely, we studied the
hyperbolic Anderson model
, in which the noise
takes the multiplicative form
u\dot{L}
. Due to the linearity in
u
, one has
an explicit Wiener chaos expansion of the solution, which is crucial
for us to obtain fine estimates of Malliavin derivatives up to second order.
Then, we combined them with Trauthwein’s improved second-order
Poincaré inequality
[
46
]
to establish the QCLTs.

The goal of the present article is to investigate the QCLT problem for the SWE with general nonlinearity. In addition, we study the asymptotic independence between the spatial integral and the solution, by developing a Poissonian analogue of the multivariate Malliavin-Stein method in
[
47
]
(see Section
4.2
).

1.1.
Stochastic nonlinear wave equation

Consider
the following stochastic nonlinear wave equation driven
by a pure-jump space-time Lévy white noise
\dot{L}

on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\hskip-11.38109pt(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u=\sigma(u)\dot{L}\\[5.0pt]
&\hskip-11.38109pt\big(u(0,\bullet),\partial_{t}u(0,\bullet)\big)=(1,0),\end{cases}

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a (deterministic) Lipschitz continuous function with
\sigma(1)\neq 0
,
and
\dot{L}(s,y)=L(ds,dy)
denotes the space-time (pure-jump) Lévy white
noise on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. To give the rigorous definition of the noise, we need to introduce several elements.

(a)

Let
\mathbb{R}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be equipped with
the distance
d(x,y)=|x^{-1}-y^{-1}|
, and
\nu
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}_{0}

satisfying the
Lévy-measure condition
2
2

2

Sometimes, we will treat
\nu

as a measure on
\mathbb{R}
with
\nu(\{0\})=0
.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\min\{1,|z|^{2}\}\nu(dz)<\infty.

In addition, throughout this paper, we will assume that
\nu
satisfies

(1.3)

\displaystyle m_{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{2}\nu(dz)\in(0,\infty).

This guarantees that the noise has finite variance, which is essential for our purposes.

(b)

Let
\mathfrak{H}=L^{2}(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z},\mathfrak{m})
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0},\,\,\,\mathfrak{m}={\rm Leb}\times\nu,

\mathcal{Z}
is the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\mathbf{Z}
,
and
{\rm Leb}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
.

(c)

Let
N
be a
Poisson random measure
on
(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z})

with intensity
\mathfrak{m}
, defined on a complete probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and
\widehat{N}=N-\mathfrak{m}
be the compensated version of
N
;
see Definition
2.1
below. Let
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
be the filtration induced by
N
, given as in (
2.1
).

(d)

For any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
with
{\rm Leb}(A)<\infty
, let

(1.4)

L(A)=\int_{A\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widehat{N}(ds,dy,dz),

where the integral is defined in the Itô sense. Then,

L(A)
is an infinitely divisible random variable with
characteristic function

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\lambda L(A)}\big]=\exp\left\{{\rm Leb}(A)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}(e^{i\lambda z}-1-i\lambda z)\nu(dz)\right\},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover,
L(A_{1}),\ldots,L(A_{n})
are independent for disjoins sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
.
See
[
2
]
for more on the Lévy noise.

The product between the unknown
\sigma(u)
and the Lévy noise
\dot{L}
is interpreted in the Itô sense. By analogy with Duhamel’s principle, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a process
u=\{u(t,x):t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a
(
mild
)
solution to (
1.2
) if
u
is
\mathbb{F}
-predictable
and satisfies the following integral equation

(1.5)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma\big(u(s,y)\big)L(ds,dy),

where

(1.6)

G_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|<t\}},\,\text{with $G_{t}=0$ for $t\leq 0$},

denotes the fundamental solution to the deterministic wave equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Here, the stochastic integral in (
1.5
) is understood in the Itô sense:

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}X(s,y)L(ds,dy)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}X(s,y)z\widehat{N}(ds,dy,dz),

whenever the right hand-side is well-defined; see
Section
2.1
for
more details on the stochastic integration.

Note that if
\sigma(1)=0
, then
u(t,x)\equiv 1
satisfies equation (
1.5
),
and is indeed the unique solution to (
1.2
). To exclude this trivial case,
we have assumed
\sigma(1)\neq 0
in this paper.

From
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
, we know that equation (
1.2
) has
a unique random field solution
u
that is
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-continuous
with

\sup\big\{\mathbb{E}[|u(t,x)|^{2}]:(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\}<\infty

for any finite
T>0
.
Moreover, if

(1.7)

m_{p}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty

for some
p\geq 2
, then

(1.8)

\displaystyle K_{T,p}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\big[|u(t,x)|^{p}\big]<\infty.

Because
\sigma
is Lipschitz, this implies that

(1.9)

\displaystyle K_{T,p,\sigma}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(t,x))|^{p}\big]\leq 2^{p-1}\big(|\sigma(0)|^{p}+\textup{Lip}^{p}(\sigma)K_{T,p}\big),

where
\textup{Lip}(\sigma)
denotes the Lipschitz constant of
\sigma
.
For
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\textup{Lip}(h):=\sup\Big\{\frac{|h(x)-h(y)|}{|x-y|}:\,\text{ $x\neq y$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\Big\}.

Since the Itô integral in (
1.5
)
has zero mean,
\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)]=1
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
.
For any
R>0
and
t>0
, we consider the (centered)
spatial integral
of the solution:

(1.11)

\displaystyle F_{R}(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}\big(u(t,x)-1\big)\,dx.

Let
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[F_{R}^{2}(t)\big]
be the variance of
F_{R}(t)
.
The main goal of this article is to show that for any fixed
t>0
,

F_{R}(t)/\sigma_{R}(t)
converges in distribution to
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

as
R\to\infty
,
where the rate of convergence can be described using the Wasserstein distance.
We recall that the
Wasserstein distance
between two random vectors
F
and
G

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

(1.12)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}(F,G)=\big|\mathbb{E}[h(F)]-\mathbb{E}[h(G)]\big|,

where the supremum runs over all Lipschitz functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1
and
\textup{Lip}(h)
defined as in (
1.10
).

The study of asymptotic distribution of the spatial integrals (
1.11
)
was first performed in the work
[
25
]
, which was partially motivated
by the mathematical intermittency.
In retrospect, one can proceed with the following heuristics.
For equations like (
1.2
), the corresponding
dynamics begin with the constant initial conditions and subject to
the spatially homogeneous noises, and thus admits stationary solutions (if
any). Then, a natural question to ask after the stationarity is that

“
is the invariant
\sigma
-algebra generated by the spatial shifts trivial?
”

Or equivalently, one can ask whether or not the solution to (
1.2
) is
spatial ergodic.

An affirmative answer to this question would lead to a law-of-large number type result,
i.e.,
F_{R}(t)/R\to 0
almost surely and in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
as
R
tends to infinity.
It is then very natural to investigate the second-order fluctuation of
F_{R}(t)
.
In the previous work
[
9
]
, we addressed these questions for the first
time for SPDEs driven by a Lévy noise, and we focused
on the linear equation, with
\sigma(u)=u
in (
1.2
).
In the current paper, we work on the stochastic nonlinear wave equation (
1.2
)
with general nonlinearity.

1.2.
Main results

We are now ready to present the first main result of this article.

Theorem 1.2
.

Recall the definition of
m_{2}
from (
1.3
).
We assume

m_{2}\in(0,\infty)
and
\sigma(1)\not=0
.
Then, the following statements hold true.

(i)

(
spatial ergodicity
)
For any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\{u(t,x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is strictly stationary and ergodic.
Consequently, the following law of large numbers holds:

(1.13)

\displaystyle\frac{F_{R}(t)}{R}\to 0\quad\mbox{in $L^{2}(\mathbb{P})$ and almost surely as $R\to\infty$}.

(ii)

(
limiting covariance
)
For any
t,s\geq 0
,

(1.14)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)]=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Cov}\big(u(t,x),u(s,0)\big)dx=:K(t,s).

Consequently,
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)\asymp R
as
R\to\infty
. Moreover,
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)>0
for all
t>0
and
R>0
.

(iii)

(
QCLT
)
Suppose that
m_{4}<\infty
. Then, for any
t>0
, there exists a constant
C_{t}>0
depending on
t
, such that

(1.15)

d_{\rm Wass}\left(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq C_{t}R^{-1/2}.

(iv)

(
FCLT
)
Suppose that
m_{4}<\infty
. Then,
the process
\{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(t):t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}

converges in law to a centered continuous Gaussian process
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}

with the covariance structure
K
as in (
1.14
).

As mentioned above, the novelty of our result is the fact that we consider a general Lipschitz function
\sigma
. In the case
\sigma(u)=u
, the techniques of
[
9
]
rely heavily on the fact that the solution and its Malliavin derivative
Du
have explicit chaos expansions. This is no longer true when
\sigma
is arbitrary. The techniques that we use in the present paper start with
a careful analysis of the integral equation (
3.2
) satisfied by
Du
, paying special attention to some predictability issues that arise for giving a rigorous meaning to this equation.
As a result, we substantially improve the main result of
[
4
]
, which showed that
Du
satisfies the integral equation (
3.2
) in the affine linear case
\sigma(u)=au+b
.
Next, we move on to deriving the following
key estimate
:

(1.16)

\|D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq CG_{t-r}(x-y)|z|

provided that
m_{p}<\infty
for some
p\geq 2

for
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every
(r,y,z)\in\mathbf{Z}
; see (
3.12
) below.
To prove (
1.16
), we begin with the integral equation for
Du_{n}
with
u_{n}
the
n
-th
Picard iteration and then proceed with applications of Rosenthal’s inequality
to obtain uniform-in-
n
bound on
\|D_{r,y,z}u_{n}(t,x)\|_{p}
.
In order to pass

for obtaining (
1.16
),
we use a weak convergence argument and the technique of differentiating
Radon measures; see Proposition
3.3
for more details.
Finally, we develop a variant of the Malliavin-Stein bound in the Poisson case (Theorem

Theorem
1.2
-(iii).

Remark 1.3
.

In our previous work. we established the QCLT
under the weaker assumption
m_{p}+m_{p/2}<\infty

for some
p\in(2,4]
, when
\sigma(u)=u
.
As mentioned before, due to the linearity in
u
, we have explicit
chaos expansions, from which we obtain bounds of Malliavin derivatives up to second order.
These bounds can be easily combined with a recent work of Trauthwein
[
46
]

so as to establish the QCLT under the said assumption.
In the present paper, we are working with very general Lipschitz nonlinearity,
then the solution
u(t,x)
is only expected to be Malliavin differentiable of first order.
Even if we may assume more regularity on
\sigma
, our method of establishing (
1.16
)
would require us to work on the recurrence equation satisfied by
D^{2}u_{n}
,
which is much more complicated than that for
Du_{n}
and cannot be easily iterated.
For these reasons, we restrict ourselves to the assumption that
m_{4}<\infty

and develop a discrete Malliavin-Stein bound (Theorem
4.1
)
that is tailor-made for our setting.

[

]
,

in
[

]
. In the current setting, we can establish the same result.

Corollary 1.4

(

)
.

Let the assumptions in Theorem

prevail

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log T}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{1}{y}\phi\big(F_{y}(t_{0})/\sigma_{y}(t_{0})\big)dy\xrightarrow[almost\,surely]{T\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}\phi(z)dz.

It follows from Theorem

We omit the details here.

The second main contribution of this article
is an asymptotic independence result for
(F_{R}(t),u)

as
R\to\infty
. This is motivated by the recent developments
[
47
,
48
]

in the Gaussian case that stemmed from an observation of L. Pimentel
[

]
.
We present a brief account of this new development in Section

.
Now let us properly formulate the notion of asymptotic independence.
It is known, e.g., from
[
20
, Section 11.3]

that the set of bounded Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}

is a separating class for the weak convergence of probability
measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Let
X,Y
be two random variables with values in

\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
respectively,
and defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Then,
X,Y
are independent if and only if

\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(X)f_{2}(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(X)]\mathbb{E}[f_{2}(Y)]
for any bounded Lipschitz
functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The above condition is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}[e^{i(\boldsymbol{s}\cdot X+\boldsymbol{t}\cdot Y)}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\boldsymbol{s}\cdot X}]\mathbb{E}[e^{i\boldsymbol{t}\cdot Y}]

for any
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
,
where
\boldsymbol{s}\cdot X
denotes the usual inner product
on the corresponding Euclidean space.
Furthermore, we say the families of random variables
(X_{R}),(Y_{R})

in
\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
(resp.) are
asymptotically independent

as
R\to+\infty
if

(1.18)

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(f_{1}(X_{R}),f_{2}(Y_{R})\big)\xrightarrow{R\to+\infty}0

for any bounded Lipschitz functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
.
With (
1.12
) in mind, one can easily see that
the above condition
(
1.18
) holds if

(1.19)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}((X_{R},Y_{R}),(\widehat{X}_{R},\widehat{Y}_{R}))\xrightarrow{R\to+\infty}0,

where
(\widehat{X}_{R},\widehat{Y}_{R})
has the same marginals as
(X_{R},Y_{R})

with
\widehat{X}_{R},

\widehat{Y}_{R}
independent.
See

[
18
]
for more on various notions of asymptotic independence.

Now let us state our main result in this direction
and postpone its proof to Section
5.3
.

.

(
Asymptotic independence

Recall (

.
Then,
for any

,

there exists a finite constant

2.1.
Basics on Itô integration

Recall that
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
is endowed with the Borel

\sigma
-field
\mathcal{Z}
and the measure
\mathfrak{m}={\rm Leb}\times\nu
.
The Poisson random measure
N
, over which
our space-time pure-jump Lévy white noise
\dot{L}
is built,
is a set-indexed family
\{N(A):A\in\mathcal{Z}\}
of Poisson random variables.
More precisely, let
\mathbf{N}_{\sigma}
be the set of all
\sigma
-finite measures
\chi

on
(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
with
\chi(B)\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}

for each
B\in\mathcal{Z}
. Let
\mathscr{N}_{\sigma}
be the smallest
\sigma
-algebra
that makes the mapping

\chi\in\mathbf{N}_{\sigma}\mapsto\chi(B)\in[0,\infty]

measurable for each
B\in\mathcal{Z}
. We recall the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A Poisson random measure
N
with intensity measure
\mathfrak{m}
is a

(\mathbf{N}_{\sigma},\mathscr{N}_{\sigma})
-valued random element
defined on a complete probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that the following two conditions hold:

•

for each
A\in\mathcal{Z}
,
N(A)
has a Poisson distribution
with mean
\mathfrak{m}(A)
;
3
3

3

If
\mathfrak{m}(A)=\infty
,
we set
N(A)=\infty
almost surely.

•

for any mutually disjoint sets
A_{1},...,A_{k}\in\mathcal{Z}

(
k\geq 1
)
,

N(A_{1}),...,N(A_{k})
are independent.

For
A\in\mathcal{Z}
with
\mathfrak{m}(A)<\infty
, we define
\widehat{N}(A)=N(A)-\mathfrak{m}(A)
. We
call
\widehat{N}
the compensated Poisson random measure on
\mathbf{Z}
.

\bullet

A natural filtration.
We denote by
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}
the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by the random variables

N([0,s]\times A\times B)
with
s\in[0,t]
and

\textup{Leb}(A)+\nu(B)<\infty
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\big(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}\cup\mathcal{N}\big)
be the

\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}
and the set
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathbb{P}
-null sets.
This gives us a filtration

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}:=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}.

\bullet

Itô integral with respect to
\widehat{N}
.

Recall that a process
V=\{V(t,x,z):t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},x\in\mathbb{R},z\in\mathbb{R}_{0}\}
is called
elementary
,
if it is of the form

(2.2)

V(\omega,t,x,z)=X(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{(a,b]}(t)\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)\mathbf{1}_{C}(z),

where
0\leq a<b
,
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,
C\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{0})
, and
X
is a bounded
\mathcal{F}_{a}
-measurable random variable.
The predictable
\sigma
-field
\overline{\mathcal{P}}

on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}

is generated by linear combinations of processes
of form (
2.2
).
4
4

4

All predictable
\sigma
-fields in this paper
are considered with respect to the filtration
\mathbb{F}
in (
2.1
).
To simplify the language, we use the term “predictable”
instead of “
\mathbb{F}
-predictable”.

For an elementary process
V
as in (
2.2
),
we define its Itô integral
as follows: for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

I_{t}^{\widehat{N}}(V):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}V(s,x,z)\widehat{N}(ds,dx,dz)=X\cdot\widehat{N}((t\wedge a,t\wedge b]\times B\times C).

Recall the notation
\mathfrak{H}=L^{2}(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z},\mathfrak{m})
.
One can easily verify the
isometry property
for the above integral:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\widehat{N}}(V)|^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|V\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\big],

which can be extended to all predictable process
V\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
.
Note that the process
\{I_{t}^{\widehat{N}}(V)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a zero-mean square-integrable

\mathbb{F}
-martingale that has a càdlàg modification
(still denoted by
I_{\bullet}^{\widehat{N}}(V)
).
This càdlàg modification has
the following (predictable) quadratic variation

\langle I^{\widehat{N}}(V)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}V^{2}(s,x,z)dsdx\nu(dz)

and therefore,

t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\big(I_{t}^{\widehat{N}}(V)\big)^{2}-\langle I^{\widehat{N}}(V)\rangle_{t}
is
a (local)
\mathbb{F}
-martingale.

Let us record a basic property of Itô’s integral, which will be used in
the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
F
be
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable.
Suppose
V\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
is predictable, then
the following equality holds almost surely:

(2.4)

\displaystyle F\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}V(r,x,z)\widehat{N}(dr,dx,dz)=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}FV(r,x,z)\widehat{N}(dr,dx,dz)

for any
t>s
.

Proof.

We only prove the result when
V
is an elementary process.
The general case will follow by approximation.
Let
V
be a process of the form (
2.2
)
and
M_{t}=\widehat{N}([0,t]\times A\times C\big)
.
Then, the left-hand side of (
2.4
) is equal to

(2.5)

FX\big[(M_{t\wedge b}-M_{t\wedge a})-(M_{s\wedge b}-M_{s\wedge a})\big].

We consider three cases:

(i) If
s\leq a
, then
FV
is clearly an elementary process.
Then, the right-hand side of (
2.4
) coincides with (
2.5
).

(ii) If
s\in(a,b]
, then
t\wedge a=s\wedge a=a
and thus,
we can rewrite (
2.5
) as

FX(M_{t\wedge b}-M_{s\wedge b})=FX(M_{t\wedge b}-M_{s}).

At the same time,
(r,x,z)\mapsto FV(r,x,z)\mathbf{1}_{(s,t]}(r)=FX\mathbf{1}_{(s,b]}(r)\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)\mathbf{1}_{C}(z)

is an elementary process,
so that the right-hand-side of (
2.4
) is almost surely equal to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}FX\mathbf{1}_{(s,b]}(r)\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)\mathbf{1}_{C}(z)\widehat{N}(dr,dx,dz)

\displaystyle=FX(M_{t\wedge b}-M_{t\wedge s})

\displaystyle=FX(M_{t\wedge b}-M_{s}).

That is, the equality (
2.4
) holds true in this case.

(iii) If
s>b
, then (
2.5
) and thus the left-side of (
2.4
)
is equal to zero, and it is also clear that the right-side of (
2.4
) is equal to
0
.

Hence, the proof is completed.
∎

\bullet

Itô integral with respect to

.

We say that a process

with

.
We say that

is

if it is measurable
with respect to the predictable

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}

on

\sigma

From the above discussion on predictability of random fields indexed by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(s,x)L(ds,dx):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(s,x)z\widehat{N}(ds,dx,dz)

for any predictable random field

From (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}g(s,x)L(ds,dx)\big|^{2}\Big]=m_{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}|g(s,x)|^{2}\,dsdx\Big].

Remark 2.3
.

(i) The Itô integral (

)
with respect to

-martingale and a zero-mean Lévy process with Lévy measure

2.2.
Basic Malliavin calculus

In this section, we recall some basic facts about Malliavin calculus in the Poisson setting.
We refer the readers to
[
40
,
27
,
29
,
30
,
34
]
for more details.

Let us begin with the well-known Wiener-Poisson-Itô chaos decomposition
that asserts that the space
L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{N\},\mathbb{P})
can be
decomposed into a direct sum of mutually orthogonal subspaces (called
Poisson chaoses); see, e.g.,
[
30
, Section 18.4]

and
[
34
, Section 9.9]
.

\bullet

Poisson chaos expansion.

Any random variable
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{N\},\mathbb{P})
can be written as:

(2.9)

\displaystyle F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}),

where
f_{n}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, are uniquely determined by
F

up to a
\mathfrak{m}
-null set.
Here,
\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}
is the set of symmetric elements in
\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}
.
Let
J_{n}(\bullet)
denote the projection operator from
L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{N\},\mathbb{P})

onto the
n
-th Poisson-Wiener chaos
\mathbb{C}_{n}=\{I_{n}(f):f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}\}
,
i.e., for
F
as in (
2.9
),

(2.10)

\displaystyle J_{0}F=\mathbb{E}[F]\quad{\rm and}\quad J_{n}F=I_{n}(f_{n}),\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}.

As in the Gaussian case (
[
32
, Proposition 2.7.5]
),
the Poisson chaoses are mutually orthogonal with

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{n}(f)I_{m}(g)]=n!\mathbf{1}_{\{n=m\}}\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}

for any
f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}
and
g\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot m}
with
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.
As a consequence, we have, with
F
as in (
2.9
), that

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(F)=\sum_{n\geq 1}{\rm Var}(J_{n}F)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n!\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}<\infty.

\bullet

Malliavin derivative.
Let
\textup{dom}(D)
denote the set of random variables

F
as in (
2.9
) with the symmetric kernels
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n!n\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}<\infty.

For such a random variable
F\in\textup{dom}(D)
, we define the
Malliavin derivative

DF
of
F
to be a
\mathfrak{H}
-valued random variable given by

\displaystyle D_{\xi}F=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{\xi}J_{n}F=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nI_{n-1}(f_{n}(\xi,\bullet)),\,\,\,\xi\in\mathbf{Z},

where for fixed
\xi\in\mathbf{Z}
,
f_{n}(\xi,\bullet)\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot(n-1)}
.
In view of (
2.11
) and Fubini’s theorem,
we can easily derive that for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[\|D_{\xi}J_{k}F\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}}\big]&=k^{2}(k-1)!\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|f_{k}(\xi,\bullet)\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(k-1)}}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\\
&=kk!\|f_{k}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes k}}=k\mathbb{E}\big[\|J_{k}F\|^{2}_{2}\big].\end{aligned}

As a result, we have

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|DF\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\|DJ_{n}F\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\big]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\mathbb{E}\big[\|J_{n}F\|^{2}_{2}\big]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nn!\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}<\infty.

Comparing (
2.14
) with (
2.12
) yields
the following Poincaré inequality:

(2.15)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(F)\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|DF\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\big]

for any
F\in\textup{dom}(D)
,
with equality when and only when
F-\mathbb{E}[F]\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
.

As we only study distributional properties,
we can assume that the Poisson
random measure
N
(from Definition
2.1
) is a
proper simple point process of the form

N=\sum_{n=1}^{\kappa}\delta_{Z_{n}},

where
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are independent random variables with values in
\mathbf{Z}
,

\kappa
is a
random variable with values in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
and
\delta_{\xi}
is the Dirac mass at
\xi\in\mathbf{Z}
. With probability one,
these points are distinct (since
\mathfrak{m}

is diffusive); see, e.g.,
[
30
, Corollary 3.7]
.
For any real
\sigma\{N\}
-measurable random variable
F
, by Doob-Dynkin lemma,
we can write
F=\mathfrak{f}(N)
for some
\mathscr{N}_{\sigma}
-measurable function
\mathfrak{f}:\mathbf{N}_{\sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define
the
add-one cost operator
by

(2.16)

\displaystyle D^{+}_{\xi}F:=\mathfrak{f}(N+\delta_{\xi})-\mathfrak{f}(N).

The following lemma asserts that the add-one cost operator coincides
with the Malliavin derivative on
\textup{dom}(D)
.
For the proof of this result, we refer to
[
27
, Theorem 3]
for part (i)
and refer to
[
41
, Lemma 3.1]
for part (ii).

Lemma 2.4
.

(i)
If
F\in\textup{dom}(D)
, then
D^{+}F=DF
.

(ii)
If
\mathbb{E}[\|D^{+}_{\bullet}F\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}]<\infty
, then

F\in\textup{dom}(D)
and
DF=D^{+}F
.

In the Poisson case, the Malliavin derivative satisfies a “
difference rule
”, instead of the chain rule encountered in the Gaussian case (e.g.,
[
32
, Proposition 2.3.8]
).
This difference rule is stated in the following.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}\in\textup{dom}(D)
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function.
Then
\phi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{d})\in\textup{dom}(D)
and

\displaystyle D_{\xi}\phi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{d})=\phi(F_{1}+D_{\xi}F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}+D_{\xi}F_{d})-\phi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{d})

for
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{Z}
.

Proof.

We write
F_{i}=\mathfrak{f}_{i}(N)
for some
\mathscr{N}_{\sigma}
-measurable function
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
,

i=1,\ldots,d
. Then, we can write

\phi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{d})=\phi\circ(\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{d})(N)
, and then
we can deduce from (
2.16
) and Lemma
2.4

(2.37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nn!\|h_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(n+1)}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nn!\|h_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(n+1)}}<\infty.

Before proving

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nn!\|\widetilde{h}_{n-1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}<\infty\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1)^{2}n!\|\widetilde{h}_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(n+1)}}<\infty.

And it is also immediate that

(2.39)

\displaystyle D_{\xi_{0}}\delta(V)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nI_{n-1}\big(\,\widetilde{h}_{n-1}(\xi_{0})\big),

where

is obtained by first symmetrizing

and then fixing the first argument to be
\xi_{0}

Now let us show

.
For this, we apply Lemma

We first rewrite (

\displaystyle D_{\xi_{0}}V(\xi)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\big(g_{\xi_{0},n}(\xi,\bullet)\big)\quad{\rm with}\quad g_{\xi_{0},n}(\xi,\bullet):=(n+1)h_{n+1}(\xi,\xi_{0},\bullet).

It is not difficult to see that the canonical symmetrization of

is given by

(2.40)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widetilde{g}_{\xi_{0},n}(\xi_{n+1},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n})&=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}h_{n+1}(\xi_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi_{\neq j}})\\
&=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}h_{n+1}(\xi_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi_{\neq j}})\bigg)-h_{n+1}(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n+1})\\
&=(n+2)\widetilde{h}_{n+1}(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n+1})-h_{n+1}(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n+1}),\end{aligned}

where

\boldsymbol{\xi_{\neq j}}
stands for the
(n+1)
arguments

(\xi_{0},...,\xi_{j-1},\xi_{j+1},...,\xi_{n+1})

Therefore, it follows from (

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n!\|\widetilde{g}_{\xi_{0},n-1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq 2\sum_{n\geq 1}n!\Big[(n+1)^{2}\|\widetilde{h}_{n}(\xi_{0},\bullet)\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}+\|h_{n}(\xi_{0},\bullet)\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\Big],

which is integrable with respect to
\mathfrak{m}(d\xi_{0})
in view of (
2.37
) and (
2.38
).
Thus,
D_{\xi_{0}}V\in\textup{dom}(\delta)

for
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every
\xi_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
and

(2.41)

\displaystyle\delta\big(D_{\xi_{0}}V\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\big(\widetilde{g}_{\xi_{0},n-1}\big).

Hence the desired commutation relation

), (
2.41

2.30

). ∎

2.3.
Predictability

In this section, we include some auxiliary results related to predictability.
We start by recalling the following definition of modification.

Definition 2.15
.

If
X=\{X(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
Y=\{Y(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}

are two processes defined on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
,
we say that
X
and
Y
are modifications of each other if

\mathbb{P}\big(X(t,x)=Y(t,x)\big)=1

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
X=\{X(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a process
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that
for any
T>0
and for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
X\big|_{[0,T]\times K}

has a jointly measurable
(
respectively, predictable
)
modification.
Then,
X
has a jointly measurable

(
respectively, predictable
)
modification on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

We only prove the case where
X
is jointly measurable,
while the same argument works for the case where
X
is predictable.

Let
E_{m}=\{\|x\|\leq m\}
for
m\geq 1

and let
X_{m}^{\prime}
be the jointly measurable modification of
X
on
[0,m]\times E_{m}
.
Then
\widetilde{X}_{m}:=X_{m}^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{[0,m]\times E_{m}}
is a jointly measurable modification of

X_{m}:=X\mathbf{1}_{[0,m]\times E_{m}}
.
Therefore, for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the event

\Omega_{t,x}=\bigcap_{m\geq 1}\{\widetilde{X}_{m}(t,x)=X_{m}(t,x)\}

has probability
1

and on
\Omega_{t,x}
, one has

(2.42)

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\widetilde{X}_{m}(t,x)=\lim_{m\to\infty}X_{m}(t,x)=X(t,x).

That is, the above limit (
2.42
) takes place almost surely
for every
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Now define

A=\{(\omega,t,x)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lim_{m\to\infty}\widetilde{X}_{m}(\omega,t,x)\,\mbox{exists}\}
.
Then, for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the event

\{\omega\in\Omega:(\omega,t,x)\in A^{c}\}\subset\Omega^{c}_{t,x}
has probability
0
,
so that by Fubini theorem, the set
A
has the full measure
with
\mathbf{1}_{A}(\omega,t,x)=1
almost surely for every
(t,x)
,
and moreover, we can deduce from (
2.42
) that

(2.43)

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}(\omega,t,x):

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\widetilde{X}_{m}(\omega,t,x)\mathbf{1}_{A}(\omega,t,x)

(2.44)

\displaystyle=X(\omega,t,x)\mathbf{1}_{A}(\omega,t,x),

where the equality (
2.44
) holds for every
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

and for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.
By definition (
2.43
), we easily see that

\widetilde{X}
is jointly measurable and
\widetilde{X}(t,x)=X(t,x)
almost surely
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Hence,
\widetilde{X}
is a jointly measurable
modification of
X
.
∎

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
X=\{X(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}

be a collection of random variables in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{n}(t,x)-X(t,x)|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}

for every

\bullet

-almost all

and therefore,

as $n\to\infty$}.

,
\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{\xi}X_{k}

has a jointly measurable modification
(and a predictable modification, if

is adapted),
and

has a jointly measurable modification.
Hence, the same thing can be said about
D_{\xi}X

by taking an almost everywhere convergent subsequence
in (
2.49
).

Hence, the proof is completed.
∎

The Malliavin-Stein approach was invented by Nourdin and Peccati
in the Gaussian setting
[
31
]
and
the general theory has been developed in their monograph
[
32
]
.
In a paper
[
40
]
by Peccati, Solé, Taqqu, and Utzet,
the Malliavin-Stein approach found its Poisson analogue;
see also the survey
[
12
]
.

In what follows, we first present the univariate Malliavin-Stein bound
in Section
4.1

and present a multivariate version in
Section
4.2

that is motivated by
[
44
,
47
]
.

4.1.
Univariate Malliavin-Stein bound

Let us begin with the
Stein’s lemma

asserting that for a real integrable random variable
Z
,

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[Zf(Z)]

for any absolutely continuous function

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f^{\prime}(Z)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
;
see, e.g.,
[
45
, Lemma 2.1]
.
This hints that
if one obtains
\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X)-Xf(X)]\approx 0
for a large enough family
of test functions
f
, one can say something about the proximity
of law of
X
to that of a standard normal random variable.
In this paper, we use the Wasserstein distance (
1.12
)
to describe the distance between random variables,
where the corresponding test functions are
1
-Lipschitz.
Taking any
1
-Lipschitz function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
we solve the following ordinary differential equation (ODE, known as
the
Stein’s equation
)

(4.1)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)-xf(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)],

where
h
is known and
f
is the unknown with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
One can easily solve the above ODE:

f_{h}(x)=e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big[h(t)-\mathbb{E}(h(Z))\big]e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2}}dt,

which is the unique solution to (
4.1
) subject to

\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2}}f(x)=0
. We call
f_{h}
the

Stein’s solution
to the Stein’s equation (
4.1
).
Via an involved careful analysis, one can derive the regularity properties
of
f_{h}
: with
\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\text{$\|f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq 2$,
$\|f_{h}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}$, and $\|f_{h}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2$};

see, e.g.,
[
45
, Lemma 2.5]
.
Now replacing the dummy variable
x
by
X
in (
4.1
)
and taking expectation on both sides yields the
Stein’s bound
:

(4.3)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}(X,Z)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{W}}\big|\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X)-Xf(X)]\big|,

where
\mathcal{F}_{W}
is the set of functions
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 2
,

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}
, and
\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2

as in (
4.2
); see also Proposition 2.1.1 in
[
50
]
.

In the following, we present the univariate Malliavin-Stein bound,
one of our main technical tools,
which is a variant of
[
40
, Theorem 3.1]

that suits our application on SPDEs.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X=\delta(v)\in\textup{dom}(D)
for some
v\in\textup{dom}(\delta)

such that
\textup{Var}(X)=1
.
14
14

14

By definition of
\textup{dom}(\delta)
,

X=\delta(v)

is a centered random variable with finite variance.

Then,

(4.4)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\big(X,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\big\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big\|_{1}+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|(D_{\xi}X)^{2}v(\xi)\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{\textup{Var}\big(\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)}+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|(D_{\xi}X)^{2}v(\xi)\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

Let us also record a useful fact before moving to the proof of Theorem
4.1
:
for a function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
as in (
4.3
),
we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle f(x+a)-f(x)-f^{\prime}(x)a=a\int_{0}^{1}\big(f^{\prime}(x+ta)-f^{\prime}(x)\big)dt

for any
x,a\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows from
\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2
that the left side of (
4.6
) is bounded by

a^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

To obtain a bound
for
d_{\rm Wass}(X,Z)
, with
Z\in\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}[Xf(X)-f^{\prime}(X)]
for
f\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
as in (
4.3
).
Note first that due to
\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}
, the above expectation is well defined.
Using
X=\delta(v)
and the duality relation (
2.21
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Xf(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\delta(v)f(X)]=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,Df(X)\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big]

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}f^{\prime}(X)\big]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbf{Z}}v(\xi)\big[f(X+D_{\xi}X)-f(X)-f^{\prime}(X)D_{\xi}X\big]\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\bigg].

It follows from (
4.6
) with
\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2
that
the second expectation in (
4.7
) is bounded by

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbf{Z}}|v(\xi)|(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\bigg]=\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

It follows that

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}(X,Z)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{W}}\big|\mathbb{E}[Xf(X)-f^{\prime}(X)]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

Hence, the desired Malliavin-Stein bound (
4.4
)
is proved, while
the second bound (
4.5
)
follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle DX,v\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[X\delta(v)]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=1.

Hence, the proof is completed.
∎

4.2.
Multivariate Malliavin-Stein bound

Recall from (
1.12
) that the Wasserstein distance between
two random vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is given by

(4.10)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2})=\sup\big\{\big|\mathbb{E}[h(\mathbf{Y}_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[h(\mathbf{Y}_{2})]\big|:\,\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1\big\},

that is, the supremum runs over all
1
-Lipschitz functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For any
1
-Lipschitz function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define
h_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(x+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})]

with
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathbf{G}
standard normal on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
Then,

h_{\varepsilon}
is continuously differentiable with
\textup{Lip}(h_{\varepsilon})\leq 1

and
\|h_{\varepsilon}-h\|_{\infty}\to 0
when
\varepsilon\to 0
,
as one can easily verify. As a result, one can rewrite (
4.10
) as

(4.11)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2})=\sup\big\{\big|\mathbb{E}[h(\mathbf{Y}_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[h(\mathbf{Y}_{2})]\big|:\,h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1}),\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1\big\}.

Now let us state Pimentel’s observation that generalizes Stein’s lemma.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
44
]
)
.

Let
X
be a real, integrable random variable
and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},...,Y_{d})
be a
d
-dimensional
random vector. Then,

X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
X
is independent of
\mathbf{Y}
if and only if

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})]=0,

for all differentiable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\to\mathbb{R}

with
\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})\in L^{1}(\Omega)
; see also Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 in
[
47
]
.

Heuristically speaking, when

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})]\approx 0

for a large enough family of test functions
f
,
we have approximate normality for
X

and the approximate independence between
X
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
To measure the approximate normality and the approximate independence,
we can use the Wasserstein distance

d_{\rm Wass}\big((X,\mathbf{Y}),(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)

with
Z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independent of
\mathbf{Y}
.
See also the discussion before Theorem
1.5
.
As in the Stein’s method, one can consider the following (generalized)
Stein’s equation: for
(x,\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{y})-xf(x,\boldsymbol{y})=h(x,\boldsymbol{y})-\mathbb{E}[h(Z,\boldsymbol{y})]\quad\text{with $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$},

where
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})
with
\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1
is given
and
f
is the unknown. After we solve the above Stein’s equation (
4.12
),
we can replace
(x,\boldsymbol{y})
by
(X,\mathbf{Y})
and take expectations at both sides
of (
4.12
), to get

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})]=\mathbb{E}[h(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})],

where
Z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\mathbf{Y}
.
Note that for any fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the above Stein’s equation is simply the one in (
4.1
).
As a result, we immediately deduce from
Section
4.1
that

(4.14)

\displaystyle f_{h}(x,\boldsymbol{y})=e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big(h(t,\boldsymbol{y})-\mathbb{E}[h(Z,\boldsymbol{y})]\big)e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2}}dt\quad\text{with $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$}

solves the Stein’s equation (
4.12
) and the Stein’s solution in (
4.14
) satisfies

(4.15)

\displaystyle\text{$\|f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq 2$, $\|\partial_{x}f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}$,
and $\|\partial^{2}_{xx}f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq 2$}.

Now taking partial derivative
\partial_{y_{j}}f_{h}
with (
4.14
),
we have

\partial_{y_{j}}f_{h}(x,\boldsymbol{y})=e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big(\partial_{y_{j}}h(t,\boldsymbol{y})-\mathbb{E}[\partial_{y_{j}}h(Z,\boldsymbol{y})]\big)e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2}}dt,

where
\phi(t):=\partial_{y_{j}}h(t,\boldsymbol{y})
is uniformly bounded by
1

for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
j\in\{1,...,d\}
.
In view of the standard Stein’s method,

\partial_{y_{j}}f_{h}(x,\boldsymbol{y})
is the Stein’s solution to
the Stein’s equation (
4.1
) with
h
replaced by
\phi
,
so that we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y_{j}}f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\quad{\rm and}\quad\|\partial_{x}\partial_{y_{j}}f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq 4;

see, e.g., Lemma 2.5 in the survey
[
45
]
. Let us summarize
the above discussion in the following lemma. See also
[
44
, Lemma 5.1]

and
[
47
, Proposition 1]
.
We would like to point out that
the second bound in (
4.16
) is new (and of independent interest although
we do not need it in this work)
and our argument avoids any direct computations
done in
[
44
,
47
]
.

Lemma 4.3

(Stein’s bound)
.

(i)
Consider the multivariate Stein’s equation (
4.12
) with
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})

and
\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1
. Then,
f_{h}
given in (
4.14
) solves (
4.12
) and it satisfies
(
4.15
) and (
4.16
).

(ii)
Let
X
be a real integrable random variable and
\mathbf{Y}
be any
random vector on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

(4.17)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\big((X,\mathbf{Y}),(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{A}_{W}}\big|\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})]\big|,

where
Z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\mathbf{Y}
,

\mathcal{A}_{W}
is the set of twice-differentiable functions

(x,\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\mapsto f(x,\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 2
,
\|\partial_{x}f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}
,

\|\partial^{2}_{xx}f\|_{\infty}\leq 2
,

\|\partial_{y_{j}}f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}
,
and
\|\partial_{x}\partial_{y_{j}}f\|_{\infty}\leq 4

for any
j\in\{1,...,d\}
.

Proof.

We only prove (ii) here. Indeed,
the bound (
4.17
) follows from
(
4.11
), (
4.13
),
and part (i) of Lemma
4.3
. ∎

Next, we present the multivariate Malliavin-Stein bound in the Poisson setting
that measures both the approximate normality of one marginal
and the approximate independence between components.
See
[
47
, Theorem 1]
for an analogous result in the Gaussian setting.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
X=\delta(v)\in\textup{dom}(D)
for some
v\in\textup{dom}(\delta)

such that
\textup{Var}(X)=1
.
Consider
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})

with
Y_{j}\in\textup{dom}(D)
for each
j\in\{1,...,d\}
.
Let
Z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be independent of
\mathbf{Y}
, then
we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm Wass}\big((X,\mathbf{Y}),(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)&\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}Y_{j})\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).\end{aligned}

Moreover, one can further bound

\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}
by
\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})}
.

Remark 4.5
.

In view of Theorem
4.1
,
the first two terms in (
4.18
) quantifies the approximate
normality of
X
, while the third term in (
4.18
)
quantifies the approximate independence between
X

and
\mathbf{Y}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}_{W}
be given as in Lemma
4.3
. We need to bound
the following expectation

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})].

First using the duality relation (
2.21
),
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Xf(X,\mathbf{Y})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\delta(v)f(X,\mathbf{Y})]=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,Df(X,\mathbf{Y})\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big]

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,Df(X,\mathbf{Y})-\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big].

Then, the first term in (
4.20
) can be coupled with
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]

in (
4.19
) so that we have

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big|\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})]-\mathbb{E}\big[\langle v,DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})\big]\big|&\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\big\|1-\langle v,DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big\|_{1}\\
&\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle v,DX\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})},\end{aligned}

where we used
\|\partial_{x}f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}

and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with (
4.9
).
It remains to bound the second expectation in (
4.20
).

Recall from Lemma
2.5
that for
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{Z}
,
we can write

\displaystyle D_{\xi}f(X,\mathbf{Y})-\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})D_{\xi}X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(X+D_{\xi}X,Y_{1}+D_{\xi}Y_{1},...,Y_{d}+D_{\xi}Y_{d})-f(X,\mathbf{Y})-\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})D_{\xi}X

(4.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(X+D_{\xi}X,Y_{1}+D_{\xi}Y_{1},...,Y_{d}+D_{\xi}Y_{d})-f(X+D_{\xi}X,\mathbf{Y})

(4.23)

\displaystyle\quad+f(X+D_{\xi}X,\mathbf{Y})-f(X,\mathbf{Y})-\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})D_{\xi}X.

Using
\|\partial_{y_{j}}f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}
for each
j
,
we can deal with the term in (
4.22
) as follows:
note that for any
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
with
a_{1}=b_{1}
,

\displaystyle|f(\boldsymbol{b})-f(\boldsymbol{a})|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{1+d}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{j}f(\boldsymbol{a}+t(\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{a}))(b_{j}-a_{j})dt\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=2}^{1+d}|b_{j}-a_{j}|,

from which we deduce that

\displaystyle|f(X+D_{\xi}X,Y_{1}+D_{\xi}Y_{1},...,Y_{d}+D_{\xi}Y_{d})-f(X+D_{\xi}X,\mathbf{Y})|\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|D_{\xi}Y_{j}|,

and thus

(4.24)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{\mathbf{Z}}v(\xi)\Big[f(X+D_{\xi}X,Y_{1}+D_{\xi}Y_{1},...,Y_{d}+D_{\xi}Y_{d})-f(X+D_{\xi}X,\mathbf{Y})\Big]\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\bigg)\right|\\
&\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}Y_{j})\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).\end{aligned}

For the term in (
4.23
), the same arguments in (
4.7
) and
(
4.8
) with
\|\partial^{2}_{xx}f\|_{\infty}\leq 2

lead to

(4.25)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{\mathbf{Z}}v(\xi)\Big[f(X+D_{\xi}X,\mathbf{Y})-f(X,\mathbf{Y})-\partial_{x}f(X,\mathbf{Y})D_{\xi}X\Big]\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\bigg)\right|\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).\end{aligned}

Therefore, it follows from Lemma (
4.3
)
with (
4.19
), (
4.20
), (
4.21
), (
4.24
),
and (
4.25
) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm Wass}\big((X,\mathbf{Y}),(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)&\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}+\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}Y_{j})\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|v(\xi)(D_{\xi}X)^{2}\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).\end{aligned}

This proves the bound (
4.18
).

Finally, recalling the relation (
4.9
),
we can further bound

\|1-\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}
by
\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle DX,v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})}
;
see also (
4.21
).

Hence, the proof is completed.
∎

5.1.
Stationarity of the solution

Recall from (

L_{t}(B)=L([0,t]\times B)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widehat{N}(ds,dy,dz),

defines a
martingale measure

[
17

The following definition
was introduced in
[
17

Definition 5.1
.

Let
g=\{g(t,x):(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\}
be a random field defined on the same probability space as

L
.
We say that
g
has property
(S)
with respect to
L
,
if the finite-dimensional distributions of
(g^{(z)},L^{(z)})

do not depend on
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
where

(5.1)

g^{(z)}(t,x)=g(t,x+z)
and
L_{t}^{(z)}(B)=L_{t}(B+z)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
with
B+z:=\{x+z:x\in B\}
.

The goal of this subsection is to show that the solution
u(t,x)
to (
1.2
)
satisfies the property (S) with respect to
L
.
Let us first establish a weaker result.

Lemma 5.2
.

The process
L
is spatially homogeneous,
in the sense that the finite-dimensional distributions
of
L^{(z)}=\{L_{t}^{(z)}(B)\}_{(t,B)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})}
do not depend on
z
.

Proof.

Define
N^{(z)}(A\times B\times C)=N(A\times(B+z)\times C)

for bounded Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
B\subset\mathbb{R}
, and
C\subset\mathbb{R}_{0}
.
Due to the translational invariance of Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
,
we see that
N^{(z)}
is a Poisson random measure on
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}

with the intensity measure
\mathfrak{m}
.
That is,
N^{(z)}
has the same distribution as
N
.
It follows that

\displaystyle L_{t}^{(z)}(B)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_{B+z}(x)L(ds,dx)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x-z)z_{1}\widehat{N}(ds,dx,dz_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)z_{1}\widehat{N}^{(z)}(ds,dx,dz_{1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)z_{1}\widehat{N}(ds,dx,dz_{1})=L_{t}(B).

By the same argument, we can show that
for any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}L_{t_{j}}^{(z)}(B_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}L_{t_{j}}(B_{j})

for any
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. This proves
the desired spatial homogeneity of
L
. ∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
u
denote the solution to the equation (
1.2
).
Then, the random field
u
satisfies the property
(S)
.
In particular,
u
is strictly
(
spatially
)
stationary in the sense that
for any
(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,(t_{k},x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}

(k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1})
and for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.2)

\big(u(t_{1},x_{1}+z),\ldots,u(t_{k},x_{k}+z)\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{=}}\big(u(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,u(t_{k},x_{k})\big).

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the Picard iteration sequence given by (
3.3
).
Since
u_{n}(t,x)\to u(t,x)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
,
it suffices to show that
u_{n}
satisfies the property (S)
for any
n\geq 0
.
We will prove this by induction on
n\geq 0
.

For
n=0
, the result is clear, since
u_{0}=1
and
L
is spatially homogeneous
in view of Lemma
5.2
.
For the induction step, we use the same argument as in the proof of

[
17
, Lemma 18]
.
This argument is based on the fact that the stochastic integral with respect to
L

has the following homogeneity property:
for any predictable process
g\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(s,y)L(ds,dy)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{(z)}(s,y)L^{(z)}(ds,dy),

where
g^{(z)},L^{(z)}
are defined as in (
5.1
).
To prove (
5.3
), we first consider the case where

g
is an elementary process of form (
2.6
).
In this case, we have
g^{(z)}(s,y)=X\mathbf{1}_{(a,b]}(s)\mathbf{1}_{B-z}(y)

and

\mbox{RHS of \eqref{homog-int}}=XL^{(z)}\big((a,b]\times(B-z)\big)=XL\big((a,b]\times B\big)=\mbox{LHS of \eqref{homog-int}}.

That is, (
5.3
) is proved for elementary processes, while
the general case follows by a standard approximation argument.
Now suppose that
u_{n}
satisfies the property (S), i.e.,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\text{$(u_{n}^{(z)},L^{(z)})$ has the same finite-dimensional distributions
as $(u_{n},L)$}.

From
(
3.3
)

u_{n+1}(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g_{t,x}(s,y)L(ds,dy).

with
g_{t,x}(s,y)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,t)}(s)G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma(u_{n}(s,y))
,
we see that
g_{t,x}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
is predictable
and we can deduce from (
5.3
) and (
5.4
)
that
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, for any
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}
,
and for any
(s_{j},t_{j},x_{j},B_{j})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}u^{(z)}_{n+1}(t_{j},x_{j})+\beta_{j}L^{(z)}(s_{j},B_{j})\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}g_{t_{j},x_{j}+z}(s,y)+\beta_{j}\mathbf{1}_{[0,s_{j}]\times(z+B_{j})}(s,y)\big]L(ds,dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}g_{t_{j},x_{j}+z}(s,y+z)+\beta_{j}\mathbf{1}_{[0,s_{j}]\times(z+B_{j})}(s,y+z)\big]L^{(z)}(ds,dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}G_{t_{j}-s}(x_{j}-y)\sigma(u_{n}^{(z)}(s,y))+\beta_{j}\mathbf{1}_{[0,s_{j}]\times B_{j}}(s,y)\big]L^{(z)}(ds,dy)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}G_{t_{j}-s}(x_{j}-y)\sigma(u_{n}(s,y))+\beta_{j}\mathbf{1}_{[0,s_{j}]\times B_{j}}(s,y)\big]L(ds,dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}u_{n+1}(t_{j},x_{j})+\beta_{j}L(s_{j},B_{j})\big].

This concludes our proof.
∎

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The proof consists of four parts as indicated by the statement of Theorem
1.2
.

\bullet

Part (i):
spatial ergodicity
.

For any fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
the strict stationarity of
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
follows from
Lemma
5.3
immediately.
The spatial ergodicity follows immediately from
[
9
, Lemma 4.2]

and the key estimate (
3.12
) (with
p=2
). The argument is identical to that in
in the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 1.1-(i)]
and thus omitted here.
See also
[
9
, Remark 1.4]
for the discussion on the almost sure convergence and

L^{2}(\Omega)
-convergence in (
1.13
). Note that the proof of part (i) only requires

m_{2}<\infty
.

\square

Recall from (
1.11
) that

F_{R}(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}[u(t,x)-1]dx.

In the following, we will establish the limiting covariance structure of the process
F_{R}
.

\bullet

Part (ii):
limiting covariance structure
.

Fix any
t,s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Using (
5.2
) and
\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)]=1
,
we can write

\mathbb{E}\big[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)\big]=\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}{\rm Cov}\big(u(t,x),u(s,y)\big)dxdy=\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\rho_{t,s}(x-y)dxdy,

where
\rho_{t,s}(x-y):={\rm Cov}\big(u(t,x),u(s,y)\big)
depends on
x,y

only through their difference.
By the dominated convergence theorem, we can obtain (
1.14
), since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)]

\displaystyle=\int_{|x|<2R}\rho_{t,s}(x)\left(1-\frac{|x|}{2R}\right)dx

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{R\to+\infty}2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t,s}(x)dx=:K(t,s)

provided that

(5.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\rho_{t,s}(x)|dx<\infty.

Let us verify the condition (
5.5
) now.
From (
2.10
) and (
3.12
), we deduce that

\displaystyle|\rho_{t,s}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\|D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)\|_{2}\|D_{r,y,z}u(s,0)\|_{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim m_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y)dydr,

and here we assume
m_{2}<\infty
in part (ii). With
G_{t-r}=0
for
r\geq t

and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t}(x)dx=t
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
we deduce immediately that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\rho_{t,s}(x)|dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x-y)G_{s-r}(y)dydr\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}(t-r)(s-r)dr<+\infty.

Therefore, the limit (
1.14
) holds true.
Next,
we prove that
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t):=\mathbb{E}[F^{2}_{R}(t)]>0
for all
t>0
and
R>0
.
We proceed as in
[
19
, Lemma 3.4]
.
Assume that
\sigma_{R_{0}}^{2}(t_{0})=0
for some
t_{0}>0
and
R_{0}>0
.
Using (
1.5
), the isometry property (
2.8
),
and the stationarity of
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\sigma_{R_{0}}^{2}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(u(t_{0},x_{1})-1\big)\big(u(t_{0},x_{2})-1\big)\big]dx_{1}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=m_{2}\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t_{0}-s}(x_{1}-y)G_{t_{0}-s}(x_{2}-y)\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(s,y))|^{2}\big]dydsdx_{1}dx_{2}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=m_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(s,0))|^{2}\big]\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\int_{-R_{0}}^{R_{0}}G_{t_{0}-s}(x-y)dx\Big)^{2}dy\bigg]ds.

It is easy to see that the integral (with respect to
dy
) in the big bracket in (
5.6
)
is strictly positive unless
s\geq t_{0}
.
Recall that the function
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(s,0))|^{2}\big]
is continuous
due to the
L^{2}
-continuity of
u
and Lipschitz property of
\sigma
,
and thus, we have
\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(s,0))|^{2}\big]=0
for any
s\in[0,t_{0}].

In particular,

0=\mathbb{E}\big[|\sigma(u(0,0))|^{2}\big]=|\sigma(1)|^{2},

which is a contradiction to our standing assumption that
\sigma(1)\neq 0
.
This concludes the proof of part (ii).

\square

\bullet

Part (iii):
quantitative CLT
.

Assume
m_{4}<+\infty
in the following. We will apply Theorem
4.1

to prove the bound (
1.15
).

Let
X=F_{R}(t)/\sigma_{R}(t)
. Since

\displaystyle u(t,x)-1

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}G_{t-s}(x-y)z\sigma(u(s,y))\widehat{N}(ds,dy,dz)

\displaystyle=\delta(V_{0})\quad\text{with $V_{0}(s,y,z)=G_{t-s}(x-y)z\sigma(u(s,y))$}

in view of Lemma
2.9
-(ii) and convention (
1.6
).
Recall that
\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)
is defined by (
1.21
), and vanishes when
r\geq t
.

Now, we can write by stochastic Fubini that

(5.7)

\displaystyle X=\delta(V_{t,R})/\sigma_{R}(t)\quad\text{with $V_{t,R}(s,y,z)=\varphi_{t,R}(s,y)z\sigma(u(s,y))$;}

see
[
9
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Note that
X
is centered with variance one. Then, we apply
Theorem
4.1
to write

(5.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &d_{\rm Wass}\big(X,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\big)\\
&\lesssim\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle DX,\tfrac{V_{t,R}}{\sigma_{R}(t)}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}_{R}(t)}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|(D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(t))^{2}V_{t,R}(r,y,z)\|_{1}\,drdy\nu(dz)\\
&=:\mathbf{T}_{1}+\mathbf{T}_{2}.\end{aligned}

Let us first deal with the second term
\mathbf{T}_{2}
.

(5.9)

\displaystyle D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)dx,

and thus by Proposition
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\|D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(t)\|_{4}\leq\int_{-R}^{R}\|D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)\|_{4}dx\lesssim|z|\varphi_{t,R}(r,y).

Therefore, it follows from the above discussion, Cauchy-Schwarz, and (
1.9
) that

(5.10)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{T}_{2}&\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}_{R}(t)}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(t)\|^{2}_{4}\cdot\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)|z|\cdot\|\sigma(u(s,y))\|_{2}\,drdy\nu(dz)\\
&\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{3}\varphi^{3}_{t,R}(r,y)\,drdy\nu(dz).\end{aligned}

Then, using
m_{3}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{3}\nu(dz)<\infty
(due to
m_{2},m_{4}<\infty
)
and

\varphi^{3}_{t,R}(r,y)\leq t^{2}\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)
,
we deduce from (
5.10
) that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{T}_{2}&\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t-r}(x-y)dx\bigg)\,drdy\\
&\lesssim R^{-\frac{1}{2}},\end{aligned}

where the last inequality follows by performing integration in the order
of
dy,dx
,
dr
, and the implicit constants in (
5.11
)
depend on
\sigma,t
but not on
R
.

Now let us bound the first term in (
5.8
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{T_{1}}=\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle DX,V_{t,R}/\sigma_{R}(t)\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{R}(t)}\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\langle DF_{R}(t),V_{t,R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})}.

Then, in view of
\sigma^{2}_{R}(t)\asymp R
,
it suffices to prove

(5.12)

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(\langle DF_{R}(t),V_{t,R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}})\lesssim R.

First, we decompose the inner product using (
5.9
) and
the integral equation (
3.2
)
for
D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)
:

(5.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle DF_{R}(t),V_{t,R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}&=\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}D_{r,y,z}u(t,x)dx\bigg)\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)drdy\nu(dz)\\
&=\mathbf{B}_{1}+\mathbf{B}_{2},\end{aligned}

where, with
\varphi_{t,R}
as in (
1.21
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{1}:

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\big|\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)\big|^{2}drdy\nu(dz)

\displaystyle=m_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)\big|^{2}drdy,

and

(5.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{2}:

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)drdy\nu(dz)

with

(5.15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{J}(r,y,z)&:=\int_{-R}^{R}\bigg[\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}G_{t-s_{1}}(x-y_{1})z_{1}D_{r,y,z}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\widehat{N}(ds_{1},dy_{1},dz_{1})\bigg]dx\\
&=\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\varphi_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})z_{1}D_{r,y,z}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\widehat{N}(ds_{1},dy_{1},dz_{1}).\end{aligned}

Then, in view of

(5.16)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{1}+\mathbf{B}_{2})}\leq\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{1})}+\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{2})},

we only need to prove

\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{k})\lesssim R
for
k=1,2
.

\star

Estimation of
\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{1})
.

Observe first that for a jointly measurable
random field
\{\Psi(r,y):(r,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\}

such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\|\Psi(r,y)\|_{2}^{2}drdy<\infty
,
we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\textup{Var}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi(r,y)drdy\bigg)}&=\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\Psi(r,y)-\mathbb{E}[\Psi(r,y)]\big)drdy\bigg\|_{2}\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\Big\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\Psi(r,y)-\mathbb{E}[\Psi(r,y)]\big)dy\Big\|_{2}dr\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\rm Cov}(\Psi(r,y),\Psi(r,y^{\prime})\big)dydy^{\prime}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}dr.\end{aligned}

Note that with
\Psi(r,y)=\big|\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)\big|^{2}
,
we can deduce from Lemma
2.10
and Corollary
3.4

that

(5.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{\rm Cov}(\Psi(r,y),\Psi(r,y^{\prime})\big)=\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime}){\rm Cov}\big(\sigma^{2}(u(r,y)),\sigma^{2}(u(r,y^{\prime}))\big)\\
&\leq\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})\cdot C_{t}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}G_{r-r_{0}}(y-y_{0})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times G_{r-r_{0}}(y^{\prime}-y_{0})|z_{0}|^{2}(1+|z_{0}|)^{2}dr_{0}dy_{0}\nu(dz_{0})\\
&\leq C_{t}(m_{2}+m_{4})\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})\cdot\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{r-r_{0}}(y-y_{0})G_{r-r_{0}}(y^{\prime}-y_{0})dr_{0}dy_{0},\end{aligned}

where
C_{t}
is the constant in (
3.23
). Using the triangle inequality,
we have the following rough bound

G_{r-r_{0}}(y-y_{0})G_{r-r_{0}}(y^{\prime}-y_{0})\leq G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime})G_{r}(y-y_{0}),

from which, we deduce that

\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{r-r_{0}}(y-y_{0})G_{r-r_{0}}(y^{\prime}-y_{0})dr_{0}dy_{0}\leq r^{2}G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime})\leq t^{2}G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime}).

That is, we have

{\rm Cov}(\Psi(r,y),\Psi(r,y^{\prime})\big)\lesssim\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime}),

with the implicit constant depending on
t
. Thus, it follows from
(
5.17
) that

(5.19)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{1})}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime})dydy^{\prime}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}dr.

Since
\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)=\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t-r}(x-y)dx\leq t\mathbf{1}_{\{r<t\}}

and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)dy=2(t-r)R\mathbf{1}_{\{r<t\}}
,
we have

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime})dydy^{\prime}&\leq t^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)G_{2r}(y-y^{\prime})dydy^{\prime}\\
&\leq 4t^{5}R,\end{aligned}

where the last step follows by performing integration first in
y^{\prime}
and then in
y
.
Combining (
5.19
) and (
5.20
) yields

(5.21)

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{1})\lesssim R.

\star

Estimation of
\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{2})
.

Recall from (
5.14
) and (
5.15
) the expressions of
\mathbf{B}_{2}

and
\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)
.
Then, it follows from (
5.17
) that

(5.22)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{2})}&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\sigma\big(u(r,y)\big)dy\nu(dz)\bigg\|_{2}\,dr\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}_{r}}\,dr,\end{aligned}

where
\mathcal{H}_{r}
denotes the second moment of the integral over
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{r}:

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)\mathcal{J}(r,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\sigma(u(r,y))\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\varphi_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})z^{\prime}dydy^{\prime}\nu(dz)\nu(dz^{\prime}).

Let us first estimate the term

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)\mathcal{J}(r,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\sigma(u(r,y))\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))]
.
Since
\sigma(u(r,y))
,
\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))
are
\mathcal{F}_{r}
-measurable,
we deduce from Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.9
-(iv) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(u(r,y))\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))\mathcal{J}(r,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\mathcal{J}(r,y,z)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\sigma(u(r,y))\varphi_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})z_{1}D_{r,y,z}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\widehat{N}(ds_{1},dy_{1},dz_{1})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))\varphi_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})z_{1}D_{r,y^{\prime},z^{\prime}}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\widehat{N}(ds_{1},dy_{1},dz_{1})\bigg]

\displaystyle=m_{2}\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma(u(r,y))\sigma(u(r,y^{\prime}))\big(D_{r,y,z}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\big)D_{r,y^{\prime},z^{\prime}}\sigma(u(s_{1},y_{1}))\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})ds_{1}dy_{1},

whose modulus is bounded by

\displaystyle\lesssim|zz^{\prime}|\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y)G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y^{\prime})\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})ds_{1}dy_{1}

using Hölder’s inequality, (
3.12
) with
p=4
, (
2.18
), and (
1.9
).
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{r}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y)G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y^{\prime})\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)\varphi_{t,R}(r,y^{\prime})ds_{1}dy_{1}dydy^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y)G_{s_{1}-r}(y_{1}-y^{\prime})ds_{1}dy_{1}dydy^{\prime}\quad\text{(using $\varphi_{t,R}\leq t$)}

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{t}(s_{1}-r)^{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{t,R}(s_{1},y_{1})dy_{1}\bigg)ds_{1}=2R\int_{r}^{t}(s_{1}-r)^{2}(t-s_{1})ds_{1}\lesssim R.

Note that the above implicit constant does not depend on
r
, so that we deduce
easily from (
5.22
) that

(5.23)

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(\mathbf{B}_{2})\lesssim R.

Hence, the desired bound (
5.12
)
follows from (
5.13
), (
5.16
), (
5.21
), and
(
5.23
).
That is, we have
\mathbf{T}_{1}\lesssim 1/\sqrt{R}
,
which together with (
5.11
) and (
5.8
),
implies the bound (
1.15
).
This concludes our proof of the quantitative CLT.

\square

\bullet

Part (iv):
functional CLT
.

We first establish the convergence of finite-dimensional distributions (f.d.d. convergence)
and then prove the tightness.

\star

f.d.d. convergence.

For the first step, it suffices to show that as
R\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(t_{1}),\ldots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(t_{m})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\big(\mathcal{G}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{G}(t_{m})\big)

for any
0\leq t_{1}<\ldots<t_{m}
. By the Cramér-Wold theorem,
it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle X_{R}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}F_{R}(t_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{\longrightarrow}}X:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\mathcal{G}(t_{j})

for any
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. From (
1.14
),
it follows that

\tau_{R}^{2}:={\rm Var}(X_{R})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}b_{i}b_{j}\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t_{i})F_{R}(t_{j})]\xrightarrow{R\to+\infty}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}b_{i}b_{j}K(t_{i},t_{j})=:\tau^{2}.

The rest of the proof is trivial if
\tau^{2}=0
. We assume that
\tau^{2}>0
,
and thus,
\tau_{R}>0
for
R
large. In this case, it suffices to show

\frac{X_{R}}{\tau_{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(law)}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad\mbox{as $R\to\infty$},

and in view of
[
32
, Proposition C.3.1]
,
we will prove the following stronger statement:

(5.24)

\lim_{R\to+\infty}d_{\rm Wass}\big(X_{R}/\tau_{R},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\big)=0.

To prove (
5.24
), we apply the Malliavin-Stein bound (
4.5
) to the variable

G_{R}=X_{R}/\tau_{R}
, which is centered and of variance
1
.
Moreover, as in (
5.7
), we can write

F_{R}(t)=\delta(V_{t,R})
with
V_{t,R}(r,y,z):=\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)z\sigma(u(r,y))
,

where
\varphi_{t,R}
is given in (
1.21
).
Then, we have
G_{R}=\delta(U_{R})
with

U_{R}=\frac{1}{\tau_{R}\sqrt{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}V_{t_{j},R}.

Therefore, the Malliavin-Stein bound (
4.5
) implies that

(5.25)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\big(X_{R}/\tau_{R},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\big)\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}\big(\langle DG_{R},U_{R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)}+\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|(D_{\xi}G_{R})^{2}U_{R}(\xi)\big\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

Since

\langle DG_{R},U_{R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{R}^{2}R}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}b_{i}b_{j}\langle DF_{R}(t_{i}),V_{t_{j},R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}
,
we deduce from Minkowski’s inequality (i.e.,

\sqrt{\textup{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i})}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y_{i})}

for square-integrable random variables
Y_{i}
’s)
that

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\rm Var}\big(\langle DG_{R},U_{R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)}\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{R}^{2}R}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|b_{i}b_{j}|\sqrt{{\rm Var}\big(\langle DF_{R}(t_{i}),V_{t_{j},R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)}.

Recall from (
5.12
) that

\textup{Var}\big(\langle DF_{R}(t_{i}),V_{t_{j},R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)\lesssim R

for
t_{i}=t_{j}=t
,
provided that
m_{4}<\infty
.
Indeed, the same argument (with minor modifications) can be used to show
the same bound for
t_{i}\neq t_{j}
, so that
with
\tau_{R}\to\tau>0
,
we have

(5.26)

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\rm Var}\big(\langle DG_{R},U_{R}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)}\lesssim R^{-1/2}

as
R
tends to infinity.

Next, we examine the second term in (
5.25
).
First, we write using the definitions of
G_{R},U_{R}

and the elementary inequality
(b_{1}+...+b_{m})^{2}\leq m(b_{1}^{2}+...+b_{m}^{2})

that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|(D_{\xi}G_{R})^{2}U_{R}(\xi)\big\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{\tau_{R}^{3}R^{3/2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|b_{i}|b_{j}^{2}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|(D_{\xi}F_{R}(t_{j}))^{2}V_{t_{i},R}(\xi)\big\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

Note that we have proved
that

\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|(D_{\xi}F_{R}(t_{j}))^{2}V_{t_{i},R}(\xi)\big\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\lesssim R

for
t_{i}=t_{j}=t
;
see (
5.8
) and (
5.11
) with
\sigma_{R}(t)\asymp\sqrt{R}
.
The same argument with very minor modifications
can lead to the same bound for
t_{i}\neq t_{j}
, and thus,

(5.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|(D_{\xi}G_{R})^{2}U_{R}(\xi)\big\|_{1}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)\lesssim R^{-1/2}

as
R
tends to infinity.

Hence, (
5.24
) follows from (
5.25
), (
5.26
), and (
5.27
),
which verifies the convergence of finite-dimensional distributions.

\star

By

)

.
The exactly same argument, together with Remark

More precisely,

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

We spilt the section into two parts: in the first part, we prove the bound
(
1.20
) and address the asymptotic independence between the spatial
integral (for a fixed time) and any finite-dimensional marginals of the random field
solution
u
; in the second part, we establish the asymptotic independence
result for the finite-dimensional marginals of
\{F_{R}(t)/\sqrt{R}:t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
and those of
u
.

\bullet

Part
I
.

We apply the multivariate Malliavin-Stein bound (
4.18
)
with

(5.29)

X=X_{R}=\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)}=\delta(V_{t,R}/\sigma_{R}(t))
as in (
5.7
)
and

\mathbf{Y}=\big(u(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,u(t_{d},x_{d})\big).

That is, we have,
with
Z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independent of
\mathbf{Y}
,

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\big((X,\mathbf{Y}),(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\|1-\langle DX,V_{t,R}/\sigma_{R}(t)\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}_{R}(t)}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|V_{t,R}(\xi)(D_{\xi}F_{R}(t))^{2}\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma_{R}(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|V_{t,R}(\xi)D_{\xi}u(t_{j},x_{j})\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi).

As already pointed out in Remark
4.5
,
the first two terms quantifies the proximity to normality of
X

and we have already established that they are bounded by
\lesssim 1/\sqrt{R}
.
It remains to bound the third term.

From (
5.7
) and (
1.21
),
we have

V_{t,R}(s,y,z)=\varphi_{t,R}(s,y)z\sigma(u(s,y))
,
so that

\|V_{t,R}(s,y,z)\|_{2}\lesssim\varphi_{t,R}(s,y)|z|,

in view of (
1.9
).
Next, applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the key estimate (
3.12
),
we can obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\big\|V_{t,R}(\xi)D_{\xi}u(t_{j},x_{j})\big\|_{1}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{Z}}\|V_{t,R}(\xi)\|_{2}\|D_{\xi}u(t_{j},x_{j})\|_{2}\,\mathfrak{m}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t\wedge t_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\varphi_{t,R}(s,y)G_{t_{j}-s}(x_{j}-y)|z|^{2}\,dsdy\nu(dz)\lesssim 1,

where the last step follows
from
\varphi_{t,R}(s,y)\leq t
,
m_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty
,
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t_{j}-s}(x_{j}-y)dy=t_{j}-s
for
s<t_{j}
.
Thus, with
\sigma_{R}(t)\asymp\sqrt{R}
, we can prove the bound (
1.20
).
Then the asymptotic independence of
X_{R}
and
\mathbf{Y}
(in the sense of
(
1.18
))
follows easily by a triangle inequality:

(5.30)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm Wass}\big((X_{R},\mathbf{Y}),\big(\widehat{X}_{R},\mathbf{Y})\big)&\leq d_{\rm Wass}\big((X_{R},\mathbf{Y}),\big(Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y})\big)+d_{\rm Wass}\big((Z^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}),\big(\widehat{X}_{R},\mathbf{Y})\big)\\
&\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{R}}+d_{\rm Wass}(\widehat{X}_{R},Z^{\prime})\lesssim R^{-\frac{1}{2}},\end{aligned}

where (i)
\widehat{X}_{R}
is a copy of
X_{R}
that is independent of
\mathbf{Y}
,
(ii) the second inequality follows from the definition of Wasserstein distance (
1.12
),
and (iii) the last inequality follows from Theorem
1.2
.
It is then clear that the condition (
1.18
) is satisfied,
as a consequence of (
5.30
).

\bullet

Part
I I
.
We proceed by proving the asymptotic independence
result for the finite-dimensional distributions of the spatial integral process
and the random field solution.
This can be done by applying the Malliavin-Stein bound as in

Part
I

with
X
replaced by finite linear combinations
of marginals of
F_{R}(\bullet)/\sqrt{R}
as in the proof of f.d.d. convergences
(see
Part (iv)
in Section
5.2
).
In what follows, we proceed differently by directing proving (
1.18
) using
the Poincaré inequality (Lemma
2.10
).

Put
\mathbf{X}_{R}=R^{-\frac{1}{2}}(F_{R}(s_{1}),...,F_{R}(s_{j}))

for any given
s_{1},...,s_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. Let
\mathbf{Y}
be as in (
5.29
),
and let
f,g
be Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d}
respectively (not necessarily bounded).
Next, we show

(5.31)

\displaystyle\text{\rm Cov}\big(f(\mathbf{X}_{R}),g(\mathbf{Y})\big)\xrightarrow{R\to+\infty}0.

Indeed, it first
follows from Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
2.5
with (
2.17
)
that

\displaystyle\text{\rm Cov}\big(f(\mathbf{X}_{R}),g(\mathbf{Y})\big)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\|D_{r,y,z}f(\mathbf{X}_{R})\|_{2}\|D_{r,y,z}g(\mathbf{Y})\|_{2}drdy\nu(dz)

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{j}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\|D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(s_{i})\|_{2}\|D_{r,y,z}u(t_{i^{\prime}},x_{i^{\prime}})\|_{2}drdy\nu(dz).

Using
\|D_{r,y,z}F_{R}(s_{i})\|_{2}\leq\int_{-R}^{R}\|D_{r,y,z}u(s_{i},x)\|_{2}dx

and (

), we can further write

\displaystyle\text{\rm Cov}\big(f(\mathbf{X}_{R}),g(\mathbf{Y})\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}G_{s_{i}-r}(x-y)dx\bigg)G_{t_{i^{\prime}}-r}(x_{i^{\prime}}-y)|z|^{2}drdy\nu(dz)

where the last step follows from the fact that

.
This proves (
5.31
)
and concludes our proof of Theorem
1.5
.

\square
