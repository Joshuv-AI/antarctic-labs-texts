Mad Honey Poisoning Caused by Grayanotoxin Toxicity in a 75-Year-Old Male Patient Presenting With Hypotension and Bradycardia in the United Kingdom: A Case Report
Authors: Ahmed Alkhafaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mad honey contains the neurotoxin grayanotoxin, which is produced by the flowers and nectar of the Rhododendron family. Bees incorporate this toxin into honey. Mad honey is consumed as a medicinal remedy in certain parts of the world, leading to persistent vagal activation and potentially serious complications, including hemodynamic instability. We report the case of a 75-year-old male patient with a medical history of hyperlipidemia and hypertension (managed with atorvastatin 20 mg and amlodipine 5 mg, respectively) who presented to the emergency department complaining of near-syncope, dizziness, lethargy, nausea, and one episode of diarrhea one hour after ingesting 20-30 mL of wild honey brought as a gift from abroad. On presentation, he was conscious and alert. Triage observations revealed a blood pressure of 65/41 mmHg, a heart rate of 45 beats/minute, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths/minute, an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air, and a temperature of 36.8°C. Physical examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus bradycardia. The patient was resuscitated with intravenous fluids and 0.6 mg of intravenous atropine, which normalized his heart rate and blood pressure within seconds. He was admitted for observation for 36 hours and was discharged without sequelae. Mad honey poisoning is rare in the United Kingdom and requires a high index of suspicion. Symptoms of hypotension and bradycardia following honey consumption should alert clinicians to this poisoning. Treatment with atropine is successful in alleviating symptoms, which typically resolve within 24 hours.

Introduction

Mad honey contains the neurotoxin grayanotoxin, which is produced by the flowers and nectar of plants in the Rhododendron family endemic to areas including Nepal, Turkey, and regions of the Himalayas. Bees incorporate this toxin into the honey they produce [1].

Mad honey is commonly consumed as a medicinal remedy in certain parts of the world, where it is believed to have therapeutic benefits for hypertension, diabetes, and sexual dysfunction. However, ingestion can lead to persistent vagal activation and potentially serious cardiovascular complications [1].

Currently, many reported cases are accidental or result from consuming honey purchased from unregulated sources, often brought back as souvenirs from endemic regions. With growing interest in natural honey products, honey poisoning may become a global problem. The toxic dose of grayanotoxin is variable, but symptoms typically occur after ingestion of approximately 15-30 mL of mad honey [2].

Certain Rhododendron species contain higher concentrations of grayanotoxin than others, with Rhododendron ponticum and Rhododendron luteum being among the most toxic [3]. In the United Kingdom, mad honey poisoning remains exceptionally rare. A comprehensive literature search identified no published case reports originating from the UK. A 2015 systematic review of 1,199 global cases confirmed that the United Kingdom did not report any cases of mad honey poisoning [2].

This case report highlights the importance of considering mad honey poisoning in patients presenting with unexplained bradycardia and hypotension, particularly when there is a history of recent honey ingestion or travel from endemic regions.

Case presentation

The case reported in this study is a 75-year-old man who presented to the emergency department complaining of near-syncope, dizziness, lethargy, nausea, and one episode of diarrhea one hour after ingesting 20-30 mL of raw wild honey. There were no other symptoms or complaints. The honey had been brought from Nepal as a gift.

His medical history included hyperlipidemia and hypertension, for which he was only taking atorvastatin 20 mg and amlodipine 5 mg, respectively. The patient and his family did not report the use of any other medications.

On presentation, he was conscious and alert. Triage observations were as follows: blood pressure 65/41 mmHg, heart rate 45 beats per minute, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation 98% on room air, and temperature 36.8°C.

Physical examination revealed no rash, with good peripheral perfusion. Chest auscultation was clear; the abdomen was soft and nontender on palpation, with hyperactive bowel sounds on auscultation; and the cardiovascular examination, aside from bradycardia, was normal. His motor and cranial nerve examinations were normal.

The electrocardiogram (Figure 1) demonstrated sinus bradycardia. Venous blood gas analysis showed a lactate level of 1.5 mmol/L (reference range: 0.5-2.2 mmol/L) and a glucose level of 6.5 mmol/L (reference range: 4-7.8 mmol/L). The full blood count and biochemical investigations (C-reactive protein, troponin, electrolytes, thyroid function, creatinine, and liver function) were essentially within normal limits.

The patient was resuscitated with intravenous fluids and 0.6 mg of intravenous atropine, which rapidly normalized his heart rate to 78 beats/minute and raised his blood pressure to 110/70 mmHg within a few minutes. He was admitted for observation and kept on telemetry for 36 hours. He was discharged home without any sequelae.

Discussion

Pathophysiology

Grayanotoxin binds to voltage-gated sodium channels on neuronal cell membranes, depolarizing them and triggering a cardioinhibitory reflex via the vagus nerve [2,3]. This mechanism manifests clinically as hypotension, bradycardia, and reduced peripheral vascular resistance [2]. Although mad honey poisoning is not a classical cholinergic toxidrome, it does possess cholinergic toxicity and affects the cardiovascular system [3].

Grayanotoxin poisoning may also be associated with gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) and neurological symptoms (dizziness, hallucinations, reduced level of consciousness, seizures) [3,4], although cardiovascular symptoms are the most frequently reported [3,5]. The specific pathway by which grayanotoxin induces emesis and other gastrointestinal symptoms is not explicitly detailed in the available literature.

Diagnosis

Grayanotoxins can be detected in blood, urine, or honey samples through liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry; however, this is primarily performed in research laboratories rather than in routine clinical practice, as no standard testing is available [6,7]. In our case, as testing for grayanotoxin was not available, the diagnosis was made clinically based on the temporal relationship between honey ingestion and symptom onset, along with the characteristic pattern of bradycardia with hypotension, and was supported by the prompt response to treatment. Other causes of bradycardia and hypotension were excluded based on history, physical examination, and investigations.

Treatment

Atropine administration causes rapid improvement in pulse rate, serving as an indicator of reversal of the cardioinhibitory reflex [2]. Intravenous fluid is usually given to help correct hypotension. If atropine and fluids are not adequate to correct bradycardia and hypotension, then epinephrine or dopamine infusion is suggested [3]. Symptoms typically resolve within 24 hours [1,3].

Conclusions

Mad honey poisoning is rare in the United Kingdom and requires a high index of suspicion for timely diagnosis. The presence of hypotension and bradycardia following honey consumption should alert clinicians to this potentially serious poisoning. In symptomatic patients, atropine may rapidly improve bradycardia and hypotension, which typically resolve within 24 hours.

Beyond the immediate clinical management, this case highlights several important lessons for emergency and acute medicine practice in nonendemic regions such as the United Kingdom. First, the globalization of food products means that rare toxicities from abroad can present to any emergency department. Second, the dramatic response to atropine in this case underscores the importance of considering reversible causes of bradycardia.

Finally, this case adds to the very limited body of literature on mad honey poisoning in the United Kingdom. Raising awareness among emergency physicians, general practitioners, and cardiologists may facilitate earlier recognition and treatment of future cases. As international travel and importation of exotic food products continue to increase, clinicians should remain vigilant for this rare but treatable condition.
