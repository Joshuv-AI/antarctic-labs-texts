Pediatric emergency department point-of-care ultrasound diagnosis of unprovoked iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis in an adolescent: A case report
Authors: Emeka Elosiuba, Ellen McCourt, Remy Toko, Samuel Ime Udo, Alain Vella, Livinus Chukwuma Chukwuma, Ken B. Yarfoh, Christopher Nwatuzor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric deep vein thrombosis is uncommon and often presents with nonspecific symptoms that may be mistaken for musculoskeletal conditions. Early diagnosis can be particularly challenging in the pediatric emergency department, as commonly used adult diagnostic tools have limited applicability in children. This case is noteworthy because the thrombosis was unprovoked, occurred in an otherwise healthy adolescent, and was rapidly identified using point-of-care ultrasound, which facilitated timely escalation and coordinated multidisciplinary care. We report the case of a 15-year-old male who presented with progressive pain and swelling of the right lower limb. Initial clinical assessment suggested a musculoskeletal injury. However, point-of-care ultrasound performed in the pediatric emergency department demonstrated loss of venous compressibility and intraluminal echogenic material within the common femoral vein. Subsequent formal duplex ultrasonography confirmed an occlusive thrombus extending from the common femoral vein into the iliac vein. This case emphasizes the diagnostic difficulties posed by deep vein thrombosis in children. It also demonstrates how point-of-care ultrasound, when used at the bedside in the pediatric emergency department, can enable early recognition of proximal thrombosis even when validated adult decision tools are unavailable.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism in children occurs far less frequently than in adults, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.07 to 0.49 per 10 000 children annually [1,2]. Nevertheless, the incidence has risen steadily in recent decades. This increase is attributed primarily to improved survival of children with complex chronic conditions, more widespread use of central venous catheters, and heightened clinical awareness [3]. The majority of pediatric deep vein thrombosis cases develop in the presence of identifiable risk factors, such as central venous catheterization, malignancy, infection, trauma, or congenital heart disease [3,4]. Consequently, unprovoked lower-limb deep vein thrombosis in an otherwise healthy adolescent remains relatively uncommon.

Clinical manifestations of pediatric deep vein thrombosis are often non-specific and may resemble common musculoskeletal or infectious conditions. This similarity frequently results in diagnostic delay [4,5]. Unlike in adults, validated clinical prediction rules such as the Wells score have not been established for children, and the diagnostic utility of D-dimer testing is limited in pediatric practice [6]. Imaging therefore assumes a central role in confirming the diagnosis.

Compression duplex ultrasonography remains the first-line imaging modality for suspected lower-limb venous thrombosis, offering high sensitivity and specificity for proximal thrombi [7]. In recent years, point-of-care ultrasound has been increasingly integrated into pediatric emergency medicine, allowing rapid bedside evaluation before formal confirmatory imaging [8]. We describe a case of extensive iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis in a previously healthy adolescent. The possibility was first raised by point-of-care ultrasound in the pediatric emergency department and later verified on formal compression duplex ultrasonography. This early recognition allowed rapid escalation of care and effective coordination among multiple specialties, ultimately leading to a good clinical outcome.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old male presented to the pediatric emergency department with a 1-week history of progressive pain in the right hip and leg. The pain was sharp in character and radiated from the thigh to the ankle. He described the severity as 8 out of 10. He also reported increasing swelling of the affected limb and difficulty with weight bearing. There was no history of trauma, fever, respiratory symptoms, recent travel, or systemic illness. The patient had been previously healthy and participated actively in sports, particularly football. He had no personal history of thrombophilia, recent immobilization, or limb injury, and no family history of clotting disorders. Before hospital presentation, he had attended primary care on 2 occasions, where the symptoms had been attributed to a musculoskeletal injury and managed with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication without improvement.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Respiratory and cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable. The right lower limb showed diffuse swelling extending from the pelvis to the foot, with increased warmth and mild pitting oedema at the ankle. The limb appeared tense and darkly pigmented, although the toes remained pink with preserved capillary refill time of less than 2 seconds. Peripheral pulses were palpable, sensation was intact in all dermatomes, and movement was preserved in all directions. There was tenderness over the calf and thigh but no bony or joint tenderness. No clinical evidence of compartment syndrome was present.

Initial laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, inflammatory markers, creatine kinase, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation profile, were all within normal limits. In view of the clinical suspicion for venous thrombosis, bedside point-of-care compression ultrasound of the right leg was performed in the emergency department. The patient was admitted for anticoagulation and multidisciplinary review. Formal duplex ultrasound confirmed an occlusive thrombus in the right common femoral vein extending proximally into the right iliac vein. Therapeutic anticoagulation was commenced with low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin). Anti-Xa levels were monitored, and after 5 days the patient was transitioned to therapeutic-dose rivaroxaban in accordance with local hospital protocol. Supportive measures included limb elevation, analgesia, graduated compression stockings, and physiotherapy. Contact sports were avoided during treatment.

During the admission and subsequent outpatient follow-up, the patient developed sustained hypertension that required treatment with low-dose amlodipine. Comprehensive investigations, including urinalysis, serum cortisol, thyroid function tests, serum metanephrines, catecholamines, haemoglobinopathy screening, autoimmune markers, and full thrombophilia screen (including lupus anticoagulant), returned normal results. Repeat imaging studies, including abdominopelvic ultrasound, inferior vena cava assessment, renal artery doppler, and urinary tract ultrasound, demonstrated no abnormalities.

A surveillance ultrasound performed 4 months later showed chronic eccentric partial thrombus within the common femoral vein extending distally into the femoral vein and proximally into the external iliac vein (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The deep femoral vein, popliteal vein, and small saphenous vein remained patent (Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11). The mid and distal femoral vein segments were free of thrombus (Fig. 9, Fig. 10), and there was no extension into the great saphenous vein (Fig. 12, Fig. 13). The common iliac vein and inferior vena cava were partly obscured by bowel gas, although the inferior vena cava appeared normal at the hepatic level (Fig. 14). Given the marked clinical improvement—complete resolution of limb swelling, reduced pain, improved limb coloration, preserved neurovascular function, and full weight-bearing without limp—and the absence of progression to phlegmasia, the multidisciplinary team, in discussion with the parents and patient, decided against catheter-directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy. The risk–benefit balance did not favor interventional procedures such as catheter-directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy. Similarly, computed tomography or magnetic resonance venography was not performed, as ultrasound had adequately defined the thrombus extent. Additionally, considering his clinical progression, there was no suspicion of May–Thurner syndrome, a rare anatomical variant associated with left-sided iliofemoral thrombus [1,4,5,8].

Anticoagulation was continued with planned long-term follow-up by the multidisciplinary team comprising pediatrics, pediatric hematology, vascular surgery, interventional radiology, physiotherapy, and the general practitioner.

Discussion

The lower-limb deep venous system is well organized, draining from the plantar venous plexuses and calf veins through the paired tibial and peroneal veins into the popliteal vein, then the femoral vein and common femoral vein, before continuing via the external and common iliac veins into the inferior vena cava. The great saphenous vein joins the common femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction, while the small saphenous vein typically drains into the popliteal vein within the popliteal fossa. These anatomical tributaries provide potential pathways for thrombus propagation.

Deep vein thrombosis in children, although uncommon, carries significant clinical importance. Most pediatric thrombotic events are associated with identifiable risk factors such as central venous catheters, infection, malignancy, trauma, or surgery [3,[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Unprovoked thrombosis in otherwise healthy adolescents, as observed in the present case, constitutes a smaller subset and may be overlooked during initial assessment [2,4].

Children with deep vein thrombosis usually complain of limb pain, swelling, warmth and erythema [4,14]. In active adolescents these symptoms overlap considerably with much more common problems such as muscle or ligament injury, cellulitis or soft-tissue infection [15]. For this reason, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion whenever a child or adolescent presents with unilateral leg swelling and pain [8,10,16].

In adults, clinical decision rules and laboratory tests such as the Wells score and D-dimer are routinely used. However, these tools have not been validated in children and may lack specificity in the pediatric population [6,17,18]. Current pediatric guidelines therefore emphasize the importance of clinical suspicion combined with appropriate imaging rather than reliance on adult-derived scoring systems [5,10,19,20].

Ultrasound imaging remains the primary diagnostic modality for suspected lower-limb thrombosis in children [7,21,22]. Characteristic findings on compression duplex ultrasonography include loss of venous compressibility, intraluminal echogenic material, and abnormal or absent Doppler flow [[21], [22], [23]]. The characteristic “Mickey Mouse sign” formed by the common femoral vein, common femoral artery, and great saphenous vein, provides a useful landmark for systematic scanning [8,22].

Point-of-care ultrasound performed by trained emergency physicians has emerged as a valuable tool for the early detection of proximal deep vein thrombosis [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Focused 2-point compression protocols can achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to formal radiology-performed studies for clinically significant proximal thrombi [23,24]. In the present case, bedside ultrasound in the emergency department identified loss of compressibility and intraluminal thrombus in the common femoral vein, prompting rapid formal confirmation and initiation of treatment.

Early diagnosis is critical because proximal iliofemoral thrombosis is associated with an increased risk of pulmonary embolism and post-thrombotic syndrome [10,11]. Post-thrombotic syndrome, characterized by chronic limb pain, swelling, and venous insufficiency, has been reported in 20%-40% of children following lower-limb thrombosis [10,14]. Prompt anticoagulation is therefore recommended once the diagnosis is established [3,9]. Low-molecular-weight heparin remains the most used initial agent in pediatric practice because of its predictable pharmacokinetics [3,11]. Direct oral anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban are increasingly used in selected patients under specialist supervision [9,11,12].

Routine thrombolysis, thrombectomy, or inferior vena cava filter placement is not recommended in children and is reserved for life-, limb-, or organ-threatening cases. Similarly, advanced cross-sectional imaging is generally avoided when ultrasound has clearly delineated the thrombus extent and there is no suspicion of anatomical predisposing factors [1,3,5].

This case underscores the importance of considering deep vein thrombosis in the differential diagnosis of unilateral limb swelling and pain in children and adolescents, even in the absence of classic risk factors. It also illustrates the potential value of point-of-care ultrasound as an adjunct that can expedite diagnosis and definitive management in the pediatric emergency setting.

Pediatric emergency department lower limb compression duplex ultrasonography: local approach

The following protocol reflects local practice in our pediatric emergency department and may not apply in every setting. Ideally, lower-limb compression duplex ultrasonography should be undertaken only by clinicians who have undergone specific training and who maintain their competence in the procedure. In situations of high acuity, immediate clinical needs must take priority over scanning. When departmental staffing is stretched, it is preferable to refer the patient directly to the inpatient pediatric team for organized radiology imaging. Importantly, any point-of-care scan performed in the emergency department must undergo formal radiological review before anticoagulation or more aggressive therapy is commenced.

The ultrasound equipment currently available includes SonoSite and General Electric Healthcare systems. To maintain patient flow, scanning time should preferably not exceed 30 minutes. Clear communication with nursing staff and the on-duty consultant is essential.

Examination sequence

Both limbs should be examined, beginning with the asymptomatic side. Compression is applied every 1-2 cm from the iliac veins to the calf. The following segments are assessed where feasible:1.Common femoral vein at the inguinal crease.2.Femoral vein tracked distally to the adductor canal.3.Popliteal vein with the knee flexed.4.Calf veins when clinically indicated.5.Iliac veins and inferior vena cava using a curvilinear probe if proximal thrombosis is suspected.

Pediatric adaptations

Compression force must be minimized to avoid distress and false-positive findings. Warmth should be maintained throughout the examination. Distraction techniques are used and sedation is avoided whenever possible. Acute thrombus may appear hypoechoic or isoechoic and therefore difficult to visualize, while chronic thrombus tends to become more echogenic with organization. Comparison with the contralateral limb is helpful when findings are equivocal.

Diagnostic criteria

A positive diagnosis of deep vein thrombosis is based on noncompressibility of the vein despite arterial deformation, presence of intraluminal echogenic material, filling defects on color Doppler, or absent phasic flow with lack of augmentation. A negative study requires complete compressibility, spontaneous phasic flow, and normal augmentation in all examined segments.

Documentation

Still images of each venous segment with and without compression, together with spectral Doppler traces, should be recorded. Thrombus dimensions are measured when feasible. Findings are reviewed with a senior clinician and clearly documented in the medical notes. If the child becomes significantly distressed, the examination is terminated and limitations are clearly recorded.
