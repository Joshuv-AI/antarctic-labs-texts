On the information behavior from quadratically coupled accelerated detectors
P. H. M. Barros ID, P. R. S. Carvalho ID, H. A. S. Costa ID

Abstract
In this work, we propose to investigate the information behavior of quantum systems through accelerated detectors quadratically coupled with a massless scalar field. In addition, we made detailed comparisons with the case of linear coupling. The perturbative method was used to evolve the density matrix that describes the interaction of the detector-field system during a finite time. The systems studied were: accelerated single-qubit, quantum interferometric circuit, and the which-path distinguishability circuit. The results on the probability transition rates show that quadratic coupling amplifies the Unruh effect. This is due to the modification of the interaction structure, allowing the simultaneous absorption of multiple quanta. Our findings showed that the information is degraded more quickly in the case of quadratic coupling, when compared to the linear case. Furthermore, this change is mainly given by the coupling constant and by an additional factor that arises in the case of quadratic coupling. Therefore, these results indicate that the nature of the coupling between the detector and the field plays a fundamental role in the behavior of quantum information in high acceleration regimes. Keywords: Relativistic Quantum Information, Quadratic Coupling, Unruh–DeWitt Detector.

Introduction

Modern theoretical physics continues to uncover deeply counterintuitive phenomena at the intersection between quantum mechanics and general relativity. One of the most intriguing examples is the so–called Fulling–Davies–Unruh effect

;

.
This implies that different quantization procedures produce ambiguous definitions of particle numbers

;

;

Aiming to formally investigate this effect and other quantum aspects of field theory in inertial reference frames, the Unruh–DeWitt (UDW) detector was proposed

;

The UDW detector serves as a conceptual instrument for probing and quantifying the particles generated as a consequence of acceleration. In this way, it can be said that particles are defined by what a particle detector detects
davies1984
.

In recent years, relativistic quantum information (RQI) has become a new research area that combines gravitational physics and quantum computing. It seeks to explore the role of relativistic effects in quantum information processing protocols

II
Unruh–DeWitt theory with quadratic coupling

II.1
The theoretical framework

For the purposes of our study, we begin by considering an UDW detector, that is, a point-like detector characterized by two energy levels: a ground state
|g\rangle
and an excited state
|e\rangle
. In this framework, it is considered that
\tau
is the proper time of the detector traveling through a world line given by
x(\tau)
. Let us consider the scenario where the detector interacts quadratically with a massless scalar field given by
\phi[x(\tau)]
via a detector monopole moment
\mu(\tau)
. Thus, the Hamiltonian that represents the quadratic interaction between the detector and the scalar field is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\lambda_{\phi^{2}}\chi(\tau)\mu(\tau)\otimes\phi^{2}[x(\tau)],

(1)

where
\lambda_{\phi^{2}}
is the strength of the interaction, and
\chi(\tau)
represents the switching function, which governs the activation and deactivation of the detector. Given the finite duration of interaction, this function satisfies the following conditions:
\chi(\tau)\approx 1
for
|\tau|\ll T
, and
\chi(\tau)\approx 0
for
|\tau|\gg T
, where
T
characterizes the interaction time.

Before proceeding with the construction of the theoretical framework of this work, it is important to emphasize one last point. As observed by
gray2018scalar
, for each coupling used we will have a different dimensionality, on the other hand, the same occurs with each coupling constant. In fact, for dimensionless switching functions, it can be shown that
[\lambda_{\phi^{2}}]\equiv-\Delta_{\phi^{2}}=3-d
(where
d
is the dimension) considering the quadratic coupling with scalar fields, in units where
[x]=1
. However, it is possible to write a dimensionless constant of the type
\Lambda=\lambda_{\phi^{2}}/\xi^{\Delta_{\phi^{2}}}
, where
\xi
is a time scale of any perturbative approximation of the detector evolution (for more details see
gray2018scalar
).
For the case of this work, we have that
d=4
and the detector evolution time scale is
\xi\sim\Omega^{-1}
, and therefore we obtain,
\Lambda=\lambda_{\phi^{2}}\Omega
.

The interaction should promote transitions in the detector from the energy state
g
to the energy state
e
, while in the field, the interaction promotes a transition from the vacuum
|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
to a particle state
|\Psi\rangle
. Furthermore, by perturbation theory up to first order in
\lambda_{\phi^{2}}
, the probability amplitude of the interaction occurring is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\phi^{2}}=i\lambda_{\phi^{2}}\langle e,\Psi|\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mu(\tau)\chi(\tau)\phi^{2}[x(\tau)]\mbox{\rm d}\tau|0_{\mathcal{M}},g\rangle.

(2)

It is important to emphasize that the monopole momentum operator
\mu(\tau)
has the function of connecting the ground state and the excited state of the detector. Furthermore, it depends only on the detector, not on the scalar field in question. Besides, using the Heisenberg representation, we have that the detector’s monopole moment operator evolves in time as

\displaystyle\mu(\tau)=\mbox{\rm e}^{i\mathcal{H}_{0}\tau}\mu(0)\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\mathcal{H}_{0}\tau},

(3)

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the Hamiltonian of the detector, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{0}|g\rangle=g|g\rangle
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}|e\rangle=e|e\rangle
. In this way, substituting Eq. (
3
) in Eq. (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)=i\lambda_{\phi^{2}}\langle e|\mu(0)|g\rangle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\chi(\tau)\mbox{\rm e}^{i\Omega\tau}\langle\Psi|\phi^{2}[x(\tau)]|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle,

(4)

with
\Omega=e-g
denoting the detector transition frequency. Thus, the probability of measuring the transition, or simply transition probability
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, is obtained by taking the square of Eq. (
4
) and summing over all possible final states
|\Psi\rangle
, in this way we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\phi^{2}}^{2}|\langle e|\mu(0)|g\rangle|^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\langle 0_{\mathcal{M}}|:\phi^{2}[x(\tau)]::\phi^{2}[x(\tau^{\prime})]:|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle,

where the sum over all possible final states allowed us to use the completeness relation
\mathbb{I}=\sum_{\Psi}|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
. Note that in Eq. (
5
) we use the standard field theoretic technique of normal ordering
MartinezRenormalized2016
, where
:\hat{A}:=\hat{A}-\langle 0_{\mathcal{M}}|\hat{A}|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
for an operator
\hat{A}
. The normal ordering technique is necessary because, when considering quadratic scalar and fermionic detector couplings there are persistent divergences that cannot be regulated either by the switching function or by smearing the detector

;
Takagi1986vacuum
;
Louko2016
.

Therefore, aiming at a more simplified interpretation we can write the transition probability as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)=\lambda_{\phi^{2}}^{2}|\langle e|\mu(0)|g\rangle|^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega),

(6)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)
is known as the detector response function
1
1

1

This function carries with it the information of how the detector responds to the interaction with the quantum field. Additionally, it is noted that the first part of the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
) depends only on the internal characteristics of the detector and the coupling with the scalar field.
, and is responsible for identifying the particles with the detector energy variation
\Omega
created due to the interaction with the quantum field. The response function is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(x,x^{\prime}),

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle 0_{\mathcal{M}}|:\phi^{2}[x(\tau)]::\phi^{2}[x(\tau^{\prime})]:|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle,

(8)

is called the Wightman function

, which corresponds to a correlation function. If the worldline along which the detector travels corresponds to integral curves of timelike Killing fields, then the Eq. (
8
) becomes invariant under time translations in the detector’s frame of reference and depends only on the time difference

;
padmanabhan1982general
, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}[x(\tau),x^{\prime}(\tau)]=\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau)
.

In this way, the double integral in Eq. (
7
) reduces to a Fourier transform of
\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau)
multiplied by an infinite time interval that naturally causes
\mathcal{F}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)
to blow up. In this case, we get around the divergence by working with the transition probability rate, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\phi^{2}}(\Omega)=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}(\Delta\tau)\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega\Delta\tau}\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau),

(9)

instead of dealing with the probability itself, in other words, we deal with the response function per unit time. Thus, it is clear that in order to know how the detector responds to the quadratic interaction with the scalar field, we must first obtain an expression for the correlation function of the system in question.

II.2
Wightman function

This section consists of obtaining the Wightman function for a detector that has quadratic coupling and uniform acceleration. However, it is well known in the quantum information community in relativistic regimes the expression for the linear case considering the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition
kubo1957statistical
;
Martin1959Schwinger
. Aware of this, through the Eq. (
121
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta t,\Delta z)=2[\mathcal{W}(\Delta t,\Delta z)]^{2}.

(10)

However, applying these results along a trajectory suffering a constant and uniform acceleration
a
in the direction
z
, for this, the intrinsic coordinate system within the detector frame is connected with the Minkowsky coordinates through the Rindler transformations

\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{a}\sinh{a\tau},\quad z=\frac{1}{a}\cosh{a\tau},\quad x=y=0.

(11)

On the other hand, it is well known that for these coordinates the Wightman function for linear coupling is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\Delta t,\Delta z)=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(\Delta t^{2}-\Delta z^{2}-i\epsilon)},

(12)

and using Eq.(
10
) is obtained

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta t,\Delta z)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{4}}\frac{1}{(\Delta t^{2}-\Delta z^{2}-i\epsilon)^{2}},

(13)

and using the elementary properties of hyperbolic functions in Eq. (
11
), you get

\displaystyle\Delta t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{a}\cosh{\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2a^{-1}}\right)}\sinh{\left(\frac{\tau-\tau^{\prime}}{2a^{-1}}\right)},

(14)

\displaystyle\Delta z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{a}\sinh{\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2a^{-1}}\right)}\sinh{\left(\frac{\tau-\tau^{\prime}}{2a^{-1}}\right)}.

(15)

Replacing these expressions in the Eq. (
13
) and using the hyperbolic property
\cosh^{2}{x}-\sinh^{2}{x}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau)=\frac{a^{4}}{128\pi^{4}}\sinh^{-4}{\left(\frac{\Delta\tau-2i\epsilon}{2a^{-1}}\right)},

(16)

and using the relationship

\displaystyle\sinh^{-4}{x}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}(x-i\pi k)^{-4}-\frac{2}{3}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}(x-i\pi k)^{-2},

(17)

where through this relationship we can finally write

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{4}}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}(\Delta\tau-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a)^{-4}-\frac{a^{2}}{6}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}(\Delta\tau-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a)^{-2}\right].

(18)

This expression represents the Wightman function for the quadratic coupled scalar field. Take note that it satisfies the KMS condition similarly to the linear coupling case.

II.3
Probability transition rates

In this section, we will look for an analytical form for the transition probability rate to a detector that interacts quadratically with the scalar field for a finite time. As we have seen in Sec.
II.1
, the transition probability rate depends, basically, on the response function of the detector, where the latter depends on the correlation function of vacuum. By employing the identity
padmanabhan1982general

\displaystyle f(u)[\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega u}\mathcal{W}(u)]=f\bigg(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\Omega}\bigg)[\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega u}\mathcal{W}(u)],

(19)

valid for any function
f(u)
that admits a power series expansion around
u=0
, we can derive an asymptotic expression for the transition probability corresponding to any analytic window function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}=\chi\left(i\frac{\partial}{\partial\Omega}\right)\chi\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\Omega}\right)\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty),

(20)

where
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)
corresponds to the infinite-time detector (for quadratic coupling)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\mbox{\rm e}^{\pm i\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime}).

(21)

Expanding
\chi(\tau)
as a Taylor series around
\tau=0
and assuming that
\chi(0)=1
and
\chi^{\prime}(0)=0
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}\approx\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)-\chi^{\prime\prime}(0)\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)}{\partial\Omega^{2}}.

(22)

Consequently, the corresponding transition probability rate is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}\approx\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)-\chi^{\prime\prime}(0)\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)}{\partial\Omega^{2}}

(23)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mbox{\rm d}(\Delta\tau)\mbox{\rm e}^{\pm i\Omega\Delta\tau}\mathcal{W}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau).

(24)

It is important to emphasize that the transition probability is sensitive to the derivatives of the switching (window) function. Therefore, an abrupt activation or deactivation of the detector can result in divergent contributions. To outsmart such divergences, we adopt a Gaussian window function given by
\chi(\tau)=\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2T^{2}}\right)
, which ensures a smooth switching on and off of the detector. Under this choice, and to leading order, the finite-time corrections to the transition probability rate can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}\approx\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)+\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}(\infty)}{\partial\Omega^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{-4}\right).

(25)

It is worth noting that the transition rate for a finite time of interaction

depends only on the transition rate that interacts during an infinite time

(26)

where we have for these two integrals the well-known solutions

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega\Delta\tau}\mbox{\rm d}(\Delta\tau)}{(\Delta\tau-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\Omega\exp{\left(\frac{2\pi\Omega k}{a}\right)},

(27)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\Omega\Delta\tau}\mbox{\rm d}(\Delta\tau)}{(\Delta\tau-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a)^{4}}

III.1
Accelerated single–qubit

III.1.1
The physical model

In quantum computing, the qubit (quantum bit) constitutes the fundamental unit of information

, effectively replacing the classical bit employed in traditional computing architectures. Unlike its classical counterpart, a qubit can exist in a coherent superposition of its ground and excited states simultaneously, a property that underpins many of the advantages offered by quantum computation

;
wilde2013quantum
;

.

Now, we undertake an investigation of the influence of the Unruh effect on the quantum coherence of a uniformly accelerated single qubit. In particular, we aim to elucidate the impact of quadratic coupling on the fundamental properties of two-level systems. To this end, we consider a model comprising a detector interacting quadratically with a scalar quantum field. Within this framework, the field, situated in a Minkowski spacetime background, is initially prepared in the vacuum state
|0_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
, while the detector is initialized in a general qubit state, namely,

\displaystyle|\psi_{\mathrm{D}}\rangle=\alpha|g\rangle+\beta|e\rangle,

(35)

where

|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1

3
3

3

The Bloch sphere provides a geometric representation that facilitates the visualization of quantum operations acting on a single qubit

.

III.1.2
Quantum coherence

It is worth mentioning that we cannot “see” a superposition in itself, but only classical states
griffiths2018introduction
. We cannot determine in advance what the outcome of the measurement will be. The only thing we can say before the measurement is that we will observe a specific state with a corresponding probability
sakurai2020modern
. On the other hand, we can obtain information about the superposition of a system through the well-known quantum coherence.

Quantum coherence is a fundamental property characterized by the presence of quantum superposition, which permits interference between distinct eigenstates
Leggett1980
.Furthermore, it is defined by the preservation of relative phases between the components of a superposed quantum state, thereby allowing essential quantum phenomena such as interference and entanglement
streltsov2015measuring
. Within this framework, quantum optical techniques constitute a vital set of tools for the manipulation and control of coherence
Glauber1963coherent
;
sudarshan1963
.

In the specific case of a two-level quantum system, the coherence between the states
|g\rangle
and
|e\rangle
is quantified using the

, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{l^{1}}_{\phi^{2}}(\hat{\rho}_{D,\phi^{2}})=\sum_{i\neq j}\mid\hat{\rho}_{D,\phi^{2}}^{ij}\mid.

(54)

(55)

where

III.2
Quantum interferometric circuits

III.2.1
The physical model

In this section, we analyze the quantum scattering circuit. The circuit is organized as follows: initially, a single qubit is prepared in a well-defined initial state. Subsequently, the application of the first Hadamard gate transforms the qubit into a superposition state. Afterward, a phase accumulation occurs due to the phase-change gate
\hat{\alpha}
. Next, a unitary operator
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
[Eq. (
38
)] induces an quadratic interaction (in a controlled manner) between the detector and the quantum field. Finally, the qubit undergoes a second Hadamard operation, after which measurements on the system are performed.

In this setup, the qubit employed in the quantum interferometric circuit is initially represented by the density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{g}}_{D,I}=|g\rangle\langle g|
. The application of the first Hadamard gate given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

(69)

transforms the state into the following expression:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}_{D,I}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|g\rangle\langle g|+|g\rangle\langle e|+|e\rangle\langle g|+|e\rangle\langle e|\right).

(70)

During the evolution between the two Hadamard gates, the probability amplitudes for finding the qubit in the internal states undergo phase accumulation. Specifically, a phase given by

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\mbox{\rm e}^{i\alpha}\end{pmatrix},

(71)

is introduced by the phase-shift gate when the qubit is in the excited state
|e\rangle
. In this way, the initial density matrix of the detector becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}_{D,I}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|g\rangle\langle g|+\mbox{\rm e}^{-i\alpha}|g\rangle\langle e|+\mbox{\rm e}^{i\alpha}|e\rangle\langle g|+|e\rangle\langle e|\right).

(72)

Consequently, the initial state of the combined system is represented as
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in},I}=\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}_{I}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\phi}
. Thus, the density matrix after the interaction is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

III.2.5

IV
Summary and conclusion

We investigate the information degradation of quantum systems under the Unruh effect through detectors quadratically coupled with a massless scalar field, and compare the findings with the case of linear coupling. The results show that, under high acceleration, coherence and probability amplitudes of the internal states decay more rapidly in the case of quadratic coupling. This stronger degradation is attributed to the coupling constant and the additional factor
(1+\overline{a}^{2})/12\pi^{2}
that arises in the quadratic interaction.

For the setup of the interferometric circuit, our findings showed that when investigating coherence as a function of phase we obtain that the bright interference fringes degrade as we increase the coupling constant. Furthermore, this fringe degradation effect occurs more strongly for quadratic coupling. For linear coupling one should use a coupling constant 10 times larger to obtain a level of fringe degradation similar to the quadratic case.

By employing interferometric visibility and which–path distinguishability, we established the complementarity relation, which enabled us to investigate the influence of quadratic coupling on the manifestation of wave–particle duality in the presence of the Unruh effect. The results revealed an amplification degradation of both wave–like and particle–like information when quadratic coupling is considered. This suggests that the nature of the coupling between the scalar field and the detector plays a significant role in amplifying the effective thermal environment induced by high acceleration.

Quadratic coupling amplifies the Unruh effect by modifying the interaction structure, allowing the simultaneous absorption of multiple quanta. Unlike linear coupling, which involves direct interactions with individual field excitations, quadratic coupling involves higher–order terms that increase the spectral density and introduce new excitation channels, resulting in a stronger response function and higher transition rates.

Therefore, these results demonstrate that the nature of the coupling between the detector and the field plays a fundamental role in determining the degree of information preservation in accelerated quantum systems. Thus, our findings advance the fundamental understanding of RQI. Overall, these results deepen our understanding of quantum information preservation and open doors to promising technological prospects.

Appendix A
Brief review of scalar field theory

In this appendix, we will briefly review quantum scalar field theory in order to clarify and fill any remaining gaps regarding the use of field theory in the main body of this work (for more details see references
lancaster2014quantum
;
birrell1984quantum
). In this way, first consider a real, massless, scalar field
\phi(x)
, it is well known that the corresponding Lagrangian density is given by
lancaster2014quantum

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{\mu}\phi(x)\partial_{\mu}\phi(x),

(100)

and furthermore, the canonical conjugate momentum corresponding to
\mathcal{L}(x)
is written as

\displaystyle\pi(x)=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(x)}{\partial[\partial_{0}\phi(x)]}=\partial_{0}\phi(x).

(101)

Thus, using the Legendre transform, we can construct the Hamiltonian, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\phi}=\int\mbox{\rm d}^{3}x\frac{1}{2}[(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}+(\nabla\phi)^{2}],

(102)

where the equations of motion lead us to the Klein–Gordon equation, and therefore we have

\displaystyle\Box\phi(x)=(\partial_{t}^{2}+\nabla^{2})\phi(x)=0,

(103)

where this equation has well-known solutions, which are plane wave solutions, and thus we obtain

\displaystyle u_{\mathbf{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mbox{\rm e}^{-ikx},

(104)

with
kx=k_{\mu}x^{\mu}=k_{0}x^{0}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}
and
k^{0}=|\mathbf{k}|
. These are called positive frequency modes with respect to
t
. One can then define the inner product,

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\right)=-i\int\mbox{\rm d}^{3}x\Big(\phi_{1}(x)\partial_{t}\phi_{2}^{*}(x)-\partial_{t}[\phi_{1}(x)]\phi_{2}^{*}(x)\Big).

(105)

Thus, it can be seen that the positive frequency modes of the equation form a complete orthogonal basis and therefore we can expand the field, and thus we have

\displaystyle\phi(t,\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{\mbox{\rm d}^{3}x}{(2\pi)^{3}2k^{0}}\Big(a_{\mathbf{k}}u_{k}(x)+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}u_{k}^{*}(x)\Big),

(106)

where
a_{\mathbf{k}}
and
a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators, respectively. Now, using the canonical quantization method, and impose the equal time commutation relations, we obtain

\displaystyle[\phi(t,\mathbf{x}),\phi(t,\mathbf{x^{\prime}})]=0,\qquad[\pi(t,\mathbf{x}),\pi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]=0,\qquad[\phi(t,\mathbf{x}),\pi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]=i\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(107)

Therefore, through the vacuum state
|0\rangle
the Fock-Space representation of Hilbert space is given by

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}|0\rangle=0\quad\text{and}\quad\langle 0|a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=0,\quad\text{for all}\quad\mathbf{k},

(108)

where this shows that vacuum is the state that contains no particles. On the other hand, the operator
a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
creates a particle in state
\mathbf{k}
,
|1_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}|0\rangle
, and in this way, we can construct several particle states with successive applications of the creation operator.

Integral calculation
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

In this work, it is observed that some integrals have emerged that have quite difficult solutions. Motivated by this, we will derive here in detail the integral written as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\mbox{\rm e}^{\pm i\Omega(\tau+\tau^{\prime})}\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\phi^{2}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}),

(122)

where
\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\phi^{2}}(\Delta\tau)
is the Wightman function. Substituing this expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\mbox{\rm e}^{\pm i\Omega(\tau+\tau^{\prime})}\Bigg\{\frac{-1}{8\pi^{4}}\Bigg[\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\left(\tau-\tau^{\prime}-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-4}+

(123)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}}{6}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\left(\tau-\tau^{\prime}-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-2}\Bigg]\Bigg\},

and making the substitutions
s=\Delta\tau=\tau-\tau^{\prime}
and
u=\tau+\tau
, and separating the integrations, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16\pi^{4}}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{(-i\Omega u)}\exp{\left(\frac{-u^{2}}{4T^{2}}\right)}\mbox{\rm d}u\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{\left(\frac{-s^{2}}{4T^{2}}\right)}\mbox{\rm d}s

(124)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-4}+\frac{a^{2}}{6}\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-2}\right].

This expression can be simplified by completing the square of the exponential of
u
, that is,

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-i\Omega u\right)}\exp{\left(\frac{-u}{4T^{2}}\right)}\quad\rightarrow\quad\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{u}{2T}+iT\Omega\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{(-T^{2}\Omega^{2})},

(125)

and then with this we obtain the following expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16\pi^{4}}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{u}{2T}+iT\Omega\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{(-T^{2}\Omega^{2})}\mbox{\rm d}u\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{\left(\frac{-s^{2}}{4T^{2}}\right)}\mbox{\rm d}s

(126)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-4}+\frac{a^{2}}{6}\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-2}\right],

however, the integral in
u
is a Gaussian integral, that is,
\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{u}{2T}+iT\Omega\right)^{2}\right]}\mbox{\rm d}u=\sqrt{\pi}
, and therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16\pi^{4}}\exp{(-T^{2}\Omega^{2})}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\exp{\left(\frac{-s^{2}}{4T^{2}}\right)}\mbox{\rm d}s

(127)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-4}+\frac{a^{2}}{6}\left(s-2i\epsilon-2\pi ik/a\right)^{-2}\right].

It is possible to simplify even further using the following variable substitutions, given by
y\equiv s/2T
, and consequently
\mbox{\rm d}y=\mbox{\rm d}s/2T
, where these transformations have the following Jacobian
J=\mbox{\rm d}s/\mbox{\rm d}y=2T
, aware of this, then one can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16\pi^{4}}\mbox{\rm e}^{-T^{2}\Omega^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm e}^{-y^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}y\left[\frac{1}{4T^{2}}\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{-4}+\frac{a^{2}}{6}\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{-2}\right].

Note that it is possible to write the exponential
\exp{(-y)}
as a Fourier transform, that is,

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm e}^{-y^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm e}^{\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm e}^{2iyk}\mbox{\rm d}\xi,

(129)

and with that, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{-T^{2}\Omega^{2}}}{16\pi^{4}}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm d}\xi\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm e}^{2i\xi y}\mbox{\rm d}y

(130)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{4T^{2}}\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{-4}+\frac{a^{2}}{6}\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{-2}\right].

Now, the next step is to solve the integral in
y
. Note that we are faced with a complex integral, and so, to solve this integral we will use the residue theorem. According to this theorem
butkov
, if
f(z)
is analytic inside a closed contour
\gamma
(and on this contour), except at a finite number of isolated singularities at
z=a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}
, all located inside
\gamma
, then

\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma}f(z)\mbox{\rm d}z=2\pi i\sum^{n}_{\nu=1}\mathrm{Res}f(a_{\nu}).

(131)

In this way, there is a formula that is valid for a pole of order
m
at
z=a
given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}f(a_{\nu})=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\lim_{z\to a_{\nu}}\Bigg\{\frac{\mbox{\rm d}^{m-1}}{\mbox{\rm d}z^{m-1}}\left[(z-a_{\nu})^{m}f(z)\right]\Bigg\},

(132)

note that this expression will be useful for our integrals since they are second and fourth order pole integrals. For our case, our integrals are written as follows

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{2i\xi y}}{\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}y,

(133)

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{2i\xi y}}{\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{4}}\mbox{\rm d}y,

(134)

using Eq. (
132
) (for
m=2
,
m=4
, and
a=\frac{i\epsilon}{T}+\frac{\pi ik}{aT}
) we obtain that these expressions have the respective residues,
\mathrm{Res}(m=2)=2i\xi\exp{(-2\pi\xi k/aT)}
and
\mathrm{Res}(m=4)=-\frac{4}{3}i\xi^{3}\exp{(-2\pi\xi k/aT)}
. Thus, substituting these residues into Eq. (
131
) one obtains

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{2i\xi y}}{\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}y=-4\pi\xi\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi k}{aT}\right)},

(135)

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{2i\xi y}}{\left(y-\frac{i\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\pi ik}{aT}\right)^{4}}\mbox{\rm d}y=\frac{8\pi\xi^{3}}{3}\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi k}{aT}\right)}.

(136)

And so our main expression becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm e}^{-T^{2}\Omega^{2}}}{24\pi^{3}}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\xi\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}\xi\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\left[\frac{\xi^{2}}{T^{2}}\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi k}{aT}\right)}+a^{2}\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi k}{aT}\right)}\right].

(137)

The sum over
k
is from
k=-\infty
to
k=+\infty
, but our choice of contour for the integral subtracts the contribution to the
sum from
k\in[-\infty,0)
leaving the result as the sum of a geometric series, and in this way we have
\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi k}{aT}\right)}=\left[1-\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi}{aT}\right)}\right]^{-1}
. Assuming these conditions, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{aT\mbox{\rm e}^{-T^{2}\Omega^{2}}}{48\pi^{4}}\left[a^{2}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}\xi+\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\xi^{2}\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}\xi\right],

(138)

where we use the expansion
\exp{\left(\frac{-2\pi\xi}{aT}\right)}\approx 1-\frac{2\pi\xi}{aT}
. Now, just as before, fortunately we are faced with Gaussian integrals, these integrals and their respective solutions are written as follows

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\pi},

(139)

\displaystyle\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\xi^{2}\mbox{\rm e}^{-\xi^{2}}\mbox{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}.

(140)

and substituting the solution of these integrals into our general expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{aT\sqrt{\pi}}{48\pi^{4}}\left(a^{2}+\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\right)\mbox{\rm e}^{-T^{2}\Omega^{2}}.

(141)

And so, we finally obtain the final expression for the integral we wanted to solve.
