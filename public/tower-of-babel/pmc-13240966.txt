Parental motivations, facilitators, and barriers to participating in a pediatric vaccine trial in Kathmandu, Nepal: A qualitative study
Authors: Charlie Firth, Neha Kumari Das, Erina Khatri, Sanjeev Bijukchhe, Sachi Chuke, Benjamin Curtis, Meeru Gurung, Sarah Kelly, Elizabeth O’Mahony, Andrew J Pollard, Arun Kumar Sharma, Shrijana Shrestha, Samantha Vanderslott, Bipin Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPediatric vaccine trials are essential for generating evidence to inform immunization policy, yet their success depends on effective recruitment and sustained participation of children and their caregivers. Understanding parental decision-making is particularly important in low- and middle-income countries, where sociocultural dynamics, trust, and access to healthcare may shape willingness to participate in research. This qualitative study explored parental motivations, facilitators, and barriers to participation in a pediatric vaccine clinical trial conducted in Kathmandu Valley, Nepal. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 33 parents whose children were enrolled in the OptImms trial, a randomized study evaluating alternative diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (DTwP) immunization schedules. Interviews were conducted in Nepali, transcribed, translated, and analyzed thematically. Trust in healthcare workers, research institutions, and scientific processes emerged as a central facilitator of participation. Social dynamics, including joint decision-making within families and reassurance from relatives working in healthcare, strongly influenced enrollment decisions. Practical facilitators, such as efficient scheduling, access to additional healthcare, and reimbursement for travel costs, further supported participation. Barriers included time constraints, geographical distance, and concerns about invasive procedures, such as blood sampling. These findings highlight the importance of trust-building, culturally sensitive communication, and logistical support in the ethical design and implementation of pediatric vaccine trials. Addressing these factors may improve recruitment and retention in future vaccine trials in Nepal and similar settings.KEYWORDS: Vaccine trials, parental decision-making, trial participation, trust, qualitative research, LMICs, Nepal

Introduction

Nepal has made considerable progress in expanding immunization coverage in recent decades, yet some gaps remain, and vaccine-preventable illnesses continue to cause significant morbidity and mortality.1,2 Addressing these challenges requires not only strengthening routine immunization but also introducing new vaccines and optimizing existing schedules to better protect children from infectious diseases.3,4 Clinical trials play a critical role in generating the evidence needed for such decisions, by assessing vaccine safety and efficacy, and by identifying strategies for implementation within real-world health systems.5

Conducting trials in settings with high disease burden, and where the vaccines are likely to be used, can improve the contextual relevance of evidence and, when linked to appropriate access and implementation strategies, support more equitable access to new vaccines.6 Local disease epidemiology, population-level biological variation, and socio-cultural dynamics can influence both vaccine effectiveness and community acceptance, underscoring the importance of context-specific evidence.7–9 However, conducting trials in low-and middle-income (LMICs) settings can raise some ethical and logistical considerations. There is a documented history of mistrust toward clinical trials in LMICs, often stemming from a colonial legacy, past instances of exploitation and improper informed consent, and limited community engagement,10–12 which can hinder recruitment and retention of participants.13,14

Previous research has shown that parental decision-making regarding children’s participation in clinical trials is shaped by a complex interplay of trust, perceived risks and benefits, social relationships, and practical considerations.15,16 Trust in healthcare providers and research institutions has consistently been identified as a key determinant of parental willingness to enroll children in research, particularly in vaccine trials.17 Parents often rely on recommendations from clinicians and the perceived legitimacy of sponsoring institutions when assessing trial safety. Social and familial influences, including spousal agreement and advice from extended family members, further shape enrollment decisions.18 Concerns about child safety, invasive procedures, and misunderstandings about research aims have been identified as common barriers to participation,18,19 while practical factors such as access to healthcare, financial reimbursement, and convenience of study procedures can act as important facilitators.20–22 Further, participants who received specific information about the trial reported a significantly more positive experience compared to those who received general information.22 Together, these studies suggest that parental decision-making is shaped by interacting social and structural factors, rather than any single determinant, and signals the value of effective communication and community engagement.

Undue inducement is a recognized concern in clinical trials conducted in LMICs, where reimbursement, or payment, for taking part in a clinical trial may influence decision-making.23–25 Families facing financial challenges may feel pressured to enroll their children as a means of securing healthcare they otherwise cannot afford.26,27 These economic pressures raise important ethical questions about informed consent and the adequacy of understanding trial risks and benefits.23,25 In addition, cultural beliefs and fears of confidentiality also significantly influence willingness to participate in clinical trials in LMICs.28,29 Ultimately, the success of such trials is shaped by more than science and regulatory considerations alone; political context, public trust, and community engagement are contributors to participation.29

Despite recognition of these factors in other LMIC settings, there is limited context-specific evidence from Nepal, particularly regarding how parents navigate trust, social dynamics, and practical considerations when deciding to enroll their children in pediatric vaccine research. This study, therefore, explores the motivations, facilitators, and barriers influencing parental decisions to participate in a pediatric vaccine trial in Kathmandu and Lalitpur districts within Kathmandu Valley, Nepal. By embedding qualitative interviews within an active pediatric vaccine trial, this study provides empirically grounded and context-specific insights into real-world decision-making processes, with direct relevance for the ethical design, recruitment strategies, and implementation of future pediatric vaccine trials in Nepal and similar settings.

Methods

Study design and approach

In this study, a qualitative approach was used to explore parental motivations, facilitators, and barriers to participating in a pediatric vaccine trial. Embedding this qualitative study within the clinical trial allowed real-time exploration of perceptions among parents and caregivers actively involved in the optimizing diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (DTwP) containing vaccine infant immunization schedules’ (OptImms) clinical trial. This randomized, five-arm, non-inferiority trial was designed to assess whether antibody levels against diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis could be maintained while adjusting the timing and dosing of the DTwP vaccine. The primary objective of the clinical trial was to identify a schedule (or schedules) that maintains early protection against pertussis prior to the booster dose and is equivalent to, or better, than the World Health Organization’s Expanded Programme on Immunization schedule. Secondary objectives included optimizing protection against multiple vaccine-preventable diseases, evaluating booster timing, and assessing co-administration strategies for additional vaccines, including the concomitant administration of typhoid conjugate vaccine with Japanese encephalitis vaccine in Nepal.30

A qualitative method was chosen due to its capacity to capture nuanced perceptions, attitudes, and social dynamics. The study was guided by a constructivist paradigm, acknowledging that social contexts, personal experiences, and interactions with healthcare systems shape perceptions and attitudes toward vaccines. This paradigm facilitated an in-depth understanding of how participants make sense of their experiences with vaccine trials. This study draws on a subset of qualitative data collected as part of a broader social science research program embedded within the OptImms clinical trial.

Study setting and participants

The primary study (OptImms clinical trial) was conducted at two urban tertiary hospitals (hospitals equipped with multi-specialties) in Kathmandu valley, Nepal: Patan Hospital, the main teaching hospital of Patan Academy of Health Sciences (PAHS), and the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital at the Institute of Medicine (IOM), Tribhuvan University, key academic and clinical institutions in Nepal. Both hospitals are public hospitals offering the routine immunization program recommended by the Government of Nepal, and the beneficiaries are from diverse socioeconomic classes within the community.

The study participants were a convenience sample of parents or caregivers whose children had participated in the OptImms trial. Participants were selected because of their direct experience of having a child participate in a vaccine trial and were selected on a first-come, first-served basis during the final appointment of the clinical trial. Parents could either complete the interview at that time or make an appointment to come back at another time.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in Nepali by EK and NKD, both Nepali public health practitioners. These one-on-one interviews provided detailed insights into participants’ perceptions and decision-making processes. These discussions were guided by an interview topic guide (Supplementary material 1) that was piloted and refined iteratively with the broader OptImms clinical trial team. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached, as determined by EK and ND. Interviews were audio recorded using external devices, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English. Accuracy was verified through cross-checking by EK, NKD, and a senior social scientist (BA) fluent in both Nepali and English. Participants were reimbursed for their time, and each interview lasted around 30 minutes. Participant demographic characteristics were derived from the wider study dataset.

Data analysis

The translated interview transcripts were analyzed following a reflexive thematic analysis approach, as outlined by Braun and Clarke,31 using NVivo version 15.0, on a line-by-line basis. Each transcript was reviewed twice: first by the interviewer (EK or NKD), who provided local contextual insights, and then by CF, a British social scientist, offering an external perspective while remaining immersed in the broader context. This dual approach facilitated both internal (emic) and external (etic) viewpoints on the data, enriching the analysis. An iterative codebook was developed and refined throughout the analysis, with regular group meetings held to review emerging themes. Emphasis was placed on the interpretations of the in-country team (EK, NKD, BA) due to their contextual expertise. The analysis employed a constructivist approach to identify themes and develop an explanatory framework. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, themes were generated based on patterns of meaning and interpretive depth rather than frequency of codes or terms; therefore, coding frequency was not used to determine thematic importance.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval to conduct this study was approved by Oxford Tropical Ethics Committee (OxTREC)(OxTREC Ref:12–20), Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) (Ref 2400) and the Institutional Review Committee – Patan Academy of Health Sciences (IRC-PAHS) (Ref: drs2002281350.A), and all participants gave informed consent.

Results

This section presents the findings from interviews conducted with parents of children who participated in a vaccine research trial in Nepal. Overall, 33 parents took part in the semi-structured interviews, with a majority being female (90.91%) and aged 25–34 years (78.79%). The participants were primarily Hindu (78.79%), had received a secondary education (54.55%), and had two children (51.52%). Their full demographic information can be found in Table 1.

The themes identified during analysis capture the motivations, facilitators, and barriers associated with enrolling children in the trial; these are exemplified through quotations throughout the paper. These themes are structured as follows: Trust, social or community influence, practical facilitators, contributing to scientific research, barriers to participation, and recruitment considerations.

Trust

Trust in healthcare workers and institutions emerged as a key facilitator for vaccine trial participation. Parents often expressed a sense of confidence in healthcare workers, symbolized in the belief that experts were conducting the study, ensuring safety and responsiveness to potential issues, and thus the overall risks were low: “I did not feel any risks. I was not so afraid because I was sure of one thing – the research will be done by an expert team from different agencies. Even if there was some problem, the solution would come from the same expert team.” In parallel, endorsement by international institutions further strengthened confidence in the trial, with some parents regarding vaccines that had previously undergone evaluation in other countries as intrinsically more credible than those lacking such prior international testing:“ … the trial that you are doing here, aren’t you doing it after it has been successful in other countries besides Nepal … it made me convinced.” Moreover, parents demonstrated a practical trust in scientific research, interpreting the trial process itself as indicative of a degree of scientific validation: “Such studies are done only after some research, why would we doubt it. Considering this, we enrolled our baby in the study.” Consequently, pre-existing trust in healthcare professionals, established institutions, and recognized scientific methodologies emerged as a central factor facilitating participation.

Social and community influence

Many parents described consulting family members, most notably spouses, prior to enrolling their children in the vaccine trial. Such joint decision-making with partners was common and reflected a shared understanding of parental responsibility: “Both of the parents, we were here at the hospital. We do not have anyone staying with us whom we need to ask for permission. So, we husband-wife decided it ourselves.” Although the majority of participants in this study were female (90.91%), final decisions regarding trial participation were often reported to rest with husbands. This pattern appears to reflect established familial structures and intra-household hierarchies, in which decision-making authority was frequently vested in male partners: “After getting advice from the doctor, I asked my husband directly.” However, other familial ties also played a significant role.

Some participants reported that relatives working within the medical field were consulted before agreeing to enroll. In such cases, professional medical advice from trusted family members acted as a facilitator: “My brother and sister-in-law also work here as staff. I learned about it from them … They said, ‘Go ahead, this is a good opportunity.’ I also asked them what the effects would be, and they assured me, ‘This is a good thing; consider yourself lucky to have this opportunity. Not everyone gets it.’” In addition to professional influences, familial networks offered reassurance through direct experiential knowledge. Participants who were aware of family members already enrolled in the trial reported greater confidence in their decision to participate: “Had there been no relatives of mine in this study, I would be scared. My sister advised me not to worry about anything and nothing bad will happen. Hearing that from my sister made me less worried.” Beyond these familial influences, decision-making was also shaped by wider social contexts, with community norms further informing participation.

For some parents, enrolling their children in the trial was likened to the familiar practice of routine vaccination, thereby reinforcing perceptions of normality and social acceptability: “Well, it’s like this – what kind of vaccine is it? We’ve always been giving vaccines, haven’t we? And the government also provides them. So, we were inclined to think it would be fine.” Overall, social influences significantly shaped parental decisions about trial participation, with some element of joint decision-making, but with husbands frequently holding the final say. Familial networks, especially relatives working in healthcare, provided additional reassurance, while community norms and the familiar process of routine vaccination fostered a sense of acceptance and safety.

Practical facilitators

The logistical convenience of trial participation emerged as an important motivating factor. Parents frequently highlighted the efficient organization of trial procedures, including clear scheduling and minimal waiting times, which was particularly valued by those managing competing work and caregiving responsibilities: “There was no waiting time for us here and whenever our child gets ill, we would treat him immediately.” However, medical benefits, including access to additional vaccines and healthcare services free of cost, were pivotal in encouraging participation: “The main thing was, at that time, it was for my baby’s health. Another additional reason was the extra chicken pox vaccine that you mentioned, which also motivated me.” Some participants acknowledged that these benefits were likely to be more compelling for certain groups, particularly individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds: “It might not matter a lot for well-off people but for a poor person, covering the vaccines that we had to pay for otherwise and the travel expenses, it seemed like a good opportunity for the poor, or me.”

Interestingly, some parents also perceived participation as an opportunity for personal learning and for gaining greater insight into their child’s health: “If I need to participate, I will, because we can understand how to keep our child healthy. We can learn a lot; we can ask you about things.” Financial support, including reimbursement for travel expenses, was perceived as a valued practical benefit, particularly among participants traveling from rural areas: “We also didn’t lose time. And right after that, we were given travel expenses, which was helpful.” In summary, logistical convenience – particularly efficient organization and reduced waiting times – emerged as a strong motivator for trial participation, alongside medical benefits, opportunities for personal learning, and financial support. Participants also noted that the perceived value of these benefits varied, with some suggesting that they were particularly salient for individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds.

Contributing to scientific research

Altruism emerged as an appreciable motivating factor for many of the parents whose children participated in the vaccine trial. Parents frequently articulated a desire to support both the research team and the wider community. For some, participation was viewed as a means of contributing to scientific advancement and improving healthcare outcomes for future generations: “if my baby’s participation helps your study, it can benefit other children in the future.” Broader altruistic motivations extended beyond individual benefit, reflecting an understanding of the potential societal impact of the research. Parents highlighted the importance of advancing vaccine knowledge to protect public health and support innovation in immunization practices: “I believe that by doing research and study about the child’s vaccine, discovering newer technology than before, exploring the feasible and suitable timing for vaccines, it will increase the child’s ability to fight the disease.” This willingness to assist those engaged in medical research highlights the altruistic mindset that underpinned the decisions of some parents to enroll in the trial. This sense of collective responsibility was driven by a commitment to improving healthcare for future generations and advancing vaccine knowledge.

Barriers to participation

Despite successfully completing the vaccine trial, parents noted several barriers that hindered their participation. One issue was discomfort, or the potential discomfort, with the medical procedures required within the clinical trial (e.g. blood samples), with parents expressing concern about the frequency and some misunderstanding the purpose of such procedure: “I knew that my baby’s blood had to be drawn four times, and I understood it was for general checkups, not for anything else. Not everyone would understand that.” Competing responsibilities also posed challenges, as parents struggled to balance work commitments with attending study appointments: “I went to work early in the morning and got here after work. I have to go to another workplace after this.” Geographical distance was another barrier, particularly for those from remote areas: “Reaching here was only the difficult thing but it was not that big of a deal.” Overall, while barriers such as discomfort, time constraints, and distance existed, parents were able to overcome these challenges to complete the study.

Recruitment considerations

Participants predominantly relied on information provided by the study staff, with comparatively limited recourse to alternative sources such as the media or local health posts. High levels of confidence in the information communicated directly by the study team appeared to reduce the perceived need for independent verification: “After being informed from here, I didn’t even search anywhere.” There was an awareness that others in the community might misinterpret research activities, particularly if they were presented poorly by the media. Participants expressed ambivalence toward media portrayals. While acknowledging that media outlets could play an important role in raising public awareness about health-related issues, several participants also voiced concerns regarding the accuracy and intent of media coverage. In particular, media reporting was perceived as having the potential to distort information, oversimplify complex issues, or sensationalize stories in pursuit of financial or commercial gain, which, in turn, could undermine trust: “Media has different ways to collect money which might be inappropriate.” Usefully, the participants themselves noted several strategies that could support the recruitment of volunteers for similar studies in the future.

They suggested that leveraging trusted community figures, such as Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs), could be a potentially effective method to recruiting future participants: “we can get it from the neighborhood, from FCHVs; they often inform us about the vaccination through home visits. We are informed by them.” Endorsements from reputable healthcare agencies were also noted as a viable option to foster participation: “In my opinion, this can be effectively done through WHO. For example, using ward offices as channels could work well.” It was also suggested that contacting pregnant women at their hospital appointments could be a useful way to reach parents even before they have given birth to their child to inform them about the study: “I think it would be better to target pregnant women when they have some free time during their checkups. After the baby is born, there’s a lot of confusion and worry, so addressing them during pregnancy checkups would be easier.” In summary, participants trusted information provided by study staff and rarely sought clarification from other sources. They were cognizant that media portrayals could distort research activities, and thus suggested strategies to improve future volunteer recruitment, including leveraging trusted community figures like FCHVs and endorsements from reputable organizations like the WHO to enhance participation.

Discussion

This study provides insight into the factors that shape parents’ decisions to enroll their child in a pediatric vaccine trial, the barriers they faced during participation, and potential strategies for improving recruitment. The findings underscore the complex interplay between trust, social dynamics, practical and altruistic facilitators, and psychological and physical barriers, and contribute to a growing body of literature on parental decision-making in pediatric vaccine trials in LMICs.

The centrality of trust

Trust, operating across multiple and intersecting domains, emerged as a foundational construct that was central to shaping parental willingness to enroll their children in the vaccine trial. The data indicate that trust in healthcare workers, and in reputable institutions, was a primary facilitator, as they promoted confidence in the safety, legitimacy, and relevance of the research being conducted. This aligns with a previous clinical trial participation systematic review that highlighted trust as a critical determinant in volunteering for such research,32 as well as literature around vaccine acceptance outside of a clinical trial context.33 Notably, trust rooted in the perception that expert-led research inherently ensures safety and a reduction in risk, aligns with previous theories of risk perception, which posit that authority and expertise can mitigate fears associated with research.34 To some extent, trust in the scientific process was also salient, as some parents expressed the idea that the trial process itself was indicative of it being low risk. This aligns with previous literature, where validation through established research processes enhances credibility.18,35 The data from this study also highlighted the role of international collaboration in reinforcing perceptions of safety; some parents associated trials initially conducted in other countries with a heightened sense of safety, reflecting COVID-19 literature that argued that global involvement in vaccine trials could confer validity of the research process.36

Social dynamics and familial validation

The influence of social networks appeared to play a pivotal role in parental decision-making, potentially reflecting the broader sociocultural context of family and community dynamics in Nepal, where health-related decisions are often embedded within collective and relational frameworks. A pattern of mutual discussion between parents, with husbands particularly having a salient role in the decision to partake, arose from the data. This reflects gender norms in Nepal, and aligns with research on patriarchal structuring in health decisions in many Asian and African countries, where the male authority figure often has significant influence on the outcome.37 This suggests that the enrollment process within future trials in Nepal could benefit from explicitly acknowledging this, by integrating a familial consultation process for prospective participants. Additionally, this study highlights the potential relevance of ‘snowball’ recruitment within vaccine research. Familiarity with individuals already involved in the trial – particularly when those individuals held familial or social influence – served to reassure participants and mitigate uncertainties associated with trial participation. These findings echo previous research where the acceptance of vaccines by someone within one’s own social circle legitimizes vaccination,38,39 and another study in Nepal that showed the majority of participants in an oral cholera vaccine trial already knew other participants taking part in the study.22 Leveraging trusted community figures has been shown to be effective at disseminating health information before,40 and in the case of Nepal specifically, FCHVs including health workers may serve as an effective endorsement for fostering trust and acceptance for future trials.41

Practicality and altruism

While trust and social dynamics played an important part in encouraging enrollment into the vaccine trial, practical benefits also significantly motivated (and facilitated) participation. Free health care services, medical treatment of participants, logistical support (compensation and reimbursements), and inter-personal communication by research staff were individual benefits of participation that were frequently cited as incentives, as also shown in previous literature.42,43 It was also noted by some participants that these may be more appealing to those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, which is a perception held by many,22 however previous literature from high income countries suggests that those from lower education and income backgrounds are less likely to participate in clinical research.44 This also reflects the demographic of participants who took part in this study, who are relatively well educated, with 55.55% of interview participants completing secondary education (class 6–12). To a lesser extent, some participants also articulated altruistic sentiments regarding their participation, expressing a sense of moral responsibility to support public health research. This mirrors findings from other contexts, for example in the context of high routine immunization performance, where the desire to contribute to scientific progress that benefits society motivates trial participation.45,46

Barriers to participation

Despite completion of the vaccine trial, participants outlined potentially significant barriers to trial participation. Notably, logistical challenges, competing priorities, and concerns about trial procedures arose. This echoes findings from other studies in Nepal, where parents have described their difficulty in finding a convenient time to make a vaccine appointment and difficulties in accessing vaccine clinics for routine appointments and vaccine trials.22,38,47 Furthermore, another important consideration for many was the acceptability of specific trial procedures, for example, blood draws. Parents in this study found the need for blood draws to be acceptable, but did note that others might not feel the same way. Although, it has been shown that with proper counseling for parents, the fears of blood sampling might not be a big factor in the decision to participate in a trial.18 Addressing these concerns necessitates communication and increased engagement that both explain the importance and purpose of such medical procedures, as well as placing them within the broader context of the overall ethical frameworks followed within medical research. If unaddressed, these barriers have the potential to negatively affect recruitment rates, protocol adherence, and participant retention, ultimately impacting trial timelines and data completeness. Addressing these concerns proactively during trial planning may therefore improve both ethical conduct and scientific efficiency.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, while it included parents from two urban tertiary hospitals, the findings may not be representative of parents from rural areas or those seeking healthcare through health posts or community health workers. Additionally, as the interviews were conducted in Nepali and translated into English, nuances in meaning may have been lost or altered during the translation process. Second, the study sample consisted of parents whose children had already participated in a vaccine trial, potentially introducing selection bias toward more positive attitudes toward research participation. Future studies could include parents who declined participation to provide a more comprehensive understanding of motivations and barriers. Third, the demographic characteristics of the participants were relatively homogeneous, with over half having completed secondary education, most being aged between 25 and 34 years, and the majority being female. This may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly as decision-making around vaccination is often shared or primarily made by male caregivers in many contexts. Future studies could explore perspectives from a more diverse sample, including a greater representation of male participants. Finally, the clinical trial that this study was embedded in investigated the timing and dosing of an existing vaccine, rather than a clinical trial of an Investigational Medicinal Product (CTIMP). This may limit the generalizability of the findings to trials involving novel or experimental vaccines where perceptions of risk and decision-making processes may differ, particularly in relation to uncertainty around safety and efficacy, and especially when additional procedures like video-recorded consent are involved. Future research should explore whether similar motivators and barriers are present when parents are considering trials with new or investigational vaccines, and how these may influence an individual’s willingness to participate, or volunteer their child. However, many of the identified facilitators and barriers – particularly those related to trust, social dynamics, and logistical support – are likely to be relevant to pediatric trials of novel or investigational vaccines, where perceived risk may be greater.

Conclusion

This study highlights that parental participation in pediatric vaccine trials in Nepal is shaped by a combination of trust, social dynamics, and practical considerations. Trust in healthcare workers, institutions, and scientific processes emerged as a central facilitator, alongside social influences such as family decision-making and community norms that frame trials as extensions of routine immunization. Practical factors, including logistical convenience, reimbursement, and access to additional health services, were key motivators, while barriers such as time constraints, discomfort, and geographical access persisted. Addressing these challenges through culturally appropriate communication, engagement of trusted community figures, and strategies to reduce logistical burden may enhance participation in future trials. These findings have implications for the ethical and effective design of vaccine trials in Nepal and potentially other low- and middle-income settings. At a broader level, these findings highlight the importance of embedding trust-building, community engagement, and strategies that account for social and logistical realities within pediatric trial design. As new vaccines are tested and introduced globally, incorporating these social and structural considerations into trial planning may be critical to improving recruitment, retention, and the ethical conduct of research.
