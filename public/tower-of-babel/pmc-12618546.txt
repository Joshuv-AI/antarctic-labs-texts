Blockchain consensus mechanism and method for peer-to-peer electricity trading
Authors: Nan Wang, Yongquan Chen, Feng Zhai, Baofeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The distributed electricity trading market constitutes an essential component of the new-type power system, wherein peer-to-peer (P2P) trading serves as a prominent mechanism. However, the advancement of P2P electricity trading faces significant impediments, including security vulnerabilities, information asymmetry, and an underdeveloped credit evaluation system throughout the transaction process.This paper proposes a blockchain consensus mechanism and methodology for peer-to-peer electricity trading. First, an access mechanism for the electricity trading resource pool is designed to construct an credit rating decision tree, thereby ensuring the security and trustworthiness of transaction nodes. Second, a smart contract based demand-response mechanism is designed, allowing prosumers to submit trading requirements and time constraints based on their operational conditions, so as to guarantee the timeliness of transactions.Third, a multi-criteria objective function with priority-based consensus and a dynamic weight adaptation algorithm are constructed. By incorporating user behavior as a key metric, malicious activities are mitigated, enabling on-chain autonomy. Consensus is achieved by minimizing the deviation between demand targets and response matching values, thereby facilitating production-consumption balance. Finally, the proposed consensus method was empirically validated in a lab environment, by conducting performance benchmarking and analysis on the blockchain, this study demonstrates its validity and practicality. It aims to provide novel research methodologies and solutions for peer-to-peer electricity trading.

Introduction

The "14th Five-Year Plan" for Renewable Energy Development proposes to "improve the market-based trading mechanism for distributed power generation, standardize trading procedures, and expand the scale of transactions"1. In this context, peer-to-peer (P2P) trading represents a novel approach that enables energy prosumers to trade directly with each other, making it particularly suitable for distributed energy systems2,3. It is widely regarded as an emerging paradigm driven by the decarbonization, decentralization, and digitalization of the energy sector4. In a fully decentralized P2P market, users can autonomously negotiate prices and complete transactions without a central authority. However, such a model often faces security challenges, including information leakage, data tampering, a lack of transaction transparency, and an underdeveloped trust evaluation system. By leveraging its decentralized and immutable nature, blockchain facilitates the establishment of a trustworthy P2P electricity trading market, thus providing a viable solution to the aforementioned issues5–10.

From an integrated technology perspective, D. Said et al. developed an advanced decentralized electricity trading framework for connected electric vehicles in parking facilities. This system leverages consortium blockchain, machine learning, and game-theoretic modeling to maximize transaction profitability11. In a related vein, Wang B. et al. introduced the CE-SDT mechanism—a distributed community energy trading system that employs a dual-token economic model to encourage user involvement in green energy production and consumption12. In the domain of forecasting and pricing, D. Said et al. also applied artificial intelligence and machine learning to predict photovoltaic power generation in smart microgrids, thereby supporting the functioning of sustainable electricity markets13. Concurrently, D. Abd Eldjalil Chekired et al. designed a dynamic pricing model for electric vehicle charging and discharging services with the objective of reducing peak load demand14. Further advancing decentralized trading, Esmat A. et al. established a fully decentralized peer-to-peer energy trading platform in which all market functions and operational rules are implemented through smart contracts, achieving full operational autonomy15. Separately, Li Hongwei et al. proposed an autonomous P2P electricity trading model for microgrid communities that integrates hybrid energy storage systems16.

From a cybersecurity risk mitigation perspective, Hasan M.K. underscored that integrating blockchain—often in conjunction with other technologies—into complex smart grids and energy trading systems calls for comprehensive threat identification and the implementation of robust countermeasures to uphold ecosystem safety and reliability17. In a closely related contribution, D. Said et al. devised a machine learning-based protocol for detecting False Data Injection attacks in P2P energy trading environments18. Later, the same research group had introduced an integrated framework that combines blockchain, the Internet of Things, and machine learning to mitigate critical cybersecurity threats in smart microgrids19. In the domain of market design, Waseem A. et al. established an operational P2P trading model for microgrids, leveraging automated systems and smart contracts to improve transaction efficiency and security20. Further advancing market performance, Liu Z. et al. incorporated information entropy theory to construct a more equitable, efficient, and risk-aware renewable energy trading framework21.

Research on consensus algorithm optimization has seen significant advances. Shen Xueli et al. proposed a grouping-based algorithm incorporating a dynamic node scoring mechanism (DS-PBFT), which improves consensus efficiency by reducing the number of participating nodes22. Fang Yonghao et al. presented a method that leverages historical transaction data, using a clustering algorithm to organize the consensus network into a dual-layer multi-consensus cluster architecture23. On the reputation-based front, Bou Abdo et al. enhanced the Proof-of-Reputation-X algorithm into the first permissionless pure reputation-based mechanism24, a theme further explored in numerous studies focused on reputation and node incentive mechanisms25–31. Ahmed et al. proposed a Trustworthy Consensus Algorithm to address data integrity and availability in microservices architectures32. Parallelly, Praveen et al. introduced Proof of Majority (PoM), a mechanism designed to enhance decentralization and reduce energy consumption33. Fan Yuqi et al. developed a Dynamic Random Byzantine Fault Tolerance algorithm, which consists of three sub-algorithms for string consistency, data verification, and binary agreement34.

In summary, while significant research exists on blockchain integration with other technologies, addressing cybersecurity risks, and automated trading systems, a gap remains in combining blockchain with target optimization algorithms. There is also a lack of studies addressing user authentication, information credibility, and behavioral oversight within decentralized energy trading platforms. Regarding consensus algorithms, existing work often depends on node stake-based voting, optimizing performance via reputation mechanisms or by limiting participant numbers. However, these approaches fall short of meeting the specific demands of P2P electricity trading, such as rapid response and efficient bilateral matching. This paper innovatively proposes a blockchain consensus mechanism and methodology specifically designed for P2P electricity trading. First, an electricity trading access decision tree ensures only authenticated and trustworthy nodes participate. Second, a consensus method based on a multi-criteria objective function identifies optimal producer–consumer matches under time constraints, enhancing both security and timeliness. Third, a credit scoring mechanism is embedded into the block consensus process to advance on-chain autonomy and prevent malicious behavior from disrupting trades. These contributions collectively address critical challenges: identity forgery, malicious activity suppression, efficient transaction execution, and accurate supply–demand matching. The proposed framework offers a novel methodology and a practical solution for ensuring the secure and stable operation of distributed electricity markets.

Design of the consensus mechanism

In response to the characteristics and security risks (e.g., information asymmetry) of peer-to-peer electricity trading, a blockchain-based trading platform was constructed. To address multiple requirements—including the authenticity and trustworthiness of trading nodes, the accurate matching of energy producers and consumers, and transaction timeliness—a blockchain consensus mechanism based on a multi-criteria objective function was designed (as shown in Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the consensus mechanism design.

Schematic diagram of the consensus mechanism design.

First, an electricity trading resource pool is established. Credit ratings within this pool are evaluated based on node identity authentication, behavioral scoring, transaction volume, and other relevant factors. Only users meeting a predefined credit threshold are permitted to join and participate in trading, thereby ensuring the authenticity and trustworthiness of all transaction nodes. Subsequently, users trigger smart contracts: demand parties set transaction targets according to their operational needs, while responding parties provide matching feedback. By employing a priority-based consensus algorithm alongside an adaptive-weighting dynamic consensus mechanism, accurate matching between production and consumption is achieved. Finally, the system automatically identifies the optimal responding party and employs a two-round broadcast verification process to reach consensus within specified time constraints, guaranteeing transaction timeliness.

Consensus mechanism process

Transaction process introduction

The overall peer-to-peer (P2P) electricity trading process consists of three key phases: obtaining trading authorization, transaction matching, and consensus achievement, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Electricity trading flowchart.

Electricity trading flowchart.

The first phase involves obtaining trading authorization. Upon logging in, a user is automatically subjected to a credit evaluation. Access to the electricity trading resource pool is then granted based on the resulting credit score, ensuring only trustworthy nodes can participate.

The subsequent transaction matching phase begins with a demand-response mechanism. A requester triggers a smart contract by defining the transaction’s requirements, target values, and time constraints. Respondents subsequently submit matching proposals. The system then employs either a priority-based consensus algorithm or an adaptive weighting-based mechanism to identify the optimal match—the respondent whose proposal deviates the least from the requester’s target value. If multiple nodes submit requests simultaneously, priority for recording the transaction is determined by their behavioral scores within the node system.

The final stage involves consensus confirmation and block recording. Once the optimal respondent is selected, the system automatically initiates the execution protocol and broadcasts the transaction across the network via the blockchain’s real-time monitoring mechanism. If no objections are raised within the predefined time window, consensus is deemed achieved, and the transaction is immutably recorded on the blockchain.

Throughout this process, the credit rating is the direct criterion for obtaining trading permissions, while the behavioral score is a key factor influencing priority for transaction write rights and is integrated into the multi-criteria objective function for consensus computation. Consequently, compliant and proactive engagement within the system yields a higher behavioral score, which increases the probability of successful transaction matching. This design fosters a virtuous cycle wherein users with strong credibility are more likely to achieve successful trades.

Transaction matching

After gaining access to the electricity trading resource pool, nodes can initiate production or consumption requests based on their actual needs and proceed to the transaction matching phase. This process, driven by user-triggered smart contracts for automatic execution, consists of two components: information writing and consensus computation, as illustrated in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Schematic diagram of the transaction matching phase.

Schematic diagram of the transaction matching phase.

During the information writing phase, the demand party submits transaction requirements—including time constraints, purchase/sale specifications, target values (encompassing behavioral score, quantity, and acceptable unit price), and remarks (such as the minimum required behavioral score for respondents and a timestamp). Concurrently, responding parties provide matching information, which includes their proposed quantity, unit price, and automatically retrieved behavioral score and timestamp. The platform validates this transaction request and executes the smart contract for network-wide broadcast.

During the system computation phase, the executed smart contract invokes the consensus algorithm. This algorithm calculates the deviation between the respondents’ multi-criteria matching values and the demand party’s target values within the specified time constraints. The respondent with the smallest deviation is selected as the target trading partner.

Consensus achievement

Upon identifying the target respondent, a smart contract is triggered to initiate a two-round broadcast consensus window under the blockchain’s network-wide supervision mechanism. This process enables comprehensive verification and monitoring of the transaction across the entire network, as illustrated in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Flowchart of consensus achievement process.

Flowchart of consensus achievement process.

Through the first network-wide broadcast, the system awaits possible reconsideration requests from users. Should no objections be raised, a second network-wide broadcast verification is triggered. If no other trading nodes raise objections after both verification rounds and the transaction time constraints are satisfied, network-wide consensus is achieved, and the block information is successfully recorded on the blockchain, marking the transaction as completed.

If, during the broadcast window periods, any prosumer raises a dispute regarding the transaction matching result, the system initiates a manual verification process after the concerned party provides supporting evidence. Once verified, the transaction re-enters the consensus validation phase.

The time constraint must satisfy t₁ + t₂ = T, where t₁ denotes the duration of the transaction matching phase, t₂ represents the time taken for the consensus achievement phase, and T is the total permitted duration for a transaction. The value of T is typically determined by factors such as the number of users in the peer-to-peer energy trading consortium blockchain, the system participation rate, and the urgency of supply and demand among prosumers, and is usually set between 2 and 10 min. The time allocation between the matching and consensus phases must be predefined within the platform’s protocol.

If consensus is not achieved within the specified time constraint T, the transaction will be deferred to the next user in sequence according to the timestamp of their production/consumption information submission.

Consensus calculation method

Credit rating decision tree

By constructing a user credit rating decision tree, user eligibility for accessing the electricity trading resource pool is determined. This tree uses identity authentication as its root node, with transaction scale and behavioral scores as internal nodes. The user’s credit rating is classified into four distinct levels: A, B, C, and D, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Access to the pool and permission to participate in electricity trading is granted only to users who achieve a credit rating of level A.Fig. 5Schematic diagram of the credit rating decision tree.

Schematic diagram of the credit rating decision tree.

Within the decision tree, the use of a digital certificate serves as the root node, representing the most fundamental criterion. Key features—primarily the user’s recent transaction scale and behavioral score—are selected as internal nodes. The leaf nodes determine the user’s final credit rating. The variables P and Q represent the optimal split points for transaction scale and behavioral score, respectively. Typically, P denotes a threshold value for the user’s recent transaction volume, while Q corresponds to a threshold for the behavioral score.

The optimal split points for P and Q are determined by applying the Gini impurity method to node t. The two consensus computation methods described above are derived from goal programming theory in operations research and management science. Each consensus computation requires only basic mathematical operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation. Since the number of these operations is fixed and the normalization procedures also involve a fixed number of operations, the computational complexity of the algorithm is constant, i.e., O(1).

The parameters are categorized into three types: basic parameters, penalty parameters, and adjustment parameters.

Basic parameters include the initial weights for user behavior, energy quantity, and electricity price, as well as the supply–demand balance ratio. Penalty parameters primarily consist of the maximum penalty value for defaults and the penalty increase rate. Adjustment parameters include the total transaction volume in the system, the total renewable energy consumption, and the adjustment sensitivity for the energy weight. The data validation—conducted through a complete process of parameter assignment, weight calculation, and objective function solution—verified the logical rationality of the algorithm in multi-user competitive scenarios. Among the prosumers, Prosumer 1 achieved the optimal overall score, which can be attributed to a high behavioral weight proportion and the absence of negative behavioral score deviation. Although this prosumer exhibited a relatively high negative deviation in transaction energy quantity and a high positive deviation in transaction price, the overall score remained the best. In contrast, Prosumer 3, characterized by a low number of successful transactions, a low renewable energy consumption ratio, and a high number of historical defaults, showed a significant negative deviation in behavioral score. Despite having no negative deviation in energy quantity and no positive deviation in transaction price, this prosumer received the lowest overall score.

The data validation results demonstrate a user behavior-oriented principle, underscoring the significant impact of user behavior on the success of electricity transactions. The effectiveness of the algorithm in regulating user operations and penalizing default behavior was verified. Furthermore, in scenarios with supply–demand fluctuations, both the energy quantity and the electricity price were dynamically adjusted in response to market changes, which aligns with market orientation and business logic.

PoW has a block time of approximately 10 min and offers relatively high security. However, its timeliness is poor—as exemplified by Bitcoin. In later stages, as the difficulty increases, the energy consumption required for block generation rises significantly.

PoS grants block validation rights based on the quantity and age of tokens held. It reduces block time to around 12 s, thereby improving transaction efficiency. That said, its security relies on the honesty of token holders and may face centralization risks, as entities with larger stakes exert greater influence.

PBFT achieves a block time of under 5 s, making it highly timely. However, it relies heavily on trust among pre-authenticated nodes and requires mandatory trust relationships. The primary node also holds substantial authority, resulting in high trust costs.

DPoS offers high fault tolerance and fast block generation. Nevertheless, the delegation mechanism introduces a degree of centralization, and participant enthusiasm during consensus tends to be low. It is often used in combination with the PBFT algorithm39.

The time-to-block for the algorithm proposed in this paper can be elastically configured according to user business requirements, thereby fulfilling the timeliness demands of electricity trading transactions. The system does not rely on node voting, avoiding inherent trust dependencies among nodes.

Security analysis

The blockchain consensus method described in this paper determines the order of blocks chronologically using timestamps to ascertain the sequence. For users submitting transaction requests simultaneously, credit scores are used to prioritize transaction permissions, thereby reducing the risk of forks in the blockchain. A multi-metric objective function is established to form a consensus model that integrates both system-calculated objective metrics and user-submitted subjective appeals, determining target values and achieving agreement. This approach eliminates the computational resource wastage inherent in PoW and avoids the over-reliance on node wealth or status seen in PoS. By implementing an access mechanism for the power resource pool, users’ operational behaviors are linked to their credit ratings. Only those meeting the required credit threshold are permitted to engage in transactions. Furthermore, within the multi-metric consensus algorithm, behavioral scoring serves as a critical factor in determining transaction success, effectively curbing malicious user activities. Laboratory validation has confirmed the correctness of the smart contract logic.

Conclusion

Promoting peer-to-peer (P2P) electricity trading is essential for constructing a new-type power system. The blockchain consensus mechanism designed for P2P electricity trading establishes a decision tree for user credit rating, develops smart contracts to support demand response mechanisms, and employs a consensus algorithm based on a multi-metric objective function. Data validation in a laboratory environment has verified the effectiveness of the proposed computational methods in preventing malicious user behavior, promoting production-consumption balance, and facilitating the integration of renewable energy. This approach achieves efficient matching, decentralization, and operational flexibility in P2P electricity trading.

However, as this consensus method relies on chronological order to determine the sequence of blocks added to the chain, its applicability remains relatively limited. It is better suited for consortium blockchains with a moderate number of nodes and bilateral transaction demands, rather than for public blockchains with a large user base. Moreover, due to constraints in the laboratory environment, the limited user data resulted in incomplete system verification. Future work will focus on refining the algorithmic model and expanding the exploration and validation of additional application scenarios, to provide insights for enhancing renewable energy integration and ensuring the security and stability of the power grid.
