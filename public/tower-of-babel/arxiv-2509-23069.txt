Every cutoff profile is possible
Lucas Teyssier

Abstract
We introduce fruit-inosculated-tree Markov chains. These chains have easily tunable parameters and are a good source of examples. In particular, we prove that every cutoff profile is possible, with any cutoff time and window size.



1
Introduction

1.1
Cutoff phenomenon

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an ergodic Markov chain on a finite state space
S
, and denote its stationary measure by
\pi
. For
t\geq 0
and
x\in S
, denote the law of
X_{t}
conditioned on
X_{0}=x
by
\mu_{x,t}
. Define the (worst case, total variation) distance to stationarity of the chain by

\mathrm{d}(t)=\max_{x\in S}\dtv\left(\mu_{x,t},\pi\right),

(1.1)

where the total variation distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
S
is given by

\dtv(\mu,\nu)=\max_{A\subset S}\left|\mu(A)-\nu(A)\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in S}\left|\mu(x)-\nu(x)\right|.

(1.2)

Definition 1.1
.

Let
((X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of ergodic Markov chains, in discrete or continuous time. We say that that
((X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 1}
exhibits a total variation
cutoff
at a (sequence of) times
\left(t_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)t_{n}\right\rfloor\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)t_{n}\right\rceil\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(1.3)

where for
n\geq 1
,
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\cdot)
is the distance to stationarity for the chain
(X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

The cutoff phenomenon was proved for random transpositions by Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
, and for the riffle shuffle by Aldous
[
Ald83
]
. It is now known for hundreds of models and in a variety of contexts, including models of statistical physics
[
LS13
,
Lac16b
,
LL19
]
, diffusions on compact Lie groups
[
Mé14
]
, random walks on Ramanujan graphs
[
LP16
]
, and for processes with non-negative curvature
[
Sal24
,
PS25
]
under mild additional conditions. We refer to the books
[
LP17
,
DF23
]
for more background.

1.2
Cutoff profiles

For some models it is possible to push the analysis further, and to also understand how the distance to stationarity behaves within the phase transition. If
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
are sequences of positive real numbers, we write
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
or
a_{n}\lll b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Definition 1.2
.

A
continuous time scaling triplet
is a triplet
((t_{n}),(w_{n}),p)
, where
(t_{n})
and
(w_{n})
are two sequences of (positive) real numbers such that
\sqrt{t_{n}}\lll w_{n}\lll t_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, and
p{\;:\;}{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
is a weakly decreasing continuous function such that
p(c)\xrightarrow[c\to-\infty]{}1
and
p(c)\xrightarrow[c\to+\infty]{}0
. We denote the set of all continuous time scaling triplets by
\CTST
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
((X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of continuous-time Markov chains. If there exists
((t_{n}),(w_{n}),p)\in\CTST
such that for every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(t_{n}+cw_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}p(c),

(1.4)

then the function
p
is called
cutoff profile
and
w_{n}
is called
window size
.

Remark 1.4
.

To be precise, cutoff profiles are defined uniquely only up to equivalence of scaling triplets, where two scaling triplets
((t_{1,n},(w_{1,n}),p_{1})
and
((t_{2,n}),(w_{2,n}),p_{2})
are
equivalent
if there exist
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
such that

(a)

p_{2}(\alpha c+\beta)=p_{1}(c)
for every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(b)

w_{1,n}=(\alpha+o(1))w_{2,n}
,

(c)

t_{1,n}=t_{2,n}+(\beta+o(1))w_{2,n}
as
n\to\infty
.

For most classical Markov chains, there is a natural choice of scaling. It is then standard to omit the equivalence relation and call the function
p
of such a scaling triplet
the
cutoff profile. Similarly, what matters for the window size is its order of magnitude, rather than the precise constant in front.

Remark 1.5
.

We also note that the sequences
(t_{n})
and
(w_{n})
may not have asymptotic equivalents: for the affine walks on the hypercube studied in
[
DG92
]
both sequences have an oscillating behaviour!

Historically, cutoff profiles were found only for a handful of Markov chains: the simple random walk on the hypercube
[
DGM90
]
, the top-to-random shuffle
[
DFP92
]
, and the riffle shuffle
[
BD92
]
.

Cutoff profiles can have different shapes. The most common one is the Gaussian shape
c\mapsto\dtv\left({\mathcal{N}}(0,1),{\mathcal{N}}(e^{-c},1)\right)
of the hypercube and riffle shuffle
[
DGM90
,
BD92
]
, and the second most common one is the Poissonian shape
c\mapsto\dtv\left(\Poiss(1),\Poiss(e^{-c})\right)
of random transpositions
[
Tey20
]
.
Less common profiles include the free Meixner shape of diffusions on quantum groups
[
FTW22
]
, and shapes related to the largest eigenvalue of random matrices as for the asymmetric simple exclusion process on a segment
[
BN22
]
.

In the last years a variety of new profiles has been found, and techniques to find cutoff profiles are blooming. Below, we attempt to provide a comprehensive list of known profiles to date.

Apart from the hypercube and the riffle shuffle, a Gaussian profile was proved for simple random walks on Ramanujan graphs
[
LP16
]
, the simple exclusion process on the segment
[
Lac16a
]
, for the averaging process
[
CDSZ22
]
, for generalized Ehrenfest urns
[
NOT22
]
(whose arXiv version also contains a short proof for the hypercube), the Bernoulli–Laplace urn model
[
OTS24
]
with many particles, and the shelf shuffle
[
CO25
]
.
Normal distributions appear as limits of various types of observables in probability. For Gaussian profiles there is often a hidden central limit theorem, and it is therefore not a surprise that this shape arises in different contexts and is the most common one.

The Poissonian shape typically appears for random walks on symmetric groups, when the last observable to mix is the number of fixed points. Note that
\Poiss(1)
is the distribution of the number of fixed points of a uniformly random permutation, asymptotically.
After being found for random transpositions, the Poissonian profile was extended to cycles of length
o(n)
in
[
NOT22
]
, is now known for conjugacy invariant walks up to a large support
[
Tey25
]
. Nestoridi
[
Nes24
]
developed a comparison technique that enabled extending this profile to chains that are not conjugacy invariant, such as star transpositions; and this technique was later used to find the cutoff profile of other related models
[
GK24
,
AN25
]
. A Poissonian profile with adjusted parameters is also known for some particle systems that can be obtained as projections of random transpositions
[
NOT24
]
. Finally, while the proofs of the aforementioned Poissonian profiles rely on the representation theory of symmetric groups to understand compensations between the eigenvectors of these chains, a more probabilistic proof of the profile for random transpositions was recently found
[
JS24
]
: it still crucially relies on representation theory for the eigenvalues, but replaces the understanding of the eigenvectors by a probabilistic extraction of the number of fixed points.

Some recently found cutoff profiles have
Tracy–Widom
shapes, which are the cumulative distribution function of the largest eigenvalue of different models of random matrices: a GUE shape for the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) on a segment
[
BN22
]
, a GOE shape for the Metropolis biased card shuffling
[
Zha24
]
, and shapes interpolating between GOE and GSE for the ASEP with one open boundary
[
HS23
]
. The ASEP can also exhibit limit profiles given by the one-point marginals of Airy processes
[
BN25
]
.

Méliot
[
Mé14
]
proved the cutoff for the diffusion on compact Lie groups and computed explicit formulas for densities at any time. However there was so far no success in computing the associated cutoff profiles. The cutoff profiles were effectively computed on some related quantum objects such as the quantum orthogonal group and for quantum random transpositions
[
FTW22
]
. Recently, Delhaye
[
Del24
]
defined a Brownian motion on the quantum unitary group, proved that it exhibits a cutoff, and found the
c>0
part of the cutoff profile.

The contributions of the largest eigenvalues taken individually are typically small in the
c>0
part of the profile, while for
c<0
, they are all large and compensate. This causes the speed of convergence to the profile to be much faster on the right part of the window, and explains why in some cases it is much harder to understand the
c<0
part than the
c>0
one. This issue leads to further complications in the quantum setting: in
[
FTW22
,
Del24
]
, the distribution of the processes are absolutely continuous with respect to the Haar state in the
c>0
part of the profile, but not in the
c<0
part. Going around the lack of absolute continuity requires extracting atoms, and the formulas of the cutoff profiles involve distributions that have atoms in their
c<0
part.

Let us also mention that for any (sequence of) discrete time Markov chain exhibiting cutoff, adding enough laziness leads to a Gaussian profile. Of course, obtaining a profile in such a way would not improve the understanding of the chain and would therefore not be interesting.

Doing the opposite can however be very interesting: in some cases there is some structure and laziness prevents us from seeing it. In these cases removing the laziness from the chain can lead to a more precise analysis of the important parameters. To give a concrete example, for conjugacy invariant walks on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with laziness
1/2
(or when considered in continuous time), the profile is inherently Poissonian but becomes Gaussian if “
w_{n}=o(\sqrt{t_{n}})
”, that is for conjugacy classes with support sizes diverging faster than
n/\ln n
.

1.3
Main results

As described in Section
1.2
, several shapes of cutoff profiles were found. It is natural to wonder if any shape is possible. We prove that this is the case.

Theorem 1.6
.

For every continuous time scaling triplet
((t_{n}),(w_{n}),p)
, there exists a sequence of continuous time Markov chains
((X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}})_{n\geq 1}
such that for every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(t_{n}+cw_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}p(c).

We may relax the continuity assumption in the definition of scaling triplets for existence. However, if it is relaxed, then the relation described in Remark
1.4
is no longer an equivalence relation and sequences of Markov chains might exhibit non-trivial behaviours in different
windows
. This contrasts with a recent result of Nestoridi
[
Nes25
]
, proving the existence of a unique scale and that the cutoff profile must be continuous, under some spectral conditions. We notably give an example of a sequence of Markov chains having a non-trivial behaviour in uncountably many
windows
in Section
4.1
, and an example of a sequence of Markov chains having countably many
nested windows
in Section
4.3
.

We write
{\mathbb{N}}=\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\}
and
{\mathbb{N}}^{*}={\mathbb{N}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}=\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}
. For discrete time Markov chains we prove the following.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(t_{n})
be a sequence of positive integers and
(w_{n})
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that
w_{n}=o(t_{n})
as
n\to\infty
. Let
p{\;:\;}{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
be a weakly decreasing function (but not necessarily continuous) such that
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to-\infty]{}1
and
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to+\infty]{}0
. There exists a sequence of discrete time Markov chains
((X_{t}^{(n)})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}})_{n\geq 1}
such that

\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}t_{n}+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t_{n}+cw_{n})-p(c)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(1.5)

In this section, we define a family of Markov chains whose parameters can be easily modified to exhibit various behaviours. The main idea is to define almost deterministic Markov chains by adding a strong drift, and to choose the length of the
branches
so that the worst starting vertex is always the same, and so that the chain reaches the
center of mass
with the desired probability at the desired time. These chains are defined below in Definition
2.3
and illustrated on Figure
1
. They can be visualized as trees whose branches all join at a single fruit. We call these Markov chains fruit-inosculated-tree (FIT) Markov chains.

3
Properties of FIT Markov chains

3.1
Hitting and staying at the fruit

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an irreducible and aperiodic (discrete time) Markov chain on a finite state space
S
. The hitting time of a state
x\in S
is the first time the chain reaches
x
:

T_{x}=\min\left\{t\geq 0\mid X_{t}=x\right\}.

(3.1)

The first return time to a vertex
x
is defined similarly by
T_{x}^{+}=\min\left\{t\geq 1\mid X_{t}=x\right\}
.
We denote the probability and expectation of the chain conditioned on starting at a state
x\in S
by
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
, and if
\mu
is a probability measure on
S
we set
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}=\sum_{x\in S}\mu(x){\mathbb{P}}_{x}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}=\sum_{x\in S}\mu(x){\mathbb{E}}_{x}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/3)
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})_{t\geq 0}
be the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. There exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(\ell_{0})
such that

\max_{x\in S}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[T_{z}\right]\leq C_{0}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Denote the transition matrix of the chain by
P
. Set
\eta=1-\varepsilon
as before, and set
\ell=\ell_{0}+\ell_{k}
. Denote the probability, starting at a state
x
, to be at
z
after
\ell
steps, by
P^{\ell}(x,z)
. Then
P^{\ell}(x,z)\geq\eta^{\ell}\geq(2/3)^{\ell}=:\delta(\ell)
, and therefore
\max_{x\in S}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(T_{z}>\ell)\leq 1-\delta(\ell)
. It follows that no matter what the starting point is,
T_{z}
is stochastically dominated by
\ell
times a geometric random variable with success probability
\delta(\ell)
, and hence its expectation is upper bounded by a constant which depends only on
\ell
, i.e. only on
\ell_{0}
since
\ell\leq 2\ell_{0}
by definition.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that the following holds. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
. Consider the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
, and denote its stationary distribution by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
.

(a)

We have
{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\left[T_{z}^{+}\right]\leq 1+2\varepsilon
.

(b)

We have
\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
.

(c)

For any fixed
t\geq 0
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(X_{t}=z)\geq 1-4\varepsilon
.

(d)

For any state
v
, and any times
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{v}(X_{t_{2}}=z)\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{v}(T_{z}\leq t_{1})-4\varepsilon
.

Proof.

(a)

We have
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}=1)=\eta=1-\varepsilon
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}=2)=\varepsilon\eta\leq\varepsilon
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
, we have
\max_{x\in S}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[T_{z}\right]\leq C_{0}
for some constant
C_{0}
depending only on
\ell_{0}
. By the law of total expectation and since
{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}\geq 3)=\varepsilon^{2}
, we therefore get

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\left[T_{z}^{+}\right]&={\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}=1)+2{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}=2)+{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\left[T_{z}^{+}\mid T_{z}^{+}\geq 3\right]{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(T_{z}^{+}\geq 3)\\
&\leq(1-\varepsilon)+2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}(2+\max_{x\in S}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[T_{z}\right])\\
&\leq 1+\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon(2+C_{0})),\end{split}

(3.3)

which is less than
1+2\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon
small enough.

(b)

By a classical property of Markov chains (
[
LP17
, Proposition 1.19]
), we have
\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)=1/{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\left[T_{z}^{+}\right]
. Moreover by (a),
{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\left[T_{z}^{+}\right]\leq 1+2\varepsilon\leq 1/(1-2\varepsilon)
. We conclude that
\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
.

(c)

By (b) we have
\dtv(\delta_{z},\pi_{\varepsilon})\leq 2\varepsilon
, where
\delta_{z}
denotes the Dirac measure at
z
. Therefore

{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(X_{t}=z)\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}(X_{t}=z)-2\varepsilon=\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)-2\varepsilon\geq 1-4\varepsilon.

(3.4)

(d)

This follows immediately from (c) and the strong Markov property.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

The bound
\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
in Proposition
3.2
(b) shows that if
\varepsilon
is small then the stationary measure
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is concentrated at
z
.

3.2
Hitting the fruit at specific times

We now estimate the hitting time of the fruit
z
starting from any state.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. There exist
C_{1}>0
and
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that the following holds. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{1})
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})_{t\geq 0}
be the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. Denote its stationary distribution by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
.

(a)

Let
1\leq i\leq k
and
\ell_{0}+1\leq j\leq\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-1
. We have

{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{j}^{i}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j)\geq 1-C_{1}\varepsilon.

(3.5)

In particular,
{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{j}^{i}}(T_{z}\geq\ell_{0})\leq C_{1}\varepsilon
.

(b)

Let
1\leq i\leq k
and
0\leq j\leq\ell_{0}
. We have

\rho_{i}(1-C_{1}\varepsilon)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{j}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}-j+\ell_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(1+C_{1}\varepsilon).

(3.6)

Proof.

Recall that by definition
\ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{k}
and
\rho_{1}+\ldots+\rho_{k}=1
.

(a)

First there is no path from
y_{j}^{i}
to
z
of length
\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j-1
or less, and a single one of length
\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j
. To be explicit we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{j}^{i}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{0}=y_{j}^{i},X_{1}=y_{j+1}^{i},\ldots,X_{\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j-1}=y_{\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-1}^{i},X_{\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j}=z).

(3.7)

Therefore by a union bound (for the probability of the complement) we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{j}^{i}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j)\geq 1-(\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j)\varepsilon\geq 1-\ell\varepsilon,

(3.8)

where
\ell=\ell_{0}+\ell_{k}\leq 2\ell_{0}
.

(b)

Proceeding as in (a), the probability to always go up in the tree until reaching
z
, and once at
x_{\ell_{0}}
to jump next to
y_{\ell_{0}+1}^{i}
(or directly to
z
if
\ell_{i}=1
) is at least
\rho_{i}(1-\ell\varepsilon)
. The upper bound then follows from the lower bound (that we use for all
i^{\prime}\neq i
): since by definition
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k}
are distinct integers and
\rho_{1}+\ldots+\rho_{k}=1
, we have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{j}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-j)&\leq 1-\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime}\leq k{\;:\;}i^{\prime}\neq i}{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{j}}(T_{z}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i^{\prime}}-j)\\
&\leq(1-\ell\varepsilon+\ell\varepsilon)-\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime}\leq k{\;:\;}i^{\prime}\neq i}\rho_{i^{\prime}}(1-\ell\varepsilon)\\
&=\rho_{i}(1-\ell\varepsilon)+\ell\varepsilon\\
&\leq\rho_{i}+\ell\varepsilon\\
&\leq\rho_{i}\left(1+(\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\rho_{i})^{-1}\ell\varepsilon\right).\end{split}

(3.9)

∎

3.3
Estimates on the distance to stationarity

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. For
1\leq i\leq k
set
t_{i}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}
. Note that since
\ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{k}
, the
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
are all distinct. We define auxiliary functions
g=g_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}
from
{\mathbb{N}}=\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\}
to
[0,1)
, and
G=G_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}
from
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
to
[0,1]
, as follows. We set
g(t_{i})=\rho_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, and
g(t)=0
for
t\in{\mathbb{N}}\backslash\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\right\}
; and for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

G(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor}g(t).

(3.10)

We also set
F=F_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}=1-G_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}
.
Intuitively, the function
g
corresponds to the hitting distribution of the fruit
z
starting at the root
x_{0}
, if we took
\varepsilon=0
(that is if the walk had a fully deterministic behaviour), and
G
is the associated cumulative distribution function (for the probability to be at
z
). Moreover, by definition,
G(x)=0
for
1\leq x\leq\ell_{0}
.

Let also
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Let
(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})_{t\geq 0}
be the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. Denote again its stationary distribution by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
and its state space by
S
. For
x\in S
and
t\geq 0
, denote the law of
X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
conditioned on
\left\{X_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=x\right\}
by
\mu_{x,t}^{(\varepsilon)}
, and set

\mathrm{d}_{x}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)=\dtv\left(\mu^{(\varepsilon)}_{x,t},\pi_{\varepsilon}\right).

(3.11)

Finally, define for
t\geq 0
the worst case total variation mixing time by

\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)=\max_{x\in S}\mathrm{d}_{x}^{(\varepsilon)}(t).

(3.12)

Let us start by estimating the probability to be at the fruit at specific times starting from specific states.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. There exist
C_{2}>0
and
\varepsilon_{2}>0
such that the following holds. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{2})
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})_{t\geq 0}
be the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. Denote its stationary distribution by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
.

(a)

Let
1\leq i\leq k
and
\ell_{0}+1\leq j\leq\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-1
. Let
t\geq\ell_{0}
. We have

{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{j}^{i}}(X_{t}=z)\geq 1-C_{2}\varepsilon.

(3.13)

(b)

Let
0\leq j\leq\ell_{0}
. Let
t\geq 0
. We have

\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{j}}(X_{t}=z)-{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t+j}=z)\right|\leq C_{2}\varepsilon.

(3.14)

(c)

Let
t\geq\ell_{0}+1
. We have

\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t}=z)-G(t)\right|\leq C_{2}\varepsilon.

(3.15)

Proof.

(a)

This follows from Lemma
3.4
(a) and Proposition
3.2
(d).

(b)

By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
(a), we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{j}=x_{j})\geq 1-\ell_{0}\varepsilon
. The result then follows from coupling the trajectories.

(c)

Recall that for
1\leq i\leq k
,
t_{i}=\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}
and
g(t_{i})=\rho_{i}
, by definition. Then the case
j=0
of Lemma
3.4
(b) can be rewritten as

\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(T_{z}=t_{i})-g(t_{i})\right|\leq\rho_{i}C_{1}\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k.

(3.16)

We deduce, since by definition of
G
we have
G(t)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k{\;:\;}t_{i}\leq t}g(t_{i})
, that

\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(T_{z}\leq t)-G(t)\right|\leq C_{1}\varepsilon.

(3.17)

Moreover, we have
\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(T_{z}\leq t)-{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t}=z)\right|\leq 4\varepsilon
by Proposition
3.2
(d). We conclude that
\left|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t}=z)-G(t)\right|\leq(4+C_{1})\varepsilon
.

∎

The next proposition estimates the total variation distance to stationarity for FIT Markov chains.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Recall that
F=1-G
. There exist
C_{3}=C_{3}(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})>0
and
\varepsilon_{3}=\varepsilon_{3}(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})>0
such that the following holds. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{3})
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})_{t\geq 0}
be the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. Denote its stationary distribution by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
. For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\left|\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-F(t)\right|\leq C_{3}\varepsilon.

(3.18)

Proof.

Let
t\geq 0
. First assume that
t\leq\ell_{0}
. Since the chain needs to do at least
\ell_{0}+\ell_{1}>\ell_{0}
steps to reach
z
from
x_{0}
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t}=z)=0
. Therefore, since
\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
by Proposition
3.2
(b), we have
\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\geq\mathrm{d}_{x_{0}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\geq\pi_{\varepsilon}(z)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
. Since
t\leq\ell_{0}
we also have
F(t)=1
. This proves that
\left|\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-F(t)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Now assume that
t\geq\ell_{0}+1
. Let us estimate
\mathrm{d}_{x}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)
for different states
x
. By Proposition
3.2
(b) and (c) we have
\mathrm{d}_{z}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\leq 4\varepsilon
. Moreover for
1\leq i\leq k
and
\ell_{0}+1\leq j\leq\ell_{0}+\ell_{i}-1
,
by Lemma
3.5
(a) and Proposition
3.2
(b) we have
\mathrm{d}_{y_{j}^{i}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\leq(2+C_{2})\varepsilon
. Let
1\leq j\leq\ell_{0}
. By Lemma
3.5
(b) and (c), and since the function
G
is (weakly) increasing, we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{j}}(X_{t}=z)\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t+j}=z)-C_{2}\varepsilon\leq G(t+j)-2C_{2}\varepsilon\geq G(t)-2C_{2}\varepsilon,

(3.19)

so by Proposition
3.2
(b),
\mathrm{d}_{x_{j}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\leq F(t)+(2+2C_{2})\varepsilon
.
Finally, by Lemma
3.5
(c), we have
|{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}}(X_{t}=z)-G(t)|\leq C_{2}\varepsilon
so by Proposition
3.2
(b) we have
|\mathrm{d}_{x_{0}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-F(t)|\leq(C_{2}+2)\varepsilon
.
It follows that

\left|\mathrm{d}_{x_{0}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\right|\leq C\varepsilon,

(3.20)

where
C
is a constant depending only on
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})
.
We conclude by the triangle inequality that

\left|\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-F(t)\right|\leq\left|\mathrm{d}_{x_{0}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-F(t)\right|+\left|\mathrm{d}_{x_{0}}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)-\mathrm{d}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)\right|\leq(C+2+C_{2})\varepsilon.\qed

(3.21)

3.4
Markov chains mixing in a bounded number of steps

Denote by
{\mathcal{A}}
the set of functions from
{\mathbb{N}}=\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\}
to
[0,1]
which are weakly decreasing and such that
\left\{0,1\right\}\subsetneq f({\mathbb{N}})
. For
f\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we set
L(f)=\max\left\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid f(t)=1\right\}
,
M(f)=\max\left\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid f(t)>0\right\}
, and
W(f)=M(f)-L(f)
. In particular,
f(t)
is equal to 1 for
0\leq t\leq L(f)
and equal to 0 for
t>M(f)
, and the only part where
f
behaves non-trivially is for
L(f)<t\leq M(f)
, that is in a
window
of length
W(f)
.
Set

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{A}}\mid W(f)\leq L(f)\right\}.

(3.22)

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{B}}
. There exists
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
such that
f=F_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}
.

Proof.

Let
1=\alpha_{0}>\alpha_{1}>\ldots>\alpha_{k}=0
be the values taken by
f
. Since the image of
f
contains 0 and 1, and at least one element of
(0,1)
, we have
k\geq 2
. Set
\ell_{0}=L(f)
. For
1\leq i\leq k
, set

t_{i}=\min\left\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid f(t)=\alpha_{i}\right\},

(3.23)

and
\ell_{i}=t_{i}-\ell_{0}
. By definition we have
1=\ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{k}\leq\ell_{0}
. Set also for
1\leq i\leq k
,

\rho_{i}=\alpha_{i-1}-\alpha_{i}.

(3.24)

We have
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\rho_{i}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\alpha_{i-1}-\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k}=1
.
Therefore setting
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{0},\ldots,\ell_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{k})
, we have proved that
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
, and by construction we have
F_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}=f
.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{B}}
. There exists a sequence of (discrete time) Markov chains
((X_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}})_{n\geq 1}
such that

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)-f(t)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(3.25)

where for
n\geq 1
,
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\cdot)
is the distance to stationarity for the chain
(X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
there exists
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})\in{\mathcal{P}}
such that
f=F_{(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho})}
. For
n\geq 1
, set
\varepsilon_{n}=e^{-n}
, and let
(X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the FIT the Markov chain with parameters. By Proposition
3.6
we have

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)-f(t)\right|\leq C_{3}\varepsilon_{n},

(3.26)

which tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

A general constraint for the distance to stationarity
\mathrm{d}(t)
of (finite irreducible aperiodic) Markov chains is its submultiplicativity “
\mathrm{d}(t)\leq 2\mathrm{d}(t)^{2}
” (see
[
LP17
, Section 4.7]
). This implies that if we have
\mathrm{d}(t)<1/2
for some time
t\geq 1
, then
\mathrm{d}(\cdot)
must decrease at least exponentially fast: there exists a constant
c>0
such that for any
s\geq 2t
we have
\mathrm{d}(s)\leq e^{-cs/t}
. This in turn implies that it is impossible for
\mathrm{d}(\cdot)
to have long low plateaux. In particular, the result of Proposition
3.8
would not hold anymore if we removed the assumption “
W(f)\leq L(f)
”.

3.5
Every discrete and continuous time profile is possible

We start by proving that every discrete time profile is possible. Note that we do not assume the limiting function
p
to be continuous, nor the
window

w_{n}
or the cutoff time
t_{n}
to diverge.
We recall and prove Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(t_{n})
be a sequence of positive integers and
(w_{n})
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that
w_{n}=o(t_{n})
as
n\to\infty
. Let
p{\;:\;}{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
be a weakly decreasing function (but not necessarily continuous) such that
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to-\infty]{}1
and
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to+\infty]{}0
. There exists a sequence of discrete time Markov chains
((X_{t}^{(n)})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 1}
such that

\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}t_{n}+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t_{n}+cw_{n})-p(c)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(3.27)

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
, set
v_{n}=w_{n}(t_{n}/w_{n})^{1/8}
. In particular as
n\to\infty
we have
w_{n}\lll v_{n}\lll t_{n}
.
Therefore there exists
n_{0}
such that for
n\geq n_{0}
we have
v_{n}\leq t_{n}/3
. For each
n\geq n_{0}
, we define a function
f_{n}{\;:\;}{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,1]
as follows:

f_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ for }0\leq t\leq t_{n}-v_{n},\\
p((t-t_{n})/w_{n})&\text{ for }t_{n}-v_{n}<t<t_{n}+v_{n},\\
0&\text{ for }t\geq t_{n}+v_{n}.\end{cases}

(3.28)

For
n\geq n_{0}
,
f_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}
and since
L(f_{n})\geq t_{n}-v_{n}\geq 2v_{n}\geq W(f_{n})
, we have
f_{n}\in{\mathcal{B}}
. It follows from Proposition
3.8
that for each
n\geq n_{0}
, there exists a Markov chain
(X^{(n)}(t))_{t\geq 0}
such that

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)-f^{(n)}(t)\right|\leq 2^{-n}.

(3.29)

Moreover, by definition of
f_{n}
and monotonicity for
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|f_{n}(t)-p((t-t_{n})/w_{n})\right|&=\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\backslash(t_{n}-v_{n},t_{n}+v_{n})}\left|f_{n}(t)-p((t-t_{n})/w_{n})\right|\\
&\leq|1-p(-v_{n}/w_{n})|+|p(v_{n}/w_{n})-0|\\
&=1-p(-v_{n}/w_{n})+p(v_{n}/w_{n}).\end{split}

(3.30)

Finally, by the triangle inequality and the change of variables “
t=t_{n}+cw_{n}
”, for
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\begin{split}&\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}t_{n}+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t_{n}+cw_{n})-p(c)\right|\\
\leq\,&\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}t_{n}+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t_{n}+cw_{n})-f_{n}(t_{n}+cw_{n})\right|+\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}t_{n}+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|f_{n}(t_{n}+cw_{n})-p(c)\right|\\
=\,&\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)-f_{n}(t)\right|+\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|f_{n}(t)-p((t-t_{n})/w_{n})\right|\\
\leq\,&2^{-n}+1-p(-v_{n}/w_{n})+p(v_{n}/w_{n}),\end{split}

(3.31)

which tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We now recall and prove Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 3.11
.

For every continuous time scaling triplet
((t_{n}),(w_{n}),p)
, there exists a sequence of continuous time Markov chains
((X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}})_{n\geq 1}
such that for every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(t_{n}+cw_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}p(c).

Proof.

Let
((t_{n}),(w_{n}),p)
be a continuous time scaling triplet. By definition,
p
is weakly decreasing,
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to-\infty]{}1
,
p(x)\xrightarrow[x\to+\infty]{}0
, and
w_{n}\lll t_{n}
. By Theorem
1.7
(applied to the same
p
and
(w_{n})
, but to
(\left\lfloor t_{n}\right\rfloor)
), there exists a sequence of discrete time Markov chains
((X_{t}^{(n,\#)})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}})_{n\geq 1}
such that as
n\to\infty
,

\sup_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}{\;:\;}\left\lfloor t_{n}\right\rfloor+cw_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\mathrm{d}^{(n,\#)}(\left\lfloor t_{n}\right\rfloor+cw_{n})-p(c)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(3.32)

Set
u_{n}=\sqrt{w_{n}\sqrt{t_{n}}}
for
n\geq 1
. Since
w_{n}\ggg\sqrt{t_{n}}
by definition of a continuous time scaling triplet, we have
\sqrt{t_{n}}\lll u_{n}\lll w_{n}
. Let
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Poisson process with parameter 1. For each
n\geq 1
, let
(X^{(n)}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}=(X_{N_{t}}^{(n,\#)})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the rate 1 continuous time analog of
(X_{t}^{(n,\#)})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with jump times given by
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Let
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For
n\geq 1
consider the event

E_{n,c}=\left\{\left\lfloor t_{n}+cw_{n}-u_{n}\right\rfloor\leq N_{t_{n}+cw_{n}}\leq\left\lceil t_{n}+cw_{n}+u_{n}\right\rceil\right\}.

(3.33)

Since
u_{n}\ggg\sqrt{t_{n}}\sim\sqrt{t_{n}+cw_{n}}
we have
{\mathbb{P}}(E_{n,c})=1-o(1)
.
Moreover, as
n\to\infty
, by continuity of
p
we have
\mathrm{d}^{(n,\#)}(t)\to p(c)
uniformly over all integers
t\in[\left\lfloor t_{n}+cw_{n}-u_{n}\right\rfloor,\left\lceil t_{n}+cw_{n}+u_{n}\right\rceil]
.
We conclude that

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t_{n}+cw_{n})\to p(c).\qed

(3.34)

4
Pathological examples

In all Markov chains for which there is a sharp phase transition and the behaviour within the phase transition is well understood, there is a unique order of magnitude to zoom in around the cutoff time, and the fluctuations observed are asymptotically continuous. However, in general, even when a sequence of Markov chains exhibits a cutoff, what happens when we further zoom in, in the phase transition, can be arbitrarily complicated. Also, the distance to stationarity can decrease peculiarly even for some conjugacy invariant random walks on groups.

4.1
Different behaviours in uncountably many windows

Let
n\geq 8
. Set
\ell_{0}=n
and
k=k^{(n)}=\left\lfloor\ln\ln n\right\rfloor
. Set
\ell_{i}=\left\lfloor n^{i/(k+1)}\right\rfloor
and
\rho_{i}=\rho_{i}^{(n)}=1/k
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Set
\varepsilon=2^{-n}
. Denote by
(W^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the FIT Markov chain with parameters
(k,\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\rho},\varepsilon)
. As previously, denote
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\cdot)
the associated distance to stationarity. This defines a sequence of Markov chains
((W^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 8}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider the sequence of FIT Markov chains
((W^{(n)}_{t})_{t\geq 0})_{n\geq 8}
defined above. For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
and every
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, as
n\to\infty
we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\left\lfloor n+cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor)\to\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }c\leq 0\\
1-\alpha&\text{ if }c>0.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Starting from the root it is impossible to reach the fruit in
n
steps or less, therefore if
c\leq 0
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}^{(n)}}(W^{(n)}_{\left\lfloor n-cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}=z^{(n)})=0
, and therefore
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\left\lfloor n+cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor)=1-o(1)
.

Assume now that
c>0
. By construction, the probability to always go up before time
\left\lfloor n+cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor
and to take one of the
(\alpha+o(1))k^{(n)}
branches of length at most
\left\lfloor cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor
is
\sum_{i\leq(\alpha+o(1))k^{(n)}}\rho_{i}^{(n)}=\alpha+o(1)
. Therefore we have
{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{0}^{(n)}}(W^{(n)}_{t}=z^{(n)})=\alpha+o(1)
, and it follows that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(\left\lfloor n+cn^{\alpha}\right\rfloor)=1-\alpha+o(1)
.
∎

In the previous example, for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the distance to stationarity at time
n+cn^{\alpha}
is asymptotically a piecewise constant function. In other words, for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there is a non-trivial limiting function when we zoom in in a window of size
\Theta(n^{\alpha})
.

4.2
Pseudo-laziness for random walks on Cayley graphs

All states of FIT Markov chains have a specific and distinct role. One may wonder if complicated mixing time behaviour can arise from very structured objects. For
n\geq 1
denote respectively by
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
the symmetric and alternating groups of index
n
; and by
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{5}^{(n)}
the conjugacy classes of transpositions and 5-cycles.

First consider the sequence of (discrete time) Markov chains whose increment measure is uniform on
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
. There the distance to stationarity is approximately
1
at time
0
, drops to
1/2
at time
1
since the walk is already almost uniform on
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
, and then decreases very slowly (the time scale is
|{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}|/|{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}|\sim n!/n^{2}
), exponentially, to 0.

Similarly, consider consider the sequence of (discrete time) Markov chains whose increment measure is uniform on
{\mathcal{C}}_{5}^{(n)}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
. There with high probability only 5-cycles are picked for a long time. The distance to stationarity is therefore close to 1 up to time
\frac{1}{5}n\ln n
where it abruptly drops to
1/2
since the walk is then almost uniform on
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
, and then decreases slowly (the time scale is
|{\mathcal{C}}_{5}^{(n)}|/|{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}|\asymp n^{3}
), exponentially, to 0.

This illustrates that even conjugacy invariant random walks on groups can have peculiar behaviours. In the examples above, this is due to the fact that to mix one needs to uniformize also the sign of permutations. These examples are almost 2-periodic, and transpositions play the role of laziness.

4.3
An example with countably many nested windows

FIT Markov chains enable constructing very diverse behaviours. It is possible to see a non-trivial behaviour at some zooming scale
(w_{n})
, with a limiting function which decreases continuously on
(-\infty,0)
from
1
to
3/4
and on
(0,+\infty)

One chain to rule them all

Denote by
{\mathcal{D}}
the set of continuous weakly decreasing functions

Let
k\geq 1
. Let

be the set of increasing

5
Comments and open questions

This section contains some comments and questions about profiles for diffusions on compact Lie groups, how to obtain profiles without understanding the eigenvectors, the separation profile for random transpositions, and the constraints on cutoff profiles for vertex-transitive graphs.

Finding cutoff profiles is often done via spectral techniques. This requires a detailed understanding not only of the eigenvalues of a Markov chain, but also of the interactions between its eigenvectors.
Méliot
[
Mé14
]
found explicit formulas for the densities of the Brownian motion on a number of compact symmetric spaces – including the classical simple compact Lie groups – and proved in Theorem 6 that all of them exhibit a cutoff phenomenon. Some computations for special unitary groups point towards compensations between the eigenvectors that involve the trace of matrices. (We note that the number of fixed points of a permutation is the trace of the associated permutation matrix.) This gives hope for finding the cutoff profiles for these diffusions.

Question 5.1
.

Can we find the profiles for the Brownian motions on compact symmetric spaces?

For the random-to-random shuffle, the eigenvalues were found in
[
DS18
]
and the cutoff was proved in
[
BN19
]
. However, the eigenvectors have complicated recursive expressions that are difficult to manipulate, and finding the limiting profile is still open, see the open question in
[
Nes24
, Section 6.2]
.
Recently, Jain and Sawhney
[
JS24
]
found another way to obtain the profile for random transpositions. Instead of understanding the compensations between eigenvectors, as done in
[
Tey20
]
(characters are sums of eigenvectors), they extract the limiting observables (the fixed points) and the associated eigenvalues using probabilistic arguments and discard the other observables via an
L^{2}
bound.
These probabilistic arguments are delicate but offer an alternative way to find profiles that doesn’t rely on understanding the eigenvectors. It might be suited for chains such as the random-to-random shuffle where the eigenvectors are cumbersome to understand.

One may also want to understand cutoff profiles for distances other than the total variation distance. Computing the
L^{2}
distance only requires knowledge of the eigenvalues. In some cases obtaining information on all eigenvalues is difficult, but upon reaching such information the
L^{2}
profile is relatively easy to compute. This is the case (in discrete time) for random transpositions and also for the random-to-random shuffle.

Another distance of particular interest is the separation distance, defined as follows in the case of transitive graphs. Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a finite connected vertex-transitive graph and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the simple random walk on
\Gamma
, in discrete or continuous time, and started at some vertex
o\in V
. For
t\geq 0
, and
x,y\in V
, denote
p_{t}(x,y)={\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}=y)
. For
t\geq 0
the minimum of likelihood at time
t
is the quantity
\mlh(t):=\min_{x\in V}p_{t}(o,x)
, and the separation distance is defined by

\dsep(t):=1-|V|\mlh(t).

(5.1)

Question 5.2
.

For random transpositions on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, in continuous time and started at the identity permutation, is it true that the minimum of likelihood is attained at
n
-cycles for every
t\geq 0
?

Let us sketch how to compute the likelihood of
n
-cycles. For
n\geq 1
, denote by
\Id_{n}
the identity permutation, and let
\gamma_{n}=(1\,\ldots\,n)
, which is an
n
-cycle. Recall that irreducible representations
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are indexed by Young diagrams, and that they index the eigenvalues
e_{\lambda}
of the chain. It also follows from the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule that characters evaluated at an
n
-cycle are equal to
\pm 1
if
\lambda
is hook-shaped, and
0
otherwise. Therefore, by Fourier inversion on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, omitting some technical details and integer parts, we have at
t_{n,c}=\frac{1}{2}n(\ln n+c))

\begin{split}n!p_{t_{n,c}}(\Id_{n},\gamma_{n})=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}}d_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}^{t_{n,c}}\ch^{\lambda}(\gamma_{n})&=o(1)+\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}\binom{n}{j}n^{-j}e^{-jc}(-1)^{j}\\
&=o(1)+\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(-e^{-c})^{j}}{j!}=o(1)+e^{-e^{-c}}.\end{split}

(5.2)

A positive answer to Question
5.2
would therefore imply the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.3
.

Consider, for
n\geq 2
, the random transposition shuffle
(X_{t}^{(n)})_{t\geq 0}
on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, in discrete or continuous time, and denote the associated separation distance to stationarity at time
t\geq 0
by
\mathrm{d}_{\textup{sep}}^{(n)}(t)
. For any
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\mathrm{d}_{\textup{sep}}^{(n)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}n(\ln n+c)\right\rfloor\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1-e^{-e^{-c}}.

(5.3)

In words, the separation profile for random transpositions is given by a Gumbel distribution.
