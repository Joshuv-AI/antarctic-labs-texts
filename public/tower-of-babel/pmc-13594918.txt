Antibiofilm activity of thymol against Salmonella Infantis on polystyrene and stainless steel surfaces
Authors: Geetha Kumar-Phillips, Rohana Liyanage, Anna LFV Assumpcao, Ruvindu Perera, Tanmaie Kalapala, Palmy Jesudhasan, Basanta R Wagle, Annie Donoghue, Komala Arsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella enterica serovar Infantis (S. Infantis) is one of many Salmonella serovars that have gained attention in the past two decades as a leading cause of foodborne illness globally. This serovar is well known for forming biofilms that are resistant to standard cleaning and disinfection protocols used in processing plants. Thymol, a plant-derived monoterpene phenol, has demonstrated antimicrobial activity against many foodborne pathogens, including Salmonella. This study investigated the efficacy of thymol in inhibiting S. Infantis biofilm formation and inactivating established biofilms on common food industry surfaces. A biofilm inhibition assay was performed by allowing S. Infantis to grow and form biofilms at 37°C and 20°C on polystyrene and stainless steel surfaces in the presence or absence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of thymol. The inactivation study examined the effects of thymol at various concentrations (0.125 %, 0.25 %, or 0.5 %) on 48 h old biofilms developed on both surfaces at 37°C and 20°C for 1, 5, or 10 min. The surviving S. Infantis in the biofilms were enumerated by serial dilution and plating. Biofilms were inactivated within 10 min to below detection limits (∼6-7 log reduction) on both surfaces by 0.5 % thymol, whereas a 0.125 % thymol dose reduced counts significantly (3-4 logs) after 1 min on polystyrene and 5 min on stainless steel. Also, treatment with sub-inhibitory concentration of thymol downregulated the expression of S. Infantis genes (P < 0.05) associated with biofilm formation (csgA, csgB, csgD, adrA, crl) and quorum sensing (luxS, sdiA). LC-MS/MS-based proteomic analysis showed that thymol downregulated the expression of proteins associated with bacterial chemotaxis and motility (CheY and ProQ), and upregulated proteins involved in functions such as survival, cell wall maintenance, cellular repair, and adaptation to environmental stresses (P < 0.05). The results suggest that thymol could be used as a natural disinfectant to control S. Infantis biofilms on plastic and steel surfaces commonly found in poultry and food processing facilities.Keywords: Salmonella, Biofilm inhibition, Biofilm inactivation, Natural antimicrobials, Food contact surfaces

Introduction

Non-typhoidal Salmonella is one of the leading causes of foodborne gastroenteritis worldwide. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that various Salmonella enterica serotypes cause approximately 1.35 million infections in the United States, resulting in 26,500 hospitalizations and 420 deaths annually (CDC, 2024). The true burden might be even higher, according to Hoffmann et al. (2025), who describe non-typhoidal Salmonella as the costliest pathogen, with an estimated annual economic burden of $17.1 billion for the U.S economy. Salmonella enterica serovar Infantis (S. Infantis), is one of the top ten serovars of Salmonella enterica, that has gained popularity in the past decade as a foodborne pathogen (Mattock et al., 2024; Torres-Boncompte et al., 2025).

Similar to other Salmonella serovars, poultry meat and products are the most common vehicles for S. Infantis (McMillan et al., 2022; Powell and Williams, 2024; Sohail and Varga, 2025). Despite the stringent control measures and strategies implemented at each level of the farm-to-fork continuum, S. Infantis remains a persistent threat to public health (Betiku et al., 2025). The persistence of S. Infantis can be attributed to several survival strategies, such as acquisition and transmission of antimicrobial resistance, presence of mobile virulence genes, and the ability to form biofilms (Mattock et al., 2024; Musa et al., 2024). Microbial biofilms are populations of the same or different species that live in harmony, embedded in a slimy extracellular matrix and capable of adhering to various surfaces in the food processing industry (Phillips, 2016). Biofilm formation begins when bacteria attach to a biotic or abiotic surface. The initial attachment is followed by multiplication and formation of a monolayer on the surface, producing extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). The EPS consists of polysaccharides, structural proteins, cell debris, and nucleic acids, acting as a protective covering while simultaneously maintaining the nutrients, oxygen, and water required by the bacteria (Aleksandrowicz et al., 2023). At this stage, bacteria become irreversibly attached to the surface, leading to the maturation and dispersal of some planktonic cells, which can then form new biofilms. Attachment to surfaces such as glass, plastic, and stainless steel via biofilm formation helps foodborne bacteria, such as Salmonella, remain viable but dormant until they invade a host organism (Harrell et al., 2021). Previous research has demonstrated that biofilms confer microorganisms with resistance to stressors in food industry environments, including sanitizers, disinfectants, and antimicrobials, thereby ensuring their survival (Alonso et al., 2024; Bridier et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2024).

S. Infantis can readily form biofilms outside its host, especially on surfaces such as plastic and stainless steel, which are commonly seen in poultry production and food processing facilities. It has been shown to form more robust biofilms, typically within 48 h, at lower temperatures (20-25°C), with optimal development observed within this range rather than at body temperature (37°C) (Musa et al., 2024; Obe et al., 2022). However, biofilm formation also depends on the attachment surface and varies among isolates (Obe et al., 2022). Prolonged incubation (up to 72 h or more) at 20°C has been shown to enhance further biofilm accumulation by S. Infantis (Bezek et al., 2023).

Various cleaning and disinfection agents are currently used in the poultry industry, such as phenolic compounds, chlorine compounds, hypochlorite, iodophors, aldehydes, peroxygen compounds, quaternary ammonium compounds, and biguanides (Hansson et al., 2025; Kabir et al., 2021). However, some of these agents are less effective in the presence of organic matter, while others must be used with caution due to their toxic effects. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that suboptimal disinfectant concentrations can lead to the persistence of Salmonella biofilms (Ziech et al., 2016). Thus, eradication of non-typhoidal Salmonella biofilms remains a significant challenge in the food processing and packaging industries, as well as poultry environments.

In this scenario, the use of plant-derived extracts benefits both the food industry and consumers. Plant extracts derived from various plant parts contain bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenes, and phenolic acids, which confer antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and therapeutic properties (Almuzaini, 2023). In the past few decades, various plant-derived extracts have been studied for their antibacterial efficacy against foodborne pathogens (Arsi et al., 2014; Quecan et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2022; Salehi et al., 2018; Sateriale et al., 2023; Upadhyay et al., 2013; Wagle et al., 2019). Thymol (2‐isopropyl‐5‐methylphenol) is a naturally occurring monoterpene phenol found mainly in the essential oils of plants belonging to the Lamiaceae family (Marchese et al., 2016; Salehi et al., 2018). The essential oils of thyme are regarded as GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) substances by the Food & Drug Administration/FDA (21 CFR 182.20). Thymol has been traditionally used for its antimicrobial, antiseptic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifungal properties (Salehi et al., 2018) and shown to possess antibacterial activity against many foodborne pathogens, including Salmonella (Di Rosario et al., 2025; dos Santos et al., 2024; Sateriale et al., 2023; Sepahvand et al., 2021). The antibiofilm activity of thymol against various bacteria has been well documented (Amaral et al., 2015; Čabarkapa et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2017; Miladi et al., 2017; Myszka et al., 2016; Orhan-Yanıkan et al., 2019; Tadielo et al., 2025). However, there is limited research on the effects of thymol on S. Infantis biofilms. Hence, the objective of this study was to investigate the antibiofilm effects of thymol on S. Infantis at 20°C and 37°C on polystyrene and stainless steel surfaces, commonly used in the food industry. Additionally, the effects of thymol on the gene expression and protein profiles of S. Infantis were also investigated.

Materials & methods

Salmonella Infantis strain and culture conditions

S. Infantis (isolated from ground chicken) was cultured in 20 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB, Hardy Diagnostics, Santa Maria, CA) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Following incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 4000 x g for 10 min at 22°C and resuspended in fresh TSB. The resuspended culture was diluted as needed for the experiments and used as the inoculum. The bacterial concentration in the inoculum was determined by plating on Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India), incubating at 37°C for 24 h, and enumerating colonies.

Preparation of stainless steel coupons

Stainless steel coupons (Type 304; diameter 5/8 inch; no. 4 finish) used for the experiments were prepared according to a previously published method (Wagle et al., 2019). Initially, stainless steel coupons were cleaned with acetone and then thoroughly rinsed with distilled water. In the next step, the stainless steel coupons were soaked in 100 % ethanol, then rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and autoclaved at 121°C for 15 min.

Extent of biofilm formation by S. Infantis on different surfaces

The biofilm-forming potential of S. Infantis was determined on two surfaces: polystyrene and stainless steel. For this procedure, a crystal violet-based microtiter plate assay was used with slight modifications (Akinola et al., 2020). S. Infantis culture in TSB (∼ 7.0 log CFU/mL) was seeded onto the wells (1 mL /well) of a 12-well polystyrene plate (Costar, Corning, NY, USA), with or without stainless steel coupon in each well. The plates were incubated at 37°C or 20°C for either 24 h or 48 h to allow biofilm formation, and samples were collected at respective timepoints. Fresh media were not replenished during the incubation period. At each sampling point, the spent media was discarded, and the wells were washed 3 times with Butterfield’s phosphate diluent (BPD). One mL of 1 % (w/v) crystal violet (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to each well, and plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The plates were then washed 5 times with BPD to remove any unbound dye, blotted dry with paper towels, and allowed to dry at room temperature. The bound crystal violet by the bacterial cells in biofilms was extracted by dissolving it in 95 % ethanol (1 mL/well). Plates were allowed to sit at room temperature for 15 min, and then the solution from the wells was transferred to a fresh 96-well plate. The A590 values were measured using a plate reader (Synergy LX, Agilent-Biotek, Santa Clara, CA).

Sub-inhibitory concentration, minimum inhibitory concentration, and minimum bactericidal concentration of thymol

We used previously published methods, with slight modifications, to determine the sub-inhibitory concentration (SIC), minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of thymol against S. Infantis (Upadhyay et al., 2017; Wagle et al., 2019). Two-fold dilutions of thymol (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) in TSB (1 mL/well) were made in sterile 24-well polystyrene plates (Costar, Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, United States) followed by inoculation with an equal volume of S. Infantis in TSB (∼ 7.0 log CFU/mL) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The growth of S. Infantis was determined by plating and enumeration on XLD plates. We also determined the SICs using a Cytation 5 multimode microplate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), as described previously by Robinson et al. (2022). Briefly, two-fold dilutions of thymol were prepared in a sterile 96-well plate containing 100 µL of TSB, and the plate was then inoculated with 100 µL of S. Infantis. Plates were placed in the Cytation 5 multimode microplate reader and incubated at 37°C for 24 h with OD600 readings taken every 30 min. The MBC was determined as the lowest concentration of thymol that reduced the growth of S. Infantis by >5.0 log CFU/mL after incubation at 37°C for 24 h (Shah et al., 2025a). Similarly, the lowest concentration of thymol that significantly inhibited the growth of S. Infantis after incubation at 37°C for 24 h was taken as the MIC. Two of the highest concentrations of thymol below the MIC that did not inhibit bacterial growth were selected as the SICs for the study.

Inhibition of biofilms by thymol on polystyrene plates

The ability of thymol to inhibit S. Infantis biofilm formation on polystyrene plates was determined using a previously published method (Wagle et al., 2019), with modifications. S. Infantis culture (∼7.0 log CFU) was added to each well (1 mL/well) of a sterile 24-well polystyrene plate, followed by the addition of 1 mL of the treatments or TSB for the control. The treatments were the two selected SICs: thymol (0.03 % or 0.015 %) and 0.156 % ethanol. All plates were incubated at two temperatures (20°C or 37°C) for 48 h, and biofilm formation was assessed at 24 h intervals. At each sampling point, the spent medium from the wells was removed, and the wells were gently washed with BPD. The wash step was repeated three times, after which 1 mL of BPD was added to all wells. The biofilms were then dislodged with a cell scraper, thoroughly mixed with BPD, serially diluted, and plated onto XLD agar plates. The bacteria in S. Infantis biofilms were enumerated after incubation at 37°C for 24 h.

Inhibition of biofilms by thymol on stainless steel coupons

We used a previously published method (Wagle et al., 2019), with slight modifications, to determine the effect of thymol on inhibiting biofilm formation by S. Infantis on stainless steel coupons. Stainless steel coupons used for the study were prepared as mentioned above. The prepared stainless steel coupons were placed in each well of a 12-well plate, to which 1 mL of S. Infantis culture (∼7.0 log CFU) in TSB was added, followed by 1 mL of treatment solutions. The treatments included TSB (control), 0.156 % ethanol in TSB, 0.03 % thymol in TSB, and 0.015 % thymol in TSB. Plates were incubated at two temperatures (20°C or 37°C) for 48 h, and biofilm formation was assessed at 24 h intervals. At each sampling point, stainless steel coupons were transferred onto a sterile plate and gently washed with BPD. After repeating the wash step three times, 1 mL of BPD was added to all wells with coupons. The biofilms from the coupons were then removed using a cell scraper into the solution, serially diluted, and plated onto XLD agar plates. The bacteria in S. Infantis biofilms were enumerated after incubation at 37°C for 24 h.

Inactivation of mature biofilms by thymol on polystyrene plates

Inactivation of mature S. Infantis biofilms by thymol was determined according to a previously published method (Wagle et al., 2019). S. Infantis culture was seeded onto 24-well plates at the rate of 1 mL/well and incubated at two temperatures (20°C or 37°C) for 48 h for the biofilms to form. Once mature biofilms were formed, inactivation was performed using 1 mL of thymol at 3 concentrations (0.5 %, 0.25 %, or 0.125 %) for 1, 5, or 10 min. The treatment solution was then discarded, and 1 mL of Dey-Engley neutralizing broth (Difco Laboratories, Sparks, MD, United States) was added to all the wells. The surviving S. Infantis in the biofilms were extracted into neutralizing broth using a cell scraper, mixed well, serially diluted, and plated onto XLD agar plates. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 24 h to enumerate the colonies.

Inactivation of mature biofilms by thymol on stainless steel coupons

Stainless coupons used for the study were prepared as mentioned above. Biofilms were allowed to form by seeding a S. Infantis culture into 24-well plates containing stainless steel coupons at 1 mL/well and incubating at 2 temperatures (20°C or 37°C) for 48 h. After biofilm formation, the steel coupons were rinsed 3 times with BPD and transferred to a sterile polystyrene plate. The steel coupons were then exposed to different concentrations of thymol (0.5 %, 0.25 % or 0.125 %) in sterile distilled water for 1, 5, or 10 min. After the treatments, the steel coupons were washed with BPD and transferred to centrifuge tubes containing sterile glass beads and 1 mL of Dey-Engley neutralizing broth. The tubes were then vortexed for a minute to detach the bacterial biofilms from the steel coupons, after which they were serially diluted and plated onto XLD agar plates. The agar plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h to enumerate colonies.

Bacterial viability assay

To assess the effects of treatments on bacterial viability in biofilms, a bacterial viability assay was performed using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight kit (Balasubramanian et al., 2023). Biofilms were formed on 24-well polystyrene plates by incubating them at 20°C in TSB with S. Infantis (∼7.0 log CFU) for 48 h. The wells were washed 3 times with BPD, treated with TSB alone (control) or with thymol at different concentrations in TSB, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The solutions from the wells were discarded, and the wells were washed gently with BPD. Afterward, 1 mL of BPD was added to all the treatments. The biofilms from the coupons were then removed using a cell scraper into the solution and stained with a LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen, Inc.) to determine bacterial cell viability. Briefly, 100 µL of bacterial cells were added to 900 µL of PBS, after which 6 µL of a stain mixture [SYTO 9 + propidium iodide] was added to the cells and incubated for 15 min at room temperature, protected from light. The stained bacterial cell solution (10 µL) was transferred to a 12-well glass-bottom plate and examined using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Itasca, IL, USA). Live cells fluoresce green, and dead cells (with compromised cell membranes) fluoresce a reddish-orange color.

Gene expression analysis of S. Infantis biofilms exposed to thymol

The effect of thymol on the expression of genes critical for S. Infantis biofilm formation was determined using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) (Shah et al., 2025a; Wagle et al., 2019). S. Infantis (∼6.0 log CFU/mL) was incubated in the presence or absence of 0.015 % thymol at 37°C for 48 h and then harvested. The total RNA was extracted using the Quick-RNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, United States), and complementary DNAs were prepared using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., CA, United States). The primers (Table 1) were designed using the PrimerQuest tool from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, IA, United States) and purchased from the same company. The amplified products were detected by using SYBR Green reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). Samples were analyzed on the QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to quantify the target genes. The 16S rRNA gene was used as the endogenous control, and the comparative critical threshold (ΔΔCt) method was employed to analyze the relative expression of the candidate genes. Similar RNA concentrations were used for cDNA synthesis to minimize concentration variation across samples and treatments. qRT-PCR analysis showed that the variation in 16S rRNA amplification cycle (Ct) values was less than 0.3 cycles across treatments, indicating that the thymol treatments did not affect Salmonella's 16S rRNA expression. These results validate the 16S gene as a stable reference gene for normalization in this experiment.

Proteomic analysis of S. Infantis in biofilms when exposed to thymol

Thymol’s effect on the proteome of S. Infantis was studied using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), as previously described (Wagle et al., 2019). S. Infantis (∼7.0 Log CFU/mL) was incubated in the presence or absence of 0.015 % thymol for 48 h at 37°C. Following BPD washing, proteins were extracted using B-PER™ bacterial protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were also extracted from an overnight pure culture of S. Infantis incubated at 37°C, to represent the planktonic cells. The protein lysates obtained from biofilm and planktonic cells were vortexed and processed using S-Trap™ sample processing technology with the S-Trap™ mini-MS sample prep kit (K02-mini-80, Protifi LLC, Fairport, NY) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were solubilized in Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS), then reduced, alkylated, and acidified. The samples were then transferred to the top of an S-Trap™ Mini Spin Column and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 sec to trap proteins. After cleaning the protein, the S-Trap micro columns were transferred into clean 2 mL sample tubes and incubated overnight at 37°C with trypsin to digest the protein. Peptides were then eluted from the digested samples using Elution buffers, and the eluted peptides were pooled as directed in the manual. The pooled samples were vacuum-dried, resuspended in 300 µL of 0.1 % formic acid, vortexed and sonicated, and then used for peptide fractionation using Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase Fractionation Kit. The manufacturer’s protocol was used to produce eight fractions from each digest. Each fraction was evaporated, reconstituted in 50 µL of 0.1 % formic acid, and transferred into Snap Ring HPLC autosampler vials for mass spectrometry. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to a Bruker AmaZon SL quadrupole ion trap mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltoniks Inc., Billerica, MA, USA) as described previously (Jannasch et al., 2024; Wagle et al., 2019). The unreviewed proteins (TrEMBL) were identified by matching MS/MS spectra to S. Infantis protein sequences available at uniprot.org using in-house MASCOT software (Matrix Science Inc., Boston, MA, United States). The proteins were identified with a false discovery rate < 1 %, using at least 1 unique peptide per protein.

Statistical analysis

We employed a completely randomized design with triplicate samples, and each experiment was repeated three times. Bacterial counts were logarithmically transformed to ensure homogeneity of variance in the bacterial inhibition and inactivation assays (Shah et al., 2025b). All results of the inhibition and inactivation assays were analyzed using two-way ANOVA at a significance level of P < 0.05 for statistical differences, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. The gene expression results were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák's multiple comparisons test. All the analyses above were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 10, San Diego, CA, USA). The results of the proteomic analysis in MASCOT files were analyzed using Scaffold Proteome Software version 5.3 (Proteome Software Inc., Portland, OR, United States). The differentially expressed proteins between treatment groups (control biofilms, treated biofilms, and planktonic cells) were determined using Student’s t-test.

Results

Biofilm formation by S. Infantis on different surfaces

The biofilm-forming potential of S. Infantis was determined on two surfaces: polystyrene plates and stainless steel coupons. Fig. 1 shows the biofilm formation of S. Infantis on the two surfaces at two different temperatures (37°C and 20°C). The mean absorbance indicates the metabolically active S. Infantis in the biofilms attached to the surfaces. Time affected biofilm formation on both surfaces, with increased biofilm observed at 48 h. However, statistical differences were found only with the steel coupons at 37°C, whereas with polystyrene plates, the effect was evident only at 20°C. It was also found that temperature affected biofilm formation, with greater biofilm formation at 20°C than at 37°C, and this effect was significant only with polystyrene plates. Stainless steel coupons at both temperatures showed no significant differences in biofilm formation.

Sub-inhibitory concentrations and minimum bactericidal concentration of thymol for S. Infantis

The SIC of thymol was determined based on bacterial enumeration performed using serial dilution and plating (Fig. 2). The lowest concentration of thymol that reduced the growth of S. Infantis significantly (>2 Log10 CFU/mL) after an incubation at 37°C for 24 h was taken as the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). The two concentrations below the MIC (0.03 % & 0.0156 %) were selected as SICs and were used for the experiments. Additionally, the lowest thymol concentration that showed no visible bacterial growth on XLD plates was taken as the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC). The results were consistent with the growth curve analysis performed with the Cytation 5 microplate reader (data not shown).

Effect of sub-inhibitory concentrations (SICs) of thymol on biofilm formation of S. Infantis on polystyrene and stainless steel surface

The effects of different SICs on the inhibition of biofilm formation of S. Infantis on polystyrene plates and stainless steel coupons are shown in Fig. 3. Polystyrene plates showed a significant reduction in biofilm formation at 37°C and 24 h post-treatment (∼2.5 log CFU/mL) with 0.03 % thymol (Fig. 3A). Although a slight reduction was observed at 48 h on the polystyrene plate compared to the control, this difference was not significant. The 0.015 % thymol SIC at 37°C showed no significant reduction in biofilm formation compared to the control at both time points on polystyrene plates. However, significant reductions in biofilm formation were observed at 37°C on stainless steel coupons at both 24 h and 48 h post-treatment (Fig. 3B) with 0.03 % thymol (∼1.3 and 0.95 log CFU/mL respectively). At 20°C, thymol at 0.03 % reduced S. Infantis counts by ∼3-3.5 log CFU/mL, whereas 0.015 % thymol reduced counts by ∼1-2 log CFU/mL on polystyrene surface compared to the control (Fig. 3C). Reduction in S. Infantis counts (∼0.5-3.5 log CFU/mL) were observed on the steel coupons at 20°C with both thymol concentrations (0.015 % and 0.03 %) at both timepoints (Fig. 3D). Although a significant decline in biofilm formation was not observed at 24 h post-treatment with 0.015 % thymol, the other treatments were significant on stainless steel coupons at 20°C.

Inactivation of mature biofilms by thymol on polystyrene plates at different temperatures

The effect of thymol on the inactivation of mature biofilms of S. Infantis developed on polystyrene plates at 37°C and 20°C is shown in Figs. 4A and 4B, respectively. The counts of S. Infantis recovered from the biofilms of control samples (no treatment) at 37°C and 20°C were ∼ 7.18 and 7.48 log CFU/mL, respectively. Treatment with 2.5 % ethanol (concentration of ethanol in 0.5 % thymol solution) showed no reduction in bacterial load as compared to controls, indicating that ethanol at this concentration did not have any effect in reducing S. Infantis counts. Treatment with thymol at 0.5 % for 1 min (Fig. 4A) resulted in a significant reduction in bacterial counts (∼5 log CFU/mL) at 37°C. For the same treatment at 37°C for 5 and 10 min, no bacteria were detected; therefore, an approximate value of 0.9 log CFU/mL (below the detection limit of 1 log CFU/mL) was assigned for data analysis. The treatments with 0.125 % and 0.25 % thymol at 37°C were able to reduce the counts by 4-5 log following exposure for 1 min, whereas after exposure for 5 and 10 min the counts were decreased by ∼ 5-6 log (Fig. 4A). Similarly, the treatment with 0.5 % thymol at 20°C (Fig. 4B) for 1 min was able to reduce S. Infantis counts significantly (∼ 6 log CFU/mL), which was observed to be 1 log CFU/mL more than the reduction seen at 37°C. As with treatments at 37°C, increasing the exposure time to 5 and 10 min at 20°C reduced the counts to below detectable levels. Additionally, treatments with 0.125 % and 0.25 % thymol at 20°C reduced the counts by 5.17-6.09 log CFU/mL, 5.85-6.36 log CFU/mL, and 6.27-6.70 log CFU/mL after 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Inactivation of mature biofilms by thymol on stainless steel coupons at different temperatures

Fig. 5 shows the effect of thymol in inactivating mature biofilms of S. Infantis developed on stainless steel coupons at two different temperatures (37°C and 20°C). S. Infantis counts in the biofilms developed at 37°C on steel coupons for the control samples, which received no treatment, were ∼ 6.6 log CFU/mL. Similarly, the biofilms in the control samples at 20°C had a slightly higher count of ∼7 log CFU/mL. As observed with the treatment on polystyrene plates, 2.5 % ethanol did not produce a significant reduction in counts compared to the controls at any time point. Additionally, increasing thymol concentration and exposure time reduced biofilm counts on steel coupons. Both at 37°C (Fig. 5A) and 20°C (Fig. 5B), the counts of S. Infantis were reduced to below detection limits only after an exposure of 10 min to 0.5 % thymol. Treatment with the lowest dose of thymol (0.125 %) for 1, 5, and 10 min reduced bacterial counts by ∼2, 3, and 4 log, respectively, at 37°C. Exposure to 0.25 % thymol also produced similar reductions in bacterial load (∼3, 4, and 5 log reductions compared to the control) at 37°C across the three selected exposure times. With a reduction in temperature from 37°C to 20°C, exposure to different concentrations of thymol resulted in slightly higher reductions in S. Infantis counts. Treatments with both 0.25 % and 0.125 % thymol at 20°C yielded nearly identical results, with reductions in bacterial counts of 4-5.5 log and 3.6-5.1 log, respectively.

Effect of thymol on the viability of S. Infantis in biofilms

The effect of different concentrations of thymol, compared to the control (no treatment), on the viability of S. Infantis in biofilms was visualized using fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 6). It was observed that the biofilm cells in the control groups had their cell membrane intact, since majority of cells were green-stained (stained with SYTO). For the biofilms treated with different concentrations of thymol, a mixture of green and red cells (dead cells stained with Propidium iodide) was observed. As the thymol concentration increased, number of red-stained cells increased, indicating increase of cell membrane damage associated with cell death.

Effect of thymol on the expression of S. Infantis genes influential on biofilm formation

The effect of thymol on the expression of genes critical for biofilm formation in S. Infantis is shown in Fig. 7. The expression of all nine genes was found to be downregulated in the biofilms treated with 0.015 % thymol as compared to the controls by ∼ 2-fold (P < 0.05), except for fimA. All the genes involved in the synthesis of extracellular polymeric substances and curli fimbriae in Salmonella (csgA, csgB, csgD, adrA, and crl) were found to be downregulated by ∼ 2-fold. Similarly, the genes responsible for quorum sensing (sdiA and luxS), antibiotic tolerance, and increased Salmonella virulence (gyrA) were also found to be downregulated. The fimA gene, responsible for Salmonella adhesion to surfaces, was downregulated; however, it did not differ significantly from the control.

Effect of thymol on the proteomic profile of S. Infantis

A total of 745 proteins were identified in the proteome profile of S. Infantis with a < 1 % false discovery rate and at least 1 unique peptide from a protein. Proteins with a fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a P value ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated or downregulated, respectively. Compared to the planktonic cells, a total of 74 proteins were upregulated, and 78 proteins were downregulated in the biofilm cells grown with 0.15 % thymol added to the media. Similarly, 60 proteins were upregulated and 75 downregulated in biofilm cells without treatment compared to planktonic cells (data not shown). Table 2 shows the significantly upregulated proteins in biofilm cells treated with 0.15 % thymol compared with those in untreated biofilm cells, as well as in planktonic cells. The SIC of thymol at 0.15 % significantly upregulated proteins (P ≤ 0.05) that are critical for bacterial survival in nutrient-limited environments (PatD, RraA), efflux pump transport (AcrA, DKR80_05365), maintaining cell wall integrity (DacC, C9E95_17050), as well as survival, cellular repair, and adaptation to stresses in the environment (YhbO, AdhP, FrdC, AtpF, SEENIN0B_03912). The downregulated proteins in biofilm cells treated with thymol were compared only with those in untreated biofilm cells (Table 3). Thymol was found to significantly downregulate proteins involved in bacterial chemotaxis (CheY, ProQ), survival in nutrient-limited conditions (GlpX, PepN, Rpe), and adaptation to stress (CDW98_08830).

Discussion

Research on the inhibition and inactivation of biofilms formed by different pathogens has been extensively studied using various natural antimicrobials; however, studies on the control of S. Infantis biofilms remain limited. It has been found that S. Infantis can readily form biofilms on surfaces such as plastic and stainless steel within 48 h, even at lower temperatures (Musa et al., 2024; Obe et al., 2022). Therefore, for this study, we developed biofilms of S. Infantis on polystyrene plates and stainless steel coupons for 48 h at 37°C and 20°C. For the inhibition study, two sub-inhibitory concentrations (below the MIC and capable of inhibiting growth and altering bacterial physiology) were selected, and these SIC doses reduced biofilm formation at both 20°C and 37°C. The SIC doses were effective in reducing the S. Infantis counts at 48 h on polystyrene and stainless steel surfaces at 20°C (Fig. 3C & 3D) by ∼ 2-3 log CFU/mL. At 37°C, we found a significant reduction of ∼ 1-2 log CFU/mL on both surfaces, except for the reduction on the polystyrene plate at 48 h, which was not significant compared to the controls (Fig. 3A & 3B). The log reduction observed at 48 h on polystyrene plates (∼0.5 log CFU/mL) was slightly lower than that observed for four Salmonella strains (∼1-2 log CFU/cm2) treated with thymol at 37°C on polypropylene coupons, as reported by Amaral and others (2015). Although thymol reduced S. Infantis biofilm formation, the reduction at 48 h was less significant than at 24 h, and our results are similar to those of Wagle et al. (2019) on Campylobacter jejuni. The exact cause of thymol's reduced efficacy over time is not known. However, it can be inferred that this may be due to volatile losses or thymol degradation or metabolism by S. Infantis.

Inactivation of mature biofilms was performed at bactericidal concentrations of thymol, based on the hypothesis that thymol disrupts bacterial cell membranes, leading to cell lysis. For this experiment, we developed biofilms on polystyrene and steel surfaces for 48 h, as determined by our crystal violet assays (Fig. 1) and previous studies (Musa et al., 2024; Obe et al., 2022), which indicate that S. Infantis biofilms mature within 48 h. Thymol at the highest concentration used (0.5 %) inactivated mature biofilms within 10 min, reducing them to below detection limits (∼5-7 log CFU/mL) on both polystyrene and stainless steel surfaces. Also, the lowest dose of 0.125 % thymol reduced counts significantly when treated for 1 min on polystyrene plates (∼ 4-5 log CFU/mL) and 5 min on stainless steel coupons (∼ 2-3.5 log CFU/mL), similar to reductions in four Salmonella strains (∼ 1-5 log CFU/cm2) obtained by Amaral et al., 2015. Studies with different phytochemicals against various pathogens have also reported reductions comparable to those observed in the present study (Guillín et al., 2021; Shah et al., 2025a, b; Upadhyay et al., 2013; Wagle et al., 2019). However, establishing the antimicrobial potential of thymol warrants further investigation into its efficacy against other Salmonella serovars.

Studies with natural antimicrobials have shown that these compounds at their SIC levels can regulate the expression of genes critical for biofilm formation and virulence (Alni et al., 2020; Palomares-Navarro et al., 2023). Research specifically on the expression of genes and proteins involved in S. Infantis biofilm formation when treated with thymol or other phytochemicals is limited. Therefore, we performed a gene expression study and a proteomic profiling to evaluate changes in gene and protein expression in S. Infantis upon treatment with 0.015 % thymol. Genes were selected based on research on the effects of various natural antimicrobials on other Salmonella serovars and S. Infantis (Carneiro et al., 2024; Guillín et al., 2021; Musa et al., 2024). Curli, one of the main structural components of biofilms, is an amyloid-like protein that promotes cell colonization of surfaces and is involved in cell-cell interactions. This protein is encoded by the genes csgBAC and csgDEFG, which encode the structural and accessory proteins of curli (Carneiro et al., 2024). Cellulose, another component of biofilm, provides structural integrity and resistance to environmental stressors (Solano et al., 2002). The CsgD protein serves as the central regulator of biofilm formation by controlling the expression of the csg operons. It is also involved in cellulose production by activating transcription of the adrA gene (Liu et al., 2014). The genes luxS, encoding the autoinducer AI-2, and sdiA, encoding the transcriptional activator protein that recognizes acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs), are involved in quorum sensing in bacteria (Carneiro et al., 2024). This study found that thymol downregulated genes involved in extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) production and quorum sensing, which are critical for biofilm formation and maintenance. All the genes tested, which are involved in the synthesis of extracellular polymeric substances and curli fimbriae in Salmonella (csgA, csgB, csgD, adrA, and crl), were found to be downregulated by ∼ 2-fold. Similarly, both quorum-sensing genes, luxS and sdiA, as well as the virulence gene gyrA, were downregulated. The fimA gene, which encodes the major fimbrial subunit, was downregulated but did not differ significantly from the control. Our results agree with those of Guillín et al. (2021), who conducted similar studies on biofilms of S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis using essential oil from Lippia origanoides, which contains thymol and carvacrol. Similar reductions in gene expression were observed by Alni et al. (2020) using essential oils of Allium sativum and Cuminum cyminum on biofilms of S. Typhimurium.

The effect of thymol on the protein profile of S. Infantis in biofilms was determined using an LS-MS/MS-based protein identification and quantification. We identified a total of 745 proteins in the proteome profile of S. Infantis, with a false discovery rate < 1 % and at least 1 unique peptide per protein. Biofilms that were developed with 0.015 % thymol in the media were found to have 27 proteins upregulated compared to biofilms developed with no treatment. When these altered proteins were compared with those expressed in planktonic cells, the number of significantly upregulated proteins decreased to 13. Also, biofilms treated with thymol showed 6 proteins downregulated compared to untreated biofilms. The usual proteins involved in curli and cellulose production in Salmonella are encoded by the csg and bcs operons, respectively (MacKenzie et al., 2017). However, in our study, we did not find these usual proteins in the proteomic profile of S. Infantis biofilms. Due to nutrient deprivation, the majority of cells in the biofilms might be in a dormant state, which slows metabolic activity, and these proteins might have been present below the detection limits of LS-MS/MS.

The proteins involved in quorum sensing (LuxS), adhesion (FimA), and virulence (GyrA) were detected in the biofilms of S. Infantis, with no significant differences observed between treatments. In our study, the proteins CheY and ProQ, which are involved in bacterial chemotaxis and motility, were downregulated in the thymol treatment group. Mature biofilms of S. Typhimurium were found to have upregulation of the gene cheY required for motility and chemotaxis (Hamilton et al., 2009). The RNA chaperone ProQ is essential for proper expression of the flagellar pathway, survival under oxidative stress, and biofilm formation (Bergman et al., 2025). Aminopeptidase N (PepN) was also downregulated in thymol-treated biofilms. The absence of this enzyme was found to help E. coli survive in sodium salicylate-induced stress conditions (Chandu and Nandi, 2003). Thus, the downregulation of PepN in S. Infantis might be an attempt to overcome thymol-induced stress. Three other proteins, namely, Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (GlpX), Ribulose-phosphate 3-epimerase (Rpe), and Isoaspartyl dipeptidase (CDW98_08830), were also found to be downregulated in thymol-treated biofilm cells. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase is one of the enzymes responsible for the irreversible steps in gluconeogenesis in Salmonella enterica (Han et al., 2025). Among the upregulated proteins were efflux pump transporters, which are typically observed in biofilms of bacteria such as S. Typhimurium and E. coli (Aleksandrowicz et al., 2023). We also report that other proteins required for bacterial survival, cell wall maintenance, cellular repair, and adaptation to environmental stresses were upregulated in thymol-treated biofilms compared with untreated control biofilms.

To conclude, thymol has demonstrated the ability to reduce biofilm formation and inactivate fully developed biofilms on polystyrene and stainless steel coupons at both 37°C and 20°°C, which are common surfaces and temperatures in food processing environments. Thymol could thus be used to decontaminate surfaces in food processing facilities, thereby reducing product contamination. However, the effect of thymol on S. Infantis in chicken carcasses or other products has not been evaluated and represents an opportunity for future studies. Additionally, investigating the impact of thymol on mixed biofilms of Salmonella serovars prevalent in poultry environments offers a promising avenue for future research.
