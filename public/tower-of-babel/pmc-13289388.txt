Maternal BMI associations with cord blood DNA methylation and mid-childhood adiposity
Authors: Grace Brolly, Miranda Gurra, Denise M. Scholtens, William L. Lowe, Marie-France Hivert, Jami L. Josefson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood obesity is common and associated with adverse health outcomes. Fetal programming via epigenetics is a potential mechanism underlying its pathogenesis. We conducted an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) on cord blood DNA to identify DNA methylation sites that may mediate the association of maternal body mass index (BMI) with offspring adiposity using data from the Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome (HAPO) Study and its follow-up study (HAPO FUS). HAPO was a prospective, multicenter, international observational study that recruited pregnant women between 2000 and 2006 for glucose tolerance testing; cord blood was collected at delivery and newborn anthropometrics were obtained. The HAPO FUS was conducted from 2013 to 2016, where the 10–14 year-old offspring underwent measures of body composition, anthropometrics, and a fasting glucose tolerance test. Eligibility for HAPO FUS included gestational age at delivery ≥ 37 weeks without major neonatal malformations. There were 3,243 samples with cord blood DNA methylation (cbDNAm) data; mean child age at follow-up was 11.5 years. The present study used cord blood DNA to conduct methylation profiling using the Infinium MethylationEPIC 850 K BeadChip. Linear regression models were used to test the association between maternal BMI and cbDNAm levels adjusting for population substructure, cell count, maternal and child co-variates; multiple testing was accounted for using Bonferroni correction. Mediation analysis tested if cbDNAm CpG sites that were associated (Bonferroni P < 0.05) with maternal BMI explained the known association between maternal BMI and child BMI.ResultsThis analysis included 3,116 mother-child pairs, 48% White, 21% Asian, 19% Black, 12% Hispanic and < 1% other race/ethnicity self-identified by the mother; 36% of mothers and 28.3% of children had an overweight or obese BMI. Maternal BMI was associated with DNAm at 7 CpG sites following adjustment including: cg00579423, cg07138793, cg12188424, cg19345626, cg20020844, cg02988288 and cg26974062. The 2 CpG sites on the TXNIP gene have been identified in previous EWAS of glucose metabolism and diabetes. Cord blood DNA methylation at cg20020844 (SP6) demonstrated mediation of 1.2% of the association between maternal BMI and child BMI z-score.ConclusionsExposure to maternal obesity in utero and subsequent differential methylation present at birth may contribute to the prenatal programing of childhood obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02119-8.

Background

The prevalence of obesity in childhood and adolescence is nearly 20% in the United States according to the recent Center for Disease Control data [1]. In 2022, over 390 million children and adolescents worldwide, nearly 20%, were overweight or obese, and adolescent obesity has quadrupled since 1990 [2]. Contributing risk factors and potential causes include genetic, psychological, lifestyle, nutritional, environmental, and hormonal factors [3, 4]. Obesity in childhood is associated with numerous comorbidities [3, 4] and often requires more complex management over time. Previous studies highlight the strong association between childhood obesity and obesity in adulthood, with obese children and adolescents around five times more likely to be obese in adulthood than those who were not obese [5]. Thus, prevention of obesity in childhood may improve health outcomes and minimize the effect of later complications [4, 5]. Maternal obesity is a risk factor for childhood obesity as established by numerous epidemiological studies including the Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome (HAPO) Follow-up Study (FUS), which showed that maternal BMI during pregnancy had significant, positive associations with childhood obesity and direct measures of adiposity at ages 10 to 14 years [6–8]. Previous publications report that maternal overweight and obesity are associated with an 89% and 264% increase in odds of child obesity, respectively [9–12]. Increased paternal BMI is also a risk factor for childhood obesity [12]. However, multiple studies illustrate that maternal BMI has a greater effect on childhood obesity than paternal BMI, supporting possible contribution of in utero programming by maternal dysmetabolism [13, 14]. Epigenetic programming via differential offspring DNA methylation (DNAm) in utero is a potential mechanistic link via which maternal BMI may program the development of childhood obesity [9, 15]. Cord blood DNAm provides an indirect proxy of in utero exposures. Cord blood DNAm levels at specific CpG sites have been associated with child adiposity measures and further have been suggested to mediate the relationship between maternal factors and childhood obesity outcomes [16, 17].

A few studies have reported associations between maternal BMI, DNAm, and childhood obesity [17–19]. Significant methylation sites vary among the cohorts. These inconsistencies may be due, in part, to limitations of cohort size, older methylation arrays, different ancestries, and/or inability to adjust for potential confounders.

The HAPO Study and HAPO FUS investigated maternal metabolism in pregnancy and offspring metabolic outcomes at birth and ages 10–14 years in a large, multinational, racially and ethnically diverse cohort [6, 8]. The present study addresses some of the above limitations of prior epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) and reports discovery of differential DNAm in cord blood linked to maternal BMI using robust methods and accounting for a diverse population background. A subsequent mediation analysis was conducted with the identified significant CpG sites to determine if cbDNAm mediated the association between maternal BMI and childhood adiposity phenotypes.

Methods

Mother-child pairs who were part of HAPO and HAPO FUS, had cbDNAm data, and child anthropometric measures at HAPO FUS were included in this analysis resulting in 3,116 samples (Additional file 1). The HAPO and HAPO FUS methods have been published [6, 8]. Protocols were approved by the institutional review board at each field center. The field centers included Bangkok, Thailand; Barbados; Belfast, Northern Ireland; Bellflower, CA, USA; Chicago, IL, USA; Cleveland, OH, USA; Hong Kong, China; Manchester, England; Petah-Tikva, Israel; and Toronto, Ontario, Canada. All participants provided written informed consent and assent, where required.

Participants

Briefly, in HAPO, conducted 2000–2006, pregnant women underwent a study visit at approximately 28 weeks’ gestation with measurements of height, weight, blood pressure, and a fasting 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) [6]. Participants diagnosed with gestational diabetes based on a fasting glucose > 105 mg/dl or a 2-hr glucose > 200 mg/dl were removed from the study. Demographic data including self-identified racial/ethnic group, smoking, and alcohol use were collected from questionnaires. Newborn weight and length were measured as previously described.

Offspring of HAPO mothers were recruited for HAPO FUS during 2013–2016 with eligibility based on completion of HAPO, gestational age at delivery ≥ 37 weeks, and no major neonatal malformations or fetal/neonatal death (Additional file: Methods) [8].

Cord blood collection and DNA methylation

Umbilical cord blood was collected at delivery, and DNA was extracted from whole blood and stored at -80 °C. DNA was quantified with the Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Genome-wide methylation profiling was conducted with the Infinium MethylationEpic Beadchip v1.0 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) array which interrogates > 850,000 CpG sites at single-nucleotide resolution. To perform bisulfite conversion, a 500 ng DNA sample was used followed by the Illumina protocol for methylation profiling. DNA standards of confirmed European White, Mexican, African-Caribbean, Han Asian, and Thailand ancestries were obtained from Coriell Institute for Medical Research (Camden, NJ) and duplicate samples were included for quality control purposes. In order to maintain comparable distributions of sex and self-reported race/ethnicity, samples were randomly assigned to column/row locations across plates. BeadChips were scanned with an Illumina iScan and then analyzed using the Illumina GenomeStudio software. Samples were run at the Center for Genetic Medicine (NUSeq Core) at Northwestern University by the same two operators as close in time as possible to minimize bias (Additional file: Methods).

Child anthropometrics

At the study visit, trained research staff measured child’s weight to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated scale and height with a stadiometer to the nearest 0.5 cm. Calibrated calipers (Harpenden, Baty, U.K.) were used to measure skinfolds at three sites: triceps, subscapular, and suprailiac to the nearest 0.1 mm. All measures were performed in duplicate, and a third measurement was done if results differed by > 0.5 kg for weight, > 1.0 cm for height, or > 1.0 mm for skinfolds. Body composition was measured by air displacement plethysmography (Bod Pod, Cosmed, Italy) which provided fat mass and percent body fat [8].

DNA methylation data processing

All preprocessing of methylation data files was performed in R (version 4.1) using the minfi R package (version 1.40.0). Processing procedures including filtering of probes by mean detection p-values and signal quality, batch correction using ComBat in the sva R package (version 3.13), quantile normalization with the minfi R package (version 1.40.0), and removal of probes on sex chromosomes, SNP-associated probes, and cross-reactive probes, leaving a total of 791,359 CpG sites for analysis [20]. After exclusions as displayed in the flowchart (Additional file 1), there were n = 3116 unique HAPO participants on which the EWAS was performed. Cord blood cell counts for B-cell, CD4T, CD8T, granulocyte, monocyte, neutrophil, and nRBC were estimated using the estimateCellCounts function in the minfi R package (version 1.40.0), specifying flow sorted cord blood Illumina Human Methylation EPIC data as the reference [20]. DNAm beta values were transformed to M-values to improve normality for statistical analysis (further details of methods provided in Additional files: Methods).

Outcomes and predictors

The primary predictor was maternal BMI measured at ~ 28 weeks gestation as a continuous variable. Since BMI was measured mid-pregnancy during the HAPO Study, comparable category limits were obtained from a regression model of BMI and gestational age at the visit to yield the following categories (kg/m2): < 22.6 (underweight), 22.6–28.4 (normal range), 28.5–32.9 (overweight), and > = 33 (obese) as previously described [21]. Maternal BMI measured mid-pregnancy was used as missingness for pre-pregnancy weight was non-random across field centers. For those participants for whom self-reported pre-pregnancy weight was available, the correlation between pre-pregnancy BMI and the measured BMI at mid-pregnancy was ~ 0.92 [22]. Child BMI z-scores were calculated according to L (lambda), M (mu), and S (sigma) curves used by the International Obesity Task Force [23]. Childhood outcomes included BMI (weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) z-score (age- and sex-specific), sum of three skinfold measurements, and body fat percent from the Bod Pod using the Lohman equation [24].

Statistical and mediation analysis

To account for population substructure, we adapted the EPISTRUCTURE approach developed by Rahmani et al. [25] which identified CpG sites that reflect ancestry-related variability based on genotype proxies on DNA methylation arrays [26]. We performed an EWAS analysis using linear regression using the lm function from the statsR package (version 4.4.0) to examine associations between maternal BMI at pregnancy (as a continuous variable) as the predictor and M-values for DNAm as the outcome. Adjustments included child sex, maternal characteristics at visit (age, parity, smoking and drinking status, gestational age at delivery, family history of diabetes), cell counts, and the 10 principal components from the adapted EPISTRUCTURE algorithm [26]. We conducted a secondary analysis stratified by sex of the child. CpG sites were selected as potential mediators based on statistical threshold at Bonferroni correction p < 0.05 with a cutoff raw p-value of 6.32 × 10− 8 (calculated by dividing the α level of 0.05 by the number of CpG sites used in the analysis, 791,359), in our discovery EWAS of maternal BMI and cbDNAm levels.

Mediation analyses were conducted using the Lavaan R package (version 0.6.20), in R-4.4.0 [27]. Models examining the potential mediation of associations between maternal BMI and childhood adiposity measures (BMI z-score, sum of skinfolds, body fat percentage) by methylation at the identified CpG sites were adjusted for child sex (excluding the models with child BMI z-score as the outcome), maternal characteristics at OGTT (age, parity, and smoking and drinking status, gestational age at delivery, family history of diabetes), cell counts, and the 10 PCs. The estimated proportion mediated was calculated by dividing the estimate for the indirect effect by the estimate for the total effect and multiplying by 100. The significance threshold was < 0.05 for the mediation analysis.

Results

This analysis included 3,116 mother-child pairs. The mean maternal BMI at the pregnancy visit was 27.6 (sd 4.9) kg/m2, which was within the ‘pregnancy-adjusted’ normal BMI category range of 22.6 to 28.4 kg/m2 [21]. The visit occurred at mean gestational age of 27.6 (sd 1.7) weeks. Maternal ancestry collected at the time of HAPO enrollment consisted of five self-reported categories: Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, and Other. At the follow-up visit, mean child age was 11.5 years (range 10–14 years). The percentage of mothers and children with a BMI in the overweight or obese category was 36% and 28.3%, respectively. We performed a subgroup analysis by sex and identified one novel CpG site at cg09792711 in males only and a novel CpG site at cg09592602 on gene CDC25B in females only. Two CpG sites on the TXNIP gene in the main analysis were identified in males only suggesting that this subgroup analysis may be limited by sample size (Additional file 4).

We used the EWAS catalog to identify previous publications of the identified CpG sites in this study, and findings are displayed in Additional file 2 [28]. The two TXNIP sites had been identified in prior studies related to maternal glucose and type 2 diabetes mellitus [28]. Additionally, we compared significant CpG sites identified in two previously published EWAS on the associations of maternal BMI with offspring cbDNAm, the Boston Birth Cohort [17] and the Pregnancy and Childhood Epigenetics Consortium (PACE) [19], to our current results in HAPO participants; these findings are displayed in Additional file 3. Although the identified CpG sites in prior studies were not replicated in the current study, PACE identified a different significant CpG site on SP6 with a negative β coefficient reflecting that higher maternal BMI was associated with lower cbDNAm, a direction of effect consistent with findings from the present study [19].

To study whether cbDNAm mediated the relationship between maternal BMI and child adiposity parameters, a mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the seven CpG sites as potential mediators of maternal BMI associations with child BMI z-score, body fat percent, and sum of skinfold thickness. Mediation by these CpG sites was not detected for maternal BMI and child sum of skinfolds or body fat percent. One site, cg20020844 within SP6, mediated 1.2% of the association between maternal BMI and child BMI z-score (total effect: 0.0887; indirect effect: 0.00107; p for mediation < 0.05). Mediation of maternal BMI and child BMI z-score was not detected for the other CpG sites.

Discussion

This EWAS investigated the association between maternal BMI and cbDNAm in the large, multi-ethnic HAPO cohort. Higher maternal BMI was associated with lower cbDNAm at seven CpG sites in TXNIP, SP6, KIAA1614, OCA2, PCDHG, HIGD2B, and BBS4. To study DNAm as a potential mechanistic link between maternal BMI and preadolescent obesity and adiposity, a mediation analysis was performed with the identified sites. One CpG site, cg20020844 (SP6), mediated 1.2% of the association between maternal BMI and child BMI z-score. There was no evidence for mediation by the other six sites.

Two CpG sites within TXNIP were identified in this study, corroborating findings from the Gen3G study and PACE consortium which identified an association between gestational maternal glycemia and TXNIP CpG sites in cb [29, 30]. In these studies, maternal hyperglycemia was associated with lower cbDNAm at TXNIP, a direction of effect in line with our observed association and the hypothesis that lower offspring DNAm is due to an adverse metabolic in utero environment that is a consequence of dysmetabolism linked to higher maternal BMI. Indeed, maternal BMI and glycemia are closely linked, so it is possible that the HAPO cohort findings can be attributed to maternal glycemia. TXNIP encodes a binding protein that inhibits thioredoxin, an antioxidant, leading to increased oxidative species, cell stress, and apoptosis [31]. TXNIP is expressed in liver, adipose, and skeletal muscle, all key metabolic tissues, and plays an important role in regulation of glucose homeostasis and hepatic glucose production [32]. The TXNIP-encoded protein has been associated with a range of metabolic and inflammatory diseases, including diabetes and cancer [33, 34]. In pancreatic beta cells, elevated glucose leads to upregulation of TXNIP expression, contributing to cellular dysfunction and eventual apoptosis [32]. Additionally, lower DNAm in adult blood at TXNIP CpG sites is associated with risk of type 2 diabetes and higher levels of inflammatory biomarkers [35, 36]. Our findings in conjunction with previous findings on the role of TXNIP in glucose regulation and beta cell dysfunction support the plausibility of TXNIP contributing to the pathogenesis of childhood adiposity-related metabolic consequences.

OCA2 (cg07138793) encodes a membrane transport protein for tyrosine and other molecules, aiding in pigmentation [37]. BBS4 (cg12188424) is implicated in Bardet-Biedl syndrome, an autosomal recessive condition characterized by childhood obesity [38]. HIGD2B (cg12188424) may play a role in mitochondrial respirasome assembly [39]. The PCDHG family (cg19345626) is likely involved in neuronal connections [40, 41]. KIAA1614 (cg00579423) encodes a protein involved in the centrosome cycle, cell polarity, and localization [42].

The CpG site on SP6, cg20020844, was the only loci where our analyses suggested a mediating role. SP6 is a transcription factor with greater expression in the placenta compared to other tissues [43]. Although publications related to cg20020844 were largely related to age and sex, findings related to SP6 methylation have been reported in other maternal-child cohorts. The PACE consortium, a meta-analysis that investigated maternal BMI and offspring cbDNAm across 19 cohorts and included 7,523 mother-child pairs, identified a different CpG site on SP6 (cg09243648) than the present study, although both studies showed lower DNAm in cord blood in relation to higher maternal BMI [19]. In the UK Pregnancies Better Eating and Activity Trial, a randomized controlled trial of a lifestyle intervention in 557 pregnant women with obesity, a different CpG on SP6 showed lower cbDNAm levels in relation to higher 1 h glucose at pregnancy OGTT, suggesting again tightly linked roles of maternal BMI and glycemia in epigenetic programming of offspring [44].

We reviewed prior publications in the EWAS catalog to study the biological plausibility of the seven CpG sites, as summarized in Additional file 2 [28]. Three sites, cg07138793 (OCA2), cg12188424 (BBS4, HIGD2B), and cg19345626 (PCDHG) did not appear in any previous publications in the catalog. The TXNIP sites, cg02988288 and cg26974062, had been identified in prior EWAS studies with type 2 diabetes mellitus and maternal glucose in pregnancy, as highlighted above [28].

Mediation analysis has been applied to determine if in utero exposures have causal effects on newborn and child outcomes, such as in studies of maternal smoking and low birthweight [45]. With data from the HAPO FUS, the present study aimed to examine beyond the neonatal period by studying associations between cbDNAm and anthropometry at 10–14 years. The mediation analysis of maternal BMI on child BMI z-score identified only one significant result, cg20020844 within SP6 with a small mediation effect of 1.2%, of which the clinical significance is unknown. These findings differ from the Boston Birth Cohort which found 14 CpG sites with mediating proportions ranging from 3.99% to 25.21% [17]. (Further discussion of the present study compared to the Boston Birth Cohort and PACE Consortium can be found in Additional File: Discussion). Although maternal factors leading to adverse in utero environment may influence the methylation status in offspring as measured by cbDNAm, this is a relatively short timeframe compared to environmental exposures that may influence early adolescent obesity risk years later. The overall negative results of the mediation analysis to child obesity risk at ages 10–14 may thus indicate other biological phenomenon rather than a causal effect. Moreover, cbDNAm may or may not capture epigenetic programming occurring in key tissues involved in obesity development, future studies could evaluate DNAm in tissues where it is hypothesized to have a role in obesity pathogenesis (e.g. brain), but this is ethically challenging in humans and might only be possible in animal models.

The HAPO cohort provides unique insight into the link between maternal exposures and childhood obesity. The study collected detailed data on over 3,000 mother-child pairs, enabling evaluation for potential confounding variables including maternal age, drinking and smoking status, and parity. None of the HAPO participants had overt gestational diabetes mellitus based on criteria at the time of the study, and the purely observational design precluded any treatment of maternal hyperglycemia. Importantly, direct measurements of maternal and child height and weight were obtained to calculate BMI using a common protocol across all field centers. HAPO FUS obtained direct measurements of child adiposity including sum of skinfolds and body fat percentage which are considered more robust measures in assessing metabolic disease compared to BMI [46]. HAPO was an epidemiological study and represented a generally healthy cohort. Women were recruited from various ancestry groups worldwide, comprising a diverse cohort. Further strengths of the study include the use of the Illumina Epic BeadChip, capable of assessing over 850,000 methylation sites whereas other studies were limited to 450,000. The study also used a stringent Bonferroni adjustment for identification of significant sites.

This study had limitations. First, there may be residual confounding as the study relied on maternal report of factors such as drinking and smoking, and data on diet, physical activity, and gestational weight gain on all participants of the present analysis were not obtained. Maternal BMI was measured mid-pregnancy, and in this analysis, we chose to use a measured weight rather self-reported pre-pregnancy weight, which may not have been accurate across participants. Additionally, the study did not collect paternal data. We accounted for population substructure using the novel EPISTRUCTURE approach, however, we acknowledge the limitations of this approach including the inability to distinguish between ancestry and environmental factors. Analyses were not stratified by ancestry groups or geographic regions due to limited sample size of each group. Differences in BMI are present across racial and ethnic groups, thus, we used BMI as a continuous measure and adjusted for ancestry to address this. Additionally, there are numerous structural causes of BMI differences including income inequality and inequities in food access, data which are not available in this cohort. Finally, while cord blood is an accessible tissue commonly used in EWAS, it may not fully reflect what is occurring in metabolically relevant tissues.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrated the association of maternal BMI with cbDNAm at seven CpG sites and identified one site within SP6 that appeared to mediate a small portion of the association of maternal BMI with child BMI in a cohort of over 3000 racially and ethnically diverse participants. Several CpG sites identified replicate findings from other maternal-child cohort EWAS that investigated adiposity-related metabolic traits. Two sites were on TXNIP which is involved in the pathogenesis of diabetes and inflammation, and one site was on BBS4, which is implicated in the monogenic obesity syndrome Bardet-Biedl Syndrome. These findings suggest that exposure to maternal obesity in utero may contribute to the biological programming of childhood obesity.
