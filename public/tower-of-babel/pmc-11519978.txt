Rethinking the four-wing problem in plesiosaur swimming using bio-inspired decentralized control
Authors: Akira Fukuhara, Mitsutoshi Sato, Hisayuki Ogawa, Tamaki Sato, William Sellers, Akio Ishiguro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA locomotor system that can function across different environmental conditions and produce a range of performances is one of the most critical abilities needed for extant and extinct animals in order to survive and maximise their competitive fitness. Recent engineering-inspired paleontological studies have reconstructed feasible locomotor patterns in extinct animals. However, it is still challenging to describe how extinct animals successfully adjust their locomotor patterns to new situations (e.g., changes in locomotor speed and morphology). In this study, we develop a novel reconstruction method based on a bio-inspired control system. We focus on plesiosaurs, an extinct aquatic reptile group which has two pairs of flipper-shaped limbs, and demonstrate that a highly optimised, flexible locomotor pattern for all four flippers can be reconstructed based on a decentralized control scheme formulated from extant animals’ locomotion. The results of our robotic experiments show that a simple, local sensory feedback mechanism allows the plesiosaur-like robot to exploit the fluid flow between the flippers and generate efficient swimming patterns in response to changes in locomotor conditions. Our new method provides further evidence how decentralized control systems can produce a pathway between extinct and extant animals in order to understand the how extinct animals moved and how these movement patterns may have evolved.Subject terms: Palaeontology, Biomechanics

Introduction

Understanding how extinct animals moved in ancient times is an important question. The reconstruction of extinct animals' locomotion provides us with key insights for understanding their lifestyles, as locomotion capability is critical for animals to feed, escape from predators, breed, and expand their habitats. Early paleontological studies have referred to the trace fossils of trackways and the locomotion patterns of living analogues to infer extinct animals locomotion style1–3. Additionally, for more quantitative estimations, scaling laws based on biological and biomechanical studies have been applied. For example, the underlying scaling rules in animal locomotion enable extant and extinct animals to be compared in terms of physical constraints (e.g., bone stiffness, muscle power, mass, and friction forces)1–4,41. Although these comparisons can be used to suggest basic parameters such as the theoretical maximum speed of extinct animals, it is still difficult to estimate the underlying, feasible locomotor patterns (e.g., kinematics of each limb, and gait patterns) of extinct species whose morphology differs drastically from that of extant animals.

Recently, reconstruction methods have been developed via engineering technologies, e.g., computer simulation and robotics, to reveal the locomotor patterns of extinct animals5–9. For example, Sellers et al.7 and Hutchinson et al.5 built musculoskeletal models of the Tyrannosaurus and reconstructed their walking patterns, suggesting that the peak stress of their bones limits their locomotion speeds. Nyakatura et al.8 built a physical robot of the Permian tetrapod Orobates and generated feasible locomotor patterns by integrating the behaviour of body fossils, trackway, trace fossils and living analogues. These engineering-inspired approaches allow paleontologists to test whether hypothetical locomotor patterns are physically feasible under specific locomotor conditions (e.g., locomotion speed, direction, and environmental conditions). However, previous simulation approaches focus on providing a locomotor pattern that works in a specific set of conditions, and do not address the question of how an animal is able to adjust its locomotor pattern to cope with varying demands in terms of both the environment and the goals of the organism. Understanding flexibility in the locomotion of extinct animals will provide us significant insights into their survival strategies in the wild.

To understand how extinct animals can use their locomotor systems in a behaviourally flexible fashion, a novel reconstruction method based on the control principle underlying the animals' flexible locomotion is proposed herein. Most animals adjust their locomotor patterns in response to changes in their situations (e.g., speed-dependent gait transition10,11) and environments12,13. Researchers in biology14 and robotics15–17 have pursued similar approaches to further understand the control mechanism underlying the locomotor behaviour in living animals. These, often collaborative, studies have suggested that decentralized control mechanisms (e.g., central pattern generator and local reflexes) are essential for generating the required locomotor flexibility in extant animals. It is natural, therefore, to assume that extinct animals exhibit flexible locomotion via a similar, flexible control system. Therefore, in this paper, we focus on the control principles underlying the way extant animals can change their locomotor behaviour as circumstances require and apply them to develop a new reconstruction method for flexible locomotion in an extinct group of animals.

As a first step, the present study attempts to address the “four-wing problem” in the plesiosaurs’ swimming style from the viewpoint of decentralized control mechanisms. Plesiosaurs are aquatic reptiles that were common throughout the Jurassic and Cretaceous18. They exhibit unique morphology, i.e., a wide range of neck lengths and two pairs of similar flipper-shaped limbs19. The four-wing problem in plesiosaur locomotion focuses on the coordination patterns between the pectoral and pelvic wing-like flippers. Living tetrapods with flippers (e.g., sea turtles, penguins, and sea lions) generate propulsive forces by the large wing-like fore limbs and steer their bodies by the smaller hind limbs. In contrast, the two pairs of flippers of plesiosaurs have very similar sizes and this allows a greater range of possibilities for thrust generation via suitable inter-flipper coordination. To identify the swimming style of plesiosaurs, researchers have investigated inter-flipper coordination by integrating knowledge from biology, biomechanics, and robotics20–22. Although various coordination patterns have been tested, these tests have concentrated on evaluating the hydrodynamics of steady state flipper kinematics which ignores the difficult problem of the control and flexibility of plesiosaur flipper coordination under varying conditions and locomotor requirements. So, this paper investigates control systems to understand how plesiosaurs succesfully adjusted their inter-flipper coordination in response to new situations, such as changes in locomotor frequency and morphology. Considering the four-wing problem from the perspective of a bio-inspired control system should therefore provide important additional information regarding the efficacy of different locomotor patterns and ultimately how they may have evolved in an animal that has transitioned from a terrestrial to an aquatic environment.

To explore the control options in the four-wing problem, we have developed a new framework for extinct animal locomotor reconstruction using bio-inspired control mechanisms that have previously been shown to be effective in extant animals (Fig. 1). Here we extend this work to hypothesize that a flexible locomotor pattern in the plesiosaurs four-wing system can be generated by the decentralized control scheme that we have previously shown is a good candidate for generating flexible locomotion in extant animals. To test the hypothesis, we built a swimming robot inspired by plesiosaur morphology and designed a decentralized inter-flipper coordination mechanism based on our previous studies23–25. This paper shows that the resultant sensory feedback control scheme allows the plesiosaur-like robot to generate the required variable coordination between the flippers to generate efficient thrust as both the locomotor frequency and the morphology of the robot are altered.

Results

The effectiveness of inter-flipper coordination patterns on locomotor performance

To evaluate the effectiveness of inter-flipper coordination in the four-wing problem, we investigated the locomotor performance metrics (propulsive forces and energetic costs) of our plesiosaur robot with altered inter-flipper coordination patterns. We describe the underwater flight motion for each flipper in terms of its periodic up-stroke and down-stroke motions with a phase oscillator. The parameter ϕi (i = fore, hind) describes the oscillator phase. Thus, the robot swam with a specific phase difference between the flippers (Δϕ=ϕfore-ϕhind), and we measured the propulsive force Nidt and energy consumption Pi in each flipper during steady swimming. The effects of the inter-flipper coordination patterns on these measurements taken from the robot platform are presented under various conditions: altered locomotor frequencies and altered distances between the robot flippers. Previous studies on four-wing problem suggest that inter-flipper coordination will affect thrust force, energy efficiency, and body balance27–29,31,32. Especially, body balance must be a crucial aspect to consider the four-wing problem because the flapping motions of the fore and hind flippers affect the rotational and translational motions of the trunk. However, in this paper we omit the balance aspect of the four-wing problem and concentrate on evaluating the effectiveness of the control scheme on propulsion and efficiency. This choice considerably simplifies the experimental design since it allows the robot to be fixed in a flow tank and swum under specific flow speeds. The details of robotic platform and the flow tank are described in the Method section and the Supplemental Materials.

Frequency dependency

As many extant quadrupeds exhibit variable gait patterns by varying their locomotor frequency to achieve fast and energy-efficient locomotion under various conditions, plesiosaurs are similarly expected to exhibit varying inter-flipper coordination patterns based on wing beat frequency. Hence, we investigated the propulsive efficiencies of the robot with various fixed phase differences between the fore and hind flippers, Δϕ, in a range of locomotion frequencies.

Figure 2A shows the thrust coefficients and propulsive efficiencies of the robot under two different locomotor frequencies. In the low frequency (f = 0.6 [Hz], ω=π/3 [rad/s]), the robot achieved a larger thrust force when Δϕ was approximately 0.4π [rad] compared with those under other inter-flipper coordination patterns as shown in Fig. 2A. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2C, the propulsive efficiency η achieved high values when Δϕ is approximately 0.4π [rad]. Consequently, the robot simultaneously achieved larger thrust forces and more efficient swimming at a specific phase difference between fore and hind flippers.

At a higher locomotor frequency (f = 1.0 [Hz]), the robot exhibited different locomotor performance values with altered phase differences. As shown in Fig. 2B, the robot achieved a higher thrust force when Δϕ was approximately 1.0π [rad]. Furthermore, the locomotor patterns with Δϕ of 1.0π [rad] also induced the robot to produce efficient swimming as shown in Fig. 2D. It is noteworthy that this in-phase coordination between the flippers (i.e., Δϕ = 1.0π) generated a smaller thrust force when f = 0.6 [Hz].

These results imply that the thrust coefficient and efficiency depend on the phase difference between the fore and hind flippers, resulting in an almost sinusoidal curve. Furthermore, the results show that the optimal phase difference changes in response to the locomotor frequency, suggesting a gait change analogous to terrestrial quadrupeds choosing to use different gait patterns during walking and running.

Morphology dependency

In addition to locomotor frequency, different species of plesiosaur possess diverse morphologies, and body proportions would be expected to affect how the animals coordinate their limbs4. For plesiosaur morphology, one important factor is that different species vary in the distance between their fore and hind flippers21. We expect the flipper distance to affect the interaction between flippers during swimming in different environments and at different speeds. Therefore, we measured the effects of this physical characteristics of plesiosaurs, i.e., the inter-flipper distance D, on locomotor performance, using the robot platform with varying phase difference Δϕ. The locomotor frequency f is common at 0.6 [Hz] at each flipper distance D.

Figure 3 shows the relationship between Δϕ and locomotor performances with different flipper distances (D = 4, 5 and 6 chord lengths). For all the inter-flipper distances, the thrust force and propulsive efficiency of the robot varied with Δϕ, resulting in sinusoidal-curve-like results at different locomotor frequencies as before. Furthermore, the phase difference that generated higher and more efficient thrust forces was seen to shift based on D: when D = 3, 4, and 5 chord lengths, Δϕ of approximately 0.0, 0.4π, and 0.6π [rad], respectively, yielded the largest thrust forces.

Physical interaction between flippers through vortices

The results of the experiments presented above show that the robot platform possesses optimal phase differences which change depending on the locomotion frequencies and inter-flipper distance. To clarify the underlying physical interaction between the robot and environment, we conducted further visualization and analysis experiments using particle image velocimetry (PIV). The details of the analyses are described in the Supplemental Materials.

The PIV analysis revealed that the vortex generated by the fore flipper reached the hind flipper during swimming. Figure 4A shows a snapshot of the vortices surround the flippers under high thrust inter-flipper coordination. The fore flipper was down-stroking and generated a counter-clockwise (CCW) vortex. The CCW vortex from the fore flipper reached the down-stroking hind flipper, resulting in an interaction with a clockwise (CW) vortex from the hind flipper. In the low-thrust coordination, as shown in Fig. 4B. The vortex from the forelimb also reached the hind flipper. The interaction between the vortices in the low-thrust coordination differed from that in the high-thrust one, and the CCW vortices from the fore flipper collided with the CCW vortices from the hind flipper. These results indicate that the phase difference between the fore and hind flippers Δϕ affected the interactions of the vortices surrounding the hind flipper producing an advantageous interaction in the high thrust case and a disadvantageous interaction in the low thrust case.

Furthermore, the PIV analysis indicates that the interaction of the vortices affected the locomotor performance of the robot. In high-thrust coordination, the CCW vortex from the fore flipper was above the CW vortex from the hind flipper, as shown in Fig. 4A. These variations in physical interaction between vortices were also observed in the plesiosaurs-like robot experiments by Muscutt et al.22. This agreement helps validate the utility of our robot platform for addressing the fundamental physical phenomena in the context of the control of inter-flipper coordination in plesiosaurs locomotion.

Emergence of flexible flipper coordination through sensory feedback mechanism

In this section, we investigate whether our proposed simple decentralized control mechanism enables the robot to generate high-thrust locomotor patterns in all the experimental conditions. This decentralized control scheme is designed using inspiration from the way a decentralized control mechanism in a quadruped robot can generate flexible gait patterns depending on locomotor speed and robot morphology that provide excellent qualitative matches to experimentally observed behaviour23–25. Each flipper has a force sensor and modulates its phase of underwater-flight motion based on a local sensory feedback mechanism. Note that there is no neural communication between the flippers to regulate the specific phase difference Δϕ, and that the resultant inter-flipper coordination pattern changes via interaction between the control system, the mechanical system, and the environment.

For this experimental setup, the speed of the flow tank was set to that used in the experiments without the sensory feedback mechanism. The parameter for the feedback gain in the controller, σi, was determined such that the robot generated a high thrust at a specific condition: ω of 1.08π [rad/s] (f = 0.54 [Hz]) and flipper distance of 4 chord length. Subsequently, in the other locomotor conditions, the feedback gain σi was fixed. The details of the tuning the feedback gain are described in the Supplemental Materials.

To investigate the response to altered locomotor frequencies, we increased the locomotor frequency of the robot, ω, from 1.2π to 2.0π [rad/s] during swimming at a constant flow speed. At the start of the experiment, an initial phase difference Δϕ of 0.0 [rad] was set as the initial condition and this value was allowed to alter in response to the action of the controller. Figure 5 shows the changes in the locomotor frequency. During low frequency swimming (ω = 1.08π [rad/s]), the phase difference Δϕ increased slightly and converged to a specific value 0.2π [rad], which is similar to the high thrust coordination patterns shown in Fig. 2A. When parameter ω changed from 1.08π to 1.8π [rad/s], the phase difference Δϕ increased and converged to a different value, 0.8π [rad]. The converged coordination patterns were similar to the high-trust patterns, as shown in Fig. 2B. The results shows that the proposed feedback mechanism caused the robot to spontaneously change the inter-flipper coordination patterns in response to the locomotion frequency and therefore always resulted in a high thrust outcome.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates, for the first time, how a bio-inspired control scheme, e.g., rhythm generator and local sensory feedback, can generate the flexible locomotor patterns of plesiosaurs swimming producing optimal thrust and efficiency in response to differing conditions. Previous engineering-inspired studies have proposed different locomotor patterns in plesiosaurs in terms of the phase difference between the fore and hind flippers Δϕ, e.g., Δϕ=π by Lui et al.26, and Δϕ =π/4 or 3π/4 by Newman et al.27 and Δϕ = 0 by Carpenter et al.28, respectively for steady state conditions without considering how this value might need to change as swimming conditions alter. Furthermore, Muscutt et al.22 demonstrated through robot experiments that the efficient inter-flipper coordination of plesiosaurs likely depends on the physical interaction between the vortices produced by the flippers22. Although these studies have established the feasible locomotor patterns in the four-wing problem, discussions regarding the mechanism enabling extinct animals to generate locomotion patterns flexibly so that they can successfully manage new situations are not considered. Our results suggest a possible simple control mechanism that can maintain the optimal phase relationship between the fore and hind flippers at differing body shapes and flapping frequencies using a decentralized sensory feedback mechanism. In our system, each flipper changes the phase of its flapping motion based on its perceived force from the environment. The hind flipper senses the external force affected by the vortex from the fore flipper (detailed analysis of the proposed model is described in the Supplemental Materials). Consequently, the robot can flexibly alter the inter-flipper coordination based on the vertices generated by the fore flipper under various locomotor conditions. Although our platform did not investigate the effects of four-wing locomotion on balance, these results suggest that the plesiosaurs could sense the physical interaction with the environment and change their swimming patterns accordingly via a similar low-level decentralized control systems. It is expected that various plesiosaurs species should have these flexibilities in inter-flipper coordination because, while they show the large diversity in neck length, the two-pairs of flippers are the common morphological features among them.

To more accurately reconstruct plesiosaurs swimming style, the effects of the proposed mechanism should be evaluated by considering the difficulty of maintaining hydrodynamic stability whilst freely moving using all four flippers for propulsion. The previous studies suggest that the inter-flipper coordination pattern will affect the whole body motion of the plesiosaurs28–32, e.g., the in-phase coordination between fore and hind flippers will lead to undesirable vertical motions of the trunk while the anti-phase coordination will lead to equally undesirable pitching motions of the trunk. Consequently, the influence of the trunk motion is essential to fully understand the difficulties inherent in the four wing problem. If the robot’s hind flipper cannot benefit from the vortices caused by the fore flipper due to large changes in the trunk’s position or posture, the proposed control mechanism may not be able to exploit the physical interaction between the flippers. However, the proposed control mechanism can perceive the trunk’s motions as it will affect the sensor values of reaction force along the ventral and dorsal direction at each flipper, Nidv (see details in Method). Therefore, the proposed model may be able to exploit physical interaction between the flippers through trunk vertical and rotational motions for generating the required situation-dependent inter-flipper coordination. By extending the proposed control model in future so that it integrates the trunk motion effect, we hope to address how a plesiosaur can change or maintain its posture in response to changes of locomotor conditions (e.g., locomotor frequency and direction). Different plesiosaurs show variation in morphology and position of centre of mass (CoM) and so the effect of the flapping motion of the fore and hind flippers on the trunk motion would be expected to differ depending on species. If we can build a robot that can address the different effects of the proposed control mechanism on swimming parameters (e.g., manoeuvrability and stability) depending on its morphology, it would shed new light on different locomotor skills such as diving and surfacing among the different species.

Our method provides an important additional line of enquiry in addition to the more commonly used analyses of morphology and phylogeny since all animals need to have effective ways of controlling their locomotion. It is likely that the control strategies used in extant animals are largely conserved from their extinct analogues, and that discovering effective control strategies that work in robots is a valuable tool for exploring such bio-inspired control schemes. The proposed inter-flipper coordination mechanism in the present study is described in the same manner as the interlimb coordination mechanism for terrestrial quadruped locomotion in our previous study24. In both mechanisms, each limb changes the phase of its locomotor motion based on the reaction force from the environment. This commonality in the control system suggests the interesting idea that the fundamental sensory-motor control mechanism might be inherited from the ancestral terrestrial quadrupedal to secondarily aquatic reptiles, i.e., plesiosaurs. Furthermore, this control system approach enables us to investigate the reconstruction of other extinct animals’ locomotion by integrating phylogenetic information. For example, nothosaurs (Triassic Sauropterygia) share a common ancestor with plesiosaurs33. They have limbs somewhat intermediate between a terrestrial leg and an aquatic flipper, and their locomotion styles are traditionally interpreted as undulatory propulsion using the entire body and long tail (e.g., Storrs34). Meanwhile, closer relatives of the plesiosaurs have a more plesiosaur-like overall body plan including four flippers while retaining ancestral non-plesiosaurian features in the skull and vertebrae, indicating the transition to paraxial locomotion (Sato et al.35,36). Investigating the changes in the control system required for such transitional forms using robotic analogues such as demonstrated here will provide further evidence for reconstructing the likely locomotor patterns of these animals.

For the further development of the proposed method, two areas need to be considered. Firstly, the biomechanics of the extinct animal needs to be elaborated for a more precise estimation of its locomotor capability. Although the robot platform presented here captured the fundamental physical interaction between the flippers effectively, a morphology gap exists between the robot system and an actual plesiosaur. For simplicity, the flippers in the robot were designed with a rigid, rectangle-shaped platform. In contrast, the plesiosaur flipper is shaped more like a tapered wing and has a small degree of flexibility owing to the series of joints that make up the flipper. Additionally, the motion of the robot flipper was assumed to be symmetrical between the up- and down-stroke, whereas the plesiosaurs would be expected to have generated larger forces during the down-stroke than in the up-stroke owing to the large surface and stout inter-flipper regions of the ventral limb girdles possessed by these animals. These biomechanical properties and the likely alteration in the kinematics will affect the physical interactions between the flipper and the environments. Therefore, incorporating more precise biomechanical properties into the robot system will provide a more accurate estimation of the transition speeds between the different inter-flipper coordinations in plesiosaurs swimming.

The second area where there is room for improvement is within the control mechanism itself to allow for a more versatile locomotor reconstruction involving the whole-body system. Although the present study demonstrated a flexible inter-flipper coordination, the interactions of plesiosaurs flippers with the movements of their elongated necks is yet to be fully investigated. The variation in neck length is one of the significant morphological characteristics of the plesiosaurs and although a few previous studies have investigated its effect via simulation or physical experiments, the functionality of the neck is still unclear. Therefore, more work is needed to understand the control mechanism underlying how the whole body (limbs as well as the components of the axial skeleton) can be coordinated in extant animals as locomotor conditions vary. Living animals achieve flexible locomotor behaviours (e.g., turning, balancing, rapid acceleration, and deceleration) by exploiting the whole-body as an integrated system. In particular, some extant swimming animals (e.g., penguins and sea lions) clearly demonstrate whole body integration in their swimming kinematics. Appropriate biomechanical analysis of their hydrodynamics and control would be expected to provide insights into the coordination mechanisms between the flippers and the neck and elsewhere that would allow us to integrate these aspects of plesiosaur morphology into a more complete locomotor reconstruction. Additionally, designing a whole-body control system will expand the scope of the proposed methodology to other extinct animals. It is easy to see that many extinct animals must have used not only their limbs, but also their heads and tails in locomotion. However, it is challenging to address whole-body coordination and investigating the control mechanisms underlying extant animal locomotion may provide insights that have the potential to have very wide utility for understanding the gait of species with unusual axial morphology: not only plesiosaurs in aquatic environments but also long-necked terrestrial animals such as sauropods and giraffes.

Methods

Decentralized inter-flipper coordination mechanism for the four-wing problem

To address the flexible inter-flipper coordination in plesiosaur locomotion considering the interplay among the nervous system (control), musculoskeletal system (body), and environment, we built a minimal body model representing plesiosaur flipper morphology. This body model comprised a trunk, and fore-, and hind-flipper units as shown in Fig. 7A. We assumed that plesiosaurs employed a flapping motion and that the contralateral flippers moved synchronously in straight line locomotion (i.e. the left flipper mirrors the movements of the right flipper) which enabled us to treat the system as laterally symmetrical and therefore we could focus on one-half of the body.

Simplification of the plesiosaur model. (A) For a simple body structure, we focused on only the flippers’ motion and disregarded effects of the neck. Left and right flippers were assumed to move synchronously. Periodic motion of left and right fore/hind flippers was described by a phase oscillator. (B) Flipper motion was simplified as a combination of flapping and feathering motions 22,42,43. The oscillator phase, i, describes periodic flipper motion for swimming forward. When i was between π/2 to 3π/2, the flipper generated a down-stroke motion. Additionally, the flipper generated an up-stroke motion when i was between 3π/2 and π/2.

Furthermore, we assumed that each flipper did not function as a paddle but instead performed underwater flight as this has been shown to perform efficiently with the plesiosaur morphology22,26,28,42,43. During underwater flight, the flipper generates a lift force for propulsion during both the down-stroke and the up-stroke. To describe the periodic motion of the flipper’s underwater flight, we employed a phase oscillator. The parameter ϕi (i = fore, hind) describes the oscillator phase. When 0< ϕi ≤ π, the flipper is on the dorsal side of the midline, as shown in Fig. 7B and, when π< ϕi ≤2π, the flipper is on the ventral side. Therefore, the down-stroke motion of the flipper is generated when ϕi changes from π/2 to 3π/2, whereas the up-stroke motion of the flipper is generated when ϕi changes from 0 to π /2 and from 3π/2 to 2π. For periodic motion, the oscillator phase ϕi rotates according to an intrinsic angular velocity ω. According to these simplifications, inter-flipper coordination can be described by the phase relationship between the phase oscillators. A simple feedforward controller would cause ϕi to change based on some predefined pattern but this would not allow the phase to change in response to situational changes, and would therefore not provide adequate locomotor flexibility in a real-world situation. Instead, we need to design a feedback control mechanism that changes ϕi based on sensory information which can accommodate varying requirements (e.g., changes in locomotion speed or morphology).

To address the question above, we chose an existing control mechanism based our previous work on generating locomotor patterns that can cope flexibly with changes in the locomotor requirements and the environment. In these studies23–25, we proposed simple interlimb coordination models to understand the control mechanism underlying extant terrestrial quadrupeds’ flexible gait patterns (e.g., walking, running)4,10. These models can reproduce varying gait patterns in response to the locomotion speeds and morphologies for a range of robots, suggesting that the local sensory feedback mechanism is essential for generating flexible interlimb coordination through physical interactions between the body and environment. Based on these results from bioinspired robotics, we developed a local sensory feedback mechanism to generate a similarly adaptable inter-flipper coordination system for four-flipper swimming.

The effects of the sensory feedback terms are explained as follows. When the flipper perceives a positive Nidv ﻿value (e.g., the reaction force at the flipper goes from ventral to dorsal), the sensory feedback term accelerates the down-stroke motion leading to larger reaction forces and decelerates the up-stroke motion leading to smaller reaction forces, as shown in Fig. 8A. By contrast, when the flipper perceives a negative Nidv value (e.g., the reaction force at the flipper goes from dorsal to ventral), the sensory feedback term decelerates the down-stroke motion generating smaller reaction forces and accelerates the up-stroke motion generating larger reaction forces, as shown in Fig. 8B. Thus, each flipper can generate underwater flight motion modulated by the physical interaction between the body and environment through this local sensory feedback mechanism.

Robotic platform

When investigating possible plesiosaur swimming styles using a physical robot model, scale effects should be considered in the development of the robotic platform, as show in Fig. 9. When designing the plesiosaurs-like robot as shown Fig. 9A, we considered the following morphological features: the symmetrical wing profile (i.e., shape of flipper section) and the ratio of the distance between the flippers and the chord length of flipper21. Furthermore, the details of the flipper properties (e.g., wing plan and materials) are simplified too since our primary goal is to address the effects of control mechanism on the flexibility of inter-flipper coordination. For underwater-flight swimming, we considered two dimensionless numbers, i.e., the Reynolds number (Re=ρUL/μ, where ρ is the flow fluid density, U the flow speed, L the average chord length, and μ the dynamic viscosity of the fluid) and the Strouhal number (St=fA/U, where f is the flapping frequency, A the flapping amplitude, and U the locomotor speed) when designing the environment (flow tank), robot system, and flipper motion.

Firstly, we designed the flow tank for the robot environment as shown in Fig. 9B. Owing to space limitations, we built a tank with a working section of 40 [cm] (width) × 45 [cm] (depth) and a length of 80 [cm]. The tank flow as shown in Fig. 9C was generated using six thrusters (T100, BlueRobotics), which were controlled by a pulse width modulation (PWM) signal. Through PIV analysis, it was determined that the average background flow speed was 23.5 [cm/s] under a defined PWM signal (the details of the flow tank is described in the Supplemental Materials). This specification of the flow tank and the requirement to match the scaling laws governed the specifications of the plesiosaurs-like robot’s size and motion.

Secondly, the robot’s size was determined based on the experimental volume and the required Re. Small flippers can avoid the boundary effects of the tank wall on the fluid in the experimental area. However, small flippers reduce the Re. Measurements on relatively large extant swimming vertebrates show that most swimmers using flapping motion swim at high Reynolds number37 and in a previous robot study of plesiosaur swimming, the robot experiments were conducted at Re = 22,00022. Therefore, we constructed the robot with flippers of 6 [cm] chord length (flipper width) and 10 [cm] flipper length (root to tip distance), and the resultant Re is 16,000 which is similar to the previous study22.

Thirdly, for the Strouhal number, we considered the typical values used by relatively large vertebrates whilst swimming and designed the robot’s flapping motion accordingly. Most swimming animals with periodic limb flapping or tail beating exhibit a St of approximately 0.3, when Re>10438,39. In Muscutt et al.’s robot plesiosaur experiment, they used St values of 0.18 and 0.36 for low and high locomotor frequencies, respectively22. We set the locomotor frequency in our robot platform based on these previous studies and specifications of the servo motors in the flipper unit (HS-5646WP, HiTec) and chose f = 0.6 and 1.0 [Hz] for different locomotor frequencies. To measure the physical interaction between the body and environment, we implemented two load cells (CZL616C, Phidgets) at the flipper unit base. The two load cells were oriented orthogonally to each other, and the load cells can detect the thrust component Niht and dorsoventral component Nidv of the net force, which combines the force generated by the flippers and the external force from the environment (e.g., drag force).
