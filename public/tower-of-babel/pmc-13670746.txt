Stimulation-stability and preclinical bench testing of a new auditory nerve interface
Authors: Taylor Stump, Stefan Strahl, Joseph Crew, Moritz Leber, Keno Hübner, Sandeep Negi, Justin Vankirk, Meredith E. Adams, Thomas Lenarz, Florian Solzbacher, Hubert H. Lim, Loren Rieth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCochlear implants are the flagship sensory neural prosthetic, enabling improved hearing and speech perception in >1 million patients with hearing loss. Despite over three decades of active work and innovations in cochlear implants, hearing performance for complex tasks such as speech-in-noise and music appreciation are still quite limited, putatively due to the challenge of selectively stimulating the auditory nerve with intracochlear electrodes through the bony cochlear wall. Our approach is to more selectively evoke hearing percepts through the development of an Auditory Nerve Implant (ANI) device that uses penetrating microelectrodes placed directly in the nerve for stimulation, and which will be evaluated through psychophysical testing as part of a first-in-human study. Stability and integrity of ANI electrodes during passive soaking and stimulation are needed to support initiation of the clinical trial, which we evaluated through thermal accelerated saline soak testing and stimulation stability testing.MethodsThe ANI electrode stability in saline was assessed by accelerated aging at ≥ 67 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C with a 1.5-year equivalent endpoint using electrode impedance and microscopy to characterize outcomes. Stimulation stability testing evaluated electrode integrity after 6.3 billion pulses at 100 μA, which is the maximum clinical stimulation intensity, for a one-year equivalent use, and 1200 μA, for a high-dose protocol. Electrode impedance, longitudinal stimulation voltage, and microscopy outcomes were used to the quantify results.ResultsAll electrodes had stable impedances around 2 kΩ at the end of the accelerated soak test, except for three with higher impedances but still below the 70 kΩ failure criteria. All but one electrode survived stimulation stability testing. Optical and electron microscopy largely showed little to no change during testing, indicating no observable tip degradation, with the exception of one soak-test device.ConclusionsThe outcomes from the studies indicate that ANI electrodes exhibit a high degree of integrity and impedance stability. Microscopy characterization proved effective for evaluating electrodes, providing complementary information to standard Backscattered Scanning Electron Microscopy (BSEM). Introduction

Auditory neuroprosthetic devices, exemplified by cochlear implants (CIs), are used to treat severe to profound hearing loss and represent the most successful neural interface technology to date. Hearing loss is the most prevalent condition amongst sensory disorders, occurring in new-borns through geriatric patients resulting from multiple mechanisms (Su and Chan 2017; Zablotsky and Black 2020; Reed et al. 2023). Cochlear implants have recently surpassed 1 million patients treated as standard of care for severe or profound sensorineural hearing loss, and have been implanted in patients as young as 6-months (Zeng 2022). CI placement in young prelingually deaf patients is highly beneficial, enabling the development of much higher levels of speech perception and language skills, while older patients benefit from restored social engagement to increase social fulfillment and decrease cognitive decline resulting from social isolation (Lenarz et al. 2022).

The use of CIs to treat infants highlights the need for device lifetimes of multiple decades, as revision surgeries to replace devices increase risks for patients and have decreased hearing performance. Such device lifetimes have been achieved with macroelectrode-based devices such as the linear array of Pt-Ir electrodes patterned from laser-cut foils and potted in silicone used for CIs, and similar approaches used for deep brain, vagus nerve, and spinal cord stimulators. Achieving similar device lifetimes for microelectrode technologies has proven much more difficult as is evident by the current lack of marketed neuroprosthetic devices using them, despite decades of effort (Ahuja et al. 2011; Normann et al. 1999), and positive outcomes in a number of studies (Hochberg et al. 2006; Musk 2019; Wairagkar et al. 2024).

While CIs are a remarkable success, there has been little improvement in key metrics such as speech-in-noise perception, music appreciation, and ability to localize sounds for more than 20 years (Devocht et al. 2016; Fetterman and Domico 2002; McDermott 2004; Limb and Rubinstein 2012). One key limitation is the low electrical conductivity of bone < 100 mS/m (Kipping et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025; Malherbe et al. 2015; Kalkman et al. 2014; Briaire and Frijns 2000). CIs use a linear array of electrodes placed within the scala tympani in the bony spiral canal of the cochlea to stimulate spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) and evoke hearing percepts. The milieu surrounding the electrodes is perilymph, a relatively conductive physiological fluid. The combination of a conductive adjacent medium, need for current to pass through bony cochlear wall, and distance between electrodes and neurons decrease the specificity of neural activation and increases the stimulation threshold currents compared to electrodes in intimate contact with neurons (Branner et al. 2001; Bierer et al. 2010). This results in the 12 to 24 electrodes used in contemporary CIs delivering 4 to 8 channels of independent percepts, which is less than the number needed for speech perception in challenging environments, refined pitch perception, and music appreciation (Zeng 2004). CI manufactures have made improvements such as designing specific electrodes used for more complete cochlear coverage and for other specialized stimulation cases (Skarzynski et al. 2019; Timm et al. 2018). Long lateral-wall electrode arrays can extend stimulation into the apical cochlea and improve access to low-frequency place cues, with clinical benefits reported for some recipients; however, apical placement alone does not fully restore the selective recruitment and high-fidelity temporal coding of low-characteristic-frequency auditory-nerve pathways (Dillon et al. 2019; Carlyon et al. 2025; Canfarotta et al. 2021; Middlebrooks and Snyder 2007, 2010; De Groote et al. 2024). Alternative approaches such as Auditory Brainstem and Midbrain Implants (ABIs and AMIs) are available as marketed devices or in development, but currently have more limited performance in speech perception tasks than CIs as recently summarized (Lim and Lenarz 2015; Lim and Anderson 2006). Intraneural microelectrode stimulation using Michigan probes with 100 μm spacing has shown impressive results in animal studies, providing more frequency-specific and localized activation of the auditory pathways than the CI, along with lower thresholds, greater dynamic ranges, and faster temporal coding (Middlebrooks and Snyder 2010, 2007; Arts et al. 2003).

Our approach is to place a penetrating intraneural electrode array directly in the lateral aspect of the auditory nerve as it approaches the modiolus (Lenarz et al. 2025), inserting the AN-USA electrodes directly into the dense axons of the auditory nerve trunk rather than stimulating the more diffuse auditory nerve (AN) in the modiolus and spiral ganglion through the bony wall of the cochlea, thereby accessing a much higher density of excitable neurons (Spoendlin and Schrott 1989). The Auditory Nerve Implant (ANI) device comprises the Blackrock Neurotech Auditory Nerve Utah Slant Array (AN-USA) and associated helical lead with Au wires, connected to an FDA-approved MEDEL SYNCHRONY2 cochlear stimulator. Additional details on the device design and fabrication will be presented in the methods. The AN-USA, shown in Fig. 1, is a 3 × 5 shank variant of the Utah slant array (USA) with 12 of the 15 electrodes electrical connected, which in turn is a variation a of the Blackrock Neurotech NeuroPort Electrode, a cortical brain-computer interface with 510(k) FDA clearance. The AN-USA has shank lengths from 0.5 to 0.9 mm across its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2 \times 2.0\, mm^2$$\end{document} area, using the slanted array geometry designed to provide localized stimulation across the cross-section of peripheral nerves (Branner et al. 2001; Aoyagi et al. 2003). The specific AN-USA geometry is optimized for the human auditory nerve that is ∼ 1.25 mm in diameter for normal hearing, but often closer to ∼ 1.0 mm with hearing impairments, and has considerable variability for both populations (Jaryszak et al. 2009; Herman and Angeli 2011; Kang et al. 2012). Stimulation with 10 × 10 Utah slant array (USA) in the above elbow aspect of median and radial nerves in volunteers has demonstrated recruitment of cutaneous percepts with small receptive fields and thresholds in the low 10 s μA (George et al. 2020; Wendelken et al. 2017) as part of studies to restore sensation to arm amputees. Changes in impedance, stimulation thresholds, and evoked percepts have been reported for USA electrodes in both animal models and human subjects (George et al. 2020; Straka et al. 2018; Wendelken et al. 2017). Recent and prior studies in pre-clinical feline models using the AN-USA and Utah array, respectively, have also demonstrated the ability to evoke percepts with thresholds in the low 10 s μA or less (Thomas et al. 2026; Rousche and Normann 1998; Hillman et al. 2003).

It is important to address some drawbacks to achieving a more direct stimulation approach. The translabyrinthine surgical approach used for the first few subjects ablates the vestibular canals, limiting the patient population for an initial study to subjects that have vestibular comorbidities, while also exploring alternative surgical approaches such as the retrosigmoid approach. Additionally, the penetrating nature of the array potentially makes the surgery less reversible than a CI placement and can cause a degree of nerve trauma as shown in histological analysis by Christensen et al. (2016), though these devices were well tolerated and caused no impairment as shown in large animal studies (Thomas et al. 2026). Significant effort was expended to optimize the lead and electrode mechanics and surgical handling to achieve proper placement and retainment of the AN-USA into the nerve (Wendelken et al. 2017). While the mechanics of replacement and retainment are more challenging than the standard CI, we have achieved good retainment in preclinical studies (George et al. 2020; Flesher et al. 2016; Wendelken et al. 2017). One such retainment development was to anchor the ANI lead in a groove precisely formed in the mastoid bone with a burr, oriented to allow the AN-USA to approach the nerve without external forces (Lenarz et al. 2025).

The focus of this study is to determine the saline soak and stimulation stability of the AN-USA as part of the fully integrated ANI device, to collect data supporting its compatibility with a first-in-human study involving a 1.5 year in-dwelling period with 1 year of stimulation. Positive outcomes from thermal accelerated aging and stimulation stability testing from the study are necessary to support initiating a clinical investigation and meet program milestones. Optical and electron micrographs of all electrodes were captured before and after soaking or stimulation as part of physical characterization to monitor for degradation. The ANI devices were immersed in 1× Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) in sealed containers, connected by an inductive link, and initial electrode impedances collected with voltage matrix measurements. Devices for thermal accelerated aging (TAA) were maintained at 67 °C and 77 °C for the equivalent of 1.5 years, with periodic impedance measurement collection. The stimulation stability study was conducted at room temperature with 6.3 Gpulses (billion pulses), similar to our recent study (Stump et al. 2025), using of 100 μA pulses, representing the upper bound of known physiological safe stimulation levels with a charge delivery based on a 1-year clinical study. Additionally, a 12× higher current of 1200 μA, a high intensity condition, was also evaluated using the same protocol representing the higher stimulation voltages that will occur in vivo and to explore the limits of stimulation stability. The electrode impedance and stimulation voltage were periodically measured during the stimulation protocol to monitor for changes associated with damage to the electrodes. Electrodes were imaged again at the study endpoint, tip metal quality was graded, and changes in the quality metric or surface morphology were analyzed. In addition, samples of the PBS solution for each test device were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to quantify ions in solution that result from stimulation induced degradation. This approach provides a thorough test of materials stability during passive soaking and with stimulation, to demonstrate the fully integrated ANI device will remain stable during our clinical trial.

Materials and methods

This study characterized two cohorts of fully integrated auditory nerve interface (ANI) devices as part of bench studies to evaluate 1) soak test stability and 2) stimulation stability of the electrodes. The thermal accelerated aging (TAA) study evaluated the soak test stability for devices (n=3) with a 1.5 year equivalent endpoint. The stimulation stability test evaluated if electrode degradation occurred while delivering the maximum quantity of charge that would occur during a 1 year implant at the 100 μA limit for clinical investigation (n=6), and high-intensity conditions of 1200 μA (n=5). All AN-USA devices were characterized before and after each respective test using optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). ANI device integration

ANI devices are fabricated by the integration of an AN-USA and the associated helically-wound gold wire lead from Blackrock Neurotech with a SYNCHRONY2 cochlear stimulator from MEDEL. The AN-USA is a 3 × 5 shank variant (Fig. 1a) of the 10 × 10 shank Utah slant array (USA), which in turn is based on the NeuroPort Electrode Utah Array (UA) from Blackrock Neurotech, a cortical brain-computer interface with 510(k) FDA clearance. The AN-USA has shank lengths from 0.5 to 0.9 mm and is approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2\ \times \ 2.0\, mm^2$$\end{document} across the array’s face where the 1.2 mm dimension is across the nerve and there is a 0.4 mm shank pitch. A new feature, the Fin, was added to improve surgical handling with forceps when placing the device in the auditory nerve under a surgical microscope and in the confined nerve exposure available.

The Fin was developed based on feedback and collaborations with the project surgeons through many cadaver surgeries used to develop the translabyrinthine approach we have adopted. The structure is implemented by attaching and overmolding a laser cut and structured 50-μm-thick Pt-Ir (90-10) foil to the backplane of the array. It is 1 mm in width, and a 1 mm portion extends normal to the array backplane, while a 2 mm portion extends along the backplane of the array to provide adequate mechanical coupling and allow the device to tolerate the loadings and surgical handling used to place it. This design was then tested through a series of cadaver surgeries, determined to notably improve surgical handling and facilitate placement of the AN-USA in the nerve, and adopted as the design to be used for our clinical trial.

A second set of ANI device improvements involve the lead used to connect the AN-USA to the SYNCHRONY2 stimulator that can be seen in Fig. 1b. The lead uses helically coiled and overmolded wires to connect the electrode array and stimulator, and is fabricated as part of the AN-USA assembly by Blackrock Neurotech. The design and surgical handling characteristics for the lead were developed and validated across >20 human cadaver surgeries, large animal pre-clinical studies (Thomas et al. 2024, 2026), and have been used as part of intraoperative studies that will be the focus of a future report. AN-USA cadaver implantation images are provided in (Figs. S14-S15). The lead body is comprised of 12 insulated Au-Pd (99-1) 25 μm wires, one for each connected electrode, and two insulated Pt-Ir (80-20) 76.2 μm wires. The Pt-Ir wires are not electrically connected and are used to control the lead’s mechanical characteristics to achieve a balance between mechanical compliance and a degree of mechanical memory. The design enables shaping the lead to control forces on the array facilitating retainment in the nerve without causing mechanical trauma to it. The Au and Pt wires are helically wound together and overmolded in medical-grade silicone to form the lead. The AN-USA is at the distal end of the lead, and a custom developed interposer used to integrate with the MEDEL SYNCHRONY2 stimulator forms the proximal aspect. The lead is 1.3 mm in diameter, conforming to the existing CI lead, facilitating the use of existing stimulator overmolding hardware, materials, and processes.

The lead will be retained in a bone groove formed by the surgeon to decouple external mechanical forces from the array and to help position it for nerve insertion, motivating the need for a repeatable lead diameter. Near the distal end of the lead, a surgical handling Wing is formed as part of the silicone overmolding process, which can be observed in Fig. 1b. The Wing is designed to allow torsional control of the lead, particularly using specialized forceps specifically designed to grasp the lead to control its position and rotation. The Wing design is based on structures used in cochlear electrodes from several manufacturers (Roland 2005; Deep et al. 2019). An additional design element instantiated into the lead body is a 5 mm segment of straight lead located just proximal to the AN-USA. This segment has more mechanical memory, allowing it to be precisely bent and twisted to facilitate implantation and retainment of the electrode array in the auditory nerve. The length of this section and use of the two Pt-Ir wires were all carefully optimized over the course of >10 separate cadaver surgeries, for the electrode array handling characteristics when used with a translabyrinthine surgical access. Specific criteria for optimization of the mechanical characteristics for the distal lead included reliable handling and forming of the lead without directly handling the array or risking damage to the shanks, forming the lead to stably position electrodes for insertion, and achieving insertion and retainment when the electrode is placed in the nerve. To obtain reliable and stable positioning of the lead a precise 1.3 mm groove is formed in the mastoid using burr bits, and the lead is pressed into the groove. The position and orientation of the groove was also optimized and validated over the course of multiple cadaver surgeries, and was deployed during a clinical investigation involving placement of wired AN-USAs in nerves of human subjects under informed consent and being treated for acoustic neuromas.

An additional component of the new lead is the interposer, which enables integration of the AN-USA with the hermetic feedthrough pins of the SYNCHRONY2 stimulator (Moritz et al. 2024). The use of an interposer is needed to electrically and mechanically join the Au electrode array wires to the Pt hermetic feedthrough pins. The Au wirebonding process requires bonds at both the electrode array and terminal end of the wires. Our interposer approach was developed through multiple rounds of research, development, and validation, and utilizes a 100 μm-thick alumina interposer with attached Pt pads and wires. MEDEL fabricates the interposer by first welding Pt foil pads to insulated and structured Pt wires and then using epoxy to attach these to the alumina interposer at precise spacings to match the feedthrough pins. Blackrock Neurotech then wirebonds the Au wires from AN-USA to the Pt pads on the interposer, and uses a thin layer of epoxy and silicone to strengthen the interposer. During integration of the ANI devices, the interposer is slotted between the feedthrough pins, and the remainder of fabrication process nominally follows standard MEDEL procedures. We adopt the MEDEL naming convention of "K1" for Kanal 1 (channel), where the channel number is defined by the SYNCHRONY2 for the 12 connected electrodes. The remaining three electrodes are not connected, and designate as NC1 - NC3 (Fig. 1a).

Optical and electron microscopy

Optical micrographs (OM) were collected with a VHX-7000 digital microscope (Keyence, Itasca, IL) including low magnification micrographs of the whole electrode array, and at 500× magnification for all individual electrodes both before and after the respective TAA and stimulation paradigms. ANI devices imaged using ring illumination, with the electrode shanks held at a roughly 45° pitch to decrease focus-stacking artifacts and improve contrast. Instrument controlled and processed Z-stacking was used to acquire extended depth of field micrographs for analysis. Micrographs collected before and after the stimulation paradigm were directly compared to determine if physical damage to the IrOx coating and electrode tip occurred. Tip metal damage was also quantitatively scored before and after testing, and comparisons made to determine the change in damage during testing. Optical imaging was also used to assign electrodes to categories of stimulated, control, or discarded, based on quality to utilize the most representative electrodes available for the studies. The Keyence analysis software and ImageJ (LOCI, University of Wisconsin) software were used for electrode surface area quantification. The length and radius of the exposed electrode were used in an expanded cone model to approximate the electrode surface areas Fig. S1. The expanded model accounts for variations in the deinsulation position and blunt tips.

Backscattered electron microscopy (BSEM) was the primary modality used for analyzing the tip metal and electrodes due to the benefit of Z-contrast (atomic number) to discriminate between materials and quantify electrode quality. Images were taken using one of two scanning electron microscopes, a Zeiss Sigma VP (Zeiss, Dublin, CA) or a JEOL JSM-IT510 (JEOL, Peabody, MA). Some imaging was performed in high pressure (low vacuum) mode (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-2}$$\end{document} mbar) to help mitigate sample charging artifacts without sample coating. A 20 kV primary beam energy was used with a working distance of 10 mm to facilitate capturing low magnification images. Overview images were taken before and after testing conditions at a 40× magnification for each array along with three progressively higher magnifications 500, 1250, and 2500× to capture overall electrode morphology and integrity, as well as more detail on the tip metal and electrodes. Supplemental materials Figs. S5-S13 provides the 1250× micrographs used for analysis from all electrodes from before and after the respective testing paradigms.

Figure 2 presents representative OM and BSEM micrographs and identifies specific surface materials. These images highlight the color and contrast mechanisms associated with the IrOx electrode material, Parylene-C shank encapsulation, silicone backside and lead potting, and silicon for the shank and array backplane. In optical micrographs, the exposed IrOx electrode sites have a dark tone with some green iridescence, where evidence of topography, fractures, and damage can be appreciated. The Parylene-C is transparent and presents as a grey tone like the silicon below it, or a darker gray when covering IrOx, and maintains a glossy sheen. The silicon is predominantly exposed at chip tips, and often has a silvery-grey tone with a finish that depends upon the surface roughness and character. Silicone is used for potting and is highly transparent, mostly revealing the underlying materials.

Contrast mechanisms for BSEM are complementary, and the technique offers much higher magnification and depth of field, making it a strong complement to OM. The exposed IrOx and Pt thin-film electrode materials have highlight tone due to their high Z, offering strong contrast with Parylene-C, silicon, and silicone, making identification of damage or changes precise. Silicon presents as a lighter mid-tone, while Parylene-C and silicone are darker mid-tones, which in combination with their spatial distribution facilitates precise materials and damage characterization.

The quality of the IrOx tip metallization and electrode integrity was scored on a scale from 1 (best) to 4 (worst), to quantify and compare the as-received electrodes to outcomes from the study endpoints. Experienced researchers independently scored the tip micrographs using a method similar to (Bjånes et al. 2025), averaging scores across the abiotic damage categories of metallization, shaft, and separation. The electrode scores were compiled into heat maps to visualize their distribution and aid in analysis of changes. EIS, CV, and VTs

The electrochemical properties of the electrode-electrolyte interface are pivotal characteristics for the stimulation performance of AN-USA electrodes. Precise measurements cannot be made from fully integrated ANI devices, as the stimulator electronics lack the capabilities of a research potentiostat; therefore, electrochemical measurements were collected from AN-USA assemblies (Figs. S2-S3) solder integrated to a simple header-pin PCB. AN-USA assemblies are the last stage for electrode arrays prior to integration with the stimulator, making them the most representative option for electrochemical measurements. These measurements are used to compare with published Utah array characteristics and enable more thorough analysis of electrical measurements collected using the SYNCHRONY2 stimulator. Measurements include electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), cyclic voltammetry (CV), and voltage transients (VT). All measurements were collected in 1× (0.15 mM) phosphate-buffered saline at room temperature with a pH adjusted to 7.4 and inside a grounded Faraday cage. Cyclic voltammograms were used to calculate the cathodic charge storage capacity (CSCc), and VT measurements were used to measure the charge injection capacity (CIC).

EIS and CV measurements were taken with a Reference 600+ potentiostat (Gamry, Warminster, PA) in a 3-electrode configuration with an ANI working electrode, saturated (4M KCl) Ag|AgCl reference (Pine Research, Durham, NC), and an 0.414 mm diameter uninsulated Pt wire counter electrode. EIS spectra were collected from 0.1 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{5}$$\end{document} Hz with 10 points per frequency decade at the open-circuit potential using a 20 mV RMS sine wave. Cyclic voltammograms utilized a 5 mV step size and 50 mV/s sweep rate for potentials inside the water window for IrOx of −0.6 to +0.8 V versus Ag|AgCl for three cycles, where the second cycle is presented and analyzed. The electrode CSCc (mC/cm2) was calculated by integrating the area under the cathodic portion of the CV curve (Cogan 2008; Negi et al. 2010). Voltage transient measurements were performed using a STG 4008 neural stimulator (MultiChannel Systems (MCS) Reutlingen, Germany) using the same 3 electrode configuration. Constant current pulses at 200 pulses per second (pps) for 1000 pulses with a pulse duration of 200 μs were applied in increments increasing by 10 μA until the maximum cathodic interface potential (Emc) reached the −0.6 V water window. Additionally, VTs were collected at 100 and 1200 μA to estimate the voltage transients that occur during the performed Stim-Stab protocols and quantify the Emc. Voltage transients were recorded using an NI-9221 ADC (National Instruments, Austin, TX) at 100 kS/s controlled by custom Matlab code similar to recent publications (Stump et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2023). The charge injection capacity (mC/cm2) was calculated based on nominal stimulator current and phase duration resulting in an Emc of −0.6 V.

Voltage matrix and impedance measurements

Measurement of electrochemical characteristics (e.g. impedance) for electrodes from fully integrated ANI devices required the use of the SYNCHRONY2 stimulator. Voltage matrix measurements (VMM) were used to collect electrode characteristics and were performed using Expert Impedance Field Telemetry (E-IFT) function in the MEDEL clinical software Maestro v9.0 (MEDEL, Innsbruck, Austria), or custom developed Python code with the research interface box (RIB2) (University of Innsbruck, Innsbruck, Austria) libraries. These measurements utilized a USB-connected and powered MAX Programming Interface Box (MAXbox) and a MAX Coil S (MEDEL, Innsbruck, Austria) to inductively power and control the magnetically attached ANI devices as depicted in Fig. 3. Voltage matrix measurements involve the stimulation of each electrode and sequentially measuring the voltage at the end of the anodic phase from the stimulated electrode and all other non-stimulated electrodes, resulting in an array of measurements as shown in supplemental materials Fig. S4. Expert Impedance Field Telemetry (E-IFT) measurements for thermal accelerated aging were collected with Maestro using 75.5 μA cathodic-leading pulses with 24.17 μs phase-widths and a 2.1 μs interphase. Use of expert mode (E-IFT) was necessary to access lower currents than the 300 μA currents used for standard IFTs. The voltage was measured at the end of the anodic pulse and Ohm’s law applied to calculate impedance. Voltage matrix measurements for the Stim-Stab paradigm were performed with custom Python code using the RIB2 libraries. These measurements used a slightly longer 26.25 μs pulse width available with RIB2. The VMM were collected at 75.5 μA during the 100 μA Stim-Stab paradigm to agree with measurements made with Maestro. A series of currents 75.5, 100, 300, 600, 900 and 1200 μA were used for each measurement cycle as part of the 1200 μA Stim-Stab paradigm to allow linearity and saturation of the stimulation voltage to be evaluated.

Thermal accelerated aging

Thermal accelerated aging (TAA) was used to evaluate the integrity of ANI devices as part of pre-clinical testing aligning with the AAMI CI86:2017 standard from §20.1 (Cochlear Implant Systems 2017), and used to support approval of our clinical investigation. ANI devices will be implanted for 1.5 years as part of our clinical study, which is used as the endpoint for our TAA test. A cohort of three (n=3) fully integrated ANI devices were submerged in 1× PBS in clean polypropylene containers for a temperature-time progression of 67 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C for 10 days (equivalent to 80 accelerated days) and 77 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C for 33 days (equivalent to 528 accelerated days) yielding a total equivalent lifetime of 1.5 years. The samples were maintained at ±0.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C of the setpoints in a HeraTherm laboratory oven (ThermoFisher Scientific, Langenselbold, Germany). The primary failure criterion was an impedance >70 kΩ, which corresponds to the highest impedance that maintains the ability to deliver 100 μA, the curent limit for the IrOx AN-USA electrodes used with our clinical study. Observed loss or damage of tip metallization will categorize the electrode as failed by the soak test. Note that electrodes K1,K2,K4,K5,K8,K10 from Device 3 had uncharacteristic damage due to handling while preparing samples imaging at the 1-year-equivalent time point.

Statical analysis and rigor

Electrode micrograph quality scores and stimulation electrode selection (n=4 per device) were independently assessed and agreed upon by subject matter experts. AN-USA devices were imaged in optical and electron microscopy to best replicate viewing angles, and facilitate accurate comparisons. Electrodes that were damaged by handling procedures were excluded from comparative analyses. Statistical comparisons on optical and BSEM scores were made using a two tailed bootstrap analysis with Bonferroni and Sidak corrections in MATLAB. The two-tailed bootstrap statistical analysis was capped at a floor value of p = 0.0001 due to computational runtime restraints. Before and after impedances of stimulation and control electrode groups (n = 4) were assessed using independent-samples Welch’s t-tests to determine whether there was a significant change in average impedance, significance denoted using conventional notation. Homogeneity of variance of pooled before and after stimulation groups were evaluated with a one way ANOVA Brown-Forsythe median. Device 2 and 4 outlier impedances were excluded from all statistical comparisons.

ICP-MS sample procedure consisted of an initial calibration curve followed by randomized method blanks, reagent blanks and samples in between four sets of internal quality controls. Isotopes of each target element were chosen to avoid spectroscopic interferences.

Thermal accelerated aging

The primary objective for the development of the ANI device is to evaluate the evoked percepts and performance from intraneural stimulation through a first-in-human study (Lenarz et al. 2025). Demonstrated device stability is a critical requirement for initiation of our clinical study, which has an in-dwelling period of 1.5 years, making this the endpoint of our accelerated aging soak test. The SYNCHRONY2 stimulator used is a marketed medical device, so has demonstrated stability; therefore, our test focuses on the AN-USA component. Devices 1 - 3 were used for the TAA testing.

Longitudinal impedance measurements, OM, and BSEM were used to quantify the electrochemical and physical stability of the electrodes. Devices were removed at 1 and 1.5 year equivalents for optical and BSEM inspection. Discussion

The core objective of device testing in this report is to thoroughly evaluate the electrode array stability for the ANI device as part of determining its suitability for a clinical investigation with up to 1.5 years in-dwelling and 1 year of stimulation. This testing aligns most closely with §20.1 "Maintenance of integrity of materials for life of the implantable device" from the CI86:2017 standard (Cochlear Implant Systems 2017), which provides a high-level framework regarding avoidance of unacceptable hazards from materials degradation. Our study has three primary components that include 1) establish the baseline characteristics of the AN electrode array, 2) evaluate integrity of electrodes for up to 1.5 years in saline, and 3) quantify the stimulation stability of electrodes at both representative and very high stimulation levels.

Evaluation of the baseline device characteristics was an important first step for the study. The quantified electrode geometrical surface area (GSA) reported in Fig. 5 agreed with prior reports for the Utah array, justifying that the results of this study are representative, as differences in surface area strongly affect outcomes from Stim-Stab testing. The design for ANI electrodes allows for small increase in tip GSAs to facilitate maintaining impedances <70 kΩ during the clinical study. Additionally, the GSA is important for interpretation of the baseline electrochemical data, where the impedance, CSCc, and CIC depend on the surface area. The impedance spectra, cyclic voltammograms, voltage transients, and the derived CSCc and CIC for the connectorized AN-USA assemblies agree with published data (Caldwell et al. 2018; Negi et al. 2010; Nguyen et al. 2026). From the voltage transients, it is important to note that we appear to be stimulating with voltages well above the water window for the 1200 μA condition, and delivering a considerable dose of charge under these conditions. A significant degree of electrode activation was observe from the Stim-Stab longitudinal voltages, whereas the electrochemical characterization only briefly stimulated electrodes; therefore it is possible that the Emc decreased during the stimulation paradigm to levels within the water window. This would require a marked drop in Emc from 1.94 to 0.6, so is considered unlikely, but cannot be directly measured with the SYNCHRONY2. The voltage transients were collected using a phase duration of 200 μs, which was also used for the Stim-Stab pulsing. A 200 μs phase duration is very long for modern cochlear implants, which typically use ∼25 μs pulses for high performance coding strategies (Lenarz et al. 2025; Zappia et al. 1990; Winn et al. 2012). Shorter pulses result in a lower voltage for a given current for electrodes like the AN-USA that have a degree of capacitive behavior at the electrode-electrolyte interface.

The second goal was to evaluate the the materials integrity and stability of electrode arrays and ANI devices using a thermal accelerated aging (TAA) study with an endpoint of 1.5 years in saline. All electrodes were classified as survived at the endpoint of the study based on their measured impedances, which served as the primary metric, marking an important milestone for our device. During the study four (4/36) electrodes which transiently crossed the 70 kΩ failure criteria and then decreased below the failure limit albeit at still elevated impedances near 30 kΩ. The passing impedances were observed despite Devices 1–3 having statistically higher (worse) electrode scores relative to Devices 4–9 used for the stimulation conditions indicating worse electrode and tip metallization character, largely due to buckled metallization damage and chipped tips. The most common trends in impedance for NeuroPort Electrodes implanted in the cortex of human subjects is for a near-term (∼1 month) increase in impedances, followed by a longer-term trend to lower impedances attributed to the tissue response and encapsulation materials changes (Downey et al. 2018; Bjånes et al. 2025). The recent large animal pre-clinical study with a percutaneous version of the AN-USA found the in vivo impedance were relatively stable within the 13 to 41-week endpoints, but did have higher impedances with averages ranging from 25 to 68 kΩ and more variation compared to saline measurements (Thomas et al. 2026). The combination TAA and pre-clinical outcomes suggest there are reasonable chances stable and useful impedances will be maintained for a clinical study with a 1.5 year in-dwelling period.

The electrode scores from OM and BSEM for the fully integrated devices are also important for interpretation of the outcomes from this study. The microscopy analysis comparing electrodes before and after soak did find that two and six electrodes from Device 2 and 3, respectively, were damaged during TAA resulting in worse scores, attributed to the clear vulnerability the buckled IrOx to delamination, the operative failure mechanism. The ability of electrodes to maintain passing impedances despite the observed damage suggests a degree of resilience, but impedances in vivo are notably higher than in saline (Sun et al. 2023; Nguyen et al. 2026) and the mechanical forces the would experience are notably worse. This suggests that avoiding in vivo use of devices with buckled metal is advisable. A small change in the device integration process has eliminated the occurrence of buckled metallization making this unlikely for future devices, therefore these studies represent a worst-case scenario for device stability testing. Additionally, comparable electrode scores and ability to appreciate damage to the electrodes between optical and electron microscopy was another important finding, indicating OM is a viable tool for quality assurance inspections, which has been adopted for this project.

Pairwise analysis between the electrode device scores shows a statistically significant difference between Device 4 and all other devices as received in each stage of the study (Before, After TAA, After 100 μA, and After 1200 μA). All devices used for the TAA protocol (Devices 1–3) were statistically scored worse worse than those used for Stim-Stab (excluding device 4). While there are differences between tip scores of devices, there was only small changes that resulted from the soak and stimulation tests themselves, when electrodes damaged by handling are excluded.

The third component was to quantify the stimulation stability of the AN-USA electrodes with the goal of demonstrating their stability at the charge delivery levels expected for 1 year of stimulation in a clinical study. The 100 μA stimulation condition represents the current limit that will be used in the clinical investigation, justified by seminal safety studies from the Bensmaia lab and multiple cortical and peripheral implants studies (Rajan et al. 2015; Fernández et al. 2021; Koivuniemi and Otto 2011; Salas et al. 2018; Fifer et al. 2022; Flesher et al. 2016; Patrick-Krueger et al. 2024). Devices 4–9 were utilized for Stim-Stab with the primary metric being the absence of abrupt increases in stimulation voltages that result from damage to the IrOx. All electrodes in the stimulated category had consistent or decreasing stimulation voltages and passed the test electrically. Devices 4 and 5 had two and one electrodes, respectively, all of which were discarded and also had high voltages consistent with open circuits throughout the study, and do not provide any evidence of stimulation induced damage. When quantified as impedances, we noted that all values dropped, often by more than 10 kΩ, and most devices had impedances of 2 or 3 kΩ at the endpoint. Note that measurements for this study used 75.5 μA pulses with 26.25 μs phase durations resulting in a pulse duration of 55.26 μs with the 2.1 μs interphase is included, roughly corresponding to a characteristic frequency of 18 kHz. This frequency is much higher than the typical 1 kHz used for impedance measurements, and at a much higher current both of which can contribute to small decreases in impedance measurements for this situation.

The decrease in impedance is consistent with the process of activation, a poorly understood process whereby electrochemical reactions decrease impedance (Stump et al. 2025; Otto et al. 2006). The longitudinal voltages with time for Devices 7 and 8 (Fig. 11 for the 6.3 Gpulse plot) highlight the differences in activation rates between stimulated and unstimulated electrodes (control and discarded). The green traces (stimulated) quickly decrease to values near 0.1 to 0.2 volts, whereas a more graduate decrease occurs for the blue (control) and brown (discarded) traces. This is consistent with the vastly lower charge delivery that occurs with only measurement pulses collected every 2 Mpulses for the simulated electrode. Nominally, the constant current pulses provided by the stimulator should generate consistent evoked activity, unless the activation process brings stimulation pulses initially beyond the compliance limit into compliance.

Microscopy characterization comparing electrodes after 100 μA stimulation to before found that electrode materials and morphology were unchanged, except for electrodes damaged just before endpoint microscopy during preparation and handling. This was true even for Device 4 which had statistically significantly worse electrode tip metal scores than others in the cohort, due mostly to many chipped tips. These results suggest that chipped tips have some degree of resilience to stimulation.

The ICP-MS characterization unfortunately provided little insight as Ir concentrations in solution were below the LoQ. Estimating the maximum possible Ir concentration in solution with full dissolution of the tip metallization suggests well less than a 2 ppb concentration would result. The Pt values were also somewhat variable, and full dissolution of the tip metal would result in even smaller concentrations due to the thinner layer and that it deposited as a PtSi material. Additionally, ICP-MS measures ions in solution, and there is evidence that delamination is the process that results in IrOx loss. Ir and IrOx are extremely resistant to dissolution even in acidic solutions; therefore, it is unlikely delaminated IrOx would contribute to the measured concentration. Previous ICP-MS results with the highest charge delivered condition of 0.30 C for stimulation of IrOx electrodes from a Utah array found 0.22 ppb concentrations of Ir (Negi et al. 2010). The much smaller saline volume used for stimulation, differences in IrOx character and processing, and differences in the stimulation paradigm, making comparisons difficult. The larger volume of PBS used to accommodate the full implant, and stimulation of only selected electrodes results in BSEM being a much more sensitive and precise method to quantify loss of tip metal, and for this reason ICP-MS were discontinued for the 1200 μA case.

The 1200 μA stimulation far exceeds the 100 μA to be used in the clinic study, and is justified by the much higher impedance and lower charge injection capacity for Utah array electrodes in vivo and desire to explore the limits of stimulation stability. Thorough electrochemical characterization of Utah arrays in rat cortex up to 25 weeks by Nguyen et al. (2026) found impedances increased by >10× and CIC decreased by 5 to 10× from 2 down to 0.2 to 0.4 mC/cm2; therefore, higher stimulation voltages than needed for the 100 μA stimulation will potentially be required to deliver this current during the clinical study. Only one stimulated electrode (Device 2 K8) demonstrated the abrupt increase in voltage, the hallmark of electrode failure. The voltage matrices collected after this failure show little voltage on surround electrodes when K8 is stimulated, strongly suggesting current is not reaching the electrode, likely due to a broken lead wire or bond pad. All other channels with high voltages maintained the voltages across the entire paradigm, and were either unstimulated control or discarded electrodes. This is consistent with the electrodes having failed prior to starting the study, and therefore do not suggest stimulation induced damage.

The BSEM micrographs provide the most precise physical characterization of IrOx and electrode damage and used for this analysis. Three electrodes stimulated with the 1200 μA paradigm from Devices 4 and 5 exhibited lost IrOx, rounded morphologies for exposed Si at the tip, and a separation between the Si and tip metal, consistent with prior reports of electrode damage (Bjånes et al. 2025; Stump et al. 2025). Interestingly, Bjånes et al. (2025) identifies human explanted 10×10 Neuroport arrays that show erosion of silicon and delamination of electrode material of connected and not connected electrodes with no statistically significant difference between them. Two damaged electrodes were from Device 4, which had the previously described ubiquitous chipped tips. The damaged electrodes had chipped tips as-received, and experienced a delaminated area of IrOx where the flake was still present in the micrograph and corrosion of the silicon shank after stimulation. Device 5 developed damage to the IrOx on the electrode tip where IrOx had delaminate from the tip prior to the study. These results indicate that damage mechanisms are active during this high-dose paradigm, but the slow rate of degradation at these extreme conditions suggest AN-USA electrodes will likely tolerate the stimulation in our 1 year clinical study.

The overall evaluation is that the devices tolerated accelerated aging and both the physiological and high-dose stimulation conditions well. Electrode impedances activated to low values, and with one exception stayed consistent or decreased, despite the presence of buckled metal and chipped tips particularly for Devices 1 - 4. Impedances in vivo will be higher, while CIC will be lower, but the data suggests there is a good chance this can be accommodated. The in vivo environment is also more complex than saline, where outcomes from in vivo are often worse than predicted by bench studies, which makes the point that better accelerated aging tests for microelectrode devices remain a critical need.

Conclusions and future work

The objective for the ANI program is to evaluate hearing outcomes achieved by more direct and selective stimulation of the auditory nerve with a new hearing prosthesis using a penetrating microelectrode array through a first-in-human study. Demonstrating the stability and integrity of electrodes for the ANI device is needed to enable the clinical investigation; therefore, we performed TAA and Stim-Stab testing to evaluate stability for saline soaking and electrical stimulation, respectively. Baseline physical and electrical characterization determined that the electrodes had an average surface area of 3.68 × 10−5 cm2, a typical value of Utah array, with one device noted for having a large variation. Measured electrochemical characteristics also aligned with expectation, with a 1 kHz impedance of 4.86± 1.81 kΩ, CSCc of 25.83± 3.62 mC/cm2, and CIC of 1.70± 0.25 mC/cm2. Bench voltage transients collected using 1200 μA pulses exceeded the water window, suggesting that the Stim-Stab paradigm at these conditions also were above the water window, but electrode activation processes made it difficult to confirm this for the study.

The primary metric for the 1.5 year equivalent TAA test was impedances of <70 kΩ, which all electrodes met at the study endpoint. The impedance of three electrodes did notably increase transiently exceeding the failure criteria before returning to passing values. OM and BSEM were used to image all electrodes before, during, and after soak testing, with OM providing useful quantification of electrode quality, and BSEM providing highly precise data. The TAA devices had statistically more initial damaged compared most Stim-Stab devices, with a buckled and poorly adhered IrOx and chipped tips being the most common damage types. Two devices showed little to no change in electrode damage, while a third device that had poor initial IrOx adhesion before the test did show delamination for 6/15 electrodes. Buckled IrOx has since been addressed by a small integration process change, which when combined with the stable electrode impedances indicates ANI device electrodes have the soak stability needed for our clinical study.

Stim-Stab testing evaluated electrode stability during 6.3 Gpulses using 100 μA, the maximum we will use in vivo to match the maximum estimated charge subjects will receive during 1 year of stimulation of 252 C. We also utilized the same protocol, but with 1200 μA pulses as a high-intensity paradigm, delivering 3024 C. No electrodes from the 100 μA study, and one electrode from the 1200 μA study had the abrupt voltage >50% voltage increase for a failed electrode, but more careful analysis of the voltage matrix suggests a broken lead wire was the cause. Given the 12× larger current amplitude of the 1200 μA protocol and associated high stimulation voltages required to drive the current, their stability suggests the electrode stimulation lifetime is compatible with our clinical use. All electrodes for the 100 μA case, with the exception 11 damaged by handling during imaging procedures and one with a variation in imaging perspective, where not effected by the Stim-Stab protocol, which represents the maximum current used in vivo. The 1200 μA Stim-Stab resulted in 3/20 stimulated electrodes being damaged, with two of those having initial damage that likely contributed to stimulation induced damage. The data suggests these very high currents were tolerated well, and corrosion of silicon, particular the material exposed before stimulation, resulted in delamination of the IrOx tip. Outcomes from the Stim-Stab studies support that ANI electrodes have sufficient stability to be compatible with the 1 year of stimulation for our clinical study.

A number of electrodes especially from Devices 1–4 had electrode damage as received, and this damage contributed to those electrode failures observed. The initial damage has been significantly decreased by a small change in the integration process, and the results suggest further optimization of the tip metallization stability is possible. This is further supported by noting that tip metal and electrode damage that is worse compared to many bench studies was observed for cortical explants after 2.5 to 5.5 years in-dwelling (Bjånes et al. 2025). These outcomes suggest that improving the electrochemical characteristics and adhesion of the IrOx tip metal to the silicon electrode shanks could contribute to longer electrode lifetimes for applications such as sensory neuroprosthetics and brain-computer interfaces.
