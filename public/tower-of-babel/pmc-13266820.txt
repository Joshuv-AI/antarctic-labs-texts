The technical evolution and persistent challenges in the surgical management of mitral annular calcification
Authors: Shinji Kanemitsu
Published: 2026

Introduction

The recent article by Rheault-Henry and Chu, “Extensive posterior bar decalcification with novel calcific emulsification to enable endoscopic minimally invasive mitral valve repair”, published in Annals of Cardiothoracic Surgery (1), addresses what remains one of the most formidable hurdles in modern valvular surgery: the “hostile” environment of the calcified mitral annulus. The authors describe a compelling case where a 67-year-old male presenting with severe mitral regurgitation (MR) and a rigid posterior calcium bar underwent a successful minimally invasive mitral valve repair facilitated by ultrasonic emulsification via the Sonopet device. While the technical success is laudable, this paper invites a broader discussion on the historical misconceptions regarding mitral annular calcification (MAC) and its rarer, more complex variants, such as caseous calcification of the mitral annulus (CCMA) and calcified amorphous tumors (CAT). By analyzing this technique alongside established surgical philosophies—including our own experiences with radical resection and conservative drainage—we can begin to refine the surgical taxonomy required to treat these diverse pathologies safely.

The historical challenge: MAC as a surgical “Gordian Knot”

MAC has been recognized as an independent risk factor for increased perioperative mortality, stroke, and atrioventricular (AV) groove disruption. Traditionally, when a surgeon encounters a rock-hard calcium bar during a mitral repair, they face a harrowing choice: proceed with a high-risk debridement that may compromise the integrity of the annulus or abandon the repair in favor of a potentially difficult valve replacement. The article by Rheault-Henry and Chu highlights a significant paradigm shift in how we approach this “Gordian Knot”. By utilizing controlled ultrasonic emulsification, they have effectively transitioned from the “brute force” of traditional scalpel-based debridement to a “fine sculpting” approach. This evolution is critical, as it addresses the core issue of MAC: the inability to seat an annuloplasty ring or achieve proper coaptation due to the rigidity of the posterior annulus.

Surgical management for severe MAC

The difficulty of managing MAC is underscored by a historical comparison of surgical outcomes. In 1996, Carpentier et al. published a landmark report on the surgical management of MAC, suggesting a two-step technique: (I) en bloc decalcification and reconstruction of the annulus, and (II) valve repair or replacement. Remarkably, in that series, valve repair was achieved in 67 out of 68 cases (2).

However, more recent data from leading Japanese institutions suggest a different reality in modern clinical practice. Uchimuro et al. reported on 61 patients with severe MAC, where the calcified annulus was completely resected and reconstructed with pericardium in the majority of cases (3). In their series, mitral valve repair was attempted in only 4 patients (6.6%), while 57 patients (93.4%) underwent mitral valve replacement. One patient (1.6%) experienced a conversion from repair to replacement due to cardiac rupture. This stark contrast in repair rates suggests that modern patients, often older and with more comorbid conditions, frequently require pericardial patch reconstruction and that the “ideal” repair rate of 98% seen in early reports may be difficult to replicate in the general surgical population.

Defining the spectrum: differentiating MAC from CCMA and CAT

One of the most frequent misconceptions in current surgical literature is the tendency to treat all annular calcification as a uniform entity. However, the management of these lesions must be dictated by their specific morphology and pathophysiology.

Typical MAC

MAC is a chronic degenerative change of the fibrous skeleton. As seen in the case by Rheault-Henry and Chu, this often presents as a solid “bar” that can be targeted for emulsification. However, a nuanced critique of the reported case is warranted. The posterior calcium bar in the presented case was only partially decalcified, and it was still possible to place ring-attachment sutures on other portions of the posterior annulus. In true clinical reality, we often encounter cases with much more extensive annular sclerosis that infiltrate deep into the ventricular myocardium. In such instances, complete reconstruction using pericardium is almost always a prerequisite for valve replacement or repair. Furthermore, an analysis of the operative video suggests that since annular dilation was minimal and the primary cause of MR was a ruptured posterior chordae, it remains a theoretical possibility that the repair might have been achieved via artificial chordae alone, potentially obviating the need for an annuloplasty ring and extensive decalcification.

CCMA

CCMA is a rare variant (0.5% to 1.0% of MAC cases) characterized by a liquefied, toothpaste-like core enclosed within a calcified shell. In our 2025 report “Surgical management for caseous calcification of mitral annulus associated with coronary artery disease” (4), we emphasized that the natural history of these lesions can be aggressive. Computed tomography (CT) imaging in our patient documented a rapid growth of the mass from 10 to 30 mm over a 5-year period, highlighting the potential for mass effect and embolic risk.

CAT

CAT represents an even rarer non-neoplastic mass that often arises from CCMA. As highlighted in the previous report “Calcified amorphous tumor with caseous calcification of mitral annulus in hemodialysis patients” (5), these tumors are particularly prevalent in patients with end-stage renal failure due to altered calcium-phosphate metabolism. In these patients, the “hostility” of the annulus is not just a matter of hardness, but of rapid recurrence and deep tissue infiltration.

Technical analysis: emulsification vs. radical resection vs. drainage

The introduction of the Sonopet device for minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) is a noteworthy technical advancement. The application of ultrasonic emulsification is particularly valuable in the minimally invasive setting, not only for solid MAC but also for liquid-core variants like CCMA. In the restricted working space of endoscopic surgery, the device’s integrated, controlled aspiration is vital in capturing friable material in situ and prevent embolic showers during intraoperative manipulation. However, its application must be compared against other established surgical strategies to determine its place in the surgeon’s armamentarium.

Ultrasonic emulsification (Sonopet)

The primary advantage of this technique is its ability to target anhydrous calcium while theoretically sparing soft tissues like muscle and blood vessels. This allows the surgeon to perform “fine sculpting” of the annulus to facilitate a repair, potentially avoiding the need for patch reconstruction in less severe cases.

Radical resection and neo-annular reconstruction

In the previous report (5), a Cavitron Ultrasonic Surgical Aspirator (CUSA) was used for complete debridement of CCMA and CAT. Because the calcification in these patients was extensive and deeply infiltrative, a radical resection followed by reconstruction of the annulus with a pericardial patch and subsequent valve replacement was performed. While more invasive, this “radical” approach may be necessary when the native annular integrity is completely lost or when there is a high risk of residual calcium causing future embolization.

Limited incision and drainage

For localized CCMA where the leaflets remain intact, we have advocated for a more conservative philosophy. In our case (4), we performed a simple incision and aspiration of the toothpaste-like core. By “collapsing” the mass and obliterating the cavity with sutures, we restored leaflet mobility and achieved a successful repair without the need for extensive debridement or decalcification.

The choice between these techniques should be individualized based on the lesion’s characteristics and the patient’s underlying comorbidities, particularly the presence of renal failure.

The embolic hazard: re-evaluating perioperative stroke risks

Perhaps the most critical concern in any MAC-related surgery is the risk of systemic embolization. Misconceptions regarding the safety of debridement often lead to two extremes: unnecessary avoidance of surgery or overly aggressive resection that triggers an embolic event. It is well-documented that CCMA carries a significantly higher risk of embolic stroke (19.2%) compared to standard MAC (11.8%). The “toothpaste-like” material within a CCMA cavity is highly friable and prone to mobilization during manipulation (6). Rheault-Henry and Chu provide encouraging data regarding the safety of ultrasonic emulsification, citing the study by Brescia et al. (7) in which the Sonopet group experienced a 0% stroke rate, compared to 7.3% in the traditional debridement group. It can be hypothesized that the simultaneous aspiration inherent to ultrasonic devices is the key mechanism for reducing embolic risk, as it captures fragments in situ before they can escape into the systemic circulation. This represents a significant improvement over traditional manual debridement using a scalpel or rongeurs, where small, sharp fragments of calcium often remain sequestered within the left ventricular trabeculations, posing a post-bypass embolic threat.

Procedural specificity and the MICS paradigm

The transition to an endoscopic minimally invasive approach for MAC is a technical feat that requires meticulous coordination. As noted by the authors, the Sonopet handle remains “somewhat bulky” for use through a small port, yet they successfully navigated this challenge using endoscopic visualization.

We must caution, however, that the success of MICS in MAC is highly dependent on patient selection. In our experience, while a minimally invasive approach via right thoracotomy may be feasible for isolated CCMA, it may not be suitable for cases with extensive calcification invading the subvalvular apparatus or in patients requiring concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting. The authors appropriately acknowledged that traditional techniques might still be necessary if the MAC is too extensive or if annular perforation occurs.

Summary and future directions

In summary, the article by Rheault-Henry and Chu represents a vital contribution to the literature on “repairable” mitral valves. Their use of ultrasonic emulsification demonstrates that with the right technology, we can move beyond the “Gordian Knot” of MAC and offer patients the durable benefits of a valve repair. However, we must remain vigilant against the generic use of the term “MAC” for all annular lesions. Just as the “Maze” procedure is often misapplied to incomplete ablation patterns, “MAC surgery” is frequently used to describe wildly different procedures. We must adhere to a strict set of goals for each pathology. Further investigation is undoubtedly required to confirm the long-term durability of repairs performed after ultrasonic sculpting. But for now, this technique provides a reproducible and safer alternative that aligns with our collective goal of minimizing surgical trauma while optimizing functional outcomes for this high-risk population. However, it is important to acknowledge that current evidence for ultrasonic emulsification is based on limited case series, and the long-term durability and safety across broader patient populations remain to be established through further clinical investigation.
