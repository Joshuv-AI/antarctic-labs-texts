Structural and mechanistic insights into the inhibition of Plasmodium falciparum MDR1
Authors: Ziyan Zhao, Jialu Li, Xinye Wang, Xiaofeng Liu, Nan Wang, Hanwen Xu, Cantao Quan, Yu Gao, Jing Zhang, Xiang Wang, Li Guo, Nobutaka Kato, Dong Deng, Xin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria, caused by the parasite Plasmodium falciparum, remains a significant global health threat, with multidrug resistance posing a major challenge to treatment. The P-glycoprotein homolog P. falciparum Multidrug Resistance Protein 1 (PfMDR1) is a key determinant of resistance to first-line antimalarials like mefloquine (MFQ) and chloroquine. ACT-451840, a clinical phase I drug, has been developed as an antimalarial candidate, but its mechanism of action and interaction with drug resistance markers remain to be fully understood. Here, we present the cryo-electron microscopy structure of PfMDR1 in complex with ACT-451840, determined at a resolution of 3.42 Å. The structure reveals that ACT-451840 binds within the central cavity and locks PfMDR1 in an inward-open conformation, inhibiting its basal ATPase activity. A structural comparison of the ACT-451840-bound state with the previously reported MFQ-bound state provides a molecular explanation for how ACT-451840 resistance mutations can lead to the sensitization of MFQ. Furthermore, a comparative structural analysis and biochemical characterization with human ABCB1 reveal the selective mechanism of ACT-451840 against PfMDR1. Our findings provide a structural basis for the inhibitory mechanism of ACT-451840, which may inform the future development of antimalarial candidates targeting PfMDR1.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Parasitology, Membrane proteins, Permeation and transport

Introduction

Malaria poses a substantial global health burden, with global estimates for 2023 at 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths. Although five Plasmodium species are known to cause malaria in humans, P. falciparum is the most lethal, responsible for 98.9% of cases within the WHO African region1–3. While two malaria vaccines, RTS,S and R21, have been rolled out in Africa, providing partial protection to children in endemic areas, chemotherapies remain the core intervention for malaria prevention and treatment4,5. To date, dozens of compounds—such as quinine (QN)6, chloroquine (CQ)7, primaquine (PQ)8, sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP)9, atovaquone (ATQ), and artemisinin (ART) derivatives—have been approved for malaria treatment10. However, regardless of their diverse chemical structures and mechanisms of action, all antimalarial agents, including the highly effective artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), have faced increasing drug resistance11–14. Furthermore, the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant P. falciparum parasites in the Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS) have significantly compounded this challenge15. This highlights the need to understand these resistance mechanisms and develop strategies to overcome them.

The P. falciparum multidrug-resistant protein (PfMDR1), an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter and a homolog of the mammalian MDR1, serves as a key marker for resistance to various antimalarial drugs16. This protein is situated on the parasite’s digestive vacuole membrane17, where it mediates the transport of a wide range of solutes and the partner drugs used in artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), including chloroquine (CQ). An increased pfmdr1 copy number is a well-established molecular marker for resistance to drugs like mefloquine (MFQ)18. Moreover, five globally prevalent single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in pfmdr1 are correlated with two distinct resistance phenotypes: CQ resistance (CQR) and ACT partner drug resistance. Regarding CQR, the K76T mutation in the P. falciparum CQ-resistant transporter (PfCRT), a CQ exporter also located on the digestive vacuole membrane, is the primary driver of reduced susceptibility to CQ19,20. However, PfMDR1 mutants, such as N86Y, can further exacerbate this resistant phenotype by reducing the influx of CQ21. The contributions to ACT partner drug resistance are even more significant, as ACTs have become the standard treatment for uncomplicated P. falciparum infections due to widespread CQR. In this context, pfmdr1 SNPs are specifically correlated with resistance to amodiaquine (ADQ), piperaquine (PPQ), mefloquine (MFQ), and lumefantrine (LMF)18,22–24. For example, the N86Y mutation in PfMDR1 has been proposed to decrease the influx of LMF into the digestive vacuole, thereby increasing the drug’s concentration at its site of action (presumably in the cytosol) and consequently leading to increased drug sensitivity in these parasites20.

Beyond its well-known role in multidrug resistance, PfMDR1 has been proposed as a potential antimalarial drug target. A phenylalanine-based compound, ACT-451840, was discovered through an erythrocyte-based phenotypic screen and exhibits potent single-digit nanomolar activity against both chloroquine-sensitive and resistant P. falciparum strains25. This compound is effective against all asexual blood stages and also blocks gamete formation26. Given its potent in vivo antimalarial activity in mouse models, ACT-451840 was advanced to a Phase I clinical study, where it demonstrated good safety and tolerability27.

The mode of action (MoA) of ACT-213615, the parent compound of ACT-451840, was determined to be distinct from that of artemisinin (ART), chloroquine (CQ), and quinine (QN) through a microarray-based gene expression analysis28. Subsequent photo-reactive affinity capture analysis confirmed a physical interaction between ACT-213615 and PfMDR126. Concurrently, resistance selection of P. falciparum under ACT-451840 treatment exclusively yielded SNPs on PfMDR1, a finding that was solidified by subsequent CRISPR-Cas9-modified P. falciparum parasites29. Notably, the PfMDR1 resistance mutations also sensitized the parasites to clinical drugs such as lumefantrine (LMF), mefloquine (MFQ), quinine (QN), and amodiaquine (ADQ), suggesting that ACT-451840 could potentially serve as an ACT partner drug to address existing drug resistance30. Given its essential functionality in drug resistance and potential as a drug target, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying PfMDR1 inhibition is essential for clarifying its role in drug resistance.

In this study, we purify PfMDR1 and validate its in vitro interaction with ACT-451840. By solving the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of ACT-451840 bound to PfMDR1, we present a detailed understanding of the compound’s mode of action (MoA). Further structural comparisons and biochemical characterization of PfMDR1 variants elucidate the mechanism of selective inhibition. Moreover, the resistance mechanism of key PfMDR1 variants is delineated through molecular modeling. Collectively, our results provide a comprehensive molecular view of the inhibitor recognition, selective inhibition, and resistance mechanisms of the clinical-stage ACT-451840, providing a molecular framework that may inform the optimization of antimalarial candidates targeting PfMDR1.

Results

ACT-451840 inhibits PfMDR1 through direct binding

The PfMDR1 protein was generated through recombinant expression using the HEK293 Freestyle expression system. The recombinant PfMDR1 was then extracted and purified in the detergent lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace), showing monodisperse profile behavior in size-exclusion chromatography. To validate the protein’s activity, we measured its ATPase activity at various ATP concentrations (Fig. 1a). The significant ATPase activity of the purified PfMDR1 confirms that the wild-type protein is functional.

Previous resistance-selection analyses have suggested PfMDR1 as a potential target of ACT-451840; however, direct in vitro evidence of this interaction was lacking. To address this, we examined the ATPase activity of PfMDR1 when treated with different concentrations of ACT-451840. In contrast to the substrate-stimulated activity observed with drugs like mefloquine (MFQ)31, ACT-451840 inhibits the basal ATPase activity of purified PfMDR1, with an inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) value of 352.0 ± 16.7 nM (Fig. 1b), thereby indicating a direct physical interaction in vitro. Notably, the binding of a substrate such as MFQ typically stimulates PfMDR1’s ATPase activity due to a substrate-induced NBD closure32, which occurs in the micromolar range31. Conversely, the inhibitory effect of ACT-451840 on PfMDR1’s ATPase activity suggests that the compound acts as an inhibitor by blocking this NBD closure. While this inhibition could also potentially involve direct interference with the ATPase domains or TMD-NBD coupling, we sought to provide a definitive structural basis for this effect. To this end, we determined the cryo-EM structure of PfMDR1 in complex with ACT-451840 to reveal how ligand binding physically inhibit the protein.

Inhibition mechanism of ACT-451840

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying ACT-451840 inhibition, the purified PfMDR1 protein was incubated with ACT-451840 prior to cryo-EM analysis. An ACT-451840 bound PfMDR1 structure was determined at an overall resolution of 3.42 Å, with a core resolution of up to 2.90 Å in the ligand-binding site (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1). Given this relatively high resolution, the contours of the inhibitor and the surrounding substrate-binding residues were unambiguously defined (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. a, b). The TMD1 (TM1-6), TMD2 (TM7-12), and NBD2 domains were well-resolved, whereas the NBD1 domain exhibited ambiguous density and was built by docking the NBD1 domain from an existing model (PDB code: 8JVH) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2c). The density of the intervening sequence between the NBD1 and TM7 was also invisible, indicating its highly dynamic nature.

The overall structure exhibits a canonical architecture of the ABCB family, in which TMD1 and TMD2 form a pseudosymmetrical V-shape via domain swapping of the fourth and fifth transmembrane helices (TMs) of each six-helices contained TMDs. The membrane-embedded regions (TM1-12) form the substrate binding and translocation tunnel, which is open to the cytoplasmic side (Fig. 1d). In agreement with our hypothesis that ACT-451840 blocks the closure of PfMDR1, the ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structure adopts a widely open, inward-facing state with the two NBDs largely separated. This conformation can be superimposed with the apo inward-open PfMDR1 structure (PDB code: 8JVH) with a root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) of 1.05 Å over 944 aligned Cα atoms (Fig. 1e).

A detailed inspection of the inhibitor binding pocket revealed the binding pose of ACT-451840. The compound sits approximately halfway along the substrate translocation path and makes extensive contact with TMs 1, 4, 5, and 6 of TMD1 and TMs 7, 10, and 12 of TMD2 (Fig. 2a). The X-shaped ACT-451840 molecule extends its phenylalanine (moiety 1) and 1-(4-cyanobenzyl) piperazine (moiety 2) groups into a cleft primarily coordinated by the helical bundle consisting of TM4-5, TM7-9, and TM12. Conversely, the cinnamic acid moiety (moiety 3) and 1-acetyl-4-phenylpiperazine moiety (moiety 4) are positioned toward the opposing bundle, involving TM1-3, TM6, and TM10-11 (Fig. 2a). The stretched ACT-451840 molecule acts as a molecular wedge within the central cavity, effectively hindering the conformational transitions and thereby inhibiting the transport activity of PfMDR1. With PfMDR1 locked in the inward-open conformational state, the digestive vacuole side of the inhibitor-binding pocket is sealed, forming extensive interactions with ACT-451840. The opposite side of this pocket, which is open to the cytoplasmic side and accessible to the solvent, contains the narrowest part of the substrate translocation path, formed by Phe331 on TM6 and Lys1079 on TM12. This narrow constriction impedes the dissociation of ACT-451840 from its current conformational state (Fig. 2b).

The engagement between PfMDR1 and ACT-451840 is mediated by both hydrophobic and polar contacts. Specifically, hydrophobic interactions contribute to inhibitor binding and involve residues Phe74 and Phe78 on TM1; Phe202 and Ile205 on TM4; Ile282, Ile286, and Leu287 on TM5; Leu324, Leu327, Ile328, Met330, and Thr334 on TM6; Tyr803 and Phe806 on TM7; and Phe1072 on TM12 (Supplementary Fig. 3a); Furthermore, Tyr290 on TM5 may form water-mediated hydrogen bonds with the carboxyl groups of the phenylalanine moieties of ACT-451840. Gln1038 and Asn1042 on TM11 are more likely form water-mediated hydrogen bonds with the carboxyl group of ACT-451840, stabilizing its 4-N-acetylpiperazine-substituted benzyl moiety. Finally, Phe331 on TM6 and Tyr1076 on TM12 both contribute to inhibitor recognition through aromatic interactions: Phe331 forms a π–π stacking interaction with the phenol moiety of ACT-451840, while Tyr1076 engages in a similar π–π stacking with the phenylalanine moiety of the inhibitor. The latter interaction is further stabilized by a hydrogen bond with Asn283 on TM5, which helps orient the Tyr1076 side chain for optimal engagement (Fig. 2c, d).

In line with the structural observations, Tyr290, Phe331, and Tyr1076, which contribute to binding through a hydrogen-bond network and π–π stacking interactions, respectively, also show a substantial decrease in ACT-451840 inhibition when mutated to alanine, with the Y290A, F331A, and Y1076A variants exhibiting significantly higher IC50 values (Fig. 2e). Notably, Phe74 forms a key boundary for both the cinnamic acid and 1-acetyl-4-phenylpiperazine moieties. The F74A mutation, which eliminates the bulky phenol group, increases the pocket volume and significantly weakens inhibitor binding, thereby substantially reducing ACT-451840’s potency (Fig. 2e). Similarly, alanine substitutions of key ACT-451840-binding residues, such Tyr803 and Asn1042, resulted in decreased potency of ACT-451840, as shown by increased IC50 values (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3b).

ACT-451840 resistance mechanism in PfMDR1 variants

Resistance selection analysis and CRISPR-Cas9-edited PfMDR1 have suggested that mutations at Gly297, Gly316, Ile322, Ala807, Met841 (along with Met924), or Tyr1076 lead to a gain of resistance to ACT-45184029. To elucidate the resistance mechanism, we mapped these mutations onto our ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structure (Fig. 3a). Of these six single or combined mutations, Gly316, Ile322, and Gly297 are located in the digestive vacuole (DV) lumen side of PfMDR1, distant from the ACT-451840 binding pocket.

Specifically, Gly297 and Ile322 are nested in a tightly connected hydrophobic environment formed by the DV lumen parts of TMs 3, 4, 5, and 6 (Fig. 3b). A bulky residue replacement at these sites, such as G297V or I322F, creates steric hindrance for the TM cluster, consequently hampering the conformational transition to the inward-open state necessary for ACT-451840 binding. Similarly, Gly316, along with surrounding hydrophobic residues like Ile301, Leu817, Phe818, and Leu823, forms part of the hydrophobic gating region on the DV lumen side of PfMDR1 (Fig. 3c). The introduction of a charged group by the G316R mutation likely imposes an energy barrier to the closure of the DV lumen side, thereby hindering substrate binding and decreasing ACT-451840 potency. In agreement with this structural observation, the PfMDR1 G316R variant exhibits a significantly increased IC50 for ACT-451840 (Fig. 3f).

The remaining three mutations are located near the ACT-451840 binding pocket, either interacting directly with the inhibitor or contacting residues involved in inhibitor binding. As mentioned previously, Tyr1076 directly contributes to ACT-451840 binding through a π–π stacking contact. The resistance mutation Y1076H abolishes this interaction with the phenylalanine moiety of ACT-451840, consequently resulting in a significantly higher IC50 of 954.3 ± 211.5 nM (Fig. 3d, f). In contrast, Met841 is a second-shell residue situated near Tyr1076. It does not make direct contact with the ACT-451840 but may indirectly affect binding by perturbing the position or dynamics of Tyr1076, thereby modulating the environment of the binding pocket (Fig. 3d, e).

In addition to the single M841I mutation, a double mutation of M841I&M924I was also identified in a step-wise selection analysis. In our structural model, Met924 on TM9 points its side chain toward Met841 on TM8, yet an intervening TM7 separates these two residues and prevents their direct contact (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). It is notable that Met924 is exposed on the surface of the membrane-embedded side and makes contact with the detergent micelle in the Cryo-EM map (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Therefore, the M924I mutation is unlikely to directly affect inhibitor binding given its surface location and distance from the ACT-451840 binding site. Given the context that the Cas9-edited M924I variant displays a low-level resistance to ACT-451840 but shows an augmented resistance when combined with M841I, M924I may not act alone to induce resistance but instead synergize the effect of M841I by affecting the dynamic motion of TMD2.

Similar to Met841, Ala807 also sits behind an inhibitor-recognition residue, Phe1072. The benzyl group of Phe1072 is sandwiched between Ala807 and the cinnamic acid moiety of ACT-451840 (Fig. 3e). Nevertheless, the A807P variant shows little effect on ACT-451840 potency in the in vitro ATPase activity analysis, suggesting an alternative mechanism for reduced susceptibility rather than weakening the ACT-451840 engagement (Fig. 3e, f). Interestingly, the A807P variant exhibited higher expression levels compared to the wild-type protein in our HEK293F expression system, while maintaining similar protein stability (Supplementary Fig. 4c). While the correlation in expression profiles between heterologous systems and P. falciparum requires further validation, this observation raises the possibility that the resistant phenotype may be linked to altered protein abundance rather than a direct loss of drug affinity. It is conceivable that elevated PfMDR1 levels could potentially necessitate higher concentrations of ACT-451840 for effective inhibition. Such a mechanism, if confirmed in vivo, would be reminiscent of the decreased susceptibility to ACT-213615 (the parent compound of ACT-451840) observed in the FCB strain, which carries two copies of pfmdr118.

Structural comparison of the MFQ-bound and the ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1

Despite the emergence of resistance mutations during ACT-451840 treatment, these same mutations can sensitize parasites to clinical drugs like LMF, MFQ, QN, and AMQ29. This finding presents a valuable opportunity for combination therapies to overcome resistance. To explore the molecular mechanism underlying the potentiated susceptibility to MFQ, we compared the ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structure with the previously reported MFQ-bound structure.

Both the ACT-451840-bound and MFQ-bound PfMDR1 structures adopt a similar inward-open conformational state. They can be aligned with a root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) of 0.996 Å over 1064 aligned Cα atoms (Fig. 4a). Both ACT-451840 and MFQ are positioned within the central substrate translocation path, where the MFQ exhibits partial overlap with the 1-acetyl-4-phenylpiperazine moiety (moiety 4) of ACT-451840 (Fig. 4a). This structural finding is supported by functional data showing that the MFQ-stimulated ATPase activity is reduced by the addition of ACT-451840, suggesting a potential competitive binding between these two molecules (Fig. 4b).

To accommodate the bulky quinolone moiety of MFQ, TMs 10 and 11 in the MFQ-bound PfMDR1 structure slightly swing toward a wider open position, and TM10 is distorted from Val946 to Arg952 compared to the ACT-451840-bound structure (Fig. 4c). The MFQ binding pocket (Pocket 2), encompassed by TMs 1, 6, 11, and 12, also nests the cinnamic acid and 1-acetyl-4-phenylpiperazine moieties of ACT-451840. The remaining moieties of ACT-451840 (phenylalanine and 1-(4-cyanobenzyl) piperazine) are accommodated by a neighboring cleft (Pocket 1), which is surrounded by TMs 4, 5, 6, 7, and 12 (Fig. 4d). Therefore, residues lining Pocket 2, including Phe331 on TM6, Gln1038 on TM11, and Phe1072 on TM12, are shared between the MFQ- and ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structures (Fig. 4d).

It has been reported that PfMDR1 mutations at Gly316, Ala807, or Met841, particularly the G316R variant, can simultaneously cause ACT-451840 resistance and enhance MFQ susceptibility. These three residues, as well as their adjacent residues, adopt highly similar configurations in both the MFQ- and ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structures (Fig. 4e). Interestingly, none of these residues are located in Pocket 2, which suggests they are not directly involved in MFQ binding.

This observation leads to the hypothesis that these mutations may potentiate MFQ susceptibility by altering the transport dynamics of PfMDR1 rather than affecting its direct binding. For instance, our previous analysis of the ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 structure revealed that the Gly316 and adjacent hydrophobic residues constitute part of the hydrophobic gating region on the DV lumen side (Fig. 3c). The introduction of a charged group in the G316R variant likely increases the energetic barrier for PfMDR1 turnover and the transport of substrates like MFQ. Consistently, in the previously reported outward-facing structure of PfMDR1, G316 is found to be solvent-exposed31. This transition from a buried hydrophobic environment in the inward-facing state to a solvent-exposed environment in the outward-facing state suggests that the G316R mutation could significantly perturb the conformational equilibrium by imposing a higher energetic penalty during the transport cycle. In agreement with this hypothesis, both the basal and MFQ-induced ATPase activities of the G316R variant are significantly higher than those of the wild-type PfMDR1 and other variants (Fig. 4f), which indicates a higher population of the NBD-dimerized conformation. Consequently, this change in transport dynamics leads to an increased distribution of MFQ in the parasite’s cytoplasm, where its primary site of action-the Pf80S ribosome-resides, thereby enhancing its parasitocidal efficiency.

Selective inhibition mechanism between PfMDR1 and human MDR1

Given the favorable safety and tolerability of ACT-451840 in Phase I clinical studies, it is of interest to unravel the molecular basis for its selective inhibition of PfMDR1 over its human homolog, MDR1 (hABCB1), for effective lead optimization. In vitro ATPase inhibition assays show that ACT-451840 exhibits negligible inhibition of hABCB1 ATPase activity (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Recent cryo-EM analysis of hABCB1 has provided various substrate- and inhibitor-bound structures in different conformational states, including the inward-facing, occluded, and collapsed-closed states, which collectively illustrate the process of NBD and central cavity closure. Among these states, the substrate translocation path of the collapsed-closed hABCB1 is tightly sealed by TMs 3, 6, 9, and 12, leaving no space for ligand engagement, even with the addition of compounds like Verapamil33 (Fig. 5a).

The substrate-bound ABCB1 structures33–38 were captured in the inward-facing and occluded states, with kinking of TM4 and/or TM10 being the most significant features distinguishing these conformations (Supplementary Fig. 5b). A structural alignment of the TM4/10-kinked hABCB1 (occluded state) with the ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 reveals a steric clash between the kinked TM4 of hABCB1 and the ligand, suggesting that ACT-451840 is incapable of binding to this conformational state (Fig. 5b).

In contrast, the inward-facing hABCB1, whether with a kinked TM10 or not, possesses a central cavity that is spatially permissive for ACT-451840 engagement (Fig. 5c). This compatibility was further confirmed by molecular docking, which showed that ACT-451840 can indeed bind within the central cavity of inward-facing hABCB1 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5c). To further address the mechanism of selectivity, we inspected the differences between the ACT-451840-binding residues in PfMDR1 and their counterparts in hABCB1. Among these key binding residues, Phe74, Asn283, Tyr803, Asn1042, and Tyr1076 on PfMDR1 are not conserved when compared to their hABCB1 counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

Structural observation suggests that the replacement of PfMDR1’s Phe74 with hABCB1’s methionine counterpart increases the volume of the binding pocket, which could facilitate motion and decrease the binding affinity of ACT-451840 (Fig. 5e). As previously mentioned, the alanine replacement of PfMDR1’s Tyr1076 impairs ACT-451840 efficacy by disrupting the crucial π–π stacking with the ligand’s phenylalanine moiety (Figs. 2c and 5e). In line with these observations, F74M and Y1076A variants in PfMDR1 significantly increase the dissociation constant of ACT-451840 (Figs. 2e and 5f), supporting the role of these nonconserved residues in selective binding.

With respect to PfMDR1’s Tyr803 and Asn1042, our aforementioned analysis showed that alanine substitution of these residues has a limited effect on ACT-451840 binding (Fig. 2e). Consistently, the Y803Q or N1042Y variants—which mimic the nonconserved nature of these residues—had a minor effect on inhibition efficacy, suggesting that the differences in these two binding residues make a limited contribution to the selective inhibition of ACT-451840 (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

PfMDR1 has long been a subject of intense interest due to its central role in the multi-drug resistance of Plasmodium parasites39,40. Its substrate polyspecificity for diverse antimalarials, such as chloroquine (CQ), mefloquine (MFQ), and lumefantrine (LMF), leads to the redistribution of these compounds away from their targets, thereby compromising their parasiticidal potency41–43. A serendipitous discovery during a screen for plasmepsin (PM) inhibitors yielded a piperazine-containing compound, ACT-213615, which was characterized as a PfMDR1-targeting agent26. Parallel structure-activity relationship research led to the optimized lead, ACT-451840, which exhibits potent activity against CQ-resistant strains and has demonstrated good safety and tolerability in Phase I clinical trials27,44. Although Phase I clinical trials indicated good safety and tolerability, the trial could not advance further due to recrudescent parasitemia, which was attributed to insufficient metabolic stability. The structural insights from our study now provide a molecular basis for understanding these pharmacological limitations, potentially facilitating the structure-guided optimization of ACT-451840 to enhance their metabolic profiles.

Our results suggest that ACT-451840 directly engages PfMDR1, as demonstrated by the inhibition of its basal ATPase activity. In agreement with the inhibited ATPase activity, the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of ACT-451840-bound PfMDR1 revealed an inward-open state, where the two nucleotide-binding domains (NBDs) are largely separated45. The X-shaped ACT-451840 molecule sits in the central pocket and extends its four moieties into distinct crevices in TMD1 and TMD2, effectively wedging the substrate translocation pathway and blocking conformational transition. Among the key recognition residues, alanine replacement of Phe74, Tyr290, Phe331 or Tyr1076 significantly decreased ACT-451840’s potency, indicating their key role in inhibitor engagement.

Previous resistance selection and CRISPR-Cas9-edited studies showed that six single or combined mutations can confer resistance to ACT-45184019,29,46. Our structural analysis reveals that mutations at Gly297, Ile322, and Gly316 perturb PfMDR1 dynamics by affecting the closure of the digestive vacuole side. The Y1076H mutation, on the other hand, compromises its crucial π–π stacking with the phenylalanine moiety of ACT-451840, thus attenuating inhibition efficiency. Mutations at Ala807 and Met841 are positioned behind Phe1072 or adjacent to Tyr1076, respectively. These structural arrangements suggest that they compromise inhibitor potency through an indirect, allosteric manner, likely by perturbing the position or dynamics of the primary binding residues. Intriguingly, it has been reported that these ACT-451840-resistant mutations, particularly the G316R variant, augment the parasitocidal effect of several clinical antimalarials like MFQ29. A structural alignment revealed that these two substrates partially overlap within the PfMDR1 central cavity. However, none of the resistance mutations are directly involved in MFQ binding. Since the G316R mutation likely hampers the transporter turnover by increasing the energetic barrier for closure at the digestive vacuole side, we hypothesize that the resulting increased cytoplasmic distribution of MFQ strengthens its potency to its major target, the Pf80S ribosome47.

This redistribution mechanism is likely further modulated by the interplay between PfMDR1 and PfCRT. While PfMDR1 facilitates the inward transport of substrates like MFQ and LMF into the digestive vacuole (DV)20,48,49, recent evidence suggests that mutant PfCRT isoforms have evolved the capacity to transport these same compounds in the opposite direction, from the DV back to the cytosol46. Consequently, the inhibition of PfMDR1-mediated sequestration, in conjunction with PfCRT-mediated efflux, proposes a coordinated mechanism that maximizes drug accumulation in the cytoplasm. This potential interplay highlights the complex role of PfMDR1 in modulating the subcellular pharmacokinetics of antimalarials within the parasite.

The favorable safety profile of ACT-451840 in Phase I clinical trials suggests high specificity for PfMDR1 over its human homolog hABCB144. A structural comparison between the multi-conformational states of hABCB1 and the PfMDR1 structure reveals that only the inward-open state of hABCB133–38 is capable of accommodating ACT-451840. Furthermore, differences in key residues within the inhibitor binding pocket lead to a weakened engagement of ACT-451840 with hABCB1 compared to PfMDR1. While ACT-451840 demonstrates high specificity, its clinical progression was primarily hindered by metabolic instability at the tert-butyl and piperazine moieties44. Our structure reveals that these labile groups are positioned in regions that may tolerate strategic modifications; for instance, the hydrophobic sub-pocket surrounding the tert-butyl group could potentially accommodate more stable bioisosteres, while the piperazine linkers could be further optimized to resist enzymatic degradation. These structural insights offer structural information that may assist in the future exploration of ACT-451840 derivatives with improved pharmacokinetic profiles.

In summary, our data suggests that ACT-451840 stabilizes PfMDR1 in an inward-open conformation, which is consistent with the observed inhibition of its ATPase activity in vitro (Fig. 6). Consequently, the increased concentration of clinical drugs, such as MFQ, LMF, QN, and AMQ, at their respective target locations in ACT-451840-resistant parasites likely explains the augmented potency of these drugs. It would be of great interest to elucidate the natural substrate transported by PfMDR1, as this will further decipher the lethal phenotype underlying PfMDR1 inhibition.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK-293F cells (ThermoFisher Scientific, R79007) were cultured in SMM293-TII Expression Medium (serum-free, complete medium; Sino Biological, M293TII) and maintained in shaking thermostatic incubators. For protein expression, the cells were transfected using linear polyethylenimine (MW 25,000; Polysciences, 23966).

Protein expression and purification

Genes encoding full-length PfMDR1 (UniProt: P13568; residues 1-1419) was synthesized and subcloned into a modified pCAG vector with an N-terminal Twin-2×Strep tag. The pcDNA3.1-hABCB1 plasmid was kindly provided by Dr. Heqiao Zhang (ShanghaiTech University). Mutant PfMDR1 constructs and hABCB1 were generated by PCR and cloned into the same vector. HEK293F cells (2 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were transfected with the plasmids and harvested 60 h post-transfection by centrifugation (3000 g, 15 min, 4 °C).

The collected cells were resuspended in binding buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5 mM EDTA; 2% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM, (Bluepus)); 0.8 µM Aprotinin; 2 µM Pepstatin A; 0.5 µg/mL Leupeptin) and solubilized for 2 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation (12,000 g, 45 min), the supernatant was collected and incubated with Strep-Tactin® XT resin (IBA) at 4 °C. The resin was washed with wash buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5 mM EDTA; 0.01% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace)), and the protein was eluted with the same buffer containing 50 mM D-desthiobiotin (Sangon).

The eluted protein was concentrated to 1 mL using a 100 kDa MWCO concentrator and clarified by centrifugation (3000 g, 5 min, 4 °C). The sample was then subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL, GE Healthcare) in buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 0.005% (w/v) LMNG). Fractions containing PfMDR1 were pooled and concentrated to 6–10 mg/mL for cryo-EM sample preparation.

Grid preparation and cryo- EM data collection

The purified PfMDR1 protein was incubated with 10 mM ACT-451840 (MCE, MedChemExpress) for 30 min and centrifuged before being applied to the grids. Three microliters of the protein sample were placed on glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh). The grids were blotted for 3.0 seconds and flash-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The plunger was operated at 4 °C with 100% humidity.

Data were collected using Titan Krios electron microscope operating at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K2 Summit detector and a GIF Quantum Energy Filter (slit width 20 eV). Movies were recorded with a pixel size of 1.1 Å. Each stack of 30 frames was exposed for 6 seconds, with a total dose rate of approximately 7.089 e⁻/Å² per stack. Automated data collection was managed using EPU. All 30 frames in each stack were aligned and summed using Patch Motion Correction tool in cryoSPARC v3.3.1. The defocus value was set from -1.2 to -2.0 µm.

Cryo- EM data processing

One thousand, eight hundred and four good micrographs were manually selected after CTF estimation in cryoSPARC 3.3.150. 554,647 particles were automatically picked and bin2 extracted with a box size of 256 pixels (rescaled to 128 pixels). After 2D classification, good particles from the best classes was used to generate an initial model. This initial model was used to create 50 templates for further particle picking. Subsequently, 1,163,300 particles were auto picked using template picker and bin2 extracted. After 2D classification, ab-Initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement, 575,779 good particles were selected and unbinned extracted with a box size of 256 pixels. Following a round of heterogeneous refinement, combined with several rounds of non-uniform refinement and local refinement, a final set of 337,745 particles were selected, resulting in a 3.43 Å map.

Model building, refinement, and validation

An atomic model was initially fitted into the cryo-EM density and subsequently adjusted and manually rebuilt using COOT v0.9.8.9351. The three-dimensional coordinates and corresponding restraint files for the inhibitor ACT-451840 were prepared with the sketcher module from the CCP4 package52. Preliminary refinements of the PfMDR1–ACT-451840 models were conducted in PHENIX v1.21.2.5419 against their respective cryo-EM maps in real space, applying restraints for secondary structure and stereochemistry53. The final structures were assessed by MolProbity analysis, including overall scores and Ramachandran plot statistics, as previously described54.

ATPase activity assay

ATPase activity was quantified by measuring inorganic phosphate released from ATP hydrolysis using a Malachite Green Phosphate Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems, DATG-200) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PfMDR1 and hABCB1 (UniProt: P08183; residues 1-1280) (0.05 mg/ml) were incubated with varying concentrations of ATP for 30 min at 37 °C, and absorbance at 620 nm was recorded. For substrate-inhibition assays, PfMDR1 and hABCB1 were pre-incubated with test compounds (dissolved in DMSO) for 10 min at 37 °C before adding ATP to 4 mM; the reaction was then incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Inorganic phosphate was quantified as above. Data were fitted using GraphPad Prism v8.4.3, and all experiments were performed in at least triplicate.

Molecular docking

This study employed molecular docking rather than molecular dynamics simulations to predict the binding mode of ACT-451840 to hABCB1. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 software55. First, AutoDockTools 1.5.6 was used to prepare all input files. The hABCB1 structure (PDB code: 8GMG) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank. All protein water molecules and ions were deleted, and polar hydrogens were added to the system. The active site coordinates were predicted by the DeepSite web server using the DCNNs (deep convolutional neural network) machine learning method, resulting in a 40 Å box in all X, Y, and Z directions. The Lamarckian algorithm was used to identify the optimal binding mode for the ligand molecule, with an exhaustiveness setting of 8, a maximum number of output conformations of 10, and a maximum energy range of 3 kcal/mol. The resulting figures were processed using ChimeraX v.1.10.156.
