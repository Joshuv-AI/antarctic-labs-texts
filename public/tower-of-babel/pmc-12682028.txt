Economic pressures threaten surgical quality in Germany: immediate policy action required
Authors: Stephan Schorn, Helmut Friess, Ihsan Ekin Demir
Published: 2025

Economic pressures are rapidly reshaping hospital care in Germany, with profound implications for surgical quality and patient outcomes. Understaffing, accelerated outpatient expansion, and centralisation mandates, combined with inadequate reimbursement, are straining even the strongest hospital systems. Without timely policy adjustments, Germany's record of universal, high-quality surgical care is at risk.

Since the Federal Government's 2022 report on the expansion of outpatient services,1 economic pressure on hospitals has intensified. Two dynamics underpin this transformation. One is the ethical imperative to use scarce resources efficiently — essential amid workforce shortages and demographic change. The other is the drive to run hospitals as profitable enterprises. These aims are not inherently contradictory: efficiency is a moral necessity.2 But when profitability eclipses clinical priorities, patient safety and outcomes can be compromised.

The expansion of outpatient surgical care illustrates this tension. On paper, shifting procedures from inpatient to outpatient settings promises both medical and economic benefits. Germany performs more inpatient interventions — from cholecystectomies to cataract operations — than most OECD countries, suggesting substantial outpatient potential. Yet barriers are structural, not cultural. Many procedures remain outside the AOP catalogue and cannot be reimbursed when performed outpatient. Others generate significant losses under a remuneration system designed for private practices that fails to account for hospitals’ higher fixed costs.3

Hospitals also provide essential services far beyond those of ambulatory centres: 24/7 readiness, comprehensive diagnostics, and specialist training. These functions are vital to Germany's healthcare ecosystem yet remain uncompensated in current payment structures. Without reimbursement that reflects these broader mandates, hospitals cannot deliver outpatient services sustainably.3

Cross-sectoral remuneration — paying the same fee for certain procedures regardless of setting or length of stay — was intended to equalise incentives. In practice, it risks deepening financial strain. As simpler, profitable cases move to outpatient practices, hospitals are left with a greater share of complex, resource-intensive patients, eroding their economic base.4 This dynamic threatens the very capacity that underpins Germany's high-quality, universally accessible hospital care.

Centralisation of complex surgery is another key reform strategy. Minimum volume requirements already apply to organ transplants and will extend to additional procedures by 2027.4 Concentrating expertise can improve outcomes, but it also carries economic and operational risks. Highly complex procedures such as pancreatectomy are far less remunerative than routine operations.5 If hospitals must prioritise such cases without adequate compensation or expanded capacity, they risk overwhelming intensive care units and losing revenue from more viable services.

Centralisation also increases workload in remaining centres. Hospitals face a difficult choice: extend waiting times — unacceptable for patients with aggressive diseases — or discontinue economically viable procedures to free capacity for complex cases. Either option jeopardises care quality and institutional stability. Germany's leading centres, internationally recognised for their multidisciplinary expertise, could become overstretched unless reforms are accompanied by targeted investments in infrastructure and workforce.

Germany's healthcare system has long been recognised for its broad access, high surgical standards, and strong training capacity. Yet these achievements are increasingly under strain from workforce shortages, economic pressures, and accelerating structural reforms. Without timely and well-calibrated action, the system's leading position — built over decades of careful investment — may prove difficult to sustain.

So what are some concrete points of action? To align economic reform with clinical realities, three priorities are essential. First, policymakers should establish a dedicated funding mechanism compensating hospitals for continuous readiness, specialist training, and the management of complex or emergency cases — core public goods that are not reflected in case-based reimbursement. Second, reimbursement frameworks must be revised through joint negotiation between health insurers, professional societies, and hospital associations to ensure that payments reflect true procedural costs and the differing mandates of hospitals and ambulatory centres. Third, any reform of outpatient expansion or centralisation must be coupled with workforce investment — including protected training time, retention incentives, and targeted support for surgical nursing — to secure long-term capacity. Together, these measures would allow Germany to pursue efficiency without compromising quality, maintaining a system that remains both economically sustainable and clinically excellent.
