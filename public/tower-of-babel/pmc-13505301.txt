Microfluidic-assisted metal nanoparticle synthesis: emerging trends toward optical sensing applications
Authors: Trung Hieu Vu, Phuong Thy Nguyen, Ngoc Xuan Dat Mai, Ngoc Quang Tran, Thi Hoa Lai, Qiongzheng Hu, Nae Yoon Lee, Bach Thang Phan, Kieu The Loan Trinh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal nanoparticles have garnered significant attention due to their unique optical properties, which are highly advantageous in various sensing applications. While conventional batch synthesis methods often suffer from poor control over particle size and morphology, microfluidic synthesis has emerged as a powerful alternative, offering precise control over reaction parameters to synthesize monodisperse nanoparticles with tailored optical properties. This review highlights recent advances in microfluidic strategies for metal nanoparticle synthesis, emphasizing their potential in the development of high-performance optical sensors. A thorough overview of the key factors is also provided, including the materials used to fabricate microfluidic devices, different fabrication techniques, and different flow regimes required to achieve the desired nanoparticle properties. Furthermore, a perspective on future research aspects is discussed based on the current prospects and obstacles in translating laboratory-scale microfluidic synthesis into scalable, practical sensing platforms.

Introduction

Metal nanoparticles (MNPs) have attracted extensive interest due to their unique physicochemical properties, such as high surface-area-to-volume ratios, tunable optical and electronic characteristics, and exceptional catalytic behavior. The development of MNPs has seen rapid advancement along with a wide range of applications, including catalysis, drug delivery, environmental remediation, and particularly in optical sensing.1–4 Indeed, noble metal nanoparticles, such as gold (Au), silver (Ag), and platinum (Pt), exhibit localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) or surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), which enhances their sensitivity to environmental changes and making them ideal candidates for optical sensing applications. In addition, transition metal-based nanoparticles such as copper (Cu), iron (Fe), and cobalt (Co) also display exceptional catalytic activity and high electrical conductivity, which can be harnessed in applications ranging from chemical catalysis to nanoelectronics.5,6 Importantly, the large specific surface area of MNPs enables dense functionalization and efficient interaction with their environment, a critical feature for applications in drug delivery and sensing.7,8 These properties allow the precise detection of chemical, biological, and environmental analytes with high specificity and sensitivity.9,10

Interestingly, the performance of MNPs is inherently size- and shape-dependent. By precisely tuning parameters such as diameter, shape, and composition, their optical, catalytic, and electronic behaviors can be modulated to meet specific applications. This versatility is the basis for the importance of MNPs as supporting materials in modern nanotechnology and sensor design. However, conventional fabrication techniques often involve trial-and-error processes and require multiple iterative steps to achieve the desired size, uniformity, and functionality. This can cause the most formidable challenge with increased resource consumption, such as substantial time and cost expenditure.11,12 Therefore, advanced synthesis strategies that offer precise control over particle size and uniformity, while also reducing cost and synthesis time, are essential for optimizing functionality and expanding their use in increasingly specialized and performance-critical applications.

Conventional synthesis methods, such as chemical reduction, seed-mediated growth, and hydrothermal synthesis, are typically carried out in bulk-phase reactors.13 While these approaches enable the production of large quantities of material, they pose challenges in achieving precise control over nanocrystal size and uniformity. These batch processes often suffer from poor control over particle morphology and size distribution, resulting in batch-to-batch inconsistencies, inefficient use of reagents, and environmental concerns due to waste generation and solvent usage.14,15 For advanced applications, particularly in sensing, it is considered that efficient synthesis of uniformly sized nanocrystals is key factor, as their size directly influences their electrical, optical and magnetic properties. Indeed, the tunability of their wavelength response and optical/magnetic properties by size modulation is one of the clearest demonstrations of size-dependent material properties of nanocrystals.16 Therefore, limitations in size and structure control during material synthesis will severely hinder reproducibility and scalability, which are key requirements for developing practical sensors.

Microfluidic technology offers a transformative alternative to conventional synthesis methods by enabling precise manipulation of fluids at the microscale.17 Unlike traditional bulk-phase synthesis, microfluidic systems allow real-time control of flow rate, mixing time, reagent concentration, and temperature. Precise control of these parameters is beneficial for the continuous and reproducible synthesis of MNPs with uniform size distributions and tunable physicochemical properties.18 Additionally, microfluidic devices offer a cost-effective platform as they require minimal reagents and are suitable for continuous or high-throughput processing, which is an essential benefit for both laboratory-scale research and large-scale industrial applications.19,20 These capabilities not only enhance reproducibility but also allow the strategic tailoring of MNPs' properties to meet performance demands in advanced applications.21,22

Lab-on-a-chip (LOC) systems have revolutionized methodologies by seamlessly integrating nanoparticle synthesis, sample processing, and detection into a single, compact device.23 This integration not only shortens the analysis time and reduces the equipment footprint, but also improves the automation and sensitivity of the measurement.19,24,25 Due to these remarkable advantages, microfluidics technology is asserting its key role in MNPs' engineering, providing a sustainable platform for the production of highly homogeneous metal nanostructures, functional flexibility, and effectively meeting the increasingly stringent requirements of new generation optical sensing technology.26 As a result, the potential applications of microfluidics in point-of-measure environmental monitoring, rapid clinical trials, and personalized medicine are extremely open, especially as the demand for portable, low-cost, and high-performance sensors continues to grow.27,28

Several previous reviews have discussed microfluidic nanoparticle synthesis, chip design, or optical/plasmonic sensing; however, these topics have largely been treated independently rather than as interconnected components of a unified sensing platform.31,35,36 To clarify the scope of the present article, Table 1 compares representative reviews according to publication year, scope, major topics, and key limitations. As summarized in Table 1, previous reviews primarily emphasize microfluidic reactor design and mixing, general nanoparticle synthesis, chip fabrication, or plasmonic sensing strategies, with limited attention to how microfluidic platform design, device materials, and synthesis pathways collectively influence the properties of MNPs and ultimately determine optical sensing performance. In contrast, this review establishes an integrated framework linking microfluidic technology, device materials, metal nanoparticle synthesis, and optical sensing, with particular emphasis on Au- and Ag-based systems for LSPR, SERS, colorimetric, and related sensing modalities (Fig. 1). We further discuss how material selection, fabrication strategies, and flow dynamics govern nanoparticle size, morphology, composition, and reproducibility, thereby enabling precise control over sensing performance. Finally, this review critically examines unresolved challenges—including reproducibility, standardization, device-material compatibility, portable readout integration, and translational scalability—and outlines future research directions toward practical, scalable, and high-performance microfluidic-enabled optical sensing platforms.

Materials and fabrication of microfluidic devices for nanoparticle synthesis

Microfluidic systems have transformed the synthesis of nanoparticles by enabling precise control over reaction conditions, ensuring uniformity in particle size and morphology.37–39 The selection of materials for fabricating microfluidic systems is fundamental to their functionality and overall performance. Microfluidic devices are commonly constructed from silicon, glass, polymers, paper, and composites, each providing unique benefits in terms of optical transparency, chemical resistance, mechanical robustness, fabrication flexibility, and cost-effectiveness.32 The diversity of available materials allows microfluidic systems to be tailored for applications ranging from fundamental research to large-scale commercial deployment (Fig. 2). In the following sections, we critically examine the principal materials used in microfluidic device fabrication and discuss their specific implications for optical sensor performance.

Materials for microfluidic devices

Polydimethylsiloxane

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone-based elastomer, is widely regarded as the gold standard for prototyping microfluidic devices due to its ease of fabrication, excellent optical transparency, compatibility with soft lithography-based fabrication and cost-effectiveness. Its flexibility enables the precise fabrication of intricate microchannel geometries with sub-100 µm resolution, which is essential for controlling fluid dynamics in nanoparticle synthesis.43,44 The PDMS was sometimes mentioned for its gas permeability, however, it should be considered based on several conditions, like the thickness of the PDMS layer.45,46 The major weak point of PDMS is its susceptibility to swelling in organic solvents (e.g., toluene or hexane), which restricts its applicability in hydrophobic reaction systems.47,48 Moreover, clean rooms are needed to get PDMS devices ready for more sophisticated uses, which increases manufacturing costs because of the infrastructure needed.49,50 Other downsides of these include getting blocked by 2D flow shapes, changing shape under high pressure, and the unwanted sticking and movement of hydrophobic compounds.18,51 Indeed, a dealing with common polymers like PLGA that tends to aggregate on the walls and clog the channels becomes more difficult due to its ability to form hydrophobic bonds with PDMS.52 Although PDMS has its limitations, it continues to be widely used thanks to its excellent biocompatibility and transparency, which make it ideal for real-time optical monitoring, like observing plasmonic resonance shifts in gold nanoparticles.53,54 A notable example is PDMS-based droplet microfluidic systems, which are extensively employed for generating monodisperse gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), where precise size control is critical for reproducible surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) applications.55–58 In some cases, the PDMS could be incorporated with glass to construct the microfluidic reactor with further enhancement depending on the expected purpose of the device which will be discussed in the following paragraph.54

Glass

Glass is a prominent material in the fabrication of microfluidic devices, offering exceptional chemical durability, thermal stability, and optical transparency—attributes that are particularly advantageous for nanoparticle synthesis under rigorous conditions. Unlike polymer-based substrates such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), glass exhibits robust resistance to aggressive reagents, including chloroauric acid (HAuCl4) and nitric acid, facilitating continuous-flow synthesis of MNPs across a broad spectrum of pH levels and elevated temperatures.19,59 This resilience renders glass microreactors highly suitable for solvothermal processes, such as the synthesis of indium phosphide (InP) nanocrystal, which necessitate temperatures exceeding 320 °C and acidic environments.60,61 Moreover, the inherent optical clarity of glass supports in situ analytical techniques like UV-Vis spectroscopy, enabling real-time monitoring of nanoparticle growth kinetics—an essential factor in optimizing plasmonic properties for optical sensing applications.62–64 Furthermore, non-specialist laboratories may more easily access microfluidics technology since glass capillary microfluidic chips can be made by hand without the requirement for specialized equipment. However, to acquire completely working chips, the manufacture process is laborious and needs experience and strong hand skills.65

Hybrid configurations that integrate glass with PDMS combine the chemical robustness of glass with the fabrication versatility and elasticity of PDMS, enabling high-pressure synthesis of complex nanostructures. For instance, such systems have been successfully employed for the controlled formation of porous copper microparticles under harsh chemical conditions, which are particularly valuable in catalytic and plasmonic sensor applications.56 The strong bond between glass and PDMS in these hybrid devices also facilitates fluid sealing and pressure resistance while maintaining optical transparency for in situ monitoring techniques such as UV-Vis or Raman spectroscopy. Beyond PDMS, thermoplastics like polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) offer advantages including low cost, optical clarity, and ease of fabrication, making them suitable for microfluidic applications.57

Acrylic

Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), commonly known as acrylic, has gained popularity in microfluidic applications due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of fabrication. Its rapid prototyping capabilities through laser engraving or milling make it an attractive option for researchers and educators seeking to develop microfluidic devices quickly.66,67 Even though, the PMMA exhibits a narrower optical transmission window compared to glass or PDMS; however, it still provides sufficient transmittance within the visible spectrum (over 400 nm) for many microfluidic and optical detection applications.68 Despite its advantages, PMMA has limitations that must be considered when designing microfluidic systems. Its susceptibility to organic solvents, particularly acetone, restricts its use primarily to aqueous or polar solvent-based reactions.59,69 This constraint has led researchers to explore innovative applications that capitalize on PMMA's strengths while working within its limitations. For instance, recent studies have successfully employed acetone as one of the factors to increase the bonding strength between PMMA layers.70 In these cases, the leaking or speed of bonding issues were reduced, demonstrating the potential for PMMA in specialized microfluidic applications where cost-effectiveness and quick development cycles are paramount.71 In addition, PMMA with surface modification strategies, such as fluorosilanization, could create a covalent bond with biomolecules, which could alter to the complex methods for treating surface of microfluidic channels. By this way, PMMA's surface adaptability contributes to significantly enhanced biosensor sensitivity, reinforcing its value as a substrate in advanced diagnostic applications.67,72 Additionally, the integration of 3D-printed polymers and emerging biocompatible resins with glass enables rapid prototyping of complex geometries for nanomaterial synthesis.73,74

Other materials

Microfluidic devices could be crafted via several kinds of polymer such as thermoset polyester (TPE), thermoplastic polymers and the family of cyclic olefin polymer. These kinds of materials possess distinct characteristics which can increase the properties of microfluidic devices such as transparency and flexibility, thereby expanding the range of options for creating disposable chips. However, similar to PDMS and PMMA, these materials exhibit sensitivity to organic solvents, which diminishes their stability in optical sensors and synthesis processes.75,76

Noticeably, silicon is one of the oldest materials which can be used for crafting microfluidic devices with various manufacturing methods like etching, depositing.77,78 However, contrast to other materials, silicon is opaque and is quite sensitive with normal alkaline solution such as KOH,77,79 which make them unsuitable for use in nanomaterials synthesis or optical sensors. Additionally, paper is also used as a material for creating microfluidic devices with great potential due to its extremely low cost-effectiveness and biocompatibility. Nevertheless, in the manner of silicon, paper shows “fatal” weakness in particle deposition/recovery. Paper-based devices allow substances to passively absorb into the cellulose matrix and then the synthesis reactions take place on-site that might result in blockage or uneven particle size/distribution.19,80,81 Moreover, paper-based devices are not optically transparent and are not suitable to use with spectrometers which require further colorimetric analysis methods for result verification.82,83

The selection of microfluidic chip materials should be application-driven, as PDMS, glass, silicon, PMMA, and paper offer different balances of optical performance, chemical and thermal stability, fabrication complexity, and scalability (Table 2).34,84 PDMS is generally preferred for rapid prototyping, academic-scale nanoparticle synthesis, and flexible optofluidic systems requiring integrated valves or programmable flow control because of its transparency, biocompatibility, and low-cost fabrication,85,86 however, swelling, surface instability, and limited solvent compatibility restrict its use in some synthesis and sensing environments.34,86 Glass and silicon are more suitable for optically, chemically, or thermally demanding systems because of their low optical background, solvent resistance, and mechanical stability, although higher fabrication costs, rigidity, and limited optical access of silicon can favor hybrid architectures.34,84 PMMA and other thermoplastics offer greater potential for scalable and cost-effective manufacturing, making them attractive for disposable and translational devices, whereas paper is particularly suited to low-cost, portable, and equipment-light optical sensing, including colorimetric, fluorescence, and SERS-based point-of-care applications, although its limited control over pressure, temperature, and complex flow restricts its use as a nanoparticle synthesis reactor.34,87 Thus, material selection should be matched to the intended synthesis conditions and sensing format rather than based on a universally superior substrate.

Microfabrication techniques

The fabrication of microfluidic systems plays a key role in determining the flow control, mixing efficiency, and particle manipulation required for nanoparticle synthesis. In general, fabrication methods can be classified as nonadditive or additive-based manufacturing.69 In this paper, a different division can be used to highlight the trend in microfluidic system fabrication, particularly traditional to modern methods and large-scale strategies such as template-based molding or particle encapsulation (Fig. 3). Each method has its own advantages and limitations in terms of resolution, material compatibility, and geometric complexity. Therefore, the choice of fabrication technique is often determined by the application—whether it is high-resolution control for optical detection or high-volume throughput for nanoparticle production.

Traditional microfabrication methods, particularly photolithography combined with etching or molding, remain widely used methods for producing microchannels. Photolithography allows precise pattern transfer onto substrates such as silicon or glass, while subsequent etching (wet or dry) or soft lithography using PDMS allows these patterns to be replicated with micro-level precision.88,89 Hot embossing is commonly used for thermoplastics such as PMMA, providing good resolution with improved structure ratios.90,91 Although requiring cleanroom infrastructure and multiple processing steps, these methods provide excellent reproducibility and optical transparency, critical for in situ monitoring of nanoparticles using techniques such as UV-Vis or Raman spectroscopy.

In recent times, more advanced manufacturing techniques such as 2D wax printing,89,90,92 polymer93 or inkjet printing for 3D modeling provided flexible, fast and cost-effective routes to fabricate complex microfluidic architectures.94 For examples, 3D printing has advanced microfluidics by enabling the rapid fabrication of complex architectures using photopolymers and thermoplastics, with techniques like fused deposition modeling (FDM) achieving channel size from 100–800 µm, suitable for applications such as core–shell nanoparticle synthesis.95 Besides that, CNC-milled microfluidic platforms using materials like polycarbonate and glass composites are also gaining attention for their precision, durability, and scalability in chemically robust nanoparticle synthesis workflows.96 Although particularly beneficial in generating complex three-dimensional flow patterns to optimize the reaction environment, current 3D printing processes are still limited by the resolution and transparency of the materials, hampering applications needing nanoscale precision or optical sensing.97–99 Particularly, the surface roughness in FDM ranging from 3.24 to 42.9 µm, can cause particle aggregation and hinder their use in nanoscale or optically sensitive applications.100 However, advances in this technology are opening a promising direction for realizing complex microchip structures in the time of the “tsunami” in microchip development worldwide.101–103

Flow types in microfluidic synthesis

Microfluidic synthesis has emerged as a transformative technology for the controlled fabrication of nanoparticles, particularly for applications in optical sensors. The ability to manipulate fluid flow at the microscale enables precise control over reaction conditions, which is crucial for achieving desirable nanoparticle properties such as size, shape, and distribution. This literature review synthesizes findings from various studies focusing on different flow types in microfluidic systems, including continuous flow, segmented flow, droplet-based flow, coaxial flow, and multiphase flow. Each flow type presents unique advantages and challenges that impact the synthesis of nanoparticles for optical sensing applications (Table 3) (Fig. 4).

Continuous flow

Continuous flow microfluidic systems have been recognized for their ability to control reaction conditions precisely, which is critical for synthesizing MNPs with tailored properties. Marre and Marre (2010) emphasize that continuous flow allows for a higher degree of control over variables such as temperature and concentration, thereby expanding the synthesis space for complex engineered nanostructures.107 This capability is particularly beneficial for applications in optical sensors, where specific optical properties of nanoparticles are desired.

In a study by Meincke et al. (2013), a continuous flow approach was employed to produce gold nanoshells, showcasing the method's scalability and the ability to customize optical properties through controlled reagent mixing.108 These findings were further supported by similar results reported by Seifert et al. (2024), reinforcing the significance of continuous flow systems in the context of developing materials for optical sensing applications109

Discontinuous flow

Segmented flow

Segmented flow microfluidic systems present another promising avenue for nanoparticle synthesis, particularly for achieving narrow size distributions. Research by Cabeza et al. (2012) demonstrates that segmented flow can significantly reduce particle dispersion, which is essential for applications in optical sensors where uniformity in particle size is crucial.110 The study highlights the impact of flow dynamics on controlling particle growth, asserting that slip velocity and internal mixing play vital roles in the synthesis process. The findings suggest that segmented flow not only enhances the uniformity of nanoparticle size but also facilitates the controlled synthesis of gold nanoparticles, making it a valuable technique for applications in optical sensing.110 Such control over nanoparticle characteristics is essential for optimizing their performance in sensor applications.

Droplet-based flow

Droplet-based microfluidic technologies have gained attention for their ability to produce nanoparticles with precise size control while addressing challenges such as channel fouling and flow rate fluctuations. Lim et al. (2014) discuss the advantages of generating droplets of varying sizes, which allows for improved control over reaction conditions.38 This flexibility is critical for maintaining consistent nanoparticle properties, which is particularly relevant for optical sensors where uniformity can influence performance. The study highlights that droplet microfluidics can enhance synthesis by enabling researchers to tailor reaction environments, thereby achieving nanoparticles with specific optical characteristics. This aligns with the review's focus on the significance of droplet-based flow in the context of optical sensing applications.

Coaxial flow

While the literature provides extensive insights into continuous, segmented, and droplet-based flows, coaxial flow remains underexplored in the context of nanoparticle synthesis. By tuning the flow velocity or channel size ratio between the inner and outer streams, mixing can be achieved through a controlled diffusion mechanism. The precursor is introduced through the inner stream, which helps prevent nanoparticle contact with the channel walls and thus reduces fouling. This approach is well-suited for fast nucleation synthesis, as mixing time can be minimized by tuning flow rates. Baber et al. (2017) present a detailed study of flow-based strategies for nanoparticle synthesis, focusing on the use of a coaxial flow reactor (CFR).111 In this work, gold and silver nanoparticles were synthesized within a CFR system, demonstrating its potential for controlled and efficient nanoparticle production. Future research should investigate the capabilities of coaxial flow in producing nanoparticles with complex architectures suitable for optical sensors.

Building on the advantages of coaxial flow systems, where controlled diffusion and minimized fouling enable precise nanoparticle synthesis, multiphase flow reactors offer an additional level of complexity and tunability. Multiphase flow systems, which involve the interaction of multiple phases (such as gas, liquid, or solid), have shown promise in synthesizing intermetallic nanoparticles. He et al. (2013) highlights the rapid and controlled synthesis potential of multiphase reactors, emphasizing their relevance for applications in catalysis and sensing.112 The ability to fine–tune reaction conditions in multiphase systems could yield nanoparticles with enhanced optical properties, furthering their utility in sensor applications.

Critical comparison and operational trade-offs

Choosing an ideal microfluidic system for the synthesis of MNPs necessitates careful consideration of fluid transport dynamics, material suitability, and operational intricacy (Table 4). Passive and active micro-mixers improve chaotic advection for sub-millisecond mixing and fast supersaturation and reproducible nucleation;113,114 however, these complex geometries elevate dynamic pressure drops, fabrication difficulty, and channel clogging risks. For thermal and reaction control, high surface-to-volume planar microreactors enable rapid heat and mass transport,115,116 while segmented droplet flows eliminate Taylor–Aris dispersion to yield narrow residence-time distributions.117,118 Nevertheless, droplet systems require continuous oil phases and surfactants that can contaminate nanoparticle surfaces and compromise downstream SERS and LSPR optical sensing performance.119–121

The stability of operation depends also on fluid dynamics and materials selection. Multi-directional hydrodynamic focusing (HF) moves precursor streams away from the channel boundaries, to avoid fouling,122,123 but creates sensitivity to pump fluctuations and non-linear flow behavior.31 From a material perspective, glass and quartz microchips provide superior solvent resistance, mechanical stability, and zero swelling compared to PDMS, albeit at higher fabrication costs and geometric rigidity.19,124 Finally, scaling up via parallelized channel arrays and 3D-printed modular architectures increases volumetric throughput,31,125 though fluid manifold imbalances can induce channel-to-channel variations in nanoparticle size and plasmonic reproducibility.19

Microfluidic-based MNPs synthesis

Several typical MNPs synthesized in microfluidics that are widely used in optical sensing will be described. The three main categories, single metal nanoparticles, hybrid metal nanoparticles, and metal nanoparticle–hydrogel composites will be discussed. The synthesis, structural characteristics, properties, and potential applications will be examined in detail.

Single metal nanoparticles

MNPs, especially noble MNPs such as Au and Ag, show special properties suitable for applications in many fields.126–129 Typically, the sensitivity of plasmon frequency to the dielectric conditions of the environment is one of the outstanding properties that creates strong development of optical sensors based on SPR/LSPR.130 Distribution, size, and morphology are important factors influencing the properties of plasmonic materials.131 Therefore, problems in ensuring the stability of the nanoparticles or their coatings are one of the barriers to the development make commercial products.

The trend of microfluidic-based synthesis of MNPs is becoming increasingly popular and highly regarded by researchers.132 Based on the properties of fluids that change when transitioning from a macroscopic to a microscopic state, because in microscopic space, precursors mix primarily through diffusion, allowing for precise control of reaction rates and synthesis stages. This process is currently implemented through two main systems: continuous flow devices and segmented flow devices. Lucas et al., relied on 3D printing technology using fused deposition modeling to create the microfluidic device to synthesize silver and nanoparticles.133 Poly(lactic acid) is used as the printing part on poly(methyl methacrylate) substrate with a design consisting of two channels to lead the reactant into the chamber and one channel to lead mineral oil with the function of creating a segmented flow, which helps the reactant process to take place better, avoiding agglomeration and adhesion of materials to the channel wall during the synthesis process. This study highlights the ease and precise control over the morphology and size of the nanoparticles by adjusting the flow rate and concentration of the reactants. However, the challenge of completely removing mineral oil reduces product purity and hinders the closed-loop automation process. Nevertheless, liquid phase removal in microfluidic systems remains preferred due to its ability to precisely control two core stages: crystal nucleation upon reaching critical saturation and nanoparticle growth (MNP) from the pre-existing nuclei.134 Therefore, the mixing rate of the reactants is one of the critical factors in ensuring the homogeneity of the morphology and structure of the material.135 To optimize the synthesis of nanomaterials in microfluidic, Yang et al. designed a multi-stage microfluidic reactor using two valveless piezoelectric pumps to improve the pump efficiency by inducing a potential difference and increase the mixing efficiency through the formation of a vortex electric field in the reaction chamber.136 The PMMA reactor was fabricated using CNC and hot-pressing technology, as described in Fig. 5a, from two PMMA sheets measuring 34 × 32 × 4, suitable for the use of a three-stage mixing mechanism: molecular diffusion, turbulent convection (via piezoelectric vibrator), and pulse mixing (from the square wave phase shift of the pump). This system allows for precise control of the size of Ag NPs, while the micro-droplet method helps to coordinate local nucleation concentrations for the successful synthesis of highly dispersed anisotropic gold nano-stars. TEM results and size distribution plots confirmed the superior effectiveness of combining these mixing techniques in controlling nanomaterial morphology. Abalde-Cela et al. proposed a method for synthesizing anisotropic gold nanoparticles with well-controlled morphology and size. The microdroplets were used to control the mixing of the reactants and to locally control the concentration of the nucleation process inside the droplets (Fig. 5b). This method successfully produced stable and highly dispersed gold nanostars, demonstrating the high application potential of nanomaterial fabrication technology in microfluidic channels.119 This method successfully produced stable and highly monodisperse gold nanostars, demonstrating the high application potential of nanomaterial fabrication technology in microfluidic channels.

The in situ synthesis of MNPs on paper microfluidic systems is attracting significant attention in the field of Point-of-Care Testing (POCT) sensors due to its simple process, high integration capabilities, and low cost.137 Instead of using pumps, capillary action in cellulose/nitrocellulose fibers is utilized to conduct the reactants, enabling Hu et al. to successfully synthesize and immobilize AgNPs for rapid mercury detection via mobile phones.138 Ag NPs were formed in one step from the redox reaction between AgNO3 and NaBH4 and ranging in size from a few to tens of nanometers, stabilized and uniformly distributed by the cellulose network without the need for additional stabilizers (Fig. 5c). With its outstanding application, the number of publications on in situ synthesis of MNPs on paper is constantly increasing in recent years.139,140 However, this method also encounters many limitations, the density of cellulose fibers on paper is poorly uniform, significantly affecting the material's homogeneity. Next, it is tough to control the material's morphology and structure because the solution's conduction in the device is mainly based on capillaries, making it difficult to interfere with the reactants' mixing process.

Hybrids metal nanoparticles

Hybrid plasmonic nanomaterials are becoming a mainstream trend in optical sensor fabrication due to their superior physical properties and flexible integration capabilities compared to single-component materials. Since plasmon frequency and optical resonance sites are strongly influenced by density, morphology, and interparticle interactions, ensuring the homogeneity and stability of the material layer is a prerequisite for optimizing sensor sensitivity. Microfluidic technology addresses this challenge by creating a reaction environment with flexible kinetic control, allowing for the addition of precursors in stages through separate microchannels in a continuous process. Typical of this approach, Fuqi et al. implemented a sophisticated fabrication process: using soft lithography to create a silicon mold, then casting polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomer to form the microchannel structure. To complete the device, the PDMS channel surface and glass substrate were treated with oxygen plasma to activate functional groups, facilitating the formation of covalent –O– (siloxane bonds), establishing a stable, tightly closed system for complex plasmonic synthesis reactions.141 The microfluidic channel, with a total size of 28 × 10 mm, utilizing a channel structure that is 300 µm wide and 130 µm high with a four-inlet and one-outlet design. The synthesis process begins with mixing AuNPs and CTAC, which is then fed into an S-shaped channel for contact with the reducing agent suspension (CTAC, AA) and AgNO3 salt. The narrow channel space combined with a controlled pumping rate helps to slow the reduction reaction, maintaining the morphological and size stability of the material, while the micro-tube system wrapped around the heating cup optimizes thermal efficiency due to its large specific surface area (Fig. 6a). Before practical implementation, COMSOL simulations were used to determine the optimal flow and concentration parameters (Fig. 6b).

Based on this principle, Knauer et al. developed a system for synthesizing Au@Ag core–shell structures (Fig. 6c), combining the advantages of microfluidic technology and automation to thoroughly overcome the inherent homogeneity and size control limitations of traditional wet chemical methods.142 The combined adjustment of 2 parameters, mixing speed according to the convection mechanism described in Fig. 6d and Ag ion concentration, allows for the precise adjustment of the thickness of the Au shell layer. The automatically set flow rate cycle (Fig. 6e) allows the system to operate continuously and accurately at different flow rates at different steps. The system was further developed and successfully fabricated and controlled many other morphological structures such as Au core – Ag shell structures,142 Ag nanoprisms,143 MOF,144etc.

Similarly to the synthesis of single metal materials, paper microfluidic chips demonstrate superior advantages in controlling the structure of hybrid materials, exemplified by the study of Hou et al. on the in situ synthesis of copper metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) by solvothermal method.145 By modifying the paper surface with chloroacetate salt to create carboxymethylated cellulose, Cu2+ ions are effectively anchored through carboxyl functional groups. Under suitable temperature and pressure conditions, the reaction between copper nitrate trihydrate and 2,4,6-tris(4-carboxyphenyl)-1,3,5-triazine forms a stable fixed MOF structure on the cellulose matrix (Fig. 6f). The effectiveness of this method was demonstrated by FESEM images (Fig. 6g and h). The Cu-TATB NPs showed high density and relatively uniform coverage in the cellulose matrix, much more than the traditional drop-casting method.

Metal nanoparticle–hydrogel composites

Noble MNPs such as Au, Ag, and Pd have been shown to have great potential for application in many fields, especially in sensors, thanks to their plasmonic properties that are sensitive to the conditions of the dielectric environment. However, stability and aggregation properties are some of the barriers to applying this group of materials. Many studies have shown that hydrogels encapsulate MNPs and improve the properties of metal nanomaterials. The interaction between these two types of materials creates many exciting features that make them more versatile in applications than single-component materials. For example, the metal nanoparticle's light-sensitive properties combined with the hydrogels' mechanical anisotropy show many applications in colorimetric-based sensors.147,148 On the contrary, the sensitive change of the structure of the hydrogel to environmental conditions leads to the modification of the optical and electronic properties of the MNPs inside it. In addition, the porous structure and homogeneous hydrogel system help to arrange, shape, and stabilize the MNPs even at high density. Metal nanoparticle–hydrogel composites are synthesized in two main directions: directly mixing MNPs and hydrogels and forming bonds through the cross-linking method, the swelling and shrinking method (ex situ), and mixing precursors, usually metal nano-salts, into hydrogels or hydrogel precursors via physical agents (heat, irradiation, UV light…) and chemical reagents. In situ showed more advantages in uniformly encapsulating MNPs in the hydrogel matrix, limiting the agglomeration of MNPs at high concentrations. Typical, Kim et al. proposed a synthesis process for high-quality gold nanoparticles uniformly dispersed in a hydrogel matrix using a fractional flow microfluidic device.149 The microfluidic was designed in a Y-shape, and gold precursor of gold(iii) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), a hydrogel precursor of poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA), and a photoinitiator were injected into the channel through one Y-shaped arm and a hydrogel precursor of poly(ethylene glycol)diacrylate (PEGDA), a reducing agent of sodium citrate was injected into the other arm. The mixture was diffusely mixed before being emulsified into mineral oil containing a surfactant. Separating precursors into small reaction chambers facilitates control of the shape and size of the material due to well-controlled heat transfer and reduces the dispersion of MNPs. At the same time, fractionation helps control reaction time and reduces agglomeration that causes channel blockage. The mixture was irradiated with UV light to induce photolysis of the photo initiator and form the hydrogel matrix, with the gold nanoparticles suspended inside the hydrogel matrix. Finally, a thermal annealing process was required to promote the growth of nanoparticles and increase the crystallinity of the MNPs.

Overall, the evolution from single-metal nanoparticles to complex hybrid structures and hydrogel composite systems reflects the trend toward materials optimization in the nanoscale era (Table 5). While single-component materials focus on exploiting original plasmonic properties, hybrid and hydrogel systems offer the potential for “self-adaptation” to the environment and superior mechanical strength. To achieve highly monodispersed nanoarchitectures and specialized surface functionalization, each of these systems needs a unique microfluidic control strategy, ranging from straightforward diffusion control to fractional flow coordination and mixing cycle automation, in order to satisfy the demanding specifications of the smart sensing and in situ diagnostics field.

SERS sensors

SERS is one of the methods that is considered an effective tool in mobile biomarker detection applications. It has advantages of high sensitivity, fast response, minimized size, simple sample preparation process, etc. The strategies for fabricating SERS sensors integrated into microfluidic channels help expand the application fields of SERS, such as detecting biomolecules, bacteria, cell analysis, and toxic substances. In other words, the microfluidic SERS chip (SERS-LoC) is one of the major advances of SERS, combining the strengths of the microfluidic chip such as low sample consumption, short reaction time, high detection efficiency, and portability with SERS technology to create a portable device that quickly detects targets with high precision and selectivity. In addition, SERS-LoC is also a solution to help SERS increase reusability and safety. SERS substrates play an essential role in SERS-LoC platforms. Colloidal substrates and stationary substrates are the two main types of SERS substrates, of which stationary substrates receive more attention due to their good stability and reproducibility. Fig. 7a shows the structure of the SERS sensor integrated on a droplet-based microfluidic chip. The SERS signal will be recorded at the contact point of the silver colloid solution and the analyte through flow control. This is a steppingstone in developing automated, integrated testing systems to ensure accuracy and minimize operating errors. Hotspots, where the Raman signal is best enhanced, generated from the surface of single nanoparticles (spherical nanoparticles, rod-shaped nanoparticles, star nanoparticles), nanoparticle assemblies (dime, trimer…), or developing plasmonic nanostructures (nano-pillar arrays, SHINERS arrays, and nanosphere lithography (NSL) arrays) thanks to the characteristic LSPR effect of this group of materials. Colloidal substrates are widely used and more popular due to their simplicity in the fabrication process, low cost, and high productivity; Zhang et al. presented a method for detecting different phenotypes of breast cancer using Raman reporter-embedded gold@silver core–shell nanoparticles (Au@Ag REPs) as a sensor probe integrated into a microfluidic chip.150 The Au@Ag REPs, before being inserted into the microchip, were modified with DNA aptamers to help identify modifications of proteins on the cell surface. Various metal nanostructures such as Au@Ag and Au@Pb have been incorporated into microfluidic chips as a way to improve and overcome the disadvantages of single material particles. However, the lack of uniformity in morphology, size, and dispersion reduces the accuracy of SERS signals in quantification. Nanomaterials synthesized on microfluidic devices are an effective alternative to optimize the process and ensure material uniformity. Adamo and co-workers synthesized AuNPs using the Turkevich method in a microfluidic structure using the cotton thread as a physical support for material formation (Fig. 7b). Besides, problems associated with the accumulation of MNPs leading to microfluidic chip clogging, instability, and poor reproducibility have been mitigated to some extent in colloidal substrates by developing shell-isolated materials. However, they are still not wholly overcome for colloidal substrates. Huang et al. fabricated a SERS-LoC sensor chip for ultrasensitive detection of sulfadiazine in aquatic samples.151 With the advantage of a simple fabrication process, a copper foil was bonded to a PDMS mold after both were treated with oxygen plasma to form a microfluidic chip. Based on the displacement reaction between copper and silver nitrate to create a SERS substrate for the SERS-LoC sensor, it only took 4 minutes for the whole process at room temperature. Ag NPs were directly formed and immobilized on the copper foil to create highly stable hot spots. In addition, this process also helps to limit the signal interference caused by surface stabilizers compared to conventional wet chemical synthesis methods. However, it is difficult to control the morphology and distribution of Ag NPs in the liquid chip, which significantly influences the sensitivity of the SERS sensor. Another strategy for incorporating SERS in a microfluidic chip was reported by Liu et al. for rapid enrichment and ultrasensitive SERS detection of bacteria.152 The SERS substrates, including ZnO nanorods array and Ag NPs, were both synthesized separately outside before being introduced into the microfluidic chip. ZnO nanoflower arrays with bacterial trapping ability were used for sample enrichment when the analyte solution was pumped through the sensor chip. After all, accuracy is still a key factor of the sensor, the decoration of Ag NPs on the branches of ZnO nanoflowers faces many difficulties in controlling the distribution and density of Ag NPs. In another study, Xie et al. established an optical system that allows the in situ fabrication of Ag@ZnO nanostructures in a microfluidic channel with higher Ag uniformity and density based on the photothermal effect (Fig. 7c).153 The Ag positions on the ZnO rods can be controlled by controlling the laser spot area. FDTD simulations (Fig. 7d) demonstrate that the enhanced electromagnetic fields are more effective at points with high Ag NPs density and at the gaps of the Ag@ZnO nanostructures. This is convincing evidence demonstrating the importance of material structure to SERS performance. Many studies have addressed the homogeneity issues of SERS substrates to ensure accuracy and reproducibility by creating immobilized substrates through micro-electro-mechanical methods, electron beam lithography,154,155 decoration of noble nanoparticles on nanoconcavity arrays,156 and focused ion beam techniques.157 Ahmed et al. employed electron beam lithography technique produced sequential gold nanoarrays on fused silica with high uniformity in particle size.158 The SERS Au arrays showed good sensitivity to interleukin protein after surface modification with thiolated DNA aptamers suggesting potential applications in biosensor chips. However, there are many obstacles in the fabrication process of material layers on dielectric substrates due to the accumulation of charge; on the other hand, the devices require complex technology, and the cost of sample fabrication is a big challenge in developing practical applications of these SERS substrates. Although SERS-LoC has been demonstrated to be a promising analytical tool, its application in place of existing systems still requires systematic research. The preparation of highly stable SERS substrates requires advanced technology and is costly. Therefore, research on improving the fabrication process is one of the factors that need to be considered in which the synthesis of SERS materials based on microfluidic chips is a promising solution.

SPR sensors

MNPs and their complexes play an essential role in the development of optical sensors. With their unique optical properties that can be tuned by various parameters of MNPs, SPR/LSPR optical sensors based on MNPs have been developed and integrated into various devices. Many strategies have been deployed in the fabrication of metallic nanomaterials to optimize their properties and control their optical properties. Among them, the synthesis of nanomaterials in microfluidic chips has received much research attention thanks to the ability to easily integrate into devices, small size, and low sample volume.160 With the ability to effectively control the flow and properties of liquids, microfluidic channels can control the size, structure, and morphology well, which significantly affects the properties of plasmonic materials. Microfluidic chips with the ability to synthesize diverse morphologies and structures of plasmonic materials such as Ag NPs,161 Au NRs,162 Ag–Au core–shell structures,163 Ag–MOFs, ZnO NFs/Ag with controlled ratio, morphology, size, and density properties show great potential to become potential candidates provide high-quality materials for the development of SPR/LSPR sensors. The primary mechanism of SPR/LSPR sensors is mainly based on the shift of the plasmon frequency spectrum (corresponding to the absorption wavelength) due to the dielectric constant change.164Fig. 8a shows the sensor structure and the influence of material layers on the refractive index of the LSPR sensor. A finite element method numerical simulation is used to describe the relationship between the geometric structure of the sensor and the LSPR resonance modes. Prism sensors with sensing mechanisms based on the reflectivity of different incident angles have the potential to be integrated into microfluidic devices (Fig. 8b) for biochemical assays. In addition, many studies have shown that the aspect ratio, size, and interaction between nanoparticles significantly affect the refractive index sensitivity of the sensor (Fig. 8c).165–167 Therefore, optimizing the sensor material is the key to developing the sensitivity of LSPR sensors. Ma et al. conducted an investigation to evaluate the influence of the morphology of gold nanoparticles on the refractive index sensitivity of the medium caused by changes in glucose concentration.168 A microfluidic platform was created that allowed the reaction parameters to be varied sequentially to find the optimal conditions for the material. In this way, the fabrication and investigation process were shortened, minimizing the subjective conditions that affect the properties of the resulting material compared to batch synthesis. The UV-Vis spectra of the samples synthesized on-chip and off-chip (Fig. 5a) demonstrate a clear improvement in the stability of this method. Thiele et al. presented a novel synthesis model for cube-shaped gold nanoparticles using a microfluidic channel instead of the previous batch synthesis.169 The microfluidic channel allowed for tight control of the three-step formation of cube-shaped gold nanoparticles, which was difficult due to the six close-packed 100-planes. The highly homogenously controlled cube-shaped gold nanoparticles showed approximately 2-fold higher refractive index sensitivity than spherical gold nanoparticles (from 104 nm per RIU to 202 nm per RIU). The microfluidic synthesis method indicated that cube-shaped gold particles could be well controlled, producing smaller and more uniform particle sizes than batch synthesis, and the reaction and incubation times could be better controlled. Channel clogging is one of the challenges of this synthesis method; however, this can be overcome by using suitable surface stabilizers. Synthesis of materials by microfluidic channels offers many improvements in the control of material properties, which offers great potential for applications in SPR/LSPR sensors which have high demands on the quality of the sensing materials. Because accuracy and uniformity are among the barriers in the commercialization of SPR/LSPR sensors, with material selection largely determining the sensor's overall performance.

Other optical sensors

The technique of fabricating MNPs in microfluidic chips has been proven to have many advantages in improving material properties, simplifying the process, and opening up many directions for development to improve the properties of many types of optical sensors. Colorimetric sensors based on precious MNPs with the advantages of simple mechanisms, low manufacturing cost, and instantaneousness are an aspect that is receiving much attention. However, sensitivity, stability, and reproducibility are still tricky problems that hinder the breakthrough development of this sensor technology. The color change in the colorimetric sensor is mainly based on the change in the plasmonic properties of the MNPs. This is manifested through the shift or change in intensity of the absorption spectrum of the material and is recognized through the color change. The changes in the state of aggregation and dispersion and the size caused by the target analyte are common causes of color change in colorimetric sensors. Meanwhile, the agglomeration properties of MNPs cause many errors in color reading. In addition, the uniformity of MNPs in terms of morphology and size is also an essential factor in ensuring the sensitivity and accuracy of the sensor. A specific absorption wavelength characterizes each size and shape of nanoparticles. Monochromatic color shifts are more easily detected with simple instruments, even with the naked eyes, than with polychromatic solutions due to the overlapping absorption spectra of different wavelengths. Paper microfluidic is an effective strategy for developing colorimetric sensors that integrate material fabrication and color detection in one step. The cellulose circuit in the paper matrix is a practical support and stabilizer for nanoparticles. Hu et al. used in situ silver nanoparticles synthesized on paper to detect Hg2+ at low concentrations based on the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of Ag NPs.138 This is one of the advances in colorimetric sensors when the specificity and sensitivity are optimized to 0.44 nM while still ensuring the simplicity and convenience of the colorimetric method. Another study to develop biochemical sensors based on the plasmonic properties of AuNPs as an alternative to conventional enzyme-based sensors was developed by Pinheiro et al.172 A simple glucose sensor was fabricated based on the color signal of Au NPs formed by the reduction reaction of Au3+ and glucose. A fixed amount of gold salt was added to the holder on the test kit in the presence of NaOH; under the presence of different glucose concentrations, Au NPs of different sizes were formed, and this was characterized by color with reported sensitivity results comparable to colorimetric sensors using similar enzymes. In addition, MNPs synthesized based on microfluidic chips also have great potential for application in developing fluorescent probes. Typically, Ortiz-Gómez and co-workers developed a fluorescent probe from silicon nanodots synthesized on a microfluidic chip based on the reduction reaction. The silicon nanodots with diameters ranging from 2–6 nm and high uniformity allowed the detection of low levels of glucose and fructose with LODs of 0.8 and 0.51 µM, respectively.173 In another approach, Nahid Pourreza et al. presented a strategy for the in situ synthesis of green Ag NPs on bacterial cellulose nano paper by a reduction method based on the decrease in the intensity of the cyanide ion-sensing plasmon peak with a detection limit of 0.012 µg mL−1.161 These results may be beneficial for simplifying and increasing the accuracy in expanding studies of integrated microfluidic composites with optical sensor chips.

Compared with the extensive discussion of nanoparticle synthesis, the analytical performance of the resulting optical sensors also requires critical evaluation. SERS provides the highest sensitivity because plasmonic nanostructures amplify weak Raman scattering through localized electromagnetic hot spots, enabling molecular fingerprinting and multiplex detection, although performance depends strongly on nanoparticle geometry, interparticle spacing, and substrate reproducibility.33,175 LSPR sensing, in contrast, is primarily based on refractive-index changes near metallic nanoparticles and is attractive for rapid, label-free detection, but it generally offers lower chemical specificity unless combined with selective surface ligands or receptors.176 Fluorescence-based sensors are widely used because of their operational simplicity, high sensitivity, and compatibility with real-time detection; notably, ratiometric designs improve reproducibility and reliability through internal calibration.177 Colorimetric sensors remain the simplest and most portable format, especially for paper-based and point-of-care assays, but their sensitivity is often lower unless enhanced by dual-mode or nanoparticle-engineered readout strategies.175 In general, no single sensing mode is universally optimal. SERS as best suited for ultratrace and multiplex analysis, LSPR for rapid label-free monitoring, fluorescence for sensitive and user-friendly quantification, and colorimetry for low-cost visual screening. Therefore, the optimal platform depends on the required balance among sensitivity, selectivity, response speed, reproducibility, stability, and practical application context.33,178

Barriers to translation and commercialization

High-throughput translation of microfluidic platforms remains fundamentally constrained by low single-channel flow rates, where merely increasing channel dimensions disrupts heat/mass transport kinetics and hydrodynamic control.179 Operational longevity is further hindered by channel fouling, clogging, high fabrication costs, and flow distribution mismatches.180 Numbering-up via optimized hydraulic manifolds offers the most viable pathway to scale volumetric yield (up to liters per hour scales) while maintaining monodispersity and encapsulation efficiency.181 However, commercial translation requires addressing Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) hurdles, integrating in-line characterization for real-time process control,182,183 ensuring compliance with GMP standards,184 and satisfying regulatory frameworks for batch-to-batch reproducibility across disposable cartridges.185

Microfluidic technology plays a crucial role in optical sensing by enabling precise control over nanoparticle properties and facilitating their integration into compact sensing platforms (Table 6). However, translating these systems beyond laboratory-scale demonstrations requires further advances in device simplification, user-friendliness, multifunctionality, and optical integration. Current approaches include off-chip coupling of optical components, such as optical fibers or pinholes, and more complex on-chip integration of light sources, detectors, and micro-optical elements. Although these strategies can improve portability and system integration, miniaturization reduces the detection volume and may limit the number of active sensing sites, thereby affecting sensitivity and overall performance. Consequently, successful commercialization requires not only reproducible nanoparticle synthesis and scalable manufacturing but also robust integration of sensing materials, optical components, and readout systems into reliable and manufacturable device architectures.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Microfluidics enables precise control of nanoparticle properties, addressing key limitations of conventional synthesis methods such as poor size uniformity and reagent efficiency, which hinder reproducibility and scalability in optical sensing applications. Because of this, microfluidics is an attractive alternative, providing a closed and highly controllable environment where reaction parameters, such as mixing time, concentration gradients and flow rates, can be precisely adjusted in real time. This results in exceptional control over nanoparticle size, morphology and monodispersity, while minimizing batch-to-batch variability. Additionally, the small reaction volumes and closed channels reduce the risk of cross-contamination and allow for safer handling of hazardous or bioactive reagents. However, widespread adoption is currently limited by challenges in device design, integration with real-time analysis, and scaling to industrial levels. Challenges to overcome in the development of microfluidic systems still face several key issues: (1) clogging and fouling: Highly viscous nanoparticles or reagents are prone to agglomeration, clogging narrow channels. This problem is particularly serious with hydrophobic materials such as PLGA or MNPs with high surface reactivity. (2) Limitations in device fabrication: The fabrication process of microfluidic chips (using PDMS, glass, or thermoplastics) is often expensive, time-consuming, and requires a clean room environment. In addition, some materials such as PDMS are not resistant to organic solvents or have absorption problems. (3) Limited material compatibility: Conventional microfluidic materials do not tolerate high temperatures, harsh solvents, or reactive chemicals, which hinders the synthesis of certain types of unique nanomaterials.

Furthermore, to fully exploit the potential of microfluidic nanoparticle synthesis, future research must address these limitations through interdisciplinary innovation – combining expertise in microengineering, materials chemistry, and point-of-care diagnostics. These efforts will be essential to evolve microfluidic systems from experimental tools to robust platforms capable of producing customized nanomaterials for a variety of applications.

Moreover, the convergence of artificial intelligence, automated microfluidics, and real-time analytical feedback will likely drive future progress in microfluidic nanoparticle synthesis and optical sensing. Machine-learning-guided and self-driving microfluidic platforms are already demonstrating the ability to optimize nanoparticle synthesis, predict structure–property relationships, and adjust reaction parameters in closed-loop operation.186,187 In parallel, the integration of optical monitoring, digital microfluidics, and wearable or point-of-care formats is expected to expand practical utility beyond laboratory settings toward decentralized and personalized sensing applications.188,189

Beyond conventional MNPs, emerging optical nanomaterials such as semiconductor quantum dots, carbon dots, and coinage-metal nanoclusters are increasingly being combined with microfluidic platforms for sensing, imaging, and diagnostics.190–193 These materials offer complementary advantages, including size-tunable emission, high photostability, and long luminescence lifetimes, that can enrich the analytical capabilities of metal-nanoparticle-based systems. Positioning microfluidic metal-nanoparticle sensors within this broader landscape of advanced optical nanomaterials will further enhance the interdisciplinary relevance and translational potential of the field. Ultimately, successful translation will still depend on overcoming remaining challenges in throughput, device robustness, standardization, quality control, and regulatory validation.

Ultimately, microfluidics will eventually be positioned as a key technology in the creation of high-performance, scalable, and application-specific nanomaterials, especially for optical sensors used in smart manufacturing, environmental monitoring, and diagnostics.
