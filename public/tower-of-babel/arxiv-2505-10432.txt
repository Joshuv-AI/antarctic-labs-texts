Score-based diffusion nowcasting of GOES imagery


Abstract
Clouds and precipitation are important for understanding weather and climate. Simulating clouds and precipitation with traditional numerical weather prediction is challenging because of the sub-grid parameterizations required. Machine learning has been explored for forecasting clouds and precipitation, but early machine learning methods often created blurry forecasts. In this paper we explore a newer method, named score-based diffusion, to nowcast (zero to three hour forecast) clouds and precipitation. We discuss the background and intuition of score-based diffusion models - thus providing a starting point for the community - while exploring the methodology’s use for nowcasting geostationary infrared imagery. We experiment with three main types of diffusion models: a standard score-based diffusion model (Diff); a residual correction diffusion model (CorrDiff); and a latent diffusion model (LDM). Our results show that the diffusion models are able to not only advect existing clouds, but also generate and decay clouds, including convective initiation. These results are surprising because the forecasts are initiated with only the past 20 mins of infrared satellite imagery. A case study qualitatively shows the preservation of high resolution features longer into the forecast than a conventional mean-squared error trained U-Net. The best of the three diffusion models tested was the CorrDiff approach, outperforming all other diffusion models, the traditional U-Net, and a persistence forecast by one to two kelvin on root mean squared error. The diffusion models also enable out-of-the-box ensemble generation, which shows skillful calibration, with the spread of the ensemble correlating well to the error.

Knowing where clouds and precipitation will be is important for everything from weather forecasts to telecommunications. Unfortunately, simulating clouds and precipitation is a challenge for physics-based approaches. Machine learning methodology can help, but older methodologies often result in blurry forecasts. In this paper, we explore a newer machine learning method from the computer science literature, named diffusion, which enables clearer and more realistic looking forecasts. 2.2
Basic idea of Diffusion models

Figure 1:
Conceptual overview of diffusion model approach for unconditional image generation with stochastic differential equations, using Equations (

Generally, diffusion models can be thought of as tools to learn, and later sample from, data distributions. A diffusion model is trained on a large set of representative samples of the distribution, typically images. For example, if a diffusion model is trained on an extensive set of sample images from a specific satellite channel, the diffusion model learns to approximate this distribution. Once trained the diffusion model can provide new samples from the distribution, i.e., it generates new (approximate) satellite imagery for that channel. This type of random but realistic image generation is called
unconditional
image generation. Although such ability is useful for many applications, we are more interested here in the ability to generate specific imagery, known as
conditional
image generation. Conditional image generation would allow us, for example, to use information from a different channel of imagery or to forecast satellite imagery 10 minutes from now by conditioning on relevant imagery.
In Sections
2
2.2
to
2
2.5
we focus entirely on diffusion models for unconditional imagery, while Section
2
2.6
discusses how to add the conditioning.

To perform diffusion image generation, these models utilize a mathematically well-described diffusion process for both the training of the underlying neural network and generating new images. Namely, to learn the distribution from a given data set of images (training set), training images are
partially destroyed
by applying a diffusion process to the image (e.g., adding noise; Fig.
1
a). The diffusion model is then trained to
restore
the structure of the images from their degenerated versions (Fig.
1
b). This process teaches the model to generate imagery of the same structure as the images in the training data set. In other words, the model learns to approximate the distribution represented by the training data set.
Diffusion models are thus based on developing a model to reverse the diffusion equation, an approach developed mathematically over 40 years ago by
Anderson 1982
. However, certain components of this approach are hard to implement in practice, and thus the full potential of this approach was made possible only once those components could be implemented through neural networks, as proposed by
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
.

2.3
Score-based Diffusion - Mathematical Foundation

The equations discussed here are all obtained from
Song et al. 2021
and
Karras et al. 2022
; however, we attempt to provide additional intuition behind the equations in those papers.
The key aspect of diffusion models is that they leverage well-described stochastic differential equations to transform images through a diffusion process (Fig.
1
), which in this application is the addition of random noise.
Physical diffusion describes the effects of the random motion of particles towards a state of maximal entropy; in our case we are using the same mathematical principles to “diffuse” the original image by applying incremental random changes to the pixel values such that eventually, those random changes completely overwhelm the original signal that was present.

During the forward process, noise is added to the original imagery, while during the backward process noise is removed to restore image structure. For ML applications using diffusion models, noise is added and then removed from original imagery during training and the model learns weights associated with this process, so that during inference the ML model can then generate an image from pure random noise. To describe this process, consider the stochastic differential equation for the forward process of diffusion (i.e., the destruction of an image by the addition of noise):

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)\;\mathrm{d}t+{g}(t)\;\mathrm{d}\bm{w}.

(1)

In Equation (
1
),
\bm{x}
is an image that is being transformed by adding random noise,
\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)
is known as the drift coefficient,
{g}(t)
is the diffusion coefficient, and
\bm{w}
is the standard Wiener process (e.g., random motion, Brownian motion). The
t
here is referred to as time and to be clear this is
diffusion time
where increased “time” represents increased noise in the image rather than a temporal relationship. Because we are using a differential equation, the reverse process can be derived (i.e., for our application a structured image can be generated from random noise). This is known as the
backwards process
and is derived in
Song et al. 2021
:

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=[\;\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-g(t)^{2}\;\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x})\;]\;\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\;\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}},

(2)

where
p_{t}(x)
is the probability of a particular noisy image
\bm{x}
under the distribution derived from the training data at noise level
t
,
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x})
is known as the
score function
, and
\bar{\bm{w}}
represents the standard Wiener process but in reverse.

As written, Equation (
2
) is abstract;
however,
Karras et al. 2022
reformulated the equation into more intuitive components using specific choices for
\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)
and
g(t)
that relate to parameters of a Gaussian noise distribution:

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\pm}=\underbrace{-\dot{\sigma}(t)\sigma(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{probability flow ODE}}\,\\
\pm\,\underbrace{\underbrace{\beta(t)\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{deterministic noise decay}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{2\beta(t)}\sigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega_{t}}_{\text{noise injection}}}_{\text{Langevin diffusion SDE}}.

(3)

Here
\sigma(t)
is the standard deviation of the Gaussian distribution of noise added to the image at time
t
,
p(x,\sigma(t)
) is a reformulation of
p_{t}(x)
in terms of
\sigma
, and
\beta(t)
is a parameter describing the relative scale of additional noise re-added during denoising to make this a
stochastic
differential equation. The first term on the right of Equation (
3
) is the probability flow ordinary differential equation (ODE) portion, which is the primary vehicle to move an image towards or away from the desired data distribution. The remaining terms on the right of Equation (
3
) represent the Langevin diffusion stochastic differential equation (SDE) which modulates the probability flow ODE by adding and removing additional random noise from the image during both the forward and reverse processes.

For now, let us focus on just the probability flow ODE,

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\dot{\sigma}(t)\sigma(t)\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t)}_{\text{score function}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4)

Section
2
2.5
will discuss adding back in the (SDE)
portion of Equation (
3
). Casting Equation (
4
) into the satellite imagery forecasting task of this paper,
\bm{x}
is the vector of noisy GOES IR images and
\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
is then how to change the images
\bm{x}
to add or remove noise. To remove noise, it is crucial to know the amount of noise in
\bm{x}
, which is described by the parameter
\sigma(t)
, where
\sigma
represents the standard deviation of the Gaussian noise that was added to the image (i.e., standard deviation of the normal curve). The parameter
t
is referred to as time in many papers, but to not get confused with the forecasting objective of this paper, view

as a distance metric, where larger

means we are farther from the original, non-noisy images.
\dot{\sigma}(t)
is the first derivative of

with respect to

.

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-t\;{\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t)}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t
.
The last term to discuss is the

In practice, spatial context is very important for the reverse flow process and as such, these distributions are high-dimensional. We provide these one-dimensional views to illustrate the intuition of how diffusion works. The partial derivative of this probability space with respect to

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t)\big)

Hopefully it is clear that if the score function could be explicitly known, then one could generate images from the training dataset from any random noise start. How do we get the score function? This is where neural networks come in.

2.4
Training the neural network

Karras et al. 2022
proved (in their Appendix B.3) that if one has access to an
ideal denoiser
(i.e., an algorithm that can return an exact copy of a member of the training set given any noised version of that image), then the score function can be computed exactly as

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x};\sigma(t))=\frac{D(\bm{x};\sigma(t))-\bm{x}}{\sigma(t)^{2}}

(5)

where the left hand side is the score function and
D(\bm{x};\sigma(t))
is the ideal denoiser. In practice, we do not generally have access to an ideal denoiser, but denoising is a task that neural networks are already known to be well-suited for, so we can leverage existing techniques to approximate this ideal denoiser.
In order to get a
good
denoiser we train a neural network to minimize the loss from denoising an image, which is typically done with the common
L^{2}
norm (i.e., Mean Squared Error):

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})}\lVert D_{\theta}(\bm{y}+\bm{n};\sigma)-\bm{y}\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(6)

Here
\bm{y}
, are the original non-noised images,
D_{\theta}
is the neural network denoiser, and

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
is a multi-dimensional normal distribution with covariance matrix
(\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
, where the dimension of the identity matrix,
\mathbf{I}
, is the number of pixels in each image. Thus,
\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
yields a matrix (image),
\bm{n}
, with independent Gaussian noise (of mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
) in each pixel. Furthermore, the expectation,
\mathbb{E}
, symbols in Eq. (
6
) indicate that the neural network is trained to minimize the
average
taken across all images in the data set and across all noise in the multi-dimensional normal distribution. Know there there is a decent amount of math behind deriving Equation
6
, but it is generally derived using the common machine learning process of Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO). Interested readers, please see the appendix of
Karras et al. 2022
. In practice the loss is also scaled by the current value of
\frac{1}{\sigma}
. The intuition here is that we want to encourage the neural network to perform better at low noise levels (small
\sigma
) because it should be closer to the true solution, so we weight the loss more heavily in that region.

Figure
3
is a visual example of a training step for the denoiser. First, we grab a batch of satellite images (
\bm{y}
). Then, we draw a random noise level for each image in the batch,
\sigma
, from a distribution defined by the user and use that to generate random Gaussian noise images,
\bm{n}
. The noise is then added directly to the images, yielding (
\bm{y}+\bm{n}
), and given to the neural network (
D_{\theta}(\bm{y}+\bm{n};\sigma)
) to be denoised. The choice of neural network architecture is arbitrary so long as it can be good at removing noise from an image. The MSE between the output of the neural network and the original images is then computed and the loss is backwards propagated through the network. This training process is repeated until the loss converges.

Take note of the double expectation in Equation
6
that implies that good sampling of the images,
\bm{y}
, the noise levels,
\sigma
, and of sampled noise images,
\bm{n}
, are required for training. Thus, in order to get a good estimate of the gradient in your learning process, diffusion will likely need a bigger batch than one would expect compared to using a plain U-Net
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
)
, because there needs to be a diverse sampling of examples and noise levels.

It is possible to develop a neural network directly to denoise the image, but
Karras et al. 2022
developed some clever scalings to make the training task simpler for the neural network. We briefly discuss these scalings here to make it easier to follow the corresponding functions in the code base.

Karras et al. 2022
actually chose a neural network,
F_{\theta}(\mbox{input};\mbox{noise level})
, that relates to the denoiser as follows:

D_{\theta}(\bm{x};\sigma)=c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)~\bm{x}\\
+c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)\underbrace{~F_{\theta}\big(c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)~\bm{x};~c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)\big)}_{\mbox{neural network}}\text{,}

(7)

where
c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)
,
c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)
,
c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)
, and
c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)
are all scalar functions of
\sigma
and transform the data such that training and generation are numerically better behaved and thus can be learned more easily by a neural network. The first function,
c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)
, is not to be confused with traditional skip connections in residual neural networks or U-Nets. Rather,
c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)
is a scalar function that provides a scaled version of
\bm{x}
such that the errors from
F_{\theta}
are not amplified. The scalar functions
c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)
and
c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)
ensure that the input and output of the neural network,
F_{\theta}
, have unit variance. Lastly,
c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)
is a scalar function that maps the original noise value so that it can be used better as a condition for the neural network. The derivation of these functions can be found in Appendix B.6 in
Karras et al. 2022
. The functions are as follows, where
\sigma_{data}
denotes the scalar variance of the training data, calculated across all pixels:

c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)=\sigma_{data}^{2}/(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{data}^{2})

(8)

c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)=\sigma\cdot\sigma_{data}/\sqrt{\sigma_{data}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}

(9)

c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)=1/\sqrt{\sigma_{data}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}

(10)

c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{4}\ln(\sigma)

(11)

We found that these functions derived in
Karras et al. 2022
worked well for our satellite nowcasting task and suspect that it could be a good starting points also for many Earth and Environmental Science tasks.

2.5
Generating images with the trained network

The goal of a diffusion model for unconditional image generation is to generate new images that belong to the training dataset from pure Gaussian noise. Thus, to start generating new images we first sample a pure noise image that has a large
\sigma(t)
value (e.g., 80). In order to translate this pure noise image into an image from the training data we leverage the trained network,
F_{\theta}
, and subsequently,
D_{\theta}
, by substituting the equivalence in Equation
5
into Equation
4

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\dot{\sigma}(t)~\sigma(t)\frac{D_{\theta}(\bm{x};\sigma(t))-\bm{x}}{\sigma(t)^{2}}~\mathrm{d}t.

(12)

Substituting the
Karras et al. 2022
choice of
\sigma(t)
as
t
yields

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\frac{D_{\theta}(\bm{x};t)-\bm{x}}{t}~\mathrm{d}t.

(13)

Notice how Equation
13
effectively states that in order to move the pure noisy image from higher noise to lower noise we can use the slope between the denoised image out of the neural network and the original noisy image. Equation
13
is written in its continuous form and like solving the Navier–Stokes equations on a regular grid we can discretize Equation
13

(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i-1})=-\frac{D_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{i};t_{i})-\bm{x}_{i}}{t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})

(14)

which results in

\bm{x}_{i-1}=\bm{x}_{i}+\big(\frac{D_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{i};t_{i})-\bm{x}_{i}}{t_{i}}\big)(t_{i}-t_{i-1})

(15)

The subscript,
i
denotes the current noisy step, and
(i-1)
denotes moving backwards towards the original data distribution, i.e., towards the goal.

\left(\frac{D_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{i};t_{i})-\bm{x}_{i}}{t_{i}}\right)
. Plugging into Equation
15
we can take a step of removing noise along this slope and end up at
\bm{x}_{i-1}
(transparent square and image in Figure 3a). This is repeated until we reach the left hand axis. This is an oversimplified example because the actual markers and lines in Fig.
4
represent changes in individual pixels in the image,
\bm{x}
. Thus, along a single trajectory in an abstract
256\cdot 256
-dimensional space.

Notice that despite updating their slope at each
t_{i}
value, the lines in Fig.
4
a are relatively straight trajectories (faint lines in background). Moreover, because the distribution
p(x;\sigma(t))
is fixed, these trajectories are completely determined by their origin on the right
y
-axis (i.e., their initial random seed). Recall that we have ignored the second half of Equation
3
in our description so far and that is why these
flows
are straight and deterministic. In practice, the second part of Equation
3
is not directly implemented in a clean way like the probability flow ODE. Instead, random walks are incorporated by injecting a user defined (i.e., hyperparameter) amount of noise during the denoising procedure. Effectively, think of the same generation procedure as in Fig.
4
a, but after we generate
\bm{x}_{i-1}
we actually add a bit more random noise in (i.e., small step in a random direction towards the right y-axis). This leads the image generation to deviate from the straight ODE paths, and enhances the ensemble image diversity across samples. An example of adding in the stochastic noise during the generation procedure is in Fig.
4
b. Trajectories now curve on their journey from the right y-axis to the left y-axis. In the
Karras et al. 2022
framework, the scale of the noise added back in is named
S_{churn}
and the amount of noise is named
S_{noise}
.

2.6
Adding in a condition

Figure 5:
A schematic describing one batch of data through the training process of a conditional diffusion model.

The description thus far has described
unconditional
image generation, that is, generating a random GOES IR image. Many tasks in the Earth and Atmospheric Sciences are much more aligned with
conditional
image generation. For our paper the task is to generate a specific GOES IR image, the image 10 minutes from now. Most papers on diffusion in the computer science literature tend to build diffusion models for text-to-image tasks
(Dall-e , ImageGen;
Ramesh et al. 2021
;
Saharia et al. 2022
, respectively)
. For these text-to-image tasks, incorporating the condition (i.e., the text prompt) is usually done by feeding it into the neural network as a text embedding (i.e., some sort of encoded vector of text). Typically the text embedding relies on known methods to translate the text prompt into tokens and a matrix of data. For our task, image-to-image, it was not initially clear how to include our image as a condition. Through some experimentation we found that including our condition (the past 2 GOES IR images) as additional channels to the input (i.e., concatenate the condition to the noisy dimension) to the denoiser U-Net worked well (Fig.
5
and Fig.
6
b).
Comparing the results of the unconditional model in Fig.
3
to the results of the conditional model in Fig.
5
, we see that the conditional model yields very low errors, while the unconditional model has very large errors, especially for the largest
\sigma
value,
\sigma=5
.
This is not surprising, as the image conditions (bottom right in Fig.
5
provide information about previous time steps, which provide a solid basis for the denoiser to predict the specific weather state we seek to predict, rather than a random weather state.

Furthermore, we found that including the image condition,
\mathbf{c}
, in this manner then the neural network training can easily be expressed in terms of the original Karras framework
(
Karras et al. 2022
)
by adding the condition,
\mathbf{c}
, to the score function, which becomes
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p\big(\bm{x}|\mathbf{c};\sigma(t)\big)
, and to the denoiser, which becomes
D_{\theta}(\bm{x}|\mathbf{c};\sigma(t))
.
The denoiser is then trained to minimize

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c}\sim p_{\text{cond}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})}\lVert D_{\theta}(\bm{y}+\bm{n}|\mathrm{\mathbf{c}};\sigma)-\bm{y}\rVert^{2}_{2},

(16)

where
p_{\text{cond}}
represents the distribution of the conditions used. In our case,
p_{\text{cond}}\approx p_{\text{data}}\times p_{\text{data}}
, because our condition consists of two images from the training dataset.
Once trained, the generation of new images is exactly the same as before, but now there is the condition passed along. One can think about the condition now informing the image generation path to a specific path in Fig.
4
, where before the score could have pointed the image generation along any arbitrary path.

This is not the only way to include a condition in diffusion models.
Rozet and Louppe 2023
,
Manshausen et al. 2024
,
Qu et al. 2024
,
Martin et al. 2025
and
Andry et al. 2025
break the score function into two tasks using Bayes rule:

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{x}|\mathrm{\mathbf{c}};\sigma(t))=\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{x};\sigma(t))}_{\text{Unconditional score}}\\
+\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\mathrm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{x};\sigma(t))}_{\text{Guiding score}}.

(17)

This consists of the usual score function shown in Equation
4
, and a second part to be estimated by assimilating observations. This method of conditioning diffusion models is known in the computer science literature as
guided diffusion
, where observations
guide
the diffusion generation of new images. This alternative method has a lot of promise in data assimilation, but it is not yet clear how the temporal information we are using for forecasting would be incorporated into their framework. This is a topic of future research.

Data and Methods

3.1
Data

The primary dataset of this paper is Channel 13 (10.3 micron wavelength) from the the Advanced Baseline Imager
(
Schmit et al. 2017
, ABI; )
on board the GOES-16 satellite, which is part of the Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite (GOES) satellite series. Channel 13 is a long wave infrared channel and is chosen because of its wide use in general meteorology, particularly for detecting clouds at all times, including both day and night. GOES-16 was launched in 2016 and has been operational since December 2017 located at central longitude of -75 W. Since the motivating goal of this paper is a global cloud forecast, we use full-disk (10 min resolution) data from April 2023 through June 2024.

A full-disk image for Channel 13 has approximately two kilometer resolution near nadir (i.e., over the Amazon) and has a raw image shape of 5,424 by 5,424 pixels. Unfortunately, for the Graphical Processing Units (GPUs) available for this project (a single NVIDIA GH-200 system, hereafter GH-200), the 5,424 by 5,424 image size is prohibitively large and involves gradient computations that would not fit into memory. Thus, we take an approach where we create our datasets by randomly choosing smaller patches of the larger image.

3.1.1
Training set selection

The training set is selected from 2023 and includes several quality control steps. From all files in 2023, we grab a random file with replacement. We check whether the previous and next time steps (minus and plus 10 mins) exist. If they exist, we open the files. From the 5,424 by 5,424 grid, we randomly sample 250 grid indices with a viewing zenith angle less than 65 degrees. This step avoids selecting imagery at the edge of the full disk where data are invalid. From these 250 grid incidences, we slice out a patch of 256 by 256 (about 500 km by 500 km, indicated as colored patches in Fig.
6
). Out of these 250 patches we only keep those that have at least 10
\%
cloud fraction. From the cloudy patches, we then include only patches that occur during the day (85 deg solar zenith). Daytime only data were chosen so auxiliary information like visible imagery and more accurate retrievals of cloud top height and base height are available for evaluation. From this final set of patches, we randomly choose 16 of them to save to avoid including too many samples from the same time step. We repeat this process until there are more than 30,000 patches for training.

3.1.2
Validation set selection

The same procedure is used to generate a validation set, but the files from January to August 2024 are used and only 1,000 total patches are used for evaluation. The primary use of this validation set is to determine the hyperparameters for image generation.

3.1.3
Test set selection

The same procedure is used to generate a test set, but the files from August 2024 to February 2025 are used and only 1,000 total patches are used for evaluation.

3.1.4
Preprocessing of images

All images are scaled prior to training the diffusion models such that the mean of the training data is zero and the variance is one. This is critical for diffusion models, because the variance scaling used for the Gaussian noise used during training and inference is highly dependent on the mean and range of the data. For example, if the noise is too small to replace the underlying data distribution, the diffusion models have a challenge generating new data from complete noise. The mean and standard deviation are calculated from the training dataset and applied to all three datasets.

3.2
Models Trained

To explore the various avenues of diffusion for cloud nowcasting we train several different types of models.
All models trained in this paper use the exact same underlying neural network architecture at their core, namely the U-Net architecture described below, but with different data sources.

3.2.1
U-Net architecture

The U-Net choice follows an online tutorial available on HuggingFace (archived into the github repository for this paper, see Data Availability). It is a fairly large U-Net
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
)
with six up- and down- sampling blocks, which contain two res-net blocks
(
He et al. 2015
)
each. There is also attention layers added at the fifth of the six total blocks and inside the bottleneck. This model has 113 million learnable parameters. We acknowledge that only using one core architecture limits the generalization of this paper, but due to long training times (order of days) on our GH-200, we are limited in our ability to perform hyperparameter tuning of the architecture. Using smaller core models (i.e., with fewer parameters) and core models without attention will likely speed up training and inference times for satellite nowcasting and is a topic of future research.

3.2.2
Baseline model (plain U-Net)

To serve as a baseline forecast, we first train the plain U-Net in the more conventional manner on the training data. That is, we train the U-Net to perform the 10 min forecast using mean squared error as the loss. This
plain U-Net
is trained using a random
80/20
split of the training data, and allowed to train until early stopping when the held out validation loss stopped decreasing for 10 epochs.

3.2.3
Diffusion models

We train three different diffusion variants that all use the U-Net as denoiser. The first diffusion model, named
Diff
follows the
Karras et al. 2022
framework, where the denoiser is denoising the 10 min forecast. The second diffusion model, named
CorrDiff
, follows the approach from
Mardani et al. 2025
and
Pathak et al. 2024
. It uses the pre-trained U-Net of the baseline model to get an initial, potentially blurry, prediction of the 10 min forecast. Then it trains a denoiser to improve the forecast of the plain U-Net. The denoiser uses the same condition as Diff, along with the estimate obtained from the pre-trained U-Net output (i.e., the input is now 4 channels). The other change compared to Diff is that the CorrDiff model predicts the difference between the plain U-Net estimate and the truth.

For all diffusion models, we train the denoisers for 1,000 epochs with a batch size of 45 and a two-batch gradient accumulation (i.e., effective batch size of 90). The networks then see more than 35 million images with various noise levels during training.
The Diff and CorrDiff diffusion models take about 5 days of training on our GH-200 with
98\%
average GPU utilization.

Given the relatively long training (evaluation) time for the Diff and CorrDiff models, we also explore a latent diffusion model
(
Leinonen et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, named
LDM
. That is, we use a pre-trained variational autoencoder (VAE) from HuggingFace to compress our original data from 256 by 256 (with one channel) down to 64 by 64 pixels (with four channels). Due to the channel dimension going up, the compression factor is only four. The choice of VAE is discussed in Section
3
3.3
, but know that we choose the autoencoder that has the smallest reconstruction loss on the GOES imagery (Table 1). We then use the compressed data to train the denoiser for diffusion in this latent space. This results in a five-times speed-up in training, where the latent model completed training for 1,000 epochs in about 1 day. We did attempt building a latent version of the CorrDiff model, but the U-Net predictions in the latent space were poor on their own and we decided to not pursue it further for this paper.

. In general, most of them have relatively poor reconstructions, showing RMSEs of two and three kelvin where any individual pixel could be upwards of 20-30 K different than the original image. 3.4
Generation details and ensembles

As described in Section
2
, diffusion machine learning tasks are split into training the denoiser and generation. With this split come a few additional hyperparameters solely linked to the generation procedure. These generation hyperparameters are centered on: how many denoising steps to take (
num_steps
); the maximum noise level to start from (

); the spacing of the steps (
rho

for the others. This assumption may be false, leading to suboptimal parameter sets, but this is an area of future research. 3.5
Autoregression

Figure 8:
Graphic demonstrating the autoregressive task.

All machine learning forecast methods trained here follow the structure of many of the data-driven weather prediction models and optical flow techniques where two images (
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{-10}
) are used to forecast a single time step (
\tau_{+10})
(Fig.
6
b and Fig.
8
). Then, the output from that single forecast can be fed back into the forecast models recursively. Forecasts of arbitrary length can then be rolled out. We choose to forecast out three hours (18 autoregressive steps). Some of the data driven models initially train on the single step prediction paradigm but then tune with a low learning rate on multiple steps. It is not clear how that could be done with score-based diffusion since the training of the neural network is separated from the image generation but this is an area of future research. Thus, no multi-step fine tuning is done.

3.6
Metrics

We choose a handful of common pixelwise metrics to get a general quantification of the forecast performance of the various methods. Those include mean error (ME), mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) all defined as:

\text{ME}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(y_{i,j}-\hat{y}_{i,j})

(18)

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|y_{i,j}-\hat{y}_{i,j}|

(19)

\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(y_{i,j}-\hat{y}_{i,j})^{2}}

(20)

where
\hat{y}_{i,j}
is the
j
th pixel in the
i
th forecasted image,
y_{i,j}
is
j
th pixel in the
i
th true image,
n
is the total number of images in the validation or testing set (which is 1000), and
m
is the number of pixels per image (which is
256\cdot 256
). Note that in the RMSE computation, we first compute MSE across the entire testing or validation set and then take the square root rather than computing RMSE for each image and then averaging those. We include mean error as a measure of bias (i.e., forecast drift).

A surprising result was the ability of the diffusion model to initiate convection close to where convection was observed despite only knowing about 20 mins of observed satellite data. Similarly to the Diff model, the ensemble mean does not have a single cold cloud feature like the single member, but does have an area of colder clouds to the NE where cumulus and convection were observed. 5
Summary and Conclusions

A challenge with many machine learning methods is the prevalence of blurry solutions that then have limited usefulness in human-in-the-loop applications (i.e., weather forecasters). Diffusion models in the literature suggest to be a solution to these blurry outputs while also providing useful uncertainty quantification through ensembles. Thus, for this paper we explored the use of score-based diffusion models to nowcast geostationary satellite imagery (i.e., GOES infrared brightness temperature). The nowcast was built with the eventual use-case of forecasting cloud and precipitation location so evaluations were centered on the evolution of cold brightness temperature regions in the imagery.

The first contribution of this work was a deep dive into the methodology of score-based diffusion models (Section
2
). In the deep dive, we discussed some of the math as well as some base intuition behind score-based diffusion models. Furthermore, we discussed how to train and use score-based diffusion models in an attempt to accelerate their use in the Earth and Environmental Sciences.

The second contribution of this work was the exploration of three different types of score-based diffusion models and how well they work for a simplified nowcasting task of satellite imagery. More specifically, we explore the use of three score-based diffusion models, a diffusion model (Diff) that follows
Karras et al. 2022
, a residual corrective diffusion model (CorrDiff) that follows
Mardani et al. 2025
and a latent diffusion model (LDM) inspired by
Leinonen et al. 2023
to forecast GOES channel 13 infrared brightness temperatures out to three hours. We showed that the diffusion models were able to nowcast satellite imagery qualitatively and quantitatively better than both a persistence forecast and a mean squared error trained U-Net (Figures 10-14). The best performing diffusion model was the CorrDiff approach followed by the Diff model and then the LDM. All diffusion models were able to not only advect the current clouds in the scene but also generate and decay clouds. We also showed that all methods were able to generate convection (i.e., localized regions of very cold brightness temperatures) despite only having two images (20 mins) of 10 micron infrared imagery and no information on the thermodynamic state of the atmosphere, the solar input, or the land surface type.

The diffusion models we showed here are far from perfect. The Diff model tends to be noisier than the CorrDiff approach, which could be detrimental to longer forecast lead times. Furthermore, all machine learning methods had a clear bias with time, some trending warm, others trending cold, which could eventually lead to nonphysical forecasts. Another challenge of the diffusion models is their increased computational cost compared to the more traditional U-Net (i.e., MSE loss). It took about five days to train the plain diffusion model and the CorrDiff model on a relatively new GPU system (e.g., GH-200). This length of training time is prohibitively long to allow for a proper hyperparameter search of the underlying architecture on our resources. Furthermore, unlike in more traditional machine learning methods, the computational cost is not isolated to training time. Because of the iterative steps of denoising, the diffusion models take substantially longer to run during inference time, 10 mins to generate a three hour forecast with 10-members for the diffusion model versus the 10 seconds for the U-Net. This added computational time also prohibited hyperparameter tuning of
generation
parameters. Thus, the choices we made are likely sub-optimal. We also explored the use of pretrained autoencoders to enable the use of latent diffusion, which substantially improved computation costs (one day to train, one min to run forecast), but results are clearly worse than for the other diffusion models. The LDM approach might improve if a specialized autoencoder is trained for satellite imagery.

This paper closely followed the methodology from
Karras et al. 2022
which was also used in
Mardani et al. 2025
,
Manshausen et al. 2024
and
Price et al. 2025
. Since the original
Karras et al. 2022
work, there have been improvements. The improvements are mainly centered on carefully choosing the machine learning architecture
(
Karras et al. 2024b
)
and having better image generation
(
Karras et al. 2024a
)
. Thus, there are improvements to the methodology directly that could improve results here. Furthermore there is work being done to lower the computational cost of diffusion models
(
Xu et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Outside of changes to the methodology, other ways to improve the satellite nowcasting would be to include additional information (i.e., more conditions) to the model input. Specifically, including simple input fields like the solar zenith angle and topography should help inform the model where in the image should be warming and what the surface type is (e.g., land vs. ocean), respectively. One could simplify the task further by breaking the task into two parts, advection and alteration. Advection could be handled well by known tools like optical flow and the diffusion model could handle the generation, growth, and/or decay of clouds. In addition to the solar zenith angle and the topography, this work could be extended to include more channels of satellite imagery. This idea of multitask learning might also help because if a multi-channel diffusion model was trained using all thermal bands, then the model could potentially learn more of the 3-dimensional structure of the atmosphere, as well as the water vapor content, which are both critical for the evolution of clouds. However, including additional input and output channels will likely take careful implementation given the already computationally intensive diffusion task.

Overall, we are encouraged by the prospect of score-based diffusion models for machine learning tasks in the Earth and Environmental Sciences. They provide often high-resolution features that appear more realistic than for the more traditional U-Net approaches of the past. Furthermore, the out-of-the-box uncertainty quantification offered from ensemble generation is an added benefit, often improving pixel-wise metrics while still having the information content of the individual members.
