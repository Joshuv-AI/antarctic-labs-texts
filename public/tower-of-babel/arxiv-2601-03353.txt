Black hole thermodynamics at null infinity Part 1: Dual Generalized Second Law
Antoine Rignon-Bret 1, and Matthieu Vilatte 2, 1 Faculté des Sciences et Technologies Université de Lorraine, Bd des Aiguillettes, 54506 Vandœuvre-lès-Nancy, France 2 Service de Physique de l’Univers, Champs et Gravitation, Université de Mons – UMONS, Place du Parc 20, 7000 Mons, Belgium

Abstract




Abstract

The generalized second law (GSL) of black hole thermodynamics asserts the monotonic increase of the generalized entropy combining the black hole area and the entropy of quantum fields outside the horizon. Modern proofs of the GSL rely on information-theoretic methods and are typically formulated using algebras of observables defined on the event horizon together with a vacuum state invariant under horizon symmetries, inducing a geometric modular flow. In this work, we formulate a dual version of the generalized second law from the perspective of asymptotic observers at future null infinity, who do not have access to the black hole area. Our approach exploits the dependence of the second law on the choice of algebra of observables and of a reference state invariant under suitable symmetries, in close analogy with open quantum thermodynamics. Using algebraic quantum field theory and modular theory, we analyze several physically motivated vacuum states, including the Hartle–Hawking state and two classes of regularized vacua. We show that, at null infinity, the monotonic quantity governing an irreversible evolution is no longer the generalized entropy, but rather a thermodynamic potential constructed from asymptotic observables. Depending on the chosen vacuum, this potential takes the form of the free energy or of a generalized grand potential built from the Bondi mass and additional (angular) mode-dependent chemical potentials. The resulting inequalities define a dual generalized second law at future null infinity, which can be consistently combined with the standard GSL involving variations of the black hole area.

A further motivation comes from the need to establish the second law for a broader class of vacuum states. The Hartle–Hawking state

To summarize, since the modular Hamiltonian is defined by both the algebra of observables and the vacuum state, such extensions may encode the second law in terms of variations of a different set of observables (e.g. the Bondi mass).

This situation closely parallels open quantum thermodynamics, where changing the reservoir to which the system is coupled modifies the late-time state (i.e. the fixed point of the dynamics) towards which the system relaxes. In ordinary thermodynamics, altering the fixed point of the dynamics corresponds to working with different thermodynamic potentials, whose variations are monotonic along physical processes and which vanish for stationary states. In this sense, thermodynamic potentials encode the second law by identifying the direction of spontaneous evolution towards an extremum of the potential
[
59
]
. For example, the entropy is the appropriate potential for an isolated system, whereas the free energy plays this role for a system in thermal contact with a reservoir. When the system can also exchange volume or particles with its environment, the relevant quantities becomes the free enthalpy or the grand potential. In these cases, the system is no longer exchanging only heat, but also work and matter. At first sight, the relevance of this discussion to quantum field theory may not be obvious, since the choice of vacuum and Hilbert space is often made a priori. However, the restriction of physically relevant vacuum states to subalgebras typically satisfies the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) conditions
[
60
,
61
]
with respect to a suitable boost Hamiltonian. In addition, the freedom in choosing the vacuum state looks equivalent to the possibility of changing the features of the reservoir at hand. The only requirement is that if one allows for alternative vacuum choices, they must be examined carefully: they should be non-singular and admit a clear physical interpretation.

An interesting setting in which to test these ideas is future null infinity. This locus and the future horizon share many similarities. Both of them are non-expanding null hypersurfaces, therefore quantizing a scalar field and defining an algebra of observables on one or the other should basically lead to analogous expressions.
The exact formulation of the second law is however dependent on the vacuum state considered, and therefore on the boundary conditions of the field at late time. As stated earlier, a naive choice would be to consider the Hartle-Hawking state at future null infinity. However that state does not provide an accurate description of the black hole. Indeed, in a seminal calculation by Hawking
[
4
,
5
]
and Wald
[
62
]
, it was shown that the late-time state of a quantum field following a gravitational collapse does not relax to the vacuum naturally associated with asymptotic inertial observers. Instead, such observers detect a steady flux of particles—at least as long as the backreaction of the quantum field on the spacetime remains negligible—in a state that deviates from a perfect blackbody spectrum due to the presence of graybody factors. Physically, these arise because although the quantum state near the horizon is purely thermal at early times, only a fraction of the outgoing modes are transmitted through the effective potential barrier surrounding the black hole. As a result, the spectrum observed at future null infinity is not exactly thermal, with high-angular-momentum modes being strongly suppressed. It was argued in
[
52
]
that one may associate suitable thermodynamic potentials with the relaxation toward this asymptotic (quasi)-equilibrium state, leading to a statement analogous to (
1.2
) but at future null infinity, statement that we call the
dual (Generalized) Second Law
. When graybody factors are taken into account, the monotonicity of the relative entropy was shown to produce additional, non-geometric contributions reflecting the non-thermal character of the stationary state. These extra terms can be interpreted as arising from effective chemical potentials induced by the potential barrier. In the limit of perfect transmission, these contributions vanish and one finds the free energy (built upon the Bondi mass) as the relevant thermodynamic potential at null infinity.

The emergence of the free energy, rather than the entropy, can be motivated on physical grounds as follows (see
[
52
]
). From the perspective of asymptotic observers, a black hole behaves as an open system that continuously emits energy and radiation at a fixed temperature (namely the Hawking temperature) towards future null infinity. In ordinary thermodynamics, isolated systems—such as a black hole together with its environment—evolve towards states of higher entropy, which explains why the generalized second law is expected to hold as a natural extension of this principle. By contrast, open systems embedded in a thermal background at fixed temperature do not generally exhibit monotonically increasing entropy; instead, their free energy is the quantity that necessarily decreases.

1

The temperature entering the definition of the free energy is that of the reservoir, not that of the system, which is not necessarily in thermal equilibrium. Strictly speaking, the relevant quantity is the Helmholtz free energy rather than the Gibbs free energy.
It is also important to note that if the emitted spectrum were exactly thermal, the energy flux at future null infinity would diverge, since radiation would arrive from all directions. More precisely, the Hartle–Hawking state—being genuinely thermal—is incompatible with asymptotic flatness: the energy density does not decay at large distances from the black hole but instead approaches that of a uniform thermal bath at the Hawking temperature. This illustrates why non-geometric corrections are indispensable for a realistic description of Hawking radiation. In practice, because high-angular-momentum modes are unable to penetrate the effective potential barrier, the radiation observed at null infinity is effectively closer to a two-dimensional flux than to radiation from a four-dimensional thermal bath at the Hawking temperature. These considerations help us to find a way to regularize the Hartle-Hawking state in order to get a finite flux at infinity. In this work, we propose two kinds of regularizations: a hard one in which we select a cut-off above which all the modes are back-scattered inside the black hole; and a soft one in which each angular mode is associated to its own effective temperature, the Hawking temperature corresponding to a perfect transmission.

The dual GSL is then studied in these three vacua (Hartle-Hawking, hard and soft regularizations) and instead of the generalized entropy one finds either the free energy or the grand potential as relevant thermodynamic potential. In the case of the hard regularization of the Hartle-Hawking vacuum, the free energy is a suitable thermodynamic potential so that the evolution of the black hole corresponds to the inequality

(1.3)

\Delta M-T_{H}\Delta S\leq 0

where
M
is the Bondi mass at two successive cut of future null infinity,
S
is the entropy of the matter fields on two successive spacelike slices ending on the corresponding cuts of null infinity, and
T_{H}
is the Hawking temperature. We can combine the dual version of the second law (
1.3
) with the one on the horizon and get

(1.4)

\Delta M-T_{H}\Delta\left(\frac{A}{4G}+S\right)\leq 0

which reduces to the generalized second law (
1.2
) if the slices extend to the same cut at at null infinity (or even at spacelike infinity).
In the case of the soft regularization, non geometric terms appear in the thermodynamic potential. The dual generalized second law can now be expressed as

(1.5)

\Delta M-\sum_{lm}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mu_{\omega l}\Delta n_{\omega lm}\text{d}\omega-T_{H}\Delta S\leq 0

where

Why so many vacuum states?

One may wonder why we have introduced that many different vacuum states. From a mathematical point of view, the GNS representation theorem asserts that given an algebraic state (the vacuum state) one can always construct a Hilbert-space representation of a unital
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
-algebra like the Weyl algebra. The representation is tight to the choice of state, as two different states generically give rise to two Hilbert spaces non related by a unitary transformation. Mathematics however do not tell how to choose the vacuum state. This depends on the physical situation at hand, what kind of process one wants to model. The study of black hole radiation and evaporation led to the construction of various vacuum state among which the Hartle-Hawking and the Unruh state. Each of them proposes a different way to model a black hole and its radiation, with more or less relevance depending on the degree of accuracy with which we want to study that process. If the Hawking radiation at late time is modeled by the Unruh vacuum, the latter cannot be written at future null infinity using a positive frequency decomposition, and its characterization under an algebra of observables at null infinity is left as an open problem. However, it is still possible to get an expression of the modular Hamiltonian starting from the thermal character of the Unruh vacuum on the past horizon and using transmission/reflexion coefficients (see the second part of this work
[
78
]
). However, as we have repeatedly emphasized, the Hartle–Hawking state is far from providing a realistic description of an evaporating black hole. This led us to propose the hard and soft regularizations of the Hartle-Hawking state at
{\cal I}_{R}
. The next Section will show how the thermodynamical potential, whose monotonicity at
{\cal I}_{R}^{+}
is the dual GSL, gets modified when we consider the latter vacua.

4
Dual Generalized Second Law

This Section constitutes the core of our work, namely to prove the dual GSL at future null infinity. As stated in the Introduction, the GSL is proven on the horizon using the monotonicity of the relative entropy between the restriction of the Hartle-Hawking vacuum state and an arbitrary state belonging to the GNS Hilbert space representing, from that vacuum state, the algebra of observables of the horizon. The two spacelike hypersurfaces between which the GSL was proven in
[
13
]
were both starting at spatial infinity
\iota^{0}
and ending at two different cuts of the future horizon. In that case the relevant thermodynamic potential is the generalized entropy. Black hole thermodynamics has therefore been unveil from a near-horizon perspective. We treat here a dual picture, focusing on asymptotic observers.

The dual GSL corresponds to the monotonicity of a thermodynamical potential
\mathcal{G}
(free energy or grand potential, depending on the vacuum state) between two spacelike hypersurfaces starting from the same cut on the horizon but ending at different cuts of
{\cal I}_{R}^{+}
. A proof involving an effective description of the process was published by one of us in
[
52
]
. This work improves the latter, as we give rigorous algebraic foundations of all the steps. In particular we defined precisely the algebra of observables at
{\cal I}_{R}
and introduced three vacua, the Hartle-Hawking state and its hard and soft regularizations, from which we constructed the associated GNS Hilbert spaces in Section
3
. Once this is done the proof basically amounts to apply again the monotonicity of the relative entropy. In the course of the proof, appears the notion of modular Hamiltonian, which we construct in Appendix
D
while in Appendix
E
we relate it to the integral of some normal-ordered stress-energy tensor. Given this relations, Einstein’s equations are used to make contact with the physical quantities (e.g. the Bondi mass
M
). Therefore all the steps of the subsequent proof are rigorously proven from the point of view of Algebraic QFT and Modular Theory. The reader is advised to look at these Appendices for more details.

Once the dual GSL is proven at future null infinity for the Hartle-Hawking state (subsection

4.3

5
Outlook and perspectives

We showed that from the point of view of asymptotic observers the spontaneous evolution of quantum fields in a perturbed Schwarzschild black hole background was equivalent to the monotonicity of a thermodynamic potential built upon the relevant geometric quantity at future null infinity, namely the Bondi mass, and eventually additional non geometric terms to which we provided a thermodynamic interpretation. Depending on the manner the Hartle-Hawking state, unsuitable for such a study due to its divergent net energy flux at
{\cal I}_{R}^{+}
, is regularized, the dual GSL states the decrease of either the free energy or the grand potential. Non geometric corrections are encoded in a set of chemical potentials modeling the back-reaction process due to the potential barrier. When the hypersurfaces are opened both at infinity and on the horizon, geometric quantities associated to both semiclassical dynamics (area and mass) enters the, now generalized, thermodynamic potential. The monotonicity of these thermodynamic potentials has been proven in this setting within the semi-classical regimes for a large class of states representing local excitations of the field on top of the Hartle-Hawking vacuum state and/or its regularizations.

The first natural extension of our work is to consider the Unruh state, which is the adapted state to consider in order to model accurately the state of a black hole after a collapse. While the different classes of vacuum states defined on the algebra of observables at null infinity were modeling some features of the Unruh vacuum, such as the fact that modes with different angular momentum are back-scattered differently by the black hole background, none of them describes accurately the late time Hawking radiation. Indeed, the dependence of the frequency is the transmission coefficients prevents us from describing the Unruh vacuum as the natural vacuum state associated to a positive frequency decomposition attached with a time parameter at maximally extended null infinity. Instead, as proposed in
[
52
]
, one should study a transmission process between the white hole horizon where the radiation is exactly thermal at Hawking temperature, and future null infinity. Due to the potential barrier, not all the angular modes will be observable by an asymptotic observer, hence chemical potentials are expected to appear in the relevant thermodynamic potential, exactly as what happened for late time boundary conditions compatible with the
\kappa_{l}
-vacua (see (
4.78
)). In
[
52
]
, preliminary computations showed that the latter depend precisely on the transmission coefficients. The more algebraic setup presented in this work can be used to put this proof on more solid grounds. Additionally, treating the Unruh state allows to deal with rotating black holes and therefore to study the dual GSL also in that context.

A second extension is based on several observations we have made along this work. On the one hand, as stated in the Introduction, finding the free energy or the grand potential in the dual GSL shows that, from the point of view of usual thermodynamics, there exists a deeper connection between quantization of a field on a non-expanding null hypersurface and the theory of of open quantum systems. One further hint towards that direction was the assumption (
2.28
), comparing a dynamical time
\tau_{D}=\frac{\langle T_{uu}\rangle_{\Psi}}{\partial_{u}\langle T_{uu}\rangle_{\Psi}}
related to the characteristic variation of the stress energy tensor, to what can be interpreted as a correlation time
\tau_{B}=\kappa^{-1}\sim\frac{\hbar}{T_{H}}
. In the theory of open quantum systems weakly interacting with a thermal bath, the typical scale at which one can rely on Markovian dynamics is tied to the inverse temperature
[
25
]
.
53
53

53

To be precise, the Markovian approximation also depends on additional timescales set by the bath spectral density, including possible ultraviolet cutoffs. However, for thermal equilibrium baths, the inverse temperature defines a universal thermal correlation timescale.
This suggests a close interplay between the relaxation of quantum fields toward a vacuum state at null infinity (e.g., the Hartle–Hawking state) and the equilibration of a small system, regarded as a subpart of a larger bath, toward a (generalized) Gibbs state. It would be interesting to unveil this analogy between black hole physics and Markovian systems, explaining in particular why, while being a very strong hypothesis, Markovianity is immediately achieved once we place ourself on a null hypersurface. Lastly, the correlation with thermodynamics of open quantum systems can be push further by going back to the expression (
4.78
). The non-geometric terms depending on the chemical potential are related to work terms. This seems to tell us that, once correctly described by an appropriate quantum state at infinity,

work could in principle be extracted from the non-thermal radiation emitted by a non-rotating black hole.

Appendix B
The spacetime approach to QFT in curved spacetimes

In Minkowski spacetime, Klein-Gordon fields form an infinite collection of harmonic oscillators, some with positive frequency while others with negative frequency. The classical field, i.e. the solution of the Klein-Gordon equation, is expanded in a basis of positive frequency modes (e.g.
~e^{-i\omega t},\omega>0
with
t
the Poincaré global time) and the Fourier coefficients

are promoted to quantum operators satisfying the canonical commutation relations. This gives the quantum version of the field from which the notion of vacuum state, one-particle Hilbert-space and Fock space are built as usual. Hence, in a nutshell, quantizing a classical field amounts first to define a space of positive frequency modes from which the one-particle Hilbert-space arises and then to construct the associated Fock space. What renders Minkowski spacetime peculiar w.r.t. other spacetimes (in particular curved ones), is the existence of a global notion of time
t
. The field is then basically quantized on a
t=\text{cst}
hypersurface.

In curved spacetimes there does not exist generically

65

65

One then selects one time, among others, and starts by considering the space of initial data of a configuration variable (the field) and its conjugate momenta (namely the derivative of the field w.r.t. the selected notion of time). This “initial" surface can be either spacelike or null and this leads to the quantization of fields on spacelike or null hypersurfaces. It is actually more convenient to step back and look at a more covariant manner to quantize the theory, without specifying any particular initial surface data nor any notion of equal time. The basic idea is to focus on the solutions of the equations of motion themselves. The fundamental commutation relations are then encoded by the symplectic form of the theory, defined on the solution space. The symplectic form plays therefore a fundamental role in the quantization of fields in curved spacetimes.

In this Appendix we start by presenting the main steps of the procedure of quantization of a field on a spacelike hypersurface, namely, the definition of an invertible symplectic form on the phase space,

B.3

As argued by Wald in

D.3
The relative entropy

Through the notion of relative entropy, the relative modular operator (
D.16
) allows to build a quantity one can use to answer the question
how close are two quantum states?
(wrt to some algebra of observables). Given two cyclic and separating states
\ket{\Psi}
and
\ket{\Omega}
for a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{A}
, Araki’s definition
[
19
]
of
relative entropy
is

(D.24)

S(\Psi||\Omega):=\bra{\Psi}\left(-\ln\Delta_{\Psi|\Omega}\right)\ket{\Psi}\,.

Often,
\ket{\Omega}
is the vacuum state. Note that if one of the states is not separating then the relative modular operator may contain some zero eigenvalues, rendering the above definition divergent. Several immediate properties of this quantity are listed below

•

when
\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\Omega}
then
S(\Omega||\Omega)=0
, there is no differences between a state and itself.

•

when there exist an unitary element of the commutant
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
(i.e.
A^{\prime\dagger}A^{\prime}=1_{\mathcal{A^{\prime}}}
) such that
\ket{\Psi}=A^{\prime}\ket{\Omega}
then
S(\Omega||A^{\prime}\Omega)=0
. This comes from the fact that, defining
\ket{\Omega^{\prime}}=A^{\prime}\ket{\Omega}
we have that for any
A\in\mathcal{A}

(D.25)

\bra{\Omega^{\prime}}A\ket{\Omega^{\prime}}=\bra{\Omega}A^{\prime\dagger}AA^{\prime}\ket{\Omega}=\bra{\Omega}A^{\prime\dagger}A^{\prime}A\ket{\Omega}=\bra{\Omega}A\ket{\Omega}\,,

where we have used first the fact that
A^{\prime}
belongs to the commutant and second that it is unitary. Eq. (
D.25
) shows that two states related by the action of an unitary element of the commutant cannot be distinguish by any physical measurement. Therefore their relative entropy should vanish, and it does.

•

given the convexity of the logarithm one as the following inequality

(D.26)

-\ln\Delta_{\Psi|\Omega}\geq 1-\Delta_{\Psi|\Omega}\,,

from which we deduce the positivity of the relative entropy

(D.27)

\displaystyle S(\Psi\lvert\lvert\Omega)=\bra{\Psi}(-\ln{\Delta_{\Psi\lvert\Omega}})\ket{\Psi}\geq\bra{\Psi}(1-\Delta_{\Psi\lvert\Omega})\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\langle{\Psi}\ket{\Psi}-\bra{\Psi}(S_{\Psi\lvert\Omega}^{\dagger}S_{\Psi\lvert\Omega})\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\langle{\Psi}\ket{\Psi}-\langle{\Omega}\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0\,,

since
\ket{\Omega}=S_{\Psi\lvert\Omega}\ket{\Psi}
and
\langle{\Psi}\ket{\Psi}=\langle{\Omega}\ket{\Omega}=1
.

•

if
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
then we have for any two states
\ket{\Psi}
and
\ket{\Omega}
that

(D.28)

S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Psi||\Omega)\geq S_{\mathcal{B}}(\Psi||\Omega)\,.

This is called the
monotonicity of the relative entropy
and it formalizes the fact that if we have at our disposal a smaller set of observables (the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
) then it will be harder to distinguish between the two states
\ket{\Psi}
and
\ket{\Omega}
.

•

for systems in which one can deal with density matrices, the relative entropy is decreasing under the action of a Complete Positive Trace Preserving (CPTP) map
\Lambda

(D.29)

S(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}}\lvert\lvert\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}})\geq S(\Lambda(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}})\lvert\lvert\Lambda(\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}}))

and can be interpreted as the fact that the two states
\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}}
are less distinguishable after the application of the channel
\Lambda
. There exists also a statement of (
D.29
) from a more algebraic point of view. For two finite dimensional algebras
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
\alpha:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
a Schwarz map,
111
111

111

A Schwarz map

satisfies:
\forall A\in\mathcal{A},\alpha(AA^{\ast})\geq\alpha(A)\alpha(A^{\ast})
and therefore preserves the positivity.
and a pair of algebraic states
(\psi,\omega)
on
\mathcal{A}
, one has
[
21
]

(D.30)

S_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi\lvert\lvert\omega)\geq S_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi\circ\alpha\lvert\lvert\omega\circ\alpha)\,.

Finally we should be sure that the definition (
D.24
) gives back the usual expression in the case where traces are well defined. We use again the states
\ket{\Omega}
and
\ket{\Psi}
which are cyclic and separating for both restricted algebras
\mathcal{A}_{I}
and
\mathcal{A}_{II}
. Recall then that

so that for any observable

\displaystyle S(\Psi\lvert\lvert\Omega)

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi}\left(-\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{II}}\right)\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{II}}\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{II}}\right)-\Tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\right)-\Tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\ln\hat{\rho}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}_{I}}\right)\,,

\hat{K}^{II}=-\int_{-\infty}^{U_{0}}(U_{0}-U):T_{UU}:_{\Omega}\text{d}U\,,

so that in terms of ladder operators of region
II
we get an expression of the form

(E.14)

\hat{K}^{II}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\omega\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\omega,II}\hat{a}_{\omega,II}-\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\omega,II}\hat{a}_{\omega,II}\rangle_{\Omega}\right)\text{d}\omega=-\frac{K_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{A}_{II}^{\cal I}}}{2\pi}\,.

Finally we get the subsequent fundamental result

(E.15)

2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(U-U_{0}):T_{UU}:_{\Omega}\text{d}U=2\pi\int_{0}^{+\infty}\omega\left(\hat{N}_{\omega,I}-\hat{N}_{\omega,II}\right)\text{d}\omega\,.

Combining the latter equation with (
E.8
) we find the advertised relation between the modular Hamiltonian and the normal-ordered stress-energy tensor, namely

(E.16)

K^{\mathcal{A}^{\cal I}}_{\Omega}=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(U-U_{0}):T_{UU}:_{\Omega}\text{d}U\,.

The one-sided modular Hamiltonian (
E.9
) are obtained by restricting the time integral of (
E.16
) to the spacetime domain corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{I}^{\cal I}
i.e. in the case at hand the region
U>U_{0}
, see (
E.12
).

This proof can be immediately extended to the four-dimensional case, and to any notion of time. In particular, if we start with the
\kappa_{l}
-vacuum states defined from the choice of times (
3.69
), one can apply the previous analysis to each sector
l
and immediately find the expression (
4.59
).

Dual GSL from the Minkowski vacuum

If there is no black hole,
{\cal I}^{+}_{R}
is a complete characteristic data surface for free massless fields. As emphasized in Section
3
, the natural state to consider for massless fields is the Minkowski vacuum
\omega_{M}
attached to the inertial (affine) time
u:=u_{+}
. Further details on its properties -most notably its symmetry structure- and on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{0_{M}}
obtained as the GNS representation induced by

3.3

[

To obtain the generalized second law in this set-up, one first has to restrict the vacuum state
\ket{0_{M}}
to a subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\cal I}\subset\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\cal I}
attached to the region
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\cal I}=(u_{i},+\infty)\times S^{2}
. The one-sided modular Hamiltonian of the Minkowski vacuum associated to the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\cal I}
is given by

(F.1)

K_{0_{M}}^{\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\cal I}}=2\pi\int_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\cal I}}(u-u_{i}):T_{uu}:_{0_{M}}\text{d}u\wedge\epsilon_{S}=2\pi\int_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\cal I}}(u-u_{i})T_{uu}\text{d}u\wedge\epsilon_{S}

that is the one-sided boost energy. It can be shown that the integral boost energy is related to the variation of the renormalized area
\bar{A}

[
53
,
112
]

v=v_{0}
. Its spacelike cross sections are perpendicular to the normal
\boldsymbol{n}=\partial_{v}
which points towards a fixed angular direction
x^{A}
on the celestial sphere. Taking the limit
v_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, the Rindler plane approaches, in the conformal compactification, a single null generator of
{\cal I}^{+}_{R}
associated with the chosen angle
x^{A}
.

114

[

In this limit, all outgoing radiation reaching
{\cal I}^{+}_{R}

x^{A}
eventually crosses the corresponding null Rindler horizon. The evolution of its cross-sectional area is therefore governed by the local null Raychaudhuri equation, which can be used to relate the accumulated energy flux to the variation of the renormalized area

(F.2)

\partial_{u}^{2}\bar{a}(x^{A})=-8\pi T_{uu}(x^{A})\epsilon_{S},\qquad\bar{a}(x^{A})=a(x^{A})-a_{0}(x^{A})

at first order, where
a

x^{A}

(F.3)

\partial_{u}^{2}\bar{A}=-8\pi\int_{S}T_{uu}\epsilon_{S}\qquad\bar{A}=-\int_{S}\bar{a}(x^{A})

{\cal I}^{+}_{R}

that is exactly similar to (
2.19

\Delta\left(\frac{\bar{A}}{4G}+S\right)\geq 0
