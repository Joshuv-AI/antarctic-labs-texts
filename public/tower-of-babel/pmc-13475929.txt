Early Mobilization and Rehabilitation Practices of Adult Patients Admitted to the ICU in Japan: A 1-Day Multicenter Point-Prevalence Study
Authors: Shinya Matsushima, Tadayoshi Nonoyama, Ryosuke Matsuki, Saori Miyagishima, Masatoshi Hanada, Hiroki Sato, Yorihide Yanagita, Shin Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Early mobilization (EM) in the ICU mitigates post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and improves functional outcomes. However, comprehensive national data on mobilization practices and patient-level barriers in Japan’s super-aged society remain limited.OBJECTIVES:To describe current mobilization and rehabilitation practices, the prevalence of EM, and factors associated with remaining in bed among adult ICU patients in Japan.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:This nationwide, multicenter, cross-sectional, 1-day point-prevalence study was conducted on February 20, 2024, across ICUs in Japan. All adult patients (18 yr or older) physically present in the ICU between 00:00 and 14:00 were included.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:The highest mobilization level was assessed using the ICU Mobility Scale (IMS). Out-of-bed mobilization was defined as IMS greater than or equal to 3, and EM as IMS greater than or equal to 3 within 48 hours of ICU admission. Patient characteristics, treatments, and perceived barriers were extracted from medical records. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with remaining in bed.RESULTS:Data were collected from 81 ICUs in 72 hospitals, and 693 patients were included in the analysis (median age, 72 yr; 68% 65 yr or older). Of these, 269 patients (38.8%) were receiving mechanical ventilation. Out-of-bed mobilization occurred in 42.6% of patients overall and in 17.8% of mechanically ventilated patients. EM within two days of ICU admission was achieved in 44.7% of patients overall and in 13.6% of mechanically ventilated patients. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that mechanical devices, particularly mechanical ventilation with an endotracheal tube, vasopressor use, sedative and analgesic administration, and coma were independently associated with a higher likelihood of remaining in bed. Conversely, the presence of delirium and higher nutritional intake were associated with a higher likelihood of achieving out-of-bed mobilization. Subjective assessments identified pathologic and psychoneurological factors as the predominant barriers.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:Despite advanced patient age, mobilization and EM rates in Japanese ICUs were comparable to those reported internationally. However, patients at the highest risk for PICS, particularly those requiring advanced organ support or intensive sedation, were the least likely to achieve out-of-bed mobilization. These findings highlight a persistent clinical dilemma and suggest the need for tailored strategies to safely expand mobilization among the most vulnerable ICU populations in super-aged societies.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the prevalence of early mobilization (EM) and out-of-bed activity in ICUs in Japan, a super-aged society, and which patients are more likely to remain in bed?

Findings: In this nationwide, multicenter, cross-sectional point-prevalence study of 693 ICU patients (median age, 72 yr), out-of-bed mobilization (ICU Mobility Scale ≥ 3) occurred in 42.6% overall and in 17.8% of mechanically ventilated patients. EM within 2 days was achieved in 44.7% of patients overall and in 13.6% of ventilated patients. Mechanical devices, intensive sedation, and physiologic instability were independently associated with remaining in bed.

Meaning: Despite advanced patient age, out-of-bed mobilization was feasible in Japanese ICUs; however, patients at the highest risk for post-intensive care syndrome, particularly those requiring advanced organ support or intensive sedation, were the least likely to mobilize, highlighting a persistent clinical dilemma in ICU rehabilitation.

Critically ill patients often have a range of systemic impairments, including but not limited to muscle weakness due to immobility and deconditioning in the ICU (1). As a result, an increasing number of patients experience severe and prolonged functional impairments after ICU discharge (2, 3). These impairments, encompassing physical, cognitive, and psychologic domains, are collectively referred to as post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) (4). PICS imposes a substantial burden not only on patients but also on their families and the healthcare system, highlighting the urgent need for effective preventive strategies.

Early mobilization (EM) and rehabilitation during ICU stays have gained widespread recognition as essential measures to prevent or mitigate PICS (5–9). Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of EM and rehabilitation (10). Although international guidelines have supported early mobilization (11, 12), the recent publication of the Japanese Clinical Practice Guidelines for Rehabilitation in Critically Ill Patients 2023 highlights a growing national recognition of its importance (13).

International point-prevalence studies have reported ICU mobilization rates and barriers (14–25). Japan has the highest proportion of older adults globally, with 29.3% of the population 65 years or older (26). Given the higher prevalence of frailty and sarcopenia in older populations (15), demographic factors may influence both ICU mobilization rates and perceived barriers.

Despite the clinical relevance of EM, no comprehensive national survey has been conducted to assess its status or associated barriers to mobilization in Japanese ICUs. Such a study is essential to better understand the unique challenges faced in the Japanese context and to inform future strategies for improving ICU care. Therefore, we conducted a 1-day, multicenter point-prevalence study to evaluate mobilization and rehabilitation practices across Japan. A point-prevalence approach allows for efficient nationwide data collection and facilitates direct comparison with international studies through the same methodology. The primary objective of this study was to assess the highest level of mobilization achieved in patients admitted to the ICU as defined by the validated IMS (27, 28). The secondary objectives were to identify and characterize the perceived barriers to mobilization.

METHODS

Design and Setting

This was a retrospective, cross-sectional, multicenter study of adult critical care mobilization and rehabilitation practices across Japan on a single weekday—February 20, 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics review committee of the Kyorin University School of Medicine (No. 2023-49-1), and informed consent was waived. All procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible ICUs were acute care units in Japan providing care for mechanically ventilated adults. Participating ICUs were recruited through professional networks and national mailing lists. A standardized Japanese-language survey, developed based on previous point-prevalence studies (14–20), was distributed to consenting sites. Similar point-prevalence methodology has also been applied in international pediatric critical care rehabilitation studies, including the Prevalence of Acute Rehabilitation for Kids in the PICU investigations (29). The questionnaire was pretested among co-investigators to ensure clarity and feasibility. Data were submitted anonymously via a secure system, and institutional approval was obtained at participating sites when required. To minimize potential practice modification, the exact survey date was not disclosed in advance.

Patient Selection

We included all adult patients 18 years and older who were physically present in the ICU at any time between 00:00 and 14:00 on February 20, 2024, in ICUs where the specific ICU management fee under the Japanese healthcare system was applied. Patients admitted to the ICU after 14:00 were excluded to minimize potential bias arising from reduced staffing and workflow constraints during late afternoon and night shifts, which could affect the feasibility of mobilization independent of patient condition. The time window was used solely to define the study population.

For included patients, mobilization and rehabilitation activities were assessed over the full 24-hour period by retrospective chart review. Pediatric patients and those admitted for non-clinical reasons, such as bed control, were excluded. Patients with conditions potentially limiting mobilization, including limb amputation, coma, preexisting paralysis, end-of-life status, or brain death, were retained to reflect real-world practice, and these conditions were documented.

Survey of Hospital and ICU Characteristics

Hospital and ICU characteristics included 1) hospital type (university hospital, community hospital, and other), and 2) ICU type (medical-surgical mixed, emergency, medical, surgical, coronary care unit or/and cardiac-ICU, stroke care unit or/and neuro-ICU). Emergency ICU, which is a specialized ICU within the emergency department in Japan, where critically ill patients are admitted directly from the emergency department for early resuscitation and intensive management before being transferred to the general ICU or ward.

Survey of Patients’ Mobility Level and Barriers to Mobilization

The level of patient mobility was assessed using the ICU Mobility Scale (IMS), a 1-point ordinal scale that indicates the highest level of mobility achieved on a given day. An IMS of 0 indicates passively lying in bed, and an IMS of 10 signifies walking independently by at least 5 meters without an aid (27) (Supplemental table S1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B667). Out-of-bed mobilization was defined per Hodgson and colleagues’ international consensus paper as “any activity where the patient sits over the edge of the bed (dangling), stands, walks, marches on the spot or sits out of bed” (30). In this study, out-of-bed mobilization was defined as an IMS of 3 or higher to ensure comparability with other international studies (17, 18, 20). In addition, EM is often defined as mobility out of bed within 48 hours of ICU admission (7, 31–33). Therefore, an additional analysis was conducted including only patients whose length of ICU stay was two days or less, and EM was considered to have been achieved when the IMS was 3 or higher.

If the patient remained in bed with an IMS score of less than 3, participants reviewed the medical records to identify the most significant subjective barrier to mobilization, as assessed by the bedside nurse and/or physical therapist. The barrier was selected from a predefined list of items (Supplemental table S2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B667) based on clinical judgment, reflecting the bedside team’s subjective assessment rather than objective patient characteristics and environment-related factors. In addition, we examined the medical records to investigate rehabilitation activities conducted in bed irrespective of whether out-of-bed mobilization was implemented, such as range of motion exercises, resistance training, etc. Investigators were instructed to select and record one applicable barrier for every patient, ensuring that no cases were left unclassified. Missing data were minimized by requiring completion of all survey items before submission.

Survey of Patient Characteristics

The patient-related characteristics collected included age, sex, BMI, length of ICU stay, primary condition leading to ICU admission, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, presence of preadmission frailty (clinical frailty scale > 4). The SOFA score was obtained at the time of ICU admission, in addition, data were collected on 1) the presence of mechanical devices (mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, intra-aortic balloon pump, percutaneous ventricular assist device, continuous renal replacement therapy), 2) medication administration (muscle relaxants, sedatives/analgesics, vasopressors), 3) arousal state and delirium (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale, Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU, Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist), and 4) nutrition intake (energy intake, and protein intake). These data were extracted from medical records as documented on the same day that the mobility level was assessed.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as frequencies and proportions for categorical data and means (sds) and medians (25th–75th percentiles [interquartile ranges]) for continuous variables. We also presented the mobility levels and barriers to mobilization on specified days, specifically for patients with mechanical ventilation, to account for the impact of mechanical devices.

Next, we classified the patients into two groups based on a review of the actual distribution of data from the study cohort for the IMS: “remained in bed” (levels 0–2) or “mobilized out of bed” (levels 3–10), and we compared patient characteristics between the two groups. Statistical differences were calculated via Student t test, the Kruskal–Wallis test, Fisher exact test, or the χ2 test.

Finally, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with remaining in bed. The dependent variable remained in bed (levels 0–2), and independent variables included treatment- and condition-related factors of interest, such as the use of mechanical devices, medication administration, arousal state and delirium, and nutrition intake. To account for potential confounding, the models were adjusted for patient characteristics and illness severity, including age, severity of illness, and ICU length of stay. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. The results of the multivariable analyses were visually summarized using forest plots to facilitate interpretation of the magnitude and direction of associations.

All p values were two-sided, and p values of 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

ICU Characteristics

Data were collected from 81 ICUs across 72 hospitals. When a hospital contained multiple ICUs (e.g., medical, surgical, or emergency), each ICU was treated as a separate unit in this analysis. (Fig. 1). Approximately half were ICUs located in university hospitals (45.7% [37/81]), followed by community hospitals (30.9% [25/81]) and others (23.4% [19/81]). Most of the participating ICUs were medical-surgical mixed ICUs (61.7% [50/81]). A total of 788 adult patients were present in the participating ICUs during the survey period. Among them, 9 patients who were temporarily admitted for bed control or other nonpathological reasons and 43 patients with missing data were excluded, resulting in 693 patients included in the final analysis. No patients were classified as being at end-of-life status or as having brain death while awaiting organ donation at the time of data collection.

Study flowchart.

Patients’ Mobility Level

Figure 2 shows the results of the IMS on the specified date. The prevalence rates of out-of-bed mobilization among all patients and patients with mechanical ventilation were 42.6% (295 of 693) and 17.8% (48 of 269), respectively. Among all patients, 29.1% (202 of 693) engaged in mobilization activities, including sitting, standing, and marching in place (levels 3–6), whereas 13.4% (93 of 693) engaged in walking (levels 7–10). There was no significant difference in the distribution of IMS levels between older adults (65 yr or older) and younger patients (less than 65 y) (Supplemental table S3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B667). On the other hand, in patients with mechanical ventilation, the majority were only able to sit, stand, or march, with only two patients able to walk, one of whom was receiving ventilation via a tracheostomy tube rather than an endotracheal tube. The median (interquartile range) length of ICU stay among all patients was 3 days (1–9) (a histogram of ICU stay duration is shown in Supplemental Fig. S4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B667). Notably, of the 300 patients within two days of ICU admission, 134 (44.7%) received EM. Even among patients receiving mechanical ventilation within two days of ICU admission, nine out of 66 patients (13.6%) received EM.

The ICU Mobility Scale (IMS) on the specified date.

Figure 3 shows the IMS scores for patients with and without mechanical ventilation, categorized by length of ICU stay (0–2 d, 3–7 d, and > 7 d). Among patients receiving mechanical ventilation, there was no significant difference in the proportion who mobilized out of bed (IMS ≥ 3) across different ICU stay durations (χ2 test, p = 0.29). However, among patients without mechanical ventilation, the proportion of those who mobilized out of bed significantly increased with longer ICU stays (χ2 test, p = 0.04).

A comparison between the group of patients who remained in bed and those who mobilized out-of-bed revealed no significant differences in age, sex, or body mass index (BMI). However, regarding the reason for ICU admission, the mobilized out-of-bed group had a greater proportion of patients admitted after elective surgery and a lower proportion of those with neurologic diseases and trauma/burns (p < 0.05) (Table 1). In addition, the mobilized out-of-bed group had lower APACHE II and SOFA scores, suggesting less severe illness, and a lower proportion of patients with preadmission frailty than the remaining in bed group (p < 0.05). In terms of nutritional therapy, the mobilized out-of-bed group consumed more energy and protein on the specified days than the remaining in bed group (p < 0.05). On the other hand, the remaining in bed group had a greater proportion of patients with mechanical devices, a higher rate of medication administration, lower Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale scores, and a lower proportion of patients without delirium than the mobilized out-of-bed group. (Table 2).

To identify factors independently associated with remaining in bed, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed comparing patients who remained in bed (IMS 0–2) with those who achieved out-of-bed mobilization (IMS ≥ 3). After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, ICU length of stay, reason for ICU admission, illness severity, and several condition-related factors were independently associated with remaining in bed. Specifically, mechanical ventilation, particularly via an endotracheal tube, was associated with a higher likelihood of remaining in bed. In addition, the presence of certain mechanical devices, administration of sedative medications, and coma were associated with a higher likelihood of remaining in bed. Conversely, the presence of delirium and higher nutritional intake were associated with a higher likelihood of achieving out-of-bed mobilization. The Rehabilitation Activities Conducted in Bed

The rehabilitation activities conducted in bed are shown in Supplemental table S6 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B667). The most frequently conducted activity was range of motion exercise (42.1% [292/693]), followed by chest physiotherapy, which included postural changes and sputum clearance assistance (14.6% [101/693]), and resistance training (14.3% [99/693]). Only a small number of cases used neuromuscular electrical stimulation (1.7% [12/693]) and in-bed cycling (0.3% [2/693]).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first 1-day, multicenter point-prevalence study conducted in East Asia, including Japan, to comprehensively characterize ICU mobilization and rehabilitation practices. In this nationwide cohort, more than 40% of ICU patients achieved out-of-bed mobilization, with EM feasible even among mechanically ventilated patients (14, 15, 20). Furthermore, EM within the initial 48 hours was achieved in 44.7% of patients whose ICU length of stay was two days or less, and even among patients receiving mechanical ventilation, 13.6% achieved EM. Taken together, these findings indicate that out-of-bed mobilization, including EM, is feasible in a substantial proportion of critically ill patients, even in a super-aged ICU population, and that age alone should not be considered a barrier to mobilization.

These findings may partly reflect structural characteristics of the Japanese healthcare system (34). In Japan, the universal health insurance system includes a financial incentive scheme that provides additional reimbursement for ICUs implementing structured early rehabilitation programs involving multidisciplinary collaboration and dedicated rehabilitation staff (32, 33). In addition, the number of physical therapists per population is relatively high compared with that in many other countries (35). However, given variability in illness severity and case mix, caution is warranted when interpreting mobilization rates across international settings. At the same time, despite increasing international recognition of EM and publication of multiple clinical practice guidelines over the past decade, point-prevalence studies conducted across different countries have continued to report relatively low mobilization rates among mechanically ventilated patients and remarkably similar barriers to mobilization (14–20). This consistency across different national healthcare systems and over time may suggest that one of the remaining challenges in ICU EM lies not only in generating evidence, but also in translating evidence into routine practice.

Beyond describing overall mobilization rates, our study provides new insight by identifying treatment-related factors, including device use and sedation practices, that are independently associated with remaining in bed using multivariable analyses. These results highlight a persistent clinical dilemma in ICU practice in which patients at the highest risk for PICS, often those requiring advanced organ support or intensive sedation, are also the least likely to achieve out-of-bed mobilization.

In multivariable analysis, mechanical devices, including circulatory support devices such as intra-aortic balloon pumps and mechanical ventilation, were independently associated with a higher likelihood of remaining in bed. Among mechanically ventilated patients, the presence of an endotracheal tube was a particularly restrictive factor for out-of-bed mobilization. These findings are consistent with previous studies focusing on mechanically ventilated patients, which have reported lower rates of out-of-bed mobilization and identified endotracheal intubation as a major barrier to mobilization (16, 18, 19).

In addition to mechanical devices, physiologic instability, which included the need for vasopressor support, the administration of analgesic and sedative medications, and states of coma, emerged as major limiting factors for mobilization. Notably, this pattern was also reflected in the subjective barriers to mobilization identified in our study, with pathologic and psychoneurological factors being most frequently cited. These findings align with prior studies reporting that such physiologic and neurologic barriers are the main reasons for delayed mobilization in mechanically ventilated patients (16, 17, 19). Importantly, although sedation often reflects illness severity and the need for physiologic stabilization, sedation practice itself may also represent a potentially modifiable barrier to mobilization. In routine ICU practice, the depth of sedation may be influenced not only by physiologic derangement but also by provider preferences, safety concerns, workflow constraints, and ICU culture. Greater adherence to Society of Critical Care Medicine clinical practice guidelines (36) and the ABCDEF bundle (37) may facilitate lighter sedation strategies and improve the feasibility of mobilization, even among mechanically ventilated patients. These findings suggest that optimizing sedation management may represent one of the most clinically actionable strategies for expanding EM practices in critically ill patients.

Randomized controlled trials, including those by Schweickert et al (5), Schaller et al (8), and Patel et al (38), have demonstrated that EM within 48–72 hours of ICU admission improves physical function and reduces delirium, even among mechanically ventilated patients. However, these trials enrolled carefully selected patients who met strict safety criteria. In routine clinical practice, many patients in the hyperacute phase remain physiologically unstable, requiring vasopressors, deep sedation, or advanced organ support, rendering mobilization unsafe or infeasible. As a result, patients at the highest risk for PICS are often those for whom mobilization is most restricted. This discrepancy underscores a persistent clinical dilemma in ICU rehabilitation: the patients who may benefit most from EM are also those least likely to receive it.

Therefore, although EM should be actively pursued whenever safety criteria are met, our findings suggest that a subset of critically ill patients may require a more individualized and staged approach to the timing and progression of mobilization. Given that skeletal muscle atrophy can progress by approximately 2% per day of immobility (39), optimizing sedation strategies, ventilator management, and hemodynamic stabilization to facilitate safe mobilization remains a critical goal in the prevention of PICS.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation has been reported to reduce ICU-acquired weakness (40, 41). Despite these reported benefits, their implementation in our cohort was minimal, underscoring a gap between emerging evidence and routine practice. Broader integration of such adjunctive strategies may be particularly relevant for patients unable to achieve out-of-bed mobilization.

A notable finding of this study is that the presence of delirium was identified as a factor associated with a higher likelihood of achieving out-of-bed mobilization in the multivariable analysis. Recent evidence suggests that EM in the ICU is effective in preventing and alleviating delirium, and it is therefore possible that patients with delirium were preferentially mobilized as part of non-pharmacological delirium management strategies.

Similarly, higher nutritional intake was identified as a factor associated with a higher likelihood of achieving out-of-bed mobilization in the multivariable analysis. Although the present study was not designed to determine causal relationships, these findings raise the possibility that patients selected for active mobilization may simultaneously receive more intensive supportive care, including nutritional optimization. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the temporal and causal relationships among mobilization, delirium management, and nutritional therapy in critically ill patients.

This study has several limitations. First, participation was voluntary and included approximately 10% of Japanese ICUs, raising the possibility of selection bias and limited generalizability. Second, data were collected on a single weekday, when mobilization practices may differ from weekends. In addition, defining out-of-bed mobilization as IMS greater than or equal to 3 may have included low-level assisted activities, such as tilt-table use or passive standing, potentially leading to overestimation of mobilization rates.

Third, the single-day, cross-sectional design precluded assessment of rehabilitation practices across different phases of ICU stay. Fourth, because the survey was conducted in 2024, ICU mobilization and rehabilitation practices may have evolved over time with increasing dissemination of rehabilitation guidelines and changes in sedation and rehabilitation strategies, potentially limiting the applicability of these findings to current ICU practice. Finally, reliance on medical record documentation may have introduced measurement bias, although prior studies demonstrate substantial agreement between documented and directly observed out-of-bed mobilization (42).

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide 1-day point-prevalence study of 693 adult ICU patients in Japan, more than 40% achieved out-of-bed mobilization, and EM within 48 hours was feasible even among elderly patients and a subset of those receiving mechanical ventilation. These mobilization rates were comparable to those reported in international studies despite Japan’s super-aged ICU population. However, mechanical ventilation, particularly via an endotracheal tube, along with physiologic instability requiring vasopressors, sedation, or coma, emerged as major barriers to mobilization. As a result, patients at the highest risk for PICS were also those least likely to mobilize out of bed. These findings underscore a persistent clinical dilemma in ICU rehabilitation and highlight the need for individualized, safety-focused strategies to extend mobilization to the most vulnerable critically ill patients.
