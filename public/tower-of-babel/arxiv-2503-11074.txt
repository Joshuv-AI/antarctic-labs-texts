Exploring the Necessity of Reasoning in LLM-based Agent Scenarios
Xueyang Zhou, Guiyao Tie, Guowen Zhang, Weidong Wang, Zhigang Zuo, Di Wu, Duanfeng Chu, Pan Zhou, Neil Zhenqiang Gong Lichao Sun

Abstract
The rise of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) signifies a paradigm shift toward advanced computational reasoning. Yet, this progress disrupts traditional agent frameworks, traditionally anchored by execution-oriented Large Language Models (LLMs). To explore this transformation, we propose the LaRMA framework, encompassing nine tasks across Tool Usage, Plan Design, and Problem Solving, assessed with three top LLMs (e.g., Claude3.5-sonnet) and five leading LRMs (e.g., DeepSeek-R1). Our findings address four research questions: LRMs surpass LLMs in reasoning-intensive tasks like Plan Design, leveraging iterative reflection for superior outcomes; LLMs excel in execution-driven tasks such as Tool Usage, prioritizing efficiency; hybrid LLM-LRM configurations, pairing LLMs as actors with LRMs as reflectors, optimize agent performance by blending execution speed with reasoning depth; and LRMs’ enhanced reasoning incurs higher computational costs, prolonged processing, and behavioral challenges, including overthinking and fact-ignoring tendencies. This study fosters deeper inquiry into LRMs’ balance of deep thinking and overthinking, laying a critical foundation for future agent design advancements.

Method

LaRMA
is an exploratory study delving into the role of reasoning capabilities in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) within agent-based scenarios, aiming to reveal their impact on performance across diverse task types and paradigms. As depicted in Figure
2
, this investigation unfolds through three key phases:
Task Segmentation for Agent Capabilities
, which dissects agent tasks into distinct dimensions to probe reasoning demands in tool usage, plan design, and problem-solving;
Selection for Generic Agent Paradigms
, which explores the ReAct and Reflexion paradigms to uncover how reasoning influences interactive and reflective task outcomes; and
Performance Assessment with Different LLMs and LRMs
, which examines a variety of LLMs and LRMs to elucidate reasoning’s practical significance using multidimensional metrics. These phases collectively form a cohesive framework to explore the contributions and constraints of reasoning in agent systems.

2.1
Step1: Task segmentation for Agent Capabilities

To explore the role of reasoning in agent performance, we categorize tasks into three primary domains, each subdivided into specific sub-tasks to systematically investigate reasoning demands.
Tool Usage

(
Li et al. 2023a
)
encompasses: 1)
Tool Awareness
, assessing whether models can proactively recognize the need for external tools when internal knowledge proves insufficient, avoiding over-reliance on potentially flawed parametric knowledge that may lead to hallucinations; 2)
Tool Selection
, testing the ability to accurately identify and select the most suitable tool from a set of options when external assistance is required; and 3)
Tool Capability
, examining if models can not only choose the correct tool but also generate precise parameters for its effective execution.
Plan Design

(
Valmeekam et al. 2023
)
includes: 1)
Plan Generation
, exploring the production of logically coherent and executable action sequences based on objectives and constraints; 2)
Plan Execution
, analyzing inference of state transitions post-execution; and 3)
Re-plan
, investigating dynamic plan adjustments in response to unforeseen events.
Problem Solving

(
Shridhar et al. 2021
;
Yao et al. 2023a
;
Yang et al. 2018
)
comprises: 1)
Real-World
, tackling tasks in simulated environments; 2)
Web Shopping
, focusing on product selection per user instructions; and 3)
Knowledge QA
, probing complex question answering through retrieval and reasoning.

2.2
Step2: Selection for Generic Agent Paradigms

In this phase, we investigate reasoning’s influence by selecting two predominant paradigms to examine its role across distinct interaction and reflection modes within agent scenarios.
ReAct

(
Yao et al. 2023b
)
enables problem-solving through real-time environmental engagement, prompting models to interleave task-related verbal reasoning traces with specific actions, fostering dynamic reasoning to create, maintain, and adjust high-level action plans while receiving immediate feedback from the environment. Formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)
(
Lauri et al. 2023
)
, this process requires iterative strategy refinement via multi-step interactions, testing a model’s capacity to integrate reasoning with decision-making under uncertainty. In contrast,
Reflexion

(
Shinn et al. 2023
)
leverages linguistic reflection to enhance performance, transforming binary or scalar feedback into detailed textual critiques that guide subsequent attempts, offering explicit directives for improvement. This approach demands robust self-evaluation and reflective reasoning to produce meaningful adjustments, iteratively boosting task outcomes through semantic guidance. Together, these paradigms provide complementary frameworks to explore how reasoning shapes agent behavior in interactive versus reflective contexts, revealing its adaptability across operational demands.

2.3
Step3: Performance Assessment with different LLMs and LRMs

This step explores reasoning’s practical implications by analyzing LLMs and LRMs across the identified tasks and paradigms, using a multidimensional approach to assess its impact on agent efficacy. Integrating ReAct’s real-time interactive dynamics with Reflexion’s iterative reflective process, we examine performance variations in
Tool Usage
,
Plan Design
, and
Problem Solving
, seeking to understand reasoning’s contribution to success. Our evaluation splits into two facets:
Performance Evaluation
, including 1)
Task Pass Rate
, measuring the correctness of task completion; 2)
Efficiency
, tracking execution time or interaction steps required; and 3)
Cost
, assessing computational resource consumption like memory or power; and
Reasoning Evaluation
, comprising 1)
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) or Reasoning
, analyzing the presence and effectiveness of reasoning traces; 2)
Thought Quality
, evaluating the coherence and relevance of reasoning outputs; and 3)
Thought Length
, gauging the extent of reasoning steps produced. This dual analysis illuminates reasoning’s benefits, limitations, and trade-offs in agent performance across diverse scenarios.

2.4
Research Questions

[RQ1]
How do the performance outcomes of LRM and LLM compare under the ReAct and Reflection paradigms of agent design?

[RQ2]
What is the impact of employing LRM on the efficiency and cost of agents across different task types?

[RQ3]
Does the combination of LLM as an execution component and LRM as a reflection component yield optimal performance in agent systems?

[RQ4]
Is the reasoning process inherent to LRM a necessary component for effective agent functionality?

3.3
Metric

To quantify the effectiveness of LLMs and LRMs across the specified tasks and paradigms, we employ a suite of rigorously defined evaluation metrics that capture distinct facets of agent performance. These metrics are formalized as follows to ensure precise and reproducible measurements.

Accuracy.
Accuracy measures the correctness of task outcomes, reflecting the agent’s ability to achieve successful completions or generate correct responses. It is defined as:

Accuracy=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}(r=1),

where
N
is the number of samples,
r
is the task completion score, and
r=1
indicates success, and
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
serves as an indicator function.

Efficiency.
Efficiency quantifies the temporal or procedural efficiency of task execution, capturing the speed or resourcefulness of the agent’s decision-making process. It is computed as:

Efficiency=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i},

where
T_{i}
is the time spent to complete the
i
th task.

Cost.
Cost assesses the computational resource demands of the agent, providing insight into the token cost of deploying LRMs. It is expressed as:

Cost=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i},

where
t_{i}
represents the number of tokens consumed to complete the
i
th task.

[RQ1]
How do the performance outcomes of LRM and LLM compare under the ReAct and Reflection paradigms of Agent design?

Response:
LRMs generally surpass LLMs in both paradigms, excelling in reasoning-heavy tasks like Plan Design, while LLMs hold an edge in execution-focused Tool Usage. Reflexion boosts performance more than ReAct, with LRMs converging faster and LLMs benefiting from prolonged iteration, reflecting distinct reasoning strengths.

4.2
Results for Efficiency and Cost

While LRMs typically surpass LLMs in accuracy across a range of tasks, our exploration uncovers a significant trade-off: LRMs often entail greater computational costs and prolonged processing times due to their deep reasoning mechanisms. To investigate this dynamic systematically, we assessed efficiency and cost metrics—namely token consumption and execution time—across three datasets. Figure
4
displays kernel density estimation plots that reveal the probability distributions of token usage and time expenditure for both LLMs and LRMs across these domains. The findings show that LRMs consistently produce elevated token counts, particularly in
Plan Design
tasks, where their multi-step reasoning yields denser intermediate outputs, with median token usage notably higher than that of LLMs. Similarly, execution times for LRMs extend considerably in
Problem Solving
tasks, averaging longer than LLMs, a consequence of the computational load from iterative reasoning traces. By contrast,
Tool Usage
tasks exhibit more comparable token distributions between the two model types, likely reflecting their dependence on execution rather than extensive reasoning. Intriguingly, LRMs such as DeepSeek-R1 demonstrate swift convergence in accuracy, yet this comes at the price of increased resource demands, whereas LLMs like GPT-4o preserve efficiency in simpler tasks. These observations highlight a delicate balance between performance enhancements and resource costs in agent scenarios, suggesting that deploying LRMs requires thoughtful consideration in contexts limited by resources or time.

What is the impact of employing LRM on the efficiency and cost of Agents across different task types?

This trade-off calls for strategic deployment tailored to task complexity and resource availability.

To investigate how LLMs and LRMs adapt to differing levels of contextual guidance, we explored their performance transitions from a Few-shot setting, where models are provided with a handful of examples, to a Zero-shot setting, where no prior examples are given. This analysis spanned
Problem Solving
datasets to gauge robustness and reasoning dependence under these conditions. The results, unveiled in Table
5

These findings suggest that while LRMs harness reasoning to navigate Zero-shot hurdles, task complexity and model design play pivotal roles in adaptability, with LLMs proving more susceptible to the loss of Few-shot scaffolding.

4.5
Dilemmas Faced when LRM as Agent

Figure 5:
Exploration of Overthinking Rates for DeepSeek-R1 and Claude3.7-sonnet.

Deepthinking or Overthinking?
During our experiments, we observed that LRMs like DeepSeek often exhibit prolonged thinking durations and excessive token consumption. To explore this phenomenon, we introduced an "Overthinking Rate" metric
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
to discern whether LRMs engage in deep thinking or veer into overthinking. We tested DeepSeek-R1 and Claude3.7 across nine tasks, with results illustrated in Figure
5
. This investigation revealed three key insights: 1) DeepSeek-R1 displays a stronger tendency toward overthinking compared to Claude3.7, suggesting a greater propensity for unnecessary deliberation; 2) in terms of thinking length, Claude3.7 consistently surpasses DeepSeek-R1 across all tasks except the two
Tool Usage
subtasks (Awareness and Selection), indicating differing reasoning depths; 3) DeepSeek-R1 most frequently overthinks in the Web Shopping task, yet it achieves genuine deep thinking in Tool Selection, highlighting task-specific variations in reasoning efficiency. These findings invite further reflection on balancing reasoning depth with computational practicality in agent design. We show some examples about Deepthinking and Overthinking in Appendix
C.2
.

Ignoring Facts.
Our investigation reveals a striking tendency in LRMs when deployed as agents: they often become deeply engrossed in their internal reasoning processes, sidelining the critical need to engage with the external environment
(
Cuadron et al. 2025
)
. Typically, an agent’s interaction with its surroundings is formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)
(
Kumar et al. 2025
)
, where, at each time step
t
, the agent derives its current action from the historical trajectory and secures real observations through direct environmental interaction. Yet, LRMs frequently diverge from this model, opting instead to generate action sequences for both the present and future steps while internally simulating corresponding observations, bypassing the acquisition of authentic environmental facts. This pattern of behavior not only undermines the precision of decision-making but also amplifies the detrimental effects of LRM hallucinations, raising profound questions about their reliability in dynamic, real-world agent contexts. We show some examples of Ignoring Facts in Appendix
D
.

To explore how best to harness these complementary strengths, we conducted experiments pairing LLMs as actors with both LLMs and LRMs as reflectors. The outcomes, detailed in Tables

Our findings reveal that configurations with an LLM (e.g., Claude3.5-sonnet) as the actor and an LRM (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 or Claude3.7-sonnet) as the reflector consistently achieve the highest accuracies, reaching standout peaks like 98.18% in Execution and 96.36% in Re-plan with Claude3.7-sonnet as the reflector. This synergy aligns with expectations: LLMs adeptly manage initial task execution, while LRMs elevate performance through their advanced reasoning during reflection. Pairings of LLMs with LLM reflectors, though competitive, generally trail LRM-reflector setups, particularly in reasoning-heavy subtasks like Re-plan, where accuracies dip to 60–70%. These results underscore the complementary interplay between LLMs and LRMs, pointing to a hybrid approach as a promising avenue for optimizing agent performance in Reflexion-based systems. Related Work

5.1
Existing Agent Paradigms and Applications

Recent advancements in agent decision-making frameworks
(
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Singh et al. 2023
;
Xiao & Wang 2023
;
Zhai et al. 2024
)
have sought to enhance cross-domain adaptability and computational efficiency through a variety of innovative approaches. Foundational models like ReAct
(
Yao et al. 2023b
)
and its offshoot Plan-and-Solve
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
establish structured reasoning-action cycles, leveraging chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
)
to integrate deliberation with execution. Building on these, systems such as BabyAGI and AutoGPT
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
employ recursive task decomposition to tackle complex challenges. The Reflexion framework
(
Shinn et al. 2023
)
introduces a pioneering verbal reinforcement learning approach
(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
, enabling agents to refine decision strategies iteratively by embedding textual feedback into episodic memory buffers. To overcome latency bottlenecks in tool-augmented systems, LLMCompiler
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
utilizes dynamically constructed Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) for parallel action execution, significantly boosting throughput over sequential designs. Contemporary frameworks like MetaGPT
(
Hong et al. 2024
)
and AutoGen
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
explore dynamic role specialization, while TDAG
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
merges real-time task decomposition with agent generation protocols. Emerging tools such as OS-Copilot
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
and AgentScope
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
offer modular interfaces for tool integration. Despite their reliance on large language models’ reasoning capabilities, these frameworks grapple with persistent challenges, including computational overhead
(
Ma et al. 2023
;
Gandhi et al. 2024
)
and fragility in specific scenarios
(
Li et al. 2024e
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Xie et al. 2024
)
, prompting further inquiry within the LaRMA framework.

5.2
Capabilities and Limitations of LRM

A defining feature of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) is their ability to generate explicit intermediate reasoning traces
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
;
Tie et al. 2025
)
, enabling the decomposition of complex problems into transparent, interpretable reasoning chains that enhance structured decision-making. Recent LRMs, including QWQ-32B-Preview
(
Qwen 2024
)
, OpenAI’s o1 and o3 series
(
OpenAI 2024b
)
, DeepSeek series
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025a
;
DeepSeek-AI 2025b
)
, Gemini-2.0
(
DeepMind 2025
)
, and Kimi-v1.5 (long/short-CoT variants)
(
AI 2025
)
, incorporate advanced deep thinking strategies to bolster this capacity. However, studies like
Xu et al. 2025
expose critical limitations: First, LRMs exhibit pronounced structural sensitivity, struggling to generalize across subtle task variations
(
Mirzadeh et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024d
)
; Second, they falter in compositional reasoning, particularly in multi-step tasks
(
Huang & Chang 2023
;
Creswell et al. 2022
;
Zhao & Zhang 2024
;
Fu et al. 2023
)
; and their logical robustness wanes under adversarial perturbations
(
Bao et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, research by
Chen et al. 2024
reveals that LRMs, such as o1, often overthink simple problems, expending excessive computational effort where streamlined execution suffices—a phenomenon our study probes within agent contexts like Tool Usage. Similarly,
Cuadron et al. 2025
investigate how this overthinking disrupts the reasoning-action balance in agentic tasks, amplifying inefficiencies and decision-making errors, which resonates with our observations of LRMs bypassing environmental interactions. Extending these insights, our work within the LaRMA framework provides a systematic, in-depth exploration of LRMs’ strengths and weaknesses in agent paradigms, illuminating their performance trade-offs and behavioral dilemmas. These findings underscore persistent gaps in LRM reasoning architectures, highlighting the urgent need for robust evaluation frameworks and innovative methodologies to align reasoning depth with practical agent functionality.

6
Conclusion

This study makes significant strides in understanding the role of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) within agent systems, offering several key contributions. By introducing the LaRMA framework, we provide a structured lens to explore the interplay between reasoning and execution, illuminating the distinct strengths of LRMs and LLMs across diverse tasks. Our investigation validates the transformative potential of hybrid LLM-LRM configurations, demonstrating their capacity to harmonize efficiency and reasoning depth for superior agent performance. Furthermore, by identifying critical dilemmas—such as overthinking and fact-ignoring tendencies—this work enriches the discourse on LRM design, highlighting the need for balanced architectures that align reasoning prowess with practical deployment. These insights lay a robust groundwork for advancing agent-based systems, guiding future efforts to refine computational efficiency, enhance environmental interaction, and develop adaptive agents capable of navigating complex, real-world scenarios with precision and reliability.

Notably, KnowledgeQA stands out with elevated token consumption across all models, hinting at its complexity. These metrics illuminate the trade-offs between reasoning depth and resource demands, providing a foundational perspective for optimizing agent design.
