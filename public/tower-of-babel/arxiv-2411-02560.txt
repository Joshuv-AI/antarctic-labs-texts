Fast and Robust Information Spreading in the Noisy 𝒫 ​ 𝒰 ​ ℒ ​ ℒ \mathcal{PULL} Model
Niccolò D’Archivio, Amos Korman, Emanuele Natale, Robin Vacus

Abstract
Efficient information spreading in stochastic multi agent systems is a core challenge when communication is noisy, bandwidth limited, and agents lack global coordination. Yet biological systems, including ant colonies and fish schools, routinely overcome these constraints. A small number of informed individuals can reliably guide large, uncoordinated populations using minimal, noisy signals. Motivated by these observations, we study how reliable information dissemination can be achieved in such bio inspired settings. A population of n n agents, each with a binary preference, includes a designated subset of source agents, and the goal is to converge to the majority preference among the sources. In the noisy 𝒫 ​ 𝒰 ​ ℒ ​ ℒ ​ ( h ) \mathcal{PULL}(h) model, each agent observes noisy messages from h h randomly sampled peers in every round. Prior work shows that convergence requires Ω ⁡ ( n / h ) \Omega(n/h) rounds even under favorable conditions. We ask how far we can push simplicity, with no synchronization at the start time and minimal message size, without compromising convergence speed. We present a quasi self-stabilizing protocol using only 2-bit messages that converges from arbitrary initial states despite severe noise and initial asynchrony. It achieves optimal convergence time O ⁡ ( ( n / h ) ​ log ⁡ n ) O((n/h)\log n) with high probability, and in particular O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) time in the snapshot regime h = Θ ⁡ ( n ) h=\Theta(n) . A key subroutine is an even simpler 1-bit protocol assuming simultaneous start, based on a natural two phase listen then amplify mechanism. Together, our results show that simple, biologically inspired protocols can achieve optimal and robust information dissemination even in highly unreliable and uncoordinated systems.

Information Spreading; Noisy Communication; Zealot Consensus; Self-Stabilization; Cooperative Transport; Crazy Ants; Natural Algorithms.

Introduction

Designing artificial multi-agent systems is challenging when agents must achieve distributed coordination and consensus under local, asynchronous, and noisy communication constraints. Yet, nature offers inspiration: for example, in ant colonies and fish schools, a few informed individuals can lead an entire uncoordinated group using minimal, noisy signals
[
31
,
42
,
32
,
19
]
. Further details on the biological motivation are provided in the
Section

12
. This motivates the study of simple, fault-tolerant algorithms for information spreading in settings with unreliable communication and severe bandwidth limits
[
29
,
14
]
.

In the spirit of
opinion dynamics
, designed to model information propagation and consensus formation in multi-agent systems
[
9
,
20
,
21
,
38
,
30
]
, we consider a population of
n
agents, each holding a binary opinion (
0
or
1
). We assume that a subset of agents, called
sources
, hold initial preferences. We define the correct opinion as the one supported by the majority of sources. The goal is for the entire population to converge on this correct opinion. This formulation generalizes the standard rumor spreading problem (where sources agree on the correct opinion) to include majority consensus (where all agents are sources). We refer to this broader problem as
majority rumor spreading
for simplicity.

Noise in communication

To capture random, unpredictable, and noisy communication, we adopt the noisy PULL(
h
) model
[
26
,
29
,
17
,
14
]
. In this model, time proceeds in synchronous rounds, and in each round every agent pulls messages from
h
randomly chosen peers (neighbors) in the network. Each message is drawn from a fixed alphabet
\Sigma
. Crucially, the channel is noisy: the received symbol may be corrupted randomly. We assume the noise is biased, i.e.
it retains the original message with probability slightly greater than
1/|\Sigma|
.
Despite this slight reliability, without further algorithmic design the information can easily get lost in the noise. This raises a central question: How can the agents quickly and reliably agree on the correct opinion under such extreme noise, using small
|\Sigma|
?

One difficulty that arises in the noisy PULL(
h
) model is that sources cannot safely signal the fact that they are sources using a designated bit, since this bit itself would be noisy.
As a consequence, the majority of messages perceived as originating from a source would in fact be artifacts of noise, rather than genuine source messages.
Conversely, without the ability to identify sources, non-sources must somehow extract information from multiple samples, most of which are unreliable.

Notably, prior work established a fundamental time barrier: even under relatively favorable conditions, any protocol requires on the order of
\Omega(n/h)
rounds to spread the information with constant success probability
[
14
]
. In other words, if each agent communicates with only a constant number of others per round (
h=O(1)
), it takes linear time
\Omega(n)
in the population size to reliably propagate a rumor; larger
h
may potentially linearly speed this up, but no algorithm can circumvent this bound.
Our goal is to approach this optimal convergence speed using the smallest alphabet
\Sigma
, and to do so under minimal assumptions about coordination among agents.

Quasi self-stabilization

To model the lack of global coordination, we consider a variant of the self-stabilization framework
[
1
]
.
A self-stabilizing protocol ensures convergence from any initial state—potentially set by an adversary—without assuming synchronized clocks or a common start time. However, the traditional model assumes complete adversarial control over all initial internal states, which may be unnecessarily strong for modeling real systems. We therefore introduce a relaxed version – quasi self-stabilization – that allows reliable access to certain information, such as the size of the population
n
, the distribution of the noise, or whether an agent is a source (see more details in Section
1.2
).

When agents start the execution at different times, several technical challenges arise. First, without synchronous counters, it is difficult to implement phase-based strategies. Early transmissions from sources may occur before some agents have even begun executing the protocol, rendering those messages effectively lost. Moreover, if agents reset their state upon “wake-up,” they risk disrupting ongoing consensus dynamics, since different subsets of the population may be at different logical times. This asynchrony complicates both correctness and efficiency guarantees: protocols must ensure convergence despite arbitrary misalignment of agents’ clocks, while simultaneously tolerating noisy communication.

Previous studies have explored self-stabilizing rumor spreading in the
\mathcal{PULL}
model, focusing on minimizing message size
[
13
,
7
,
37
,
8
]
, but without addressing noisy communication. Conversely, Boczkowski et al.
[
14
]
analyzed the noisy
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model and showed that with any constant-size alphabet, convergence still requires
\tilde{\Omega}(n/h)
rounds, even assuming synchronized start. In this work, we address both dimensions of unreliability: noisy messages and arbitrary, uncoordinated initial configurations.

Main contribution

We answer the above challenge by designing protocols for noisy majority rumor spreading, emphasizing a tiny message alphabet and robustness to communication noise and/or arbitrary start conditions.

Our main result is a quasi self-stabilizing majority rumor spreading algorithm, called SSF, that uses a 4-symbol alphabet (only 2 bits of information per message) and, under the reasonable source regime in which a constant fraction of agents is non-sources, operates correctly from arbitrary initial states: even if agents wake up at different times or start with arbitrary opinions, the system will converge to the truth. It achieves convergence in
O\left(\frac{n}{h}\log n\right)
rounds w.h.p. In the extreme well-connected case of
h=n
, it achieves
O(\log n)
rounds, an exponential improvement over naive gossip. For general
h
, the upper bound matches the
\Omega(n/h)
lower bound up to a logarithmic factor, which could be attributed to high probability guarantee of SSF, versus the constant probability guarantee of the lower bound.

To our knowledge, SSF is the first protocol to solve noisy majority rumor spreading with such a small message alphabet in a quasi self-stabilizing manner, advancing the state of the art in fault-tolerant consensus in multi-agent systems subject to strong noise.

Two points carry most of the technical work. First, the technical master lemma (
Lemma

5
) gives, across the full range of noise levels
\delta
, a precise
\delta
-dependent sample requirement for producing the small bias that majority boosting subsequently amplifies. This estimate is not a routine application of standard concentration or normal-approximation tools such as the Berry–Esseen theorem: both the frequency of informative samples and their conditional bias change between the source-dominated and noise-dominated regimes. Second, for
SSF
we prove that the agents’ weak opinions remain mutually independent even though non-sources transmit their weak opinions. This independence is what makes the subsequent majority amplification valid.

1.1
Overview of key algorithmic ideas

SSF (which is formally described in Sec.
2.2
and Alg.
1
) builds on a simple 1-bit protocol, SF, which assumes a simultaneous start and achieves fast majority rumor spreading through a two-phase process. SSF extends this method to the quasi self-stabilizing setting by embedding lightweight synchronization directly into the protocol, eliminating the need for a global clock.

The two-phase process of SF may be of independent interest. In Phase 1, agents collect
h
samples per round to form a weak opinion—a guess correct with probability slightly above
1/2
. This is achieved by having sources continuously broadcast their bit, while non-sources contribute minimal interference. Weak opinions are computed using only first-hand information—direct observations of other agents—to maintain independence and avoid bias from relayed noise. This is informally further explained in
Section

2.3
and formalized in
Lemmas

5
,
6
and
7
.

In Phase 2, agents share their weak opinions and update by majority rule, amplifying the bias to achieve consensus with high probability. This boosting step resembles mechanisms used in prior works on majority dynamics under noise
[
26
,
29
]
.

For such a procedure to work, it is desirable that all weak-opinions are mutually independent, so that the majority of weak-opinions would be correct with high probability.
However, having non-sources relay information between themselves can cause dependencies. Therefore, in our algorithms, weak-opinions are computed in a way that is oblivious of the information obtained (and the randomness involved) by other non-sources. Hence, in a sense, the weak-opinions are based on “first-hand” information, that is, information that comes directly from the sources, despite the fact that sources are not easily detectable.

The quasi self-stabilizing protocol SSF implicitly cycles through these phases and uses the additional symbols (beyond binary opinions) to coordinate phase transitions and recover from inconsistent states. Instead of relying on an external synchronization subroutine, as in
[
13
]
, which may be difficult to implement in the presence of noise, SSF integrates coordination directly into the message structure. The first bit indicates whether the sender is a source (1) or not (0), while the second bit conveys the weak opinion. Of course, upon observation, these bits may be flipped due to noise.
Agents compute weak opinions by applying a majority vote over the second bits of messages marked as coming from sources, and update their actual opinion, which is kept internally, via a majority over all received second bits, regardless of the value of their first bit. This design allows SSF to converge in near-optimal time, despite both noise and lack of coordination, using only 2-bit messages.

1.2
Problem Definition

Consider a set
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
of agents, where each agent
i
holds a binary opinion
Y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
.
The population contains a subset called
source agents
. Each source agent is initially given a
preference
in
\{0,1\}
(which may differ from its opinion) and will never change it.
We let
s_{1}
(resp.
s_{0}
) denote the number of sources with preference
1
(resp.
0
). The
bias
is defined as
s:=|s_{1}-s_{0}|
, and we require that
s\geq 1
.
The preference supported by the strict majority of sources is called
correct
.
The goal is for all agents, including sources, to eventually adopt the correct preference as their opinion as fast as possible.

Observe that the challenge of retrieving the correct opinion given a certain bias (
s=|s_{0}-s_{1}|
) can be very different depending on the number of sources (
s_{0}+s_{1}
). To illustrate this point, consider two cases: (1)
s_{0}=0
,
s_{1}=1
, and (2)
s_{0}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
,
s_{1}=n-s_{0}
. In Case 1 the system attempts to retrieve a bit from a single source agent, while in Case 2 all agents are sources, making the notion of sources meaningless, and leading to a majority consensus problem.

The Noisy
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model

Agents exchange information over the complete graph
K_{n}
, using an alphabet of messages, termed
\Sigma
, which is assumed to be of constant size. Time proceeds in discrete rounds, where in every round the following happens.

1.

Each agent (source or non-source) chooses a message
\sigma\in\Sigma
to display.

2.

Every agent samples
h
agents in
I
, uniformly at random with replacement. In particular, Agent
i
may sample twice the same agent, and may also sample itself.

3.

Every agent receives a noisy version of the messages displayed by its sampled peers. Formally, we model the noise using a stochastic matrix
N\in\bbR^{|\Sigma|\times|\Sigma|}
, called
noise matrix
, where each row sums to 1 and all entries are non-negative. If Agent
i
samples Agent
j
, displaying a message
\sigma\in\Sigma
, then Agent
i
observes message
\sigma^{\prime}
with probability
N_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
.

4.

Every agent
i
may update its opinion
Y^{(i)}
, and its internal state (that may include counters, and other variables).

The decisions of an agent may depend on all the messages that it received so far, and possibly on the agent’s coin tosses (in the case that the agent’s protocol is probabilistic).
We assume that the random events involved in sampling, noise, and agents’ coin tosses are all independent (across both rounds and agents).
As a standard convention, we say that an event
A
happens with high probability (w.h.p.) if
\mathbb{P}(A)=1-\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2})
.

Definition 1
.

For
\delta\in[0,1/|\Sigma|]
and a (stochastic) noise matrix
N
, we say that

•

N
is
\delta
-lower bounded if
N_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\geq\delta
for every
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
.

•

N
is
\delta
-upper bounded if

N_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\leq\delta,\quad\text{for every }\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma,

(1)

which, in particular, implies that
N_{\sigma,\sigma}\geq 1-(|\Sigma|-1)\delta
.

•

N
is
\delta
-uniform if there is equality in
Equation

1
, i.e.,

N_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=\delta,\quad\text{for every }\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma,

which, in particular, implies
N_{\sigma,\sigma}=1-(|\Sigma|-1)\delta
.

Quasi self-stabilizing setting

We assume that at time
0
(unknown to the agents) an adversary may choose arbitrary values for every mutable local variable. In
SSF
, these variables are the opinion
Y^{(i)}
, the weak opinion
\tilde{Y}^{(i)}
, and the contents and size of the message multiset
M^{(i)}
; in particular, the multiset may initially contain fake samples purportedly gathered before time
0
. More generally, mutable counters, clocks, and phase variables, if present, may also be corrupted independently. Thus, agents need not share a logical starting time.

The problem instance and the read-only protocol parameters are not mutable state. The source set and source preferences may be chosen arbitrarily as part of the instance, but thereafter each agent reliably knows whether it is a source and, if so, its own fixed preference. Agents also have hard-wired access to a constant approximation of
n
and to the noise matrix
N
. While precise knowledge of
N
is required in the general case, for symmetric noise an upper bound on the noise parameter
\delta
suffices
1
1

1

Symmetric noise can arise, for example, due to symmetric environmental conditions, as appears to be the case in cooperative transport by ants (see more details in Section
4
). In general, when considering biological contexts, knowledge assumptions (such as an upper bound on
\delta
) may plausibly reflect information encoded through evolutionary adaptation.
.

We call the setting quasi self-stabilizing because only the mutable state is adversarially corrupted. The protected source role and preference are natural immutable inputs: they define the problem instance and hence the correct preference, rather than representing state generated by the protocol. Similarly, the estimates of
n
and
N
are fixed environmental parameters used to configure the protocol. For any such instance, the preference that is more frequent among sources is called the
correct
preference, and correctness requires every agent to adopt it.

Convergence

We say that a protocol solves the
noisy majority rumor spreading
problem in time
\mathcal{T}
w.h.p if all agents adopt the correct preference as their opinion in at most
\mathcal{T}
rounds w.h.p. Note that with this definition, even the sources whose preference is incorrect must adopt as their opinion the correct preference. We additionally say that a protocol solves the problem in time
\mathcal{T}
in a quasi self-stabilizing manner if, starting with any initial configuration of the mutable state (in the sense described above), all agents adopt the correct preference as their opinion in at most
\mathcal{T}
rounds and retain it for a polynomial number of rounds, say
n^{3}
rounds, w.h.p.

Lower bound on convergence time

Let us first state the lower bound theorem in
[
14
]
. That paper assumed that all sources have the same (and therefore correct) preference, which means, in particular, that the bias
s
is the same as the number of sources.
The main focus therein was the case of pairwise interactions, that is,
h=1
, however their proof, that can be found in
[
15
]
, applies for general

\delta

Theorem 2

(Theorem 4 in
[
15
]
.)
.

Fix a non-source agent
u
and an integer
h
. Any rumor spreading protocol in the noisy
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model with alphabet

and
\delta

\Omega\!\left(\frac{n\delta}{s^{2}h(1-|\Sigma|\,\delta)^{2}}\right)
rounds
in order to guarantee that the opinion of

is correct with probability at least
2/3
.

This lower bound holds even assuming that all parameters, including the number of agents

, the noise matrix
N
, and the number of sources

, are known to the protocol’s designer.

In particular, this theorem implies that for a single source (i.e.,
s=1)
, constant sample size
h
, and constant noise
\delta>0
, the rumor spreading time is
\Omega(n)
, even if correctness is guaranteed with only a constant probability per agent.

[
17
]

showed that for constant parameters as above, if convergence is required w.h.p then the techniques in
[
14
]
can be modified to yield an

lower bound. The authors in
[
17
]
also
present a protocol that is unlikely to be found in nature but achieves rumor spreading in

.

We note that, if a protocol guarantees correct convergence after
\mathcal{T}/h
rounds with a sample size

, then, given enough memory, agents could simulate it with a smaller sample size

h^{\prime}=1
, allowing them to adopt the correct opinion after
\mathcal{T}
rounds (essentially by collecting messages for

\mathcal{T}
in the
\mathcal{PULL}(1)
model, can be extended to a lower bound
\mathcal{T}/h
in the
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model. However, it is unclear whether an upper bound in the
\mathcal{PULL}(1)
model would directly translate to a speed-up of
h
in the
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model.

1.4
Our results

Before stating our results, we note that our modeling choices (see Section
1.2
) are directly motivated by the lower bound in
[
15
]
. As mentioned, that paper established a linear-time barrier of
\Omega(n)
for rumor propagation under random pairwise interactions, concluding that fast information spreading is impossible when communication is noisy, its pattern is completely random and limited to pairwise exchanges. However, it remained unclear which assumptions were fundamentally responsible for this limitation.
Our results clarify this point by isolating the role of sampling: we show that increasing the number of samples per round (while maintaining the other constraints of the model) is sufficient to overcome the linear barrier.

Quasi self-stabilizing protocol

Our main result is a highly efficient quasi self-stabilizing rumor spreading algorithm called SSF, that uses 2-bits per message.

.

Protocols and Analytical Intuition

Here, we present two of our protocols and the intuition behind their construction. A last protocol is deferred to

In

Section

5

\delta
-uniform using results of
[
5

For this purpose,
upon receiving a message

to obtain a new random message
\sigma^{\prime}
, and then run the original protocol on
\sigma^{\prime}
.
The combination of the noise matrix

and the artificial noise ends up being a noise matrix

which is
\delta
-uniform for some suitable
\delta>0
.
A formal definition of this notion of reduction is given in

Definition

16

Theorem

When
|\Sigma|=2

Specifically, showing that uniform noise can be achieved for a general

-uniform matrix is stochastic, so that the strategy can be implemented.

In

Section

3

we consider the setting with uniform noise, presenting two related protocols, Source Filter (SF) and Self-stabilizing Source Filter (SSF).
The former protocol requires agents to start the execution simultaneously at the same time, while the latter is self-stabilizing, at the cost of reduced efficiency and an increased
minimal communication alphabet.
Since both protocols rely on similar ideas, we present their analysis in a unified manner.

2.1
Source Filter (SF)

Here, we describe our fastest protocol, whose pseudo-code is given in

Algorithm

2

The protocol uses a communication alphabet that corresponds to the opinion’s set, namely,
\Sigma:=\{0,1\}
. We refer to

Section

7

to show how the protocol can be adapted to work with a larger alphabet.
The execution is divided into three phases (where the first two phases are symmetric).
The protocol relies on a simultaneous wake-up assumption, which is used to synchronize the clocks of agents.
Each of the first two phases lasts for
\lceil m/h\rceil
rounds.
Since every agent observes
h
samples per round, it collects at least
m
messages in each of the first two phases.

•

Phase 0.
All non-sources display 0, while sources display their preference. In addition, all agents count the number of 1-opinions that they observe during the phase.

•

Phase 1.
All non-sources display 1, while sources display their preference. All agents count the number of 0-opinions they observe during the phase.

Keeping Phases 0 and 1 separate is precisely what allows
SF
to use a 1-bit alphabet. Across the two phases, each non-source contributes once to the 0-background and once to the 1-background, so these contributions cancel in the comparison, whereas every source keeps displaying its preference. A single phase would not provide this cancellation and would require another mechanism to distinguish source information, such as the extra source-flag bit used by
SSF
.
At the end of Phase 1, each agent
i
compares the number of
1
-messages observed in Phase 0, with the number of
0
-messages observed in Phase
1
, and then sets its
weak-opinion

\tilde{Y}^{(i)}
to be the opinion with the greater number (breaking ties randomly), i.e.,
\tilde{Y}^{(i)}:=\mathds{1}\{\texttt{Counter}_{1}>\texttt{Counter}_{0}\}+\mathds{1}\{\texttt{Counter}_{1}=\texttt{Counter}_{0}\}\cdot\text{Bernoulli}(\frac{1}{2})
.
Note that the
0
-messages observed in Phase 0 and the
1
-messages observed in Phase 1 are ignored.

The third phase is devoted to boosting the bias.

•

Majority Boosting phase.
In every round
t
during this phase, each agent displays its opinion
Y_{t}^{(i)}
, which is initially equal to its weak-opinion (computed as above), and then updated from time to time by sampling the opinions of several other agents and taking the majority.
More specifically, the Majority Boosting phase is divided into
10\,\log n+1
sub-phases. At the end of each sub-phase, every agent
i
sets its opinion to be the majority over all messages it received during this sub-phase. All sub-phases except the last one have the same duration, which is chosen in order to allow each agent to gather at least
w=\frac{100}{(1-2\delta)^{2}}
messages per sub-phase. Finally, the last sub-phase lasts long enough for agents to gather at least
m
messages.

By design, the Majority Boosting phase takes
\lceil w/h\rceil\cdot 10\,\log n+\lceil m/h\rceil
rounds, which
is at most
the duration of the first two phases
(
Lemma

9
).
Therefore, the running time of
SF
is
\mathcal{O}(m/h)
rounds.

By the symmetric construction of the algorithm and since the noise is uniform, we expect the number of messages wrongly counted as 1s in Phase
0
, to be the same as the number of messages wrongly counted as 0s in Phase
1
.
Since there are more sources with the correct preference, we also expect that they observe more correct messages originating from sources.
Together, these two observations imply that
\tilde{Y}^{(i)}
is correct with a probability larger than
1/2
.
By setting
m
to be large enough, we can get this probability to be at least
1/2+\sqrt{\log(n)/n}
(see more details in
Section

2.3
), which is sufficiently high to allow the Majority Boosting phase to be efficient and reliable.

In addition, we show that the value of
\tilde{Y}^{(i)}
depends only on the random samples received by Agent
i
, the noise affecting these samples, and the coin tosses used by the agent to break ties. Since all these random variables are mutually independent among agents, so are the weak-opinions.

2.2
Self-stabilizing Source Filter (SSF)

We next present the quasi self-stabilizing protocol SSF, whose pseudo-code is given in

Algorithm

1
.
The protocol uses a communication alphabet of size 4, namely,
\Sigma:=\{0,1\}^{2}
, meaning that each message is simply composed of 2 bits. We refer to

Section

7

to show how the protocol can be adapted to work with a larger alphabet.
In every round
t
, each agent
i
maintains a weak-opinion
\tilde{Y}_{t}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
as well as an opinion
Y_{t}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
that is held internally. The first bit of each message indicates whether or not the sender is a source. For sources, the second bit corresponds to their preference, whereas for non-sources, it indicates their weak-opinion.
In addition, each agent
i
holds a multi-set in her memory, whose content in round
t
is denoted by
M_{t}^{(i)}
. We design
M_{t}^{(i)}
such that it can store
m^{\prime}
messages received by the agent, with

m^{\prime}=\frac{\delta n\log n}{(1-4\delta)^{2}}+n.

In every round, Agent
i
adds all messages received in this round to
M_{t}^{(i)}
. If this results in
|M_{t}^{(i)}|
exceeding
m^{\prime}
(which happens every
\lceil m^{\prime}/h\rceil
rounds), the agent updates its opinion and weak-opinion based on the content of the multi-set, and then empties it. Specifically,
on such a round, which we refer to as an
update round
for Agent
i
, the following occurs:

•

the new weak-opinion of Agent
i
, namely,
\tilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}
, is computed as the majority over the second bits of all messages in
M_{t}^{(i)}
whose first bit is 1 (breaking ties uniformly at random).
Recall that in absence of noise, such a bit would correspond to the preference of a source agent.

•

the new opinion of Agent
i
, namely
Y_{t+1}^{(i)}
, is computed as the majority over the second bits of all messages in
M_{t}^{(i)}
(breaking ties uniformly at random).

Note that in a self-stabilizing framework, the multi-sets
\{M_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i\in I}
are initialized arbitrarily. In particular, they may start with different sizes, in which case the update rounds of different agents may not occur simultaneously. This behavior is illustrated in
Figure

1
, where we compare executions of
SSF
initialized with homogeneous versus heterogeneous sizes (over
i\in I
) of the multisets
\{M_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i\in I}
.

Importantly, the larger number of sources with the correct preference (say,
1
), together with the uniform noise, imply that each agent is more likely to receive the message
(1,1)
than
(1,0)
, even after noise is applied. In turn, this implies that each weak-opinion
\tilde{Y}_{t}^{(i)}
is correct with a probability larger than
1/2
.
This is formally proved in

Lemma

7
.

We remark that the fact that for every round
t
, all weak-opinions
\{\tilde{Y}_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i\in I}
are mutually independent is less obvious than for Algorithm
SF
.
As mentioned, the weak opinion of an agent is updated according to the second bits of messages that were received with a first bit that equals 1. If such a message
\sigma
originated from a non-source agent, then it must have been corrupted by noise. Therefore, because of the noise being uniform, the distribution of the second bit of
\sigma
is independent of the second bit of the original message (which is the weak opinion of the sampled non-source agent).
This implies that, eventually, the weak-opinion of each agent
i
is independent from the behavior of all other non-sources.
Finally, notice that, after
2\lceil m/h\rceil
rounds, each agent has updated its opinion and weak-opinion twice. For every agent
i
, the set
M_{t}^{(i)}
is emptied after its first update, and from now on, it will only contain messages that were actually sampled (and not included in the arbitrary initial configuration), which allows the protocol to be self-stabilizing.

2.3
Weak Opinions: Correctness Intuition

Here, we give an overview of the computations ensuring that the weak-opinions are correct, in both Algorithms
SF
and
SSF
. Formal statements are collected in
Section

3
, and the corresponding proofs are deferred to
Section

6
.

Fix an agent
i
.
For both protocols, for the sake of analysis, we map the messages used to compute the weak-opinions of Agent
i
, to a set of i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{k}\}_{k=1,\ldots,m}
taking values in
\{-1,0,+1\}
.
The general idea is that
X_{k}=+1
(resp.
-1
) if the corresponding messages suggest that the correct preference is
1
(resp.
0
), whereas
X_{k}=0
if the corresponding messages do not carry relevant information.
For instance, in Algorithm
SSF
, we define one
X_{k}
for each message in the memory of the agent. When a message has a first bit equal to
1
(i.e., it is tagged as coming from a source), the corresponding
X_{k}
is assigned value
+1
or
-1
depending on the second bit of the message, and otherwise, it is assigned value
0
.
Instead, in the case of Algorithm
SF
, each
X_{k}
corresponds to a pair of messages, one received in Phase 0 and one in Phase 1.
When these messages are either both equal to
1
, or both equal to
0
, then the corresponding
X_{k}
is assigned value
+1
or
-1
respectively. Conversely, when these messages are different,
X_{k}
is assigned value
0
.
Next, consider the sum
X=\sum_{k}X_{k}
.
The protocols and the
X_{k}
s are defined so that the weak-opinion of Agent
i
is
1
iff
X>0
(leaving the tie-breaking mechanism aside).
W.l.o.g, we will assume that the correct preference is
1
, and focus on showing that
X>0
with probability at least
1/2+\Omega(\sqrt{\log n/n})
.
This requires a fine-grained analysis of the distribution of
X
, that classical concentration inequalities do not provide directly.

Recall that a Rademacher random variable takes values in

\{-1,+1\}
. Although the
\{X_{k}\}
variables are not Rademacher, since the number of non-zero
X_{k}
s is
\ell\approx m\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)
, we show that
X
is distributed roughly as a sum of
\ell
i.i.d Rademacher random variables.
Moreover, each variable in this sum equals
1
w.p.
p=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1\mid X_{k}\neq 0\right)
.
We can then apply the following lower bound, derived directly from existing literature and proved in
Lemma

23
:

\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi e}}\cdot\min\{(p-\tfrac{1}{2})\sqrt{\ell},1\}.

Since we are excluding possible ties (for now), it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)
, hence a sufficient condition for having
X>0
with probability at least
1/2+\Omega(\sqrt{\log n/n})
, is (up to a constant factor):

\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\ell}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}.

(3)

This condition reflects the need to gather sufficient information from the variables
X_{k}
. Specifically,
\ell
represents the number of
X_{k}
s that provide any useful information, while
p-\frac{1}{2}
quantifies the extent to which each non-zero
X_{k}
contributes in correctly inferring the correct preference.
In order to estimate
\ell=m\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)
, we show that for both protocols,

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)\geq\delta+\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}(1-|\Sigma|\delta).

The first term corresponds to corrupted messages, while the second corresponds to uncorrupted messages from the sources (in
SSF
, there is another factor of
(1-|\Sigma|\delta)
that is due to the more complex pairing of
X_{k}
-messages; but we ignore it here for the sake of clarity).

We distinguish two regimes in computing
p-\tfrac{1}{2}
:

•

If

\delta<\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}(1-|\Sigma|\delta)
,
then each non-zero
X_{k}
is more likely to result from a direct observation of a source without distortion, than to be a consequence of noise.
Hence, each non-zero
X_{k}
is relatively informative,
and we can show that both protocols

p-\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{s}{4(s_{0}+s_{1})}
.

•

Conversely, when

\delta\geq\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}(1-|\Sigma|\delta)
,
each non-zero
X_{k}
is quite likely to be the consequence of a corrupted message, hence
p-\frac{1}{2}
cannot be too large; in fact, we show that for both protocols, we only have

p-\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{s}{n}\frac{(1-|\Sigma|\delta)}{8\,\delta}
.
However, this drop in quality is offset by the greater number of non-zero
X_{k}
s, enabling agents to accumulate enough information.

The above computations of
p
and
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)
are presented in

Lemma

6

for
SF
, and in

Lemma

7

for
SSF
.
From these quantities, we derive a sufficient condition on
m
that applies to both protocols, in

Lemma

5
.
Specifically, with the above bounds, in the regime where
\delta\geq\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}(1-|\Sigma|\delta)
, it suffices to choose
m
such that

m\geq\frac{\delta n\log n}{s^{2}(1-|\Sigma|\delta)^{2}}
,
to satisfy
Equation

3
.
However, to ensure the correctness of the weak opinion across a broader range of parameters, additional additive terms for
m
are necessary.

4
Discussion and Conclusions

We investigated the problem of disseminating a binary opinion in noisy, stochastic multi-agent systems, where communication is constrained and initial coordination may be absent. In the noisy
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model, we show that even under severe noise and minimal message sizes (1–2 bits), fast and robust convergence to the sources’ plurality opinion can be achieved. Our results establish tight upper bounds for convergence time across all values of the sampling parameter
h
, demonstrating that increasing
h
linearly accelerates dissemination and mitigates the effects of noise.

A useful way to interpret our protocols, in particular Algorithm
SF
, is as a two-stage process. In the first stage, agents independently sample the system without relaying information, thereby avoiding interference and forming reliable local estimates. In the second stage, agents exchange these estimates and amplify the emerging majority. This separation between estimation and amplification appears to be a robust design principle for information dissemination under noise.

To model the lack of synchronization in the starting time, we adopt the framework of self-stabilization, which may be stronger than what is required in natural systems. Nevertheless, we show that a simple protocol achieves fast (quasi) self-stabilizing convergence. This comes at the cost of increasing the message size from 1 to 2 bits, highlighting a tradeoff between bit complexity and self-stabilization. It remains open whether a simple and efficient self-stabilizing algorithm using 1-bit messages exists.

Our work also emphasizes simplicity in communication rather than in internal computation. This choice is motivated by systems such as social insects, where individuals may perform sophisticated local computations but face severe communication constraints
[
27
,
23
]
. Accordingly, our protocols rely on minimal message sizes, while allowing agents to carry out non-trivial operations, such as counting. Exploring the tradeoff between communication complexity and internal computational simplicity remains an interesting direction for future work.

Our results also provide a theoretical perspective on collective behavior observed in biological systems (see also Section
12
). In cooperative transport by longhorn “crazy ants”
[
31
,
32
,
28
]
, directional information is conveyed indirectly through the motion of a shared load. Prior work
[
31
]
showed that such information transfer is possible under certain population sizes, although scalability to large groups remained unresolved.
The noisy
\mathcal{PULL}(h)
model captures different regimes of such indirect communication. When the noise in estimating the aggregate force
F
scales as
\sqrt{F}
, corresponding to large
h
, our results imply that even a single informed agent can quickly influence the entire population. In contrast, higher-variance estimates of
F
can be associated with more localized sampling (constant
h
), for which fast dissemination from a small number of sources is impossible, but can be recovered by increasing the number of sources.

Overall, our results suggest that sensing aggregate tendencies, as observed in cooperative transport and related systems
[
31
,
34
,
32
,
43
]
, can enable efficient and scalable information propagation even under strong noise and minimal communication.

[
5

to demonstrate that, given a

6.1
Preliminary Results in Probability

Definition 19

(Rademacher distribution)
.

We say that a random variable
X
follows a Rademacher distribution with parameter
p
, and write
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(p)
, if it takes value in
\{-1,1\}
and
\mathbb{P}\left(X=1\right)=p
.

The following result is a direct observation concerning binomial random variables with a small success probability parameter
p
.

Claim 20
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\left(n,p\right)
. If
n\,p\leq 1
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(X=1\right)\geq\frac{1}{e}n\,p.

Proof.

Let
u_{1}=1
, and
u_{n}=(1-\frac{1}{n})^{n-1}
for
n\geq 2
.
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is decreasing and
\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}u_{n}=\frac{1}{e}
, so
u_{n}\geq\frac{1}{e}
for every
n\geq 1
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X=1\right)=n\,p\,(1-p)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\geq n\,p\,u_{n}

(since
p\leq\tfrac{1}{n}
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{e}n\,p,

which concludes the proof of
Claim

20
.
∎

As outlined informally in
Section

2.3
, our analysis will later introduce random variables
X_{k}
that take values in
\{-1,0,1\}
.
We will focus specifically on the non-zero variables, treating them as Rademacher variables.
The following result formalizes this approach and addresses potential dependency issues.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}
be i.i.d. random variables taking values in
\{-1,0,1\}
. Let
Y
be the number of
X_{i}
with non-zero value, i.e.,

Y=\Big|\{i=1,\dots,m\,:\,X_{i}\neq 0\}\Big|.

Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
. Conditioning on
\{Y=r\}
,
X
is distributed as a sum of
r
Rademacher random variables with parameter
p=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=1\mid X_{i}\neq 0\right)
.

Proof.

Let
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Let
\mathcal{E}(a)
be the event that non-zero elements of
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}
corresponds to non-zero elements of
a
, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}(a):=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\left\{|X_{i}|=a_{i}\right\}.

Similarly, we write
\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)
to denote the same event, without the condition on the element at index
k
:

\mathcal{E}_{k}(a):=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{m}\left\{|X_{i}|=a_{i}\right\}.

Note that all
\{\mathcal{E}(a)\}_{a\in\{0,1\}^{m}}
are disjoint. Moreover, writing
|a|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
, we have

\{Y=r\}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\{0,1\}^{m}\\
|a|=r\end{subarray}}\mathcal{E}(a).

Since all
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}
are mutually independent,
X_{k}
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}
are also independent.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1\mid X_{k}\neq 0,Y=r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1,Y=r\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0,Y=r\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1,\mathcal{E}(a)\right)}{\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0,\mathcal{E}(a)\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1,\mathcal{E}(a)\right)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0,\mathcal{E}(a)\right)}

(when
a_{k}=0
,
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0,\mathcal{E}(a)\right)=0
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1,\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)\right)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0,\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)\right)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=r\\
a_{k}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)\right)}

(
X_{k}
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}(a)
being independent)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\neq 0\right)}=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=1\mid X_{k}\neq 0\right).

Moreover, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X=x\mid Y=r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}=x\mid Y=r\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(a)\mid Y=r\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}X_{i}=x\mid\mathcal{E}(a)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(a)\mid Y=r\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i:a_{i}=1}X_{i}+\sum_{i:a_{i}=0}X_{i}=x\mid\mathcal{E}(a)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(a)\mid Y=r\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i:a_{i}=1}X_{i}=x\mid\mathcal{E}(a)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(a)\mid Y=r\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i:a_{i}=1}X_{i}=x\mid\bigcap_{i:a_{i}=1}\{X_{i}\neq 0\}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{X_{i}\text{ i.i.d.}}{=}\sum_{|a|=r}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(a)\mid Y=r\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}=x\mid\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\{X_{i}\neq 0\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}=x\mid\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\{X_{i}\neq 0\}\right),

which concludes the proof of
Lemma

21
.
∎

The next lemma recalls an existing lower bound on the probability to obtain more “heads” than “tails”, when throwing a biased coin
m
times.

Lemma 22

(From Lemma 9 in
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\theta>0
and
B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2+\theta)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B>\frac{m}{2}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(B<\frac{m}{2}\right)\geq\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\pi}}\cdot g(\theta,m),1\right\},

where

g(\theta,m)=\begin{cases}\theta\,\left(1-\theta^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}&\text{if }\theta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}&\text{if }\theta\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B>\frac{m}{2}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(B<\frac{m}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(B\geq\frac{m}{2}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(B\leq\frac{m}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\lceil m/2\rceil}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\,p^{k}(1-p)^{m-k}-\sum_{k=\lceil m/2\rceil}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\,p^{m-k}(1-p)^{k},

and then we proceed as in the proof of
[
29
, Lemma 9]
. Please note that in this paper, the definition of
g
contains a typographical error; the correct definition is the one provided here.
∎

Next, we rewrite the previous statement in a more convenient way for our analysis.

Lemma 23
.

Let
0\leq\theta<1/2
and
m>0
, and consider i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}\sim\mathrm{Rad}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\theta\right)
.
Then,
X=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi e}}\cdot\min\{\sqrt{m}\,\theta,1\}.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let
B_{i}=(X_{i}+1)/2
, and let
B=\sum_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
. By definition,
B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,\frac{1}{2}+\theta)
and
\{X>0\}=\{B>\frac{m}{2}\}
.

•

First, consider the case that
\theta\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
. By
Lemma

22
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)\geq\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\pi}}\cdot\frac{(1-1/m)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}}{\sqrt{m}},1\right\}\geq\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\pi e}},1\right\},

where we used the fact
x\mapsto(1-1/x)^{\frac{x-1}{2}}
is decreasing and that
\lim_{x\to\infty}(1-1/x)^{\frac{x-1}{2}}=e^{-1/2}
.

•

Now, consider the case that
\theta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
.
In that case,

\left(1-\theta^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\geq e^{-1/2},

and by
Lemma

22
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\pi e}}\theta,

which concludes the proof of

.

∎

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Y=1\right)\cdot\big[\mathbb{P}\left(X>0\mid Y=1\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X<0\mid Y=1\right)\big]

\displaystyle\geq 8\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}.

\displaystyle\left(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{claim:low_expectation}}\right)

This concludes the proof of
Lemma

5
.
∎

6.2.1
Correctness of weak-opinions in Algorithm

We now prove

Proof.

Let

Recall that in each of the first two phases of Algorithm
SF

Let

By construction, Agent

By applying a similar reasoning to Phase 1, we compute for every

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{1}}{n}\delta+\left(1-\frac{s_{1}}{n}\right)(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{1}}{n}(1-\delta)+\left(1-\frac{s_{1}}{n}\right)\delta

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{0}}{n}\delta+\left(1-\frac{s_{0}}{n}\right)(1-\delta).

Let us define the random variables
\{X_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\{1,\dots,m\}}
s.t.

\displaystyle X_{k}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }(A_{k}^{(i)},B_{k}^{(i)})=(1,1)\\
0\quad\text{if }(A_{k}^{(i)},B_{k}^{(i)})=(0,1),\text{ or }(A_{k}^{(i)},B_{k}^{(i)})=(1,0)\\
-1\quad\text{if }(A_{k}^{(i)},B_{k}^{(i)})=(0,0).\end{cases}

Let

.
Informally, each
X_{k}^{(i)}
is assigned value
+1
or
-1
if the corresponding pair of messages tends to indicate that the correct preference is respectively
1
or
0
, and it is assigned value
0
otherwise.
Recall that
C_{t}^{(i)}
denotes the value of the coin toss performed by Agent
i
in round
t
.
By definition of Algorithm
SF
, we have

\tilde{Y}^{(i)}=1\iff\begin{cases}X^{(i)}>0,&\text{or}\\
X^{(i)}=0\text{ and }C_{t}^{(i)}=1.\end{cases}

(15)

Note that the value of the messages received in Phases 0 and 1 only depends on the identity of the sampled agents, and on the realization of the noise. As a consequence, each

it follows that all

Moreover, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=1\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=0\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=-1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=0\right).

The proof of the following result is straightforward.

Claim 28
.

For every
s_{0},s_{1}\in\bbN
such that
s_{0}+s_{1}\leq n
, we have that
\frac{s_{0}s_{1}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{4n}

Let
x=s_{0}/n
and

. We have

\frac{s_{0}s_{1}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{4n}\iff x(S-x)\leq\frac{S}{4}\iff P(x):=x^{2}-Sx+\frac{S}{4}\geq 0.

If

,

is always positive, and if
S=1
,

has a unique root at

. In both cases,
P
is non-negative, which concludes the proof of
Claim

28
.
∎

In order to apply

5
to

, we first verify that all its conditions are satisfied.

Claim 29
.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)\geq(1-2\delta)^{2}\cdot\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}+\delta

(16)

Moreover, if

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=1\mid X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{n}\frac{(1-2\delta)}{8\,\delta},

(17)

while if
\delta<\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}(1-2\delta)

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=1\mid X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{4(s_{0}+s_{1})}

(18)

Proof of
Claim

29
.

We first prove
Equation

16
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s_{1}}{n}(1-\delta)+\left(1-\frac{s_{1}}{n}\right)\delta\right)\cdot\left(\frac{s_{0}}{n}\delta+\left(1-\frac{s_{0}}{n}\right)(1-\delta)\right)

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\delta+(1-2\delta)^{2}\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{2n}.

This establishes

16
.
From

19
, since
1-2\delta\leq 1-\delta\leq 1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)\leq 2\,\delta+(1-2\delta)\frac{s_{0}+s_{1}}{n}.

(20)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=-1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=1\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}^{(i)}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s_{1}}{n}(1-\delta)+\left(1-\frac{s_{1}}{n}\right)\delta\right)\cdot\left(\frac{s_{0}}{n}\delta+\left(1-\frac{s_{0}}{n}\right)(1-\delta)\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{s_{1}}{n}\delta+\left(1-\frac{s_{1}}{n}\right)(1-\delta)\right)\cdot\left(\frac{s_{0}}{n}(1-\delta)+\left(1-\frac{s_{0}}{n}\right)\delta\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=1\mid X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}^{(i)}=-1\mid X_{k}^{(i)}\neq 0\right)

\frac{(1-2\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{\delta^{\prime}}=A\cdot\left(1-\frac{2}{A}\right)^{2}=\frac{(A-2)^{2}}{A}=\frac{\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1-2\delta}{\delta}\right]^{2}}{2+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1-2\delta}{\delta}}=\frac{1}{4\delta}\cdot\frac{(1-2\delta)^{2}}{2\delta+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\delta)}\geq\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{(1-2\delta)^{2}}{\delta},

where in the last inequality we have used the fact that

Hence,

\frac{n\delta^{\prime}\log n}{s^{2}(1-2\delta^{\prime})^{2}}\leq 6\cdot\frac{n\delta\log n}{s^{2}(1-2\delta)^{2}}.

Therefore,

satisfies
Equation

8
with
\delta^{\prime}
, and
Theorem

follows from our analysis under uniform noise (see

13

Consequently, each

\delta

\mathcal{R}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}^{(i)}

.(i).

Proof of (ii)

7
Reducing the size of the alphabet
\Sigma

In this section we show how a protocol designed for a communication over a specific alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}
can be adapted to work with an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
(with fixed size), without changing the asymptotic of the convergence time of that protocol. We only require that
|\Sigma|\geq|\Sigma^{\prime}|\geq 2
. The idea is quite simple. Assume for example that we have a protocol designed to work with a binary alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}=\{0,1\}
. Then, if the given arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
has even cardinality, we divide
\Sigma
into 2 groups of equal size, one associated to “0” and the other to “1”. Then, whenever an agent receives
s\in\Sigma
which belongs to the “0” group (resp., “1” group), it runs the protocol as if it had received a 0-message (resp., 1-message). In the case
|\Sigma|
is odd, the first
|\Sigma|-1
symbols are treated as in the even case, and each time the last symbol in
|\Sigma|
is received, it is treated as either “0” or as a “1”, each with probability
1/2
.

More precisely, let
\Sigma^{\prime}
be the alphabet of a given protocol. We are mainly interested in the case
|\Sigma^{\prime}|=2
(in
SF
and
USF
) and
|\Sigma^{\prime}|=4
(in
SSF
).
Let
\Sigma
be an arbitrary alphabet and
N
be the corresponding arbitrary
\delta
-bounded noise matrix. Assume
|\Sigma|\geq|\Sigma^{\prime}|
, and
|\Sigma|=q|\Sigma^{\prime}|+r
, for some
q,r\geq 0\in\mathbf{Z}
, with
r<|\Sigma^{\prime}|
. W.l.o.g. assume
\Sigma=\{0,1,\ldots,|\Sigma|-1\}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=\{0,1,\ldots,|\Sigma^{\prime}|-1\}
.
We define a filter
F
that probabilistically maps
\Sigma
to
\Sigma^{\prime}
, so that the first
q|\Sigma^{\prime}|
elements in
\Sigma
are deterministically distributed in
|\Sigma^{\prime}|
groups of size
q
, each group being associated to a different symbol in
\Sigma^{\prime}
, while the remaining
r
elements are distributed uniformly at random among all the elements of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Formally,
F:\Sigma\mapsto\Sigma^{\prime}
s.t., if
i<q|\Sigma^{\prime}|
, then
F(i)=\lfloor i/q\rfloor
w.p. 1, while, if

Uniform Source Filter (USF)

Algorithm
SF
assumes that the system designer knows many of the system’s parameters, namely: the noise matrix
N
and constant approximations of
n
, the number of sources
s_{0}+s_{1}
, and the bias
s
. However, it is possible to weaken this assumption by repeatedly guessing estimates for the parameter
m
, which governs the duration of the phases, and hence controls the amount of information gathered in the phases.

Here, we introduce the Uniform Source Filter (
USF
) that only requires agents to know the noise matrix
N
and a constant approximation of
\log n
. The pseudo-code can be found in
Algorithm

3
. In
USF
, each agent runs multiple instances of
SF
. Initially, each agent executes
SF
for
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
times using an optimistic guess for
m
, specifically
m^{(0)}=h
. Each agent then registers all output opinions, for each execution of
SF
. If all these outputs of an agent are the same, the agent adopts that opinion. Then, each agent repeats the process, doubling the guess for
m
from the last attempt, i.e.,
m^{(i+1)}=2m^{(i)}
, and so on. The agents terminate when
m^{(i)}
reaches the most pessimistic guess, i.e., whenever
m^{(i)}\geq m(n,s_{0}+s_{1},s)
from
Equation

2
, by assuming
s=1
,
s_{0}+s_{1}=n
.

It is important to note that the output opinion of one instance of
SF
is not used by other instances. This ensures that the outputs are independent of one another. Note also that, for any guessed value for
m
, the probability of an incorrect output opinion from a single execution of
SF
is at most
1/2
. As a matter of fact, in the design of
SF
, if the agents don’t gather enough messages during phases 0 and 1, it is likely that none of those messages come directly from a source, and therefore most agents start the majority boosting phase with a random opinion. Hence, if the guessed value for
m
is too small, we do not expect the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
outputs of
SF
to be consistent, and the agents will retry with a larger value of
m
. In contrast, if the estimate
m^{(i)}
is at least as large as
m(n,s_{0}+s_{1},s)
(see
Equation

2
), then the outputs from the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
repetitions of
SF
will be consistently correct w.h.p.
Since the running time of Algorithm
SF
is
\mathcal{O}(m(n,s_{0}+s_{1},s)/h)
rounds, Algorithm
USF
ensures convergence after
\mathcal{O}(m(n,s_{0}+s_{1},s)/h)\log n
rounds.

As mentioned, convergence of opinions does not imply that agents stop their actions.
Since agents do not have precise knowledge of the system’s parameters, it is not clear how they can ensure that they terminate soon after they converge.
Instead, using their constant approximation of
\log n
, the agents can terminate after a polynomial time in
n
. Specifically, the protocol could safely terminate after the agents have attempted the most pessimistic guess of
m^{(i)}\geq m(n,s_{0}+s_{1}=n,s=1)=\Theta\left(\frac{n\log n}{(1-|\Sigma|\delta)^{2}}\right)
from
Equation

2
. Using their constant approximation of
\log n
, the agents could know that they reach this state only after
\mathcal{O}(\frac{poly(n)}{h(1-|\Sigma|\delta)^{2}}+\log n)
rounds.

Implications to house-hunting by ants

In this section we interpret the process of
house-hunting
in ants through the lens of our findings.
House-hunting is observed in ant species such as
Temnothorax
. When their nest is damaged, these ants embark on a house-hunting process to find and select a new site for their colony
[
33
,
41
]
. Suppose the possible nest locations have already been identified, and the remaining task is to reach a consensus on the best site. This process can be divided into two phases. In the first phase, scout ants are sent to evaluate the quality of different sites — a process that can be expected to be noisy. Upon completing an evaluation, a scout ant returns to the nest and begins a
tandem run
, leading another ant from the nest to the site. The leader moves slowly enough to keep the follower engaged, with regular pauses to ensure the follower remains close. Communication occurs through frequent physical contact, such as antennal touching. This allows the leader to adjust pace and direction, maintaining coordination with the follower, which can in turn, learn the path to the new site. When reaching the site, the newly recruited ant assesses it herself and, upon returning to the nest, repeats the tandem run process. The rate of tandem runs for a particular site depends on its quality, but each tandem run is time-consuming delaying the assessment phase. While relaying quality estimates between ants could have sped up the process, it might have also introduced errors due to the difficulty of accurately communicating those measurements. Instead, tandem runs, though slow, allow ants to gather first-hand information by evaluating the sites themselves.
In the language of this paper, one may interpret the ants’ strategy as investing time (performing tandem runs) to increase the number of sources (and thereby the bias) instead of attempting to relay estimates. The second phase involves a quorum-sensing mechanism, where if a sufficient number of ants are present at a site, that site is chosen by the colony. This phase bears similarities to the majority-consensus phase.
