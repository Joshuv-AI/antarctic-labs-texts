Laparoscopic surgery for subcapsular hematoma of the liver with preeclampsia: a case report
Authors: Guangyu Liu, Hongmei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubcapsular liver hematoma (SLH) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of pregnancy. And SLH is a severe manifestation of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy. Early prevention, prompt identification, and multidisciplinary management are crucial. We present a case of SLH successfully treated by laparoscopic surgery.Case DescriptionThis paper described the case of a 37-year-old Chinese woman (gravida 6, para 0) who was diagnosed with preeclampsia and hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets (HELLP) syndrome upon admission. After admission, the patient was found to have elevated blood pressure and positive urine protein. She developed right upper abdominal pain before delivery, and abnormal liver function and thrombocytopenia during the perinatal period. The patient subsequently developed a grade III SLH and received treatment involving cesarean section followed by laparoscopic hematoma evacuation. The fetus was rapidly delivered, with Apgar scores of 10 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes. And the patient recovered well after delivery.ConclusionsThis case provides a valuable idea for managing incidentally detected hepatic subcapsular hematoma rupture during cesarean delivery. This therapeutic strategy not only minimizes iatrogenic surgical trauma under the premise of ensuring maternal-fetal safety but also facilitates multidisciplinary collaboration and the accumulation of high-quality clinical evidence. Consequently, it furnishes a safe and effective therapeutic paradigm for the management of rare and life-threatening complications in obstetric practice.

Introduction

Subcapsular liver hematoma (SLH) is a rare yet potentially fatal spontaneous hematoma that can arise during pregnancy. It is frequently associated with preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome—characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets—and may progress to hepatic rupture. HELLP syndrome is regarded as a severe phenotype within the spectrum of preeclampsia, occurring in approximately 10–20% of cases with severe disease (1). SLH has been reported in less than 2% of pregnancies complicated by HELLP syndrome (2-4), and the maternal mortality is between 17% and 59% (4,5). The right liver lobe is more frequently affected in subcapsular hematomas (6). Given the high risks associated with such pregnancy complications and their detrimental impacts on maternal and fetal outcomes, timely and accurate identification, prompt clinical diagnosis, and standardized evidence-based intervention are clinically essential. Herein, we report a case of a pregnant woman with preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome complicated by a grade III hepatic subcapsular hematoma, which was successfully managed with laparoscopic hematoma evacuation. The patient exhibited favorable outcomes, including a minimally invasive surgical wound and accelerated postoperative recovery. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://gpm.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gpm-25-33/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

The patient, a 37-year-old Chinese woman with a history of six pregnancies (gravida 6, para 0), four previous histories of induced abortion and one history of premature rupture of membranes at 21 weeks of pregnancy. Due to a history of multiple induced abortions and one spontaneous abortion during the second trimester of a previous pregnancy, laparoscopic cervical cerclage was performed prior to this pregnancy.

Due to secondary infertility, this pregnancy was achieved via in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer. Given the presence of multiple intermediate-risk factors for gestational hypertension-including elderly primiparity (maternal age >35 years) and in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer, oral administration of aspirin at a daily dose of 75 mg was initiated at 12 weeks of gestation and continued until 36 weeks of gestation. Throughout the entire gestation, blood pressure remained within the normal range and urine protein tests were negative. The patient’s pre-pregnancy platelet count was 194×109/L, with fluctuations ranging from 92×109/L to 102×109/L during pregnancy, and 97×109/L upon admission. Following laparoscopic cervical cerclage, cesarean section was deemed the most appropriate mode of delivery. Consequently, at 39 weeks of gestation, the patient was admitted for an elective cesarean section.

Upon admission, the patient’s initial blood pressure was recorded at 166/106 mmHg, which decreased to 160/102 mmHg after a period of rest. Urinalysis revealed significant proteinuria (++), with a urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio of 2.02, and a 24-hour urinary protein quantification of 0.622 g. The patient had no overt edema and was free of subjective symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, or headache. Based on clinical manifestations and laboratory findings, a diagnosis of severe preeclampsia was established. Magnesium sulfate was immediately administered for anticonvulsant prophylaxis, and nifedipine tablets were given for blood pressure control while further necessary examinations were conducted. Abdominal ultrasound did not reveal any avert organic lesions in the liver (Figure 1), gallbladder, pancreas, or spleen. However, urinary ultrasound detected bilateral renal calculi, accompanied by left ureteral dilation and left hydronephrosis. Ultrasonographic examination revealed 0.6 cm fluid collections in both pleural cavities, with no peritoneal effusion identified. Cardiac and obstetrical ultrasounds showed no abnormalities, and fetal heart rate monitoring returned normal results. Laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin concentration of 147 g/L, a platelet count of 97×109/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 18 U/L, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 22 U/L. Following treatment, the patient’s blood pressure decreased significantly to 128/85 mmHg and subsequently stabilized within the range of 109–128 mmHg systolic and 63–86 mmHg diastolic.

The normal liver on ultrasound scan.

Before surgery, the patient experienced a sudden onset of right upper quadrant abdominal pain without accompanying nausea, vomiting, or other symptoms. She was immediately transferred to the operating room for cesarean section. During the procedure, approximately 1,000 mL of dark red, non-clotting blood was discovered in the peritoneal cavity. The fetus was rapidly delivered, weighing 3,610 g, with Apgar scores of 10 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes. The uterus was carefully inspected, and no rupture was identified. After suturing the uterine incision, the bilateral adnexa and pelvic cavity were examined, revealing no significant abnormalities. During the surgery, further palpation of the upper abdomen detected swelling of the left lateral lobe of the liver, and a consultation with hepatic surgeons was initiated to assist with further management.

Although the patient’s vital signs remained stable, persistent minor bleeding was noted. Following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion, a relatively minimally invasive surgical approach—laparoscopic exploration—was selected. Intraoperatively, approximately 100 mL of dark red blood was observed in the splenic area, and a hematoma was present on the diaphragmatic surface of the liver. A 4 cm × 2 cm rupture was identified in the liver capsule of segment III (Figure 2), accompanied by active bleeding, with the deepest point of the rupture reaching 3 cm. It was a grade III SLH (7). After electrocoagulating the active bleeding sites on the hepatic capsular surface, the capsular tissue over the diaphragmatic aspect of the left lateral hepatic lobe was meticulously incised. The hematoma was then gradually dissected along the anatomical interface between the hematoma and intact hepatic parenchyma. Subsequently, subcapsular hematoma evacuation of the liver was performed and the capsule incision was covered with absorbable hemostatic gauze. Then an abdominal drainage tube was placed in the upper abdomen. The operation went smoothly.

During the operation, blood was found accumulated in the splenic area, and the diaphragmatic surface of the liver was covered with a hematoma. There was a 4 cm × 2 cm rupture in the liver capsule of segment III of the liver.

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for further management, including antihypertensive therapy, antispasmodic therapy, reinforcement of uterine contractions, infection prevention, and prophylaxis against deep vein thrombosis. On the second postoperative day, after stabilization of the condition, the patient was transferred back to the ward (Table 1). During hospitalization, laboratory results deteriorated within 17 hours after cesarean section. Specifically, liver function tests showed an upward trend, with serum AST peaking at 221 U/L and ALT at 229 U/L; the platelet count reached a nadir of 68×109/L, and the hemoglobin level declined to 89×109/L, while other parameters remained largely unchanged (Table 1). Accordingly, a diagnosis of HELLP syndrome was established, and oral hepatoprotective agents were administered. Relevant laboratory parameters showed significant improvement upon re-examination on the second postpartum day.

A transabdominal ultrasound performed 5 days after cesarean section revealed a hepatic hematoma measuring 6.1 cm × 1.6 cm (Figure 3). In view of the absence of significant abdominal drainage fluid, the drainage tube was subsequently removed. All laboratory parameters had normalized, and the patient demonstrated satisfactory clinical and biochemical recovery, leading to her discharge on postoperative day 5.

Discussion

Definition and therapy

Subcapsular hepatic hematoma is a rare yet potentially fatal complication of HELLP syndrome. In our situation, between 2010 and the end of 2024, among 200,805 deliveries, 295 cases of HELLP syndrome were identified, of which only one case developed a subcapsular hepatic hematoma. Clinically, hepatic subcapsular hematoma typically presents as right upper quadrant pain, frequently accompanied by nausea and vomiting (8,9). Ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) offer valuable means for rapid diagnosis. In cases of non-ruptured hematomas, conservative management—including close hemodynamic monitoring, serial imaging surveillance, blood product transfusion, and even hepatic artery embolization—can be considered (10,11). Conversely, for patients with suspected rupture of the subcapsular hematoma, particularly those exhibiting hemodynamic instability, immediate surgical intervention by an MDT is indicated (12). Owing to the absence of established clinical guidelines for managing hepatic subcapsular hematoma, a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment is essential, taking into account factors such as gestational age at onset, the timing relative to delivery, hemodynamic status, and the presence or absence of rupture and bleeding (6,9).

Our highlights

In our case, during the cesarean section, a large amount of blood accumulation in the abdominal cavity was found, and the tension of the liver capsule in the left liver lobe was palpated to increase. Despite these findings, the patient’s vital signs remained stable. Therefore, after discussing with the liver surgeon, laparoscopic exploration was performed, and successfully controlling the hemorrhage. The overall treatment outcome of this patient was good. The surgical wound was small, and the postoperative recovery was fast. Furthermore, all postoperative monitoring parameters demonstrated minimal fluctuations and rapid normalization within clinically expected ranges. This case provides a valuable idea for managing incidentally detected hepatic subcapsular hematoma rupture during cesarean delivery. However, this surgical approach is only applicable to patients with stable vital signs and minimal bleeding within a short period. In cases of massive hemorrhage occurring acutely, laparotomy may be more appropriate. Furthermore, during the diagnosis and treatment of this patient, bedside ultrasound could be considered promptly upon the detection of abdominal pain—an aspect that warrants optimization in future clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the early identification of subcapsular hematoma of the liver is not only of great significance but also serves as a critical turning point in the overall management of the patient’s condition. The prompt and precise detection of this potentially life-threatening anomaly through advanced imaging techniques-such as high-resolution ultrasonography, CT, or magnetic resonance imaging-ensures that intervention can be initiated before the hematoma exacerbates into a more complex and hazardous situation. Moreover, its treatment necessitates comprehensive assessment of the treatment plan, which is best achieved by an MDT.
