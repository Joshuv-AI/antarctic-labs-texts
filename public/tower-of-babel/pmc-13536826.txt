Cancer of unknown primary: the evolution of tissue of origin identification in the artificial intelligence era
Authors: Xinyue Zeng, I. Weng Lao, Yifeng Sun, Qifeng Wang, Xiaowei Zhang, Zhiguo Luo, Xiaoyan Zhou, Midie Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer of Unknown Primary (CUP) presents substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges owing to its heterogeneous nature and the absence of an identifiable primary tumor site. This review provides a structured search of the pathogenesis, epidemiological characteristics, and limitations of traditional diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for CUP, with an emphasis on the evolution of Tissue of Origin (TOO) identification techniques. Recent advances in precision medicine have accelerated the development of machine learning–based TOO identification tools, representing a paradigm shift in CUP diagnostics. Deep learning (DL) algorithms that integrate multi-omics data (such as genomics and transcriptomics) with clinical features have markedly enhanced the accuracy of tracing tumor origin, and artificial intelligence (AI) driven TOO models are increasingly being incorporated into clinical practice, offering new insights for pathological diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognostic evaluation. Nevertheless, several challenges remain, including issues of data standardization, model generalizability, and interpretability. Ethical considerations related to data privacy, algorithmic fairness, and clinical implementation also warrant careful attention. Future research should focus on establishing standardized multi-center databases, developing more interpretable AI models, and fostering multidisciplinary collaborative strategies for CUP management. Through continued refinement of technical solutions and regulatory guidelines, TOO identification is anticipated to progress from research to routine clinical application, ultimately supporting precise and personalized care for patients with CUP.

Introduction

Cancer of Unknown Primary (CUP) refers to a clinical diagnosis where the primary site of the tumor cannot be identified after standard imaging and pathological examinations [1, 2]. CUP accounts for 3–5% of all cancers and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The median age at diagnosis is 60 years, and it is more prevalent in males [1, 3–5]. However, recent studies suggest a possible decline in CUP incidence, potentially due to improvements in CUP diagnostic tools and enhanced imaging capabilities for detecting minute tumors [6]. For instance, Gu et al. reported that 68Ga FAPI PET/CT demonstrated higher accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity than 18F-FDG PET/CT in identifying the tissue origin of head and neck CUP [7].

CUP can be classified into various types based on the metastatic sites or organs, including lymph nodes, liver, lung and bone [8]. The most common histological subtypes found in CUP are adenocarcinoma (40%-83. 6%), followed by undifferentiated carcinoma (5%-29%) and squamous cell carcinoma (6. 6%-23. 6%) [8–14]. From the perspective of tumor tissue origin, the most frequent primary tumor sites are breast (21. 8%), gastroesophageal (12. 6%), and lung (11. 5%-27%). Furthermore, liver or bile duct tumors (4.2%-8%), kidney or adrenal tumors (1.5%-8%), colorectal tumors (7%-8%), and neuroendocrine tumors (4. 6%) has also been reported [8, 12, 15].

Clinically, CUP patients often have a relatively short natural history, and present signs and symptoms depending on the sites of involvement, hence preventing them from receiving standardized treatment in the absence of a precise diagnosis [1, 3]. Besides, about one-third of CUP patients have multiple organs involvement at the time of diagnosis [16]. Traditional diagnostic approaches, primarily relying on histopathology and imaging, often have limited capacity to accurately identify the tumor origin in CUP, particularly in cases with poorly differentiated or metastatic lesions. To cope with these challenges, state-of-art technologies and approaches are being explored in recent years to identify the tissue of origin (TOO) of CUP, with the aim of potentially guiding clinicians in tailoring personalized and targeted treatment regimens for CUP patients, with the goal of improving their overall survival and improving their quality of life.

To provide a clear overview of the contemporary clinical management paradigm for CUP, Fig. 1 summarizes the stepwise diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm. The algorithm begins with initial clinical evaluation and tumor biopsy, followed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) for lineage classification—distinguishing epithelial (CK7 + vs. CK7–) from non-epithelial tumors (melanoma, hematolymphoid, germ cell, sarcoma). If the primary site is identified, patients receive site-specific therapy. In confirmed CUP cases, patients are stratified into favorable and unfavorable subsets based on clinical presentation. Favorable subsets (e.g., breast-like, ovary-like, oligometastatic) proceed directly to site-specific therapy, whereas unfavorable subsets (e.g., poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma) undergo substantial molecular profiling. This framework provides the background for the subsequent discussion on how multi-omics technologies may help address the unmet clinical needs in CUP diagnosis and therapy.

This narrative review aims to provide a contemporary overview of molecular diagnostic technologies for tissue-of-origin (TOO) identification in cancer of unknown primary (CUP). To inform this review, we surveyed the PubMed database from inception to June 2026 using keywords including “cancer of unknown primary”, “CUP”, “tissue of origin”, “genomics”, “transcriptomics”, “epigenomics”, “proteomics”, “multi-omics”, “DNA methylation”, “gene expression profiling”, “next-generation sequencing”, “artificial intelligence”, and “machine learning”. Given the heterogeneity of available studies—ranging from retrospective cohort studies to prospective clinical trials and proof-of-concept methodological investigations—we adopted a narrative synthesis approach. Priority was given to CUP-specific evidence; methodological studies without direct CUP application were included only as indirect supporting evidence and are clearly identified as such throughout the text. No formal systematic review protocols or quantitative meta-analyses were performed.

Current treatment status of CUP

Chemotherapies

Based on the provisional histopathological subtypes, patients with cancer of unknown primary generally received empirical treatment taxanes and platinum [1, 3, 9, 17]. Adenocarcinoma, despite being the most common type of CUP, however, has no standard treatment and so most cases use cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimens (such as 5-fluorouracil and cisplatin), with poor patient response and survival rates [18–20]. Gatalica et al. found that identifying biomarkers that predict drug response may be associated with improved survival outcomes in CUP patients [21]. With an exception for female patients of abdominal CUP, who received treatment analogous to peritoneal serous papillary adenocarcinoma, as surgical debulking followed by combination chemotherapy with platinum and paclitaxel is considered to be an optimal approach [22, 23].

Cervical lymph node metastasis of unknown primary origin accounts for approximately 5% of all head and neck cancers [24], with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) being the most common histological type [25]. For head and neck tumors, surgery and radiotherapy are indispensable parts, and in some cases, a combination of cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil can be used for treatment [26–30]; At present, the incidence rate of HPV related primary unknown squamous cell carcinoma is on the rise, accounting for 3–5% of all head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) [31]. The best treatment includes neck dissection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy or concomitant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and neck dissection after definitive CRT [31, 32].

The treatment options for patients with poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma are inconsistent. Etoposide in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy is recommended by several studies [1, 3, 21, 33–35], which have been reported to double the response rates and the overall survival [36], while another meta-analysis indicated that no chemotherapy regimen was associated with a statistically significant survival benefit.

Overall, the prognosis for CUP patients is still poor, with only about 20% of them achieving an average survival of around 10 months. This highlights the need for careful selection of optimal chemotherapeutics, and additional studies are warranted to evaluate the efficacy of different treatment options [37].

Targeted therapies

Targeted therapies for CUP are gaining increasing attention. Early studies have used bevacizumab and erlotinib as second-line treatments for patients with CUP and found that this combination was reported to be associated with a median survival time of 7.4 months [38]. In subsequent studies, the use of bevacizumab and erlotinib as first-line treatments, combined with a chemotherapy regimen of carboplatin and paclitaxel, was associated with a patient response rate of 53%. The median survival time and 2-year overall survival rate were 12.6 months and 27% [23], respectively, suggesting that additional studies are warranted to evaluate this regimen.

However, a recent multi-center retrospective study reported that targeted therapy is effective in some cases but has not demonstrated consistent clinical benefits in all patients [39]. The discrepancy in the above study results may be partially attributed to the extreme heterogeneity of CUP, with different tissue origins of CUP exhibiting significantly different treatment responses to targeted drugs. Therefore, in CUP cases, careful selection based on cytomorphological criteria and continuous communication with the clinical team is important for formulating targeted therapy plans [40]. Furthermore, molecularly targeted therapies already available for other solid tumors are being considered for application in CUP patients [41], indicating that this field merits further investigation. In a study involving 131 patients, a comparative analysis was conducted between the site-specific treatment group (n = 50) and the empirical chemotherapy group with paclitaxel and carboplatin (n = 51). The results showed that site-specific treatment guided by molecular analysis did not lead to a significant improvement in 1-year survival rate [42]. However, a recently conducted CUPISCO multi-center randomized phase II clinical trial across 34 countries is designed to evaluate the use of broad genomic profiling (CGP) at diagnosis to guide molecularly targeted treatment strategies, with the goal of improving the prognosis of newly diagnosed CUP patients. Results showed that molecularly guided therapy (MGT) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in progression-free survival compared to standard platinum-based chemotherapy [43].

Immunotherapy

In recent years, with the development of immunotherapy, more studies have explored its application in CUP patients. This combinatorial strategy has recently been evaluated in the Fudan CUP-002 phase II trial [44], which assessed an anti-PD-1 antibody plus nab-paclitaxel and bevacizumab as second-line treatment for CUP. Among 48 patients, the objective response rate was 54.2%, the disease control rate was 95.8%, and median progression-free and overall survival were 13.1 and 25.1 months, respectively. These findings indicate that immunotherapy-containing regimens may represent a viable second-line option for select CUP patients, but confirmation in larger, randomized studies is needed. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, especially PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, have shown preliminary therapeutic activity. For example, the results of an open label phase II study showed that the application of nivolumab in patients with CUP may provide some clinical benefit [45]. Another study indicated that CUP patients treated with pembrolizumab showed improvements in response rate and survival [46]. Despite these preliminary findings, immunotherapy for CUP still faces challenges. For instance, a CUP patient experienced immune-mediated nephritis during Nivolumab and Ipilimumab treatment [47], indicating that immunotherapy can be associated with immune-related adverse events that require close monitoring and management during treatment. Furthermore, combination strategies involving molecularly guided chemotherapy and immunotherapy have recently been explored in CUP treatment [48]. Moreover, the potential synergistic effect of radiation therapy in immunotherapy is an interesting field of research. Studies have suggested that radiation therapy can have “side effects”, namely immune effects observed outside the radiation treatment site, which may improve the response of CUP patients to immunotherapy [49]. In summary, the current landscape of immunotherapy for CUP is evolving. Although additional studies are needed to further validate its efficacy and safety, existing results indicate that immunotherapy combined with molecular characteristic analysis offers new perspectives for the treatment of CUP patients. Future research will continue to explore how to optimize these strategies to potentially maximize patient outcomes.

Site-specific therapies

With advances in TOO identification tools, site-specific therapy based on TOO may be associated with improved outcomes. Targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy has been reported to achieve complete remission in some CUP patients, highlighting the importance of molecular testing and personalized treatment [50]. In the Fudan CUP-001 randomized controlled trial, patients assigned to site-specific therapy guided by a 90-gene expression assay [51] for tissue-of-origin prediction achieved a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 9.6 months, compared with 6.6 months in those receiving empirical chemotherapy, corresponding to a 32% reduction in the risk of progression. Concurrently, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of ≥ 3 grade treatment-related adverse events between the site-specific therapy group and the empirical chemotherapy group, suggesting that site-specific therapy has a generally acceptable safety profile [52]. Therefore, these findings suggest that TOO-guided site-specific therapy may improve treatment outcomes compared with empirical chemotherapy [53]. Similar findings were reported in a recent French nationwide study, which reported that patients receiving MTB-guided treatment including site-specific or targeted therapies, achieved a median overall survival of 18.6 months versus 11.0 months with empiric therapy (HR = 0.61, p = 0.04), further validating the clinical benefit of TOO-directed and molecularly guided strategies in clinical settings [54].

Taken together, the therapeutic strategies for CUP detailed above face inherent clinical limitations: empirical chemotherapy confers only modest survival benefits, while targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and site-specific regimens all depend on accurate identification of the tumor’s tissue of origin to guide appropriate patient stratification and personalized treatment planning. The absence of a confirmed primary tumor site remains the fundamental bottleneck preventing CUP care from shifting from empirical management to precision oncology. Driven by this unmet clinical need, tissue-of-origin (TOO) identification technologies have evolved substantially, advancing from conventional histopathological evaluation to sophisticated molecular profiling and emerging multi-modal detection approaches. The following section surveys the developmental landscape of TOO diagnostic tools, encompassing the established application of molecular diagnostic technologies as well as the current status and prospects of novel detection methodologies.

Development of TOO identification tools

The diagnosis of CUP remains a challenge in the medical field. Currently, histopathology and IHC remain the standard approaches for diagnosing CUP [55]. CUP diagnoses is a multi-step process, first to obtain a sufficient amount of appropriately prepared tumor tissue or cytology samples, followed by a panel of IHC markers, with necessary clinical and laboratory information [3]. Pathological assessment can help determine the primary cancer type of the malignant tumor by combining the microscopic pattern, immunoprofile and clinical setting of the patients. In the current era, based on targeted molecules and immunotherapy, the systematic application of appropriate IHC combinations may help classify most undifferentiated cancers [56]. For example, the “two-step immunohistochemical diagnostic strategy” has been reported to be able to identify the primary lesion in 77% of Cancer of Multiple and Unknown Primary (CMUP) patients in selected studies [57]. However, this high diagnostic yield is achieved under optimized conditions in a specific patient subset. In routine clinical practice, conventional diagnostic procedures—including standard histopathology and limited IHC panels—still identify the tissue of origin in only approximately 30% of unselected CUP patients [58], particularly when tumors are highly metastatic or poorly differentiated [5]. This substantial gap between optimal and routine diagnostic performance underscores the urgent need for more advanced molecular and multi‑modal approaches. Fortunately, with the development of technology, the current CUP diagnosis has expanded into multiple areas such as molecular analysis, pathomics and imageomics (Fig. 2). The rapidly developing TOO identification technology offers new possibilities to the diagnosis of CUP, may gradually influence the diagnostic process, decision support, and patient management.

The TOO identification tools outlined above do not exist in isolation, rather, they are sequential milestones in the evolution of medical technology. Their emergence and application collectively illustrate a pathway of diagnostic paradigm evolution. From the initial phase reliant on morphology, to the notable integration of molecular biology, and onward toward a future defined by multi-omics convergence and artificial intelligence, each stage has sought to address key limitations of its predecessor. This technological evolution is illustrated below (Fig. 3), providing a historical context and developmental framework for understanding the current diversified landscape of diagnostic tools.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the initial reliance on morphology and limited IHC panels (Era I) has been progressively supplemented by molecular diagnostic technologies (Era II), such as gene expression profiling and targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS). These methods have provided genomic and transcriptomic signatures for TOO classification. The subsequent integration of multi-omics data (Era III), particularly DNA methylation profiling, has further enhanced diagnostic resolution by capturing stable, tissue-specific epigenetic patterns. Currently, efforts are focused on harnessing artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning (DL) (Era IV) to fuse multi-omic data with digital pathology images and clinical information, with the goal of developing predictive, multimodal TOO classification models. The following sections will detail the development, application, and impact of these key technologies along this evolutionary trajectory.

The application and development of molecular diagnostic technology in TOO identification

Tumors originating from different tissues exhibit distinct genomic alteration profiles, including gene deletions, point mutations, chromosomal rearrangements, and aneuploidy [59] technologies such as gene expression profiling, gene microarrays, microRNA analysis, and DNA methylation studies have been increasingly explored to predict TOO [60–63]. In these instances, molecular detection techniques have shown potential for application [55]. Studies have reported that gene expression profiling can predict tumor types, with reported overall accuracy ranging from 83% to 97. 4% [51, 52, 64–70]. Anita et al. developed a 2160-gene classification model based on gene expression profiling for the differential diagnosis of HNSCCs and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), reporting an accuracy rate of 81. 6% in 38 FFPE samples of HNSCC and 38 FFPE samples of LUSC [71]. For example, a study involving 84 patients with known sources of primary and metastatic tumors revealed that 83% of patients could correctly classify their primary cancer through gene expression tests, to primary lung adenocarcinoma [72]. Sarah E et al. [65] reported an overall sensitivity of 87% and subtype sensitivity of 82% for 92 gene expression tests related to tumor types. Mark G et al. [64] expanded the study to encompass 95% of systemic tumors, finding sensitivities of 87% and 85% for 30 major tumor types, respectively. A 90-gene expression assay (Canhelp-Origin (Canhelp Genomics, China)) based on real-time PCR technology has been developed and evaluated in multiple studies, reporting overall accuracy ranging from 88.5% to 97.4% for tumor type prediction [51, 68, 70, 73–76]. This test represents the sole molecular diagnostic tool available in China and has been endorsed by the China Anti-Cancer Association guideline for diagnosis and treatment of cancer of multiple and unknown primaries (2025 edition). It offers a clinically accessible option for TOO identification, though its performance relative to other platforms require further comparative study. Compared with other commercially available or research-based TOO assays worldwide, the 90-gene expression assay has several distinguishing features. Unlike EPICUP (DNA methylation-based, 87% accuracy in CUP cohorts [77]), the 90-gene expression assay is based on RT-PCR technology, making it more accessible in routine clinical laboratories in China and providing a faster turnaround time (approximately 3–5 days) compared with NGS-based approaches (typically 7–14 days). The 92-gene expression assay (CancerTYPE ID, bioTheranostics, USA), which achieved 87–92.5% accuracy in global cohorts, also uses an RT-PCR platform [64]. In comparison, the 90-gene assay was developed and validated primarily in Chinese populations with 21 predefined tumor types, while CancerTYPE ID covers a broader range of tumor types and has been validated in global cohorts. A large multicenter validation study of the 90-gene expression assay reported an overall accuracy of 94.4% (95% CI: 0.93–0.96) across 21 tumor types [70], whereas published estimates for the TOO test and CancerTYPE ID have been reported as 87-87.8% in their respective studies.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the 90-gene expression assay should be noted. First, this assay was developed and clinically validated only for 21 predefined tumor types [70]; Its discriminatory performance for rare tumors, esophagogastric neoplasms, and pancreaticobiliary tumors remains unclear and requires further verification by additional studies. Second, the test mandates a minimum tumor cellularity of ≥ 60% in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens [70]. Metastatic tumors in clinical practice frequently exhibit extensive necrosis, which fails to meet this criterion. In addition, the assay imposes requirements on sample quantity, a limitation especially prominent for small biopsy specimens that require 30-micron unstained slides. Third, as an expression-based test, it does not detect actionable mutations (e.g., EGFR, ALK, BRAF), necessitating additional NGS testing for targeted therapy selection. This limitation is shared by other expression-based assays such as the TOO test and CancerTYPE ID [64]. Fourth, while the assay has been validated in large Chinese cohorts [70, 73, 78], its performance in other ethnic populations requires further investigation. Additionally, like other TOO identification technologies, the 90-gene expression assay should be interpreted within the context of the patient’s clinical presentation, as no single molecular test can replace a thorough clinical and pathological evaluation [3, 62, 70, 79]. Identifying driver mutations or gene rearrangements in cancer cells through various molecular tests [34, 80, 81], particularly next-generation sequencing technology, is also a crucial tool for determining the origin of CUP tumor [62]. However, due to insufficient evidence suggesting that this method may improve CUP outcomes, current management guidelines do not recommend routine testing for gene expression and epigenetic factors [61].

In summary, the advancement of new omics technologies has contributed to progress in TOO research, with particular relevance for the diagnosis and personalized treatment of CUP. Genomics and TOO

High throughput sequencing technologies, such as whole genome sequencing and targeted sequencing, enable the detection of gene mutations, copy number changes, and gene rearrangements in tumors. By analyzing the mutation spectrum in depth, researchers have identified genomic alterations associated with specific cancer types, identifying oncogenes associated with specific cancer types, and providing key clues for determining TOO. Davis et al. explored the application of genomic analysis in CUP diagnosis in 2020, highlighting its potential in identifying tumor sources [94]. This potential is now being realized through the development of dedicated computational models that translate raw genomic data into actionable predictions of TOO. For instance, the CUPPA [82] algorithm, trained exclusively on somatic mutation profiles from whole-genome sequencing, can correctly predict the primary tumor site in approximately 80% of CUP cases, highlighting the information content within the genomic landscape. Beyond established panels, the frontier of genomics is being pushed by novel analytical approaches. Recent research has reported that machine learning models, such as large language models (LLMs) [83], can be trained on genomic mutation data alone to perform TOO classification, achieving 67.7% accuracy in prospective validations and showcasing the adaptability of AI in interpreting genomic “language”.

Epigenomics and TOO

DNA methylation profiling technologies, such as genome-wide methylation microarrays and targeted bisulfite sequencing, can reflect the tissue-specific development history of cells. By deciphering these stable epigenetic signatures, researchers can provide information about the original identity of metastatic tumors, even when conventional pathology fails. This approach provides a discriminative tool for determining the TOO, independent of genetic mutations. In clinical applications, Foundation One Liquid CDx, as a broad genomic analysis detection based on 324 cfDNA genes, has been reported to have clinical utility in the diagnosis of solid tumor-derived cancers [95]. Furthermore, differences in methylation status across tumors of different tissue origins have also been explored for determining the TOO. For instance, Moran et al. constructed a DNA methylation detection method (EPICUP) based on a microarray platform to predict the tissue origin of CUP, and predicted the TOO in 188 out of 216 CUP patients (87%) through analysis of their DNA methylation status [77]. Recently, a CatBoost-based methylation classifier of 106 features successfully distinguished among five common squamous cell and urothelial carcinomas and achieved an accuracy of 98. 79% (490/496) in the training set of PanCanAtlas dataset, demonstrating potential utility in identifying the origin of metastatic SCC [96].

Transcriptomics and TOO

With the widespread application of RNA sequencing technology, researchers can analyze the gene expression profile of tumor cells, thereby providing insights into the molecular characteristics of specific tumor types [97]. This technology can identify small gene expression differences and can also be used to match unknown TOOs with known tumor types, potentially improving the success rate of tumor origin identification. For example, Viber et al. ‘s study showed that transcriptome data helped identify the tumor origin of CUP patients, addressing some limitations of traditional pathology [98]. The aforementioned 90-gene expression assay (Canhelp-Origin) [51, 68, 70, 73–76] based on quantitative real-time PCR, an accuracy of 97.4% in identifying both primary and metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), offering a potential complementary tool for TNBC diagnosis especially in challenging metastatic cases [68] and male breast cancer [76]. Beyond single-site tracing, this 90-gene signature resolves a key clinical dilemma: distinguishing multiple primary squamous cell carcinomas (MPSCCs) from metastases. Xu et al. [69] applied it to patients with synchronous/metachronous SCCs across head and neck, esophagus, and lung. By comparing expression profiles across lesions, it inferred clonal relationships, informing staging and therapy decisions. Furthermore, analysis of gene mutation profile differences between different tumor types via NGS can provide clues regarding the tissue origin of tumors. Alexander et al. employed NGS to detect mutations in 468 cancer-related genes in tumor tissues from 141 CUP patients and, in combination with artificial intelligence algorithms, successfully predicted the tumor tissue origin in 95 patients (67. 4%) [99].

Proteomics and TOO

The application of mass spectrometry analysis and protein chip technology has advanced our understanding of protein expression and modification in tumor tissues. These cutting-edge technologies help researchers identify specific protein markers and provide information for the diagnosis of tumor types. A comparative proteomic analysis of canine hepatobiliary neuroendocrine tumors and normal tissue microenvironments has provided new insights, offering an improved understanding of the mechanisms of tumor formation and potentially informing future research directions in human tumor research [100]. In addition, recent research has reported findings: proteins in the extracellular vesicles of tumor-derived plasma may serve as indicators of the origin of tumors, offering new possibilities for non-invasive detection [101]. At the same time, scientists have identified proteins specific to tissues and cell types using machine-learning techniques based on large-scale proteomic data [102], providing a useful tool for cancer research. This allows extraction of relevant information from massive and complex data. Hanibal Bohnenberger et al. [103] performed quantitative proteomics analysis on LUSC and HNSCC, quantifying 4,957 proteins in 44 LUSC and 30 HNSCC tumors, and identified 518 proteins with differential expression between LUSC and HNSCC. Supervised machine learning was then used to derive a proteomic signature that could distinguish between LUSC and HNSCC, reporting diagnostic accuracies of 90. 5% and 86%, respectively. In addition, Song Q et al. [104] performed non-targeted quantitative proteomic analysis on treatment-naive primary tumor samples (10 common sites and 7 rare sites) from 333 squamous cell carcinoma patients and developed a machine learning-based SCC TOO classifier, reporting 100% sensitivity and specificity in the internal validation set. Xu et al. [105] developed a machine learning based proteomic classifier specifically for SCCUP. Their 39-protein panel demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (AUC up to 0.971) across multiple cohorts. In addition, they translated this proteomic signature into a practical five-marker immunohistochemistry panel, representing a step toward clinical translation. Beyond diagnosis, their work also provided insights into the metabolic and immune drivers for SCC progression and metastasis.

Collectively, the omics technologies discussed above have shown promise in TOO identification in CUP. However, each approach presents a unique balance of strengths and limitations (Table 2). Discrepancies in study design, patient cohorts and evaluation criteria across existing publications create inconsistent benchmark conditions. However, direct comparisons of performance metrics across different omics technologies and TOO prediction models are inherently challenging. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity values cited throughout this review are derived from heterogeneous studies that differ considerably in cohort composition, sample types (tissue versus liquid biopsy), validation strategies (internal versus external), and the proportion of true CUP cases versus known primary tumors. These methodological variations, coupled with the overall scarcity of prospective head-to-head comparative studies covering the full clinicopathological spectrum of CUP, largely preclude definitive conclusions regarding the relative superiority of any single TOO detection platform. As a notable exception, Gong et al. [105] performed a head-to-head comparison of diagnostic accuracy between the 39-protein panel and Canhelp-Origin across seven common squamous cell carcinoma cases, providing rare direct comparative evidence in this domain. Nevertheless, this evidence is confined to squamous cell carcinoma populations and cannot be extrapolated to the broader heterogeneity of CUP, meaning a generalized consensus on cross-platform performance remains unresolved. Future research should prioritize standardized benchmarking frameworks and independent validation cohorts to enable more meaningful cross-platform evaluations.

Application status and development of new detection technologies in TOO

In recent years, researchers have made a number of advancements in the identification of TOO, supported by a growing array of research tools and materials, alongside a continually expanding research field. The emergence of new technologies, such as genomics, liquid biopsies, and multi-modal analysis, has generated renewed interest in this area, potentially overcoming the limitations of traditional methods [62]. The application of genomics facilitates the detection of specific mutations within a patient’s tumor cells, serving as unique “labels” for different tumor types, which may assist in identifying the tissue of origin based on the distinctiveness of tumor gene mutations [62, 64, 72]. Previous tumor tracing efforts relied on invasive tissue biopsies, often causing considerable discomfort to patients. The advent of liquid biopsy techniques allows for timely acquisition of tumor information through the analysis of circulating free DNA in a patient’s blood. This method is not only efficient and convenient but also captures the dynamic changes in the tumor in real-time [106–108]. Innovations in imaging analysis are particularly noteworthy, especially the application of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) technology for analyzing CT, MRI or HE images. By leveraging deep learning on imaging and pathological data, subtle pathological changes can be accurately identified, significantly enhancing diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. These technological advancements equip clinicians with powerful tools, allowing them to thoroughly analyze multi-dimensional data and make more precise judgments when confronted with complex cases.

Liquid biopsy, a non-invasive and easily accessible method, has been increasingly explored in clinical practice to reflect the overall status of tumors and facilitate real-time monitoring. Common biomarkers include circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), tumor-educated platelets (TEPs), exosomes, microRNA, circulating RNA, and others [62, 106–109]. Currently, ctDNA testing is primarily employed for monitoring recurrence after radical treatment of early-stage diseases, and it may play a role in cancer screening and diagnosis in the future [106, 108]. mRNA sequencing of 283 platelet samples by Myron G Best et al. [109] demonstrated that TEPs achieved an accuracy of 71% in identifying the primary tumor location across various tumor types.

Advancements in radiomics technology have provided new approaches for identifying TOO. Previously, it has been challenging to interpret tumor characteristics when confronted with complex imaging data. Now, radiomics can extract high-dimensional features to tumor characteristics from intricate data, potentially aiding clinical decision-making. Taking uterine sarcoma as an example, radiomics, supported by imaging biomarkers, has shown potential in improving the understanding of tumor characteristics and assisted in treatment planning [110]. The application of radiomics in lung cancer management has been extensively explored. Detailed analysis of imaging features may make tumor typing and treatment response assessment more precise, strongly supporting the accurate identification of TOO [111]. With advancements in data science, the potential of radiomics is being explored. Data science can enhance the efficiency of imaging data analysis and extract additional information, potentially informing support for clinical decision-making [112]. Furthermore, the combination of radiomics and IHC biomarkers offers a perspective on identifying the TOO. Researchers have integrated established and emerging IHC biomarkers into MRI, potentially enhancing the analysis of tumor pathological features and improving the accuracy of tumor origin identification [113].

The application of pathomics in tumor origin identification has garnered increasing interest in modern medical research. Additionally, the emergence of cytology-based deep learning models has provided new approaches for predicting the TOO of CUP. Lu et al. [114] employed weakly supervised learning-assisted pathology to analyze whole slide image (WSI), potentially enabling faster diagnosis. Although their study was not limited to CUP, this approach may be particularly relevant for metastatic cases that necessitate numerous IHC testing and related clinical or radiological evaluations. Tian et al. ‘s TORCH model is among the first to utilize artificial intelligence for analyzing cytological images of pleural and ascitic fluid to predict cancer origin. This model achieves area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.953–0.991 and 0.953–0.979, respectively, for cancer diagnosis and TOO prediction, outperforming pathologists’ predictions in this specific study context [115]. This model provides a novel auxiliary diagnostic tool for clinicians to identify the primary site of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary (SCCUP). Recently, Wang Qifeng et al. together with the research and development team of the Canhelp Origin assay developed and validated a pathological large model (Deepath SCC). Relying on 4,217 digital pathological images of routine H&E-stained slides, this model achieves overall accuracy of 91.2% and 86.1% in internal test set and independent external validation set, respectively, for TOO prediction of five common types of squamous cell carcinoma [116]. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) research has highlighted the significance of pathology in understanding tumor heterogeneity in general cancer populations, offering pathologists a perspective on the study of CUP. Pathologists integrate genetic information and histological features to provide support for identifying TOO, thereby informing clinical decision-making [117]. Multi-omics data integration enables pathologists to better understand tumor heterogeneity and its origin, which is particularly vital for identifying TOO. Multi-omics fusion analysis integrates WSI with genomic information, thereby presenting tumor characteristics in a holistic manner [117]. These studies highlight the importance of utilizing genomics and imaging techniques in pathological diagnosis, contributing to the field of TOO research.

Development of TOO analysis tools

While the molecular and imaging technologies detailed in the preceding sections have considerably expanded the diagnostic toolkit for TOO identification and enabled multi-dimensional characterization of tumor biology, they also yield high-dimensional, multi-modal and highly heterogeneous datasets. Conventional statistical and bioinformatic approaches are limited in their ability to integrate these disparate data modalities and decode the complex non-linear biological signatures that distinguish tumor origins, representing a notable bottleneck to further gains in diagnostic precision. Machine learning offers a valuable analytical paradigm to overcome this challenge, with inherent strengths in multi-source data fusion, complex pattern recognition, and the generation of potentially standardized TOO predictions. It can also assist in identifying potential tumor types by examining pathological images and genomic data, providing clinicians with valuable decision-making references [85, 114, 118, 119]. This section reviews the development of machine learning–based TOO analysis tools, surveying the applications of diverse machine learning algorithms in TOO tracing, then focusing on the notable effects of deep learning technologies on CUP diagnosis and clinical decision-making, as well as their current limitations and barriers to clinical implementation.

Applications of different machine learning algorithms in TOO identification

As a branch of AI, machine learning (ML) primarily makes predictions by employing mathematical algorithms to identify patterns in data. With the ongoing research into CUP, ML has shown potential applications in predicting and classifying TOO. ML can be categorized into several major types: supervised learning (SL), unsupervised learning (USL), semi-supervised learning (SSL), reinforcement learning (RL), ensemble learning (EL), and DL. By analyzing multi-dimensional features from molecular omics, radiomics, and pathomics, these algorithms may assist physicians in identifying TOO. Currently, tools for identification of TOO primarily developed based on traditional ML algorithms, such as support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), and XGBoost, which have been utilized to predict the tissue origin of CUP. For instance, a SVM classifier developed by Leonhard Stark et al. [120] achieved an overall accuracy of 87%. He B and Sun et al. employed RF algorithms, reporting average prediction accuracies exceeding 80% across multiple cancer types [51, 79, 118]. Common methods in EL include AdaBoost and XGBoost, which leverage the combination of weak learners to progressively reduce errors and create stronger learners [121, 122]. Some researchers have also concurrently established classifiers using multiple algorithms such as RF, SVM, and logistic regression, and comparing the efficacy of different algorithms in TOO detection [123–126] to identify models with improved performance. Compared to traditional ML algorithms, DL are capable of processing complex data patterns and feature extraction. Common DL models include multilayer perceptrons (MLP), convolutional neural networks (CNN), recurrent neural networks (RNN), and generative adversarial networks (GAN) [127]. DL is increasingly used in cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment selection, and in the CUP field, it has shown promise in TOO identification. Currently, multiple studies have reported utilizing ML algorithms to train on various omics data, such as histopathological images [114], cytological images [115], somatic passenger gene mutation profiles [128], transcriptomic data [129], and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) methylation data [130], while establishing TOO identification tools. These studies not only offer tools for the clinical management of cancer but also contribute to the development of precision medicine approaches. In summary, ML algorithms have shown potential in CUP research. However, it is important to note that traditional ML algorithms and modern DL methods each have strengths and limitations in TOO research, and their combination may improve diagnostic accuracy [127]. The integration of traditional ML techniques with modern DL models offers new methods for predicting the TOO of cancer. In the future, with the ongoing enhancement of data collection and further optimization of algorithms, it is anticipated to provide more accurate and personalized support for the precise diagnosis and treatment of CUP.

Transformations and impacts of DL technologies in the diagnosis and decision-making of CUP

Transforming the diagnostic process of CUP

In diagnostic workflows, deep learning technology offers valuable pattern recognition capabilities (Fig. 4). Its potential for automation may facilitate the processing of extensive medical data, constructing multi-layered neural networks and automatically extracting significant features, thereby reducing the need for manual intervention. For instance, Lu et al. utilized weakly supervised learning to aid pathological methods in analyzing tissue slice images, enabling swift diagnosis of CUP and distinguishing between primary and metastatic tumors, reducing the diagnostic time. This may be particularly relevant for metastatic cases that require extensive IHC testing, as well as clinical or radiological correlation for diagnosis [114]. Deep learning tools can effectively process complex molecular biological data, quickly and accurately classifying tumor samples. For example, by analyzing transcription factor expression profiles from RNA sequencing data of biopsy specimens, the deep learning tool GeneCT demonstrated performance in TOO classification across multi-center and pan-cancer samples, offering a potential approach for TOO identification [119]. Furthermore, deep learning shows potential in integrating diverse medical data, such as imaging, genomics, and methylation data, which may offer new insights for CUP diagnosis. For instance, the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) combined pathological imaging with transcriptomics and proteomics to identify predictive histological features associated with key clinical outcomes in cancer, establishing a multimodal classification system with an AUROC of up to 0.979 for TOO prediction [131]. In summary, the application of deep learning may influence the CUP diagnostic process, potentially addressing clinical needs.

Impacting decision support for CUP

In the realm of decision support, the application of DL techniques in personalized medicine may gradually influence traditional healthcare models. By analyzing a patient’s genomic data, imaging scans, and clinical information, it can identify biomarkers, potentially assisting clinicians in developing individualized treatment regimen for each patient. Enhancing patient management for CUP

Traditional patient monitoring methods have historically depended on periodic testing, which may miss critical information and cause delays in addressing disease progression. DL may overcome this challenge by analyzing real-time data streams to capture relevant signals. DL offers potential solutions for patient monitoring, demonstrating potential advantages in tracking treatment responses and changes in disease status. By dynamically analyzing biomarkers, it may detect shifts in a patient’s health, to assess treatment efficacy and enable timely interventions. For instance, Conway et al. [130] employed DL to analyze cfDNA methylation data during patient follow-up, identifying relevant information and monitoring patient status to inform management decisions. Furthermore, researchers have utilized DL technology to analyze patient imaging data, combined with changes in serum markers, to track tumor responses and physiological indicators [134–139]. This may inform treatment decisions and potentially impact patient outcomes and quality of life. Moreover, DL may facilitate personalized patient management strategies [140, 141]. It acts as a dedicated health manager; tailoring follow-up plans by identifying each patient’s biomarker changes. This precise monitoring approach not only enhances the sensitivity and accuracy of monitoring but also reduces unnecessary hospital visits and healthcare costs for patients. Thorough patient education is an essential component of enhancing disease management. DL technology can convert complex medical data into easily understandable information, enabling patients to grasp their health status better and making previously obscure medical information accessible.

Challenges of AI enabled TOO analysis

Despite the considerable promise of AI in tumor characterization and precision treatment, its clinical application faces several challenges. These challenges primarily concern data quality, sample diversity, model interpretability, and clinical deployment. First, many studies are constrained by insufficient sample sizes and dependence on single data sources, which limits the generalizability of their findings [142]. The performance of AI models often declines markedly when applied to data from different institutions, sequencing platforms, or patient populations. Building representative and diverse datasets is essential for model training and validation, yet such data remains scarce in CUP research. Thus, there is an ongoing need for more clinical trials and data sharing initiatives to support model development. Second, external validation remains a major gap in the current literature. Many models are trained and tested on single-institution datasets, such as TCGA, without validation in independent external cohorts. This undermines confidence in their generalizability to clinical settings [142]. Third, the interpretability of AI models, particularly deep learning approaches, raises considerable concern. These models often function as “black box“ [114, 143], making their decision-making processes difficult to comprehend and potentially deterring clinicians from adopting them. Enhancing transparency and interpretability, commonly referred to as Explainable AI (XAI) [144–146] is regulatory approval. Fourth, data heterogeneity across studies—including differences in sequencing platforms, sample types, preprocessing pipelines, and patient demographics—complicates cross-study comparisons and may limit model generalizability [142]. This issue is especially pertinent in CUP, where diagnostic samples may originate from diverse anatomical sites and clinical contexts. The successful application of AI in biobanking hinges on the availability of high-quality, standardized data that adhere to FAIR principles [147]. However, the CUP research community currently lacks such uniformly standardized, large-scale biospecimen and data repositories, which constitutes a fundamental bottleneck for AI model development and clinical translation. Fifth, the regulatory and implementation landscape for AI-based diagnostic tools is still nascent. Under current frameworks such as the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Regulation (IVDR) and Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) [148, 149], AI-driven software intended for medical use must satisfy stringent requirements regarding clinical evidence, data bias mitigation, and post-market surveillance prior to clinical deployment [150]. Moreover, integrating these tools into existing clinical workflows—including electronic health records, pathology systems, and molecular tumor boards—requires substantial infrastructure investment and personnel training [151]. Sixth, from an ethical standpoint, data privacy and algorithmic bias must be prioritized when deploying AI technologies. Patient health information is highly sensitive, and robust data security measures are essential to safeguard privacy. Concurrently, algorithmic biases may lead to inequitable diagnostic or therapeutic outcomes for patients from diverse backgrounds. To mitigate these risks, strategies such as data encryption, strict access controls, and regular audits of AI models using multi-dimensional datasets should be implemented to ensure fair and impartial patient care (Fig. 5).

As articulated by Tran and colleagues, the convergence of AI and biobanking is reshaping repositories into intelligent, adaptive infrastructures that serve both as the foundation for AI model training and as beneficiaries of AI-driven optimization [152]. From this perspective, the challenges facing AI-based TOO prediction in CUP—data heterogeneity, limited external validation, and lack of standardized biospecimen repositories—may be addressed through the development of intelligent biobanking systems capable of automated quality control, predictive analytics, and privacy-preserving synthetic data generation [152]. Multi-modal TOO tracing model

While the AI models reviewed above have demonstrated the feasibility of computational approaches for TOO prediction and shown encouraging diagnostic performance by leveraging individual data modalities, most remain confined to unimodal frameworks that rely solely on genomics, transcriptomics, or imaging data. Each data modality provides unique insights into tumor tissue identity, yet no single modality is fully exhaustive; their inherent technical biases and biological limitations persist when used in isolation, posing a notable constraint on diagnostic accuracy, robustness, and clinical applicability.

Driven by recent breakthroughs in deep learning architectures and multi-modal data fusion methodologies, integrative multi-modal models that explicitly combine omics profiles, imaging features, and clinical metadata represent a promising direction to address this limitation. These models are designed to capture complementary signals across data types and model complex cross-modal associations, thereby potentially delivering more robust TOO tracing.

This section surveys multi-modal TOO tracing frameworks, covering the core principles and construction strategies of such models, the practical applications of multi-omics integration in TOO identification, as well as the prevailing challenges confronting multi-modal tumor origin tracing technologies and corresponding potential solutions.

Construction of the multi-modal TOO tracing model

The multimodal TOO identification model cleverly integrates multiple omics data such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics using ML algorithms, which may enhance the recognition accuracy of TOO. The construction of a multi-modal TOO tracing model mainly involves the following steps and techniques: (1) data collection and integration, (2) feature extraction and selection, (3) model construction and training, and (4) model evaluation (Fig. 6).

Construction process of multimodal tissue of origin identification model. A: Data preparation and feature extraction; B: Building and training multimodal TOO identification model; C: Evaluation of multimodal TOO identification model; D: Deployment and monitoring TOO identification model

The first step in building this model is to obtain high-quality multimodal data, including genomic data, transcriptome data, proteome data and so on [153–156]. Then, it’s unifying these data from different sources into one framework and providing a basis for subsequent analysis. Standardization methods such as Z-score standardization or log conversion are usually used to make different omics data and have comparability.

Feature extraction is the process of extracting relevant information from raw data. For genomic data, select key mutation sites and copy number variations as features [59]; For transcriptome data, differential expression analysis is performed using tools such as DESeq2 and edgeR to identify genes that are significantly upregulated or downregulated [156]; And for proteomic data, specific proteins closely related to tumor characteristics, such as tumor markers, are selected. In the feature selection stage, ML algorithms such as RF and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression are commonly used [59, 81, 153]. Through cross-validation, the most discriminative feature set is selected, which can reduce the dimensionality of the data and improve the effectiveness of the model.

When constructing multimodal TOO traceability models, EL or DL methods are commonly used to improve prediction performance. EL, such as RF [59] or XGBoost [154, 157], combines multiple weak classifiers to enhance the accuracy of the model; DL utilizes neural networks (such as CNN or RNN) to enable models to automatically learn features from complex multimodal data, achieving automatic extraction and learning of feature layers. During the model training process, cross-validation is used to evaluate model performance, and hyperparameters are continuously optimized to prevent overfitting of the model [153, 154]. Meanwhile, by integrating data from different modalities, the model becomes more robust and reliable in TOO recognition.

After the model is trained, a rigorous evaluation is needed to verify its effectiveness in practical applications. The evaluation indicators include accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [154, 156]. Models that perform well in these evaluations may be considered for further application in tumor research and clinical treatment.

Applications of multi-modal omics in TOO

In recent years, multi-omics-based analytical methods have gained prominence in the field of TOO identification. By leveraging multi-modal data integration techniques to fuse genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic data, a data complementarity model is created that presents a broader view of tumors and facilitates the development of more efficient multi-modal biomarkers [158]. Multi-omics analysis methods that combine genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data to generate detailed tumor molecular characterization profiles may enhance the accuracy of TOO identification and provide data support for personalized treatment [131]. For instance, integrative analyses based on multi-omics data and molecular regulatory networks can identify TOO-related DNA methylation sites and establish classifiers, which may enable tracing metastatic cancers back to their primary sites [159]. Furthermore, DNA methylation data can be integrated with image-based deep learning models to achieve greater predictive power for TOO [85]. The studies demonstrate the potential of multi-modal data integration of CUP tissue traceability. Currently, multi-modal approaches that combine imaging techniques (such as CT and MRI), histopathological examination, molecular markers, and immunohistochemical marker analysis are widely employed in CUP diagnosis, becoming powerful tools for clinicians in combating tumors [160, 161]. However, it should be noted that the exploration of different tumor origins is a long journey, and the accuracy of diagnosis requires further improvement, which is the focus of efforts by countless researchers and physicians.

Looking to the future, the integration and application of technologies will be important. The variational autoencoder (VAE) developed by Muta Tah Hira et al. [162] extracts potential features from multi-omics datasets of ovarian cancer, demonstrating that the performance of integrated multi-omics analysis may outperform that of single-omics approaches. This not only aids in a deeper understanding of tumor heterogeneity but also enables us to take a significant step toward identifying TOO. Furthermore, patient-derived organoid models have shown promise, perfectly preserving the genomic and proteomic characteristics of patient tumors, allowing researchers to identify tumor-related mutations and gene variations in vitro. By comparing organoids with original tumor tissues, we can track genetic and phenotypic changes in tumors and gain a deeper understanding of tumor pathogenesis, which is valuable for tumor origin research and offers a new approach for CUP research [163].

Challenges and solutions of multi-modal technology in tumor of origin

Despite the potential of multimodal technologies in deciphering the TOO, significant challenges persist.

Complexity of data integration

Multimodal omics data, including genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, originate from diverse sources, possess varying resolutions, and employ distinct measurement units, complicating the integration process. The characteristics from different omics may also vary in biological significance, leading to potential information loss or increased errors during data fusion [164]. To address this issue, establishing unified data standards is needed. We can utilize public databases (such as SEER, TCGA, ICGC, etc.) and use the encompassed field information as the foundation for data storage and sharing, ensuring consistent data formats. Furthermore, data fusion algorithms like autoencoders and GANs in deep learning offer new approaches for sharing information between different omics, making the integration process more seamless and efficient.

Selection and validation of biomarkers

Selecting biomarkers from the vast features of multimodal data is like searching for a needle in a haystack. Additionally, ensuring the consistency and clinical relevance of these biomarkers across different omics is challenging. Currently, many studies suffer from inadequate sample validation, potentially undermining the credibility of the biomarkers [165]. To address this challenge, we need to establish a multi-tiered clinical trial validation process to ensure the effectiveness and reliability of the biomarkers.

Limitations of Data Processing and Analysis Techniques: Processing multimodal data necessitates complex algorithms and models, placing significant demands on the data. However, current analytical methods frequently struggle to manage large volumes and high dimensionality, leading to unstable outcomes and reduced computational efficiency [166]. Therefore, there is a need to develop innovative machine learning algorithms. Techniques such as ensemble learning methods and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) can effectively tackle high-dimensional data challenges, mitigating the issues caused by dimensional accumulation and improving data processing and analytical efficiency.

Impact of tumor heterogeneity and microenvironment

Tumor heterogeneity is prominently exhibited across multiple levels, including genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics. The cells, matrix components, and chemical mediators within the tumor microenvironment continuously affect tumor cell behavior and therapeutic responses, significantly complicating the accurate identification of tumor origins [167]. However, the emergence of new technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers promise. By integrating various omics features of tumors with machine learning models, we can gain deeper insight into characterizing the cellular subpopulations within tumors. It is conceivable that, in the future, we may uncover single-cell-level molecular markers that assist in tumor tracing.

Development of precision medicine for CUP based on TOO traceability

While the multi-modal TOO tracing approaches outlined above have represented notable methodological advances in multi-source data integration and diagnostic accuracy, their translation into clinical practice remains constrained by persistent challenges—including inconsistent data standardization, limited clinical biomarker validation, inherent analytical complexity, and confounding tumor heterogeneity. As a result, despite its compelling theoretical promise, multi-modal TOO identification has yet to fully realize its clinical potential for CUP management.

Nevertheless, the clinical utility of TOO tracing is not contingent on perfect diagnostic accuracy. Even partial, robustly validated information about tumor tissue origin may meaningfully inform clinical decision-making for CUP patients, potentially enabling more precise prognostic stratification and rational selection of site-specific or targeted therapeutic regimens. In this sense, advances in TOO technology may already provide a useful foundation for shifting CUP care from empirical, one-size-fits-all management toward precision oncology.

Against this backdrop, the final section examines the translation of TOO traceability insights into precision medicine strategies for CUP. It first explores biomarker-driven frameworks for prognostic assessment, then discusses the formulation of individualized treatment plans guided by TOO information, and concludes by outlining the remaining translational barriers and proposing future directions to advance the clinical implementation of TOO-guided CUP care.

Biomarkers and prognostic assessment

TOO traceability serves as a tool, revealing the molecular and biological characteristics of tumors. For example, tumor markers such as CA19-9 are closely associated with the prognosis of pancreatic and upper gastrointestinal tumors; PSA is linked to prostate cancer; and AFP serves as an important prognostic indicator for hepatocellular carcinoma patients [168]. In addition, the expression of highly sensitive markers like TRPS1, INSM1, and P16, along with the detection of markers such as HPV and EBER, is valuable for TOO identification. A 90-gene expression assay has developed the Canhelp Origin molecular testing tool, which incorporates 90 tissue characteristic genes and has been reported to be effective in identifying the tissue origins of most CUPs [8]. Accurate tissue traceability can assist clinicians in predicting patient survival and treatment response more accurately, potentially improving patient prognosis. Today, advanced genomic and epigenomic technologies can identify a wider range of biomarkers, providing a molecular foundation for tumor traceability [58]. Additionally, in clinical practice, physicians can monitor dynamic changes in these biomarkers to determine the prognosis of patients.

Formulation of personalized treatment plans

The relationship between specific biomarkers and patient survival varies across different types of CUP [169]. In recent years, significant advances have been made in TOO identification technology for CUP, allowing us to more effectively recognize the tissue characteristics of tumors with unknown origins. This advancement may assist doctors in formulating personalized treatment plans that better align with patients’ actual conditions [62]. In the vast majority of CUPs, the most frequently mutated gene is TP53, found in 49%-55% of cases, followed by KRAS at 16%-20% and CDKN2A at 18. 6%-22% [170–172]. Currently, targeted therapies for EGFR, MEK, BRAF, HER2, and KIT have demonstrated efficacy in some studies. Compared to traditional treatments, targeted drug strategies based on tissue traceability may be associated with improved response rates and survival [172]. Recently, researchers have identified NTRK gene fusions in some CUP patients and confirmed through small-scale clinical trials that these patients respond well to NTRK inhibitors, achieving notable therapeutic effects [173]. In addition, through TOO analysis, doctors can identify which patients are most likely to benefit from immunotherapy, allowing for more precise treatment. This finding suggests that TOO identification technology may inform new directions for existing treatment strategies and may aid in identifying new biomarkers to guide therapy. In a study, researchers achieved targeted management of patients with different tumor origins through molecular (including NGS from tissue and/or cell-free DNA) and immunoassays (including PD-L1, TMB, and MSI testing) [169]. Therefore, by considering both the TOO information and the molecular characteristics of patients, it is possible to reduce empirical treatment. TOO identification may be used to optimize clinical treatment strategies, enabling certain patients to benefit from specific targeted therapies and immunotherapy. In the future, we must continue to explore and optimize relevant technologies to improve the overall management of CUP and advance towards true precision medicine.

Existing challenges and future development directions

Although TOO traceability technology has shown some success in clinical settings, and numerous challenges persist. For instance, effectively integrating data from various sources to determine the best treatment plan, as well as applying these new technologies in clinical trials, are current focal points of research [174]. Ensuring quality control during sample collection and analysis is important. Additionally, as data volume increases, the complexity of accurately interpreting and utilizing this data also rises, requiring expertise and extensive experience to overcome these difficulties. Furthermore, for TOO traceability technology to be implemented in clinical practice, a certain infrastructure and technical support are required, especially under conditions of limited detection and analysis resources.

The success of TOO traceability research depends on collaborative efforts and cooperation among multiple fields, including pathology, medicine, and data science. Interdisciplinary collaboration not only fosters new discoveries but also accelerates the development of disciplines. While various TOO identification methods already exist, a thorough evaluation of their effectiveness in precision medicine still requires further investigation [175]; combining artificial intelligence and machine learning may enhance the analytical capability of TOO data and provide support for clinical decision-making. Moreover, further investigation of biomarkers, particularly those associated with different tumor types, will enhance the clinical application value of TOO traceability.

Conclusion

The precise identification of TOO in CUP has evolved considerably, driven by advances in genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and AI‑driven multi‑modal integration. However, significant challenges remain, including data heterogeneity, limited external validation, model interpretability, and regulatory barriers. Addressing these obstacles will require interdisciplinary collaboration, standardized data‑sharing platforms, and the development of explainable AI systems that can be integrated into routine clinical workflows. With continued refinement of both technical solutions and regulatory frameworks, TOO identification is expected to transition from a research tool to a cornerstone of personalized CUP management, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes.
