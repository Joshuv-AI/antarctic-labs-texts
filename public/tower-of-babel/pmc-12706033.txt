Climate-Informed flood damage assessment in the cropland area across the midwestern USA
Authors: Rehenuma Lazin, Xinyi Shen, Emmanouil Anagnostou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we developed a climate-informed convolutional neural network (CNN) model to estimate flood damages (in acres) in cropland areas (corn and soybean) across the midwestern USA. We first trained and evaluated the CNN model using gridMET datasets from 2008 to 2020, which serve as the reference dataset for downscaling the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 5 (CMIP5) projections. We then applied the downscaled climate variables in the CNN model to estimate crop damages for the historical baseline period (1976–2005) and the future mid-century period (2041–2070). Results indicate a wide range of damages, spanning from a − 40% to a + 120% by mid-century in the Midwestern counties of the United States. Most climate models project higher damages in Iowa during the early season and lower damages in Minnesota counties during late-season flooding. Due to the varying trends of the environmental variables in the climate models, our model shows discrepancies in projected crop damages. Despite these uncertainties, the findings of this study provide valuable insights into potential future patterns of flood-related crop damages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27288-z.

Introduction

Food security is a complex and challenging issue the world is facing today. United Nation’s one of the sustainable development goals (SDGs) is to ensure zero hunger globally by the end of 2030. According to the latest report of the Food and Agricultural Organization (FAO)1, between 720 and 811 million people in the world faced hunger in 2020, which is around 10% of the world population. The conditions could be worsened by climate change2, as agricultural production is governed in large part by climate conditions3. Moreover, natural disasters often cause damage and disruption to crop availability. Hence, to achieve the zero-hunger goal and prepare better for the future, knowledge of climate risks on crop production needs to be extensively studied.

Climate extremes, particularly floods, cause significant damage to agricultural land and pose a great threat to food security. Flood damage to crops occurs by waterlogging (when the roots are inundated) or by complete submergence4 during the planting or growing season. In a changing climate, it is anticipated that in many parts of the world, seasonal precipitation will shift5, and warmer rainfall over snow may increase flood risks in the future6,7. As a result, floods may occur more frequently, crop loss might become higher8–18, and ensuring sufficient food for an increasing population will become more challenging8. A recent study9 reported that increasing spring floods in the future might cause more crop loss in the midwestern USA. Another study10 observed a 21% increase in rice damage due to floods in the 2050s by developing a regression-based stage-loss economic model from the MRI-CGCM3 climate model in Vietnam. The combination of the increasing food demand with increasing flood-damaged crop production could lead to widespread global unrest and hunger in the future. Hence, the task of securing food for the increasing population and reducing the damages of natural disasters is critical to social security and sustainable development.

Downscaled meteorological forecasts containing precipitation, temperature, and radiation that trigger floods are produced at fine spatiotemporal resolution (4 km) for the next century11. Hence, it is possible to anticipate future flood-damaged croplands using a damage prediction tool. While physical models12–16 can successfully simulate the floods, estimating flood-related crop damages involves understanding the complex interaction between crop growth and its resistance to flooding17–27. Moreover, estimation through physical processes requires detailed hydrologic variables such as flood duration, depth, and flow velocities, which are expensive to obtain. Other challenges in mechanistically assessing crop damages include the lack of knowledge on crop growth stage (vegetative or reproductive stage) and cultivation management, which are often unavailable in the flood-affected region. Recent drone-based community efforts18 can provide such information, although their coverage remains limited in scale.

Acknowledging these limitations, data-driven machine learning or deep learning models have been proved quite successful in recent years. A novel study in deep learning19 showed remote sensing driven deep learning-based models such as convolutional neural networks (CNN) and Long-Short-Term-Memory (LSTM) can estimate crop yields with reliable skills. The good performance of the CNN model in flood damage assessment in the cropland area using meteorological variables has motivated us to extend the application of the model by incorporating projected weather variables in future climate. We extract meteorological predictors from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 5 (CMIP520), which provides projections for the twenty-first century spanning from 2006 to 2100 in two carbon emission scenarios, referred to as representative concentration pathways (RCP) 4.5 and 8.5.

In this study, we illustrated the climate change impacts on the flood-damaged cropland trends over the Midwestern USA. Specifically, we predicted the flood-damaged cropland area (in acres) at the county level in future climate scenarios. We selected the counties of Iowa, Illinois, Minnesota, and Missouri (Fig. A1 (a) in the Appendix) since in this region soybean and corn are mostly damaged by flood almost every year. A recent study reported that precipitation extremes have increased in these parts of the United States, indicating that climate change may exacerbate the flooding impacts in the future21. Fig. A1 (b) in the appendix shows the past flood-damaged area (in acres) for corn and soybean in the selected counties. The data shows that more damage occurs during the early season (May-June) for both crops compared to the late season (July-November). Hence, two separate models are developed to predict early- and late-season flood-damaged cropland areas in the Midwestern USA.

We used the CNN model that we developed in our previous research22 to estimate flood-damaged cropland area for corn and soybean. The performance of this CNN model in the previous study demonstrated a mean relative error within ± 25% for unseen years and a relative root mean square error ranging from 35% to 75% in most counties. Here, for future climate impact analysis, we used the model version with meteorological forcing inputs for both early and late seasons. The results of this study can provide important predictions to the scientific community and policymakers for necessary initiatives to reduce the negative impact of future climate change on food security.

Results

In this section, we describe the results on the model performance and crop damage trends due to flood under future climate scenarios.

Four-fold cross-validation by leaving one group of counties out.

In the K-fold cross-validation process, each fold is trained for up to around 2,500 iterations. The performance was measured by root mean square error (RMSE) at every 500 iterations. Initially, the RMSE of both training and validation sets is high, eventually decreasing almost monotonically with subsequent training steps to a low converged value (see Fig. 1). The iteration stops after around 10,000 iterations, when the RMSE of the validation set starts to increase, to prevent overfitting. Since the damage is greater from early-season flooding, the RMSE of the early-season prediction is also higher than that of the late season for both crops.

In Fig. 1, the dotted line represents the training results of our previous study with North American Land Data Assimilation System (NLDAS) meteorological inputs (reference), and the solid line shows the results of the current model with gridMET data. For corn during the early season, the RMSE at convergence is similar to the NLDAS reference model (around 8,000 acres). During the late season for corn, the convergence RMSE with gridMET is slightly higher than the previous model. Again, for soybean, during the early season, at the end of training, the lowest RMSE becomes close to 4,000 acres, close to the reference NLDAS model. In the late season, for soybean, the convergence RMSE with the gridMET (3,000 acres) is higher than the reference model (2,000 acres). The reason for the relatively low performance for soybean during the late century (compared to the NLDAS model) could be the exclusion of the important predictor, longwave radiation, in the current model.

Predicting damage for each county.

We also evaluated the performance of the trained model with the gridMET dataset spatially by showing it in terms of all-year mean actual damage (2008–2020), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), RMSE, percentage of RMSE (ratio of RMSE to mean actual damage), Bias (difference of the predicted and observed damages), and correlation coefficient. For both crops the damages are higher in Iowa, Illinois, lower Minnesota, and upper Missouri in both early season (above 5,000 acres) and late season (above 3,000 acres).

Predictions for unseen years

To assess how the model performs on unseen data from different years, we select four specific years for each crop. During each training iteration, data from one year is entirely excluded for all counties and reserved for testing. Cross-validation and subsampling during training are still based on groups of counties. As a result, four models are trained and validated, each time leaving out one year’s data for testing while using the remaining years for training and validation.

Based on high and low damage years (as shown in Fig. A1 (b) in the Appendix), we selected 2008, 2014, 2016, and 2017 for corn and 2008 and 2015–2017 for soybeans. Each boxplot represents the distribution of errors across all the counties. Relative error is calculated by dividing the relative difference between predicted and observed values by the mean damages. As shown in Fig. 3, the model’s relative error falls within ± 0.25 (the dotted lines) for most counties (as indicated by the median of the boxplots) across both seasons for corn and soybeans.

However, the model exhibits higher error for soybeans in 2016, a low-damage year (Fig. A1 (b) in the Appendix), particularly during the late season. There is substantial variability in relative error across counties for all tests. We attribute this to limited data availability on similar events, resulting in a weaker relationship between the predictors and crop damage patterns. With longer data records the model performance can be improved. Overall, the model can predict damages for extreme high-flood years like 2008 and 2014 (corn) and 2015 (soybean) within ± 0.25 error, demonstrating that the CNN model is suitable for climate impact analysis.

Predicting damages under future climate scenarios.

We predicted the crop damages due to flood with the inputs from the selected CMIP5 models to the CNN model for the baseline (1976–2005) and the future mid-century (2041–2070) period. By comparing the damages of the baseline and the future mid-century from the corresponding CMIP5 model, we observed the probable changes in the damage patterns over the Midwestern USA for corn and soybean. For each county, we estimate the % change of the damage by computing the relative changes in damages between the baseline and future period.

Figure A3 shows the model-estimated damages (in acres) for corn based on the CMIP5 projections for the RCP 4.5 and 8.5 in the early and late seasons. Almost all the models indicate higher damages in comparison with the baseline for the majority of the counties (median of the boxplots). However, in the early season, BNU-ESM shows slightly lower damages in the future in the RCP 8.5 scenario. For better visualization, we presented the spatial pattern of the damage trends in the future in % change over the Midwestern counties of the study area. As shown in Fig. 4, different models show damages differently in terms of sign and location. For the early season in RCP 4.5, CNRM-CM5 shows higher damages in Missouri, while MIROC-CHEM shows higher cropland loss in the Iowa, Illinois, and Minnesota region. In RCP 8.5, IPSL shows significantly higher damages (above 100%) in the southern Minnesota and Iowa region. Discrepancy in the sign and magnitude of the spatial damage pattern is also noticeable in the case of the late season. In RCP 4.5, in the Iowa, Illinois, and Missouri region, BNU-ESM, IPSL, and MIROC-ESM show higher damages (above 25%), while CNRM-CM5 and CanESM2 show a decreasing damage trend (below − 10%) in these regions. Overall, in the late season, the majority of the models show decreasing damage (below − 25%) for corn in the counties of Minnesota.

Discussion and study limitation

Different GCM models exhibit variable future crop damage trends by floods. We analyzed the projections of each predictor weather variable from the GCM models to understand the diverse crop loss patterns. Projections of each weather predictor also indicate variation in terms of sign and magnitude. For example, as shown in Fig. 7, compared to the baseline period, BNU-ESM shows a lower precipitation trend and decreasing trend for humidity in both RCP scenarios. Warm and humid conditions deteriorate corn health during flooding due to the development of fungi and crop diseases23. Fig. S1 in the supplementary materials shows that precipitation and humidity are both important predictors for early-season corn damage estimation, and with high precipitation and high humidity, corn damage increases. Since in projections from BNU-ESM (Fig. 7), both precipitation and humidity do not show an increasing trend, a lower corn damage trend (Fig. A3 in Appendix) is observed in the future mid-century. On the contrary, Soybean can survive better in cool and cloudy or humid condition24. Therefore, at lower humidity soybean damage increases. In case of soybean along with precipitation and humidity, temperature (both maximum and minimum) and radiation also play important roles in the early season flood damages (Fig. S1). Cool, cloudy days and cool, clear nights increase the chances of the survival of a flooded soybean crop25. As shown in Fig. 7, minimum or nighttime temperature is projected to be higher (above 5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm \:^\circ{C}}$$\end{document}) in both RCP 4.5 and 8.5 by the MIROC-ESM, and MIROC-ESM-CHEM, BNU-ESM, and CanESM2 model against the baseline temperature in most of the counties. As a result, these models show higher soybean damages in the early season (upper panel of Fig. A4 in the appendix).

The varying trend of each environmental predictor (Fig. 7) such as precipitation, temperature, radiation, and humidity, contribute to the degree of uncertainty and disagreement among the climate models regarding the future crop damage trends. Each climate model represents the interaction of these environmental predictors differently that cause divergent climate projections. Additionally, the spatial variability of these predictors at county level can further amplify discrepancies in the projections. Local factor like the topography can influence how environmental conditions can affect crop health. Acknowledging these uncertainties, climate model agreement map (Fig. 6) can provide valuable insights for future risk management.”

Crop damages caused by floods do not only depend on the precipitation but also depend on the surrounding temperature, radiation, and humidity (Fig. S1 in the supplementary materials). Predictor importance tests of the CNN model developed in this study indicate that during the late season flooding, crop damages are mainly triggered by other variables than precipitation. In the late season floods, both soybean and corn are at the harvesting period and need more oxygen for survival. Temperature plays a significant role, as a warm atmosphere accelerates depletion of stored energy26 and oxygen through respiration. Thus, in the late season, the higher damage trend of corn and soybean can be attributed to the higher temperature trend by the climate models (Fig. 7).

The limitations of the study include the consideration of the changes in the meteorological variables only. The actual crop damage depends on cloud cover, soil moisture conditions prior to flooding, crop phenology, and rate of soil drainage. But these factors are not considered in the modeling framework. The morphological property HAND and crop masks are assumed to be the same in the future, while in reality, these properties change rapidly. Large uncertainties are also inherited from the disagreement among climate models. Moreover, due to the limited availability of the crop loss data (2008–2020), we could not evaluate the model performance for the baseline period (1976–2005) of the climate models. Again, due to the unavailability of the downscaled CMIP6 projections, we could not incorporate updated projections in this study. In future research, we plan to downscale the CMIP6 projection and analyze the crop damage trends due to future flooding. Despite these limitations, the future crop damage trends reported in this study can help farmers, stakeholders, and policymakers reduce the flood losses in the croplands by taking initiatives and effective planning.

Conclusions

Climate change is likely to hamper the progress of future global food security through disruption in agricultural production. Hence, knowledge of climate risks along with damage assessments can help plan for improving sustainability and reduce losses. In this study, we mainly focus on estimating the future crop damages by floods across the midwestern USA. Here, we extend the application of a deep learning model by combining it with projections from a climate model in flood damage estimation. To reduce the computation, we consider the climate models that project higher precipitation in the future across the Midwestern counties. The findings of this study indicate that:

Despite the disparity in the sign and magnitude of the damage trends, knowledge of possible flood damage patterns for specific crops under future climate scenarios can help adopt initiatives, such as slightly changing the crop calendar by planting ahead or harvesting early according to the changing climatic conditions, preparing budgets for the insurance agencies in case of extreme damages, and educating farmers about climate change and how they can be affected by the crop damages. In the future, the pre-trained CNN model can be used to study climate change impacts on the data-scarce, critical flood zones. The model can be improved by considering changes in the future crop pattern, rather than considering the constant crop masks in the future. Moreover, adding other properties into the model, such as initial soil moisture condition and drainage facilities in the field, can improve the model performance.

Methodology

Data for training

We used input predictors such as precipitation, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, solar radiation, and specific humidity that provide insights about flood dynamics. We also incorporated a static topographical predictor, Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND27,28 which represents the relative elevation of a location to its nearest stream, a commonly used flood-topographic index. We first trained the model with the meteorological variables from the reference gridMET dataset from 2008\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-$$\end{document}2020 and evaluated the model performance with respect to the trained baseline model with the NLDAS dataset in our previous study. Then we used the trained model (with gridMET29 inputs) to predict future crop damages by flood projections from CMIP5 models. We retrieved the projection of the meteorological weather variables from Multivariate Adaptive Constructed Analogs (MACA11 which is a statistical method for downscaling global climate models for the USA region. In MACA the variables are downscaled to 4 km spatial resolution at a daily scale with respect to the reference gridMET dataset29. In this study, we utilized CMIP5 projections because the MACA dataset currently offers downscaled climate products only for CMIP5.

For both early and late seasons, we used the data at 3-day intervals. We aggregated 3-days total value for precipitation and averaged the 3 days daily value for other predictors. Then we extracted each predictor from pixels masked by the annual cropland maps (see Fig. 8a) available from 2008 to 202030. For future years, we considered the crop pattern the same as the most recent crop mask of 2020.

As target data, we used the monthly flood-damaged corn and soybean area per county from the Risk Management Agency (RMA) at the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA RMA31 and aggregated them as corresponding seasonal damages. We used 2008\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-$$\end{document}2020 to train and validate the model. Due to the wide magnitude range of the damage, we converted it to a logarithmic scale.

Input data structure and model architecture

We used the 3D histograms of the meteorological variables as inputs to train the model22,. As shown in Fig. 8a, as step 1, we extracted the values from each predictor using the binary crop mask. In step 2, we calculate the histogram of 32 bins for each time step. We then computed the 1D histogram for the cropland pixels at 32 evenly distributed bins at each time step, and finally, in step 3, we stacked a predictor’s 1D histograms of all time steps to form one 2D histogram image, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{p=1\:}^{T\times\:b}$$\end{document}where the pixel values indicate the counts for the corresponding bin and time step. For the static HAND data, we duplicated the bin count at each time step of the histogram. Finally, stacking the 2D histogram images of all predictors, we get the input 3D histogram images, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{H}}^{T\times\:b\times\:p\:}$$\end{document} to input the CNN model, where the superscript T refers to the time series, the superscript \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:b$$\end{document} refers to the histogram bins, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} refers to the predictors. Thus, during early season histogram size is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{H}}^{20\times\:32\times\:6\:}$$\end{document}, and in the late season histogram is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{H}}^{50\times\:32\times\:6\:}.$$\end{document} By generating a histogram, we assumed the permutation invariance holds—that is, the distribution of cropland pixels spectral is more informative in estimating the total damaged areas of croplands than the geolocation of these pixels. Most importantly, by incorporating 1D histograms derived from the 2D images of each predictor, we achieved dimensionality reduction and reduced computational costs significantly.

The input 3D image (see Fig. 8b) is subsequently passed through the CNN architecture. Each CNN block consists of one stride 1 convolution, a stride 2 convolution, and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) as the activation function. After stride 1 convolution, the output channel numbers are kept the same as the inputs and doubled after stride 2 convolution. The channel numbers are optimized during hyperparameter tuning. We used stride-2 convolution instead of the pooling layer to down-sample the channels. After three convolutional layers, the outputs are flattened and used as input to the fully connected (FC) layer, and in the end, the final output of this regression-based CNN is the flood-damaged area in acres. Each convolution is followed by batch normalization with a batch size of 32. We used the Adam optimizer for faster convergence and a 0.5 dropout rate to prevent overfitting.

We performed K-fold cross validation to train the model and divided the K-fold groups in a way that each group contains 25% randomly selected counties from each state. Such randomness can help prevent the model overfitted to a specific location and by training the model based on the county groups, we preserved the extreme high damage year in both train and validation sets31. We also test the model performance for the unseen years by holding out one flood year data of all the counties from the training and validation dataset. Thus, this study aimed to provide a comprehensive picture of the damage caused by floods under future climate scenarios.

Climate model selection for future flood damage assessment

To assess the possible range of future climate impact on the flooding in the cropland, we first observed the changes in the precipitation trend during the mid-century (2041\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-$$\end{document}2070) against the historical baseline period (1976\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-$$\end{document}2005) by comparing the climatology of the seasonal total precipitation. Figure 9 shows the precipitation trend in the midwestern counties (in % change) as boxplots from all 20 CMIP5 models for both the RCP 4.5 and 8.5 in the early (May-June) and late (July-Nov) seasons. We selected the models that show a positive precipitation trend for both RCP scenarios. We assumed that a positive precipitation trend may cause more floods in the future and demonstrated how different climate scenarios showing an increase in precipitation may be translated into an increase of flood damages on croplands. Thus, to cover the varied range of the precipitation trend, we selected CNRM-CM5 (more than + 25% in both seasons), Can ESM2 (more than 35% in the early season), IPSL-CM5B-LR, and MIROC-ESM-CHEM, MIROC-ESM, and BNU-ESM (more than + 10% in the early season) based on the results in most of the counties (median of the boxplot). Since the damages are usually higher in the early season, we selected the models that show a positive or higher precipitation trend in the May-June period.
