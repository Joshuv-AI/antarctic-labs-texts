Construction of quaternary stereocenters via Ru-catalyzed asymmetric ring-closing metathesis
Authors: Shaofeng Li, Qingjing Yang, Rongjun Hu, Yichuan Zheng, Xiaojing Gao, Junjie Cao, Jianchun Wang, Bo Chen, Chen Xu, Xiangyou Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsymmetric olefin metathesis, which forms C = C double bonds while simultaneously inducing chirality in molecules, is highly valuable in chemical synthesis. The investigation of strategies for the creation of quaternary stereocenters has mostly focused on Mo-based catalytic systems, whilst the utilization of user-friendly Ru-catalysts has been rarely reported. Here, by precisely synthesizing stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts via controllable C–H activation, we accomplish highly efficient asymmetric ring-closing metathesis (ARCM) for the production of chiral heterocyclic compounds with quaternary stereocenters. These Ru-catalysts facilitate the ARCM of trienes with distinct olefins for the synthesis of six-membered ethers and amines, as well as ARCM of trienes with all-terminal olefins to yield five- and six-membered ethers. Mechanistic investigations involving the initiation of the ARCM reaction by ring-opening metathesis of a “single-addition” cyclopropene derivative provide strong evidence for the interconversion between the essential exo and endo Ru-alkylidenes, and DFT calculations help to further substantiate the favored pathway and the stereo-controlling mechanism. This study provides an attractive approach for the design and production of chiral Grubbs catalysts, thereby promoting a variety of asymmetric olefin metathesis processes.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Catalyst synthesis, Asymmetric catalysis

Introduction

Asymmetric ring-closing metathesis (ARCM) is a straightforward and efficient approach to generate quaternary and tertiary stereocenters. Although Mo-catalyzed ARCM reactions have been used to synthesize a variety of cyclic ethers and amines with quaternary and tertiary stereocenters with high enantioselectivities1–10, little success has been achieved in this area by employing user-friendly Ru-catalysts11–25, and the reported Ru-catalyzed ARCM reactions have been largely restricted to the construction of tertiary stereocenters. The recently developed Ru-catalyzed asymmetric ring-closing ene-yne metathesis (ARCEYM) can efficiently construct quaternary stereogenic centers, but the substrate scope needs to be expanded26,27. Chiral Ru alkylidene complexes adopt a rigid octahedral structure with six coordination-sites (Fig.1a). The different orientations of alkylidenes can lead to three possible side-bound and three possible bottom-bound transition states28–31. As a result, the chiral environment of the four-membered stereo-determining transition states will become increasingly intricate and ambiguous, significantly reducing the enantioselectivity of the processes. This could be the primary reason for the relatively poor performance of the chiral Ru-catalysts in asymmetric olefin metathesis. The previous reports by Hoveyda32–36 and Grubbs37–39 indicated that stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts exhibit a commendable level of efficacy. This is mainly caused by the proximity of the catalysts’ stereogenic Ru-center and the chirality of the substrates inside the metallacyclobutane intermediates, as seen in Fig. 1b. Consequently, stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts capable of accurately regulating the stereo-determining transition states would enhance the widespread application of asymmetric olefin metathesis.

Development of stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts and the application in the asymmetric synthesis of quaternary stereocenters.

a The complication of stereo-control for Ru-catalyzed asymmetric olefin metathesis. b The advantages of stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts in asymmetric olefin metathesis. c This work: selective synthesis of stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts by tunable C–H activation and construction of quaternary and tertiary stereocenters via Ru-catalyzed asymmetric ring-closing metathesis (ARCM).

This work reports the accurate synthesis of stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts with Δ (right-handed) or Λ (left-handed) configuration through controllable C–H activation, and demonstrates their competence in a broad scope of ARCM reactions that remain challenging by Ru-catalysts (Fig. 1c). We recently reported Ru-catalyzed, skeleton-dependent ARCM to construct quaternary stereocenters40. However, the substrates were limited to trienes with cyclic amides. Furthermore, the ARCM of all-terminal trienes is particularly difficult, regardless of whether Ru- or Mo-catalysts are used2–4,7–10,38. In this study, quaternary stereocenters were generated in excellent enantioselectivities by ARCM-based desymmetrization of trienes with various structural skeletons, particularly including substrates with all-terminal olefins. Additionally, ARCM-based kinetic resolutions yielded good enantioselectivities for both trienes and ring-closing metathesis products bearing quaternary or tertiary stereocenters. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents an ARCM‑based kinetic resolution for the construction of quaternary stereocenters. Finally, mechanistic studies suggest that rapid equilibria exist between exo and endo Ru-alkylidene intermediates. By integrating this essential data with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we were able to propose the favored reaction pathway and justify the stereo-controlling mechanism.

Results

Catalyst Development

In order to achieve a broad scope of ARCM to construct tertiary and quaternary stereocenters within various structural skeletons, the first objective of this study is to selectively and accurately produce cyclometalated, octahedral stereogenic-at-Ru-catalysts with specific configurations. In such catalysts, the strong electron-donating capabilities of both the C–Ru bond and the NHC ligand limit the alkylidenes to occupying either the 3 or 5 positions in the Ru-octahedral geometry (Fig. 1a)30, corresponding to Δ- or Λ-configuration of the Ru-center respectively41. As illustrated in Fig. 2, through bis-substitutions of Ru-di-chloride or Ru-di-OTf complexes (bearing N-adamantyl-N’-aryl substituted chiral NHC ligands) with pivalates (-OPiv) and subsequent pivalate-assisted selective C–H bond activation, we endeavored to synthesize a range of Ru catalysts (Fig. 2). The activation of C–Hb bonds, resulting in the production of Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalysts, became more advantageous at the temperature of 50 oC. Conversely, the activation of C–Ha bonds, leading to the development of Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts, became more favorable at 0 oC. Based on these findings, it may be inferred that Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalysts exhibit greater thermodynamic favorability, while Λ-(S, S)- Ru catalysts demonstrate greater kinetic favorability. N-aryl substituents of the NHC ligands also play a role in the selectivity of C–Ha or C–Hb bond activation. It seems that with sterically less bulky N-aryls, such as N-mesityl, C–Hb activation resulting in the Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalysts becomes more advantageous, while with sterically bulkier N-aryls, such as N-DIPP (DIPP: 2,6-diisopropylphenyl), C–Ha activation leading to the Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts becomes more favorable.

One-pot synthesis of Δ-(S, R)- and Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts.

Thermodynamic control favors C–Hb activation at 50 °C to give Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalysts (Left column). Kinetic control favors C–Ha activation at 0 °C to give Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts (Right column).

We also noticed that the ratios of Δ-(S, R)- and Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts varied. Therefore, we would like to investigate if the C–H activation-based cyclometallation processes are reversible (Fig. 3a). Firstly, we employed 1H NMR, using 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene as the internal standard, to observe the changes in the diagnostic benzylidene peaks of a mixture containing 0.7:1 of Δ-(S, R)-Ru-catalyst 2A-OPiv and Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalyst 2B-OPiv in the presence of pivalic acid (HOPiv) (Fig. 3a, left). After 12 hours, a large amount of 2B-OPiv was converted to 2A-OPiv, and a mixture of 2A-OPiv and 2B-OPiv was obtained in 88% yield with a ratio of 7.9:1. The conversion from the Δ-(S, R)- Ru-catalyst 1A-OPiv to its thermodynamically less favored Λ-(S, S)-counterpart was also examined (Fig. 3a, right). We further stirred the pure 1A-OPiv with 5 equivalents of HOAc at 50 oC for 19 hours, which resulted in a mixture of Ru-catalysts 1A-OAc and 1B-OAc in 79% yield with a ratio of 6.1:1. Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalyst 1B-OAc was possibly derived from Δ-(S, R)-Ru-catalyst 1A-OPiv through HOAc assisted C–Ru bond cleavage and subsequent C–H activation. The two findings shown in Fig. 3a supports that Δ-(S, R)- and Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts are interconvertible under acidic conditions.

Investigating the reversibility of the C–H activation processes and efficient synthesis of thermodynamically less favored Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts.

a Conversions between Δ-(S, R)- and Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts. b H/D exchange experiments. c Synthesis of Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalyst with sterically unhindered NHC ligand.

Secondly, we subjected a mixture consisting of Ru-catalysts 2A-OAc and 2B-OAc at a ratio of 1:1.9 to d1-acetic acid at 50 oC (Fig. 3b), and we obtained a 4.2:1 ratio of 2A-OAc and 2B-OAc after 19 hours. Particularly, Ha of the Ru-catalyst 2A-OAc was observed to be deuterated. Due to overlaps of the Ha-region of 2A-OAc in 1H NMR, the percentage of the deuteration was determined by using its nitrato analogue 2A-NO3. The deuterated Ru-catalyst 2A-OAc was plausibly derived from Ru-catalyst 2B-OAc via deuterated carboxylic acid-assisted cleavage of the C–Ru bond and carboxylate-assisted C–H activation of the Ru-dicarboxylate intermediate. Previous reports indicate that these reactions follow a concerted metalation-deprotonation (CMD) mechanism42, and the results in Fig. 3a, b collectively demonstrate the reversibility of the CMD-mediated C–H activation process and the difficulty in the selective synthesis of thermodynamically less favored Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalysts.

Then, we would like to demonstrate how to preferentially synthesize thermodynamically less favored Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts bearing sterically unhindered N-aryl groups, such as 2B, which were difficult to be obtained using the one-pot C–H activation procedure depicted in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 3c, taking Ru-complexes containing N-adamantyl-N’−2,6-diethyl-4-methyl phenyl substituted NHC ligand for example, we envisioned to increase the selectivity of the C–H activation by differentiating the basicity as well as the steric hindrance of the two anionic ligands in the Ru-complexes. When 2 C was treated with NaOAc, the chloride ion was substituted by the acetate to form an intermediate 2E. The in situ generated 2E immediately undergoes C–H activation to form a 2:1 ratio of Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalyst 2B-OPiv and Δ-(S, R)-Ru-catalyst 2A-OAc. When 2D was subsequently treated with NaOAc and NaOPiv, 2 F was generated in situ. It is pleasing to observe that the ratio increases to 7:1 with the Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalyst being dominant during the C–H activation. In the intermediate 2 F, the pivalate (-OPiv) is more basic as well as larger than the acetate (-OAc), resulting in the preferential C–Ha activation and leading to the preferential formation of Λ-(S, S)-Ru-catalyst.

Reaction development

With reliable methods for selectively synthesizing Δ- or Λ-Ru catalysts, we then investigated whether Ru-catalyzed ARCM of trienes could be used to enantioselectively generate quaternary stereocenters (Fig. 4). Trienes 1 and 2 are two representative substrates, with the former having a unique 1-terminal olefin and enantiotopic 1,1-disubstituted olefins and the latter having all-terminal olefins. For triene 1, Δ-Ru catalysts exhibit moderate to good enantioselectivity. The substitution pattens on the chiral NHC backbones were initially examined. Catalyst 4 A with two point-chiralities in the NHC backbone was tested, and it produced 1a with 84% ee albeit in a low yield. The use of Ru-catalyst 5 A with a single point chirality in the NHC ligand provides 1a with 54% yield and slightly lower enantioselectivity. By changing the single point chirality in the NHC backbone from C2 to C3, Ru-catalyst 1 A can catalyze ARCM of 1 in 90% yield and 87% ee. Increasing the steric bulk of the C3-substitution in the NHC ligands to i-propyl (6 A) and t-butyl (7 A) groups has a deleterious effect on both the enantioselectivity and reactivity of the ARCM reaction. Ru-catalysts 8 A with N−2,6-dimethyl-5-fluorobenzene and 2 A with N−2,6-diethyl-5-methylbenzene exhibit comparable reactivity and enantioselectivity to Ru-catalyst 1 A. Λ-Ru catalysts were also investigated for the ARCM reaction of 1, however, these catalysts typically yielded 1a with low yields and limited enantioselectivities. In the case of triene 2, Δ-Ru catalysts mostly produce 2a in high yields, although with moderate enantioselectivities. We subsequently examined the Λ-Ru catalyzed ARCM of 2 to determine if improved enantioselectivity could be achieved. Designed as the left-handed counterpart of Ru-catalyst 2 A, 2B allows for the ARCM of 2 with excellent enantioselectivity and reactivity. Ru-catalyst 9B with i-propyl substituted NHC ligand produces 2a with somewhat reduced enantioselectivity. Increasing the steric bulk of the N-aryl group through 2,6-di-i-propyl substitution (3B) has no influence on enantioselectivity but dramatically reduces the reaction’s reactivity. Ru-catalysts 10B and 11B with unsymmetrical substituted N-aryl groups in the NHC ligands were also tested for the ARCM of 2, however, only moderate enantioselectivities of 2a were obtained. Overall, for both types of substrates 1 and 2, Δ- or Λ-Ru catalysts can now achieve enantioselectivities comparable or even superior to those reported for Mo-based catalysts2,7,8.

Screening of Ru-catalysts for ARCM to construct quaternary stereocenters.

General condition: 1 or 2 (0.1 mmol), Ru catalyst (5 mol %), PhMe, 23 °C, 48 h. Yields were determined by crude 1H NMR using 1,4-dinitrobenzene as an internal standard. Enantiomeric excesses (ees) were determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

With optimal Ru-catalysts for trienes 1 and 2, we then attempted to broaden the scope of substrates for both types of ARCM reactions (Fig. 5). Firstly, ARCM-based desymmetrization of prochiral trienes with unique 1-terminal olefin and enantiotopic 1,1-disubstituted olefins was evaluated (Fig. 5a). Various oxygen- and nitrogen-containing heterocycles with remote homoallylic quaternary carbon centers were synthesized in high enantioselectivities. A wide range of substituents at the pro-prochiral centers, including aryls, hetero-aryls, esters, amides, ketones and alkyls, were all well-tolerated under the catalysis of Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalysts 1 A or 8 A. During the synthesis of cyclic ethers 3a-11a, we observed that the electronic properties of aryl substituents have little effects on the enantioselectivities, and good ees can be attained even with hetero-aryl groups. Nevertheless, the enantioselectivity is more largely affected by the steric effects of the aryl groups, leading to a slight reduction in ees for the products containing ortho-substituted aryls, such as 8a and 9a. The electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectrum of 4a, both experimental and calculated, revealed that the primary enantiomer has an (S)-configuration (Supplementary Fig. S50). Cyclic ethers 12a-14a with carbonyl-based substituents at the quaternary stereocenters were also obtained in good to excellent enantioselectivities. In the case of chiral cyclic amines 15a-26a, a variety of phenyl and hetero-aryl groups were tolerated, and the obtained ees were typically larger than 90%. The presence of smaller alkyl substituents, such as methyl group, resulted in a decrease in enantioselectivity (25a). Conversely, larger alkyl substituents, such as i-propyl group, yielded high enantioselectivity (26a).

Substrate scope for the enantioselective synthesis of quaternary carbon centers through Ru-catalyzed ARCM.

a Trienes with unique 1-terminal olefin and enantiotopic 1,1-disubstituted olefins. b Trienes with all-terminal olefins. General condition: Triene (0.1 mmol), Ru catalyst (5 mol %), PhMe, 23 °C, 48 h. Yields of isolated products were given. Ees were determined by HPLC.

We then focused on ARCM reactions of unhindered, all-terminal olefins, which have been confirmed to be particularly challenging (Fig. 5b)37. To our delight, under the catalysis Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalyst 2B, the synthesis of both five- and six-membered cyclic ethers with allylic quaternary stereocenters was achieved with high enantioselectivities. Substitutions of various aryls and hetero-aryls at the stereocenters were also well-tolerated. It is worth noting that triene 32 with the bulky adamantyl group has both good reactivity and enantio-control. We also noted that the enantioselectivities produced by six-membered cyclic silyl ethers (33a-38a) were excellent, and ees values are generally above 95%. Overall, the findings presented in Fig. 5 indicated that this Ru-catalyzed ARCM provides an effectively complementary method for the creation of quaternary stereocenters.

Inspired by the success of Ru-catalyzed ARCM-based desymmetrization, we hypothesized that these stereogenic-at-Ru catalysts should also be applicable to ARCM-based kinetic resolution, given that both desymmetrization and kinetic resolution are belonging to chiral recognition processes. However, ARCM-based kinetic resolution of racemic substrates with quaternary stereocenters has never been reported previously. As shown in Fig. 6, racemic allyl amines (39-44) with various substitutions at the quaternary stereocenters underwent the Ru-catalyzed kinetic resolution very smoothly, and in each case both the recovered starting materials and cyclic products were obtained in good enantioselectivities at approximately 50% conversions. To our delight, Ru-catalyzed ARCM-based kinetic resolutions of racemic allyl amines (45-53) with tertiary stereocenters have broader applicability and higher resolution efficiency. On the one hand, a variety of substitutions at the stereocenters were well-tolerated, including aryl, heteroaryl, alkyl, ester, and even phosphonate groups, and on the other hand, the selectivity factors (s) are generally exceeding 100.

Ru-catalyzed ARCM-based kinetic resolution.

Calculated conversion, conv = eea/(eea+eeb); Selectivity factor (s) = ln[(1-C) (1- eea)]/ ln[(1-C) (1+ eea)]. Yields of isolated products were given. Ees were determined by HPLC.

Mechanistic Investigations

Having achieved highly efficient ARCM-based desymmetrization and kinetic resolution, we then would like to investigate the enantio-determining transtion state and preferred reaction pathway. As shown in Fig. 7, for Δ-(S, R)-Ru 1 A catalyzed ARCM of triene 1 to produce (S)−1a, the initial reaction can generate an exo Ru-alkylidene complex INT1, acting as the true catalyst. We also use ring-opening metathesis of the highly reactive and “single-addition”43 cyclopropene 54 to initiate the ARCM reaction. This leads to the formation of an exo Ru-alkylidene intermediate G that bears an opposite configuration at the Ru center with the pre-catalyst 1 A (Please see Supplementary Fig. 69–Fig. 71 for the confirmation of G). Then G can initiate ARCM of 1 to generate an endo Ru-alkylidene complex INT2 as the true catalyst. Although the true catalysts INT1 and INT2 for these two reactions have different configurations at the Ru-center, product 1a with the same absolute stereochemistry as well as the same level of enantioselectivity was obtained. This strongly indicates that the two key alkylidene species, INT1 and INT2, are interconvertible under the reaction condition.

Mechanistic investigation of ARCM of triene 1.

Comparison of ARCM of 1 using Ru-catalysts with different stereochemical configurations at the Ru-centers. ARCM of 1 initiated by Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalyst 1 A (Upper). ARCM of 1 initiated by Δ-(S, R)-Ru catalyst G (Lower).

We also conducted kinetic studies of 1A-catalyzed ARCM reaction of triene 1, as shown in Fig. 8 (also see details in Supplementary Fig. 72), indicating that both the substrate and the catalyst were determined to first-order-dependent. However, the equilibrium between pre-catalyst 1 A and true-catalyst INT1 (See details in Supplementary Fig. 73) suggests that the formation of INT1 by the reaction of triene 1 with pre-catalyst 1 A should not be rate-limiting. Because the pre-equilibrium is established, the concentration of INT1 is proportional to that of triene 1, leading to an overall first-order dependence on the substrate.

Finally, we undertook computational studies employing DFT to elucidate the favored reaction pathway and the origins of enantioselectivity (Fig. 9)30,44–52. All four reaction pathways leading to (S)−1a (pathways B and C) and (R)−1a (pathways A and D) were calculated (See details in the Supplementary Fig. 74). In the pathways commencing from the exo complex INT1, an enantio- and rate-determining metallacyclobutane formation occurs (TS1-S), characterized by a barrier of 13.5 kcal/mol, which is 2.5 kcal/mol lower than that of TS1-R (Fig. 9a, left part). This favored transition state benefits from a diminished 1,3-diaxial interaction. Notably, the 2-methylallyl group exhibits a more pronounced 1,3-diaxial interaction than the planar phenyl group, as indicated by the proximity of the H…H distance in TS1-R (Fig. 9b, top part). In contrast, while the pathways originating from the endo complex (INT2) reveal a lower energy barrier for the cycloaddition step, it is the subsequent cyclo-reversion that becomes enantio-determining (Fig. 8a, right part). Due to the same 1,3-diaxial interactions, pathway D is favored over pathway C (15.8 vs. 17.5 kcal/mol). Here, the steric repulsion between the aryl group in the catalyst and the metallacyclobutane plays an important role in favoring pathway B over pathway D (Fig. 8b, bottom part). Overall, the calculated ∆∆G‡ for enantioselectivity aligns closely with the experimental value (2.3 vs. 1.7 kcal/mol).

Computational studies on the reaction pathway and observed enantioselectivity.

a Calculated energy profiles for reaction pathways. b Optimized enantio-determining transition states.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the accurate synthesis of Δ-(S, R)- and Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts. Differentiating the basicity and size of the two carboxylate ligands on ruthenium in the C–H activation step resulted in an effective strategy for the selective synthesis of thermodynamically less favored Λ-(S, S)-Ru catalysts. With these chiral Ru catalysts, we presented highly efficient ARCM to construct heterocyclic compounds with quaternary stereocenters. High enantioselectivities were obtained for the ARCM of trienes with various structural skeletons. These catalysts also enabled Ru-catalyzed ARCM-based kinetic resolutions, and both recovered trienes and cyclic products bearing quaternary or tertiary stereocenters were obtained with high enantioselectivities. Furthermore, using a designed ring-opening metathesis of “single-addition” cyclopropene derivatives to initiate the ARCM reactions, our studies demonstrated rapid equilibria between the key exo and endo Ru-alkylidene intermediates. Finally, DFT calculations help to identify the enantio-determining transition state and elucidate the favored reaction pathway. It is hoped that the findings of this study will inspire and enable continued development of chiral Grubbs catalysts for the promotion of diverse chemical transformations based on asymmetric olefin metathesis.

Methods

General procedures for asymmetric ring-closing metathesis-based desymmetrization: In an argon-filled glovebox, triene 1-38 (0.1 mmol, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in PhMe (0.3–1 mL). To this solution was added catalyst 1 A, 8 A or 2B (0.005 mmol, 5 mol%). The reaction vial was capped and stirred for 24-48 h at room temperature. The crude mixture was concentrated and directly subjected to flash chromatography to afford the desired products 1a − 38a. Pure products were submitted to HPLC to determine ee.

General procedures for asymmetric ring-closing metathesis-based kinetic resolution: In an argon-filled glovebox, racemic allyl amine 39 − 53 (0.1 mmol, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in PhMe (1 mL). To this solution was added Ru-catalyst 1 A (0.005 mmol, 5 mol%). The reaction vial was capped and stirred (at 0 oC, 23 oC or 40 oC depending on the substrate) until the reaction conversion reached at approximately 50%. Conversions of reactions were detected by analyzing crude 1H NMR spectroscopy. Then the reaction mixture was concentrated and directedly subjected to flash chromatography to afford recovered 39a − 53a and ring closing metathesis-products 39b − 53b. Pure products were submitted to HPLC to determine ee. Conversions (C) were calculated by the following equation: C = ee1/(ee1+ee2), wherein ee1 is the enantiomeric excess of the products 39a – 53a and ee2 is the enantiomeric excess of the products 39b – 53b. Selectivity factors (s) were calculated according to Kagan’s equation: s = ln[(1 - C)(1 – ee1)]/ln[(1 - C)(1 + ee1)].
