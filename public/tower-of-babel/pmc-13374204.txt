Prediction of HIV-1 sensitivity to broadly neutralizing antibodies using statistical distribution sampling (SDS) technology
Authors: Lily He, Kaixin Pan, Youlin Shi, Kun Peter Li, Yuhua Ruan, Dan Li, Hengjian Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately predicting HIV-1 sensitivity to broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) is a critical step in advancing the development of effective therapeutic and preventive strategies against HIV-1. Due to the virus’s extraordinary genetic diversity and rapid mutation rate, traditional experimental methods for assessing bNAb sensitivity are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and limited in scalability. However, current machine learning approaches prioritize feature significance while neglecting to address class imbalance and systematic biases in multi-laboratory datasets. To address these limitations, we develop the Statistical Distribution Sampling (SDS) method, a novel statistical framework that systematically addresses data imbalance and cross-institutional variance. Firstly, the amino acid sequence is converted into a numerical vector via the k-string method. Then, antibodies are classified as effective or ineffective based on IC50/IC80 thresholds, with a stratified random subset selected as the Sampling generator. Each vector dimension’s distribution is characterized through histogram analysis and kernel density estimation. Afterwards, 500 data points are sampled from the distribution of each column for each category. The assembled Statistical Distribution Sampling (SDS) matrix integrates column features, which combined with the Sampling generator, produces the final training dataset. Antibody efficacy predictions are ultimately generated through integration with the Random Forest algorithm. This approach enables comprehensive prediction of all database sequences while achieving strong predictive performance. Through 10 independent trials per antibody, our method achieved statistically superior AUC and ACC performance compared to the LBUM and SLAPNAP benchmarks. Moreover, our method demonstrates significantly lower standard deviation across 10 runs than both counterparts, proving its consistent robustness across diverse antibody scenarios.Keywords: HIV-1, bNAbs, Statistical distribution sampling (SDS), Sampling generator, Random forest

Introduction

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus type 1 (HIV-1) remains a global health challenge, with millions of people living with the virus around the world [1, 2]. One of the key obstacles in combating HIV-1 is its extraordinary genetic diversity and rapid mutation rate, which enable the virus to evade immune responses and antiretroviral therapies [3]. Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) have emerged as a promising tool in the fight against HIV-1 due to their ability to target conserved regions of the viral envelope and neutralize a wide range of viral strains [4, 5]. These antibodies hold significant potential for both therapeutic applications and vaccine design. However, the effectiveness of bNAbs varies across different HIV-1 strains, necessitating accurate prediction of viral sensitivity to specific bNAbs [6, 7, 9, 10].

Traditional experimental methods for assessing HIV-1 sensitivity to bNAbs are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and often limited in scalability [12–16]. The CATNAP database provides detailed neutralization data for monoclonal antibodies, including IC50 and IC80 [17–19], which quantify the potency of bNAbs against HIV-1 strains [20]. In the version used here, repeated IC50/IC80 measurements for identical sequences from multiple laboratories are consolidated into a single aggregated value via geometric mean, rather than provided as independent replicate measurements. By combining information from the CATNAP database with virus sequence data, machine learning methods employ computational models to predict bNAb sensitivity, providing an effective and convenient approach for this challenge. Many machine learning–based methods have been proposed in recent years [10, 21]. These techniques can identify complex patterns and relationships within large datasets, enabling the prediction of viral susceptibility to bNAbs with high accuracy [22, 23, 25]. These algorithms are primarily aimed at identifying important features and then combining them with traditional machine learning methods for classification. However, because the data in the database originate from multiple laboratories, and the numbers of effective and ineffective samples are highly imbalanced for many antibodies, relying solely on feature-based classification results in unsatisfactory predictive performance for many antibodies.

To address the limitations of the methods described above, we aim to develop robust predictive models that can accelerate the development of bNAb-based interventions.This study explores the Statistical Distribution Sampling (SDS) method from a statistical perspective to predict HIV-1 sensitivity to broadly neutralizing antibodies. Our method is designed to achieve a key methodological breakthrough, enabling robust evaluation by effectively leveraging the full dataset while strictly maintaining complete independence between the test data and all stages of model development, thereby ensuring both scalability and methodological rigor in performance assessment. In addition, by combining the Random Forest method [26], we predict antibody effectiveness. Through this method, we can predict all sequences in the database and obtain reliable prediction results, demonstrating robust performance across various antibodies. The insights gained from this approach have the potential to significantly advance HIV-1 research and contribute to the global effort to combat this persistent pandemic.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The envelope glycoprotein amino acid sequence of each HIV-1 isolate was obtained from the HIV Sequence Database (updated 2024-1-4) in Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) website [20]. The corresponding neutralization activity data (IC50/IC80 titers) were sourced from the sub-database CATNAP (Compile, Analyze and Tally NAb Panels) of HIV Sequence Database. The sequence and neutralization activity data (IC50/IC80 titers) used in our analyses may be publicly accessible via the following link: https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/components/sequence/HIV/neutralization/main. Meanwhile, we use the IC50/IC80 titers as indicators to download sequences, and viruses without sequence data are excluded. To standardize cross-laboratory IC50 measurements in the LANL HIV Sequence Database [20], we implement binary classification following thresholds established in prior HIV-1 neutralization studies [8, 11, 22]. Specifically, IC50g/mL is classified as resistant (label 0), and IC50g/mL as sensitive (label 1), consistent with the threshold used by Hake and Pfeifer (2017) and Rawi et al. (2019). For IC80, we adopt the threshold of g/mL, which aligns with clinical studies defining neutralization sensitivity [8]. Thus, IC80g/mL is classified as resistant (0) and IC80g/mL as sensitive (1). These thresholds ensure biological relevance and comparability with existing literature. In predictive modeling, IC50 serves as the response variable. Antibody efficacy prediction directly correlates with IC50/IC80 thresholds, where lower values indicate susceptibility. The final analysis included 32 antibodies with IC50 data and 28 antibodies with IC80 data, with their respective distributions summarized in the Tables 1 and 2.

The antibodies included in our analysis were selected from the CATNAP database based on data availability and quality considerations. Specifically, we required antibodies to have neutralization titer data (IC50 or IC80) available for a sufficient number of viral strains, with corresponding envelope glycoprotein sequences present in the HIV Sequence Database. Furthermore, to ensure a well-defined classification task, we only retained antibodies that, after applying the standardized IC50 (g/mL) or IC80 (g/mL) thresholds, exhibited a meaningful distribution of samples across both sensitive (label 1) and resistant (label 0) categories. This selection process yielded the final sets of 32 and 28 antibodies for IC50 and IC80 analyses, respectively, which are detailed in Tables 1 and 2 and represent major epitope categories.

The HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein employs multiple evolutionary mechanisms to evade neutralizing antibody binding. The HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein exhibits high glycosylation, genetic variability, and conformational flexibility, which collectively mask neutralization-sensitive epitopes and prevent antibody recognition. Consequently, the envelope glycoprotein sequence analysis provides a rational basis for predicting viral resistance to broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs).

We classify bNAbs into six structural categories based on their critical epitope-binding residues: V1/V2-Glycan, V3-Glycan, CD4-binding loop, Fusion peptide, Subunit interface, and MPER-targeting antibodies. Category-specific distributions are summarized in the Table 1.

K-mer encoding

All datasets underwent preprocessing prior to analysis. Viral sequences are transformed into feature vectors using k-mer encoding. Namely, given a sequence , we implement a sliding window approach (1-step increments) to systematically analyze 2-mer, enumerating their frequency distributions.

For example, for 2-mer analysis, we calculate combinatorial frequencies across 400 possible amino acid pairs 2-mers, transforming each sequence into numeric feature vectors through this combinatorial enumeration. In the calculation process for this article, non-standard residues are filtered during quality control. We implement combinatorial feature engineering, generating 8420-dimensional feature vectors through concatenation of 1-mer (20), 2-mer (400), and 3-mer (8000) frequencies. 3-mer analysis of the 20 canonical amino acids generates sparse, high-dimensional feature matrices containing sufficient discriminative information, precluding the need for higher-order k-mer () calculations.

Accuracy (2) is defined as the ratio of correctly predicted samples to the total number of samples. Specifically, it is calculated by dividing the number of correctly classified samples by the total number of samples in the dataset. The formula for accuracy is:

Accuracy is an intuitive and interpretable metric that directly reflects the overall proportion of correctly predicted samples, which is easily understandable for readers. Although Accuracy is susceptible to class imbalance, in our dataset, the class distributions of some antibodies are approximately balanced ( e.g., VRC26.08, CH01, VRC03). Thus, the ACC results can provide additional practical information about the model’s overall predictive performance.

Since accuracy alone may not fully capture the model’s performance, especially in cases where the dataset is imbalanced, we add the Area Under the Curve (AUC) metric, which measures the model’s ability to distinguish between different classes. AUC is derived from the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and provides a summary of the model’s classification performance across different thresholds.

To assess the robustness and stability of our predictive models against variations in data partitioning, we employed a 10-iteration Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) strategy for model evaluation. Specifically, for each antibody, its dataset was randomly partitioned into training (75%) and test (25%) subsets, and this random splitting and subsequent model training and testing process was repeated 10 times independently. The final performance metrics were derived by averaging the results across all 10 runs.

To ensure fair comparison among different prediction methods (SDS, LBUM, and SLAPNAP), identical random train-test splits (75%/25%) were shared across all methods in each iteration. This eliminates selection biases from data partitioning, ensuring performance differences reflect algorithmic variations rather than sampling artifacts.

Classifier selection

Six antibodies (10-1074, 35022, 4E10, b12, PGT145, PGT151) are evaluated against six antigenic regions (V1/V2-Glycan, V3-Glycan, CD4-binding loop, Fusion peptide, Subunit interface, MPER) using IC50 values. Given the limited sample size, four interpretable machine learning algorithms: Random Forest [26], Decision Tree [27], XGBoost [28], and LightGBM [29] are prioritized over neural networks.

Performance is measured through the average of ten runs. RF demonstrated superior predictive capability (Fig. 7), establishing it as the optimal classifier. For consistency and to avoid hyperparameter-tuning bias, we used fixed default hyperparameters for all candidate classifiers throughout this selection process. Prediction performance on IC50 dataset with 75% data as Sampling generator

The IC50 dataset comprises 32 distinct antibodies. For each antibody-response pair, 75% of observations are allocated to Sampling generator, with the remaining 25% strictly isolated as an independent test set. Bootstrapped samples are generated. The sets and are combined to form the training set . A fixed 25% of the data is rigorously excluded from all training phases, maintaining complete independence between the test set and training materials to prevent information leakage, thereby enabling robust evaluation while strictly preserving complete independence between the test data and all stages of model development.

As shown in Fig. 8, three antibodies (DH270.1, VRC07, VRC26.08) exhibited marginally reduced neutralization efficacy compared to LBUM. However, LBUM displays substantial measurement variability (>10% SD across all antibodies), whereas our method achieved superior stability with <5% SD in most cases. This measurement variability (10% vs <5%) quantitatively validates our method’s enhanced robustness.

As shown in Fig. 9, the forest plot compares the predictive performance of the SDS and LBUM methods across all tested antibodies, with the area under the curve (AUC) as the primary metric. Overall, the SDS method demonstrated consistently superior performance, achieving an overall mean AUC of 0.901, which was significantly higher than the LBUM method’s overall mean AUC of 0.836. For every antibody tested, the mean AUC value of the SDS method (red squares) was higher than that of LBUM (blue circles), with the majority of SDS AUC values exceeding 0.85. Furthermore, the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for SDS (red horizontal lines) were generally narrower than those for LBUM (blue horizontal lines), indicating greater stability and reliability in SDS predictions. For example, for antibody 10-1074, the SDS method yielded an AUC of 0.895 (95% CI: 0.884–0.910), whereas LBUM showed an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI: 0.644–1.000), highlighting both the higher accuracy and robustness of SDS. These results collectively confirm that the SDS method outperforms LBUM in terms of both predictive accuracy and result stability across the entire panel of antibodies.

Discussion and conclusions

In this paper, a method for predicting HIV-1 sensitivity to broadly neutralizing antibodies (SDS) is developed, which mainly addresses the imbalance of effective and ineffective data for IC50 and IC80 of HIV bNAbs in practical use, which can otherwise result in poor predictive performance. By sampling the Sampling generator, we obtained balanced samples for various types, and then combined them with machine learning methods for classification. Our method demonstrated superior IC50/IC80 prediction performance compared to the LBUM and SLAPNAP methods.

For the sake of fairness, we first randomly selected 75% of the data as the Sampling generator. We sampled 500 data points from the IC50/IC80 = 1 and IC50/IC80 = 0 classes, and added 75% of the randomly selected data to form a training set. For the LBUM and SLAPNAP methods, 75% of the data was directly used as the training set. Ultimately, all three methods tested the remaining 25% of the data independently. The results from the independent test set show that our method outperforms the other two methods in most cases under both IC50 and IC80 indicators, and its standard deviation over ten runs is significantly lower than that of the other methods, reflecting the robustness of our approach.

Moreover, our method allows the formation of a training set by sampling, and multiple experiments confirmed that there is no overlap between the sampled data and the original data. As a result, our method can also test all of the original data without any intersection between the test set and the training set. For the sake of fairness, both the LBUM and SLAPNAP methods are tested using all data, which creates overlap between their training and test sets. Therefore, their reported results would likely be lower if complete separation were maintained. Nevertheless, in this scenario, our method still demonstrates clear advantages for most antibodies. Additionally, the standard deviation of our method across ten runs is substantially lower than that of the other methods.

The comparative analysis of feature encoding strategies and sequence design space (SDS) utilization yields critical insights for antibody activity prediction. Taking antibody 10-996 as a representative example, we summarize the key findings in Table 7. Specifically, the binary encoding strategy combined with a decision stump classifier (Binary encoding + no SDS + Decision Stump) achieved the lowest AUC value of 0.6667. By contrast, replacing the decision stump with a random forest (RF) classifier under the same binary encoding framework (Binary encoding + no SDS + RF) resulted in a substantially higher AUC of 0.9408, which significantly outperformed the decision stump-based counterpart. This observation indicates that the random forest algorithm contributes predominantly to the performance improvement. When binary encoding was replaced with k-mer encoding (k-mer + no SDS + RF), the AUC value only increased marginally to 0.9474 (a relative improvement of 0.7%), demonstrating that k-mer encoding contributes minimally to performance gains under the non-SDS setting. Notably, the integration of k-mer encoding, SDS sampling, and random forest classification (k-mer + SDS + RF) yielded the highest AUC of 0.9737, representing a 2.6% improvement over the k-mer + no SDS + RF model. These results confirm that our proposed SDS sampling strategy exerts a distinct and effective role in enhancing predictive performance.

To quantify the contribution of SDS-sampled data to predictive performance, we evaluated model performance across three training data configurations, as shown in Table 8. The model trained on raw training data only achieved an AUC of 0.9474, establishing a baseline; the model trained on SDS-sampled data alone reached an AUC of 0.9605, outperforming the raw-only baseline and demonstrating that SDS-sampled data itself carries strong predictive signal; and the model trained on the combined raw and SDS-sampled data yielded an AUC of 0.9737, which represents the highest performance among the three configurations. These results clearly illustrate that both data sources contribute positively to predictive performance, with SDS-sampled data not only providing independent predictive value but also synergizing with raw data to further enhance model accuracy, thus validating the effectiveness of our SDS sampling strategy.

A noteworthy finding from our comparative analyses is that our method, which focuses on 3-mer amino acid frequency features and disregards inter-feature correlations beyond this window, outperforms alternative methods retaining long-range intercorrelations. When k=4 for k-mer encoding, as shown in Fig. 4, the resulting model showed only a slight decrease in predictive performance. This result reinforces that short-range local sequence patterns (within 3-mer) capture the key information for antibody sensitivity prediction, while longer sequence fragments ( 4-mer) contribute little additional value and may even introduce marginal redundancy. Biologically, this observation implies that proximal amino acid mutations play a more critical role in modulating antibody sensitivity than distant correlated mutations, consistent with the view that local sequence conformations are key determinants of antigen-antibody binding interactions.

Future work should investigate the impact of mutations at specific loci on antibody sensitivity by incorporating positional and structural information into the k-mer encoding framework. This could be implemented through three complementary methods: first, integrating position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs) to weight k-mers according to their evolutionary conservation at distinct sequence positions; second, appending the average positions of each type of k-mers appearing on a protein sequence as an additional dimension to the k-mer vector; third, incorporating known structural data of epitope regions to assign higher weights to k-mers that map to spatial domains proximal to critical antibody binding sites. This multi-dimensional encoding strategy would enable the model to capture both the sequence composition and the positional/structural context of k-mers, potentially improving the predictive power for antibody sensitivity.

Furthermore, the current SDS framework is designed for predicting sensitivity to single monoclonal antibodies and does not predict the efficacy of antibody combinations, which are increasingly important in therapeutic and preventive strategies against HIV-1. Extending the method to model the synergistic effects of multiple bNAbs is a valuable direction for future research.

Finally, it is important to note a limitation inherent in our feature representation: the k-mer encoding (with k up to 3) focuses on local sequence patterns and, combined with the independent sampling in SDS, does not explicitly capture longer-range correlations or potential compensatory mutations between residues that are distant in the sequence (beyond the 3-amino-acid window). While this design choice contributed to the method’s stability and performance in our study, it means that such long-range interactions are not modeled.
