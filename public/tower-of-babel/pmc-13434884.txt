Development and validation of a MedDRA-based signal detection construct for drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment
Authors: Christopher D. Breder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The FDA’s Guidance for an industry evaluating drug effects on the ability to operate a motor vehicle describes the need to evaluate drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment. However, the guidance does not provide instruction or the means to implement the analysis.Objective:The proposed Construct provides a means to operationalize the evaluation of adverse event datasets for this disorder.Design:A noninterventional pharmacoepidemiologic methods development study using routinely collected health data.Methods:Preferred terms for the Construct were derived by mapping the domains of concern cited by the Guidance to the corresponding MedDRA terms. Additional terms were derived from the MedDRA preferred terms cited in the warning section of labels from drugs with a warning about driving or operating machinery. The performance of the Construct was evaluated by comparing adverse events for the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database for drugs considered to be of high risk versus low risk for this condition using descriptive statistics and a nonparametric test.Results:A Construct of PTs (neurobehavioral impairment term list; NITL) was obtained after identification of 55 drugs with the Warning in their FDA-approved label for driving or operating machinery based on drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment. Additional terms not covered in the labels were derived from the five domains of interest cited in the Guidance for a total of 52 PTs. Comparison of the difference in the proportion of FAERS reports with the NITL terms in drugs with and without the relevant labeling was nominally significant (p < 0.0001; Kruskal–Wallis ChiSquare approximation).Conclusion:A MedDRA-based Construct was generated for the selection of adverse events related to drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment. The Construct discriminated high- from low-risk drugs based on publicly available adverse event profiles. Further evaluation with clinical trial data will be needed to facilitate its use for signal detection in safety analysis.

Introduction

Drug development focused on the central nervous system space has been increasingly active; particularly since 2005 where one notes an acceleration of the number of phase II and III trials starts (Figure 1). 1

Central nervous system disorder study starts per year as derived from ClinTrials.Gov.

In response to this increase in investigational new drug and new drug applications, the Food and Drug Administration (“The Agency”; FDA) produced a draft guidance on Evaluating Drug Effects on the Ability to Operate a Motor Vehicle. 2 Their rationale was that driving reflected a complex behavior requiring attention and executive function and that monitoring for potential drug-induced effects on this activity would be a final stage in a tiered strategy of safety monitoring, for example, receptor and nonclinical observations in the pre-IND phase, through clinical observations in large phase II and III patient trials. Ultimately, this surrogate testing could result in a formal Driving Test if the data suggested that a greater degree of characterization was needed (c.f., Ref. 3). Within the Guidance, the agency suggested five broad functional domains. . .important for driving ability [that] should be assessed with increasingly focused studies if accumulating data suggest[ed] a risk of clinically meaningful driving impairment.

However, no instructions were provided as to how this assessment was to be done; in fact, the text states, . . .This guidance does not address the specific methods or instruments used to collect data. From an industry Medical Monitor’s or Agency Safety Reviewer’s perspective, several pathways are possible for such analysis. To begin, one could use the MedDRA preferred term (PT) hierarchy to produce a Construct of terms directly corresponding to these domains. 4 While seemingly valid because of the 1:1 relationship between the deficits and PTs, this would produce a limited list of about 8–10 PTs,1,2 with limited sensitivity. This Construct could be supplemented by extracting the PTs from labels of drugs with the Warnings that are based on the Guidance. Such a Construct would have face validity from its sources being the Guidance and corresponding FDA-labeled adverse events. We used this methodology to generate a composite, curated set of PTs, the neurobehavioral impairment term list (NITL).

Methods

This is a noninterventional pharmacoepidemiologic methods development study using routinely collected health data extracted in part on searches from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS; Search Period 1969—10/1/2025; Extracted—10/1/2025). Accordingly, the report was prepared with the conventions in the RECORD-PE checklist. 5

Construct performance

The Construct performance was qualitatively evaluated tested by using PTs in Table 2 to extract “Adverse Events of Interest” from 12 “High-Risk” drugs (containing the Driving Warning in their Warning Section of Labeling) and 12 “Low-Risk” drugs (those having no or minimal nervous system or psychiatry-related adverse events in conclusion of their labels).

While generic names are used in this manuscript, FAERS data were obtained from the branded product to ensure that only the single-entity drug data was considered, in the event of errant submission of the original data, and to mitigate the possibility that the generic form may have a different profile through its composition or timeframe of use (Data range from 1969 to 2025; Extracted 01 October 2025). 10 PTs in the FAERS datasets matching the Construct terms were extracted with the NITL Construct according to the procedure of Breder et al.. 11 For each PT in the Construct, for each drug, in order to normalize the report count, the following was computed used the JMP® software platform (SAS, Cary, NC) 12 :

For a descriptive comparison of the two drug sets, the median pi for high-risk versus low-risk sets, graphical demonstration used the JMP® Graph Builder function. A statistical comparison of the high- and low-risk groups pi was performed with a Kruskal–Wallis test, ChiSquare approximation to generate a Cohen’s d, nominal p value, and a description of the groups’ variance using JMP®.

Results

Assembly of the construct

The original domains from the Guidance were mapped to 11 PTs, 9 of which were unique. These 9 mapped to 7 unique HLTs (Table 1). Querying the FDALabel app for human prescription drug NDAs with a warning related to driving or operating machinery yielded 96 unique generic drug names. Labels were abstracted for PTs in the Warning section yielding 47 unique PTs. Of the 9 PTs derived from the Guidance domains, 4 were already included in this group from the Label Warnings, so the remaining 5 were added to the total Construct, yielding a list of 52 PTs (Tables 3 and 4).

Evaluation of the neurobehavioral impairment construct

The Construct was used to extract the matching terms from FAERS data of 24 approved drugs. 9 Drugs in the low-risk group had a median number (IQR) of 18.5 (8.25) PTs detected with the Construct, while the high-risk group had 23.5 (5). The proportion of terms related to neurobehavioral impairment from the total number of FAERS reports per drug was reported as the pi. The median (IQR) proportion of the high-risk group was 0.1088 (0.0893) and the low 0.0185 (0.0166; Figure 2). The difference between the proportions of the risk groups was nominally significantly different (p < 0.0001; Kruskal–Wallis ChiSquare approximation; Cohen’s d = 0.92). While for most terms, the median proportion of events for each PT is qualitatively greater for the high-risk group, certain terms, including Somnolence PT, Seizure PT, Amnesia PT, Sedation PT, and Tremor PT appear most differentiated (Figure 3).

Discussion

Numerous drug development safety packages present a concern regarding a signal for neurobehavioral impairment that may require an assessment such as the driving test.13,14 While there is an expectation that the safety will be fully characterized, the Guidance is not explicit in how clinical trial data should be analyzed in this regard. Often, there is a relevant SMQ that may be fit for purpose, such as that for Drug abuse, dependence, and withdrawal (SMQ) as a means to characterize abuse liability. While there are about seventeen SMQs related to central and peripheral nervous system, our analysis suggests, none seemed appropriate for this cause. None of the SMQs one would expect to have the terms related to this condition had overlapping PTs with our Construct. This was a major motivating factor in addressing this gap in the safety armamentarium.

A principal consideration in this exercise was to determine what the Guidance actually represented. For example, is it specifically concerned with the person’s capacity to drive or was there some other, more encompassing intent, such as a concern for drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment, for which driving was only a surrogate. The latter interpretation was chosen based on the text of the Guidance and in the public discourse. 14

Interestingly a similar effort has been applied in the nonclinical arena. In an attempt to develop a standardized method to compare drugs’ adverse effects on the nervous system, Uthayathas et al. 15 developed a scale, “Drug Effects on the Nervous System” (DENS). The DENS scale detected dose-dependent side effects of risperidone on cognitive, sensorimotor, and autonomic functions in nonhuman primates, demonstrating the utility of such standardized tools.

Summary of methodology

In this endeavor, a 52 PT-Construct was generated based on the domains identified in the FDA’s Guidance for Industry Evaluating Drug Effects on the Ability to Operate a Motor Vehicle and the drug Labels that carry a Warning regarding driving. The Construct provides a means to operationalize the evaluation of adverse event databases for signals based on the domains identified in the guidance. While the preferred terms directly mapping to the Domains provide a very specific probe, they would be wholly insensitive to the task for which they would be used. The PTs from the Warnings provide a source of terms with face validity for the task.

There is little published methodology for validation of a Construct or SMQ,4,16 with the possible exception of applying some expert opinions.17,18 The graphic comparison between AE data from drugs containing the Warning and those from drugs with few CNS-related AEs in their Label provides an initial indication of the performance of the Construct. Typically, when using FAERS data, analyses such as the probability reporting ratio or certain Bayesian metric are the most rudimentary way of presenting the report data. 19 In this study, there was not an intent to evaluate the individual Drug-AE report numbers in terms of disproportionality. Conversely, simply using the report numbers would not be appropriate since it is not normalized. Proportional distribution of adverse event counts across SMQ terms is used here solely as a descriptive normalization to compare patterns of SMQ term composition between drug groups. It is not intended as a disproportionality statistic nor as a validated signal detection metric. When the goal is to evaluate distributional differences between Constructs, proportional normalization is a standard descriptive approach and prevents volume differences from dominating results.20,21 Further research may evaluate what threshold of reporting (i.e., what pi) is considered a safety signal or what is needed appropriate for such a regulatory outcome as a Warning in the drug label.

A consideration for this type of methodology is the variance between versions of MedDRA. Construction of a term list with a different version could potentially result in different results. Standard procedure for this type of analysis would be to use a term list based on the same version as the source material. 4 Therefore, it the methodology used in generation the NITL is critical in the interpretation of the results.

Utility of the construct

The drug-induced neurobehavioral Construct, like any other set of terms, including the SMQs, is not meant to serve as a diagnostic tool. The introductory materials for SMQs [11] from the MSSO states, Standardised MedDRA Queries (SMQs) are tools developed to facilitate retrieval of MedDRA-coded data [emphasis] as a first step in investigating drug safety issues in pharmacovigilance and clinical development. Like the fishing net, to which they are metaphorically compared, the catch from using these tools must be examined and sorted. Chang et al. 4 reported that as many as 30% of SMQ searches in regulatory submissions do not report verifying their findings through means such as corroborating labs, vital signs, or narratives. The utility of the list is as valuable as the effort to sort through the cases and corroborate those deemed clinically relevant. In this spirit, a list with more terms than simply those to which the Guidance domains map, ensuring maximal sensitivity, seems appropriate.

Limitations

The NITL is limited in capturing the adverse event PTs suggested in the Guidance and those in the Warnings section of the appropriate labels. It is anticipated that even with using these criteria, the list of PTs will increase in time with the approval of more psychotropic drugs. Investigators may have their own preferences and modify the list, much as what is described with the “modified SMQs” from the MedDRA system. 4

Conclusion

A term list was generated to capture adverse events of drug-induced neurobehavioral impairment from datasets, such as those generated in clinical trials and public reporting systems. The list contains PTs generated from the domains discussed in the FDA’s “Driving Guidance” and from the labels of drugs containing Warnings, regarding Driving and Operating Machinery, which represents one of the more consequential situations where neural impairment is of concern. The list is able to discriminate between high- and low-risk drugs based on the proportion (pi) of their FAERS Reaction Terms that are included in the list. This Construct may be useful in the analysis of clinical trial and public AE reporting datasets.
