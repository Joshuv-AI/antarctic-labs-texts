In Regard to Hsu et al
Authors: Ilker Sengul, Demet Sengul
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the article by Hsu et al,1 which presents valuable insights into the potential utility of a multimedia educational tool accessed via QR code for breast cancer patients undergoing radiation therapy (RT), particularly for management of radiation dermatitis. This study presented a 4-minute video explaining RT mechanisms, self-care, precautions, and toxicity grading to patients. It was well received, with a high percentage of participants reporting satisfaction, improved understanding, emotional support, and reduced anxiety. However, the limitations of the study’s design—a single-arm pilot study conducted at a single center—should be considered when interpreting the findings. The modest sample size and the nonrandomized nature mean that although the findings are promising, they should be interpreted with caution. Without a control group receiving standard education or no video, it is not possible to definitively conclude that the observed benefits are solely attributable to the video intervention itself. Other factors, such as the attention received by participating in a study or standard care practices, could have contributed to the reported positive outcomes. In addition, the study used a satisfaction survey administered at the first clinic follow-up within 4 weeks after completing RT. Recall bias might affect the accuracy of self-reported data, as well as factors such as how frequently patients watched the video or when they watched it most often. This is particularly relevant for the authors’ conclusion regarding the “crucial” timing of watching the video. Although the study suggests that the video’s perceived efficacy was unaffected by patient characteristics such as age, education, occupation, or social support, the limited sample size and single-arm design might limit the power to detect such associations. Similarly, the finding of no significant difference in Skindex-16 scores between conventional and hypofractionated RT groups, although interesting, also stems from a subgroup analysis within this modest sample. Rigorous, larger-scale, randomized controlled trials are necessary to confirm these preliminary findings, establish the true efficacy of such video interventions compared to standard care, and overcome the limitations inherent in this pilot study’s design. Thus, we commend Hsu et al for shedding light on this uncommon yet significant matter for breast cancer physicians1, 2, 3, 4 and health care providers.
