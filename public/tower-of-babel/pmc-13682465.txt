Endothelial indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 drives high-tryptophan diet–mediated thrombosis in cancer-bearing mice∗
Authors: Asha Jose, Saran Lotfollahzadeh, Anna Natrakul, Arjun Patel, Kashvi Sethuraman, Kylla Przekop, Maria Del Carmen Piqueras, Arathi Ranga, Howard J Cabral, Nathanael Fillmore, Katya Ravid, Vipul C Chitalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Endothelial IDO1 drives venous thromboembolism via the Trp-Kyn axis in cancer-bearing mice.

Introduction

Cancer-associated thrombosis remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and noncancer-related mortality in patients with solid tumors.1,2 Epidemiologic studies demonstrate that patients with cancer have up to a ninefold increased risk of venous thromboembolism, particularly in metastatic disease and malignancies such as pancreatic, gastrointestinal, lung, and colorectal cancers.3, 4, 5 Despite advances in anticoagulation strategies, risk stratification remains imprecise, and current predictive models rely largely on clinical variables rather than mechanistically defined biomarkers. Cancer-related inflammation, endothelial injury, treatment toxicity, and metabolic dysregulation collectively contribute to this heightened thrombotic risk.6 Therefore, a clearer understanding of tumor-driven vascular pathways is essential to improve both prediction and prevention of thrombotic events.

Emerging evidence indicates that tumor-associated metabolic reprogramming contributes directly to vascular dysfunction. Beyond classical mediators such as tissue factor (TF)–bearing microvesicles, platelets, and inflammatory cytokines, cancer-derived metabolites have been shown to actively modulate endothelial phenotype.5,7 Our previous study demonstrated that tryptophan (Trp) metabolism through the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1)–kynurenine (Kyn) axis enhances venous thrombogenicity in tumor-bearing mice.8, 9, 10 Pharmacologic inhibition of IDO1 reduced circulating Kyn levels, restored endothelial anticoagulant balance, and attenuated thrombus formation, implicating dysregulated Trp metabolism as a driver of cancer-associated thrombosis.9

IDO1 is the rate-limiting enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of Trp to Kyn and is frequently upregulated in tumors as part of an immune-evasive program.11,12 Although immunologic consequences of IDO1 activation have been extensively studied,13,14 its endothelial-specific role in venous thrombogenesis has remained undefined. Kyn exerts pleiotropic vascular effects, including modulation of TF, plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1), oxidative stress pathways, and aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling, positioning the Trp-IDO1-Kyn axis as a central metabolic regulator of endothelial homeostasis.9

In the present study, we provide direct genetic evidence defining the vascular role of IDO1 in cancer-associated thrombosis. Using complementary IDO1 global knockout (gKO) and inducible IDO1 endothelial-specific deletion models in a syngeneic MC38 tumor system, combined with dietary modulation of Trp, we dissect the endothelial-intrinsic contribution of IDO1 to systemic Kyn production and venous thrombogenesis. These studies establish endothelial IDO1 as a mechanistic link between tumor-driven metabolic reprogramming and prothrombotic endothelial signaling.

Methods

Mice

IDO1 gKO mice (B6.129-Ido1tm1Alm/J; strain 005867) and wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J (strain 000664) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). IDO1f/+ (heterozygous) mice were acquired from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Sugar Land, TX) (C57BL/6JSmoc-Ido1em1 (flox)Smoc; strain NM-CKO-2106753). Cdh5-CreERT2 mice (C57BL/6-Tg [Cdh5-cre/ERT2]1Rha; strain 13073-MPF) were purchased from Taconic Biosciences Inc (NY). As shown in supplemental Figure 1, IDO1f/f (homozygous) mice were crossbred with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice to generate IDO1 endothelial-specific knockout (ECKO) mice on a C57BL/6 background. IDO1 ECKO mice were injected intraperitoneally with tamoxifen (TAM; 75 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days to induce endothelial IDO1 deletion.15 IDO1f/f mice received the same tamoxifen regimen and served as controls. Genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction using the following primers: IDO1f/f forward: TCCAGTGTCCTTAGCAAAGCC, IDO1f/f reverse: TGGAGCATAGACAGCACTTGG, Cdh5-CreERT2 forward: GCCTGCATTACCGGTCGATGCAACGA, and Cdh5-CreERT2 reverse: GTGGCAGATGGCGCGGCAACACCATT. All mice were maintained under standard conditions (25°C, ∼50% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Boston University (protocol number: 430092001).

MC38 cell syngeneic tumor model

Mouse MC38 colon carcinoma cells (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; catalog no. SCC172) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. Cells were trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free media at 1 × 104 cells per μL, and injected subcutaneously into the flanks of mice at 1 × 106 cells in 200 μL of Matrigel (growth factor–poor Matrigel, VitroGel Matrix, TheWell Bioscience, Washington, DC; VHM01) using a 23-gauge needle. Tumor growth was monitored weekly for 4 weeks using calipers (VWR International, Radnor, PA; 3417), and tumor volume was calculated as volume = length × width2.2

IVC stenosis model

Mice were maintained on purified diets containing either normal (0.2%) or high (1.2%) Trp for 3 days once the tumor reached a threshold volume of 300 mm3. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and partial blood flow stasis was achieved by ligating the inferior vena cava (IVC) distal to the left renal vein using a 6-0 polypropylene suture. Ligation was performed over a 30-gauge needle as a spacer. Detailed procedures are described in our previous study.16 After 48 hours, mice were euthanized, and thrombi formed distal to the ligation site were harvested for analysis. During the experiments, a total of 6 mice died after IVC ligation. Experiments were continued with all other surviving mice to monitor thrombosis (Figures 1F and 5E).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or standard error of the mean, as appropriate. Normality was assessed graphically and using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-factor analysis of variance was used to examine interactions between diet and tumor status. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Multiple comparisons were adjusted where necessary. Based on a previous study,9 6 to 10 mice per group provided adequate power to test the hypothesis. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Other methods, including tissue analyses, are presented in the supplemental Material.

Results

Generation and validation of IDO1 gKO

To investigate the role of IDO1 in cancer-associated thrombosis, we used IDO1 gKO mice. The IDO1 gene was disrupted by replacing exons 3 to 5, which encode critical catalytic regions, with a β-galactosidase-neomycin resistance cassette and introducing a translational stop codon in exon 2 (Figure 1A). Western blot analysis (supplemental Method) confirmed the absence of IDO1 protein in IDO gKO mice (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 2).

IDO1 gKO and age- and sex-matched WT mice, both with MC38 tumors, were assigned to 2 groups based on diet (normal or high Trp) (Figure 1C). A syngeneic tumor model was established using MC38 cells in 8- to 12-week-old WT/IDO gKO female mice. Once tumors reached ∼300 mm3 (supplemental Table 1), mice underwent partial ligation of the infrarenal IVC, an established model of acute deep vein thrombosis that reduces venous blood flow. Notably, 48 hours after ligation, thrombi were harvested, and clot weights normalized to body weight were used as a quantitative measure of thrombogenicity. As shown in Figure 1D, there were no significant differences between control and IDO1 gKO in tumor volume in both male and female mice. Histological analysis of tumors derived from IDO1 gKO mice demonstrated representative features of compact colon cancer cells without significant necrosis or hemorrhage (Figure 1E). Thrombus in the IVC was assessed by measuring clot weight normalized to body weight. Both in male and female mice, a high-Trp diet significantly increased thrombus formation in tumor-bearing mice compared with a normal-Trp diet (Figure 1F; ##P = .0015; ##P = .001 in female and male control mice, respectively). IDO1 gKO male mice exhibited a greater reduction in thrombus weight on a high-Trp diet than females. In this case, statistical comparison confirmed significant differences in thrombus development between control and knockout mice on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (Figure 1F; ∗∗P = .0012; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001) in female and male mice, respectively, indicating that IDO1 is required for Trp-mediated thrombogenicity.

Endothelial prothrombotic markers

Immunofluorescence analysis (supplemental Method) of IVC sections revealed that high-Trp diet–fed tumor-bearing IDO1 gKO mice displayed a significant reduction in endothelial expression of TF compared with controls (Figure 2A-B; ###P = .0029). Comparison of control mice on normal vs high-Trp diets showed a significant increase in TF expression (Figure 2A-B; ∗∗∗P = .0013). PAI-1 also showed a significant reduction compared with control and IDO1 gKO mice fed a high-Trp diet (Figure 2C-D; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001) and was significantly elevated in control mice on the 2 different diets (Figure 2C-D; ####P < .0001). Similarly, von Willebrand factor (VWF), a marker of endothelial activation, was also significantly reduced in mice fed a high-Trp diet, both in control and IDO1 gKO mice (Figure 3A-B; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001), and was significantly elevated in control mice on different diets (Figure 3A-B; ####P < .0001). There was no significant difference in the expression of thrombomodulin (TM) when fed a high-Trp diet in control and IDO1 gKO mice. In comparison with mice on a normal diet, those given a high-Trp diet exhibited lower levels of TM expression in the control group (Figure 3C-D; ∗∗P < .0274).

Trp-Kyn metabolism in IDO1-deficient mice

Targeted metabolomics (supplemental Method) of mouse serum demonstrated that, on a normal-Trp diet, tumor-bearing control or IDO1 gKO mice showed no significant differences in circulating Trp levels (Figure 3E). Although the high-Trp diet increased substrate availability, circulating Trp levels did not increase significantly, potentially owing to enhanced metabolic utilization within the tumor microenvironment and peripheral tissues, as also observed in our earlier study.9 Interestingly, a significant reduction in circulating Kyn levels was observed in IDO1 gKO mice on a high-Trp diet compared with control mice on the same diet (Figure 3E; ∗∗∗P = .0038). Kyn level was significantly elevated in control mice on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (Figure 3F; ∗∗∗P = .0059).

Generation and validation of IDO1 ECKO mice

To examine the endothelial-intrinsic contribution of IDO1 to tumor-associated thrombosis, we generated IDO1 ECKO mice by crossing IDO1f/f mice with Cdh5-CreERT2 transgenic mice. Successful genotyping distinguished floxed heterozygous, homozygous, and Cre+ lines (Figure 4A). To functionally validate the endothelial deletion, we used a tamoxifen-induced recombination strategy (Figure 4B). Western blot analysis of isolated vascular cell lysates confirmed a marked reduction of IDO1 protein in IDO1 ECKO mice compared with Cre− and tamoxifen-treated controls (Figure 4C; supplemental Figure 3), demonstrating efficient and specific recombination. Immunofluorescence staining of the IVC endothelium further corroborated the endothelial loss of IDO1 in Cre+ mice, with robust IDO1 signal observed in controls (∗∗∗∗P < .0001), but nearly absent in IDO1 ECKO vessels (Figure 4D-E, ∗∗∗∗ P < .0001).

Loss of endothelial IDO1 protects against tumor-induced thrombosis in mice fed a high-Trp diet

To determine whether endothelial IDO1 influences venous thrombogenicity, we designed a dietary and tumor challenge experiment and implemented a tamoxifen-based induction workflow (Figure 5A). A syngeneic tumor model was established using MC38 cells in 8- to 12-week-old Cre+ and Cre− IDO1f/f mice. Once tumors reached a threshold size (supplemental Table 2), mice were fed either normal-Trp or high-Trp diets and underwent the same procedure as IDO gKO mice (Figure 5B). Tumor growth kinetics were comparable across dietary groups and genotypes, with no significant difference in tumor volume (Figure 5C). Hematoxylin and eosin staining suggested similar tumor architecture among all groups (Figure 5D). Strikingly, endothelial IDO1 deletion markedly reduced clot formation on a high-Trp diet in male mice (Figure 5E; ∗P = .0175), demonstrating that endothelial IDO1 is required for diet-driven enhancement of tumor-associated thrombosis. A significant increase in clot weight was observed in the control group on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (Figure 5E; ∗∗∗P = .0086).

Endothelial IDO1 drives TF, PAI-1, and VWF upregulation in the endothelium

To examine the level of molecular mediators associated with thrombosis, we performed immunofluorescence staining of IVC sections (Figure 6). High-Trp diet–fed tumor-bearing IDO1 ECKO mice displayed a significant reduction in endothelial expression of TF compared with control mice on the same diet (Figure 6A-B; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001), indicating that endothelial IDO1 is essential for TF upregulation. Comparison of different diet groups in control mice showed a marked increase in TF expression on a high-Trp diet (Figure 6A-B; ####P < .0001). PAI-1 also showed a significant reduction compared with control and IDO1 ECKO mice on a high-Trp diet (Figure 6C-D; ###P = .0003). PAI-1 was also significantly elevated in control mice on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (Figure 6C-D; ∗∗∗P = .0014). Together, these results demonstrate that endothelial IDO1 is a key amplifier of prothrombotic signaling in tumor-bearing hosts. In a similar way, VWF was significantly reduced in high-Trp diet–fed IDO1 ECKO mice compared with control mice (Figure 7A-B; ###P < .0001). VWF was significantly elevated in control groups on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (∗∗∗P = .0018). Together, these results suggest that endothelial IDO1 promotes a proadhesive, activated endothelial phenotype in tumor-associated thrombosis. There was no significant difference in the expression of TM in the control and IDO1 ECKO mice when fed a high-Trp diet. High-Trp diet–fed control mice showed reduced TM expression compared with a normal diet in control mice (Figure 7C-D; ∗∗P < .0033).

Endothelial IDO1 modulates circulating Trp metabolism and Kyn levels

Serum metabolomics demonstrated that dietary Trp loading increased circulating Trp levels in the control group on a high-Trp diet (Figure 7E; ∗P = .0102). There was no significant difference in Trp levels between control and IDO1 ECKO mice fed a high-Trp diet. Notably, IDO1 ECKO mice exhibited reduced Kyn accumulation on a high-Trp diet (Figure 7F, ∗∗∗∗P < .0001), and elevated Kyn levels were observed in the control groups on a high-Trp diet compared with a normal diet (Figure 7F, ∗∗∗P = .0083). These results indicate that endothelial IDO1 represents a mediator of high-Trp diet–associated thromboinflammatory responses in cancer-bearing mice.

Discussion

This study provides genetic evidence that endothelial IDO1 is a key contributor to cancer-associated venous thrombosis. Using both IDO1 global and endothelial-specific deletion models, we demonstrate that loss of IDO1 reduces systemic Kyn accumulation, attenuates endothelial prothrombotic signaling, and contributes to reduced IVC thrombus formation in tumor-bearing mice. These findings extend previous pharmacologic observations and establish endothelial IDO1 as a mechanistic mediator, rather than a mere correlate of tumor-associated thrombogenesis.

Although our earlier report of pharmacologic inhibition of IDO1 pointed to its role in cancer-associated thrombosis in mice,9 a concern has remained about possible drug off-targets. Our current study involving a total body knockout of IDO1 in mice alleviates this concern. Importantly, our data also position endothelial IDO1 as a metabolic amplifier within the vascular compartment. Elevated Trp availability in the context of malignancy enhances Kyn production, which in turn promotes endothelial activation characterized by increased TF, PAI-1, and VWF expression. We recognize that inhibition of clot formation was greater in global KO mice than IDO1 ECKO (comparing Figures 1F with 5E), possibly because of its partial inducible deletion in these cells (Figure 4C) and/or because other tissue sources of IDO1 contribute to the thrombotic phenotype, yet endothelial-specific deletion was sufficient to blunt systemic Kyn levels under high-Trp conditions (Figure 7D), suggesting that, in addition to Kyn, other factors might contribute to endothelial IDO1-mediated thrombosis. Overall, our studies point to the vascular endothelium not only as a downstream target but also as a contributor to circulating metabolite pools. These findings support a feed-forward metabolic-vascular loop in which tumor-driven IDO1 activity reinforces endothelial dysfunction and thrombosis.

Mechanistically, Kyn integrates inflammatory, oxidative, and transcriptional programs within the endothelium, including activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor-dependent pathways and NF-κB signaling.17, 18, 19, 20 Through these mechanisms, IDO1 activity shifts the endothelial phenotype toward a procoagulant and proadhesive state by promoting TF expression at both transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels. Importantly, TM expression was not significantly altered, suggesting that IDO1-driven thrombosis may occur primarily by augmenting procoagulant signaling rather than by broadly suppressing all anticoagulant pathways. Kyn induces endothelial activation through oxidative stress, generating reactive oxygen species that reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, increase vascular permeability, and enhance platelet adhesion.18,21 Kyn also amplifies inflammatory signaling by stimulating the production of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and interleukin-1β, creating a feed-forward loop that exacerbates endothelial injury and supports thrombus formation.22,23 Inflammatory cytokines and redox imbalance may also stimulate Weibel-Palade body release, resulting in increased VWF secretion and procoagulant surface activity.9,24 In contrast, the mechanisms by which endothelial IDO1 regulates PAI-1 expression remain unclear. Collectively, these mechanistic insights establish IDO1-mediated Kyn production as a metabolic regulator that integrates tumor-derived signals with vascular dysfunction, inflammation, and thrombosis.

Recent evidence suggests that endothelial IDO1 signaling may interact with complementary pathways25,26 involved in vascular dysfunction. Both A2B adenosine receptor signaling27,28 and hypoxia-inducible factor29 signaling regulate endothelial barrier function, inflammation, and thrombosis, and both are induced under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions. Although their interaction with endothelial IDO1 was not examined in the present study, these pathways may cooperate to promote thromboinflammatory responses and warrant further investigation.

Emerging evidence also suggests that microRNAs (miRNAs) provide an additional posttranscriptional layer regulating IDO1 expression and downstream immune-vascular responses. miR-448 and miR-153 directly target the IDO1 3′untranslated region and suppress its expression, thereby enhancing CD8+ T-cell responses in cancer models.30,31 In addition, miR-142-5p has been shown to regulate IDO1 expression in tumor-associated endothelial compartments through cytokine and epigenetic mechanisms, linking miRNA signaling to immune and vascular dysfunction.32 Collectively, these findings suggest that miRNA-mediated regulation may fine-tune IDO1-driven metabolic and thromboinflammatory pathways.

Clinically, these findings have important implications. IDO1 activity and elevated Kyn levels have been documented in multiple malignancies and correlate with adverse outcomes.33 Our results suggest that the Kyn-to-Trp ratio could serve as a mechanistically informed biomarker to refine thrombotic risk prediction beyond existing clinical scores. Therapeutically, selective targeting of the endothelial Trp-IDO1-Kyn pathway may provide a strategy to reduce cancer-associated thrombosis and preserve systemic IDO1-mediated immune regulation.34, 35, 36, 37 Emerging endothelial-targeted delivery approaches, including nanoparticle34, 35, 36 and gene-based platforms,38 may enable vascular-specific modulation of IDO1 and minimize systemic effects.

Several points merit consideration. The MC38 syngeneic colorectal cancer model was selected because of its robust inflammatory and immunometabolic characteristics in immunocompetent mice, as well as the clinical relevance of CRC-associated thrombosis,39,40 yet it does not encompass the heterogeneity of human malignancies. Variability in tumor architecture and the lack of consistent terminal tumor mass measurements across cohorts represent additional limitations in assessing tumor burden. Using the Cdh5-CreERT2 mouse model, the functional contribution of IDO1 deletion in nonendothelial lineages, including immune cells, cannot be fully excluded.41,42 Future in vitro studies will further define other cellular effects of Trp and Kyn on endothelial function. In addition, dietary Trp enrichment represents a mechanistic tool rather than a direct clinical parallel. Future studies across diverse tumor models, longer-term thrombosis paradigms, and translational studies involving human cohorts will be necessary to define the therapeutic window and biomarker potential of endothelial IDO1 targeting.

In summary, our findings identify endothelial IDO1 as a pivotal metabolic-vascular nexus in cancer-associated thrombosis. By genetically isolating the endothelial contribution to Trp metabolism, we demonstrate that the Trp-IDO1-Kyn axis directly links tumor metabolic reprogramming to venous thrombus formation. Therefore, targeting endothelial IDO1 may represent a rational strategy to mitigate thrombotic risk in patients with cancer and preserve systemic hemostatic balance.
