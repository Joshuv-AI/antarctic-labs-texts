Safety and Feasibility of Bedside Electrical Impedance Tomography With Hypertonic Saline Contrast for Assessing Pulmonary Perfusion in Mechanically Ventilated Children With Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: A Pilot Study
Authors: Jing Zhang, Shuang Liu, Ning Li, Wei Li, Dong Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) offers a noninvasive, radiation-free imaging alternative, but its perfusion evaluation capability in children is understudied. Hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT may address this gap, enabling real-time guidance for individualized respiratory support.OBJECTIVES:This study aims to systematically assess the safety and efficacy of hypertonic saline-enhanced bedside EIT in evaluating pulmonary perfusion in pediatric patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).DESIGN:Single-center prospective observational study.SETTING AND PARTICIPANTS:Pediatric ARDS patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation were enrolled at the PICU of the Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University, China, from December 29, 2024, to August 7, 2025.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:Each participant received a central venous bolus of 0.2 mL/kg (up to a maximum of 10 mL) of a 10% hypertonic saline. EIT data were collected from 2 minutes before the injection to 1–2 minutes following it. Key parameters, including dead space ventilation fraction, intrapulmonary shunt fraction, and ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) matching percentage, were analyzed to evaluate lung pathophysiology and inform therapeutic strategies. The study monitored eight patients (four males, four females; ages ranging from 4 mo to 11 yr).RESULTS:All procedures were well-tolerated, with no adverse events, including hypernatremia. In five patients who also underwent contrast-enhanced CT, EIT findings were consistent with CT results. Seven patients demonstrated matched V/Q ratios (74.97–80.39%), while one patient with pulmonary embolism exhibited increased dead space ventilation (37.03%) and decreased V/Q matching (60.71%).CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:Hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT is a safe, effective, and feasible bedside method for evaluating pulmonary perfusion in mechanically ventilated children with ARDS. These findings implied potential associations with the CT outcomes, indicating its significant potential for guiding targeted respiratory management. Although promising, these results stem from a small sample size, necessitating further validation in larger studies.

KEY POINTS

Question: What are the safety and efficacy of hypertonic saline-enhanced electrical impedance tomography (EIT) examination among pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients?

Findings: This single-center prospective observational study found that hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT findings were consistent with CT results. No adverse events such as hypernatremia were found.

Meaning: Hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT is a safe, effective, and feasible bedside method for evaluating pulmonary perfusion in mechanically ventilated children with ARDS.

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is marked by inflammation-induced pulmonary exudation, consolidation, and widespread endothelial injury (1). These pathologic alterations can lead to hypoxic vasoconstriction and microvascular thrombosis, which, in turn, contribute to heterogeneous pulmonary perfusion. Such conditions may result in significant ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, further aggravating ventilator-induced lung injury (2). Although single-photon emission CT is regarded as a relatively accurate method for evaluating lung ventilation and perfusion (3), it poses considerable risks for critically ill ARDS patients. CT, a common diagnostic tool for ARDS, is often impractical for mechanically ventilated pediatric patients due to the inherent radiation exposure and logistical difficulties involved in patient transport. Consequently, bedside techniques like electrical impedance tomography (EIT), which facilitate the monitoring of both ventilation and perfusion, hold significant clinical relevance.

EIT is a noninvasive, radiation-free technique that allows continuous and dynamic monitoring of lung ventilation at the bedside (4). It involves the application of a low-amplitude electrical current via electrodes positioned on the thorax, while detecting the resulting changes in impedance due to ventilation. The generated signals are reconstructed to generate real-time images of regional lung function (2). Hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT for assessing pulmonary perfusion leverages the principle of first-pass imaging with a contrast agent (5). Research performed on adults has shown that hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT is a safe and effective method for evaluating V/Q matching and quantifying intrapulmonary shunt (2, 6–8). However, the application of this technique in pediatric populations is still under investigation, only a few case reports have been published (9, 10).

This study focused on pediatric patients diagnosed with ARDS who were receiving invasive mechanical ventilation in an PICU in China (2024–2025). Key parameters such as dead space ventilation fraction, intrapulmonary shunt fraction, and the V/Q matching ratio were analyzed. In this study, we investigated the safety and operational feasibility of hypertonic saline-enhanced EIT in pediatric population.

METHODS

This single-center prospective observational study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Institute of Pediatrics on December 12, 2024 (Ethics Review Approval Number: SHERLL2024122). All research procedures were carried out in line with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation at our institution and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised thereafter. Before their inclusion, written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants.

STUDY DESIGN AND POPULATION

This observational physiologic study was conducted in the ICU of the Capital Center for Children’s Health from December 29, 2024, to August 7, 2025. Patients were eligible for enrollment if they met the following criteria: between 1 month and 16 years old, diagnosed with ARDS according to the 2023 Second International Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Pediatric ARDS (11), required prolonged invasive mechanical ventilation for more than 24 hours, and had a central venous catheter (either in the internal jugular or femoral vein) for hypertonic saline administration. Exclusion criteria included having an implanted cardiac pacemaker or defibrillator, local skin infections or open wounds at the site for electrode placement, severe hypernatremia, hemodynamic instability as defined by the inability to achieve normal blood pressure values despite fluid resuscitation and vasoactive support, and specific body mass index (BMI) thresholds. The age-specific mean arterial pressure (MAP) thresholds, as outlined in the 2024 Phoenix Criteria for Pediatric Sepsis and Septic Shock, were as follows: for infants between 1 and 11 months: MAP less than 38 mm Hg; for children between 1 and 2 years: MAP less than 43 mm Hg; for 2–5 years: MAP less than 44 mm Hg; for 5–12 years: MAP less than 48 mm Hg; and for 12–17 years: MAP less than 51 mm Hg (12). Furthermore, a BMI exceeding 50 kg/m2 and severe thoracic deformities were grounds for exclusion. Children who were difficult to sedate or exhibited persistent spontaneous breathing were also excluded.

EIT MEASUREMENT AND DATA ACQUISITION

EIT measurements were conducted using the PulmoVista500 device (Draeger Medical Equipment [Moislinger Allee 53-55, 23542 Luebeck, Germany; Revalstrasse 1, 23560 Luebeck, Germany]). An EIT belt containing 16 surface electrodes was positioned around each patient’s thorax at the level of the fourth intercostal space, specifically around the nipple line, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The evaluations were carried out under controlled mechanical ventilation conditions. To mitigate hemodynamic interference and minimize motion artifacts, an end-expiratory hold maneuver was executed using the ventilator’s expiratory pause function (2, 13, 14). Sedative drugs such as propofol at 0.2 mL/kg and neuromuscular blocking agents such as rocuronium at 0.6 mg/kg were administered as necessary to maintain controlled ventilation and suppress any spontaneous breathing efforts.

When the EIT monitor displayed a plateau in the global time-impedance curve, the operator immediately administered a bolus of 10% hypertonic saline at 0.2 mL/kg (maximum 10 mL) within 2 seconds via a central venous catheter (Fig. 1). The pediatric dosage was calculated as 0.2 mL/kg based on the adult dose (10 mL of 10% hypertonic saline), with consideration of child weight of 50 kg. Therefore, patients in this study received 10% hypertonic saline at 0.2 mL/kg, with a maximum single dose not to exceed 10 mL (2). Electrical impedance data were collected from 2 minutes pre-injection to 2 minutes post-injection (Fig. 1).

Electrical impedance tomography-based pulmonary ventilation distribution before and after saline injection. The curve in the V0–V1 interval reflect the cyclic changes in thoracic electrical impedance induced by respiration and was used to generate pulmonary ventilation images. The curve in the P0–P2 interval indicates the variation in overall thoracic impedance caused by the injection of hypertonic saline during breath holding. The P0–P1 interval correspond to the arrival and accumulation of saline in the right heart, while the P1–P2 interval shows its passage into both lungs. The P1–P2 interval was used in the reconstruction of pulmonary perfusion images.

ANALYSIS OF VENTILATION/PERFUSION MATCHING RESULTS AND SAFETY ASSESSMENT

The Draeger EIT Data Analysis Tool, Version 6.3 (Luebeck, Germany) was employed to convert the EIT data into bin format. Subsequently, lung ventilation and perfusion were analyzed using the Perfusion_ZZ3 software (Luebeck, Germany). Ventilation distribution images were generated by analyzing tidal impedance variations over at least five consecutive respiratory cycles (from phase V0 to V1 in Fig. 1). The functional perfusion map was developed by calculating the slope of regional impedance-time curves after saline bolus injection (P1 to P2 in Fig. 1) (15). To minimize the impact of cardiac activity on the perfusion image, the initial impedance fall segment of the global time-impedance curve was excluded from the analysis (P0 to P1 in Fig. 1). Ventilated and perfused regions were defined as pixels with values exceeding 20% of the maximum pixel value in the functional ventilation and perfusion maps, respectively. Based on this analysis, the following parameters were derived: dead space, defined as lung regions that were ventilated but not perfused; intrapulmonary shunt, which refers to lung regions that were perfused but not ventilated; and V/Q matching, indicating lung regions that were both ventilated and perfused. A V/Q matching value below 60% signifies a considerable V/Q mismatch.

Based on the results obtained from EIT-derived V/Q assessments, clinical management was tailored accordingly. An increase in dead space suggested the presence of pulmonary embolism or hypoperfusion. Conversely, a rise in intrapulmonary shunt indicated conditions such as atelectasis or consolidation. Significant V/Q mismatch, characterized by a matching ratio of less than 60%, necessitated immediate intervention. Therapeutic approaches included prone positioning for conditions dominated by shunting, bronchoscopic lavage for suspected mucus plugs or lobar collapse, adjustments to ventilator settings to optimize positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and tidal volume, and careful fluid management in cases of volume overload. Post-procedure serum electrolyte levels were closely monitored to ensure the safety of the EIT examination, while a comparative analysis with contrast-enhanced chest CT was conducted in hemodynamically stable patients.

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous data that followed a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± sd (x̄ ± sd), whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as median with interquartile range (median [Q1–Q3]).

RESULTS

The study included eight pediatric patients (four males and four females) between 4 months and 11 years old, all diagnosed with ARDS (median oxygenation index [OI], 8.8 [7.2–10.2]). The etiologies of ARDS were predominantly pneumonia-related complications, such as pulmonary embolism and hemorrhage, along with underlying conditions including congenital heart disease and immunodeficiencies. Five of the patients underwent concurrent chest CT scans. The duration of invasive ventilation varied between 4.1 and 26.3 days, with one case extending due to central hypoventilation. The length of hospital stay ranged from 9 to 93 days as shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B665). Hypertonic saline (0.2 mL/kg, maximum 10 mL) was administered via femoral (n = 2) or jugular veins (n = 6), as outlined in Supplementary Table 1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B665). A single injection was sufficient to obtain satisfactory global time-impedance curves in all pediatric patients.

Findings and Clinical Outcomes

EIT monitoring demonstrated satisfactory V/Q matching in seven patients, with matching ratios between 74.97% and 80.39%. All these patients underwent a weaning trial and were successfully weaned. No adverse reactions associated with the sedatives or neuromuscular blocking agents, such as hypotension, were recorded. Notably, one patient with pulmonary embolism exhibited a reduced V/Q ratio (60.71%) along with elevated dead space ventilation (37.03%). After receiving thrombolytic therapy, this patient was also successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation. Detailed results are detailed in Supplementary Table 1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B665).

Furthermore, five patients underwent concurrent contrast-enhanced chest CT scans. There was correspondence between EIT-based lung ventilation and perfusion and the findings from the CT imaging. A detailed comparative analysis is illustrated in Figure 2.

Electrolyte Changes Following EIT Examination

Serum sodium and chloride levels were assessed before and after the EIT procedure. Before the examination, sodium levels were 135 mmol/L (134–138 mmol/L), and chloride levels were 106 mmol/L (102–110 mmol/L). After the examination, sodium levels increased to 137 mmol/L (135–139 mmol/L), while chloride levels decreased to 104 mmol/L (102–107 mmol/L). The median changes noted were an increase in sodium of 2 mmol/L (–2 to 2 mmol/L) and a decrease in chloride of –2 mmol/L (–3 to 0 mmol/L). Detailed results are detailed in Supplementary Table 3 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B665). Throughout the monitoring period, no adverse events such as hypernatremia, hyperchloremia, or acid-base imbalance were observed in any patient.

DISCUSSION

This study provide further evidence that EIT-based V/Q matching is feasible and safe in children with ARDS. The results are correlated with CT findings, suggesting that EIT as a functional, radiation-free imaging modality for guiding personalized therapy in critically ill children. EIT is increasingly being applied as a bedside-compatible, noninvasive, and radiation-free monitoring technique, which is particularly valuable in pediatric critical care. By utilizing central venous saline boluses, EIT offers a promising method for diagnosing the causes of respiratory failure while enabling real-time monitoring of V/Q matching during mechanical ventilation. Numerous animal studies and clinical trials in adults have shown a strong correlation between EIT-derived perfusion parameters and established reference standards such as CT and positron emission tomography (PET)/CT, while also confirming the safety of this technique (2, 5, 16). However, research into its application within pediatric populations remains limited, with only a few case reports currently available (9, 10).

Currently, hypertonic saline is commonly employed as a contrast agent in clinical practice. Adult studies typically use a bolus of 10 mL of 10% hypertonic saline (2, 6), although some protocols have opted for 5% saline (4, 9, 17) or 7.5% saline (8). However, there are no standardized dosing guidelines for pediatric patients. In animal experiments, the relative lung perfusion assessed by EIT with central venous injection of different concentrations of saline was compared with that assessed by PET. The root mean square errors for the differences in relative perfusion between PET and EIT using 10%, 5%, and 3% saline were 4.4%, 4.5%, and 5.7%, respectively (18). This indicates that when 10% saline was used as a contrast agent, the difference in assessing regional lung perfusion between EIT and PET was the smallest. Many clinical studies have also confirmed the safety of 10% saline as a contrast agent (2, 6, 14, 19). In our study, we administered 10% hypertonic saline at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg (up to a maximum of 10 mL) in children, demonstrating both reliable imaging accuracy and a favorable safety profile. All patients were mechanically ventilated under adequate sedation and neuromuscular blockade, and no impact on impedance or baseline stability was observed. Each child received a single injection. Diagnostic-quality images were successfully obtained in all cases, with no significant electrolyte abnormalities reported. If spontaneous breathing or other motion artifacts were recorded during the injection, the procedure was repeated one to two times at the discretion of the clinical team. For patients who received multiple assessments in a single day, the total dose of 10% hypertonic saline was capped at 0.9 mL/kg/d. This dosage provides an equivalent mass of sodium chloride (in grams) as 10 mL/kg of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, which is the standard volume used in pediatric shock fluid resuscitation. 0.9% saline solution and 10% hypertonic saline are not equivalent in osmotic pressure and hemodynamic effects. Owing to its high osmotic pressure and high conductivity (19), 10% hypertonic saline is better suited as a contrast agent for pulmonary perfusion studies. Both adult studies and our own findings indicate that a single injection is safe. However, there are currently no reports on what dose would be safe if multiple injections are required. All patients in this study received a single injection. Therefore, further research is needed to determine the appropriate volume of 10% saline that can be administered to different patients, along with monitoring of hemodynamics and the internal environment. Considering the potential risks associated with 10% hypertonic saline, we will investigate the effectiveness of injecting 5% sodium chloride solution in a larger sample size in future.

Our study serves as an initial exploration of this technique in pediatric ARDS patients, including one as young as 4 months and 26 days. With the exception of one individual diagnosed with pulmonary embolism, all participants exhibited V/Q matching greater than 60%, although intrapulmonary shunts were present in all cases (range, 5.96–21.57%). Notably, one infant with congenital heart disease (atrial septal defect and pulmonary hypertension) and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia was classified as having moderate ARDS (OI, 9.2) and was ventilated with a PEEP of 10 cm H2O; this patient demonstrated the lowest shunt fraction among nonembolic cases. This observation suggests that higher PEEP may mitigate intrapulmonary shunt, and EIT-based V/Q monitoring can facilitate the selection of appropriate PEEP. Similarly, it can also enable clinicians to evaluate the effects of prone positioning (20). Each patient in our study received targeted interventions following EIT evaluation, including prone positioning and bronchoalveolar lavage, with successful extubation for all. However, the limited sample size precluded any establishment of prognostic correlations. Studies in both adult and pediatric ARDS patients have reported that the EIT-guided PEEP titration can improve lung compliance, reduce mechanical power and driving pressure, while decreasing the risk of ventilator-associated lung injury (21, 22).

In clinical practice, there is a notable gap in straightforward bedside methods for monitoring V/Q matching. Notably, EIT addresses this challenge by combining functional and anatomical assessments to evaluate both ventilation and perfusion simultaneously. In the current study, patient 8 (Fig. 2E) with left pulmonary embolism, exhibited a dead space fraction of 37.03% and a V/Q match ratio of 60.71%. These results align with previously established thresholds that indicate significant V/Q mismatch (2). Following thrombolytic therapy, the patient was successfully weaned from invasive mechanical ventilation. In contrast, patient 1 (Fig. 2A), who also had pulmonary embolism, presented with a right lower lobe embolism complicated by consolidation. CT imaging revealed a substantial hypoperfused area in the right lower lobe along with a filling defect in the right lower pulmonary artery. EIT-based perfusion imaging showed a complete absence of ventilation and perfusion signals in the right dorsal region, while the V/Q match in other lung areas was recorded at 80.39%. This case highlights a critical limitation of EIT in analyzing V/Q match: when embolism and parenchymal consolidation occur in the same region, EIT may struggle to differentiate the contributions of each condition. The incidence of pulmonary embolism in children is lower than in adults. However, during the 2023–2024 outbreak of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in China, many cases of pulmonary embolism were reported as a complication of this infection (23). In our study, patient 1 is a typical example, who presented with both pulmonary necrosis caused by M. pneumoniae pneumonia and pulmonary embolism. However, the diagnostic utility of EIT in this specific context is limited, thus requiring integration with CT imaging and comprehensive clinical evaluations.

This study serves as an initial exploration of safety and efficacy but does have several limitations. The patient cohort primarily consisted of individuals with mild-to-moderate ARDS, lacking severe cases, and the overall sample size was limited. Other limitations, such as heterogeneity of cases, no reproducibility measures, no clinical outcomes attributable to EIT, no predefined hypothesis, and 2D EIT limitations in small pediatric chests, should be recognized. Future research should aim to include a larger number of pediatric patients and conduct assessments at earlier stages of the disease. Evaluating V/Q matching, intrapulmonary shunt, and dead space ventilation percentage could provide valuable insights into the clinical status of patients and inform subsequent treatment strategies. Additionally, conducting dynamic EIT comparisons before and after saline infusion, both pre- and post-treatment, may prove beneficial for assessing therapeutic efficacy.

CONCLUSIONS

Ten percent hypertonic saline bolus-based EIT showed good safety and efficacy in mechanically ventilated children with ARDS. However, due to the limited sample size, its application should be tested in the early assessment of severe ARDS in pediatric patients in larger samples in the future. We also plan to conduct further research on 5% saline bolus in the future.
