The Equalizer: Introducing Shape-Gain Decomposition in Neural Audio Codecs
Samir Sadok, Laurent Girin, Xavier Alameda-Pineda

Abstract
Neural audio codecs (NACs) typically encode the short-term energy (gain) and normalized structure (shape) of speech/audio signals jointly within the same latent space. As a result, they are poorly robust to a global variation of the input signal level in the sense that such variation has strong influence on the embedding vectors at the output of the encoder and their quantization. This methodology is inherently inefficient, leading to codebook redundancy and suboptimal bitrate-distortion performance. To address these limitations, we propose to introduce shape-gain decomposition, widely used in classical speech/audio coding, into the NAC framework. The principle of the proposed Equalizer methodology is to decompose the input signal—before the NAC encoder—into gain and normalized shape vector on a short-term basis. The shape vector is processed by the NAC, while the gain is quantized with scalar quantization and transmitted separately. The output (decoded) signal is reconstructed from the normalized output of the NAC and the quantized gain. Our experiments conducted on speech signals show that this general methodology, easily applicable to any NAC, enables a substantial gain in bitrate-distortion performance, as well as a massive reduction in complexity.

The quantization mechanism that maps the continuous embedding vectors into discrete tokens is a central component of any NAC. Several quantizer design strategies have been used in NACs, most of them inherited from classical methods

The resulting quantizers can operate at fixed, scalable, or adaptive bitrates
[

[
19

Despite their diversity in DNN and VQ design, it is striking to notice that most modern NACs share the same above-mentioned general structure. However, the complex
non-linear
mapping made by the encoder has a direct major consequence that is at the same time trivial, questionable, and, a bit surprisingly, not discussed in the literature (to our knowledge): Two signals that are colinear in the input (waveform) space, i.e. identical up to a gain factor, are generally mapped to embedding vectors that are
not
colinear.
As a result, the latent representation at the output of the encoder entangles the signal amplitude and structural content into a single vector space. This contrasts sharply with the common assumption that, on a short-term basis, speech/audio signals often have a similar general shape (e.g. spectral content) and different energy levels. In other words, the shape of a speech/audio signal can be invariant to magnitude. This realistic assumption is largely exploited in many speech/audio processing techniques. For example, it is at the core of the linear prediction coding (LPC) technique, which is widely used in speech analysis and speech coding, and that separates the gain and normalized spectral envelope encoded by the prediction coefficients
[
2
,
17
]
. Another example is cepstral modeling, in which the first coefficient encodes the energy and other coefficients encode the normalized spectral envelope on a logarithmic scale
[
24
,
3
]
. This general
shape-gain decomposition
principle can be summarized as follows: at a signal segment level, i.e. a short-term frame of waveform samples or any vector representing this short-term frame, the signal is decomposed into a gain (representing its magnitude or energy) and a shape vector (representing its normalized direction, envelope, spectral shape, etc.). Most importantly for our study, shape-gain decomposition is widely-used in classic speech/audio codecs, in particular when using VQ: It is processed before the application of VQ to the normalized shape vector and
scalar
quantization to the gain
[
9
,
21
,
14
,
10
,
27
]
. Examples of standardized codecs that use such
shape-gain VQ
include CELP
[
26
]
, AMR
[
4
]
, and Opus
[
29
]
, to name a few. Note that shape-gain VQ is compatible with the use of a
linear
transform before quantization (and inverse linear transform after quantization; e.g. the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) in MPEG-2 AAC codecs
[
5
,
20
]
), as opposed to the non-linear encoder and decoder of NACs.

Shape-gain VQ has several major advantages over direct VQ. First, it generally improves the rate-distortion performance. Indeed, let us consider the typical case of several input vectors with a similar general shape and different energy levels.
With direct VQ, the vector quantizer must allocate multiple codewords to represent what is essentially the same acoustic structure at different loudness levels. This results in a very large codebook with many different codeword subsets, each subset encoding the same shape and different gain values. In contrast, with shape-gain VQ, these vectors are encoded with the same vector codeword plus a scalar gain prototype value that requires a very limited bitrate. Therefore, the size of the VQ codebook can be significantly reduced. This not only generally leads to a better rate-distortion performance, but this also enables to drastically decrease the complexity of the codecs, which is directly related to the codebook size, since the search of the optimal codeword in the codebook is often the most computationally demanding operation in signal compression
[
9
]
. In addition, the use of normalized vectors has been shown to help stabilize the design of VQ codebooks
[
34
]
.

A bit surprisingly, to our knowledge, modern neural codecs, including SoundStream, EnCodec, and DAC, have not considered integrating the shape-gain decomposition principle. Instead, as already mentioned, they let the amplitude and shape of an input vector be encoded jointly in the corresponding embedding vector.
Although NACs already impress with their performance in terms of data rate-distortion trade-off, this approach is problematic.
If a simple gain variation induces a significant directional change in the latent space, this leads to suboptimal codebook design and usage and reduced robustness to input signal variability. This problem may be solved, and thus performance significantly improved (and/or complexity significantly reduced), by introducing shape-gain coding in the NAC framework. Finally, explicit control over signal energy can bring more interpretability in the NAC framework.

In the remainder of this paper, we address this problem in two steps. First, in Section
2
, we report a series of empirical observations that demonstrate and quantify the sensitivity of current state-of-the-art NACs to a global gain variation of the input waveform, illustrating the detrimental entanglement of amplitude and shape in the latent space. Second, in Section
3
, we introduce The Equalizer, a methodological framework that explicitly integrates shape-gain decomposition into the neural coding pipeline as a solution to this problem.
Section
4
presents a series of experimental results obtained with speech signals, which demonstrate that the proposed Equalizer approach not only ensures robust performance invariance across a wide range of input gain levels, but also leads to substantial gain in performance in terms of bitrate-distortion trade-off and complexity reduction.
By revisiting the classical shape-gain decomposition and quantization within a modern learning framework, we aim to contribute to bridge the gap between classical signal processing wisdom and contemporary NAC design, combining robustness to input signal variability, interpretability, and rate-distortion-complexity efficiency with the flexibility of learned representations.

2
An Experimental analysis of the Sensitivity of Neural Audio Codecs to the Input Gain

In this Section, we motivate the introduction of the shape-gain decomposition in the NAC framework by conducting an empirical analysis of the effect of an input signal global level variation over the distribution and quantization of the embedding vectors in a representative NAC. The presented experiments aim at answering the following two fundamental questions: (i) Does input gain variation primarily affect the norm, the direction, or both in the latent space? (ii) What is the impact of input gain variations on the stability of the resulting discrete codes?

In short, our empirical analysis demonstrates (i) that gain and shape are strongly entangled in the latent space of current NACs, and (ii) a strong sensitivity of the NACs (and thus of their codebook design) to the input signal gain. This gain sensitivity is not merely a benign scaling effect, but a structural property that affects codeword allocation and utilization at inference time and codebook design at training time. All this comes in sharp contrast with the shape-gain decomposition principle that underlie classical speech/audio coding, as discussed in the introduction. These findings motivate the need for a global NAC framework that explicitly controls or factors out the input signal gain.

3
Proposed Method: The Equalizer

In this section, we present a general methodology, that we call The Equalizer, for exploiting the shape-gain decomposition of speech/audio signals in the NAC framework. We then implement this methodology for a typical NAC, derived from the widely-used EnCodec model, and show that the proposed method enables a large increase in the performance of this codec in terms of bitrate-distortion-complexity trade-off.

3.1
General Principle and Method Overview

As discussed in Section
1
and illustrated in Section
2
, one notable problem encountered with NACs is that their non-linear encoder entangles the gain and shape information of the input signal into a single embedding vector. If we want to resort to shape-gain decomposition to improve coding efficiency, obviously, applying it at the embedding vectors quantization level, i.e. after the encoder, is not an option: This is simply too late.
Instead, we propose to apply the shape-gain decomposition before the encoder, i.e. at the input signal level. The corresponding shape-gain recomposition is applied at the output of the decoder. The general workflow of the proposed Equalizer methodology is illustrated in Figure
2
.

In some more details, this process is done classically on a short-term frame basis: Each short-term frame of the input waveform is normalized in energy before being sent as an input to the NAC encoder (Block 1 in Figure
2
). In this way, input vectors with similar shape and different gains are first transformed to similar normalized vectors before being fed to the encoder, resulting in similar embedding vectors that can be quantized with the same codeword. Note that this requires retraining the NAC on normalized data (see details in Section
4.1
). The gain of each short-term frame is quantized separately with scalar quantization and then transmitted to the output of the decoder (Block 3). There, the sequence of quantized gains and the corresponding decoded normalized shape vectors are recombined using overlap-add synthesis to generate the final output waveform with restored energy profile (Block 4).

It can be noted that, in the present study, the shape-gain decomposition and recomposition steps (as well as the gain quantization) are implemented as modules that are external to the NAC. In other words, the shape-gain decomposition and shape vector normalization are not applied directly to the vectors taken as input by the encoder. Instead, a complete waveform is reconstructed from the sequence of normalized short-term frames before being sent to the NAC. In the present study, we refer to this process as
frame-wise gain equalization
—or simply
equalization
for concision---of the input waveform.
2
2

2

Not to be confused with spectral equalization widely used in audio processing.
Similarly, we retrieve the complete decoded equalized waveform at the decoder output and apply
frame-wise gain restoration
, i.e. deequalization, to this waveform. This is because of two reasons: First, we do not necessarily have easy access to the input vectors that are actually sent to the encoder and that may result from some specific pre-processing of the input waveform. The same is true for the output vectors just after the decoder. This depends on the NAC, its implementation, and the ‘transparency’ of the code. Second, we want to provide a proof of concept of the proposed methodology that remains general and easily applicable to any NAC with a minimal amount of work. Implementing the shape-gain decomposition/recomposition on the actual short-term input/output vectors processed by the encoder/decoder of a specific target codec does not change the foundations of the proposed method. In the case where access to these vectors is easy, applying the proposed methodology directly to them is straightforward.

Note also that, in this study, we do not implement our methodology in a streaming mode, where all processes in the pipe-line would be applied consistently on a causal frame-by-frame basis, but this is totally feasible without difficulty.

3.2
Implementation Details

Equalization of the Input Waveform

Following the standard methodology in speech/audio analysis-synthesis
[
24
,
27
,
3
]
, the input signal
\mathbf{s}
is first centered and then segmented into overlapping frames
\mathbf{s}_{w}[m]
using an analysis window
\mathbf{w}
of length
N
and shift
H
:

\mathbf{s}_{w}[m]=\mathbf{s}(mH:mH+N-1)\odot\mathbf{w},

(2)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.
Then, for each frame, the gain, here denoted
g_{m}
, is computed as:

g_{m}=\|\mathbf{s}_{w}[m]\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left|\mathbf{s}_{w}[m,n]\right|^{2}},

(3)

where
n
represents the intra-frame sample index ranging from
0
to
N-1
. This scalar value represents the local energy of the speech/audio signal, and the succession of
g_{m}
values over the frames represents its energy temporal envelope (or profile). The normalized shape vector, denoted
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{w}[m]
, is then obtained by:

\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{w}[m]=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{w}[m]}{g_{m}+\epsilon},

(4)

where
\epsilon=10^{-12}
is a small constant used to prevent numerical instability if
g_{m}
is equal to zero.

To reconstruct a complete equalized waveform, which is the signal form intended at the encoder input, the individual normalized signal frames are multiplied by a synthesis window
\mathbf{w}_{s}
and recombined using an overlap-add (OLA) procedure
[
24
,
27
,
3
]
:

\bar{\mathbf{s}}(n)=\sum_{m}\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{w}[m,n-mH]\cdot\mathbf{w}_{s}(n-mH).

(5)

The settings of the short-term analysis and resynthesis are given in Section
4.1
and are motivated in the Appendix. The resulting signal
\bar{\mathbf{s}}
retains the fine time-frequency characteristics of the original input while exhibiting a constant energy profile.
This allows the NAC to focus exclusively on the structural characteristics of the waveform.

Shape Vector Quantization

The equalized waveform
\bar{\mathbf{s}}
is sent to the NAC, which will thus process the corresponding sequence of normalized shape vectors. The NAC is based on EnCodec
[
6
]
.
It employs a convolutional-based encoder-decoder network that performs temporal downscaling with a specific striding factor, reducing the temporal resolution into a compact latent space. Following the architecture proposed in SpeechTokenizer, we have replaced the original two-layer LSTM following the convolution blocks in EnCodec with a two-layer Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM). This modification aims to augment the semantic modeling ability of the network by capturing dependencies from both past and future contexts within the sequence. Each embedding vector obtained at the output of the encoder is quantized using RVQ. This method uses multiple cascaded quantization layers, where each successive layer quantizes the residual error of the preceding stage using a different codebook
[
9
,
21
]
. This hierarchical approach enables to drastically reduce the complexity of the encoding at each stage, while ensuring high-fidelity reconstruction when a sufficient number of layers is used.

Gain Quantization

In parallel to the shape VQ, the gain
g_{m}
is encoded using a non-linear scalar quantizer. Given that human perception of loudness is logarithmic, we apply
\mu
-law quantization

[
9
]
to the gain values. The
\mu
-law transformation is defined as:

F(g_{m})=\texttt{sgn}(g_{m})~\frac{\ln(1+\mu|g_{m}|)}{\ln(1+\mu)},

(6)

where we set
\mu=255
. This mapping provides a finer quantization resolution for small amplitudes, which is critical for maintaining audio quality in low-energy signal portions. On the decoder side, the inverse
\mu
-law transformation is applied to restore the gain to its original dynamic range.

Output Signal Deequalization

The final stage of the proposed Equalizer method is the
deequalization
of the output signal
\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}}
, i.e. recovery of the original signal level dynamics. This process is similar to the equalization of the input waveform, except that here the quantized gain
\hat{g}_{m}
is applied to each short-term frame of
\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}}
to restore its original loudness (up to quantization errors):

\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{w}[m]=\hat{g}_{m}\cdot\left(\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}}(mH:mH+N-1)\odot\mathbf{w}\right).

(7)

Finally, the output waveform
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
is obtained again using OLA synthesis:

\hat{\mathbf{s}}(n)=\sum_{m}\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{w}[m,n-mH]\cdot\mathbf{w}_{s}\left(n-mH\right).

(8)

With a proper choice and setting of the analysis-resynthesis parameters (see the Appendix), the overall process ensures that the signal maintains high perceptual quality. By decoupling the gain and the shape during all the processing phases and recombining them only at the final step, the Equalizer effectively preserves both the global dynamics and the fine-grained spectral details of the original audio.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Data

All experiments were carried out on 16-kHz speech signals. All models were trained using the
LibriSpeech-100
dataset
[
22
]
, randomly divided into
90
% for training and
10
% for validation. The evaluation relies on the standard
test-clean
partition (
2,620
utterances from
40
distinct speakers,
\approx 5.4
h) to ensure zero overlap with the training data. To demonstrate the interest of the proposed Equalizer method, we followed the same line as in Section
2
, using test signals modified by a global gain as in equation (
1
), for a wide range of input gains, from
-12
to
+12
dB (with a
2
-dB step). Note that the test data at
0
dB have the same average level as the training data. In other words, the input gain variation can be seen as a relative deviation from the average level of the training data.

Baselines

To isolate the impact of the proposed shape-gain decomposition and Equalizer methodology, we conducted parallel experiments with an ablated baseline that uses the exact same architecture, the same training dataset and training procedure, and the same bitrate configuration as the Equalizer, but without shape-gain decomposition. In this baseline, the encoder processes the raw waveform directly (i.e. without equalization) and the latent representation jointly encodes amplitude and structure, as in any conventional NAC. Regarding bitrate configuration, four different values of codebook size
C
were tested, namely
C=1024
,
512
,
256
, and
128
(note that
C
is the size for one RVQ stage and the same value of
C
is used at each stage).
In addition, we compare our approach against several state-of-the-art NACs, including SpeechTokenizer, DAC, and BigCodec, which serve as external reference models. Note that in the present study, we do not aim at providing a new sophisticated competing NAC with performance at the level of or beyond state-of-the-art performance. Rather, we aim to propose a general methodology easily applicable to any NAC, and demonstrate the potential of this methodology to increase coding performance in terms of bitrate-distortion trade-off and/or complexity reduction.

NAC Training Set-up

To ensure a fair comparison, the baseline and the NAC within the proposed Equalizer pipe-line were trained in the same configuration, except that for The Equalizer, every utterance in the training set was equalized.
All models were trained for
100,000
iterations using the Adam optimizer with
\beta_{1}=0.9,\beta_{2}=0.99
and an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}

[
13
]
.
We used a batch size of 16 randomly cropped audio segments of 3.0 seconds.
Regarding the optimization objective, we adopted the standard loss configuration employed in EnCodec
[
6
]
without modification.
Specifically, the reconstruction objective combines a time-domain
L_{1}
loss with a multi-scale spectral loss consisting of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
distances over Mel-spectrograms computed at various resolutions. This is augmented by the standard codebook commitment loss and adversarial losses.
Training was distributed across 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

Equalization/Deequalization Set-Up

For the analysis-resynthesis parameters of the proposed Equalizer method, we used the Kaiser-Bessel Derived (KBD) window with parameter
\beta=4.0

[
23
]
, for both analysis and synthesis, with
N=640
, and
H=320
. This choice results from a set of pilot experiments described in details in the Appendix.

Evaluation Metrics

We evaluated the quality of the coded speech signals using three widely adopted objective metrics: (i) The Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI) measure assesses how intelligible the resynthesized speech is
[
28
]
; (ii) Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) is a measure of the perceived quality of the resynthesized speech that accounts for factors such as distortion, noise, and other artifacts
[
25
]
; And (iii) the Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR) is defined as the power ratio between the original speech signal and the resynthesis error signal
[
16
]
; it is made invariant to a global amplitude rescaling of the signal. All three metrics are intrusive, i.e. they require the original reference speech signal.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented The Equalizer, a methodology for improving the performance of neural speech/audio coders by decomposing the input signal into normalized shape, coded by an NAC, and scalar gain, separately quantized with a scalar quantizer. Although this decomposition has been widely used for decades in classic speech/audio codecs, to our knowledge it has not yet been proposed within a NAC framework. Importantly, the decomposition is made before the NAC encoder and the corresponding recomposition is made after the decoder. In the present study, such equalization and deequalization are implemented as external modules, which can be easily applied to any state-of-the-art NAC, but they can also be applied internally on the actual vectors used as encoder input or retrieved as decoder output. Experiments have shown that The Equalizer enables a substantial improvement of the coding performance in terms of bitrate-distortion trade-off and a possibly massive reduction of the complexity of the NAC quantizer.

The present study focused on the equalization and deequalization of the input/output waveforms without explicitly examining the effect of the normalization on the embedding vectors. The NAC’s vector quantizer was simply retrained on the “new” embedding vectors (corresponding to the normalized encoder input) using the same structure and training process as the baseline. Future work will analyze the effect of input vector normalization on the distribution of the embedding vectors and explore the use of more sophisticated VQ techniques to exploit the characteristics of this distribution, with the aim of further improving the overall Equalizer pipeline.
