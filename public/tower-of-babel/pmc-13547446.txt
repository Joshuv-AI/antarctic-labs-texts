Negotiating Medication Adherence in Everyday Life with Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Emilie Ljungström, Henrik Hjortswang, Michael Eberhardson, Katarina Pihl Lesnovska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, and IBD unclassified, is a chronic condition that can affect daily life. Maintenance therapy is essential, yet non-adherence remains common and may compromise treatment outcomes. This study aimed to explore how adults with IBD understand, negotiate, and enact medication adherence as part of everyday self-care.MethodsInductive, qualitative, semi-structured interviews were conducted with adults diagnosed with IBD at a gastroenterology clinic in Sweden. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using content analysis.ResultsFifteen interviews were conducted (9 ulcerative colitis, 5 Crohn’s disease, 1 IBD-U). Three main categories were identified: illness perceptions, healthcare perceptions, and treatment perceptions. Medication adherence was described as an ongoing process shaped by how participants interpreted symptoms, adapted to life with IBD, and evaluated the need for treatment. Participants weighed the perceived benefits of medication against concerns about adverse effects and often combined medical treatment with lifestyle-based strategies for symptom management. Trusting communication, continuity of care, and access to relevant information facilitate adherence, while practical treatment burdens and uncertainty regarding disease activity undermined adherence.ConclusionMedication adherence in IBD is embedded within everyday self-care and influenced by illness perceptions, treatment beliefs, practical challenges, and healthcare encounters. Clinicians should acknowledge patients’ concerns, dietary practices, and lifestyle strategies alongside medical treatment. Person-centred communication, clear treatment goals, and shared decision-making may support adherence and strengthen self-care.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), comprising Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, often begins early in life. Symptoms arise from intestinal mucosal inflammation and typically follow a relapsing course, with frequent, urgent, and sometimes bloody stools together with abdominal pain. The global prevalence of IBD is increasing, and the disease is socially debilitating and reduces quality of life substantially.1 Multiple studies support the importance of maintenance therapy in preventing flares and achieving remission—characterized by mucosal healing and the absence of symptoms.2–5 Maintenance treatment of IBD includes aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, and biologic therapies, while corticosteroids are primarily used for short-term induction of remission. Nutritional and other supportive intervention may also form part of the overall management of IBD,5 Symptom control is often the main concern for patients, while healthcare professionals may aim for mucosal healing. This discrepancy may become relevant when patients experience symptom relief despite persistent inflammation, which potentially influences the perception of need for treatment.6

Adherence is “the extent to which the patient’s behavior corresponds with agreed recommendations from the health care provider”.7 The term adherence is preferred to compliance, which has been criticized for excluding patient involvement.8

Non-adherence remains a major barrier to favorable treatment outcomes.9,10 In ulcerative colitis, adherent patients have an 89% chance of maintaining remission, compared with 39% among non-adherent individuals.11 About 30% of patients with IBD are non-adherent.11,12 More broadly, adherence to long-term therapy for chronic conditions in developed countries averages 50%.7

Medication adherence can be understood as an integral part of self-care in chronic illness. Self-care and self-efficacy are related but distinct constructs. In chronic illness, self-care has been conceptualised as the process through which individuals maintain health, monitor bodily changes, and manage symptoms or treatment demands.13 Self-efficacy refers more specifically to confidence in one’s ability to perform behaviours. Self-care has been described as involving the interrelated processes of self-care maintenance, self-care monitoring and self-care management. In this study, self-care was used as the broader conceptual frame for understanding how adherence to treatment was embedded in everyday illness management, whereas self-efficacy was treated as a sensitising concept rather than as a separate analytical category. Although previous qualitative studies have identified treatment beliefs, healthcare experiences and information needs as important influences on adherence, less is known about how patients integrate symptom interpretation, treatment concerns, practical routines and healthcare relationships into everyday medication-related decision-making and self-care.14,15 Rather than focusing primarily on individual barriers to adherence, this study takes a broader perspective by exploring how adults with IBD understand, negotiate and enact medication adherence as part of everyday self-care, with particular attention to illness perceptions, treatment beliefs, practical barriers and facilitators, and experiences of communication with the IBD care team.

Materials and Methods

Design & Setting

This was an exploratory qualitative study using individual semi-structured interviews and conventional content analysis. The analysis was inductive in the sense that categories were developed from the interview data rather than derived from a pre-existing theoretical coding framework. The Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness, the Perceptions and Practicalities Approach and the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation were used as theory-informed interpretive frameworks in the introduction and discussion, not as deductive coding templates.

Researcher Positioning

Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private room at the gastroenterology outpatient clinic/the participant’s preferred location between November 2021 and June 2022.

Only the participant and interviewer were present during the interviews. The interviews lasted 35–75 minutes, with a mean duration of 47 minutes. The interviewer was a registered nurse specialized in IBD and had clinical experience from the outpatient setting, but the participants had not had recent clinical contact with the interviewer. This was explicitly addressed before each interview to reduce potential role-related power imbalance. Participants were informed that there were no right or wrong answers and that participation would not affect their care. Interviewers were guided by the interview guide (Supplementary file 1), while appropriate follow-up questions were used to clarify and deepen participants’ responses without leading them towards particular answers. Reflexivity was addressed through continuous discussion within the research group regarding how the interviewer’s clinical background, gender and pre-understanding could influence data collection and interpretation.

Participant, Recruitment and Data Collection

Participants were purposively invited using a maximum-variation strategy to obtain diversity in age, gender, diagnosis, and disease duration. “Significant comorbidity” referred to for example severe cognitive impairment, severe psychiatric illness, ongoing malignancy or other severe somatic disease judged to dominate the participant’s care needs or ability to participate in an interview. Two patients piloted the interview guide to assess comprehensibility and relevance of the questions. These pilot interviews were included in the analysis because only minor wording changes were made. This pilot testing should not be interpreted as patient and public involvement in the design, conduct or dissemination of the study. Data collection continued until the research group judged that the material had sufficient information power in relation to the study aim, sample specificity, quality of interview dialogue and analytical focus. After 13 interviews, additional interviews were conducted because new nuances were still emerging; after 15 interviews, the research group judged that the material contained sufficient depth and variation to address the study aim.

Analysis

All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. The first author first read all transcripts several times to obtain an overall understanding and identify meaning units relevant to the aim. Meaning units were condensed while preserving the core meaning and were coded close to the data. Codes were then compared for similarities and differences and sorted into preliminary subcategories and categories (Table 1). The preliminary coding and category structure were discussed repeatedly within the research group. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and by returning to the transcripts until consensus was reached. Ethical Considerations

All patients were enrolled by informed consent. All personal data were handled confidentially. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Swedish Review Authority (No. 2021–01275).16

Results

The analysis generated three main categories describing factors that influenced how participants viewed and managed medication adherence in the context of everyday life with IBD: illness perceptions, healthcare perceptions, and treatment perceptions. Conceptual model over the main- and subcategories.

The category illness perceptions comprised three subcategories: Understanding and accepting life with IBD, Recognising disease activity, and Fear and uncertainty about the future. Together, these findings described how participants interpreted their symptoms, adapted to the disease, and assessed their need for treatment.

The category healthcare perceptions included the subcategories Continuity and a trustful dialogue and Need for information. These experiences influenced participants’ confidence in healthcare recommendations and their willingness to adhere to treatment.

The category treatment perceptions consisted of the subcategories Risk–benefit assessment and Practical aspects of medication. Participants continuously weighed perceived benefits against concerns about adverse effects, while also managing practical challenges associated with medication use.

Overall, medication adherence was not described as an isolated behaviour but as an ongoing process shaped by illness experiences, treatment beliefs, and interactions with healthcare professionals.

Illness Perceptions

Most of the informants had made changes in their life to adapt to IBD. They considered abdominal symptoms important for interpreting the disease, but extra intestinal symptoms were described by some. The degree of acceptance varied but was achieved to a higher extent by those with longer disease duration or higher age. Fear was common, mainly concerning aggravated disease, surgery, endoscopy, and stepping up treatment. Poor daily functioning or a sense of restraint affected patients’ illness perception, resulting in a sense of being sick or disabled.

Understanding and Accepting Life with IBD

The informants had to different degrees changed their lifestyle to manage living with IBD. Some made changes only during flares; others permanently changed their daily routines. Stress-reducing activities were key to disease management, as well as keeping a diet. Relaxation and physical exercise were also repeatedly mentioned. Patients expressed the importance of the proximity to a toilet. Some informants actively searched for strategies to manage or cure their disease and would try anything. Several expressed the need to find effective measures themselves, rather than taking advice from healthcare. Many stated that their lifestyle, especially exposure to stress and certain food, highly affected their symptoms and could aggravate symptoms. Some informants believed that the disease could be self-healed by a yet unknown method or alternative medicine. Only a few patients believed that nothing could affect the course of the disease. Several participants described bowel symptoms as important warning signs, but functional limitation often appeared more decisive than symptom intensity alone. A participant explained: I would say that it very much depends on how much it affects one’s daily life. Not so much on what the stools look like if it’s blood or not or whatever. It’s more like how I function at work or among people that determines how ill I feel. (male 38 y)

Recognizing Disease Activity

Most of the informants described bowel-related symptoms as the main factor behind their understanding of the disease, encompassing increased stool frequency, bloody stools, urgency and pain. Many patients described extra intestinal symptoms, such as skin problems, fatigue, palpitations, dizziness, depression and lack of appetite. A few informants with Crohn’s disease were uncertain about their symptoms and could not fully describe symptoms indicating a worsening of their disease. Fear and Uncertainty About the Future

Fear related to various aspects of the disease, such as endoscopy, cancer, surgery, stepping up treatment and side-effects, was repeatedly mentioned. Some informants reported that they had family members or friends diagnosed with cancer which caused concern of developing cancer themselves. One informant, with a friend passing away due to misdiagnosis, commented that every single healthcare contact is extremely crucial. A few patients mentioned concerns about mortality and the fragility of life. Vague symptoms could trigger fear of serious disease. In other cases, fear was linked to a lack of knowledge or understanding, and for some, even led to avoidance of healthcare altogether. Several participants also described fear towards medication based on previous experiences of side-effects or drug-related fear overall. Healthcare Perceptions

The importance of regular contact and a personal relationship with one physician was striking and a trustful dialogue was especially significant. The informants expressed the need of information about the healthcare system, diagnosis, and treatment.

Continuity and a Trustful Dialogue

Most of the informants emphasized the importance of a continuous, trustful, and personal relationship with one physician. However, continuity was less important than respectful behaviour and incidents were mentioned that had led to distrust. Reasons were often not being taken seriously or having to go through examinations without implicit consent. Some participants specifically requested a dialogue about food, exercise, and other areas with limited evidence.

One patient reported stopping medication, because of a lack of discussion around lifestyle matters and no clear arguments that would convince him to continue taking the medication. A few informants expressed guilt about low adherence to medication or not performing adequate self-care. One patient admitted lying to healthcare s about adherence to the prescribed medicines to appear as a “well-behaved” patient. A few informants described situations in which they did not agree with the description and conclusions presented by the physician, leading to distrust. For some patients, consultations with multiple physicians offering divergent explanations and advice which contributed to diminished trust in healthcare providers. Dietary restrictions were generally not endorsed by clinicians; consequently, patients did not alter their eating habits and attributed disease exacerbations to the absence of dietary recommendations.

Informants described delayed diagnosis, either due to late contact with primary care, or the lengthy process of repeated contacts with primary care before referral to an IBD clinic which was perceived as unsatisfactory, evoking feelings of not being taken seriously. A few informants felt bitter or suspicious to healthcare in general and primary care in particular. Some patients reported experiencing emotional distress or traumatic reactions related to colonoscopy, medical treatment, and/or surgery. One informant was concerned that the pharmaceutical industry bribed doctors to prescribe other drugs than preferred medication, while another individual described having to fight for a more advanced and expensive medication. Need for Information

Information needs remained unmet regarding treatment, the healthcare system, the diagnosis, and expectations for living with IBD. Several informants described confusion about care organization, including unclear follow-up plans and uncertainty about when to contact the IBD unit. Many sought information elsewhere when healthcare professionals did not provide sufficient explanations. Some wanted individualized information about medication mechanisms and the consequences of non-adherence, while others preferred minimal details as long as treatment worked. A few participants wished they had received earlier information about disease severity, believing it could have improved their illness perception and adherence. Online information-seeking was common and often helpful. One participant found peer experiences more informative than verbal or written material from healthcare providers. Treatment Perceptions

The main finding is patients’ ambivalence towards medication, where many felt divided about the risks and benefits. The concern about side-effects was central and medications were stopped due to lack of necessity, adverse effects and practical aspects with dosage, size and number of tablets. Some considered the lifestyle change approach a priority or at least equally important as medication.

Risk/Benefit Assessment

Several informants discussed weighing the risks of medication against the risk of disease progression reflecting ambivalence. Informants reasoned back and forth: some noted that side effects could cause harm including cancer, while pausing treatment might exacerbate symptoms. Experienced as well as anticipated side effects were key in the perception of medication, and to some previous experience of lack of treatment efficacy lowered their motivation to adhere to prescribed therapeutics.

Some informants considered medication “unnatural”, claiming that it cannot be healthy to be exposed to foreign substances “not belonging in the body”, and that adjustment of lifestyle and diet is a safer remedy as well as alternative. A few informants viewed their medication as “strong”. Several patients perceived that medications could allow a less restrictive lifestyle or reduce sensitivity to stress. Practical Aspects of Medication

Practical aspects that reduced adherence were two or more daily doses, large or many tablets and recommendations about mealtimes in relation to medication intake, in addition to medication being unavailable or hard to get. Some informants described difficulties with administration of suppositories and rectal solutions due to frequent bowel movements, especially in conjunction with night-time and quality of sleep, as well as a fundamental dislike of inserting something into the rectum. Discussion

This study shows that medication adherence in IBD is negotiated within everyday self-care rather than enacted as a discrete treatment behaviour. Participants interpreted the need for medication through bodily monitoring, daily functioning, prior experiences, treatment beliefs and interactions with the IBD care team. The findings therefore extend existing adherence literature by illustrating how practical barriers and perceptual factors are embedded in broader self-care processes. While previous studies have highlighted treatment beliefs, side-effects and healthcare experiences as determinants of adherence, our findings demonstrate how these factors interact in everyday life as patients continuously evaluate symptoms, treatment necessity and personal strategies for disease management. In line with the Perceptions and Practicalities Approach, adherence was shaped both by participants’ beliefs about medication necessity and concerns, and by practical issues such as regimen burden, administration difficulties, access and cost.17,18 The findings are also compatible with the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness, in which medication-taking can be understood as self-care maintenance, symptom interpretation as self-care monitoring, and decisions regarding medication use, healthcare-seeking or lifestyle changes as self-care management.13 Participants described medication use as a continuous balancing act between perceived benefits and concerns. This finding is consistent with previous research and with the Necessity–Concerns Framework, which emphasises that adherence is influenced by patients’ evaluations of treatment necessity relative to concerns about adverse effects.15,17 In our study, concerns were not limited to documented risks but also included uncertainty about future consequences, perceptions of pharmaceuticals as unnatural, and the symbolic burden of being reminded of a chronic illness through daily medication use. Such concerns appeared to contribute to ambivalence, particularly among participants who felt well or had been in remission for prolonged periods.

Previous research has emphasized the importance of trustful communication in IBD care and has shown that access to timely care during disease flares may be more important to patients than continuity alone. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that continuity, trust and respectful dialogue were closely linked to medication-related decision-making. Participants wanted opportunities to discuss concerns regarding treatment, diet, symptom interpretation and lifestyle factors. When such discussions were absent, some questioned treatment recommendations or sought information elsewhere. Therefore, according to our results, it is important that healthcare professionals use a person-centred approach openly discuss patients’ beliefs, experiences and preferences, including those related to diet and lifestyle, as part of shared decision-making. NICE guidelines similarly emphasise that decisions not to follow recommended treatment should be approached with curiosity and respect rather than judgement. Such an approach may strengthen adherence by addressing concerns that otherwise remain unspoken.19

A prominent finding was the importance of acceptance and adaptation to living with IBD. Participants varied in the extent to which they had integrated the disease into their lives, and this appeared to influence both symptom interpretation and treatment decisions. Acceptance may function as an adaptive coping response when individuals acknowledge the realities of a chronic condition while remaining actively engaged in managing their health. In contrast, difficulties accepting the disease may contribute to avoidance, uncertainty and challenges incorporating long-term treatment into everyday routines. Our findings suggest that successful adaptation may facilitate adherence by helping patients view medication as a component of disease management rather than as a symbol of illness and loss of control.

Experiences of illness among family members or acquaintances also appeared to shape participants’ beliefs about health threats, treatment risks and healthcare-seeking behaviour. These observations are consistent with the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, which highlights how illness perceptions and emotional responses influence coping and health-related behaviour. Participants often relied on personal experiences and observations rather than biomedical explanations alone when evaluating treatment decisions. This may partly explain why adherence decisions were sometimes based on perceived wellbeing or daily functioning rather than clinical indicators of disease activity. Such interpretations are important because they may influence whether patients perceive ongoing medication as necessary.15

Several participants expressed the view that disease control could be achieved through lifestyle measures such as dietary modifications, stress reduction or physical activity. Although these strategies were often described as complementary to medical treatment, some participants perceived them as alternatives to medication. Such beliefs may influence adherence when patients consider lifestyle interventions sufficient for disease control. In our study, some participants also described delaying healthcare contact until symptoms had substantially affected their ability to function in everyday life. Together, these findings illustrate how adherence decisions are closely linked to personal understandings of disease causation, symptom interpretation and treatment necessity.

Participants also described difficulties distinguishing disease-related symptoms from non-specific bodily changes and uncertainty regarding when healthcare contact was warranted. These findings underline the importance of clear, individualised information and a shared understanding of treatment goals. Current treat-to-target strategies emphasize mucosal healing as the long-term treatment goal5 which may contrast with patients’ focus on symptom relief, when they are unaware that subclinical inflammation can persist despite the absence of symptoms. This discrepancy may influence medication adherence in two ways: when inflammation persists despite symptom relief, patients may perceive less need for treatment; conversely, when symptom persists in the absence of active inflammation, patients may question the need for and effectiveness of treatment. In both situations, discrepancies between symptoms and objective disease activity may influence adherence, potentially increase the risk of disease progression. Knowledge and health literacy have previously been identified as important contributors to adherence and self-management in chronic illness. Supporting patients in understanding the rationale for treatment, expected disease trajectories and signs of disease activity may therefore strengthen both self-care and adherence.15 In line with previous research participants in the present study described a need for information and opportunities to discuss treatment concerns, and disease related experiences. However, our findings further suggest that access to information alone may not be sufficient; how information is discussed and integrated with patients’ own experiences, beliefs and everyday circumstances may also shape medication-related decision.

Self-efficacy did not emerge as a distinct analytical category and should therefore not be interpreted as a central finding. Nevertheless, participants’ accounts contained elements related to confidence, agency and decision-making, particularly when discussing symptom management and treatment choices. Similarly, quality of care was not evaluated as a specific outcome in this study. Rather, participants’ descriptions of continuity, respectful communication and access to information suggest that healthcare experiences shape the conditions under which adherence-related self-care takes place. The findings should therefore be interpreted as illustrating how healthcare relationships influence adherence and self-care, rather than as evidence regarding self-efficacy or quality of care as separate constructs.

The findings may be relevant to healthcare settings beyond Sweden. Further qualitative studies are needed to explore if and how these findings apply in other healthcare settings. We also suggest that future studies involve patients in the development of individualized support programs that facilitates medication adherence and strengthen self-efficacy.

Methodological Considerations

The first author is a registered nurse specialised in IBD at the outpatient clinic where the study was performed. The informants have not had any recent healthcare contact with the first author. To diminish the power imbalance between researcher and participant, the interviewer wore private clothes and carefully thought about how the interviewer and the participant were seated. All sessions started by explaining the purpose of the study and that no answer is considered right or wrong.

The clinical experience and understanding of this group of patients may have impacted on the interpretation of the study results. Furthermore, gender may have influenced the analysis of the data, as the first author might relate more strongly to female perspectives. To minimize potential bias, the co-authors triangulated the findings and reached consensus on both the data collection procedures and the entire analytic process. The results are strengthened by the inclusion of relevant quotations to illustrate the different categories. Data from male informants was double-checked to ensure correct interpretation. We believe these measures improve the credibility of the study. The inclusion of informants aimed at achieving maximum variation of gender, disease duration, and age as well as obtaining rich data. However, the balance between ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease is skewed in favour of the former, which could affect the findings.

Conclusion

Medication adherence in IBD was experienced as a negotiated part of everyday self-care, shaped by patients’ symptom interpretation, daily functioning, acceptance of chronic illness, treatment beliefs, practical medication burden and communication with the IBD care team. Non-adherence may therefore reflect not only practical barriers or forgetfulness, but also deliberate evaluations of medication necessity, perceived risks and alternative strategies for managing the disease. Supporting adherence requires person-centred, non-judgemental dialogue that acknowledges patients’ concerns including those related to diet and lifestyle choices, clarifies treatment goals, and integrates medication-taking into individual self-care routines. Clear communication about the difference between symptomatic relief and mucosal healing may further support informed treatment decisions.
