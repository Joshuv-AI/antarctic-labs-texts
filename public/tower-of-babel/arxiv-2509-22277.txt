Online Firefighting on Cactus Graphs
Max Hugen, Bob Krekelberg, Alison Hsiang-Hsuan Liu

Abstract
The firefighting game is an important problem in theoretical computer science. It has been broadly studied in the offline model, including results on computational complexity for various graph classes. In this work, we investigate the online setting and the competitive complexity , which has been relatively less explored. In the online setting, the graph is given in advance, but the sequence of available firefighters is revealed in an online manner. The online algorithm aims to save as many vertices as possible by deciding in each round which vertices to protect, without knowing in advance when and how many firefighters will become available in the future. It is known that the online firefighting is 2 2 -competitive on trees [ 2 ] , which suggests that the problem is relatively easy on trees. We extend the study to graphs containing cycles. We first show that the presence of cycles gives a strong advantage to the adversary: cycles create situations where the algorithm and the optimal solution operate on different game states, and the adversary can exploit the uncertainty in the firefighter sequence to trap the algorithm. Specifically, we prove that even on a tadpole graph (a cycle with a tail path), no deterministic online algorithm achieves a competitive ratio better than Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) , where n n is the number of vertices. We then propose an O ⁡ ( n ) O(\sqrt{n}) -competitive algorithm for 1 1 -almost trees, which contain at most one cycle and generalize tadpole graphs. We further generalize this algorithm to cactus graphs, in which multiple cycles may appear, but no two share more than one vertex, and show that the online firefighting problem on cactus graphs remains O ⁡ ( n ) O(\sqrt{n}) -competitive. Finally, since cactus graphs have treewidth at most 2 2 , we study a variant where firefighters are released in pairs, that is, each round an even number of firefighters is available. Surprisingly, in this setting, the competitive complexity is significantly reduced, and we prove that the problem is at most 3 3 -competitive. The main technical challenges lie in both algorithm design and analysis, since the algorithm and the optimal solution may break different cycles and thus operate on different residual graphs. To overcome this difficulty, we design a charging framework that carefully partitions the vertices saved by the optimal solution and charges them to the vertices saved by the algorithm. Specifically, the charging scheme is carefully constructed to ensure that each vertex saved by the algorithm is charged at most a constant number of times.

Introduction

The
online firefighting game
proceeds in rounds as follows.
Initially, the algorithm is given a graph with a designated fire source.
At the beginning of each round, the algorithm is informed of the number of firefighters available in that round and assigns them to protect unburned vertices.
Subsequently, every unprotected neighbor of a burning vertex catches fire, concluding the round.
The online algorithm aims to maximize the number of saved vertices, without knowing in advance how many firefighters will be available in future rounds.

We evaluate the performance of an online algorithm for the firefighting game using
competitive analysis
.
An algorithm
ALG
is
c
-competitive if
\sup_{\mathcal{I}}\frac{\text{OPT}(\mathcal{I})}{\text{ALG}(\mathcal{I})}\leq c
, where
\text{ALG}(\mathcal{I})
denotes the number of vertices saved by
ALG
on instance
\mathcal{I}
, and
\text{OPT}(\mathcal{I})
denotes the maximum number of vertices that can be saved on
\mathcal{I}
.
From the complexity perspective, the competitive ratio of an online problem is defined as the infimum competitive ratio achievable by any online algorithm for the problem.

Introduced by Hartnell in 1995
[
5
]
, the firefighter problem can be seen more generally as a model for a threat spreading through a network.
For example, the problems of minimizing the spread of misinformation or threats in social or computer networks, immunizing a population against a virus, and reducing the spread of an invasive species in an ecosystem are also captured by this model.

The firefighting problem is known to be a computationally hard problem.
Even for restricted graph classes such as bipartite graphs
[
7
]
and even for trees of maximum degree
3

[
4
]
, the problem is shown to be NP-hard.

On the positive side, several approximation algorithms are known for the firefighting problem.
For example, the greedy algorithm is shown to be a
\frac{1}{2}
-approximation on trees.
[
6
]
,
and also, there also exists a polynomial time approximation scheme for the firefighting problem on trees.
[
1
]
.

The online variant of the firefighting problem was introduced by Coupechoux et al. in 2019
[
2
]
.
They showed that the greedy algorithm on trees in the online version of the problem is
2
-competitive and optimal.
Also, in the fractional version of the problem, in which a fraction of a firefighter can be assigned to a vertex to partially protect it, Coupechoux et al.
[
2
]
show that the greedy algorithm is
2
-competitive on trees.
Furthermore, in the special case where the number of firefighters is bounded by
2
, Coupechoux et al.
[
2
]
propose a
\phi
-competitive algorithm on trees, where
\phi
is the golden ratio.
Later, Demange et al.
[
3
]
investigated sufficient conditions for online containment of fires on infinite grid graphs.
They show that for an algorithm to contain the fire, there needs to exist a turn
t
such that the sum of firefighters released until (including) turn
t
is at least
16t
.

In this work, we study the competitive complexity of the online firefighting game.
It is known that the game is
2
-competitive on trees, indicating that the problem is relatively simple in this setting.
This raises the question of how the competitive complexity evolves on broader classes of graphs.
Surprisingly, we find that with the addition of a single extra edge to a tree, the problem is no longer constant-competitive.

Theorem 1
.

No deterministic online algorithm can achieve
o(\sqrt{n})
-competitiveness for the online firefighting problem on tadpole graphs.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary deterministic online algorithm
ALG
.
Let the input graph
G
be an
(\alpha+1,\beta+1)
-tadpole graph, consisting of a cycle of
\alpha+1
vertices and a path of
\beta+1
vertices joined at a common vertex
r
, where
r
is the unique degree-
3
vertex of
G
.

In the first round, the adversary releases one firefighter.
If
ALG
protects any vertex on the cycle, the adversary stops releasing more firefighters.
In this case,
OPT
protects the neighbor of
r
on the path and saves
\beta
vertices of the path (where
r
is burned), whereas
ALG
saves only the single protected vertex.

If
ALG
protects a vertex on the path in the first round, then the adversary releases one more firefighter in the next round and then stops.
In this case,
ALG
can save at most
1+\beta
vertices (the initial protection and the path).
Meanwhile,
OPT
can protect two vertices on the cycle: one neighbor of
r
in the first round, and the vertex adjacent to the other neighbor of
r
on the cycle in the second round.
Thus,
OPT
saves at least
\alpha-1
vertices on the cycle.

Therefore, the competitive ratio is at least
\frac{\text{OPT}((G,r,(f)_{i\geq 1}))}{\text{ALG}((G,r,(f)_{i\geq 1}))}\geq\min\{\frac{\beta}{1},\frac{\alpha-1}{1+\beta}\}
.
By setting
\alpha=\beta^{2}+1
, we obtain
\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta+1}\approx\beta
.
Since the number of vertices in
G
,
n=\alpha+\beta+1=\Theta(\beta^{2})
, the competitive ratio of
ALG
is at least
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.
∎

We then design an
O(\sqrt{n})
-competitive algorithm for
1
-almost trees, that is, graphs formed by adding at most one extra edge to a tree.
Equivalently, a
1
-almost tree contains at most one cycle, thereby generalizing tadpole graphs.
More specifically:

Theorem 2
.

There is a
(6\sqrt{n}+1)
-competitive algorithm
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
for the online firefighting game on
1
-almost-trees, which is asymptotically optimal.

By further generalizing our algorithm to cactus graphs with multiple cycles, we find that the number of cycles does not increase the difficulty of the problem from the perspective of competitive complexity.
Specifically:

Theorem 3
.

There is a
(15\sqrt{n}+1)
-competitive algorithm
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
for the online firefighting game on cactus graphs, which is asymptotically optimal.

Since cactus graphs have treewidth at most two, any cactus graph admits a tree decomposition in which each bag has size at most three.
A natural question then arises: what happens if firefighters are released in a multiple of the treewidth?
Surprisingly, we find that the special instance on cactus graphs, where an even number of firefighters is available in each round, significantly reduces the competitive complexity of the firefighting problem:

Theorem 4
.

There is a
3
-competitive algorithm
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{E}}
for the online firefighting game on cactus graphs with even firefighters in each round.

A natural approach is to adapt the greedy algorithm for trees in
[
2
]
.
Although the greedy algorithm and the optimal solution may protect different vertices in each round, the tree structure guarantees that there is a unique path from the fire source to each vertex.
Thus, any vertex available to the optimal solution is also available to the algorithm, unless it has already been saved by the algorithm.
The analysis then charges the vertices saved exclusively by the optimal solution to those saved by the algorithm, according to the greedy nature of the algorithm.
Additionally, the number of vertices protected by both the optimal solution and the algorithm is at most the number of vertices saved by the algorithm, which implies that the optimal solution saves at most twice as many vertices as the algorithm.

However, the availability guarantee no longer holds when the graph contains a cycle.
In this case, the shortest distance between a vertex and the fire source (i.e., the earliest time the vertex can be burned) can be increased by “breaking” a cycle that overlaps the shortest path from the vertex to the fire source.
Consequently, if the online algorithm breaks the cycle differently from the optimal solution, the two strategies result in different residual graphs, which invalidates the charging framework.

To tackle this issue, we design an algorithm for
1
-almost trees that only follows the greedy choice when the immediate gain (i.e., the number of vertices saved by this choice) is significant.
Otherwise, the algorithm instead breaks a cycle in such a way that it delays the earliest time at which a significant number of vertices would be burned, in the hope of securing a future gain.

In cactus graphs, multiple cycles may be present.
This introduces further misalignments between the algorithm’s strategy and that of the optimal solution: not only in how to break a cycle, but also in which cycle to break.
We address this issue with a “cool-down timer” mechanism that balances cycle-breaking and greedy choices.
Specifically, when the greedy choice does not yield a significant immediate gain, the algorithm breaks a cycle.
Depending on how long this break delays the burning of the remaining subgraph, the algorithm switches to the greedy choice whenever a firefighter becomes available during this period.
Once a greedy choice is made or the timer expires, the algorithm is again free to break a cycle.

The misalignment between the algorithm’s and the optimal solution’s residual graphs also makes the analysis challenging.
We design a charging framework, which first partitions the vertices saved by the optimal solution into independent parts, and then charges each part to a subset of vertices saved by the algorithm.
The number of parts may grow depending on the configuration of the optimal solution.
Thus, the challenge is to partition the vertices saved by the optimal solution appropriately (based on different graph classes and algorithms) and to select the subsets of vertices saved by the algorithm such that no vertex saved by the algorithm is charged more than a constant number of times.

In Section
2
, we introduce the critical components of our algorithm design, together with the analysis framework and the key properties that ensure its feasibility.
In Section
3
, we demonstrate how to apply our design and analysis framework to
1
-almost trees and prove Theorem
2
.
Section
4
further extends the framework to cactus graphs, establishing Theorem
3
.
In Section
5
, we adapt the framework to analyze a special case on cactus graphs where firefighters arrive in pairs, and show constant competitiveness.
Owing to space constraints, most proofs are deferred to the full version in the appendix.

The graph

Moreover, a cycle is a

In the online firefighting game, the instance is

We denote

Let the

In each round

In this work, we consider the cases where

At any time in the firefighting game, a vertex can be

,

A vertex is

A vertex

Conversely, a vertex is

r

In the middle of the game, a vertex may not be burned yet, but there are some paths between it and the fire source

The algorithm’s goal is equivalent to saving as many vertices as possible.

A vertex

to

Conversely, all the vertices covered by

of

.
More generally, a vertex

is covered by a set of vertices

if every path from

to

, is defined analogously.

2

2

Naturally, we only consider

.

Intuitively, the covered set of a set of vertices

can be seen as the set of vertices that are saved by protecting all vertices in

Equivalently, it is the set of vertices that are saved by placing firefighters on each vertex in
S

Note that by this definition,

can be larger than
\bigcup_{v\in S}\kappa(v)

The

Let
\Psi^{\text{A}}
denote the set of all vertices protected by our algorithm

By definition,

, and

Similarly,
\Psi^{*}

Notions for the algorithm

We follow the reduction procedure on online firefighting on trees by Coupechoux et al.
[
2
]
.
At the beginning of a round, the algorithm sees the graph
G
with a fire source
r
.
At the end of the round, after the algorithm’s decisions of protecting a set of available vertices
P
, and the fire spreading to all unprotected neighbors of
r
, all burning vertices are contracted with
r
into a new fire source
r^{\prime}
.
Moreover, all vertices in
\kappa(P)
are removed from the graph.
The new graph
G^{\prime}
with the new fire source
r^{\prime}
is the input to the algorithm in the next round.

By this reduction procedure, the algorithm always sees exactly one burning vertex, which is the fire source, and all other vertices are available for being protected.

In our work, we investigate graphs that are
1
-almost-trees or cactus graphs.
With a slight abuse of the definition of
1
-almost-trees, which is now defined as a graph with
at most
one cycle, the graph classes
1
-almost-trees and cactus graphs are closed under vertex extraction.
Therefore, we can focus on the algorithm in one round from now on, and the algorithms and analysis apply to the whole game.

Protecting a cycle vertex “breaks” the cycle, transforming it into tree branches in the reduced graph.
Given a root cycle
\widetilde{C}
and a vertex
v
on it, we denote
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash v)
as the subgraph induced by
\{r\}\cup\kappa(\widetilde{C})
with
v
being protected and
\kappa(v)
being removed.
Formally,
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash v)=G[\{r\}\cup\kappa(\widetilde{C})\setminus\kappa(v)]
.
We extend this notation to
T_{e}(\widetilde{C}\backslash(u,v))
, where
(u,v)
is an edge of the root cycle
\widetilde{C}
, to denote the subgraph induced by
\{r\}\cup\kappa(\widetilde{C})
with the edge
(u,v)
removed.

Let
\texttt{dist}(G,u,v)
denote the shortest distance between vertices
u
and
v
in graph
G
.
Our algorithm heavily relies on two measurements: the number vertices in
G
that are
temporally safe
with respect to
d

count(G,d)
, and the
tolerance
of a cycle vertex
u
in a root cycle
C
with respect to a natural number
n
,
\texttt{tol}(u,C,n)
.
Formally,
count(G,d)
is the number of vertices in
G
that have the shortest distance to
r
of at least
d
.
That is,
count(G,d)=|\{v\in V\mid\texttt{dist}(G,r,v)\geq d\}|
.
Note that by the definition,

is a monotone function.
More specifically,
count(G,d_{2})\leq count(G,d_{1})
for any
0\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
for all cactus graph
G
.
The notion of tolerance
\texttt{tol}(u,C,m)
is defined as the largest integer
d
such that, in the induced subgraph
T(C\backslash u)
, there are at least
m
vertices whose distance from the root is at least
d
.
Formally,
\texttt{tol}(u,C,m)=\max\{d\in\mathbb{N}\mid count(T(C\backslash u),d)\geq m\}
.
Intuitively, the tolerance
\texttt{tol}(u,C,m)
measures how far the fire can spread in the subgraph
T(C\backslash u)
while still leaving at least
m
vertices temporally safe.
Given an edge between two cycle vertices
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
, we also extend the notion to
\texttt{tol}_{\texttt{e}}((u_{i},u_{j}),C,m)=\max\{d\in\mathbb{N}\mid count(T_{e}(C\backslash(u_{i},u_{j})),d)\geq m\}
.

Notion of analysis

We first introduce critical components of our analysis.
First, we narrow down the optimal solutions we are comparing against to a structured set,
non-redundant
optimal solutions:

Lemma 5
.

There exists a non-redundant optimal solution, where every cycle contains at most two protected vertices.

Proof.

Consider an optimal solution
OPT
where there is a cycle
C
with three distinct protected vertices on
C
.
Let
u_{0}
be the vertex on
C
that has the smallest shortest distance to
r
.
That is,
u_{0}=\arg_{u\in C}\min\{\texttt{dist}(G,r,u)\}
.
Label the vertices on

in cyclic order as

.
Note that an algorithm can only protect an available vertex.
Assume that

,

, and

We can remove the protection on

\Psi^{\text{A}}=(\psi^{\texttt{A}}_{1},\psi^{\texttt{A}}_{2},\cdots)

3.1

: The algorithm for

Recall that by the reduction procedure, we focus on a round

\widetilde{G}
with a fire resource
\tilde{r}
and
f_{i}

In round

\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
iteratively assigns the firefighters to one of the vertices in

until there is no firefighters left.
In each iteration, if
\tilde{r}
is not involved in any cycle (that is, there is no root cycle),
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
protects the heaviest neighbor of
\tilde{r}
.
When
\widetilde{G}
has a root cycle,
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
makes decisions between protecting the heaviest neighbor of
\tilde{r}
, protecting the root cycle with two firefighters, or break the root cycle.
At the end of one iteration, the protected vertices
v
and
\kappa(v)
are removed from
\widetilde{G}

The algorithm
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
makes decisions greedily if there is no root cycle in the input graph
\widetilde{G}
.
That is, it assigns the firefighters to the first
f_{i}
“heaviest” neighbors of
\tilde{r}
that have the largest weights.
Formally, the firefighters are iteratively assigned to unprotected

with the highest

In the case where the input graph
\widetilde{G}

\widetilde{C}

w(v_{1})\geq w(v_{2})\geq\cdots
.
When there are at least two firefighters available, we protect the cycle using two firefighters if
w(\widetilde{C})>w(v_{1})+w(v_{2})
.
Otherwise, we protect
v_{1}
using one firefighter, and the procedure iteratively continues (with
v_{1}
removed from the candidates of vertices that need to be protected).

When there is a root cycle
\widetilde{C}
in the input graph
\widetilde{G}
and there is only one firefighter, the algorithm protects

if
w(v_{1})\geq\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.
Otherwise, when the immediate gain from protecting

is not significant enough, the algorithm “breaks” the cycle by protecting the vertex

with the larger
\texttt{tol}(u,\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})})
(tolerance with respect to
\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}

Recall that the tolerance

Intuitively, the “cycle-break” procedure protects the cycle vertex that maximizes how far the fire can spread while still leaving at least

Structural properties of

We first show that for

Lemma 9

(The advantage of breaking cycle on
N(\tilde{r})\cap C
)
.

In a
1
-almost tree
\widetilde{G}
with a fire source
\tilde{r}
and a root cycle
\widetilde{C}=(r,u_{1},\cdots,u_{p})
, for every
u_{h}
, one of the following holds for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
:
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)+w(u_{1})
or
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{p}),d)+w(u_{p})
.

count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)+w(u_{1})\text{, or}

count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{p}),d)+w(u_{p}).

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary
u_{h}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Without loss of generality, assume that the shortest path from
u_{h}
to
r
passes through
u_{1}
.

Consider an arbitrary vertex
v\notin\kappa(u_{1})
that is counted in

count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)
.
By the fact that
v
is counted in
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)
, the shortest distance from
v
to
r
in
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h})
,
\texttt{dist}(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),v,r)\geq d
.
Since
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h})
is a tree, let
P_{vr}
be the unique path from
v
to
r
.
Similarly, let
P^{\prime}_{vr}
be the unique path from
v
to
r
in
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1})
.
Note that
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1})
is a path from
r
to
u_{2}
union all
\kappa(u_{k})
for
k=2,3,\cdots,p
.

Since
P_{vr}
contains
u_{1}
, then let
u_{a}
be the first cycle vertex on
P_{vr}
(which goes from
v
to
r
).
The arc
(u_{a},u_{a+1},\cdots,u_{h},u_{h+1},\cdots,u_{p})
is a subset of
P^{\prime}_{vr}
, and thus
\texttt{dist}(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),v)\geq\texttt{dist}(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}))\geq d
.
Therefore,
v
is also counted in
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)
.

Finally, the vertices in
\kappa(u_{1})
might be counted in

but never counted in

T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1})
is the subgraph induced by the vertices in

.
Therefore, it is proven that
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)+w(u_{1})
.

The same argument applies when the shortest path from
u_{h}
to

passes through

.
∎

Next, we bound the performance of breaking the cycle on the vertex
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
picks in terms of how many vertices can have a safe distance from the fire source due to the breaking.

Lemma 10

(Quality of
Break-cycle
)
.

Let
\widetilde{G}
be the input graph with a root cycle
\widetilde{C}=(r,u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{p})
.
Let
\hat{u}
be the vertex protected by the
Break-cycle
procedure.
For every vertex
u_{h}
and every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\frac{count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)}{count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash\hat{u}),d)+1}\leq 2\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{C}=(r,u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{p})
be the vertices on
\widetilde{C}
with
u_{1}=\hat{u}
.
Denote the tolerance of
\hat{u}
by
d_{\max}=\texttt{tol}(\hat{u},\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})})
.
By the definition of tolerance,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash\hat{u}),d_{\max})\geq\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.

By the monotonicity of the
count
function,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash\hat{u}),d)\geq count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash\hat{u}),d_{\max})\geq\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.
On the other hand, for any
u_{h}
,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)
can include at most every vertex in
\kappa(C)
.
Thus,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq w(\widetilde{C})
.
Therefore,
\frac{count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)}{count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)+1}\leq 2\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.

Since
d_{\max}
is the tolerance
\texttt{tol}(\hat{u},\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})})
,
d_{\max}
is the maximum distance the fire can spread
\tilde{r}
in
T(\widetilde{C}\backslash\hat{u})
such that there are still at least
\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
vertices unburned.
Thus, for any

,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)<\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.
Moreover, since
\hat{u}=u_{1}
,
\texttt{tol}(u_{1},\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})})\geq\texttt{tol}(u_{p},\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})})
, and
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{p}),d)<\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.
Additionally, by
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{A}}
and Observation
8
(
3
), the procedure

only happens when

for all vertices
v\in V
.
Therefore, by Lemma

,
count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{h}),d)\leq\max\{count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{1}),d)+w(u_{1}),count(T(\widetilde{C}\backslash u_{p}),d)+w(u_{p})\}<\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}+\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}=2\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C})}
.
∎

4
Cactus graphs

4.1
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
: Algorithm for cactus graphs

Similar to the previous case,
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
proceeds in rounds, each consisting of several iterations.
In each iteration, if there are enough firefighters to protect the heaviest component adjacent to the fire source,
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
makes the greedy choice.
Moreover, if only one firefighter is available but the greedy choice guarantees a gain of at least
\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C}_{1})}
, where
\widetilde{C}_{1}
is the heaviest root cycle in the current graph, the algorithm still follows the greedy choice.

The critical case occurs when only one firefighter is available and the greedy choice does not guarantee a sufficiently large immediate gain.

In this situation, as in the strategy for
1
-almost trees,
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
breaks a root cycle
C
by protecting a vertex
u
on the cycle that maximizes
\texttt{tol}(u,\widetilde{C},\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C}_{1})})
, thereby ensuring that at least
w(\widetilde{C}_{1})
vertices lie as far from the fire as possible (in terms of distance from
r
).
Unlike in
1
-almost trees, however, a cactus graph may contain multiple root cycles.
A difficulty arises because if the algorithm repeatedly breaks cycles due to the lack of immediate gain, it may fail to secure many vertices that could otherwise be saved.
To address this, we introduce a
cool-down timer
mechanism: once a cycle is broken, the algorithm enters a cool-down period during which it must follow the greedy choice whenever new firefighters become available.
The rule for selecting a vertex to break a cycle is thus adapted to this mechanism.
The detailed description is in Algorithm
2
.

We first find the cycle vertex adjacent to the fire source
r
that has the largest tolerance in the sense that if the edge between it and
r
is removed, the distance that the fire can spread while keeping
\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C}_{1})}
vertices remaining unburned is maximized.
Let
\widetilde{C}_{*}
be the cycle containing this vertex
u_{*}
with the largest tolerance
d_{\max}
.
Now the
ImprovedBreak
tries to find an alternative cycle vertex on
\widetilde{C}_{*}
while keeping the invariant “
at least
\sqrt{w(\widetilde{C}_{1})}-w(u)
vertices in

This alternative chooses a break-cycle vertex farther from the fire source, while exposing only a limited number of additional vertices to the risk of being burned compared to breaking at a neighbor of
r
.
We show in Lemma

that our cycle-breaking decision according to
ImprovedBreak

\hat{u}
on cycle
\widetilde{C}_{*}
, saves at least a
\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}
fraction of the vertices that would be saved by breaking at any other cycle vertex on any cycle.
The setting of the cool-down timer ensures that whenever a firefighter is released before the timer expires, the greedy choice protects at least as many vertices as the remaining available ones on the path that defines the timer.

(Protection quality in cool-down period)

4.2
Competitive analysis of
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}

For analyzing
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
on cactus graphs, we first introduce a concept of
delay
.
A vertex
v
is
delayed
by another vertex
p
if when
p
is protected,
v
is not covered, but the time that fire reaches
v
is strictly delayed.
In other words, a delayer
p
of
v
is on the shortest path from
r
to
v
, but not on every path from
r
to
v
.
That is,
p
is
v
’s

if
p
is on the shortest path from
r
to
v
in
G
and
v\notin\kappa(p)
.
We general this concept and say that a vertex
v
is delayed by a set of vertices
S
if
S
contains at least one delayer of
v
and
S
does not cover
v
(that is,
v\notin\kappa(S))
.
Moreover, given a vertex
p
, we define its
delayed set

\texttt{D}(p)
by the set of all vertices that are delayed by
p
.
For a set
S
of vertices, its delayed set
\texttt{D}(S)
is defined by
\bigcup_{p\in S}\texttt{D}(p)
.
Given
\psi^{*}\in\Psi^{*}
, we denote
\texttt{D}_{\Psi^{*}}(\psi^{*})
as
\texttt{D}(\psi^{*})\cap\Psi^{*}
.
That is,
\texttt{D}_{\Psi^{*}}(\psi^{*})
is the set of vertices protected by
OPT
and delayed by
\psi^{*}
.

Recall that in algorithm
\text{ALG}_{\texttt{C}}
, at most three vertices can be protected on the same cycle.
We call such cycle vertices
peers
.
In what follows, we may refer to a cycle vertex and its peers.

Partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
of
\Psi^{*}

Given the set
\Psi^{*}
of vertices protected by
OPT
, which by Lemma
5
is non-redundant.
We partition
\Psi^{*}
into the three

of parts, where there can be multiple parts of Type
b
or

.

[Type a:]

1.

This type has at most one part
P^{(a)}
, which consists of all non-cycle vertices in
\Psi^{*}
that are not in
\texttt{D}(\Psi^{*})
.

2.

For each cycle vertex
\psi^{*}_{t}\in\Psi^{*}
that is the only vertex in the cycle protected by
\Psi^{*}
,
\psi^{*}_{t}
and the set of vertices protected by
\Psi^{*}
and delayed by
\psi^{*}_{t}
form a part.
That is, given such a vertex
\psi^{*}_{t}
, they form a Type-
b

P^{(b)}_{t}=\{\psi^{*}_{t}\}\cup\texttt{D}_{\Psi^{*}}(\psi^{*}_{t})
.
Note that
P^{(b)}_{t}
might be empty for some

.
We call

the
representative vertex
of
P^{(b)}_{t}
.

3.

For each cycle

that contains two vertices
\psi^{*}_{t}
and
\psi^{*}_{s}
, and at least one of
\psi^{*}_{t}
and
\psi^{*}_{s}
is not delayed by
\Psi^{*}
, these two vertices and their delayed sets form a Type-

.
That is,
P^{(c)}_{t,s}=\{\psi^{*}_{t},\psi^{*}_{s}\}\cup\texttt{D}_{\Psi^{*}}(\psi^{*}_{t})\cup\texttt{D}_{\Psi^{*}}(\psi^{*}_{s})
.
For some pair of
t
and

might be empty.
We call

and

of
P^{(c)}_{t,s}
.

Lemma 19
.

The partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is a proper, cycle-respecting partition of
\Psi^{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, a non-redundant optimal solution on a cactus graph protects at most two vertices on the same cycle.
According to the definition of Type-
c
parts,
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is cycle-respecting.

By the definition of delayers that cannot cover the delayed vertex, in a cactus graph, a vertex cannot be delayed by a non-cycle vertex.
Moreover, every delayed vertex is delayed by exactly one non-delayed vertex
\psi^{*}_{p}\in\Psi^{*}
(since we define the delayers as strictly delaying the time the fire will reach), and there is no other vertex protected by
OPT
in the cycle that contains
\psi^{*}_{p}
.
Thus, any delayed non-cycle vertex is “attached” to its delayer, which is a single cycle vertex on a cycle, and belongs to a Type-
b
part.
Furthermore, consider any pair of cycle vertices on the same cycle that are both protected by
OPT
and delayed by another cycle vertex in
\Psi^{*}
.
These two vertices will also belong to the Type-
b
part that corresponds to their delayer.
Therefore, every vertex in
\Psi^{*}
is captured by exactly one part.
∎

\chi

\mathbf{b}
or Type-
\mathbf{c}
