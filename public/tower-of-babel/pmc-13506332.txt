Tissue-resident immune cells drive genetic risk in autoimmune and lung diseases
Authors: Benjamin J Schmiedel, Cristian Gonzalez-Colin, Vicente Fajardo-Rosas, Job Rocha, Hayley Simon, Johannes Ottensmeier, Ignacio E Ramírez-Bernabé, April Cano, Angel De la Cruz Castillo, Elizabeth Márquez-Gómez, Brendan Ha, Jason A Greenbaum, Lindsey Chudley, Judith Cave, Aiman Alzetani, Edwin Woo, Michael Shackcloth, Serena J Chee, Vivek Chandra, Mitchell Kronenberg, Bjoern Peters, Christian H Ottensmeier, Anusha-Preethi Ganesan, Pandurangan Vijayanand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommon genetic variants are associated with risk of lung and autoimmune diseases. However, we have a limited understanding of the pathological effects of these disease variants, specifically in tissue-resident immune cells at the frontier of infection and disease in the lung. To address this, we performed single-cell expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analysis across 29 distinct immune cell subsets isolated from lung tissue. Colocalization analysis of lung immune cell eQTLs with genome-wide association study (GWAS) signals from lung diseases, and infectious and autoimmune diseases, implicate disease-associated variants and their target genes functioning in a single or restricted group of cell types. Several genes, including ZFP57, showed significant colocalization between lung immune cell eQTLs and GWAS signals from multiple systemic and organ-restricted autoimmune diseases, highlighting shared genetic mechanisms underlying the risk of disease. Overall, our study shows that disease-associated variants from a wide range of autoimmune diseases impact gene expression in tissue-resident immune cell types (https://lung.dice-database.org).Subject terms: Immunogenetics, Autoimmunity, Immunological disorders

Main

Genetic factors play a key role in determining the risk of infectious and immune-mediated diseases, and can be leveraged to identify pathological mechanisms and new targets for diagnostics, prognostics and therapies1,2. The genetic risk linked to diseases can be captured in an unbiased way by genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which have identified genetic variants accounting for a significant fraction of the heritable risk associated with a wide range of immune-mediated diseases or traits3. However, connecting these disease-risk variants to functional outcomes, and thereby identifying disease mechanisms and therapeutic targets, has been challenging. This is mainly due to the lack of studies in the disease-relevant cell types mediating the pathological effects of the variants. As the effects of genetic variants on gene expression can be highly cell type-specific4, it is important to survey a broad set of disease-relevant cell types.

Recent single-cell profiling studies of human tissues affected by infectious and immune-mediated diseases, along with functional studies in animal models, have implicated various tissue-resident immune cell types such as tissue-resident-memory T cells (TRM cells), B resident-memory cells (BRM cells), tissue-resident natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages as key drivers of barrier immunity and tissue pathology5. However, due to the ease of sampling blood, most expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies to determine how risk variants influence gene expression in specific cell types have focused on analyzing immune cell types in the blood, overlooking those that are primarily resident in the tissues. Meanwhile, studies that analyzed diverse, unseparated cell types in whole tissues lack the resolution needed to identify effects of disease-risk variants that are specific to tissue immune cell types6. To address these issues, eQTL studies in purified populations of immune cells isolated from tissues of interest are needed.

The human lung represents an ideal tissue source for studying effects of disease-risk variants in immune cells for several reasons: (1) a sufficient amount of unaffected tissue can be obtained from living individuals undergoing surgery for early stage cancer; (2) lung immunity plays a crucial role in respiratory infections; (3) the lung is the primary target tissue for many immune-mediated diseases such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and sarcoidosis; (4) the lungs can be affected in some patients with multi-organ autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, lupus erythematosus and scleroderma; and (5) tissue-resident immune cells across different organs share a core cis-regulatory landscape and transcriptional features7, such that effects observed in one organ can be extrapolated to others.

Results

Single-cell atlas of lung immune cell types

Although several single-cell lung atlases have been reported (Supplementary Table 1), limitations due to the number of cells and individuals studied preclude high-resolution eQTL analysis of various lung immune cell types. Therefore, we first generated a large-scale single-cell transcriptomic dataset of 1,150,336 immune cells isolated from surgically resected lung tissue samples of 120 living individuals (mean of 9,255 cells per individual; Fig. 1a–d). We ensured representation of different immune cell types from each individual by sorting sufficient numbers of the five major immune cell populations in the lung tissue (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Unbiased clustering analyses of each cell type revealed 29 transcriptionally distinct immune cell subsets (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4), which showed substantial variations in the relative proportions across individuals (Fig. 1c).

Given that biological sex is an independent risk factor for immune-mediated diseases as well as severity of infectious diseases8,9, we explored its impact on gene expression in specific subsets of lung immune cells. Over 1,700 genes showed significant sex-biased changes in their expression levels, with many genes showing prominent changes in specific cell types (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Table 5b). Importantly, most of the sex-biased transcripts in tissue-resident immune cell subsets did not show significant differences in circulating immune cells isolated from blood (Extended Data Fig. 4c). The sex-biased transcripts were enriched in several functionally important pathways (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Notable examples of transcripts showing increased expression in male individuals include TGFB1 transcripts in CD8+ MAIT cells and TNF transcripts in several lung immune subsets. Furthermore, ENTPD1, which encodes CD39, showed prominent male-biased expression in regulatory T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4d,e). Transcripts that showed female-biased expression include those encoding amphiregulin, a growth factor promoting the restoration of tissue integrity after damage associated with inflammation10, and chemokines XCL1 and XCL2 which play an important role in the recruitment of conventional type 1 dendritic cell (cDC1)11. Furthermore, transcripts coding for the immunomodulatory surface receptors CD226 (DNAM-1), TIGIT and CD96 showed female-biased expression in several T cell subsets. These findings suggested that sex-biased changes in gene expression are likely to modulate key functions of specific tissue-resident immune cell types and influence host responses in infection and autoimmunity.

eQTLs in tissue-resident immune cells

To assess the relative importance of tissue-resident immune cells in contributing to the genetic risk of disease, we generated transposase accessibility profiles for the five major lung immune cell types and examined the enrichment of disease-risk variants in accessible regions (Extended Data Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 6). Our enrichment analysis also included published assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC–seq) datasets12–14 from lung immune and structural cell types to assess reproducibility and compare their contributions. We observed that disease-risk variants associated with lung diseases, such as asthma and COPD, and systemic immune-mediated diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, were more enriched in ATAC–seq peaks in many of the five major lung immune cell types compared to lung structural cell types and neural cells (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 6f,g). This finding suggests that disease-risk variants perturb the function of cis-regulatory elements in specific lung immune cell subsets, thereby influencing their gene expression and cellular functions, potentially contributing to disease.

To directly explore the association of disease-risk variants with gene expression in tissue-resident immune cell subsets, we first performed eQTL analysis by independently testing the association of 6,879,375 common genetic variants with gene expression in each of the 29 lung immune cell subsets (Fig. 2a,b, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 7). Genes that show genotype-dependent changes in gene expression, that is, those associated with eQTLs, are hereafter referred to as eGenes. We discovered 3,550 unique eGenes across all the lung immune cell subsets, and fine-mapping analysis15,16 identified at least one credible set of putative causal variants for 3,254 eGenes. Comparison of eQTL effect sizes across immune cell subsets using multivariate adaptive shrinkage (MASH)17 revealed broad sharing of genetic effects, with strongest sharing observed among lineage-related cell types, consistent with shared cis-regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 2b). Shared eQTLs also displayed high concordance in both the effect size and the direction across the immune cell subsets analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). Despite extensive sharing, a substantial fraction of eQTLs linked to 894 eGenes showed cell type-specific effects, particularly when comparing lineage-related cell types with those from distinct lineages (Fig. 2b,c).

To assess sex-biased genetic effects, we repeated the eQTL analysis across lung immune cell types separately in male and female individuals (Supplementary Table 7e). Significant sex differences in eQTL effects on gene expression were observed for only a small subset of genes (n = 117; referred to as sex-biased eGenes), with most effects being concordant in both sexes (Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). The expression of the vast majority (>98%) of these eGenes did not differ significantly between cells from male and female individuals (Extended Data Fig. 6c). This finding is consistent with previous studies18 reporting that sex-biased genetic effects on gene regulation are largely uncoupled from sex-biased differences in gene expression. For example, although the transcript encoding the protein kinase AKT3, involved in T cell function19, showed stronger eQTL effects in cells of female individuals compared to male individuals, its expression was not significantly higher in female individuals (Extended Data Fig. 6e).

To identify mechanisms in specific immune cell subsets that may mediate the genetic risk of specific diseases, we colocalized lung immune cell eQTLs with GWAS signals from lung diseases, and infectious and autoimmune diseases (Supplementary Table 8). We discovered 492 (13.9%) eGenes with disease associations (Fig. 2d, Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 9a). When compared to the eGenes previously reported by the Database of Immune Cell Epigenomes (DICE) in circulating immune cell subsets isolated from blood4,20, 954 eGenes displayed prominent genotype-dependent associations with their expression specifically in lung immune cell types. Furthermore, when compared to eGenes provided by the OneK1K21 single-cell cis-eQTL study in circulating immune cells and other eQTL resources22,23, we identified a substantial number of new genetic associations and eGenes specific to tissue-resident immune cell subsets (Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 7f,g). A notable example in CD4+ TRM cells was ZNF683, which encodes for a transcription factor (TF) critical for the development of TRM cells24 (Fig. 2e–g and Extended Data Fig. 7c). It is noteworthy that carriers of the rs12037810-A allele, which increases ZNF683 transcripts specifically in CD4+ TRM cells, showed a nonsignificant trend toward higher frequencies of these cells in their lungs (Fig. 2g). This indicates a potential role of this ZNF683 eQTL in modulating the magnitude of CD4+ TRM responses in human lung. Other examples of eGenes that showed prominent effects specifically in lung immune cells when compared to circulating cells include XCL1 and XCL2, which show strong associations in NK cells and specific CD4+ and CD8+ T subsets in the lungs (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 7d) and the TF encoded by CUX1, which showed significant associations specifically in CD16+ non-classic monocytes isolated from lung tissue (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 7e). Overall, these results highlight the importance of studying tissue-resident immune cells to identify disease-associated genes in cell types relevant to disease pathogenesis.

eQTLs in lung immune cell types are enriched for genes in immune function pathways

To identify biological pathways impacted by eQTLs in lung immune cell types, we performed an overrepresentation analysis of their target eGenes in myeloid cells, NK cells, B cells, and CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. This analysis showed significant enrichment of eGenes in several pathways linked to immune cell function and metabolism (Supplementary Table 10). For example, eGenes in CD16+ NK cells were enriched for genes in the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathway, including multiple members of the killer-cell lectin-like receptor family (KLRC1, KLRC2, KLRC3, KLRC4 and KLRG1) and killer immunoglobulin receptors (KIR2DL1, KIR3DL1, KIR3DL2 and KIR3DL3) (Fig. 3a). Notably, the genotype-dependent associations with the expression of KIR2DL1, KIR3DL1, KIR3DL2 and KIR3DL3 were more pronounced in CD16+ NK cells isolated from lung tissue compared to blood4 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The eQTLs in lung myeloid cells and NK cells were significantly enriched for genes in the Reactome database pathway called ‘Immunoregulatory interactions between a lymphoid and a non-lymphoid cell’ (Supplementary Table 10). Many of these eGenes were located near the KIR gene locus and encode members of the leukocyte immunoglobulin (Ig)-like receptor (LILR) family, including activating (LILRA2, LILRA4 and LILRA6) and inhibitory (LILRB1 and LILRB3) cell-surface receptors and leukocyte-associated Ig-like receptor 1 (LAIR1) and LAIR2 (Fig. 3a). Another notable eGene in this pathway encodes the inhibitory receptor CD300a, which impacts antiviral and antitumor immunity25. The rs2670827 variant associated with CD300A expression displayed opposing effects in NK cells and CD8+ TRM cells, illustrating how the same variant can differentially modulate effector functions across immune cell types (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The interferon signaling pathway, which is critical for antiviral immunity, was also enriched for several eGenes, including IFNAR1, IRF5, IRF6, MX1, MX2, GBP3, GBP5, IFITM1, IFITM2 and IFITM3 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 11). The expression of IFNAR1, which encodes for interferon receptor 1, was associated with COVID-19 risk variants exclusively in lung CD4+ TRM cells (Fig. 3b), highlighting cell type-restricted effects of some variants. We found that IRF5 eQTLs colocalized with GWAS signals from systemic autoimmune diseases associated with lung inflammation and fibrosis, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis and Sjögren’s disease26–28. IRF5 encodes a TF that drives proinflammatory programs in macrophages and T cells, promoting inflammatory disease in animal models29,30. Notably, the disease-risk allele (rs2004640-T)29, located in the IRF5 promoter region, was predicted to disrupt the binding motif of the repressive TF ZNF21931. This allele is associated with increased expression of IRF5 in lung myeloid cell types and CD4+ TRM cells, implicating these cell types in disease pathology (Fig. 3c,d and Supplementary Fig. 6). Another disease-risk variant in the same haploblock, rs3807306, previously reported to modulate expression of IRF5 in reporter assays32, was located in a chromatin-accessible region and also associated with increased IRF5 expression, suggesting that this variant may also be functionally important.

The interferon-induced transmembrane protein IFITM3 is an important antiviral protein that inhibits viral entry and replication, thereby enhancing host cell resistance to viral infection33. In a mouse model of influenza infection, IFITM3 protects lung TRM cells from viral infection and promotes their persistence at sites of infection34. Importantly, human studies have shown that variants associated with decreased expression of IFITM3 are associated with poor outcomes after influenza and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection35,36. However, the cell types that mediate the effects of these genetic variants are not fully known. We found that the previously reported influenza-risk allele (rs34481144)-T37 at the IFITM3 promoter was associated with reduced IFITM3 expression across multiple lung immune cell types, with the most pronounced effects observed in the lung CD16+ NK cell subset (Fig. 3b,e,f). These results suggest that, in carriers of this risk allele, lung NK cells are likely key contributors to the genetic risk underlying influenza severity. Furthermore, fine-mapping analysis of IFITM3 eQTLs identified rs14408 as a credible variant (posterior inclusion probability (PIP): 1.0 in CD4+ TRM cells; 0.82 in CD8+ TRM cells) with stronger associations in lung T cell subsets, particularly TRM cells, compared to rs34481144 (Fig. 3b,e–g). It is noteworthy that the credible variant rs14408 is located in the promoter of the neighboring gene IFITM2, which directly interacts with the IFITM3 promoter, suggesting that distinct regulatory mechanisms may govern IFITM3 expression in specific lung immune cell types (Fig. 3f,g). Notably, this credible variant, rs14408, is in tight linkage disequilibrium (LD) (Dʹ = 0.98) with the GWAS variant rs7115703 linked to lung function impairment38 that is also associated with IFITM3 expression in CD16+ NK cells, and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Other antiviral interferon response genes in the IFITM locus, including IFITM1 and IFITM2, also showed genotype-dependent association in lung immune cells, although with weaker effects (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7b,c). Together, these findings suggest that common genetic variants alter the expression of several antiviral and immunomodulatory genes in specific tissue-resident immune cell populations, thereby shaping host responses to infections and autoimmunity. The examples highlighted here represent only a small subset of eGenes identified across the lung immune cells; the full dataset is available for querying any gene of interest at https://lung.dice-database.org.

Lung immune cell types and genes contributing to the genetic risk of lung diseases

COPD is a multi-factorial disease, primarily triggered by smoking and exposure to airborne pollutants, but genetic predisposition contributes substantially to disease susceptibility, with heritability estimates from twin and family studies ranging between 30% and 40% (refs. 39,40). GWAS aimed at identifying common genetic variants associated with lung function decline—the hallmark of COPD—have identified >1,000 risk loci and, when imputed into a polygenic risk score, these variants strongly predict COPD risk in smokers38,40. We found that the GWAS signals associated with COPD were significantly enriched in ATAC–seq peaks in all the major lung immune cell types (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that COPD risk variants have the potential to influence gene expression in these cell types. To assess the contribution of lung immune cell types to COPD heritability, and to compare this with contributions from circulating immune cell types and whole tissues, we applied mediated expression score regression (MESC)3 to estimate genetic effects mediated by gene expression levels in lung immune cell types, circulating immune cell types (OneK1K study)21 and selected whole tissues (Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Consortium6). We found that, on average, 15–20% of COPD SNP-based heritability was mediated by cis-eQTL-driven gene expression in lung immune cell types. By contrast, the mediated proportion was lower (~10%) for circulating immune cell types or bulk tissues (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11a). Similar estimates of gene expression-mediated heritability were obtained using a complementary method—causal-transcriptome-wide association studies (cTWAS)41 (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11b). Together, these results suggest that lung immune cell types are likely to contribute to the genetic risk of COPD.

Next, to identify the putative causal genes and lung immune cell types contributing to this genetic risk of lung-function impairment and COPD, we performed independent colocalization analysis of GWAS signals and eQTLs for the 29 lung immune cell subsets (Fig. 4a,b, Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 9). We identified 241 genes with colocalizing signals in at least one lung immune cell subset. The largest numbers of colocalizing signals were observed in lung CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, specifically TRM cells, T effector memory (TEM) cells and Granzyme K-expressing positive (GZMK+) subsets (Fig. 4a,b and Extended Data Fig. 9). As expected, the colocalized genes were significantly enriched in key biological pathways linked to the function of the adaptive immune system (Supplementary Table 10). For example, reduced lung NK cell, CD4+ TRM cell and CD8+ TRM cell expression of the colocalized gene IFITM3 (risk allele: rs7115703-T), critical for the generation and persistence of lung TRM cells34, was associated with the risk for impairment of lung function (Fig. 4b).

Genetic signals at the 17q21 locus from a few COPD and lung function GWAS colocalized with cis-eQTLs associated with the expression of ORMDL3, GSDMB and GSDMA in several lung immune cell subsets, except myeloid cells (Fig. 4b,c, Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Given the well-established association of genes at the 17q21 locus with asthma susceptibility42, we next assessed whether the genetic signals identified in COPD and asthma GWAS are also colocalized. Several COPD GWAS signals showed significant colocalization with asthma GWAS signals, including those that are associated with ORMDL3 expression (Extended Data Fig. 9b and Supplementary Tables 9b–d), indicating shared genetic mechanisms underlying susceptibility to both COPD and asthma. However, in some GWAS studies, the less precise definitions of patients with COPD, potentially including individuals with asthma or asthma–COPD overlap syndrome, may confound these results43. Fine-mapping analysis of the ORMDL3 eQTLs identified multiple credible sets, with rs4065275 as a credible variant with the strongest association in lung CD8+ TRM cells (PIP: 0.39; Fig. 4d). This variant is in strong LD with several COPD risk-associated variants in the 17q21 locus (Supplementary Table 9d). Notably, lung TRM cells from COPD risk-associated rs4065275-GG carriers expressed increased ORMDL3 transcript levels relative to rs4065275-AA genotype carriers (Fig. 4c). The same rs4065275-G allele is also associated with asthma risk and, given the role of ORMDL3 in regulating cytokine production in circulating T cells42, it is plausible that ORMDL3 variants may alter the functional potential of lung TRM cells to influence pathogenesis of both COPD and asthma. Other notable examples of colocalized genes that showed prominent associations in lung CD4+ and CD8+ T cells include ITCH, which encodes a ubiquitin ligase that acts as a negative regulator of T cell activation and differentiation44. FLOT1 encodes a lipid raft-associated protein important for the function of caveolae that influences the shape and motility of CD8+ T cells in vivo45. Overall, COPD-risk variants showed significant associations with gene expression in lung immune cell types, particularly T cells, suggesting that these genes may play a role in shaping T cell-driven airway inflammatory responses in COPD.

Asthma is another common chronic lung disease where genetic factors play a substantial role, with heritability estimates ranging from 50% to 90% in twin studies46. We identified 127 eGenes with significant colocalization between GWAS signals associated with asthma and lung immune cell eQTLs. Notably, 72 of these 127 eGenes (57%) were also linked to the genetic risk of lung-function impairment and COPD. For most of these shared eGenes (86%), the genetic signals associated with eGene expression in asthma and COPD also showed significant colocalization (Extended Data Fig. 9b and Supplementary Table 9b), indicating shared genetic mechanisms underlying susceptibility to both lung diseases. Although CD4+ T cells are considered central orchestrators of asthma pathogenesis47, many colocalized asthma-risk genes showed prominent effects in both lung CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets, suggesting a potential role also for lung CD8+ T cells in asthma. Notable colocalized genes that may influence CD8+ T cell function include FADS1 and FADS2 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8), both coding for fatty acid desaturases essential for the synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), which, along with its metabolites, have been shown to regulate immune cell proliferation and function48. Furthermore, we detected significant associations with asthma-risk variants for the transcript encoding the chemokine CCL20, which recruits CCR6-expressing T cells49, and JAZF1, which encodes a TF that has been reported to inhibit inflammatory responses by modulating the nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway50 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 9).

The expression of a multiple sclerosis risk-associated gene, AHI1, showed association with asthma-risk variants in many lung immune cell types, including T cells51. Fine-mapping analysis identified rs13197384, located in the AHI1 promoter region, as a credible variant with the strongest association in lung CD4+ TRM cells (PIP: 0.31). The asthma-risk allele (rs13197384-A)52 was associated with reduced expression of AHI1 and was predicted to reduce the binding of the TFs YY1 and EGR1 (Fig. 4e–g). Notably, Ahi1-deficient T cells displayed defects in proliferation and cytokine production in mouse models51, implying that this gene may also modulate the function of lung immune cells to influence asthma, a hypothesis that requires testing in relevant disease models. Overall, our colocalization analysis implicates several lung immune cell types and new genes as contributors to the genetic risk of lung diseases.

Genes associated with systemic autoimmune diseases

A wide range of systemic autoimmune diseases, such as sarcoidosis, systemic sclerosis, SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s disease and dermatomyositis, commonly manifest with symptomatic lung disease characterized by inflammation and fibrosis53–58. GWAS and phenome-wide association studies have shown substantial overlap in genetic signals across autoimmune diseases, suggesting that shared molecular mechanisms may underlie the genetic risk of systemic autoimmune diseases and associated lung pathogenesis40,59. Furthermore, tissue-resident immune cells across different organs share common core transcriptional programs, suggesting that disease-risk variants that affect tissue-resident cells in lungs are also likely to impact those in other organs60,61. Therefore, to identify the putative causal genes in tissue-resident immune cells that are associated with autoimmune-risk variants, we colocalized cis-eQTLs of lung immune cell subsets and GWAS signals from 32 immune-mediated diseases (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9a). We identified 281 colocalized genes in at least one lung immune cell type and many of these genes, such as ORMDL3, GSDMB, FADS1, FLOT1, PSMB9, TUFM and AIF1, were associated with genetic risk of multiple autoimmune diseases as well as chronic lung diseases like asthma and COPD (Fig. 5a,b and Extended Data Fig. 10). The genetic signals associated with autoimmune and chronic lung diseases also showed significant colocalization (Supplementary Table 9b), consistent with shared genetic mechanisms underlying disease risk. Several colocalized genes displayed cell type-restricted effects, for example, AIF1 cis-eQTLs showed effects only in lung CD8+ T cell subsets. AIF1 encodes a calcium-binding protein involved in inflammatory processes and its function has mainly been studied in myeloid cells62; however, our genetic analysis implicates a role for this gene in modulating the function of tissue-resident CD8+ T cells to impact disease (Fig. 5c).

We identified 40 genes showing significant colocalization between lung immune cell eQTLs and GWAS signals associated with sarcoidosis, a systemic autoimmune disease that primarily affects the lungs53 (Extended Data Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 9a). Notably, MESC analysis suggests that a substantial fraction of the SNP-based heritability for sarcoidosis was mediated by gene expression levels (eQTLs) in lung immune cell types (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11), indicating their contribution to the genetic risk of sarcoidosis. ZFP57, one of the top-colocalized genes, showed genotype-dependent expression across multiple lung immune cell types (Fig. 5b,d–f and Supplementary Table 9a). Associated with increased expression of ZFP57, the C-allele of the credible variant rs2747329 was predicted to enhance the binding of OVOL2, a TF that has recently been shown to play a role in the development and function of mouse macrophages63 (Fig. 5e,f). Importantly, ZFP57 cis-eQTLs also colocalized with GWAS signals from several other autoimmune and lung diseases, suggesting a broader role in autoimmunity (Supplementary Table 9). ZFP57 is a TF that regulates genomic imprinting64; however, its function in immune cells has not been explored. Knockdown of ZFP57 expression in monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) resulted in altered expression levels of transcripts encoding for several cytokines and chemokines involved in immune cell trafficking and inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 12), suggesting a potentially important role for ZFP57 in modulating the genetic risk of sarcoidosis and other autoimmune diseases by regulating the function of MDMs.

Lung B cell expression of FAM167A and BLK was significantly associated with the risk of SLE, rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 11). BLK is known to play an important role in the function of B cells65, whereas FAM167A, a poorly characterized protein, has recently been shown to modulate the NF-κB signaling pathway in cancer cell lines66. Another lung B cell candidate gene linked to SLE risk is TSPAN3, which encodes a tetraspanin protein involved in antigen presentation and cell migration67 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 11). Thus, autoimmune-risk variants may impact the function of lung B cells and influence disease pathogenesis by modulating the expression of these genes.

The risk of systemic sclerosis, an autoimmune disease that causes progressive fibrosis of the lung, was associated with the expression of 36 colocalized genes in lung immune cell subsets (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 11). Gene expression-mediated heritability in lung immune cell types ranging from ~30% to 40% was higher than that in circulating immune cell types (<20%) (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11). Notable colocalized genes are IL12RB1 and IL12RB2, which encode the β1 and β2 subunits of the interleukin (IL)-12 receptor, essential for signaling IL-12, as well as other IL-12 cytokine family members, IL-23 and IL-3568. It is noteworthy that the systemic sclerosis-risk variants associated with the expression of IL12RB1 showed prominent effects in CD4+ and CD8+ TRM cell subsets, whereas variants linked to IL12RB2 expression displayed effects that were restricted to lung NK cells (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 11d–g). Given the important role of the IL-12 signaling pathway in modulating the function of T cells and NK cells69, it is likely that systemic sclerosis-risk variants modulate this pathway to influence the pathogenicity of TRM cells and NK cells, a hypothesis that needs further validation in relevant disease models. Overall, our findings highlight that disease-risk variants from a wide range of systemic autoimmune diseases impact gene expression in lung tissue immune cell types, suggesting a role for these genes and specific tissue-resident immune cell types in contributing to the shared genetic risk of autoimmunity.

Discussion

Despite the critical importance of tissue-resident immune cells in lung diseases, autoimmunity and infectious diseases, very little is known about their contribution to the genetic risk associated with susceptibility to these diseases. This is largely due to a lack of studies that examine the effects of disease-associated variants on tissue-resident immune cells. A recent eQTL study of lung cells70 was underpowered to robustly detect eGenes across many major and rare immune cell populations, due to limited cell numbers for NK cells, B cells, CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells, and consequently lacked the resolution to compare genetic effects across distinct lung immune cell populations (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1b). To address these gaps, the DICE project generated eQTL datasets in 29 transcriptionally distinct immune cell subsets isolated from cancer-free lung tissues of 120 individuals. Our colocalization analysis revealed several genes in tissue-resident immune cells that were associated with GWAS signals from immune-mediated and infectious diseases. Given the conserved transcriptional programs of tissue-resident immune cells across organs7, genetic insights obtained from lung-resident immune cell populations can be extrapolated to their counterparts in other organ tissues, including the synovium, salivary gland, skin and intestinal tissue where they are implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s disease, dermatomyositis and inflammatory bowel disease.

For allergic diseases such as asthma, where CD4+ T cells are viewed as key orchestrators of airway inflammation, we identified colocalizing genes in both the CD4+ and the CD8+ T cell populations in the lung tissue, suggesting that CD8+ T cells may also contribute to the genetic risk and potentially to disease pathology. The pathogenesis of COPD, another common chronic lung disease, has been primarily attributed to aberrant responses of lung structural cells to cigarette smoke and other airborne pollutants. Our analysis points to a contribution of lung immune cells to the genetic risk of disease, with many GWAS signals colocalizing to genes expressed in lung CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets, implicating these genes and cell types as potential mediators of COPD pathogenesis.

Although our study provides valuable insights into how disease-associated variants affect gene expression in tissue-resident immune cell types, it has some limitations. First, our analysis is restricted to a single organ and predominantly to immune cells isolated from the lung parenchyma, which does not fully capture the diversity of immune cell populations or regulatory mechanisms present across different tissues. Although sharing core properties, tissue-resident immune cell types also display tissue-specific expression and cis-regulatory profiles71, therefore extending similar analyses to immune cells isolated from diverse organs and airway wall tissues, as well as lung structural cells, will be critical for uncovering the full spectrum of cell type-specific and tissue-specific genes underlying disease risk, especially for asthma where immune cells in the airway wall tissue play a key pathogenic role. Second, disease itself induces unique cellular and transcriptional states that may not be observed in non-diseased tissues; therefore, analyzing tissue-resident immune cell types from individuals with specific diseases is likely to uncover additional variants that influence disease-associated cell types and their gene expression programs. Third, because the immune cells were obtained from cancer-free lung tissue of patients with underlying lung cancer, their phenotype may be influenced by cancer, smoking and other comorbidities. In addition, preservation and enzymatic processing of lung tissue samples have precluded the analysis of granulocyte populations that are sensitive to these conditions. Fourth, although heritability analyses indicate a significant contribution from gene expression in lung immune cells, these estimations are model-dependent and may be influenced by factors such as LD structure, shared regulatory programs and biased due to eQTL discovery. Finally, while we identified several disease-associated genes, cell types and potential causal variants, functional validations will be required to confirm their role in disease pathogenesis.

Similar to the DICE datasets of circulating immune cells, the new DICE dataset of lung immune cells (https://lung.dice-database.org) will also: (1) serve as a valuable resource to the research community for defining genes and tissue-resident immune cell types associated with disease-risk variants; (2) serve as a valuable reference dataset for both the relative abundances and transcriptional profiles of immune cell subsets in human lung tissue, derived from a large cohort of 120 individuals; and (3) provide cis-regulatory maps of immune cell types isolated from lung tissue, along with potential causal eQTLs linked to disease-associated genes, which can be utilized to develop polygenic risk prediction scores associated with specific cell types and pathways.

Methods

Study and details of individuals included in this cohort

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the South Central—Hampshire A Research Ethics Committee (14/SC/0186) and the Ethics Committee of La Jolla Institute for Immunology (LJI); written informed consent was obtained from all individuals before sample collection. Treatment-naive patients with newly diagnosed non-small cell lung cancer undergoing standard-of-care surgical resection were prospectively recruited at University Hospital Southampton NHS Foundation Trust, UK and Liverpool Heart and Chest NHS Foundation Trust Hospital, UK, from 2018 to 2021. Individuals tested positive for hepatitis B, hepatitis C and human immunodeficiency virus were excluded from the study. The cohort consisted of 128 individuals, with 59 (46.1%) women and 69 (53.9%) men and a median age of 71 years (range 45–91 years); demographic, clinical and laboratory metadata are provided in Supplementary Table 2a.

Sample processing

Fresh, macroscopically uninvolved, non-tumor-bearing regions of lung parenchyma were collected during surgical resection of lung tumors. To ensure that lung tissue was free of cancer, uninvolved tissue was collected at least 5 cm away from the tumor margin and the tumor resection margins were confirmed by the pathologist. Lung tissue samples were processed immediately by dicing in <1-mm3 pieces, cryopreserved in freezing medium (90% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide) in multiple cryovials and stored for subsequent analyses. The lung tissue samples were then processed in groups of five samples using a combination of mechanical and enzymatic dissociation to yield single-cell suspensions, as described previously74. In brief, non-tumor lung tissue was cut into small fragments and incubated with 5 ml of Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 0.15 Wünsch units (WU) ml−1 of Liberase DL (Roche) and 800 units ml−1 of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 15 min using a gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). The dispersed cells were then passed through a 70-µm filter, centrifuged and resuspended in MACS buffer (phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2 mM EDTA and 2.0% FBS). For isolating and phenotyping of CD45+ cells from tissue, the dispersed cells were then incubated with FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec), labeled with Hashtag TotalSeq-C antibodies (for single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq)) and stained with a mixture of fluorescence-conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table 13). Cells were then sorted by flow cytometry using a FACSAria Fusion Cell Sorter (Becton Dickinson) and the gating strategy shown in Extended Data Fig. 1. For each experimental batch (n = 13), CD45+ cells of five to ten individuals were sorted into separate collection tubes for each predefined cell population based on cell-surface markers (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 13). All flow cytometry datasets were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.10.0).

Cell isolation and preparation of scRNA-seq libraries

Of the 128 individuals enrolled in this study, we generated scRNA-seq data from 120 individuals with the clinical diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma (n = 77), lung squamous cell carcinoma (n = 41), lung adenosquamous carcinoma (n = 1) or lung sarcomatoid carcinoma (n = 1); details are provided in Supplementary Table 2a. For each experimental batch, eight to nine tubes with pooled cells from five to ten individuals were utilized to prepare scRNA-seq libraries (10x Genomics); details are provided in Supplementary Table 3. For each sequencing library (n = 96), about 50,000 cells from 10 individuals were pooled by FACS into low-retention 1.5-ml collection tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific), containing 0.5 ml of a 1:1 solution of PBS to FBS supplemented with RNase inhibitor (Takara Bio, 1:100). After enrichment by FACS, the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, centrifuged for 5 min (600g at 4 °C), gently resuspended in ice-cold PBS, supplemented with 0.04% ultra-pure bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) and processed following the manufacturer’s instructions (10x Genomics). The scRNA-seq library preparation was performed following the manufacturer’s recommendations for the 10x Genomics 5ʹ Tag v2.0 chemistry kit with feature barcoding technology for cell-surface proteins. The complementary DNA libraries were generated with 13 cycles of amplification; the 5ʹ-Tag gene expression libraries and the corresponding hashtag libraries were generated separately with 13 and 7 cycles of amplification, respectively. The libraries were quantified and pooled in equivalent molar concentrations and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform with the following read lengths: read 1—100 cycles; read 2—100 cycles; i7 index—10 cycles; i5 index—10 cycles.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

Reads from each single-cell gene expression library were demultiplexed, aligned to the genome reference GRCh38 (pre-built reference ‘2020-A’) and collapsed into unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts using 10x Genomics’ Cell Ranger Software Suite (v3.1.0). Independent libraries of the same predefined immune cell population were aggregated by using the module aggr. As NK cells and B cells were pooled during FACS and prepared as combined libraries, the resulting data were aggregated for downstream transcriptome profile-based cell-type deconvolution. Supplementary Table 3b lists the gene expression libraries (n = 96) included in each aggregation (according to the predefined immune cell population), as well as its associated metrics and Supplementary Table 3c shows the final metrics from each aggregation. The merged data for each predefined immune cell population were transferred to the R statistical environment for analysis based on Seurat (v3.2.2)75. Only genes expressed in at least 0.1% of the cells from each aggregated dataset were kept for analysis. Sex and age of the individuals (as indicated in Supplementary Table 2a) were included in the Seurat object’s metadata.

Cells were assigned to a specific individual using two strategies: first, cell barcodes with gene expression profiles and data from hashtag oligos (HTOs; cell hashing by oligo-tagged antibodies) were classified into one of three possible classes (singlet, doublet or negative) as previously described20. Second, in a library-specific manner, cell barcodes were assigned to individuals on the basis of the transcriptomes’ genetic variants by applying Demuxlet (v1.0)76. Here out of 3,920,137 genetic variants, 1 × 106 variants overlapping gene body regions with minor allele frequency (MAF) >0.05 were randomly selected and provided to the algorithm with the default parameters recommended in ref. 76 for better estimation of doublets. The input vcf file was filtered using vcftools (v.0.1.13)77 and sorted according to the order output by 10x’s Cell Ranger count via application of the script sort_vcf_same_as_bam from popscle helper tools76. Cell barcodes without HTO data and classified as Doublets or Ambiguous by Demuxlet were left without an assigned individual’s identity (about 4.5% of the cell barcodes for the average aggregated dataset). The final assignment of a cell to a specific individual was achieved by a sequential approach: cell barcodes were classified based on HTO data if available. Subsequently, cell barcodes without HTO data were assigned to specific individuals using genetic variants overlapping sequenced transcripts by Demuxlet. Cells classified as doublets based on HTO data during the deconvolution stage were excluded from all further analyses. Furthermore, potential remaining multiplets (mainly same-individual multiplets), as well as low-quality cells, were removed using the criteria listed in Supplementary Table 3g. Quality control (QC)-feature cutoffs were tailored according to the predefined immune cell populations to account for cell-type variability in the distribution of raw QC features (Extended Data Fig. 2).

UMI counts in each aggregated dataset were normalized using the function NormalizeData. We selected the most variable genes with a mean UMI expression >0.01 accounting for a specific percentage of the total variance after standardization using the variance stabilizing transformation (vst) method (function FindVariableFeatures). The percentage of standardized variance selected for each aggregated dataset (and at each round of analysis) is indicated in Supplementary Table 3h. By applying the function ScaleData, normalized UMI counts were further centered and scaled per gene and across cells. At this stage, the variables corresponding to the number of UMI counts and the percentage of mitochondrial UMI counts per cell were regressed out (argument vars.to.regress). As each aggregated dataset of predefined immune cell populations consisted of multiple individual sequencing libraries (range: 12–47), we handled any potential batch effects by applying Harmony (v0.1.0)78, objectively removing the noise introduced by this variable (function RunHarmony). We identified cell clusters representing subpopulations of each predefined immune cell population by applying the functions FindNeighbors and FindClusters (the specific number of principal components (PCs) is provided in Supplementary Table 3h). Variables that influence the clustering analysis, namely the percentage of standardized variance and the number of gene expression PCs, were selected out of different options (standardized variance: 20%, 25% or 30%; gene expression PCs: 25 or 30) based on the setting that best reflected the expected biology in each aggregated dataset. UMAP embeddings were calculated with the function RunUMAP where 40 PCs were provided.

The aforementioned steps were applied in multiple cleanup rounds to each aggregated dataset until none of the following undesired clusters were found: (1) a cluster representing contamination (for example, a cluster representing B cells in the aggregated dataset of CD4+ T cells); (2) a cluster of cells showing enrichment for the heat-shock protein stress response that has been previously associated with the handling of cryopreserved samples79; or (3) a cluster of cells showing expression of genes previously identified in the signature of proliferating cells80. At least two (for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) and up to five (required for NK cells) rounds of analysis were necessary for a dataset to meet these criteria depending on the predefined immune cell population. As NK cells and B cells were sequenced in pooled libraries, the cell subsets were deconvolved into separate datasets in the first round of analysis where the B cell receptor-specific genes were not taken into consideration to avoid introduction of noise. After that, multiple cleanup rounds were applied to the aggregates of NK cells and B cells as described, where, if a cluster represented the opposite cell type, the respective cells were recycled back into the other aggregated dataset. Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 3h.

Based on the gene expression profiles, number of genes and UMI counts detected, we did not observe any major differences in the scRNA-seq libraries (n = 96) across the immune cell populations in any QC step. To examine whether observed variations in cellular composition or gene expression are affected by histological type of lung cancer, we applied principal component analysis (PCA). In brief, PCA was performed for each predefined immune cell population on the donor-wise pseudo-bulk (averaged) profiles, considering only the highly variable genes accounting for 30% of the total variance (defined as described above). Associations between histological type of lung cancer and each of the top ten PCs were tested using Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test, with the resulting P values corrected applying the Benjamini–Hochberg method across PCs for each immune cell population. The histological type of lung cancer, clinical or laboratory metadata did not make a major contribution to the variance in cell frequencies or gene expression in tissue-resident immune cell populations (Extended Data Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3o).

Utilization of published scRNA-seq datasets including cells from human lung and airways

For reference, we selected studies that reported on scRNA-seq datasets containing cells from human lungs and airways (Supplementary Table 1a). In most cases, the number of single-cell transcriptomes reported for each immune cell population assessed in our study were retrieved directly from the paper or Supplementary information. For the study by the Tabula Sapiens Consortium60, raw data were downloaded from figshare and the immune cell populations defined under the metadata column ‘cell_ontology_class‘ were manually assigned to each of the five immune cell populations reported in this study. Single-cell differential gene expression analysis and GSEA

Population-specific markers enriched in each cell subset within each predefined immune cell population were identified using the function FindAllMarkers in Seurat (v3.2.2)75 with MAST (v1.12)81 as the statistical framework. The results are listed in Supplementary Table 4. On average, 114 individuals were considered for this analysis (range: 44–120 individuals).

Cell isolation and preparation of scATAC–seq libraries

Of the 128 individuals enrolled in this study, we generated scATAC–seq data from 28 individuals (11 (39.3%) women and 17 (60.7%) men); details are provided in Supplementary Table 2a. For each experimental batch, tubes with pooled cells from four to eight individuals were utilized to prepare scATAC–seq libraries (10x Genomics); details are provided in Supplementary Table 6a–e. For each sequencing library (n = 23), up to 30,000 cells were pooled by FACS into low-retention 1.5-ml collection tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific), containing 0.5 ml of a 1:1 solution of PBS to FBS supplemented with RNase inhibitor (1:100). After enrichment by FACS, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, gently resuspended in lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 5 min. After isolation, the nuclei were washed, incubated in transposition mix at 37 ˚C for 1 h and directly employed for the generation of Gel Beads-in-emulsion (GEM) and barcoding following the manufacturer’s recommendations (10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell ATAC Reagent Kit v1.1). The scATAC–seq libraries were generated with nine cycles of amplification, purified, quantified and pooled in equivalent molar concentrations, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform with the following read lengths: read 1—100 cycles; read 2—100 cycles; i7 index—10 cycles; i5 index—16 cycles.

Analysis of scATAC–seq data

For analysis of scATAC–seq data, the sequencing data were pre-processed using Cell Ranger ATAC (v2.0.0). We used the cellranger-atac count pipeline to align demultiplexed sequencing reads to the GRCh38 reference genome (refdata-cellranger-arc-GRCh38-2020-A-2.0.0), generate single-cell accessibility counts and create arrow files for each sequencing library (n = 23); details are provided in Supplementary Table 6. The libraries were then aggregated separately for each predefined immune cell population (n = 5). The downstream analyses were performed in R (v4.0.1) using the R package ArchR (v1.0.2)83. To define high-quality and exclude low-quality cell barcodes, we first calculated transcription start site (TSS) enrichment scores and number of unique fragments for each cell barcode. For the sequencing libraries of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, barcodes with a TSS enrichment score ≥10 and ≥3,162 unique fragments (corresponding to a value of 3.5 on a logarithmic scale) were classified as cells. Similarly, for the sequencing libraries of myeloid cells, NK cells and B cells, barcodes with a TSS enrichment score ≥8 and ≥3,162 unique fragments were classified as cells. Doublets were removed using the functions addDoubletScore and filterDoubles with default parameters using the ArchR package83. Briefly, in silico doublets are created by mixing reads from individual cell barcodes; these synthetic doublets are projected into the UMAP embedding and their nearest neighbors are identified as doublets and removed from downstream analyses. For dimensionality reduction and visualization, we employed ArchR using latent semantic indexing (LSI) and singular value decomposition followed by UMAP. We used the function addIterativeLSI to perform dimensional reduction by providing the top 30,000 variable features and the first 40 dimensions, excluding those highly correlated with library sequencing depth (coefficient of determination, r2 > 0.75). Next, the single-cell chromatin accessibility profiles were clustered using the function addClusters with default parameters and utilizing Seurat’s Louvain clustering approach75 in the function FindClusters. Batch correction was conducted using Harmony78 to account for technical variability between the sequencing libraries. For each predefined immune cell population, we performed two rounds of dimensional reduction, batch effect correction and clustering to identify and exclude low-quality cell clusters. In the first round, we excluded only clusters comprising cells from a single sequencing library and undefined cluster with <100 cells. In the second round, we removed low-quality cell clusters defined by low TSS enrichment scores and/or low numbers of unique fragments and clusters, with high numbers of unique fragments when compared to other cell clusters, likely representing multiplets that escaped the initial cleanup steps.

To verify the identity of cell subsets within each predefined immune cell population, we performed cluster annotation using two independent approaches. First, we employed the chromatin accessibility data to calculate gene activity scores using the function addGeneScoreMat, which implements an exponential weighted function that accounts for the activity of distal regulatory regions (intergenic regions), promoters and the gene body, in a distance-dependent manner. Hence, gene activity scores serve as a proxy for gene expression and allow identification of the expression of relevant marker genes for a given cell subset. Second, we directly integrated the single-cell chromatin accessibility data and gene expression data to transfer the cluster annotations called in our scRNA-seq data. Here ArchR’s integration method aligns chromatin accessibility and gene expression profiles by comparing gene activity scores with gene expression values and identifies similarities in the two datasets to then assign corresponding cluster annotations.

At single-cell resolution, chromatin accessibility data are highly sparse and essentially binary, rendering peak calling analysis for single cells unfeasible. To overcome this problem, we aggregated the chromatin accessibility profiles of individual cells into pseudo-bulk profiles for each predefined immune cell population (n = 5) or immune cell subset (n = 27), as defined by our clustering analysis (details are provided in Supplementary Table 6). For each pseudo-bulk profile, we performed peak calling to define open chromatin regions using MACS284 in ArchR using the functions addGroupCoverages and addReproduciblePeakSet with default parameters. For data visualization, we created bigBed and bigWig files using the function getGroupBw with customized parameters (tile size: 10 bp; normalization: ReadsInTSS; maxCells parameter: NULL). The University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser was used to display the tracks.

Based on the chromatin accessibility profiles, fragment counts, fragment size and TTS enrichment, we did not observe any major differences in the scATAC–seq libraries (n = 23) across the immune cell populations in any QC step.

Utilization of published scATAC–seq datasets including cells from human tissues

To analyze chromatin accessibility data in human tissues published in refs. 12–14, these datasets were downloaded and re-analyzed as described above. In brief, the cellranger-atac count pipeline was used to map the reads to GRCh38; the fragments files were transferred to R and analyzed with ArchR. Cell barcodes with a TSS enrichment score ≥8 and ≥3,162 unique fragments were classified as cells. After removal of doublets, dimensionality reduction, batch correction and clustering, cell clusters with <100 cells were removed and gene activity scores were calculated. Reported marker genes were used as a reference to annotate the cell populations previously described by the authors.

Generation of libraries for WGS

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed for the 120 individuals with scRNA-seq data. In brief, for each individual, genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN), quantified and sheared using a Covaris E220 Focused-Ultrasonicator. The sonicated DNA was utilized for library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina kit (NEB, cat. no. E7645L) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina kit (NEB, cat. no. E7600S). After cleanup using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Life Science), the DNA samples were amplified by PCR (five cycles). The libraries were then purified and size selected to 300–800 bp using AMPure XP beads according to the manufacturer’s protocol, quantified using PicoGreen double-stranded DNA quantification reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the size distribution was accessed using a 5200 Fragment Analyzer Automated CE System (Agilent Advanced Analytical). The purified WGS libraries were then subjected to 150-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Read alignment, quantification and QC were performed as described below.

Processing of WGS data

The WGS libraries were sequenced to a median coverage of 30.8× (mean coverage: 32.4×); details are provided in Supplementary Table 7a. The fastq files were processed with a pipeline set up based on GATK Best Practice recommendations85 and the pipeline used by the GTEx Consortium6. Reads were mapped with BWA-MEM (v0.7.17)86 against the human GRCh38 reference genome and Picard tools (v2.26.1) were used to mark duplicated reads. The coverage and mapping statistics were calculated using CollectAlignmentSummaryMetrics and CollectWgsMetrics on the bam files of each sample. BaseRecalibrator and HaplotypeCaller tools from GATK (v4.2.2.0)85,87 were used for base quality score recalibration and variant discovery for each sample, respectively. The reference files were obtained from GATK (https://console.cloud.google.com/storage/browser/genomicspublicdata/resources/broad/hg38/v0/) and the variant files were merged for each individual using the GATK tools GenomicsDBImport, GenotypeGVCFs and GatherVcfs. Multi-allelic variants were split into multiple biallelic sites by bcftools norm (v1.9)88 and noncanonical DNA sequences were removed. Variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) was performed for SNPs and inserts and deletions (indels) independently using VariantRecalibrator and ApplyVQSR was used to remove variants with <99.8% and <99.9% sensitivity level for SNPs and indels, respectively. CalculateGenotypePosteriors was used to calculate genotype quality scores (GQ) based on reference allele frequencies from the 1,000 Genome Project89. Variants with read depth (DP) <3 and/or GQ < 20 were assigned as missing. Variants were removed based on two filters: (1) Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium P < 1 × 10−6 and (2) genotype missingness rate of ≥5%. To impute missing genotypes, BEAGLE (v5.2)90 was used without mapping reference files (java -Xmx24g -jar beagle.jar gt=(vcf.file) nthreads=(threads) out=(output. prefix) impute=false). Our WGS analysis resulted in the detection of a total of 24,206,695 genetic variants after QC and phasing (10,277,274 genetic variants with MAF ≥ 0.01; 6,879,375 genetic variants with MAF ≥ 0.05; Supplementary Table 7b).

Mapping of DICE eQTLs and statistical analyses

Utilizing the scRNA-seq gene expression data, cluster-specific pseudo-bulk profiles were calculated as average gene expression (Seurat-normalized values) across all cells of a given individual, including only individuals with ten or more cells for a given cell cluster. Due to limitations in the numbers of cells isolated, our study was not powered to examine genetic effects in very rare immune cell subsets such as innate lymphoid cells, basophils and mast cells. The pseudo-bulk expression was normalized using the edgeR package91. To account for potential population stratification, PCA was carried out using Plink (v1.9)92 on an LD-independent variant set (plink –file input –indep-pairwise 200 100 0.1) with MAF ≥ 0.05. Identified by genotype PCA, most variance was already captured by PC1, whereas additional genotype PCs did not substantially explain the variance in our cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3a,b), confirming a uniform population structure. Furthermore, we compared our cohort with the individuals studied in the 1,000 Genome Project89, using a shared set of LD-independent variants with MAF ≥ 0.10 (358,942 genetic variants). In our cohort of 120 individuals, this analysis identified 119 (99.2%) individuals with European ancestry and 1 (0.8%; ID 1421) individual with African ancestry (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The variance of the population structure captured by the first genotype PC was therefore included as covariate in the downstream analyses. Notably, applying additional genotype PCs did not significantly alter the resulting eGene count in any cell cluster (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

To account for batch effects, confounder factors and other sources of variability in gene expression, we also applied the probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) approach93. The effect of this approach was tested by adding up to 20 PEER factors to the analysis and executing the eQTL analysis for each cell cluster, independent of the size of the cell cluster. Increasing the number of PEER factors applied as covariates resulted in substantially reduced eGene counts (Supplementary Fig. 3c). To maximize the eGene discovery in this study, we included the first two PEER factors in the downstream analysis. Then, we fitted linear models of the form lm(PEER ~ covariate) in R and extracted the adjusted coefficient of determination (R2) values for covariates identified in ≥10% of individuals. Similar analyses were performed using the major PCs that influenced variance in gene expression. The clinical and laboratory covariates examined contributed minimally to the gene expression variance in each immune cell subset, as shown for both PEER factors and major PCs (Supplementary Fig. 1b,c).

To perform eQTL analysis, Snakemake (v7.14.0)94 was used to set up a workflow dependent on the Matrix eQTL package (v2.1.1)95 to fit a linear model between the pseudo-bulk gene expression profile in a given cell cluster and all identified genetic variants located within a ±1-Mb window around the annotated TSS of a given gene. The first genotype PC, two PEER factors, sex and age of the individuals were included as covariates in this analysis. To identify genes with at least one significant eQTL, the nominal P values for each gene were corrected for multiple testing at the gene level using eigenMT96. To correct for the number of genes tested, a Benjamini–Hochberg false recovery rate (FDR) was calculated using the eigenMT-corrected P values (Padj (FDR)).

When compared to cell cluster with high cell counts, our eQTL analysis identified lower numbers of eGenes in small cell cluster. To determine the bias of cell counts per individual and the number of individuals included in the eQTL analysis, these factors were independently downsampled in the largest cell cluster in our study (CD8+ TEM cells: 213,590 cells from 120 individuals). Upon execution of the eQTL analysis on these downsampled datasets, we observed that the number of identified eGenes positively correlated with the proportion of cells per individual and the number of individuals used in the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3d). In conclusion, sparse data—either a limited number of cells contributing to the pseudo-bulk gene expression profile per individual for a given cell cluster or scRNA-seq data from a too small cohort of individuals—limit the value of the eQTL analysis. For the determination of cis-acting eQTLs, affected genes (eGenes) and their comparison across different cell clusters, an expression threshold was applied to eliminate lowly expressed genes (transcripts detected in <1% of cells or <5% of individuals of a given cell cluster) and only significant associations were captured (raw P < 0.0001 and Padj (FDR) < 0.05). Despite these stringent criteria and exclusion of donors with low cell counts, in 23 out of 29 immune cell subsets all the eQTLs identified had a minor allele count (MAC) of ≥10, the minimum recommended by GTEx97. Most cis-eQTLs (~94%) identified in rare cell subsets (6 out of 29) had a MAC ≥ 10. Notably, for the vast majority of eGenes identified (>95%) across the lung immune cell subsets, the peak cis-eQTLs had a MAC ≥ 10. Given the potential for higher false-positive rates when testing SNPs with a lower MAC (<10), we have included the details of MAC for the peak cis-eQTL–eGene pairs identified (Supplementary Table 7d).

We performed fine-mapping to determine casual genetic variants associated with each eQTL signal using the SuSiE package (v0.12.35)15,16. In each cell cluster, and for each identified eGene, we used the same set of genetic variants previously tested for eQTL associations (genetic variants located within a ±1-Mb window around the annotated TSS) and controlled for effects of covariates (as in eQTL analysis) by regressing out the normalized pseudo-bulk expression values, following the suggested steps in the SuSiE GitHub repository. We utilized the SuSiE susie_rss function on covariate-adjusted genotypes and gene expression profiles, the eQTL-derived summary statistics (β and standard error) and set the maximum number of credible sets to 10, the minimum coverage probability of each credible set to 0.95 and the minimum absolute correlation between genetic variants in a credible set to 0.5 (susie_rss(bhat = ss$betahat, shat = ss$sebetahat), R = cov2cor(crossprod(X), n = n, var_y = var(Y)), L = 10, coverage=0.95, min_abs_corr=0.5, max_iter = 200, estimate_residual_variance = TRUE). The estimated PIP for SNPs from a credible set for an eGene are plotted in the respective figures; the count of credible sets per eGene is listed in Supplementary Table 7.

To provide a robust comparison and assessment of cis-eQTL–gene pair replication across the 29 distinct immune cell subsets analyzed in this study, we estimated the sharing of cis-eQTLs utilizing complementary approaches: (1) we applied the MASH method to jointly model the effect size of top cis-eQTLs for each immune cell subset6,17. For each pair of immune cell subsets, we considered the top cis-eQTLs that were significant (lfsr < 0.05) in at least one of the two immune cell subsets and plotted the proportion of these that were shared in magnitude and direction by using the get_pairwise_sharing function in R (with a factor of 0.5 and sign concordance of posterior effect estimates). (2) We estimated the replication degree using Pi1 statistic6 and (3) computed the correlation of cis-eQTL effect sizes and the fraction of concordant effect directions across pairs of immune cell subsets (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). Furthermore, we identified cell-specific eQTL–gene pairs with MASH-based lfsr < 0.05 in a single immune cell subset (Fig. 2c). Together these analyses provide a robust comparison of eQTL sharing across the different cell clusters.

To assess effect concordance of identified cis-eQTLs and eGenes, we compared the effects of lead genetic variants to published eQTL analyses from bulk and scRNA-seq data provided by the eQTL Catalogue project (33 studies; downloaded in October 2025)23, the ImmuNexUT atlas22, two previous releases of the DICE project (analyzing immune cells in blood from a different cohort of 91 individuals)4,20 (Supplementary Table 7f) and eQTL datasets from the GTEx Consortium6 (Supplementary Table 7g).

Curation of public GWAS datasets

All GWAS statistics were obtained from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas), the Pan-UK Biobank project (https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org), the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative (releases 5–7) and the Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative (GBMI). Overall, we downloaded 1,749 raw GWAS datasets along with their available metadata (that is, number of cases, number of controls and genome annotation) from 745 studies for 45 immune-mediated human diseases or traits (details are provided in Supplementary Table 8). Notably, publicly available GWAS datasets are inconsistently formatted, often lack relevant information and strongly vary in data quality. To address these challenges and curate and standardize these heterogeneous GWAS datasets (classified as datasets with nonsummary or summary statistics), we subjected all downloaded datasets to a processing pipeline consisting of these steps: data cleaning, liftover and QC. The implementation of this pipeline enabled high-throughput data processing and standardization, removal of SNPs with missing information relevant in downstream analyses, alignment of the genome reference assembly and standardization of the formatting as input data for our downstream analysis. Briefly, our data-cleaning approach focused on three important features of GWAS datasets: genomic coordinates, assignment of effect and noneffect alleles, and SNP-specific statistics. For datasets with summary statistics, we removed SNPs with missing information for chromosome or position and SNPs in noncanonical or mitochondrial chromosomes. Then, we backfilled any missing allelic information using dbSNP (v156) and aligned effect and noneffect alleles to match the alternative (ALT) and reference (REF) alleles in dbSNP, respectively. The gradient direction of the effect size estimates was adjusted when alleles were interchanged. Subsequently, we retrieved the following SNP-specific summary statistics: GWAS association P values, effect-size estimates, standard error, z-score and MAF. If not provided within the dataset, standard error and z-score were re-calculated utilizing GWAS association P value and an effect-size estimate.

Datasets with nonsummary statistics were processed utilizing the same protocol with the following modifications: where applicable, an SNP’s reference SNP cluster (rs)ID no. was used to retrieve chromosome and position, and SNPs with missing coordinates or located in noncanonical or mitochondrial chromosomes were removed. Missing allelic information was backfilled using dbSNP, and effect and noneffect alleles were aligned to match ALT and REF alleles, respectively, and GWAS association P values and effect-size estimates were extracted if available. Furthermore, to adjust the genome reference assembly and align all GWAS datasets to GRCh38, we utilized the LiftOver tool99 for datasets that were provided in a genome build different from GRCh38. As a final step, we calculated the fraction of recovered and curated SNPs for each GWAS dataset to validate the quality of our processing pipeline (mean: 99.5%; median: 100%). In total, of the downloaded and processed 1,749 GWAS datasets (from 745 studies), we recovered and curated GWAS information for 1,423 datasets (from 645 studies), which were employed, if suitable, in our different downstream analyses (Supplementary Table 8). To identify GWAS loci in each dataset, we used a window size of 500 kb upstream and downstream of each genome-wide significant genetic variant (GWAS association P < 5 × 10−8) and merged all overlapping regions, defining independent GWAS loci as associated regions (containing one or more genome-wide significant genetic variants) located at least 1 Mb away from each other; counts of GWAS loci and associated eGenes are provided in Supplementary Table 8.

Enrichment of disease-risk variants in chromatin accessibility sites

We utilized GARFIELD (v2)100 to investigate the enrichment patterns of disease-risk variants in chromatin accessibility sites, identified by ATAC–seq analysis in immune cell populations isolated from human lung tissue. The GARFIELD software evaluates functional enrichment using a logistic regression model that accounts for allele frequency, distance to the TSS of the nearest gene and the number of LD proxies (r2 > 0.8; correlation based on the UK10K dataset provided by the software) to extract a set of independent variants and annotate those to a known cis-regulatory element. In brief, we combined a total of 711 GWAS datasets with chromatin accessibility sites identified by ATAC–seq in our immune cell subsets and chromatin accessibility data of human tissues previously published in refs. 12–14. To reduce selection bias and improve reproducibility, we analyzed multiple GWAS datasets for each disease or trait, thus minimizing inconsistencies in the results (Supplementary Table 8). In brief, we combined relevant GWAS datasets for various diseases or traits with chromatin accessibility sites identified by ATAC–seq in our five main immune cell populations (pooled from specific immune cell subsets to avoid technical noise due to limited cell counts) and chromatin accessibility data of human cell populations isolated from fetal lung tissue12, adult lung tissue13 and brain tissue14. A representative visualization plot is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2a; the corresponding list of utilized scATAC–seq datasets is provided in Supplementary Table 6f. Across the 102 ATAC–seq datasets utilized in this analysis, odds ratios (OR) for enrichment were calculated at the GWAS P value thresholds 1 × 10−1 to 1 × 10−8 and adjusted for multiple tests using Bonferroni’s corrected P values to identify significant enrichment patterns. Results from multiple GWAS datasets for each disease or trait are summarized by displaying median OR values in bar plots at each respective GWAS P-value threshold (1 × 10−1 to 1 × 10−8) grouped for each cell or tissue type. Results are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 6g.

Complex trait associations of DICE cis-eQTLs utilizing colocalization analysis

To examine the possibility of disease-associated genetic variants influencing gene expression in immune cells as a potential mechanism underlying disease pathogenesis, we performed analyses to link cis-eQTLs associated with gene expression in our immune cell subsets (n = 29) and genetic variants associated with disease risk from the curated GWAS datasets to prioritize candidate genes and pinpoint immune cell populations relevant to disease pathogenesis.

As low-density SNP coverage in GWAS datasets dampens the discovery power of our gene prioritization analyses20,101, we estimated the LD of genome-wide significant genetic variants (GWAS association P < 5 × 10−8) in all GWAS datasets with <5 million total SNPs. In brief, we used Plink (v1.9)92 to calculate LD based on the European (EUR) superpopulation data in the 1,000 Genomes Project89 and retrieved significant GWAS variants (lead SNPs) and SNPs in tight LD (r2 > 0.8) from a respective GWAS dataset. To determine whether the association of GWAS variants with disease phenotypes is mediated through regulation of gene expression by a genetic variant (cis-eQTL), we employed colocalization analysis using the COLOC framework102,103. COLOC analysis estimates the posterior probability that, in a given gene locus, two related phenotypes—GWAS signals and cis-eQTLs associated with gene expression—share the same causal genetic variant(s). The COLOC framework computes approximate Bayes factors for association with each trait and integrates prior probabilities to evaluate posterior support across five competing hypotheses: no causal variant for either trait (H0), a causal variant affecting only the GWAS trait (H1), a causal variant affecting only gene expression (H2), two distinct causal variants independently affecting each trait (H3) or a single shared causal variant affecting both traits (H4). Notably, the discovery using the COLOC framework is limited in complex gene loci with multiple independent signals. To reduce this bias in our COLOC analysis101 and to maximize the number of potentially independent signals in each gene locus, we partitioned the input datasets into LD-independent haploblocks utilizing the estimated genetic structure of CEU ancestry (EUR) as reference104. To this end, we converted the CEU dataset to GRCh38 utilizing the LiftOver tool99. For each GWAS dataset, we extracted the case–control ratio and retrieved the available SNP-specific summary statistics (effect size estimate (β), standard error of β, GWAS association P value and MAF). Subsequently, we divided all datasets into LD-independent haploblocks and utilized all haploblocks containing at least one significant GWAS variant (GWAS association P < 5 × 10−8) or cis-eQTL (Padj < 0.05) for our COLOC analysis. For each tested eGene–haploblock pair, both GWAS summary statistics and cis-eQTL data were applied to the coloc.abf function of the COLOC package to estimate the posterior probability of a single causal variant being shared between disease risk and gene expression20,101.

Notably, most LD-independent haploblocks with colocalizing cis-eQTL signals were associated with a single eGene (Supplementary Fig. 12a,b). The prior probability (p12) of a genetic variant being associated with both GWAS phenotype and gene expression was determined by sensitivity analysis, as recommended103, and set at 1 × 10−5 to maximize discovery (Supplementary Fig. 12c). For several genes and GWAS datasets highlighted in the paper, we examined sensitivity plots to estimate the minimum p12 prior required to support evidence of colocalization for both new and well-established disease-associated genes such as ORMDL3 and GSDMB for asthma (Supplementary Fig. 12c–f). Highly stringent priors (p12 = 1 × 10−6 or 1 × 10−7) reduced the power to detect even well-established asthma risk genes such as ORMDL3 and GSDMB in certain immune cell subsets or GWAS. For example, the well-established asthma-risk gene ORMDL3 showed colocalization signals that remained robust even under highly stringent prior values in all but one of the asthma GWAS tested, including the largest asthma GWAS meta-analysis73 (Supplementary Fig. 12c,d). In contrast, the other well-established asthma-risk gene, GSDMB, was more sensitive to prior probability threshold, with asthma associations only detected when p12 values were in the range of 1 × 10−5 to 1 × 10−6; a highly stringent prior probability of 1 × 10−6 detects colocalized signals only in a few of the asthma GWAS studies in CD8+ TRM cells while missing associations with other immune cell types (Supplementary Fig. 12c,e). Therefore, to avoid missing potentially important colocalized genes, we reported all colocalized genes with a prior probability (p12) = 1 × 10−5. In addition, results derived from conservative prior probabilities (p12 = 1 × 10−6 or 1 × 10−7) are included for all colocalized genes (Supplementary Table 9a). An independent colocalization test was performed for each immune cell population (n = 29) and each GWAS summary statistic. An eGene was said to have evidence of colocalization when the posterior probability of colocalization of a GWAS variant and a cis-eQTL linked to the eGene (PP4) was >0.5 and the PP4-to-PP3 ratio was ≥5 (refs. 20,101). For a given eGene, we selected the haploblock with the strongest colocalization signal (PP4) to summarize the results in Supplementary Table 9a.

All heatmaps providing results from our colocalization analysis show summary data after summarizing the results from multiple GWAS datasets for each disease or trait at the gene level, displaying the results for the strongest association. Similarly, results from single immune cell subsets (n = 29) were collapsed into the respective major immune cell population (n = 5), displaying the results for the strongest association. The Padj values of cis-eQTLs linked to expression and defined risk alleles were retrieved from the respective immune cell subset and GWAS dataset. The results of independent colocalization analyses for each suitable GWAS dataset (n = 690) and each immune cell subset (n = 29) are reported for every colocalized gene with a disease or trait in any GWAS dataset in Supplementary Table 9a.

Colocalization of GWAS signals between two diseases or traits

To explore whether eGenes shared between two diseases or traits also share the same genetic signals, we performed independent colocalization analyses of the GWAS signals (haploblocks) between the two diseases or traits from relevant GWAS datasets (harboring at least one variant with PGWAS < 5 ×10−8). We calculated the fraction of GWAS signals (haploblocks) that harbor colocalized genetic variants in pairwise comparisons (Extended Data Fig. 9b). For every colocalized eGene (shared between two diseases or traits), we assessed whether the underlying GWAS signals are also shared (colocalized) between the two diseases or traits (Supplementary Table 9b).

Heritability analysis

To quantify genetic effects on disease mediated by cis-eQTLs modulating gene expression in lung immune cell subsets, we utilized MESC3 to explain the enrichment and overlap between GWAS loci and cis-eQTLs and, to estimate expression-mediated disease heritability. For 32 diseases or traits, we utilized GWAS datasets with a minimum of 100 significant variants that also included relevant input data required for MESC analysis (Supplementary Table 8). We performed MESC analysis using default parameters, as described3, on GWAS and cis-eQTL summary statistics generated for lung immune cell subsets, circulating immune cell types (OneK1K study21, processed by the EMBL-EBI eQTL Catalogue23) and selected tissues (GTEx Consortium6). Studies that yielded a total heritability (h2g) <0 or >1 in GTEx lung tissue were excluded from further analysis. Results from relevant GWAS studies (n = 51) for each cell type or tissue are displayed in Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11a.

We applied single-group cTWAS41 to jointly model variant-level and gene-mediated effects by integrating GWAS summary statistics with expression prediction models41,105. cTWAS was run using default parameters unless otherwise specified, including both protein-coding and noncoding genes. GWAS summary statistics were pre-processed as described above. Variant identifiers were harmonized across GWAS summary statistics, LD reference panels and expression prediction models. LD was estimated using publicly available UK Biobank LD panels (hg38). cTWAS was applied using prediction models of GTEx v8 bulk eQTL datasets for selected tissues106–108. In addition, prediction models were trained on our datasets using previously described methods101. Briefly, PrediXcan was applied with minor modifications to generate prediction models for each lung immune cell subset. Models were trained on normalized pseudo-bulk expression matrices and genes expressed in <5% of donors and 10% of cells were excluded. Prediction models were restricted to protein-coding and long noncoding RNA genes. Expression levels were adjusted for standard covariates, including PCs and PEER factors. Elastic net models were trained using variants within ±1 Mb of each gene and models were retained based on tenfold crossvalidation performance (correlation >0.1 and P < 0.05). Gene-level z-scores were computed using compute_gene_z by integrating GWAS z-scores with expression prediction weights. A minimum of 100 genes per GWAS was retained to ensure adequate power for downstream analyses. Gene and variant data were aggregated at the haplotype block level. Prior inclusion probabilities and group prior variance were estimated using an expectation-maximization algorithm, with the variance structure set to shared_all to allow shared variance across genes and SNPs. The maximum number of iterations was increased from 50 to 150 to ensure convergence across tissue–trait combinations. Estimated hyperparameters were used for heritability estimation.

Heritability was estimated using the proportion of variance explained (PVE) implemented in summarize_param. Group-level PVE was estimated as follows: PVE (group) = (τ2 × π × m)/N, where τ2 is the group prior variance, π the prior inclusion probability, m the number of predictors in the group (group being gene or SNP) and N the GWAS sample size. The total PVE was defined as the sum across all groups: PVE (total) = ∑PVE (group). The proportion of heritability mediated by gene expression was defined as: gene PVE/total PVE. The results are displayed in Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11b.

Analysis to identify TF motifs perturbed by genetic variants

Genetic variants associated with disease risk were prioritized as potentially functional based on their overlap with cis-regulatory elements in the current dataset and crossvalidation, using previously published data of the DICE project4,20,72,101. The TF motifs perturbed by genetic variants were obtained from the HaploReg v4.2 database109 and the position weight matrix of the respective TF motifs was retrieved from ENCODE110 and JASPAR111. Public ChIP–seq datasets were retrieved from ENCODE112: ZNF219 in Hep G2 cells (ENCSR243BPI_ENCFF081BFL); YY1 in K562 cells (ENCSR000BKU_ENCFF674DGV); EGR1 in K562 cells (ENCSR000BNE_ENCFF822TXT).

Validation of gene function in immune cells

Knockdown studies utilizing anti-sense oligonucleotides (ASO; AUM BioTech) were performed on stimulation of MDMs generated in vitro from CD14+ monocytes of six healthy individuals with confirmed ZFP57 expression4. In brief, CD14+ monocytes were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells by MACS using the Classical Monocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and cultured with macrophage–colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; 100 ng ml−1) in the presence of AUMsilence ZFP57 ASO (pool of four independent molecules each targeting ZFP57 messenger RNA) or nontargeting AUMscramble ASO (utilized with 2.0 µM) for 10 d. The culture medium (RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% GlutaMAX, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% nonessential amino acids and 1% penicillin–streptomycin) and M-CSF were replenished on days 3, 5 and 7 without disturbing the cells (Supplementary Fig. 10a). On day 4, knockdown efficiency was analyzed by real-time PCR for transcript levels of ZFP57, using YHWAZ as the housekeeping gene. Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN); cDNA was reverse transcribed with the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Life Technologies); real-time PCR was employed using the Fast Start Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche); data were acquired on a QuantStudio 6 Flex (Applied Biosystems); all results are presented in arbitrary units relative to the expression of YWHAZ as the housekeeping gene (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 13. On day 10, MDMs were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (0.8 µg ml−1) for 6 h, then washed, harvested and prepared in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Micro Kit and SMART-seq2 library preparation was performed as previously described113. The RNA-seq libraries were quantified, pooled in equivalent molar concentrations and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq X sequencing platform. All libraries were sequenced to reach a minimum of 20 million 150 × 150-bp pair-ended sequencing reads and RNA reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38 with STAR v2.7.1a114. Differentially expressed genes in stimulated MDMs on treatment with AUMsilence ZFP57 ASO pools (knockdown condition) or nontargeting AUMscramble ASO (control condition) were identified by independent pairwise comparisons using DESeq2115 and transcripts were considered as differentially expressed with a baseMean >100, a Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected Padj < 0.05 and log2(fold-change) > 0.5. The results are provided in Supplementary Table 12.

Gene annotation enrichment analysis

Gene annotation enrichment analysis was performed through two independent methods provided by ConsensusPathDB (CPDB; tool’s web release v35) and Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; tool’s web release v6.8). For both tools, the analysis was carried out with default settings.

Quantification, statistical analysis and data display

Processing of data, applied methods and codes are described in the respective sections in Methods. The number of individuals, samples and replicates analyzed and the statistical tests performed are indicated in the figure legends. GraphPad Prism software (v10.2.3) was used for generating graphs and performing statistical significance tests. BED tracks, ATAC–seq peaks, ChIP–seq peaks and HiChIP interactions were visualized using the UCSC genome browser and Washington University epigenome browser. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments and randomization was not performed. Unless otherwise stated, data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, but our sample sizes were similar to those generally used in eQTL studies, including previous publications4,20. Any samples failing established QCs were eliminated from downstream analysis; assay details and QCs on scRNA-seq and ATAC–seq samples are provided in Supplementary Tables 3 and 6, respectively. Single-cell differential gene expression analysis was performed using the package MAST in R (v1.12)81. A gene was considered as differentially expressed utilizing the threshold Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected Padj < 0.05 and log2(fold-change) > 0.25. For marker identification of clusters, differential gene expression analysis was applied through the function FindAllMarkers from Seurat (v3.2.2)75. To call sex-biased transcripts in each immune cell cluster, multiple comparisons were made (one per cluster) between biological sexes, where the age of the study individuals was considered as a covariate. To identify cis-eQTLs, the full set of SNPs with MAF ≥ 0.05 (measured and imputed) was tested for association with the expression of sequenced genes using the Matrix eQTL package v2.1.195. For the determination of cis-acting eQTLs, affected transcripts (eGenes) and their comparison across cell populations, the following thresholds were applied: raw P < 0.0001, Padj (FDR) < 0.05 for transcripts expressed in >1% of cells or >5% of individuals in this study. Utilizing the results from SuSiE for fine-mapped eGenes, a single representative ‘peak cis-eQTL’ from a credible set for each eGene was chosen for a given transcript, prioritized for proximity or overlap with ATAC–seq peaks in the respective immune cell population and the predicted modulation of a relevant TF-binding motif utilizing the HaploReg v4.2 database109.
