Longitudinal investigation of the HbA1c/HDL-c ratio as a potential biomarker of cognitive decline among Chinese older adults: evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Jianzhun Chen, Liuhui Zhu, Jie Liu, Jieyu Chen, Chunyu Liang, Chenxi Liu, Fang Wang, Yangfan Zhu, Xinglong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCognitive impairment has become a critical public health challenge in China's aging population, with growing evidence linking its progression to dysregulated glycolipid metabolism. The hemoglobin A1c to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HbA1c/HDL-C) ratio, a novel composite biomarker reflecting integrated glycolipid homeostasis, has recently demonstrated prognostic value in metabolic disorders. Based on these insights, this population-based study leverages longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline in older Chinese adults.MethodWe included 813 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, who had complete data on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and cognitive function at baseline. The HbA1c/HDL-C ratio was calculated as the ratio of HbA1c (%) to HDL-C (mmol/L). Cognitive decline was defined as a decrease in the global cognitive score over time, assessed using a validated cognitive battery. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and incident cognitive decline. Sensitivity analyses included generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) and restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to explore potential non-linear relationships. Subgroup and RCS analyses were also performed on HDL-C levels to further investigate underlying mechanisms.ResultsAfter adjusting for various risk factors, our analysis revealed a significant association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and an increased risk of cognitive decline in individuals aged 60 and older. Compared to the Q1 group, the highest hazard ratio (HR) was observed in the Q2 group (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.16–2.02, P = 0.002). The HR was 1.36 (95% CI: 1.03–1.81, P = 0.029) for the Q3 group versus Q1, and 1.40 (95% CI: 1.05–1.88, P = 0.024) for the Q4 group versus Q1. Results from the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) were consistent with those from the Cox regression, supporting the primary findings.Furthermore, Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) regression analysis indicated a non-linear relationship between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline, with an inflection point at a ratio of 2.06. Specifically, ratios below this threshold were associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline (HR: 2.31, 95% CI 1.32–4.04, P = 0.003), whereas the predictive ability was attenuated when the ratio exceeded this value (P = 0.269). Additional analysis suggested that this non-linearity may originate from a non-linear association between HDL-C and cognitive decline.ConclusionOur findings indicate a significant yet nonlinear relationship between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and the incidence of cognitive decline in individuals aged 60 and above. This nonlinearity may stem from a nonlinear relationship between HDL-C and cognitive decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25367-4.Keywords: HbA1c/HDL-c ratio, cognitive decline, CHARLS

Introduction

Between 1980 and 2020, China underwent profound demographic shifts characterized by rapid urbanization, large-scale internal migration of working-age adults from rural to urban areas, and a sustained increase in the older population. Over these four decades, the shares of urban residents, internal migrants, and adults aged 60 years or older rose sharply—from 19.4% to 63.9%, from virtually zero to 20.2%, and from 6.9% to 18.7%, respectively. By 2020, the corresponding population sizes had reached 902 million urban dwellers, 285.6 million internal migrants, and 264 million older adults.1–6 This dramatic transformation in the age structure and distribution of the world’s second most populous country has created substantial challenges for safeguarding the health and well-being of older Chinese [1].

Cognitive decline, characterized by progressive deterioration in core cognitive domains such as comprehension, attention, and executive function, poses a substantial global health burden, particularly in aging populations [2].This neurocognitive continuum often progresses to debilitating outcomes such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, with Mortality associated with dementia is 2.3 times higher than in cognitively healthy individuals [3]. Recent epidemiological data reveal alarming rates in China, where 6.04% of adults aged ≥ 60 years meet dementia criteria, while 15.54% exhibit MCI manifestations [4]. The growing elderly demographic has driven an annualized 5.6% increase in dementia incidence since 2015, imposing unsustainable strain on healthcare infrastructure and long-term care systems [5].

Mounting evidence implicates diabetes-related phenotypes—particularly chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance—as modifiable risk factors that amplify dementia pathogenesis [6].Concurrently,dyslipidemia patterns characterized by depressed high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) alongside elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triglycerides (TG) have demonstrated significant neurovascular toxicity, mediated through pro-atherogenic and neuroinflammatory pathways [7].Therapeutically, HDL-C exerts atheroprotective effects via reverse cholesterol transport from peripheral tissues to hepatic clearance sites, as well as anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacities that mitigate cerebrovascular endothelial dysfunction. [8]Beyond lipid homeostasis, the clinical relevance of integrated biomarkers continues to expand. Emerging evidence supports the (HbA1c/HDL-C as a novel predictor of multi-system morbidity, reflecting synergistic dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism [9]. For example, an analysis based on NHANES reported a 1.32-fold increase in cardiovascular mortality risk (95% CI: 1.14–1.53) per standard deviation increase in HbA1c/HDL-C among cancer survivors [10]. Similarly, studies using the CHARLS cohort have established significant associations between elevated HbA1c/HDL-C ratios and incident stroke, as well as progression of carotid atherosclerosis [11, 12].Given the established link between metabolic dysregulation and neurodegeneration, we hypothesize that the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio may serve as a composite biomarker for predicting cognitive trajectory.. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a longitudinal analysis using the CHARLS database, employing standardized neurocognitive assessments and rigorous covariate adjustment to examine this relationship in older Chinese adults.

Methods

Study design and population

Our study utilized data from the CHARLS cohort, a large—scale, prospective national cohort that includes individuals aged 45 and older [13]. Through a multi—stage, stratified probability sampling design, CHARLS achieved national representativeness by randomly selecting participants from 450 communities across 150 counties in 28 provinces of China.Baseline data was collected from June 2011 to March 2012 (Wave 1) by trained staff via face—to—face interviews. Socio—demographic factors and health status were assessed biennially using a standardized questionnaire.Initially, Wave 1 recruited 17,708 participants, with follow—ups conducted every two years: Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, Wave 4 in 2018, and Wave 5 in 2020.

Cohort selection and criteria

This study employed a cohort design, using data from participants initially enrolled in 2011 as baseline. We included individuals aged 60 who had no history of memory—related diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or Parkinson’s disease (PD). Further exclusion was carried out based on the following criteria:

missing data on HbA1c HDL—C LDL-C or CRP;

missing data on HbA1c HDL—C LDL-C or CRP;

lack of information regarding diabetes,hypertension stroke,heart diease history;

lack of information regarding diabetes,hypertension stroke,heart diease history;

missing details on age, education, smoking, marital and reproductive status, or alcohol consumption history;

missing details on age, education, smoking, marital and reproductive status, or alcohol consumption history;

exclusion of those who did not complete all of the cognitive function tests.

exclusion of those who did not complete all of the cognitive function tests.

After these inclusion and exclusion steps, 1,426 participants were left for subsequent analysis.Among the follow—up cohort, due to the extensive cognitive tests, some participants missed certain tests each year. We ultimately decided to exclude those who lacked the relevant cognitive tests in all four subsequent waves. Consequently, 813 elderly individuals were determined as the final cohort for this analysis. The detailed design process is presented in Fig. 1.

Study design

Exposure assessment

All participants had fasted overnight for at least 8 h before trained medical staff collected fasting venous blood samples to ensure the accuracy of metabolic measurements. The blood samples were then centrifuged and frozen at −20 °C. Within two weeks, they were transported to the central laboratory in Beijing for analysis. Upon arrival, the samples were stored in a deep freezer at −80 °C until testing. HbA1c levels were measured using borate affinity high—performance liquid chromatography, while HDL—C levels were determined by enzymatic colorimetry [11].

Outcome assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using methods adapted from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) in the US [14]. Participants completed face—to—face evaluations across four cognitive domains: orientation, memory, calculation, and drawing.Orientation and calculation were assessed via the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS). The orientation section included stating the year, month, date, day of the week, and current season, with a maximum score of 5 (1 point per correct item). For calculation, participants serially subtracted 7 from 100 five times, earning 1 point for each correct answer, with a maximum of 5.Immediate word recall was tested by presenting 10 random words and counting how many the participant could recall immediately. Delayed word recall was assessed after the participant completed surveys on depression, calculation, and drawing. The memory score, the sum of immediate and delayed word recall, had a maximum of 20 (1 point per word).

Drawing ability was tested by asking participants to copy a picture of two overlapping pentagons, with 1 point awarded for a correct copy. The total cognitive score was the sum of orientation (5), calculation (5), memory (20), and drawing (1), with a maximum of 31 [15, 16].Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers at participants’ homes or local community centers [17].

Cognitive decline was defined as a reduction of ≥ 3 points in the cognitive score at any follow-up assessment relative to the baseline cognitive score.. Participants with a cognitive score below 3 in 2011 were excluded from the analysis [18, 19].

Covariate assessment

Our study considered demographics, anthropometrics, behavioral factors, health conditions, and laboratory test results as potential covariates in the HbA1c/HDL-C and cognitive function association.Demographic variables included age, LDL-C,gender (female/male), education (primary school or below, middle school or high school, college or university) [20], marital status (married/others), and residence (rural/urban).Behavioral factors covered smoking (no/yes, indicating never smokers vs. former/current smokers) and alcohol consumption (no/yes, based on drinking in the past year) [21].

Health conditions included hypertension,stroke,heart diease,and diabetes. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg, or a physician diagnosis/current antihypertensive use. Diabetes was diagnosed based on FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a physician diagnosis, or current hypoglycemic drug use [22].

Statistical analysis

We first described the baseline characteristics of each group. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests.

To evaluate the association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline, we divided participants into four groups based on quartiles of the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and performed Cox regression analysis, using follow-up time as the timescale to calculate HR and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). As a component of the sensitivity analyses, GLMMs were additionally implemented to test the putative association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline. The GLMMs yielded odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CIs for this exposure–outcome relationship [23].We applied three models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, education, residence,marital status,smoking,drinking behaviors), Model 3 (adjusted for hypertension, diabetes,stroke,heart diease,LDL-C and CRP based on Model 2),.

To examine the potential non-linear relationship between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline, we used multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to visualize the dose—response relationship. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were applied to determine the optimal number of knots, with 4 knots chosen based on the lowest AIC and BIC values in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Our analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline. To further explore this, we conducted RCS analysis of HDL-C and cognitive decline, and performed subgroup Cox regression analyses.All statistical analyses were completed using R software version 4.4.3 (http://www.R-project.org). A p-value < 0.05 was recognized as reaching statistical significance (two-sided).

Discussion

Based on our findings, the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio is a strong predictor of cognitive decline risk in the Chinese population. When the ratio was categorized into quartiles, individuals in the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups showed a significantly elevated risk of cognitive decline compared to those in the Q1 group. Furthermore, the predictive power of the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio was particularly strong when the ratio was ≤ 2.06. We also explored the potential mechanism behind this non-linear relationship and found that it may be partly explained by the association between HDL-C and cognitive function. Although the ratio shows promise, its utility as a formal screening tool requires future studies specifically designed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy, such as calculating sensitivity and specificity against clinical diagnoses..

HbA1c, which reflects the glycated fraction of hemoglobin, directly correlates with blood glucose metabolism, as its synthesis rate is intrinsically linked to the glucose concentration surrounding red blood cells. Hyperglycemia can enhance glucose permeability in the brain, leading to increased intracellular and extracellular glucose levels in regions such as the midbrain and striatum. This, in turn, induces neuronal glucotoxicity through various mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and the accumulation of methylglyoxal (MGO), a precursor of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), all of which contribute to cognitive decline and dementia [24]. Studies have also established a connection between elevated HbA1c levels and structural/functional brain alterations associated with cognitive impairment, as well as with Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers such as tau protein [25, 26] Conversely, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) primarily functions in reverse cholesterol transport, facilitating the movement of cholesterol from peripheral tissues, including vascular walls, back to the liver for metabolism, thereby promoting lipid homeostasis. HDL-C also exerts protective effects by counteracting vascular endothelial damage induced by hyperglycemia, enhancing insulin sensitivity, improving pancreatic β-cell function, reducing the risk of diabetic vascular complications, and alleviating neuroinflammation, all of which contribute to a reduced risk of cognitive impairment [27, 28]. However, the relationship between HDL-C and cognitive decline appears to follow a U-shaped rather than a linear pattern [29, 30]. Subgroup sensitivity analyses further corroborate the reliability of these findings. Although research on the correlation between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognition is currently limited, the potential of this ratio as a biomarker in other diseases, coupled with the established roles of HbA1c and HDL-C in cognitive function, provides a compelling rationale for further investigation into its association with cognitive decline.

Our study found that compared to the Q1 group, the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups all showed hazard ratios (HRs) greater than 1 with statistically significant p-values, indicating elevated risks of cognitive decline. However, the HRs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 did not increase progressively, suggesting a non-linear relationship—contrary to our initial hypothesis of a linear association.

Given that diabetes is an established independent risk factor for cognitive impairment and HbA1c is a key diagnostic marker for diabetes, higher HbA1c levels would theoretically predict a greater likelihood of cognitive decline. Conversely, higher levels of HDL-C—often termed "good cholesterol"—are associated with a lower probability of cognitive decline [31]. Thus, a higher HbA1c/HDL-C ratio would be expected to correlate with increased risk. However, our results revealed a non-linear relationship between the ratio and cognitive decline.

To explore this, we conducted subgroup analyses by dichotomizing the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and performing RCS regression. Subsequent Cox and RCS regression analyses on HDL-C revealed that both HDL-C and the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio were strongly associated with cognitive decline only when HDL-C levels exceeded 40 mg/dl. Below this threshold, the association was no longer significant. This finding explains why the HR was highest in the Q2 group compared to Q1 and decreased thereafter. As the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio increases (reflecting a decrease in HDL-C), once HDL-C falls below 40 mg/dl, the risk of cognitive decline does not continue to rise with further reductions in HDL-C, resulting in a non-linear trend in HRs with increasing ratio values.

As shown in Table 5, HDL-C was found to be negatively correlated with cognitive decline solely when HDL-C levels were above 40 mg/dl. This correlation was not significant when HDL-C levels were below 40 mg/dl. We posit the following reasons for this observation. First, pharmacotherapy may be a contributing factor. Low HDL-C levels indicate dyslipidemia, which often necessitates medication. Certain treatments might alter HDL's composition or function. For instance, some lipid-lowering drugs can elevate HDL-C levels but may not significantly enhance their functionality [30]. Secondly, when HDL-C levels are low, the composition of HDL particles may undergo changes. For example, there may be an increase in triglycerides on the surface of HDL particles, accompanied by a reduction in lysophosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin. When HDL-C composition changes in this manner, its normal functions may be compromised [32].One studies utilizing the NHANES database investigated the relationship between the HbA1c/HDL-C/HDL-C ratio and Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), revealing a non-linear relationship [33]. However, they did not examine HDL-C's direct relationship with these outcomes. Our study provides a more in-depth exploration of the non-linear association between HDL-C and cognitive decline..

In conclusion

Leveraging the well-established CHARLS cohort, our research established a positive correlation between both baseline and cumulative HbA1c/HDL-C levels and the risk of cognitive decline in individuals aged 60 and older. Our findings suggest a potential nonlinear association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and the risk of cognitive decline in older adults. This discovery has implications for clinical and public health practice: firstly, this ratio could potentially serve as a biomarker for assessing the risk of cognitive decline in the elderly; secondly, early screening and targeted interventions could be considered for older adults with specific metabolic characteristics, such as a high HbA1c/HDL-C ratio, to delay the process of cognitive decline.A key implication of our findings is the generation of the hypothesis that interventions aimed at lowering the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio (e.g., via improved glycemic control or raised HDL-C) might reduce the risk of cognitive decline. However, we clearly state this remains speculative and must be tested in future interventional or randomized controlled trials..Notably, this predictive advantage is more pronounced when the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio is below 2.06. These findings carry substantial public health implications, providing valuable insights for enhancing cognitive decline risk assessment and informing early prevention strategies.Our current study focused on global cognitive decline. We now state that investigating the association of the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio with specific cognitive domains (e.g., memory, executive function) and its ability to predict dementia incidence are crucial next steps.

Limitation

This study has several limitations.

First, although we employed the Cox proportional hazards model to examine the prospective association between the baseline HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and the time to the first occurrence of cognitive decline, and the results from the sensitivity analysis using the generalized linear mixed model were consistent, it must be acknowledged that the Cox model based on a single baseline measurement cannot capture the dynamic changes in exposure and covariates during follow-up. Limited by the database structure, exposure variables were only collected at baseline and in 2015, lacking consecutive annual measurements; therefore, we were unable to construct high-quality time-varying covariate models (such as time-dependent Cox models or lagged variable models), which might lead to biased estimation of the associations. Second, cognitive decline was defined as a reduction of ≥ 3 points in the total cognitive score from baseline at any follow-up visit. While this fixed threshold is widely used in similar studies, it may not fully account for individual differences in baseline cognitive ability or ceiling/floor effects, and its sensitivity to changes in cognitive trajectory might be limited.

Third, there was significant potential for selection bias in the study sample: out of an initial 17,708 CHARLS participants, only 816 (4.6%) were included in the final analysis, primarily due to missing cognitive test and baseline data. This high exclusion rate might result in a sample over-representing individuals with better health literacy and follow-up compliance, thereby potentially skewing the estimated cognitive trajectories towards a more "stable" pattern and limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader elderly population.

Fourth, although we adjusted for various demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and clinical comorbidities (including stroke, heart disease, and CRP), there remains the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as dietary patterns, physical activity levels, other components of metabolic syndrome, markers of oxidative stress, and medication use (e.g., statins or glucose-lowering drugs).

Fifth, during the extended follow-up, loss to follow-up and death as a competing risk could affect the interpretation of the results. Although the lost-to-follow-up population and the retained population showed no significant differences in most baseline characteristics (except for age), loss to follow-up might still introduce selection bias. Concurrently, this study did not use competing risk models (such as the Fine-Gray model) to account for the impact of death on the risk of incident cognitive decline, potentially leading to an overestimation of the cumulative incidence.

Sixth, the suggested non-linear association between the HbA1c/HDL-C ratio and cognitive decline and its inflection point (2.06), identified through restricted cubic spline analysis, require validation in an independent external cohort; the reliability of its clinical utility remains to be further confirmed.

Finally, as the study population consisted of Chinese adults aged ≥ 60 years, the generalizability of the findings to younger populations or different ethnic groups should be approached with caution.
