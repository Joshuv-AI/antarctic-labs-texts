Cardiovascular Diseases and All‐Cause Mortality Risk in Patients With Prehypertension and Noncommunicable Diseases: Results From the Kailuan Study
Authors: Ruiying Zhang, Liuxin Li, Yanxiu Wang, Junjuan Li, Chunyu Ruan, Shouling Wu, Shuohua Chen, Renling Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIt remains unclear whether prehypertension combined with noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) is associate with higher risks of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and all‐cause mortality. The study aimed to investigate the associations of prehypertension with CVD and all‐cause mortality in individuals with or without NCDs. The prospective cohort study included 56 114 participants from the Kailuan Study. Participants were divided into four groups: optimal blood pressure (BP) without NCDs, prehypertension without NCDs, optimal BP with NCDs and prehypertension with NCDs. CVD events and all‐cause mortality were followed up until December 31, 2018. Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to assess the associations of prehypertension with the risk of CVD and all‐cause mortality among patients with or without NCDs. Sensitivity and stratified analysis were performed to verify the robustness of the results. During a median follow‐up of 9.04 years, 3367 CVD events and 5754 all‐cause mortality had occurred. Compared with optimal BP alone, the multivariable adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for CVD were 1.36(1.23–1.51) for prehypertension alone, 1.82(1.55–2.14) for optimal BP and NCDs and 2.33(2.10–2.58) for prehypertension and NCDs (all P < 0.001). For all‐cause mortality, the corresponding HRs (95%CI) were 1.13(1.04–1.21), 2.07(1.85–2.32) and 1.85(1.72–2.00) (all P < 0.001). Results were consistent in sensitivity and stratified analysis. Notably, females with prehypertension and NCDs showed the highest all‐cause mortality risk. Prehypertension significantly increased CVD risk in patients with NCDs. However, a paradoxical all‐cause mortality association was seen, with higher risk among NCDs patients with optimal BP versus prehypertension.Keywords: all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular diseases, noncommunicable diseases, prehypertension

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of mortality globally [1]. The global Burden of CVD 1990 to 2023 estimated nearly 626 million prevalent CVD cases and 19.2 million CVD‐attributed deaths [1]. Prehypertension (blood pressure 120–139/80–89 mmHg) affects 25%–50% of adults worldwide, with a prevalence of 41.3% among Chinese adults aged ≥ 18 years [2, 3, 4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that prehypertension significantly increases the risk of total CVD [5], stroke [6], coronary heart disease (CHD) [7], myocardial infarction (MI) [8] and mortality [9, 10]. However, inconsistent findings persist. A prospective Chinese rural cohort study reported higher risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), CVD death, and stroke, but not MI in prehypertensive versus normotensive individuals [11]. A well‐designed meta‐analysis identified prehypertension as a predictor for cardiovascular mortality but not all‐cause mortality [12]. A recent retrospective cohort study demonstrated that prehypertension increased the risks of CVD mortality and all‐cause mortality by 1.85 fold and 1.09 fold, respectively [10].

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) such as CVD, chronic respiratory diseases, cancer, obesity, diabetes mellitus and mental illness are collectively responsible for almost 75% of non‐pandemic‐related deaths globally [13]. NCDs disproportionately affect people in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where nearly three quarters of global NCDs deaths and 82% of premature deaths occur [13]. CVD are responsible for the majority of NCDs‐related deaths accounting for 44% (at least 19 million) of all these deaths [14]. Beyond traditional CVD risk factors, other NCDs including chronic respiratory symptoms [15], cancer [16, 17], obesity [18], diabetes mellitus [19] and mental illness [20] have also been confirmed to be associated with the risk of both CVD and mortality.

Beyond these associations, elevated BP itself is a major contributor to NCDs burden. Raised BP contributes significantly to NCDs, accounting for 13% of the leading risk factors in order of magnitude [21]. A recent study further demonstrated that the prevalence of NCDs (diabetes mellitus, heart disease, stroke, and memory‐related disease) increases with ascending BP level [22]. Given the high burden of NCDs and NCDs potential interplay with blood pressure, it is plausible that prehypertension may exert differential effects on cardiovascular outcomes and mortality depending on the presence or absence of NCDs. Some studies have shown increased CVD risk in patients with prehypertension combined with metabolic syndrome [23] or diabetes mellitus [24], few have explored whether prehypertension is associated with elevated CVD or all‐cause mortality risk in patients with other NCDs (e.g., chronic respiratory symptoms, cancer, liver disease, kidney disease, and mental illness). Our previous Kailuan study found that diabetes mellitus modified the association of prehypertension with CVD and all‐cause mortality [25], however, whether such modification extends to other NCDs remains unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association of prehypertension with CVD and all‐cause mortality in individuals with and without NCDs, using data from the Kailuan Study.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The Kailuan Study is an ongoing prospective cohort study(trial registration #: ChiCTR‐TNC‐11001489) initiated in 2006 within the Kailuan community of Tangshan city, a large modern littoral city southeast of Beijing, China, aiming to investigate risk factors associated with common chronic diseases, such as MI, stroke, and cancer. Detailed study design and procedures have been described previously [26]. From June 2006 to October 2007, a total of 101 510 adult participants (81 110 males and 20 400 females, aged 18–98 years), were recruited from 11 hospitals in the Kailuan community. All participants underwent questionnaire surveys, clinical examinations and laboratory tests upon enrollment and were followed up every two years thereafter since 2006. Participants were followed up until their death or until December 31, 2018. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committees of Kailuan General Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection.

Based on the baseline status of prehypertension and NCDs, all participants were classified into four groups: “optimal BP without NCDs,” “prehypertension without NCDs,” “optimal BP with NCDs,” and “prehypertension with NCDs.” Participants in the “prehypertension with NCDs” group served as the index group. For each index participant, we randomly selected one control from each of the other three groups among individuals who attended the annual health examination in the same calendar year when the index participant was identified as having “prehypertension with NCDs.” Controls were matched 1:1 to index participants by age (±2 years) and sex. Index participants for whom no eligible control could be found in any of the three control groups, as well as all unmatched potential controls, were excluded from the analysis. The final analytical sample therefore comprised only successfully matched index participants and their corresponding matched controls. After matching, we excluded participants who had prevalent cardiovascular disease at baseline. The final analytical cohort included 56 114 participants (Figure 1). Follow‐up for index participants commenced at the time of diagnosis (i.e., the date of the health examination when the criteria for prehypertension with NCDs were met). For each matched control, follow‐up started on the same calendar date as the corresponding index participant's diagnosis.

Assessment of Prehypertension

BP was measured in the right upper arm using a clinically calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer with the participant in a sitting position. At least two BP measurements were taken after 5 min of rest in a seated position. BP was then measured again if the difference between the two measurements was ≥5 mmHg in either systolic or diastolic BP. We used the average value of the BP measurements for the diagnosis of prehypertension. Prehypertension was defined as BP of 120–139/80–89 mmHg, excluding individuals with hypertension or those taking antihypertensive medication according to the 2003 Seventh Joint National Committee on Hypertension (JNC 7) report [2].

Assessment of NCDs

NCDs included diabetes mellitus, kidney disease, liver disease, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary heart disease and other diseases (e.g., rheumatic disease, mental illness, myasthenia gravis, Parkinson's disease, aplastic anemia). NCDs were diagnosed based on patient‐confirmed medical records from public secondary‐level or higher medical institutions affiliated with Kailuan Group within the preceding year.

Assessment of Outcomes

The main outcomes in the present study were the first occurrence of clinical CVD (including MI, ischemic stroke (IS), hemorrhagic stroke (HS), or all‐cause mortality). The database of CVD events was obtained from the Municipal Social Insurance Institution and Hospital Discharge Register and was updated annually during the follow‐up period. An expert panel collected and reviewed annual discharge records from 11 local hospitals to identify patients with suspected CVD. For suspected CVD events identified by ICD‐10 codes and self‐reported questionnaire data, a panel of three experienced physicians annually reviewed and adjudicated medical records for suspected cases. MI was diagnosed by the patient's clinical symptoms, electrocardiogram, and dynamic changes in myocardial enzymes in accordance with the World Health Organization's Multinational Monitoring of Trends and Determinants in Cardiovascular Disease criteria [27]. The diagnosis of stroke was based on neurological signs, symptoms, and imaging examinations, including computed tomography scans or magnetic resonance imaging according to the World Health Organization criteria [28]. The data of all‐cause mortality were collected from local government vital statistics offices. All the participants were followed from baseline examination up to the date of first CVD diagnosis, date of all‐cause mortality, or December 31, 2018, whichever came first, as the end of the follow‐up period.

Data Collection

Information on demographic characteristics (sex and age), personal health history (CVD, use of antihypertensive, hyperglycemic, and lipid‐lowering drugs), and lifestyle characteristics (smoking status, alcohol consumption habits, and physical exercise habits) was collected via face‐to‐face standardized questionnaire interviews at each physical examination, as detailed elsewhere [29]. A current smoker was defined as someone who smoked a mean of ≥1 cigarette per day during the preceding year. An alcohol consumer was defined as someone who drank a mean of ≥ 100 mL of alcohol per day for at least the preceding year. Participants were categorized as engaged in physical exercise if they performed exercise ≥ 3 times per week for ≥ 30 min on each occasion. Participants were asked to wear light clothes and be barefoot when measuring anthropometric indices. Height and weight were measured to nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, respectively, by trained physicians using a standardized protocol. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). Blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein after an 8‐ to 12‐h overnight fast. Hypertension was defined as a self‐reported specialist‐diagnosis history of, or use of anti‐hypertensive drugs or systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, during the follow‐up period. Tubes were centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at room temperature (22°C‐25°C). After separation, plasma samples were used within 4 h. All biochemical indicators were measured using an autoanalyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at the central laboratory of the Kailuan General Hospital. The biochemical indicators included fasting blood glucose (FBG), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC) and hypersensitive C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous, normally distributed data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (x̅±s), and comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA. Continuous, skewed data were summarized as median and interquartile range (25%, 75%) and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for multiple groups comparisons. Categorical variables were summarized by number and percentage (%), and were compared using chi‐square tests (χ2 tests) between groups. Incidence of CVD and all‐cause mortality were reported as rate/1000 person‐years (PY). The cumulative incidence of the CVD and all‐cause mortality were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the difference in cumulative hazard risks was compared with a log‐rank test. We used Cox proportional hazards model to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of incident CVD and all‐cause mortality among participants with prehypertension and NCDs versus those with optimal BP alone, prehypertension alone, and optimal BP with NCDs. The multivariable adjusted models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, FBG, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, hs‐CRP, smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity.

Stratified analysis was performed among participants by sex and age (< 60 and ≥ 60 years old). We tested effect modification by age and sex via multiplicative interaction terms in fully adjusted Cox models, and Wald χ2 tests evaluated interaction significance. Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm the robustness of our main findings. First, to check whether different NCDs subtypes obscure disease‐specific effects, we excluded participants with diabetes mellitus, COPD, kidney disease and cancer, respectively. Second, outcome events which occurred within the first or second year of the follow‐up period were excluded to minimize the potential reverse causation. Furthermore, to explore whether the observed associations varied by NCDs types or disease burden, we performed stratified analyses by individual NCDs subtypes—including diabetes mellitus, cancer, and kidney disease—and by the number of coexisting NCDs subtypes.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A two‐side P‐values < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

In this cohort study, after adjusting for multiple CVD risk factors, participants in “prehypertension and NCDs” group exhibited the highest risk of CVD events relative to those with other groups. Notably, the risk of all‐cause mortality was higher in participants with “optimal BP and NCDs” than in those with “prehypertension and NCDs.” It is noteworthy that females with “prehypertension and NCDs” had the highest risk of all‐cause mortality. Robust and consistent results were maintained across stratification and sensitivity analyses. Collectively, these findings showed that prehypertension combined with NCDs confers the highest CVD risk, whereas the all‐cause mortality pattern differs among participants with NCDs.

Earlier studies have yield inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between prehypertension and CVD [30, 31]. However, recent studies with sufficient statistical power have established that prehypertension as an independent risk factor for CVD. For instance, a meta‐analysis of 47 cohort studies, adjusting for multiple CVD risk factors, revealed that prehypertension increased the risk of total CVD by 40% [32]. In a Chinese rural cohort with a median of 12.5 years, Duan et al. [11] found that individuals with prehypertension had higher risks of MACE and stroke, but not MI. Even in young adults (aged 18–45 years), a normal BP level (120–139/80‐89 mmHg) was associated with increased cardiovascular risk relative to optimal BP (<120 and 80 mmHg) [33]. Consistent with these recent findings, our study confirmed that prehypertension was associated with elevated risks of total CVD and its major subtypes. Moreover, we found that the coexistence of prehypertension and NCDs significantly amplified CVD risk, with increases of 97% for total CVD, 87% for stroke, and 121% for MI. Previous studies have demonstrated that prehypertension increased cardiovascular risk in specific subgroups, such as individual with diabetes mellitus [24, 25] or metabolic syndrome [23]. Our findings extend this evidence to a broader spectrum of chronic NCDs. Notably, we observed a clear dose‐response relationship between the number of coexisting NCDs subtypes and CVD risk: compared with optimal BP, the HRs increased progressively from prehypertension alone to prehypertension with two or more NCDs subtypes (HRs = 2.947). This gradient provides empirical evidence that cumulative NCDs burden directly contributes to the graded adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Collectively, these findings emphasize the clinical importance of early detection of prehypertension and targeted interventions in patients with NCDs for mitigating the global CVD epidemic.

Previous studies investigating the association between prehypertension and all‐cause mortality have shown conflicting results. A meta‐analysis, which included 1 129 098 participants from 20 prospective cohort studies, demonstrated that prehypertension was associated with an increased risk of CVD‐specific mortality, but not with all‐cause mortality [12]. In contrast, data from the PRERU MIGRANT Study found that individuals with prehypertension had a 2.1‐fold higher risk of 10‐year all‐cause mortality compared with those with normal BP [9]. Meng et al. [10]. also demonstrated that the HRs (95%CI) for prehypertension were 2.09 (1.5–2.92) for all‐cause mortality. Our study further identified prehypertension as an independent risk factor for all‐cause mortality, irrespective of NCDs status. Moreover, patients with prehypertension and comorbid NCDs exhibited a higher risk of all‐cause mortality compared with those with prehypertension alone. These findings were consistent with a recent study by Huang et al. [24], who reported a significant association between prehypertension and increased all‐cause mortality risk stratified by diabetic status. In the stratified analysis of our study, it was also found that prehypertension combined with diabetes mellitus was an independent risk factor for all‐cause mortality (HRs = 1.47). Paradoxically, our prior analysis of the same cohort demonstrated that prehypertension correlates with elevated all‐cause mortality risk exclusively in nondiabetic participants, while diabetes mellitus modifies this association [25]. We attribute this inconsistency to the methodological limitations of our previous study, which only performed simple stratified analyses of prehypertension and diabetes mellitus for all‐cause mortality. In that analysis, prehypertension was associated with increased all‐cause mortality only among non‐diabetes mellitus participants. However, the significant interaction P‐values (all <0.01) indicated that the risk direction for all‐cause mortality was consistent across the prehypertension only and prehypertension comorbid with diabetes mellitus. Our prior finding lacked statistical significance likely due to a small sample size of prehypertension comorbid diabetes mellitus participants (n = 3778), whereas our study included a substantially larger cohort (n = 12 488). Furthermore, unlike previous studies, we employed the “Gmatch” matching strategy, incorporating and grouping participants through exact matching by age and sex. This approach ingeniously constructed multiple “quasi‐experimental” settings, thereby overcoming the confounding effects of age and sex—two strong confounders for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all‐cause mortality—on the study results. Collectively, the results of our current study were more robust and credible. Importantly, our investigation extends these observations by including not only patients with diabetes mellitus but also those with other chronic NCDs.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed that participants with optimal BP and comorbid NCDs had a 22% higher risk of all‐cause mortality than those with prehypertension and NCDs. This finding was inconsistent with clinical expectations. To clarify the underlying causes and verify the robustness of this novel observation, we performed a series of sensitivity and stratified analyses from multiple perspectives. First, in the sensitivity analyses, we sequentially excluded participants with diabetes mellitus, COPD, kidney disease, and cancer, and further conducted stratified analyses by these specific NCDs subtypes. The results remained consistent with our main findings, indicating that the observed association was not attributable to the unique pathophysiological or clinical features of any single NCDs subtype. Second, a notable concern in observational studies is that preexisting, undiagnosed severe illness may lower BP and simultaneously increase early mortality, thereby introducing reverse causality. To address this, we performed sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed CVD events within the first 2 years of follow‑up or who died within the first 1–2 years of follow‐up. The stable risk estimates after exclusion confirmed that reverse causality is unlikely to account for the excess all‐cause mortality in the optimal BP with NCDs group. Third, the severity of NCDs may have influenced our results. We acknowledge that direct measures of NCDs severity (e.g., diabetes mellitus complications, cancer stage) were not available in our dataset. To partially address this limitation, we have completed subgroup analyses stratified by the number of coexisting NCDs subtypes. For all‐cause mortality, participants with optimal BP and two or more NCDs subtypes demonstrated the highest hazard (HRs 3.501, 95%CI: 2.706‐4.529), exceeding mortality risk observed in prehypertension complicated by two or more NCDs subtypes. This consistent dose‐response gradient directly provides empirical evidence that cumulative NCDs disease burden explains the graded adverse outcomes we observed. The results of this analysis suggested that the observed associations were unlikely to be substantially confounded by unmeasured disease severity. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms underlying this unexpected clinical paradox remain unclear, warranting further rigorous clinical and mechanistic investigations for in‐depth clarification.

In addition, the current study showed that, unlike males, female participants with prehypertension and NCDs exhibited the higher risk of all‐cause mortality than those with optimal BP and NCDs. To formally test whether sex modified the association between NCDs status and all‐cause mortality, we performed a formal interaction test. The likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without the interaction term yielded a P for interaction <0.001 indicating statistically significant interaction between sex and NCD status on all‐cause mortality. This significant interaction confirms that the effect of NCDs status on all‐cause mortality differs between sexes, supporting the need for sex‐stratified interpretations. Several potential explanations may account for this observation. First, female patients with prehypertension exhibited elevated atherogenic index of plasma [34], which has been demonstrated to be significantly and positively correlation with all‐cause mortality [35]. Second, estrogen decline (e.g., postmenopause) may disrupt vascular protection in prehypertensive females with NCDs, increasing mortality susceptibility [36]. Third, high clinical inertia and underrecognition in the management of prehypertension in females may plausibly explain the higher all‐cause mortality observed in females with prehypertension and NCDs [37]. Given above potential confounding mechanisms, further in‐depth investigations are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

The finding of our study indicated that early detection and intervention of prehypertension among NCDs patients are needed to be implemented to reduce the risk of all‐cause mortality. We recommend establishing a precision screening and stratified management system for patients with “prehypertension and NCDs.” Incorporate prehypertension into primary care NCDs routine screening, establish a “BP+NCDs” combined screening mechanism for NCDs patients to enable early detection via health check‐ups, family doctor services, and medical consortium referrals, and enhance risk stratification in high‐risk subgroups(e.g., postmenopausal women) by increasing BP monitoring frequency. For patients with prehypertension and NCDs, a risk stratification intervention plan should be developed: low‐risk individuals should prioritize lifestyle modification, while intermediate‐to‐high risk patients should initiate “lifestyle intervention+ drug preparation” management. The formulation of BP management policies for patients with NCDs is urgent yet challenging.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is a large prospective study to investigate the impact of prehypertension on the risk of CVD and all‐cause mortality among NCDs patients. Second, the follow‐up time of the study is relatively long and the potential confounders have been adjusted, so the conclusion is reliable. Several limitations of our study should also be acknowledged. First, although we have adjusted for multiple confounders and performed sensitivity analysis, some confounding factors may still be present. Notably, regarding the finding that patients with optimal BP and NCDs had a higher risk of all‐cause mortality than those with prehypertension and NCDs, we cannot ruled out the potential confounding effects of NCDs severity and clinical medications use on this result. Second, the NCDs category in our analysis included heterogeneous conditions such as diabetes mellitus, COPD, kidney disease and cancer, which have distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and prognoses. By pooling these diverse conditions together, we may have obscured disease‐specific effects of prehypertension on CVD and all‐cause mortality. To explore this, we performed separate sensitivity analyses and stratification analyses stratified by the major NCDs subtypes, the results remained consistent with our primary results. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as a composite estimate across NCDs, and the results may not apply equally to each participant NCDs subtype. Third, the diagnosis of prehypertension and hypertension was based on single‐day BP measurements, which may misclassify BP levels in participants with white‐coat effect or masked hypertension (MH). Psychological anxiety and stress responses are direct triggers for white‐coat hypertension (WCH) in clinical settings. The Kailuan cohort study, comprising Kailuan Group employees with biennial health examination at a fixed center with consistent medical staff, benefits from participants’ familiarity with the clinical settings and medical service providers, reducing their anxiety. Previous study reports WCH prevalence of 10%∼29.2%, predominantly in females, older adults(age ≥ 60 years), and those with a higher BMI [38]. In contrast, our study population consists of 83% males with a mean age of 55.29 years. Consequently, the prevalence of WCH in our study cohort is significantly lower than 29.2%. Masked hypertension (MH) is another type of misclassification when assessing BP statuses solely based on single‐day BP measurements. However, given that the prevalence of MH in our cohort would likely be balanced across groups, we believe this misclassification would bias the results toward the null, making our observed associations conservative. Furthermore, given our sufficiently large sample size, we contend that the impact of WCH and MH on our study outcomes is unlikely to meaningfully alter the observed findings. Fourth, the Kailuan study cohort predominantly consisted of males(83.6%) from a single community in northern China, limiting the generalizability of our findings to women, other regions, or more diverse populations. Therefore, external validation in broader, more representative cohorts with balanced sex distributions and diverse geographic and cultural backgrounds is urgently needed to confirm the observed associations.

Conclusion

Prehypertension was associated with a significantly elevated risk of CVD events among NCDs patients. However, a paradoxical association was observed for all‐cause mortality, with higher risk in patients with optimal BP and NCDs than in those with prehypertension and NCDs. These divergent findings underscore the complexity of BP management in NCDs patients and highlight the need for further research to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to determine optimal BP targets in NCDs patients.
