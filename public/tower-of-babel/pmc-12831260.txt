Nitric oxide-releasing nanocarriers integrated with ginsenoside compound K in dissolvable microneedles for androgenetic alopecia
Authors: Guanghui Sun, Jianlin Liu, Guorui Jin, Jing Zhao, Xiaoyang Li, Xueqing Yu, Yu Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is recognized as a prevalent androgen-mediated disorder characterized by progressive follicular miniaturization and irreversible hair loss, primarily driven by the pathological elevation of dihydrotestosterone (DHT) that disrupts hair follicle microenvironment and induces follicular cells damage. This condition frequently results in profound psychosocial distress and diminished quality of life among affected individuals. In this study, we introduced a novel microneedle-based drug delivery system (CK/GSNO@Lip MN) for the synergistic treatment of AGA. The system was ingeniously engineered for the co-delivery of Ginsenoside Compound K (CK) and S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO), which possessed distinct hydrophobicities and release kinetics. Specifically, the hydrophobic drug CK activated the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway to upregulate β-catenin expression, while concurrently modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and alleviating excessive inflammatory responses. Meanwhile, the hydrophilic nitric oxide (NO) donor GSNO released NO to enhance angiogenesis and synergistically regulated inflammatory homeostasis in conjunction with CK, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for hair regeneration. Ultimately, CK/GSNO@Lip MN achieved significant hair regeneration by modulating the hair follicle microenvironment and promoting the proliferation of follicular cells. This innovative therapeutic strategy provided a comprehensive approach to AGA treatment, with promising potential for broader dermatological applications. Introduction

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is a common non-scarring form of hair loss. AGA significantly impacts patients’ psychological health and quality of life [1, 2]. Current treatments for AGA mainly include pharmacological therapies, such as minoxidil, and surgical interventions. However, these methods are hampered significant limitations, including suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, varying efficacy across individuals, and the risk of potential side effects, highlighting the need for more effective and safer alternatives [3–5]. Achieving this requires thorough understanding of the underlying mechanisms of AGA. In AGA individuals, excessive production of dihydrotestosterone (DHT) suppresses the proliferation of dermal papilla cells (DPCs), thereby disrupting the normal hair cycle composed of the anagen, catagen, and telogen phases [6, 7]. Furthermore, the dysregulated microenvironment—marked by persistent inflammation, overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and vascular injury—collectively suppresses DPCs function and compromises hair follicle regeneration. Given the multifactorial nature of AGA, improving the hair follicle microenvironment while stimulating DPCs activity and restore the normal hair growth cycle presents a promising approach to treatment [8–10].

Inflammatory cytokines and ROS are critical factors influencing the cellular microenvironment [11–13]. Excessive levels of these factors can accelerate the premature senescence of DPCs, thereby inhibiting the transition of hair follicles (HFs) from the telogen to the anagen phase [6, 14]. Ginsenoside Compound K (CK), a bioactive metabolite derived from ginsenosides, has been shown to promote cell proliferation while also exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [15–17]. The regulatory effects of CK on key cellular signaling pathways, such as PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, and MAPK, have been well-documented [18, 19]. Specifically, CK could inhibit the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, which help to mitigate excessive inflammatory responses and reduce the overproduction of ROS in the hair follicle microenvironment. Moreover, CK has been reported to regulate β-catenin expression in various cell types [20, 21]. Considering the essential role of PI3K-AKT/β-catenin signaling in hair follicle development and DPCs proliferation, this multi-target regulatory capacity suggests that CK may serve as an effective therapeutic candidate for AGA [22, 23].

In addition to regulating the inflammatory microenvironment, another key challenge in the treatment of AGA is impaired angiogenesis [24, 25]. NO, a gaseous free radical, is a key endogenous mediator involved in vascular homeostasis and tissue repair. At low and sustained doses, NO promotes angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell proliferation and neovascularization which is essential for tissue regeneration in ischemic or vascular-deficient areas [26, 27]. However, at high doses, NO can induce cytotoxic effects, including oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis, highlighting the need for precise control over NO release in therapeutic settings [28]. Among various NO donors, S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO), an endogenous signaling molecule with proven biocompatibility, has garnered significant attention for both its precisely tunable NO release kinetics and inherent biosafety advantages, positioning it as a promising therapeutic candidate for clinical translation [29].

Despite the potential of CK and NO for AGA treatment, their direct application to hair follicles is singnificantly limited due to the hydrophobic nature of CK and the risk of severe toxicity associated with high concentrations of NO. The unique bilayer organization of liposomes confers dual-compartment loading capability, permitting simultaneous delivery of hydrophilic therapeutics and hydrophobic agents for synergistic combination therapy. Additionally, liposomes can significantly prolong drug release, minimizing dosing frequency and reducing potential side effects linked to rapid drug release [30].

To achieve effective drug release and utilization of liposomes, it is essential to overcome the stratum corneum barrier and deliver the drug to the dermis, where hair follicles are located. Microneedle-based drug delivery systems offer several advantages over conventional methods [31, 32]. These systems are minimally invasive, enabling painless administration and improving patient compliance. By temporarily disrupting the stratum corneum, microneedles enhance transdermal delivery of drugs, such as proteins, peptides, and nanoparticles. Moreover, they enable localized and controlled drug release, minimizing systemic side effects while improving therapeutic efficacy. The use of biocompatible and biodegradable materials of microneedle further ensures safety and reduces environmental concerns. Given these advantages, microneedle systems provide a promising platform for treating androgenic alopecia.

In this study, we developed a novel transdermal microneedle system (CK/GSNO@Lip MN, Scheme 1), where the liposomal simultaneously encapsulated CK and GSNO. The in vivo and in vitro experiments have demonstrated that CK/GSNO@Lip MN could regulate the microenvironment of DPCs by inhibiting excessive inflammatory factors, ROS and promoting angiogenesis, thus provided a favorable microenvironment for DPCs proliferation, facilitating the transition of hair follicles from the telogen phase to the anagen phase. Transcriptomic analysis further confirmed that CK/GSNO@Lip MN could activate the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, upregulating β-catenin to stimulate DPCs proliferation. Moreover, in terms of hair regeneration, CK/GSNO@Lip MN achieved comparable effects to minoxidil, while requiring significantly lower application frequency and exhibiting no obvious side effects. Materials and methods

Materials

Soybean Lecithin (97%), Cholesterol (99%), Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA, Mw = 9000–10000 Da) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ginsenoside Compound K (96%) was sourced from the Biomedicine Institute of Northwest University (Shaanxi, China). 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT, 1.0 mg/mL) was purchased from Macklin. Testosterone (TS, 1.0 mg/mL) and Minoxidil (99%) were purchased from Solarbio. L-γ-Glutamyl-S-nitroso-L-cysteinylglycine (GSNO, 97%), Lipopolysaccharides (LPS), β-mercaptoethanol, 1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (Dil, 98%), Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 99%), and Interferon-γ (IFN-γ, 98%) were purchased from MCE.

Preparation of liposomal nanoparticles co-encapsulating CK and GSNO (CK/GSNO@Lip)

The lipid nanoparticles encapsulating CK and GSNO (CK/GSNO@Lip) were prepared using the film dispersion method. First, 150 mg of soybean lecithin and 50 mg of cholesterol were dissolved in a solution of 7 mL anhydrous ethanol and 3 mL chloroform. Next, 20 mg of CK was added to the above solution and stirred magnetically at 37 °C for 30 min until dissolved. The aboved solution was transferred to a rotary evaporator and evaporated at 37 °C for 30 min until completely dried, and then transferred to a vacuum drying oven for 12 h. Subsequently, 5 mg of GSNO was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water at 4 °C, and the GSNO solution was added to the dried flask, maintaining the mixture in a 4 °C water bath for 30 min. Finally, the solution was treated ultrasonically, resulting in the formation of the lipid nanoparticles CK/GSNO@Lip. Pure lipid nanoparticles (Lip), lipid nanoparticles encapsulating CK (CK@Lip), and lipid nanoparticles encapsulating GSNO (GSNO@Lip) were synthesized using the same method.

Preparation of microneedles loaded with liposomal nanoparticles co-encapsulating CK and GSNO (CK/GSNO@Lip MN)

The CK/GSNO@Lip MN was prepared using a double-casting molding method. Initially, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) negative molds were fabricated by casting PDMS onto a copper microneedle template. Subsequently, a 25% (w/v) PVA solution was prepared as the microneedle matrix. CK/GSNO@Lip solution was then added to achieve a final concentration of 100 µg of CK and 25 µg of GSNO in the MN system. A 30 µL aliquot of the resulting solution was injected into the cavities at the tips of the PDMS mold under vacuum to ensure uniform filling. Afterward, additional PVA solution was applied to the mold surface to form the backing layer. The assembly was dried overnight until complete hardening, and the microneedles were carefully demolded to obtain CK/GSNO@Lip MN [33, 34]. Using the same procedure, blank microneedles (MN), microneedles loaded with CK-lipid nanoparticles (CK@Lip MN), microneedles loaded with Dil/GSNO-lipid nanoparticles (Dil/GSNO@Lip MN) and microneedles loaded with GSNO-lipid nanoparticles (GSNO@Lip MN) were prepared.

Characterization of nanoparticles and microneedles

The morphology of CK/GSNO@Lip MN was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SIGMA 360, ZEISS, Germany), while CK/GSNO@Lip nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan). The particle size of CK/GSNO@Lip was analyzed with a laser particle size analyzer (DLS, Mastersizer 3000 + Ultra, Malvern Panalytical, England). The drug loading capacity of CK has been evaluated using rhodamine B as a model drug with laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, N-SIM-E, Nikon, Japan). Optical images of the microneedles at various angles were captured using an optical camera. To evaluate mechanical performance, the microneedles were placed horizontally, with the tips facing downward onto fresh pig skin, and the force required for penetration was measured using a universal testing machine (UTM, 3400, Instron, England).

CK and GSNO releasing profile from CK/GSNO@Lip MN

The microneedles were placed in a dialysis bag and immersed in a centrifuge tube filled with PBS for dissolution. Dialysate samples were collected at various time intervals (10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h). The concentration of CK was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Arc HPLC, Waters, America). The NO concentration in the samples was measured using a Griess reagent assay and a microplate reader (ELISA Reader, iMARK-680, BIO-RAD, America).

Construction of cell models

Previous studies have established a cell injury model using DHT. DPCs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates, and after 24 h, the normal culture medium was replaced with a solution of DHT (20 µM). After 48 h, the DPC cell injury model was successfully established [35]. Based on previous studies, a working concentration of 1 µM was selected for Minoxidil.

Based on previous research, THP-1 monocytes were exposed to 50 ng/mL PMA for two days and then differentiated into the M0 phenotype in RPMI 1640 medium. Subsequently, they were induced with IFN-γ to differentiate into the M1 phenotype, successfully establishing the inflammation cell model [36].

Cell proliferation and cell migration

DPCs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into a plate. After 24 h, different groups of microneedle extract solutions were added, and the cells were incubated for another 48 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader. For the scratch assay, DPCs (2 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded into a 6-well plate. After 24 h, a scratch was made across the bottom of each well, followed by the addition of different groups of microneedle extract solutions. Cell migration was imaged after 48 h of incubation. The proliferation and migration assays for HUVECs were conducted following the same protocol.

The synergistic effect of CK/GSNO@Lip MN on protecting inflammatory injured DPCs

THP-1 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in the lower chambers of a 24-well transwell plate. Macrophage differentiation was achieved with 50 nM PMA (48 h). In the second step, 100 ng/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ were added to the culture medium, and the cells were further cultured for 48 h to induce differentiation into M1 macrophages. The transwell chambers were then transferred to the 24-well plate containing pre-cultured DPCs. After the drugs were added to the medium, the cells were stained using an AM/PI assay kit at 24 and 48 h, and cell viability and death were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope [37]. In vitro anti-inflammatory experiments refer to experimental methods in previous studies [38].

Detection of intracellular ROS and NO

DPCs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 24-well confocal dishes. After 24 h, microneedle extracts from different groups were added, and cells were cultured for an additional 24 h. Then, DCFH-DA (10 µM) was introduced and incubated for 10 min, followed by washing with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using LSCM. For intracellular NO detection, the same procedure was applied, replacing the fluorescence probe with DAF-FM DA to measure the intracellular release of NO [39]. Quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Tube formation experiment

Based on previous research, 100 µL of pre-cooled Matrigel was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gel solidification. Resuspended DPCs (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded onto the gel, and the extraction solution containing different components was subsequently added. Tubule formation was evaluated after 4–6 h. Quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software [40].

AGA model establishment, treatment and performance evaluation in vivo

This study was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Northwest University (Approval No.: NWU-AWC-20240733 M). Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old) were obtained from the Air Force Medical University (Xi’an, China). The AGA model was induced by daily subcutaneous injections of testosterone (5 mg/kg) following the removal of dorsal hair [41]. Microneedle treatments were administered every two days, with regular monitoring and recording of hair regeneration on the back skin. At the end of the experiment, the skin, internal organs, and plasma of mouse were collected. The skin and organs were subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, while plasma was centrifuged to separate the serum. The follicle diameter was determined from the H&E-stained skin sections of mice. For each hair follicle, measurements were taken at the upper, middle, and lower regions, and the average of these three values was recorded as one data point. The mean follicle diameter for each mouse was then calculated by averaging multiple follicle measurements. All length measurements were performed using ImageJ software. Inflammatory cytokine levels in the serum were measured using ELISA.

Western blot experiment

The cell culture and drug treatment procedures were performed as described in the cell proliferation assay. After incubation, cells were placed on ice, and RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (100:1:1) was added. The cells were scraped from 6-well plates, lysed thoroughly, and centrifuged to isolate the supernatant. The protein samples were then mixed with loading buffer and stored at −20 °C for later analysis. Western blotting (WB) was performed, and target protein bands were visualized using an ECL imaging system. Quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing was conducted with the support of BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. Initially, cells were lysed with Trizol, and mRNA was randomly fragmented for cDNA synthesis using random oligonucleotide primers. Following normalization of DNA libraries from multiple samples, they were pooled in equal volumes and sequenced in paired-end mode (PE150) on the Illumina platform. The resulting image files were converted to FASTQ format using the sequencing platform software, and data quality was assessed using Fast QC v0.11.8. Finally, comprehensive analysis was performed using BGI’s proprietary data mining system, providing high-quality data to support subsequent investigations.

Statistical analysis

At least three independent replications for each data. Data were presented as means ± standard deviations. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. One-way or two-way ANOVA was used to analyze significant differences at different thresholds: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of CK/GSNO@Lip

In this study, CK/GSNO@Lip was prepared using the thin-film dispersion method by combining soy lecithin, cholesterol, CK, and the NO donor GSNO through a one-step self-assembly process. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of CK were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to optimize the loading concentration (Fig. 2A). The results showed that at a concentration of 2 mg/mL of CK, CK achieved an EE of 88.43% and an LE of 11.28%, both of which were relatively high. As the concentration of CK in the liposomes increased, the EE decreased, while the LE gradually increased, which was attributed to the limited capacity of the liposomes to encapsulate CK. The sizes of CK/GSNO@Lip, CK@Lip, GSNO@Lip, and Lip were below 110 nm, indicating that the liposomes could facilitate improved cellular uptake (Fig. 2B). The particle size of CK/GSNO@Lip, CK@Lip, and GSNO@Lip slightly decreased compared to Lip, but the change was minimal and not statistically significant. In addition, the zeta potential plays a crucial role in determining the stability of nanoparticles in solution. As shown in Figure S1, the loading of CK and GSNO into Lip did not cause a significant change in the surface potential, indicating that the incorporation of these agents had no adverse effect on the stability of the nanoparticles. The prepared liposome solution was transparent and colorless (Fig. 2D). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to observe the surface structure (Figs. 2E-F), with results consistent with the particle size characterization data. Long-term stability is crucial for the clinical application of transdermal drug delivery systems [42]. Therefore, the stability of CK/GSNO@Lip was evaluated by measuring particle size over 21 days at 4 °C (Fig. 2C). After 21 days, no significant changes in particle size distribution were observed, and no aggregation occurred. The above results confirmed the successful preparation of CK/GSNO@Lip.

Fabrication and characterization of CK/GSNO@Lip MN

In this study, CK/GSNO@Lip MN was prepared using PVA (Fig. 2G). Optical microscopy confirmed that the microneedles’ structure was consistent with the ideal dimensions, with tip heights of approximately 750 μm and base widths of approximately 300 μm (Figs. 2H, I). SEM images showed that each microneedle was evenly arranged. The microneedles exhibited smooth surfaces, sharp tips, and dimensions aligned with the mold specifications (Fig. 2J). Based on the initial amounts of CK and GSNO used during the preparation of CK/GSNO@Lip, the theoretical concentrations of both components can be calculated. However, as shown in Fig. 2A, the EE of CK was 88.43%. Therefore, the final concentration of CK in the CK/GSNO@Lip solution was 1.60 mM. Considering the total volume of the microneedle tips, the mass fractions of CK and GSNO in each microneedle patch were determined to be 3.5% and 2.2%. The mechanical strength of CK/GSNO@Lip MN was evaluated to confirm its effectiveness in delivering drugs to the dermis. The results indicated that CK/GSNO@Lip MN generated a force of 0.25 N/needle (Fig. 2K), which, as reported in previous studies, is suitable for subcutaneous drug delivery [43]. After 24 h of application of microneedles to the dorsal of mice, few visible traces of microneedle use were observed, and after 48 h, the skin recovered as before, indicating minimal damage to the skin (Fig. 2L). Under CLSM, Rhodamine B, as a model drug, was observed to be uniformly distributed at the microneedle tip, demonstrating even distribution of CK (Fig. 2M) [44]. The release profiles of CK and NO from CK/GSNO@Lip MN were assessed over 48 h. NO released 50% of its total concentration within 6 h and peaked at 81% by 24 h (Fig. 2N). CK released approximately 30% within the first hour, followed by a gradual release, achieving a maximum of 57% at 48 h (Fig. 2O). This difference was attributed to the varying solubility properties of CK and GSNO in water, with the hydrophilic GSNO exhibiting a faster release rate [45]. In summary, the aboved results confirmed that CK/GSNO@Lip MN effectively extended the release period of CK and NO and decreased the frequency of drug administration.

Biocompatibility of CK/GSNO@Lip MN

Next, the biocompatibility of CK/GSNO@Lip MN was evaluated. CK notably enhanced DPCs proliferation at a concentration of 4 µM, whereas GSNO exerted optimal effects at 2 µM (Figs. 3A, B). Next, the DHT-induced DPCs damage model was established for further evaluation of the bioactivity of CK/GSNO@Lip MN. The optimal DHT concentration for this model was determined (Fig. 3C), and at a concentration of 20 µM, DPCs proliferation was significantly inhibited, with cell viability reduced to about 75% compared to the normal group. This concentration was chosen for the model group in subsequent experiments. To investigate whether CK could be internalized by DPCs, Dil, a lipophilic fluorescent dye, was used as a model compound and evaluated its cellular uptake efficiency. We synthesized Dil/GSNO@Lip MN to simulate the CK/GSNO@Lip MN. The results revealed numerous red fluorescent dots concentrated around the nuclei, which indicated that CK could be effectively internalized by DPCs (Fig. 3D). Next, DAF-FM DA was used to detect intracellular NO. After incubation with microneedle extracts, intracellular NO concentration progressively increased over 24 and 48 h (Figs. 3E, F and Fig. S2). The experimental results confirmed that CK and NO were effectively internalized by the cells following 48 h incubation.

The effects of CK/GSNO@Lip MN on DHT- induced damage in DPCs and HUVECs

Activation of DPCs to proliferate and migrate to hair loss areas was critical for hair regeneration in affected regions [46, 47]. To assess the promotive effects of CK/GSNO@Lip MN on DPCs and HUVECs, cell viability was first measured. The experimental groups were divided into the following six groups: Normal, Model, CK@Lip MN, GSNO@Lip MN, CK/GSNO@Lip MN, and Minoxidil. Compared to the normal group, cell viability in the model group was significantly reduced. However, after treatment with CK and NO, the DPCs viability improved, and the combined treatment of both drugs significantly enhanced DPCs viability. In comparison, the positive normal group treated with minoxidil exhibited a modest promotive effect, which was less effective than CK/GSNO@Lip MN. Notably, the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group achieved a viability increase of 122% (Fig. 4A). To further investigate the effects of CK/GSNO@Lip MN on DPCs and HUVECs migration, a scratch assay was performed. As shown in Figs. 4B-E, the model group treated with DHT exhibited significant inhibition of cell migration. In contrast, the CK@Lip MN and GSNO@Lip MN groups moderately promoted cell migration, with migration rates of 19.5% and 40.3%, respectively. Notably, the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group demonstrated the most pronounced effect, achieving a migration rate of 62.3%. These findings suggested that CK and NO exhibited significant synergistic effects in promoting the proliferation and migration of DPCs and HUVECs. Furthermore, their combined application was more effective than minoxidil in directly enhancing cell viability. The reason for this synergistic effect may be that CK and NO collectively contribute to the regulation of the microenvironment, which in turn facilitates the proliferation and migration of DPCs [24].

CK/GSNO@Lip MN enhanced the viability of DPCs by modulating their surrounding microenvironment

The microenvironment surrounding DPCs played a critical role in regulating their proliferation and migration, which was essential for maintaining hair follicle function and growth [48, 49]. Studies showed that inflammation and excessive ROS in the DPCs microenvironment impaired cell viability and function, while atrophied capillaries further hindered the regenerative capacity of hair follicles [50, 51]. To explore the mechanisms underlying the proliferative and migratory effects of CK/GSNO@Lip MN, key microenvironmental factors, including inflammation, ROS levels, and angiogenic activity, were examined. Several scalp biopsy studies in patients with AGA demonstrated that tissue inflammation in hair follicle regions correlated with disease progression [52, 53]. To simulate the inflammatory microenvironment of hair follicles, DPCs were co-cultured with macrophages using transwell chambers (Fig. 5A). After 48 h of induction, the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were elevated, indicating successful induction of M1 macrophages (Fig. 5B, C). After 48 h of co-culture between DPCs and M1 macrophages, DPCs viability was evaluated by AM/PI staining. The model group exhibited significant cell death, with only 0.65% of living DPCs remaining. In contrast, the CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil treatment groups significantly improved DPCs viability, with viability rates exceeding 110% (Fis. 5D, E, Fig. S3). Directly evaluating the effect of CK/GSNO@Lip on macrophage polarization provides deeper and more direct evidence for elucidating its anti-inflammatory mechanism. Accordingly, we have conducted additional experiments focusing on the regulatory effect of CK/GSNO@Lip MN on macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. As shown in Figure S4, CK/GSNO@Lip MN treatment significantly reduced the proportion of M1-type macrophages compared with the Model group, while simultaneously promoting the transition to the M2 phenotype. These new data strongly demonstrate that CK/GSNO@Lip MN does not merely suppress the levels of inflammatory cytokines but actively modulates macrophage polarization by inhibiting the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and enhancing the anti-inflammatory and reparative M2 phenotype.

ROS levels were next evaluated using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. CK/GSNO@Lip MN reduced intracellular ROS to 0.25% of the levels observed in the model group, demonstrating its ability to effectively alleviate oxidative stress in the hair loss area. Similarly, the minoxidil group reduced ROS levels to 2.3% of those in the model group. While CK@Lip MN and GSNO@Lip MN also showed some ROS-scavenging effects, their efficacy was significantly lower than that of CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil (Fig. 6A).

Finally, the angiogenic effects of CK/GSNO@Lip MN were investigated. Both CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil groups exhibited more densely packed tube-like structures (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of branching points, total vessel length, and vessel meshes were significantly increased in the CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil groups compared to normal (Figs. 6C-E). These results underscored the ability of CK and GSNO to promote endothelial cell angiogenesis and their synergistic benefits.

In summary, CK/GSNO@Lip MN modulated the DPCs microenvironment by reducing inflammation and oxidative stress while enhancing angiogenesis, thereby restoring normal cellular function and supporting hair follicle regeneration.

CK/GSNO@Lip MN treated AGA by regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

To further investigate the mechanism of action of CK/GSNO@Lip MN, DPCs were cultured under normal conditions and treated with the drug for 24 h, followed by transcriptomic analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the gene expression profile of the CK/GSNO@Lip MN-treated group was significantly different from that of the DHT-treated model group (Fig. 7A). After differential expression analysis, there were 2,915 significantly altered genes between the model and normal groups, and 1,112 significantly altered genes between the model and CK/GSNO@Lip MN groups (Fig. 7B). Further comparison of common differentially expressed genes revealed that 614 genes were significantly upregulated and 676 genes were notably downregulated following treatment with CK/GSNO@Lip MN (Fig. 7C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 7D) indicated that after CK/GSNO@Lip MN treatment, genes involved in key cellular proliferation processes, including angiogenesis, cell migration, collagen deposition, and extracellular matrix formation, were significantly upregulated, while genes associated with inflammation and oxidative stress were markedly downregulated, as revealed by GO enrichment analysis (Fig. S5). KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 7E) showed that CK/GSNO@Lip MN primarily activated the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Analysis of PI3K-AKT pathway-associated gene expression between the model and CK/GSNO@Lip MN groups revealed significant changes in genes related to AKT, GSK3β, β-catenin, and e-NOS (Fig. 7F, Fig. S6, 7). To validate the observed alterations in gene expression, western blot (WB) analysis was performed. Analysis demonstrated that CK/GSNO@Lip MN treatment leads to increased phosphorylation of AKT and GSK3β, along with elevated expression levels of β-catenin and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (e-NOS). Furthermore, the NO released from CK/GSNO@Lip MN was shown to upregulate vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, thereby enhancing angiogenesis (Fig. S8). These results suggested that the system not only activated intracellular signaling pathways that promoted DPCs growth but also modulated the microenvironment to support DPCs proliferation.

In vivo evaluation of the CK/GSNO@LipMN on perifollicular microenvironment remodeling

To assess the hair regeneration capability of CK/GSNO@Lip MN, AGA model was established, followed by different treatments, including four groups: Normal, Model, CK/GSNO@Lip MN, and Minoxidil (Fig. 8A). Minoxidil was administered once daily, whereas CK/GSNO@Lip MN was applied every other day. After microneedles were applied to the mouse skin, drug diffusion was monitored using in vivo imaging (Fig. 8B). The results showed that the drug achieved maximum diffusion within 24 h, with the majority of the diffusion process completed by 48 h. The results shown in Fig. 8C indicated that no significant hair growth was observed in the model group even by day 21, confirming the successful establishment of the AGA mouse model. However, the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group achieved a hair regeneration coverage of over 90%, comparable to that of the minoxidil group at the same time (Fig. 8C, D, Fig. S7). Moreover, mice in the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group also showed no signs of adverse skin reactions such as erythema or swelling, indicating that CK/GSNO@Lip MN is relatively gentle on the skin and does not cause significant irritation (Fig. 8C Fig. S7). Hair follicle density of the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group was also significantly higher (Fig. 8G). Furthermore, the mammalian hair cycle is an exquisitely orchestrated process whereby the HF undergoes distinct phases of growth (anagen), regression (catagen), and rest (telogen), the induction of anagen is associated with rapid and abundant cell proliferation [54, 55]. H&E staining of skin tissue on day 21 revealed a significantly increased number of anagen hair follicles in the CK/GSNO@Lip MN treatment group, characterized by elongated and deeply rooted structures. In contrast, hair follicles in the model group predominantly remained in the telogen, exhibiting a vesicle-like morphology (Fig. 8E). Additionally, the presence of hair follicles in the anagen phase was also confirmed by follicle diameter changes in the H&E Sects [56, 57]. On day 21, H&E staining revealed that both the Minoxidil and CK/GSNO@Lip MN groups exhibited hair follicle diameters comparable to those of the Normal group, with the CK/GSNO@Lip MN group achieving a remarkable growth rate of 187% (Fig. 8F).

The SEM images showed that both the CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil groups exhibited regenerated hair with a thicker diameter compared to the model group, with diameters exceeding 40 μm, and no significant differences were observed between these two groups (Fig. 9A). The transition of hair follicles from the resting phase to the anagen phase is closely related to rapid and abundant cell proliferation [54, 58]. The IF results showed that CK/GSNO@Lip MN treatment significantly increased the expression levels of β-catenin and VEGF (Figs. 9B, D, E, Fig. S10). The Fig. 9G showed that the Ki67 expression in the CK/GSNO@Lip MN and minoxidil groups was significantly higher compared to the model group, indicating that CK/GSNO@Lip MN can effectively promote cell proliferation. Additionally, the CD31 levels of CK/GSNO@Lip MN in the hair follicles of the hair loss area were highest (Figs. 9C, F), indicating that CK/GSNO@Lip MN can effectively promote angiogenesis around hair follicles. It was observed that the skin thickness of the model group mice was significantly thinner than that of the other groups, which may have been caused by the stimulation of testosterone, leading to extensive cell death and consequently a reduction in skin thickness. Finally, to assess the biocompatibility of CK/GSNO@Lip MN in vivo, the H&E-staining of five major organs were analyzed (Fig. S11). No toxicity was observed in the major organs of the mice, demonstrating the excellent biocompatibility of CK/GSNO@Lip MN.

These results suggested that CK/GSNO@Lip MN could promote the activation of DPCs, proliferation of hair follicle cells, and the transition of hair follicles from the telogen to the anagen. Compared to minoxidil, CK/GSNO@Lip MN exhibited a superior effect in promoting hair regeneration, even with a lower dosing frequency. Compared with minoxidil, which typically requires daily application, CK/GSNO@Lip MN provided sustained drug release for up to 48 h, significantly reducing the need for frequent application. Furthermore, CK/GSNO@Lip MN demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, reducing the risk of side effects such as irritation and allergic reactions. Thus, CK/GSNO@Lip MN provided an improved treatment option with reduced application frequency and minimized adverse effects.

Conclusion

In this study, the CK/GSNO@LipMN system was successfully constructed by encapsulating liposomes loaded with CK and GSNO, aiming to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects for the treatment of AGA. This innovative MN system exhibited excellent drug delivery capability, enhanced bioavailability of CK and prolonged release of NO. The results demonstrated that the innovative CK/GSNO@Lip MN system, which integrates NO-releasing nanocarriers with ginsenoside CK in dissolvable microneedles, effectively delivered therapeutic agents to DPCs, significantly promoting their proliferation via activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and upregulation of β-catenin expression. Moreover, the results demonstrated that CK/GSNO@Lip MN synergistically enhanced hair regenerative outcomes by modulating the cellular microenvironment-specifically by promoting angiogenesis, alleviating oxidative stress, and suppressing local inflammation. The CK/GSNO@Lip MN system also exhibited high biocompatibility and favorable therapeutic effects, underscoring its safety and practicality as a novel drug delivery method. It served not only as a multi-component nanocomposite biomaterial offered an effective, minimally invasive intervention for AGA but also as a promising platform for addressing various types of medical conditions.
