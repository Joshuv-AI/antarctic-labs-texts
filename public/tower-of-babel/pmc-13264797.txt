Development and validation of a machine learning model integrating spectral computed tomography-derived three‑dimensional quantitative parameters and clinical features for predicting minimal extrathyroidal extension in papillary thyroid microcarcinoma
Authors: Zuhua Song, Jie Huang, Xinwei Wang, Yuwei Chen, Ya Zou, Liang Lv, Xiaodi Zhang, Zhuoyue Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative identification of minimal extrathyroidal extension (mETE) in papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC) remains clinically challenging due to the limited sensitivity and specificity of conventional imaging. Although spectral computed tomography (CT) enables three‑dimensional (3D) quantitative tissue characterization, its integration with clinical data for mETE prediction has yet to be systematically evaluated. This study aimed to develop and validate multiple machine learning (ML) models integrating spectral CT 3D parameters with clinical features to predict mETE in PTMC patients.MethodsA total of 253 patients with pathologically proven PTMC who had undergone spectral CT were retrospectively enrolled in this study and randomly assigned to either a training cohort (n=177) or a testing cohort (n=76). Patients with prior thyroid surgery, poor image quality, missing clinical data, or other malignancies were excluded. We collected 12 clinical features along with 13 spectral CT 3D quantitative parameters. mETE status was confirmed by postoperative histopathology. Feature selection was performed using Pearson correlation analysis and the Boruta algorithm. Predictive models were developed using 4 ML algorithms: light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), multilayer perceptron classifier (MLP-C), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) and logistic regression (LR). We evaluated the predictive models using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value were all calculated. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values were used to interpret variable contributions.ResultsA total of 253 patients (170 mETE-positive, 83 mETE-negative; mean age 39.77±10.71 years) were included. Four variables were selected for inclusion in the final model, namely 3D-arterial enhancement fraction (AEF), BRAF V600E status, capsular contact status, 3D-arterial phase (AP)40keV. LightGBM, MLP-C, AdaBoost, and LR model demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic efficacy, with the AUCs of 0.99, 0.95, 0.91, 0.88 and 0.78, 0.77, 0.81, 0.80 in the training and testing cohorts, respectively. The SHAP method showed that the top three variables ranked by contribution degree were: 3D-AEF, BRAF V600E status, capsular contact status.ConclusionsML models that incorporate spectral CT 3D parameters and clinical features offer a promising approach for preoperative prediction of mETE in PTMC. Following external validation in multicenter cohorts, these models may serve as adjunctive tools to inform clinical decision-making and risk stratification.

Introduction

The incidence of thyroid cancer in China has increased markedly, and it now ranks as the third most common cancer (1). Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounts for over 90% of all pathological types of thyroid cancer. With the widespread adoption of imaging techniques, its incidence has increased significantly. This rise is driven primarily by papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC), defined as PTC with a diameter of ≤1 cm, which constitutes the major portion of the case increase and represents more than 50% of all newly diagnosed PTC cases annually (2-4). Extrathyroidal extension (ETE) is a key pathological indicator for assessing tumor aggressiveness. It is classified into minimal ETE (mETE), which refers to extension only into the perithyroidal soft tissues or sternothyroid muscle, and gross ETE (gETE), involving invasion into surrounding muscles, trachea, larynx, blood vessels, and/or esophagus (5). As an independent risk factor for lymph node metastasis in PTMC, mETE is associated with reduced disease-free survival and consequently serves as a key indicator of poor prognosis (6,7). The clinical significance of mETE has been a subject of debate, culminating in its removal from the tumor staging criteria for differentiated thyroid cancer in the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system (8). This change was based on evidence that mETE alone does not impact overall survival. However, the essence of clinical decision-making lies in balancing active surveillance against surgical intervention. For low-risk patients, active surveillance can prevent unnecessary surgery, thereby reducing complications and healthcare costs, whereas for patients with indicators of progression risk such as mETE, it often necessitates an escalation in management, which includes increased frequency of active surveillance or timely surgical intervention to ensure optimal outcomes. The early detection and accurate assessment of mETE remain a clinical challenge (6,9). Therefore, the accurate early prediction of mETE is essential for achieving precise risk stratification and guiding personalized clinical management in PTMC patients.

Ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT) are the primary imaging modalities currently used for evaluating ETE in PTC, with US being the preferred method (5). Numerous studies have attempted to identify US features predictive of ETE. For instance, irregular margins, microcalcifications, and tall shape have been associated with aggressive behavior, and specifically, the degree of tumor contact with the thyroid capsule has been identified as a strong predictor for ETE in PTMC (10,11). However, the diagnostic accuracy of US is highly variable. Previous meta-analyses and systematic reviews have reported that its sensitivity and specificity for detecting ETE (including both mETE and gETE) in PTC or PTMC range from 25% to 100% and from 13% to 93%, respectively (9,12). This wide variability may be attributed to the heterogeneity in the composition of mETE and gETE across studies, inconsistencies in US diagnostic criteria, and significant operator dependence. This subjectivity limits the reliability of US as a standalone tool for mETE prediction. Radiologists can assess gETE by subjectively interpreting conventional CT signs of tumor invasion into adjacent structures (13,14). Notably, even the combination of contrast-enhanced CT and US has not improved the diagnostic accuracy for mETE in PTMC (15). This finding underscores the fundamental limitation of conventional imaging: they rely on morphological changes that occur late in the disease process and cannot reliably capture the subtle biological aggressiveness that characterizes mETE. While histopathological examination is the gold standard for diagnosing ETE, it is an invasive procedure. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an objective and accurate method to predict mETE to improve clinical decision-making.

In this context, we propose that spectral CT can address the fundamental limitations of US and conventional CT. Unlike these modalities, spectral CT enables the quantification of specific material concentrations, such as iodine and effective atomic number, providing insights into tumor perfusion and metabolic activity that is not accessible with conventional imaging (16). Although spectral CT entails higher costs and is not universally accessible, particularly in primary care settings, we do not advocate its use as a routine screening tool to supplant US. Instead, we propose its targeted deployment as a problem-solving modality in clinically equivocal cases, where it may enhance risk stratification and inform decisions regarding surgical intervention or intensified surveillance, ultimately contributing to the prevention of both undertreatment and overtreatment. Previous studies have demonstrated that energy CT holds diagnostic value in evaluating foci and lymph node metastases in PTC (17,18). However, conventional approaches typically rely on multi-parameter data from a two-dimensional region of interest on the largest lesion slice, failing to capture the volumetric heterogeneity of the entire tumor. Threedimensional (3D) analysis demonstrates lower variability and higher repeatability in quantitative assessments, while also providing more comprehensive information on tumor heterogeneity by analyzing the entire tumor (19,20). 3D quantitative parameters from spectral CT also show predictive potential for Ki-67 proliferation index in PDAC (21). Current research on the combined evaluation of spectral CT 3D parameters with other clinical indicators for assessing mETE in PTMC remains relatively limited.

Machine learning (ML) has been widely used to discern key risk factors and prognostic markers for various diseases due to its excellent data processing capabilities and ability to understand complex correlations between variables (22,23). This study therefore aimed to develop and evaluate multiple ML algorithms that integrate spectral CT-derived 3D quantitative parameters with clinical features for the preoperative prediction of mETE in PTMC patients. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0035/rc).

Methods

Patients selection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Chongqing General Hospital (No. KY S2023-083-01), which waived the requirement for informed consent. This was a retrospective diagnostic model development and validation study conducted at Chongqing General Hospital. Between August 2022 and February 2024, this study retrospectively enrolled 376 patients with pathologically confirmed PTMC with mETE who underwent preoperative spectral CT for pretreatment assessment. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (I) patients who had undergone surgery and other related treatments before CT examination (n=24). (II) Patients with suboptimal image quality (n=51). (III) Patients with missing or incomplete clinical data (n=45). (IV) Patients with concurrent primary malignancies (n=3). Finally, 253 patients were included in the study.

Clinicopathologic information

Clinical characteristics were retrieved from the electronic medical records, including age, sex, peripheral blood neutrophil (N), lymphocyte (L), monocyte (M), and platelet (P) counts. We calculated the following peripheral inflammatory markers: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = N/L, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = P/L, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) = L/M, systemic immune inflammation index (SII) = (N × P)/L, systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) = (N × M)/L. All PTMC patients underwent preoperative US‑guided fine‑needle aspiration biopsy for cytological sampling of thyroid nodules. Qualified specimens were further analyzed for BRAF V600E mutation status.

The study outcome was the presence of minimal extrathyroidal extension (mETE), which was defined histologically as tumor only extends through the thyroid capsule or microscopic infiltration into the adjacent anterior cervical strap muscles.

Spectral CT image acquisition

Image acquisition was performed on a spectral CT scanner (IQon spectral CT, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The following scanning parameters were employed: tube voltage, 120 kVp; smart mAs; turn-around time, 0.5 s; detector layers, 64 mm × 0.625 mm; matrix, 512×512. Non-ionic contrast media (Ioversol, 350 mgI/mL) was injected into the median elbow vein at a rate of 3–4 mL/s using an automatic high-pressure injector, following the standard dose of 1.5 mL/kg body weight. Arterial phase (AP) scanning was triggered automatically upon reaching a threshold of 110 Hounsfield units (HU) within the descending aorta at the tracheal bifurcation. The venous phase (VP) scan commenced following a fixed 30-second delay.

Conventional imaging features

Two subspecialists in head and neck imaging (A1 and A2, with 5 and 8 years of thyroid imaging experience, respectively), blinded to all pathological results, independently evaluated the conventional imaging features on both US and CT in all patients with PTMC. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus through discussion; if persistent, a third head and neck imaging specialist (A3, with 15 years of thyroid imaging experience) served as the final adjudicator. The following conventional imaging features were evaluated: microcalcifications, margin, the contact status between the nodule and the thyroid capsule on US (24), thyroid edge interruption (defined as a partial, often “bitten cookie”-shaped defect of the normal high-density thyroid contour replaced by low-density tumor) on CT (25).

Spectral CT quantitative parameters analysis

The AP and VP spectral-based imaging data were imported into the post-processing software [IntelliSpace Medicina Scientia (ISMS) Version 3.1.0, Philips Healthcare]. The software automatically generated iodine concentration (IC) image, monoenergetic images (40, 70, 100 keV), and effective atomic number (Zeff) image. A 3D volume of interest (VOI) of the tumor were manually delineated layer by layer on the axial slices by two abdominal radiologists independently. Inter-observer and intra-observer reproducibility analyses were performed on 30 randomly selected patients. The VOIs of 30 patients were re-delineated by the same radiologist after a 1-month interval and by another radiologist. The software automatically generated the quantitative parameters, including 3D-40keV, 3D-70keV, 3D-100keV, 3D-Zeff, and 3D-IC in the AP and VP. The slope of spectral HU curve (λHU) was calculated as follows: 3D-λHU = (3D-40keV − 3D-100keV)/(100 − 40); 3D-arterial enhancement fraction (AEF) = 3D-ICAP/3D-ICVP. The quantitative parameters extracted from these patients were subsequently assessed using the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Feature selection

All 253 patients were randomly allocated to either the training or testing cohort in a 7:3 ratio. First, variables were screened using the Pearson correlation coefficient, with an absolute threshold set at 0.9. Following this initial screening, feature selection was performed using the Boruta algorithm—a wrapper method built around random forest.

Building and assessment of models

The final identified significant variables were incorporated into four ML algorithms—light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), multilayer perceptron classifier (MLP-C), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) and logistic regression (LR)—were implemented to construct the predictive models. LightGBM is a tree-based gradient boosting algorithm known for its high efficiency and strong performance on tabular data [38]. MLP-C is a type of artificial neural network capable of modeling highly complex, non-linear decision boundaries. AdaBoost is an ensemble method that sequentially fits weak learners (typically shallow trees) while adaptively weighting misclassified instances. LR was chosen as the baseline linear model due to its simplicity, interpretability, and widespread use in clinical prediction research. A five-fold cross-validation method was performed on the training cohort. The testing cohort was used to independently verify each of the four models.

The performance of the 4 ML models was evaluated on both training and testing cohorts using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Key metrics, including the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, were computed. To determine whether the differences in predictive performance among the four models were statistically significant, we performed pairwise comparisons of the AUCs using the DeLong test. Additionally, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), precision, recall, and the F1 score were derived. The corresponding confusion matrices for each classifier were presented for the training and testing cohorts. To enhance model interpretability, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values were applied to quantify and visually demonstrate the contribution of each feature to individual predictions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with Python (version 3.10.9) and SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM). Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages, continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation or median, as appropriate. A two-tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variable selection

Twenty-five candidate variables (13 spectral CT 3D quantitative parameters and 12 clinical characteristics) were initially considered. Following the removal of highly correlated features (|r|>0.9), 15 non-collinear variables were retained for subsequent analysis. Following ranking by the Boruta algorithm, 4 of the 15 variables were validated as important, while 1 was deemed tentative. The 4 confirmed important features (3D-AEF, BRAF V600E status, 3D-AP40keV, and capsular contact status) comprised the final set for model construction. Discussion

In this study, we established four ML models based on clinical characteristics and 3D quantitative parameters for predicting mETE in PTMC and demonstrated good evaluation performance. These results indicate that the ML models can serve as a robust, practical tool for identifying PTMC patients with mETE prior to surgery.

Considering that individual differences have a significant impact on the evaluation of quantitative parameters (26), we adopted 3D quantitative analysis to more accurately evaluate the relationship between spectral CT parameters and histopathological differentiation. In contrast to 2D analysis, which captures only incomplete tumor regions, 3D analysis encompasses the entire tumor volume. This comprehensive approach provides a more reliable evaluation of tumor characteristics and minimizes observer-dependent variability (27). In our study, we observed a substantial increase in 3D-AP40keV CT value and 3D-AEF values in the mETE-positive PTMC group compared to the mETE-negative group in the training cohort. These parameters are simple to obtain and clinically practical. Nodules exhibited a significantly greater standard deviation in HU on 40-keV monochromatic images compared to traditional CT, indicating that the lower-energy images provide enhanced sensitivity to iodine density gradients and may better characterize intra-nodule heterogeneity. As a hemodynamic parameter obtained from dual-energy CT, AEF is calculated as the ratio of arterial to venous iodine concentration (ICAP/ICVP). This ratio, governed by tissue blood flow and iodine uptake dynamics in respective phases, serves as an indirect indicator of tumor vascular supply (28). PTC with ETE demonstrated greater neovascularization and blood supply (29). This translated into a higher iodine uptake rate and more pronounced early-phase enhancement, in contrast to the lower uptake observed in mETE-negative PTMC. Consequently, high 3D-AEF and 3D-AP40keV CT values are associated with increased tumor vascularity and potentially greater aggressiveness in PTMC Crucially, 3D-AEF was identified as the most critical predictive variable, highlighting its potential as an imaging biomarker for PTMC.

Our study also found that BRAF V600E mutation status and capsular contact status were identified as clinical characteristics for differentiating between mETE-positive and mETE-negative PTMC. Previous study has reported that the BRAF V600E mutation is significantly correlated with mETE (30). The BRAF V600E mutation is the most common mutation and genetic driver in PTMC and is associated with aggressive tumor behavior. Normally regulated by the mitogenactivated protein kinase pathway, BRAF kinase becomes constitutively activated upon the V600E mutation, thereby promoting tumor progression (31,32). The degree of tumor exposure to the capsule was significantly associated with both parenchymal invasion and marginal disruption of the thyroid gland (33,34). Lee et al. identified the extent of tumor-capsule contact as the most accurate predictor for ETE in PTMC, based on a dedicated assessment of capsular involvement (33). According to our SHAP summary plot analysis, the presence of a BRAF V600E mutation and significant capsule contact are positively associated with a higher risk of mETE in PTMC.

ML is a subset of artificial intelligence with excellent data processing capabilities and the ability to understand complex correlations between variables. In this study, ML models were constructed based on four classifiers, namely LR, AdaBoost, LightGBM, and MLP-C, for assessing mETE in PTMC. All four models demonstrated stable performance, achieving AUCs >0.85 in the training cohort and >0.75 in the validation cohort. However, the notable drop in AUC from 0.99 in the training cohort to 0.78 in the validation cohort for the LightGBM model indicates that overfitting may have occurred. Several factors may have contributed to this overfitting. LightGBM employs a leaf-wise tree growth strategy that can produce overly complex trees and lead to overfitting if key hyperparameters such as num_leaves and min_data_in_leaf are not sufficiently regularized. Although we employed five-fold cross-validation during training, we did not use nested cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, which is recommended to obtain unbiased performance estimates. To mitigate overfitting in future studies, this issue should be addressed through both technical refinements—such as more aggressive regularization, early stopping, or simpler models—and the inclusion of larger, multi-institutional datasets to improve model generalizability. Despite this overfitting concern, it is noteworthy that all models maintained AUCs >0.75 in the validation cohort, suggesting that even the overfitted LightGBM retained clinically useful predictive capacity. A comprehensive assessment using P-R curves and confusion matrices revealed that all four models demonstrated robust performance in the validation cohort, achieving high predictive accuracy for mETE status and maintaining elevated precision across a range of recall thresholds. The inherent “black-box” nature of ML models complicates output interpretation. To address this, we employed SHAP values to quantify feature importance and directional contribution, where features with a positive SHAP value support a diagnosis of mETE. Based on the stable LightGBM model, SHAP analysis—visualized via feature importance and summary plots—was conducted to intuitively explain predictions, thereby enhancing model transparency and facilitating clinical translation.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the sample size is relatively limited, and more importantly, the study lacks an independent external validation cohort. While internal validation using a random split provides initial evidence of model stability, it does not guarantee generalizability to patients from other institutions, different geographic regions, or those scanned with alternative CT equipment. Second, we observed evidence of overfitting in the LightGBM model, as indicated by the large discrepancy between training and validation AUCs. This suggests that despite careful feature selection and cross-validation, the model may have captured dataset-specific noise rather than fully generalizable patterns. Future studies should employ nested cross-validation and more rigorous regularization to address this issue. Additionally, the key molecular biomarker (BRAF V600E status) integrated into the model is not universally accessible preoperatively, which may restrict its clinical generalizability. Therefore, our findings should be considered preliminary, and the model requires rigorous external validation in large-scale, multicenter prospective studies before any clinical application can be considered.

Conclusions

We constructed four ML models integrating 3D quantitative parameters and clinical features for the early prediction of mETE in patients with PTMC, and all models exhibited robust accuracy. This finding validates 3D quantitative parameters from spectral CT as reliable indicators of mETE and highlights their potential clinical utility as biomarkers.
