Abstract
. We show that the set of atoms of the limiting empirical marginal distribution in the random 2 2 -SAT model is ℚ ∩ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1) , for all clause-to-variable densities up to the satisfiability threshold. While for densities up to 1 / 2 1/2 , the measure is purely discrete, we additionally establish the existence of a nontrivial continuous part for any density in ( 1 / 2 , 1 ) (1/2,1) . Our proof is based on the construction of a random variable with the correct distribution as the the root marginal of a multi-type Galton-Watson tree, along with a subsequent analysis of the resulting almost sure recursion.



1.
Introduction and main result

1.1.
Introduction

Random constraint satisfaction problems provide a flourishing research ground at the crossroads of different disciplines such as mathematics, physics and computer science. One of their key representatives is random
k
-SAT, a model well-known for its notorious analytic hardness.

The most accessible member of the random
k
-SAT family is random
2
-SAT, which stands out through its early success history: In 1992, it was the first random constraint satisfaction problem whose satisfiability threshold was established rigorously
[
9
,
17
]
, an achievement that was followed by even finer analyses of its scaling window
[
7
,
16
]
. In contrast, the existence and location of the satisfiability threshold of random
k
-SAT for
k\geq k_{0}
, where
k_{0}
is a large absolute constant, have only been established comparatively recently in the breakthrough paper
[
15
]
, while a corresponding result for smaller values of
k
is still not available. In a certain sense, the early success on random
2
-SAT reflects the fact that
2
-SAT is in P, while
k
-SAT for
k\geq 3
is (presumably) not.

Turning to counting problems, the distinction between
k=2
and
k\geq 3
through the lens of computational hardness is not as clearly cut: Determining the number of solutions is a #P-complete task, even restricted to
2
-SAT.
Indeed, the timeline of the subsequent results closely matches those considerations on complexity:
After first results
[
1
,
20
]
, the typical number of solutions of random
2
-SAT formulae throughout the satisfiable regime was established in 2021
[
2
]
(and later refined in
[
10
]
), roughly 40 years after the identification of the satisfiability threshold, and even after the seminal work of
[
15
]
.

A hallmark of the increased complexity can be found in the statements of the respective results: While the satisfiability threshold in random
2
-SAT occurs at the point where the number of clauses asymptotically exceeds the number of variables, the formula for the asymptotic number of solutions from
[
2
]
is considerably more involved. Indeed, the proofs in the line of work
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
15
,
20
]
, as well as many other articles, are based on deep ideas from statistical physics. As a consequence, the formula for the exponential growth rate of the number of solutions from
[
2
]
, which confirms corresponding predictions from statistical physics
[
19
]
, involves the evaluation of a functional at a probability measure
\pi_{d}
on the unit interval, where
d\in(0,2)
encodes the average number of clauses in which each variable appears. However,
\pi_{d}
is only implicitly characterised by a stochastic recursion (the so-called
density evolution
).
What is more,
the associated recursion is of a form that is not directly approachable by available results on stochastic recursions of an affine linear or max-type, for example.

The main purpose of this note is to provide a first step towards a better understanding of the distribution
\pi_{d}
, which one may think of as the limit of the probability that a uniformly chosen variable within a uniformly random solution is set to ‘true’. As we will see, for some values of
d
,
\pi_{d}
is mixed atomic and continuous. This diverse structure arises from an inhomogeneity among variable marginals which is absent in other random constraint satisfaction problems, such as random
k
-NAESAT or random graph
q
-coloring, where permutation symmetry ensures that all variable marginals are uniform. In random
k
-XORSAT and random linear equations, the algebraic structure ensures that the variable marginals are either uniform or Dirac measures
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
. Even in random
k
-SAT for large values of
k
, the differences between variable marginals are less pronounced because of stronger concentration properties
[
14
,
15
]
.

Simply speaking, the complex marginal structure in random
k
-SAT is owed to the fact that the variables are highly sensitive to imbalances in their local neighbourhood: A variable that appears in many more clauses non-negated than negated will tend to be set to ‘true’ in a significant portion of the solutions, giving rise to an effect suggestively called
populism
in
[
4
]
. As a consequence of the neighborhood fluctuations between different variables, a rather intricate distribution over different marginals arises upon choosing a variable uniformly at random.
The inhomogeneous marginal landscape in random
k
-SAT is also closely related to non-concentration effects that preclude an analysis via the very same techniques that have been successfully employed in the study of other, ‘symmetric’ models such as random
k
-NAESAT (see e.g. the discussion in
[
3
, Sections 1.1 and 3.3]
or
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
22
]
), random graph
q
-coloring (e.g.
[
12
]
) or random systems of linear equations
[
6
]
.

As random
2
-SAT is analytically far more accessible and better understood than random
k
-SAT for
k\geq 3
, a detailed study of its marginal structure in terms of the effect of neighbourhood fluctuations suggests itself. We hope that a more detailed understanding of
\pi_{d}
and its properties will also shed light on the question what kind of marginal structures one can observe in random constraint satisfaction problems with neighbourhood fluctuations, and are not aware of results of a similar kind for other models.

To formally speak about variable neighbourhoods, we represent a given

Proof of Proposition

. Instead of working with the marginals

\ell\geq 0
and a variable node
y
of
\boldsymbol{T}^{(2\ell)}
, let
\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)}
denote the sub-formula of
\boldsymbol{T}
rooted at the variable node
y
, truncated at depth

, and define

(3.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{y}^{(\ell)}=\log(\mu_{\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=1)/\mu_{\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=-1)).

This quantity is well-defined as any tree-formula
\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)}
has both a satisfying assignment

with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=1
and a satisfying assignment
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\in S(\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)})
with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{y}=-1
. Moreover, if
\psi

), we have the relation

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{T}_{y}^{(2\ell)}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=1)=\psi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{y}^{(\ell)}).

Finally, for a variable node
y
of
\boldsymbol{T}
, let
\boldsymbol{d}_{y}
denote the size of its offspring in
\boldsymbol{T}
,

the associated pendant variables and
\boldsymbol{a}_{y_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{y_{\boldsymbol{d}_{y}}}
its pendant clauses.
By the recursive nature of marginals on finite tree formulas (see (

(3.5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{y}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{y}}\mathrm{sign}(y,\boldsymbol{a}_{y_{i}})\log\frac{1+\mathrm{sign}(y_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{y_{i}})\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{y_{i}}/2)}{2}.

The basic idea of the proof of Proposition

is to re-use a contraction argument from

This gives

3.2.1.
Convergence of the log-likelihood ratios

We first show that

(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
and
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{j}}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
are
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-Cauchy sequences for any
j\geq 1
. Indeed, as the trees
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{j}
are identically distributed Galton-Watson trees, we immediately obtain the following.

Fact 3.2
.

For any
j\geq 1
, the sequences
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
and
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{j}}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
are identically distributed.

Thus, it is sufficient to consider the sequence
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
. We first establish the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
and
d\in(0,2)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell+1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]\leq\frac{d}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell-1)}}\right|}\right].

Proof.

First, using induction, we will check that for all
\ell\geq 0
,
\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}|]<\infty
. For this, observe that
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(0)}=0
, from which the claim follows in the case
\ell=0
. For
\ell\geq 1
, assume that
\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell-1)}|]<\infty
. Thanks to (
3.5
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\mathrm{sign}(o,\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\log\frac{1+\mathrm{sign}(o_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}}\right|}\right]\leq d\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\log\frac{1+\mathrm{sign}(o_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{1}})\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{1}}/2)}{2}}\right|}\right].

Now, since both the derivatives of
x\mapsto\log\frac{1-\tanh(x/2)}{2}
and of
x\mapsto\log\frac{1+\tanh(x/2)}{2}
are bounded by one in absolute value, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq d\left({\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{1}}}\right|}\right]+|\log 2|}\right)=d\left({\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o}}\right|}\right]+|\log 2|}\right)<\infty

due to Fact
3.2
and the induction hypothesis.

Next, fix
\ell\geq 1
and recall that
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}=\mathrm{sign}(o,\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}}),\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathrm{sign}(o_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})
for
i\geq 1
.
Thanks to (
3.5
), we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell+1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{-\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i}}^{(\ell)}/2)}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}}\right|}\right].

Due to independence, the last expression can be further simplified as

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell+1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i}}^{(\ell-1)}/2)}{{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell)}_{o_{i}}/2)}}}\right|}\right]\leq d\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}/2)}{{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell)}_{o_{1}}/2)}}}\right|}\right].

Now by an analogous line of computation as in
[
2
, Proof of Lemma 4.3]
, as the functions
z\mapsto\log(1+\tanh(z/2))
and
z\mapsto\log(1-\tanh(z/2))
are increasing and decreasing, respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{\log\frac{1+\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}/2)}{{1+\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell)}_{o_{1}}/2)}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\int_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}}^{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}\frac{\partial\log(1+\tanh(z/2))}{\partial z}{\mathrm{d}}z}\right|=\int_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}\wedge\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}^{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}\vee\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}\frac{1-\tanh(z/2)}{2}{\mathrm{d}}z,

\displaystyle\left|{\log\frac{1-\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}/2)}{{1-\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell)}_{o_{1}}/2)}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\int_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}}^{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}\frac{\partial\log(1-\tanh(z/2))}{\partial z}{\mathrm{d}}z}\right|=\int_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}\wedge\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}^{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}\vee\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}\frac{1+\tanh(z/2)}{2}{\mathrm{d}}z.

Conditioning on
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}
, we thus obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell+1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]\leq\frac{d}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{1}}^{(\ell-1)}}\right|}\right]=\frac{d}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell-1)}}\right|}\right],

where the last equality is once more a consequence of Fact
3.2
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

For any
d\in(0,2)
,
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
is an
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-Cauchy sequence.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell+1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}}\right|}\right]\leq\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(0)}}\right|}\right]=\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(1)}}\right|}\right],

as
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(0)}
=0. Thus, for arbitrary
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell_{2})}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell_{1})}}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{2}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell+1)}_{o}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell)}_{o}}\right|}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(1)}_{o}}\right|}\right]\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{2}-1}\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{\ell}\leq\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\frac{1-\left({d/2}\right)^{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}}}{1-d/2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(1)}_{o}}\right|}\right].

As
d<2
, this shows that
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 1}
is an
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-Cauchy sequence.
∎

As
\mathcal{L}^{1}
is complete, Corollary
3.4
implies that
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
converges in
\mathcal{L}^{1}
. We denote the
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-limit of
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
by
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
. As a next step, we show that
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
converges also almost surely to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

For any
d\in(0,2)
,
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
converges almost surely to its
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-limit
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
.

Proof.

By considering the
\limsup
as
\ell_{2}\to\infty
in (
3.9
), we obtain the bound
\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}|]\leq(d/2)^{\ell}\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(1)}_{o}|]/(1-d/2)
for
\ell\geq 0
. For
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary, Markov’s inequality thus implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left({\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}}\right|\geq\varepsilon}\right)\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}}\right|}\right]}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(1)}}\right|}\right]}{\varepsilon(1-d/2)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{\ell}<\infty.

By the first Borel-Cantelli lemma
|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}|\geq\varepsilon
only finitely often. As a consequence,
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)})_{\ell\geq 0}
converges almost surely to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

For any
d\in(0,2)
,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
satisfies the almost sure relation

(3.10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=\sum_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}(-\boldsymbol{s}_{j})\cdot\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{j}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{j}}/2)}{2}.

Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
has distribution
\rho_{d}
, where
\rho_{d}
is the unique fixed point of
\mathrm{LL}_{d}
in
(\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),W_{2})
from Lemma
2.1
.

Proof.

Taking the limit
\ell\to\infty
in (
3.5
) for

and using the almost sure convergence from Corollary
3.5
yields the almost sure relation (
3.10
). To identify the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
, let
\rho^{(\ell)}
denote the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(\ell)}
for

, and observe that
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}^{(0)}=0\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. The distribution
\rho^{(\ell)}
for

-\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\mathrm{sign}(o,\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\log\frac{1+\mathrm{sign}(o_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}\quad{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize d}}}{{=}}}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}\mathrm{sign}(o,\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\log\frac{1+\mathrm{sign}(o_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{o_{i}})\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{(\ell-1)}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2},

-SAT tree factor graphs

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{T}=T)>0

4.1.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}:=\left\{{\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1):T\text{ is a finite rooted tree with root }o}\right\}

be the set of root marginals of
2
-SAT formulae encoded by finite rooted tree factor graphs. As a preparation to Proposition

, we first prove two small lemmata.

Lemma 4.1
.

q\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}\Longrightarrow 1-q\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
.

Proof.

Assume that
q\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
and let
T
be a finite factor tree with root
o
and
\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=q
. Define
T^{\prime}
to be the finite factor tree that has the same graph structure as
T
, but where the original edge labels of
T
have been multiplied by
-1
. Then
S(T^{\prime})=\{-\sigma:\sigma\in S(T)\}
, and in particular both formulas have the same number of solutions. Correspondingly the proportion of solutions of
T^{\prime}
where the root is set to
1
is identical to the proportion of solutions of
T^{\prime}
where the root is set to
-1
. In other words,
\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=-1)=1-\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=1-q
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

p,q\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}\Longrightarrow\frac{p}{p+q}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
.

Proof.

Assume that
p,q\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
and let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two finite factor trees with roots
o_{1},o_{2}
and
\mu_{T_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o_{1}}=1)=p
and
\mu_{T_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o_{2}}=1)=q
. Consider the tree
T
that is built from
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
by joining them through a new root
o
as follows. The root
o
has exactly one
(-,+)
child and one
(+,+)
child. At the
(-,+)
child, we append the tree
T_{1}
at
o_{1}
. At the
(+,+)
child, we append the tree
T_{2}
at
o_{2}
. According to (
2.2
),
\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=\frac{p}{p+q}
.
∎

We are now in the position to show that every rational number occurs as the root marginal of a rooted 2-SAT factor tree.

Proposition 4.3
.

We have

\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1).

Proof.

First, observe that
\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
, as for any finite factor tree
T
with root
o
,
\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)
is nothing but the fraction of solutions
\sigma
of the finite tree formula
T
in which the root is set to
1
. Moreover, it cannot happen that all solutions
\sigma
have
\sigma_{o}=1
(or
\sigma_{o}=-1
), as assigning the value
-1
to
o
can always be completed to a valid solution of
T
by tracing the implications down the tree. Thus,
\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
.

The basic proof idea for the opposite inclusion
\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}\supseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
is to build deeper and deeper trees with more complex root marginals, using smaller subtrees along with the recursive computation of marginals that is possible in trees. Using Lemmata
4.1
and
4.2
, we can now proceed by means of a nested induction.

Induction beginning (I.B.):
We show that
\{\frac{1}{k}:k>1\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
by induction on
k\geq 2
.

Induction beginning:
1/2\in\mathbb{A}_{T}
. Let
T
be the tree of height
0
that just consists of an isolated root variable
o
. We then have
\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=\frac{1}{2}
, so
\frac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
.

Induction step:
1/k\in\mathbb{A}_{T}\Rightarrow 1/(k+1)\in\mathbb{A}_{T}
. Assume that
1/k\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
and let
T
be a finite rooted factor tree with root
o
such that
1/k=\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)
. Consider the tree
T^{\prime}
that is built from
T
as follows: At level zero, there is the root variable
o^{\prime}
. It has exactly one
(-,+)
child. At the
(-,+)
child, we append the tree
T
at
o
. According to (
2.2
),

\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=\frac{\frac{1}{k}}{\frac{1}{k}+1}=\frac{1}{k+1}.

Thus,
\frac{1}{k+1}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
. This concludes the proof of the outer induction beginning
I.B.
.

Induction step.
We next show that for any fixed
r\geq 1
,
\{\frac{r}{k}:k>r\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
implies
\{\frac{r+1}{k}:k>r+1\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
.

Therefore, the
induction hypothesis (I.H.)
is that for some
r\geq 1
,
\{\frac{r}{k}:k>r\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
(
I.H.
). We will show that
\{\frac{r+1}{k}:k>r+1\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
by induction on
k>r+1
.

Induction beginning:
k=r+2
. As
\{\frac{1}{k}:k>1\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
according to the (outer) induction beginning
I.B.
,
1/(r+2)\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
. This implies that
(r+1)/(r+2)=1-1/(r+2)\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
, using Lemma
4.1
.

Induction step:
(r+1)/k\in\mathbb{A}_{T}\Rightarrow(r+1)/(k+1)\in\mathbb{A}_{T}
. Assume that
(r+1)/k\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
for some
k\geq r+2
. By the (outer) induction hypothesis
I.H.
,
r/k\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
. As a consequence of Lemma
4.1
, also
(k-r)/k\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}
. Now Lemma
4.2
implies that

\frac{r+1}{k+1}=\frac{\frac{r+1}{k}}{\frac{r+1}{k}+\frac{k-r}{k}}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{T}}.

This concludes the induction, which in turn clearly shows that any rational number in
(0,1)
is contained in
\mathbb{A}_{T}
.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Proposition
1.4

We now have all our ducks in a row. Let
q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
. According to Proposition
4.3
,
q=\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)
for some finite 2-SAT factor tree
T
with root
o
. Moreover
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{T}=T)>0
. On the event
\{\boldsymbol{T}=T\}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{o}=1)=q
. Thus
\pi_{d}(\{q\})\geq\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{T}=T)>0
, from which we conclude that
\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{p.p.}}
.

5.
Proof of Proposition
1.5

To prove the inclusion
\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{p.p.}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
as well as the existence of a non-trivial continuous part in
\pi_{d}
for
d\in(1,2)
, in Section
5.1
we analyse the distribution of the random variable
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}
from Proposition
3.1
on the event
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
, which has non-zero probability for
d\in(1,2)
. As finite trees cannot give rise to a continuous distribution, this is a natural candidate for the continuous part of
\pi_{d}
, and in fact, Lemma
5.1
below shows that this part is purely continuous. In Section
5.2
, we will then use a similar line of argument to show that the support of the continuous part
\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}}
is
[0,1]
. The proof of Proposition
1.5
is given in the final Section
5.3
.

5.1.
Infinite trees have no atoms

The main step in the proof of Proposition
1.5
is Lemma
5.1
, which we state and prove in this section.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
d\in(1,2)
and
x\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)=0.

To prove Lemma
5.1
, it will, once more, be more convenient to work with log-likelihood ratios. To this end, recall the random variables
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o},(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{j}})_{j\geq 1}
from Section
3.2.1
as well as the almost sure relation (
3.10
). We then consider the maximal point mass

\vartheta^{\ast}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=z||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)\in[0,1]

of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
conditionally on having sampled an infinite tree. Observe that the supremum is attained in some value
z^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=z^{\ast}||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)=\vartheta^{\ast}
.
We further define

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}=\left\{{i\in[\boldsymbol{d}_{o}]:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|<\infty}\right\},\quad\mathcal{T}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=\left\{{i\in[\boldsymbol{d}_{o}]:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|=\infty}\right\}.

We may then decompose the sum over subtrees (
3.10
) into a contribution from the infinite and the finite subtrees:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}=:\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}.

This decomposition is well-defined as all single subtree contributions are finite almost surely.
The first observation underlying the proof of Lemma
5.1
is an application of the following fact to the decomposition (
5.2
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
be two independent, real-valued random variables.
Then

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y}=z)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Y}=z).

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{X}}
denote the distribution of
\boldsymbol{X}
. Then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y}=z}\right)=\int\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{Y}=z-x}\right)\,d\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{X}}(x)\leq\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Y}=z^{\prime})\int 1\,d\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{X}}(x)=\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Y}=z^{\prime}).

∎

Note that in the decomposition
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
from (
5.2
),

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
are conditionally independent given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
.
Indeed, among all the grandchildren
o_{1},\ldots,o_{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}}
of the root
o
, by Poisson thinning,
the numbers
\boldsymbol{d}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
of grandchildren
i
satisfying

\{|\boldsymbol{T}_{o_{i}}|=\infty\}
and
\{|\boldsymbol{T}_{o_{i}}|<\infty\}
, respectively,
follow independent Poisson distributions.
Since the condition
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
only restricts
\boldsymbol{d}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}>0
,

\boldsymbol{d}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
remain conditionally independent,
which implies that
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
are also conditionally independent. Then,
an application of Lemma
5.2
to the decomposition
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
conditionally on the event
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
, yields the bound

(5.5)

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\ast}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=z\mid|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=z\mid|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty).

The next lemma establishes a reverse bound from which it will be immediate that
\vartheta^{\ast}=0
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
d\in(1,2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=z\mid|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)\leq\vartheta^{\ast}/2.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{N}
as well as
\emptyset\not=I\subseteq[t]
. By the law of total probability, it is sufficient to show that for any such choice of
t
and
I
, for all
z\in\mathbb{R},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=z\mid\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{d}}_{o}=t,\forall i\in I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|=\infty,\forall j\in[t]\setminus I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|<\infty}\right)\leq\vartheta^{\ast}/2.

To this end, let
i_{\ast}=\min I
be the ‘first’ infinite subtree. As a first step, we show that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({-\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}/2)}{2}=z\Big|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{d}}_{o}=t,\forall i\in I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|=\infty,\forall j\in[t]\setminus I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|<\infty}\right)\leq\vartheta^{\ast}/2.

For now, assume that
z>0
. Given the number
t
of subtrees of the root
o
as well as the indices
I
of the infinite subtrees, the subtrees are independent. Recalling the mutually inverse functions
\psi,\varphi
defined in (
2.1
) and that
z>0
, the left hand side of (
5.7
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({-\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}\log\left({\psi(\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}})}\right)=z\big|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{d}}_{o}=t,\forall i\in I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|=\infty,\forall j\in[t]\setminus I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|<\infty}\right)

(5.8)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}=1}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}=\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\varphi\left({\mathrm{e}^{-z}}\right)\big||\boldsymbol{T}_{i_{\ast}}|=\infty}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}=\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\varphi\left({\mathrm{e}^{-z}}\right)\big||\boldsymbol{T}_{i_{\ast}}|=\infty}\right),

where we have conditioned on
\{\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}=1\}
for
z>0
, as
\log(\psi(\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}))<0
almost surely.
On the other hand, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}_{i_{\ast}}|=\infty\}
agrees with the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
. Thus, we obtain (
5.7
) for
z>0
. Of course, the case
z<0
can be treated analogously by conditioning on
\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}=-1
in (
5.8
). Finally, in the case
z=0
, the first conditional probability in (
5.8
) is
0
, since given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}_{i_{\ast}}|=\infty
},
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}<\infty
almost surely. Otherwise, as
\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty)>0
for
d>1
, and using again that the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}_{i_{\ast}}|=\infty\}
agrees with the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
,
\pi_{d}
would have an atom in
1
, which is ruled out by Remark
1.2
. Therefore,
-\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}\log(\psi(\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}))\neq 0
, so that (
5.7
) indeed holds for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

The claim now follows from another application of Lemma
5.2
: To this end, given the number
t
of subtrees of the root
o
as well as the indices
I
of the infinite subtrees, we decompose
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
further as

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=-\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{\ast}}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i_{\ast}}}/2)}{2}+\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{i_{\ast}\}}(-\boldsymbol{s}_{i})\log\frac{1+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{i}}/2)}{2}=:\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}^{(2)}.

Using conditional independence of the subtrees and (
5.7
), we thus obtain from Lemma
5.2
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}^{(2)}\mid\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{d}}_{o}=t,\forall i\in I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|=\infty,\forall j\in[t]\setminus I:|\boldsymbol{T}_{i}|<\infty}\right)\leq\vartheta^{\ast}/2,

and therefore (
5.6
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

It follows directly from the combination of (
5.5
) and
Lemma

5.3
that

\displaystyle 0\leq\vartheta^{\ast}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=z||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}=z||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty)\leq\vartheta^{\ast}/2,

which can hold only if
\vartheta^{\ast}=0
. This means that the law of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
conditional on
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
has no atoms. As
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=\psi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o})
and
\psi
is injective, the claim follows.
∎

5.2.
Infinite tree support

It follows from Proposition
4.3
and Lemma
5.1
that the restriction of the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}
to the event
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty\}
is purely discrete, while its restriction to
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
is purely continuous.

Proposition

4.3
shows that the pure point support of the discrete measure
A\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}\in A,|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty)
is
\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
. We next identify the support of the continuous measure
A\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}\in A,|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For
d\in(1,2)
,
\pi_{d}
has a non-trivial continuous part
\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}}
with
\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}})=[0,1]
.

Proof.

As discussed at the beginning of this subsection, the continuous part
\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}}
of
\pi_{d}
is given by the restriction of the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}
to the event
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
. In particular, as
|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty
with strictly positive probability for
d\in(1,2)
,
\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}}\not\equiv 0
.

To establish that
\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{d,\mathrm{c}})=[0,1]
, we will once more analyse the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
and show that its support is the whole real line
\mathbb{R}
. To this end, we will again condition on a specific tree structure, which is sufficient for our purposes.

Fix
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
; we will then argue that
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}\in[a,b]|\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty}\right)>0
.
To do so, recall the decomposition
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
of the log-likelihood ratio into a contribution from the finite and the infinite subtrees of the root
o
from (
5.2
). As mentioned previously,
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}\in\{\pm\infty\}|\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty}\right)=0
as a consequence of conditional independence and Proposition
4.3
. Similarly,
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}\in\{\pm\infty\}|\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty}\right)=0
, as otherwise, using that
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}\in\mathbb{R}
almost surely, we could write
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}=\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
and it would follow that
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}\in\{\pm\infty\})>0
. This however contradicts Remark
1.2
.
As a consequence, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}\in\left[{c-\frac{b-a}{4},c+\frac{b-a}{4}}\right]\big|\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty}\right)>0.

On the other hand, given
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty\}
,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o_{2}}
has the same distribution as
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty\}
. As
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=\psi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o})
, depending on the values of
\boldsymbol{s}_{2}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}=-\boldsymbol{s}_{2}\log\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o_{2}}
or
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}=-\boldsymbol{s}_{2}\log(1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o_{2}})
.
Proposition

4.3
thus yields that
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
given
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty\}
is a discrete random variable with countable pure point support that is dense in
\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}\in\left[{\frac{3a+b}{4}-c,\frac{3b+a}{4}-c}\right]\big|\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty}\right)>0.

Consequently, abbreviating
{\mathcal{E}}:=\{\boldsymbol{d}_{o}=2,|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}|=\infty,|\boldsymbol{T}_{2}|<\infty\}
, by the independence of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}
given
{\mathcal{E}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}\in[a,b]|{\mathcal{E}}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{inf}}\in\left[{c-\frac{b-a}{4},c+\frac{b-a}{4}}\right]\big|{\mathcal{E}}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o,\mathrm{fin}}\in\left[{\frac{3a+b}{4}-c,\frac{3b+a}{4}-c}\right]\big|{\mathcal{E}}}\right)>0.

It follows that the support of the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o}
given
\{|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty\}
is
\mathbb{R}
. The desired result then follows from
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=\psi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{o})
in combination with the continuity and surjectivity of
\psi
.
∎

5.3.
Proof of Proposition
1.5

Let
x\in(0,1)
. According to Proposition
3.1
iii), we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle\pi_{d}(\{x\})=\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty}\right),

where we set
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({|\boldsymbol{T}|=\infty}\right)=0
for
d\in(0,1]
.
However, Lemma
5.1
implies that in fact for any
d\in(0,2)
, (
5.9
) reduces to

(5.10)

\displaystyle\pi_{d}(\{x\})=\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty}\right).

Now by Proposition
4.3
,
\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}_{o}=x||\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty}\right)
can only be positive for
x\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
, from which it follows that
\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{p.p.}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
.
Furthermore, for
d\in(0,1]
,
|\boldsymbol{T}|<\infty
almost surely. Hence,
\pi_{d}
is a discrete measure for
d\in(0,1]
.
Finally, for
d\in(1,2)
, the existence of a non-trivial continuous part with support
[0,1]
is proven in Lemma
5.4
.
