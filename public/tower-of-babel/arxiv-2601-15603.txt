On the Nonasymptotic Scaling Guarantee of Hyperparameter Estimation in Inhomogeneous, Weakly-Dependent Complex Network Dynamical Systems
Yi Yu, Yubo Hou, Yinchong Wang, Nan Zhang, Jianfeng Feng, Wenlian Lu

Abstract
Hierarchical Bayesian models are increasingly used in large, inhomogeneous complex network dynamical systems by modeling parameters as draws from a hyperparameter-governed distribution. However, theoretical guarantees for these estimates as the system size grows have been lacking. A critical concern is that hyperparameter estimation may diverge for larger networks, undermining the model’s reliability. Formulating the system’s evolution in a measure transport perspective, we propose a theoretical framework for estimating hyperparameters with mean-type observations, which are prevalent in many scientific applications. Our primary contribution is a nonasymptotic bound for the deviation of estimate of hyperparameters in inhomogeneous complex network dynamical systems with respect to network population size, which is established for a general family of optimization algorithms within a fixed observation duration. While we firstly establish a consistency result for systems with independent nodes, our main result extends this guarantee to the more challenging and realistic setting of weakly-dependent nodes. We validate our theoretical findings with numerical experiments on two representative models: a Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible model and a Spiking Neuronal Network model. In both cases, the results confirm that the estimation error decreases as the network population size increases, aligning with our theoretical guarantees. This research proposes the foundational theory to ensure that hierarchical Bayesian methods are statistically consistent for large-scale inhomogeneous systems, filling a gap in this area of theoretical research and justifying their application in practice.

II
Problem Formulation

In many complex network dynamical systems, the collective behavior is governed by node-specific parameters. Hierarchical Bayesian model provides a framework to model these inhomogeneous parameters as being drawn from a probability distribution that is itself governed by a set of unknown hyperparameters. Estimating these hyperparameters is key to understanding, simulating, and predicting the system’s dynamics. A dynamical system can be formally described in either continuous or discrete time:

\begin{matrix}\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=g_{i}({\mathbf{s}}_{1}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{s}}_{n}(t);\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\\
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(0)&\sim p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}),\\
\theta_{i}&\sim q(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}),\end{aligned}\right.&&\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad t\geq 0.\end{matrix}

(3)

In applications, the SNN model is often described in continuous time form (
3
), while the SIS model is typically formulated in discrete time form (
4
):

\begin{matrix}\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)&=g_{i}({\mathbf{s}}_{1}(t-1),\dots,{\mathbf{s}}_{n}(t-1);\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}),\text{ for }t=1,2,\dots\\
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(0)&\sim p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}),\\
\theta_{i}&\sim q(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}),\end{aligned}\right.&&\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{matrix}

(4)

In the above systems
3
and
4
,
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)\in{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the state of the
i
-th node at time
t
, and
g_{i}
is the function governing the dynamics of the
i
-th node, which is determined by parameters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\in\Theta
. And the parameters are independently sampled from a distribution
q(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
determined by a hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathrm{dyn}}}
, and
p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})
is the initial distribution of the nodes’ states, which is determined by a hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{prob}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathrm{prob}}}
. Denote the dimension of the whole hyperparameter space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
as
h\triangleq h_{\mathrm{dyn}}+h_{\mathrm{prob}}
. The dynamics hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
and the probability hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
are unknown and need to be estimated. Denote the system states at time
t
as
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)=({\mathbf{s}}_{1}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{s}}_{n}(t))
. An
r_{t}
-dimensional observation of the system is often a result of an operator on the entire system state, that is,
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=f_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
with
f_{t}:{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
being the operator at time
t
, where
r_{t}>0
represents the dimension of the observation space. In this work, we focus on the continuous time case (
3
) for clarity of presentation, while the discrete time case (
4
) can be treated similarly with proper continuous approximation schemes.

From the perspective of the measure transport map, we can connect the hyperparameters to the system’s evolution by expressing the distribution of the coupling of the system states and parameters at any time
t
with the measure transport map
[
75
,
114
]
. That is, under suitable conditions, see the regularity of Monge-Ampere equation
[
114
,
66
]
, there exists a proper map
G_{t}:{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)\mid\theta\right)=

\displaystyle G_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(0);\theta),

(5)

\displaystyle p_{t}({\bm{S}},\theta)=

\displaystyle p_{0}(G_{t}^{-1}({\bm{S}};\theta)\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})\cdot\det(\nabla G_{t}^{-1}({\bm{S}};\theta))q^{n}(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}),

(6)

where
p_{t}
is the joint probability distribution density function of the states
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)
and parameters
\theta
. By integrating out the other nodes’ states and the parameters in (
5
), we obtain the marginal distribution of the state of a single node at time
t
, that is,

p_{t,i}(s)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times\Theta}p_{0}(G_{t}^{-1}({\bm{S}};\theta)\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})\cdot\det(\nabla G_{t}^{-1}({\bm{S}};\theta))q^{n}(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{S}}_{-i}\mathrm{d}\theta,\text{ for }i=1,\dots,n,

(7)

where
{\bm{S}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
and
{\bm{S}}_{-i}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{n})
. Notice that this marginal distribution
p_{t,i}(s)
is a function parameterized by the true hyperparameters
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
and
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
, which means that the marginal distribution of each state at time
t
is determined by the initial distribution and the dynamics. This explicit dependence is the foundation of our estimation framework. We will henceforth denote this hyperparameter-dependent marginal distribution of the
i
-th state at time
t
as
\tilde{p}_{t,i}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
.

Since the distribution
\tilde{p}_{t,i}
depends on the hyperparameters, the states
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)
for
t\geq 1
contain the information of
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
and
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
. To formalize this connection, we first combine the dynamics and initial state hyperparameters into a single vector that
{\mathbf{h}}\triangleq({\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
, which lies in the hyperparameter space
{\mathcal{H}}\triangleq{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{prob}}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathrm{prob}}+h_{\mathrm{dyn}}}
. We then leverage the Rosenblatt transformation theorem
[
90
,
59
,
64
]
to establish a direct functional relationship between the hyperparameters and the system states. The theorem guarantees the existence of a transformation
\psi_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}^{n}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
which maps a random variable
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t)=({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
, where
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, drawn from a known base distribution
\mu
, for example, the uniform distribution, to the system states
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)
, provided both distributions are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. That is, for
t\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)=

\displaystyle\psi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),\qquad{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t)\sim\mu.

(8)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\psi_{t,i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

In this work, we focus on the case where the node states are identically distributed at any given time, i.e.,
\tilde{p}_{t,i}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})=\tilde{p}_{t,j}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
and
1\leq t\leq T
. Under this assumption, the transformation
\psi_{t,i}
can be simplified to a component-wise form, where a single function
\psi_{t,i}=\tilde{\psi}_{t}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\tilde{\psi}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
, is applied to each component of the underlying random variable:
\psi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}})=(\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),\dots,\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}))
. A key property of this structure is that it preserves the dependency between base variables
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t)
from the system states
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)

[
59
,
58
]
.

Thus, the observation can be written as
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})=f_{t}(\psi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}))
, where we write
{\mathbf{h}}
explicitly to stress the dependence on the hyperparameters. It is important to note that the time-dependence of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t)
stresses that the realization of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t)
at time
t
is not necessarily identical to
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}(0)
, while its underlying distribution
\mu
remains time-invariant.

We concatenate the sequence of observations till time
t
into a single observation vector:

{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}})\triangleq({\mathbf{y}}_{1}({\mathbf{h}}),\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}({\mathbf{h}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{r_{T}}.

(9)

A standard approach for estimating the hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}
is to minimize an empirical objective function, which measures the discrepancy between observations simulated with a candidate hyperparameter,
{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}})
, and the true observations recorded,
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}=\left({\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{\star},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}^{\star}\right)
, which is assumed to be generated by
{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}=\hat{f_{t}}({\mathbf{S}}^{\star}(t))
, where
{\mathbf{S}}^{\star}(t)
is the system states evolved given true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
with noisy observation operator
\hat{f_{t}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{t}}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
. This objective function is typically defined using a distance metric
{\mathcal{D}}
in M-estimation
[
107
,
124
,
9
,
74
]
:

L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})={\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}}),{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}).

(10)

The expectation of the empirical objective function
L_{n}
over sample distribution is denoted as its deterministic form
L^{\star}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
. In data-rich scenario, the deterministic objective function
L^{\star}
is minimized at the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, that is,

L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})=\min_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}).

(11)

This M-estimator framework is general and encompasses many classical estimation problems. For example, in Least Squares Estimation (LSE), the empirical objective function is the average of squared residuals

\theta&\sim\delta_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(\theta),\end{aligned}\right.\quad t=1,\dots,T.\end{matrix}

(12)

where
\delta_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(\cdot)
is the Dirac-delta function at
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
. In this formulation, a new input
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
is sampled and transformed by a time-invariant function

at each time step, and the observation is mapped by an identity operator

. The only hyperparameter to be estimated is the dynamics hyperparameter

(
Hyperparameter Estimation via Observation of Complex Network Dynamical System
)
.

For a complex network dynamical system (
3
), with the notations described above, an algorithm

is performed to obtain estimation
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}={\mathcal{A}}(L_{n})
for true hyperparameter

merging the observation that

L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{T}\|{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}\|^{2}.

(13)

where the norm is a mixture of Euclidean norm in the product space
\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{r_{T}}
that
\|{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}\|^{2}=\|{\mathbf{y}}_{1}({\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}}+\cdots+\|{\mathbf{y}}_{T}({\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{y}}_{T}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{r_{T}}}
. For simplicity, we omit the subscript
\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
in the norm notation
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}}
without ambiguity.

Remark 1
.

In most applications, the observations at each time are of the same type that
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{T}
,
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{T}
and
\tilde{f}_{1}=\cdots=\tilde{f}_{T}
. However, in some partially observable situations, the type of observations change at different time
t
, thereby they are described in different space
\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
with different dimmension
r_{t}
. This setting enlarges the generality of observations’ type used for the estimation problem.

The observation duration
T
and the network population size

are two critical factors in the consistency theory of hyperparameter estimation problem. Consistency with respect to the observation duration

can be formulated as a classical Nonlinear Least Squares Estimation problem, which is well-studied in the literature
[

,
7
,

,

,

,
94
]
. In contrast, our analysis focuses on the less-explored problem of consistency with respect to the population size

for a fixed observation duration

, demonstrating how the convergence of the estimator
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
to the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
is controlled by the network population size

.

Our primary contribution is to establish this consistency with respect to

in the challenging and highly relevant setting of weakly-dependent nodes. This is an extension beyond existing works, which have typically been limited to either systems with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) nodes
[
30
]
or classical setting where consistency is analyzed with respect to sample sizes as in (

). Since networks in real world are defined by their interconnections, the assumption of nodes’ states evolving independently is often unrealistic. Our analysis confronts this complexity by providing a theoretical guarantee for systems where dependencies exist but decay across the network

. While we first establish a baseline result for the i.i.d. case to build our framework, our main theorem extends this guarantee to the more general and practical weakly-dependent setting. In the following sections, we will introduce the necessary preliminary definitions and lemmas before presenting our main results. We will first detail the consistency of the estimate

for the Problem

III-A

Preliminary

Firstly, we make some assumptions on the dynamical system.

Assumption 1
.

The dynamics
g_{i}
in the dynamical system (
3

Assumption 2
.

In (
3
), the initial distributions
p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})
and
q(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
characterized by the hyperparameters satisfy the following conditions:

1.

the initial state distribution
p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})
and the parameter distribution
q(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
are supported on compact set, that is,
\mathrm{supp}\left(p_{0}\right)=\left\{s\mid p_{0}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}})\neq 0\right\}
and
\mathrm{supp}\left(q\right)=\left\{\theta\mid q(\theta\mid{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})\neq 0\right\}
are compact sets in
{\mathcal{S}}
and
\Theta
, respectively,

2.

the probability density functions
p_{0}
and
q
are Lipschitz continuous with respect to the hyperparameters
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
and
{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
, respectively.

The Assumption
1
is an essential condition in the elementary study of dynamical systems, which guarantees the solution’s continuous dependence on the initial conditions and parameters
[
6
]
. Combined with Assumption
2
, it guarantees the existence of Lipschitz continuous measure transport map
\psi_{t}
in (
8
).

In many practical applications, the observation operator
f
takes the form of nonlinear average of the states, for example, in the Susceptible-Infectious-Susceptible (SIS) model, the observation, infection rate, is the average of the infected states; in the Spiking Neural Network (SNN) model, the observation, Local Field Potential (LFP), is the average of the neuron’s membrane voltage
[
49
]
. Therefore, in this paper, we consider the observation operator
f_{t}
of average form.

Assumption 3

(Observation operator)
.

The observation operator
f_{t}
is an operator, that is, for every
t\geq 1
, we have

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})=f_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)),

(14)

where
\phi_{t}:{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
.

Combined with the above transport map
\tilde{\psi}_{t}
, the observation can be rewritten as
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)(t),{\mathbf{h}})
, where
\varphi_{t}\triangleq\phi_{t}\circ\tilde{\psi}_{t}
.

Then, we make an assumption on the optimization algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Assumption 4

(Optimization algorithm)
.

The optimization algorithm can find the minimizer of
L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})
for all
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. That is, the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
takes the objective function
L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})
as input and outputs an estimator
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
, which satisfying

L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})=\min_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}L_{n}({\mathbf{h}}),\text{ where }\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}={\mathcal{A}}(L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})).

(15)

Remark 2
.

The assumption for the algorithm can be relaxed to that the algorithm can find an approximate minimizer
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
of
L_{n}
, that is,

L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})\leq L_{n}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star}).

(16)

In the following analyses, we take the exact minimizer condition Assumption
4
for simplicity, while all these proofs can be conducted similarly under the approximate minimizer condition (
16
).

Remark 3
.

The existence of the minimizer can be guaranteed by several mild conditions. For example, if
{\mathbf{h}}\mapsto L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})
is lower semi-continuous for all
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
and
{\mathcal{H}}
is compact, then a minimizer
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
exists for all
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
and at least one measurable minimizer exists under some additional conditions
[
41
,
84
,
109
]
.

Only the ability of algorithm for finding the minimizer of objective function is not enough to guarantee the consistency of the estimator, because the objective function may have multiple minimizers. The deterministic objective function related to the estimation problem should be identifiable at the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
. We give the definition of identifiability and strictly identifiability as follows.

Definition 1
.

An estimation problem or its corresponding deterministic objective function
L^{\star}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
is
identifiable
, if
L^{\star}
is uniquely minimized at
{\mathbf{h}}={\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
.

Suppose
d:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
is a metric on
{\mathcal{H}}
. We say that an estimation problem or its corresponding deterministic objective function
L^{\star}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
is
strictly identifiable
, if
L^{\star}
is identifiable at the unique minimizer
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
and

\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})>L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star}),\qquad\forall{\epsilon}>0.

(17)

The identifiability condition is crucial and general for the consistency of the estimator
[
117
]
. Without this condition, it is not guaranteed for an estimation algorithm to find the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
even if the deterministic objective function
L^{\star}
is provided and the algorithm is effective as defined in Assumption
4
.

The following lemma shows that an identifiable and continuous objective function within compact hyperparameter space is strictly identifiable.

Lemma 1
.

If the objective function
L^{\star}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is identifiable and continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}
is compact, then
L^{\star}
is strictly identifiable at the unique minimizer
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{H}}
is compact, for
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we have

\left\{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right\}={\mathcal{H}}\setminus\left\{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})<{\epsilon}\right\}

(18)

is a compact subset of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Thus the infimum
\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})
can be attained at some
\tilde{{\mathbf{h}}}\in\left\{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right\}
by the continuity of
L^{\star}
. Recall that
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
is the unique minimizer of
L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})
, thus

\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=L^{\star}(\tilde{{\mathbf{h}}})>L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star}).

(19)

That means that
L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})
is strictly identifiable.
∎

To obtain the consistency of the estimator, we leverage the fact from M-estimator
[
107
,
124
,
9
,
74
]
that the discrepancy between the true parameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
and the estimator
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
attained by an algorithm can be bounded by the difference between the empirical objective function
L_{n}
and the deterministic objective function
L^{\star}
. To illustrate this statement, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose the deterministic objective function
L^{\star}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly identifiable at the unique minimizer
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, and
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
is the estimator obtained by an algorithm satisfying the Assumption
4
, then for any
{\epsilon}>0
, we have

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\eta({\epsilon})}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|\right],

(20)

where
\eta({\epsilon})
is a positive constant determined by
{\epsilon}
and
L^{\star}
.

Proof.

Since
\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})>L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\geq L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})+\eta,

(21)

Thus, when
d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}
, we have

L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})\geq\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\geq L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})+\eta.

(22)

For example, we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
.
Therefore, we have the contain relationship that

\displaystyle\left\{d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})\geq L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})+\eta\right\}

(23)

\displaystyle=\left\{L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq\eta\right\}.

Then, observe that

\displaystyle L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})

\displaystyle=L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})+L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})

(24)

\displaystyle\leq L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})+L_{n}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\|L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})+L_{n}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|L^{\star}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})-L_{n}(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n})\|+\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|.

The first inequality is due to Assumption
4
of the algorithm that
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
is the minimizer of
L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})
.

Therefore,

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|\geq\frac{\eta}{2}\right).

(25)

Finally, by the Markov inequality, we have

{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|\geq\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|\right].

(26)

∎

This property is denoted as stability in various researches
[
89
,
47
,
13
]
.

The work turns out to bound the supremum of the empirical objective function and the deterministic objective function with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

To bound the right term of (
20
), we use the well-known chaining method of Sub-Gaussian process to bound the expectation of the supremum. It is necessary to recall some definitions that will be useful in the following, which can be found in textbooks such as
[
113
]
.

Definition 2

(Sub-Gaussian Random Variable)
.

A random variable
{\mathbf{x}}
is called
sub-Gaussian
with
variance proxy
of
\sigma^{2}
if
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}]=0
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda{\mathbf{x}}}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(27)

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
are sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{2}
respectively, then

1.

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i})^{2}
,

2.

if
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
are totally independent, then
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}
.

Definition 3

(Sub-Gaussian Process)
.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}
be a index set and
d
the metric on
{\mathbb{T}}
. A random process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}
on the metric space
({\mathbb{T}},d)
is called
sub-Gaussian
if
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]=0
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(Z_{t}-Z_{s})}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}d(t,s)^{2}/2}\text{ for all }t,s\in{\mathbb{T}},\lambda\geq 0.

(28)

Definition 4

(Separable Process)
.

A random process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}
is called
separable
if there is a countable set
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that

Z_{t}\in\lim\limits_{s\to t,s\in{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}Z_{s},\text{ for all }t\in{\mathbb{T}}\quad a.s.

(29)

where
x\in\lim_{s\to t}x_{s}
means that there exists a sequence
s_{n}\to t
such that
x_{s_{n}}\to x
.

Remark 4
.

For random process which is continuous with respect to
t
, that is
t\to Z_{t}
is continuous, the separability of this random process is followed by the separability of the index set
{\mathbb{T}}
. Therefore, if the index set
{\mathbb{T}}
is separable, that is
{\mathbb{T}}
has a countable dense subset
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
, then the continuous random process
Z_{t}
with respect to
t
is a separable process.

Definition 5

(
{\epsilon}-
net and Covering Number)
.

A set
N
is called an
{\epsilon}-
net
for
({\mathbb{T}},d)
if for every
t\in{\mathbb{T}}
, there exists
\pi(t)\in N
such that
d(t,\pi(t))\leq{\epsilon}
. The smallest cardinality of an
{\epsilon}-
net for
({\mathbb{T}},d)
is called the
covering number

N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})\triangleq\inf\left\{|N|:N\text{ is an ${\epsilon}-$net for }({\mathbb{T}},d)\right\}.

(30)

Lemma 3

(Dudley’s Theorem
[
27
,
28
]
)
.

Let
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}
be a separable sub-Gaussian process on the metric space
({\mathbb{T}},d)
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup\limits_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}Z_{t}\right]\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}.

(31)

Lemma 4

(Bound of Entropy Metric)
.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{h}
,
h>0
, if the metric space
({\mathbb{T}},d)
is bounded, such that
\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})=\sup_{t,s\in{\mathbb{T}}}d(t,s)<\infty
, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}\leq 2\sqrt{3h}\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}}).

(32)

Proof.

By the volume property of Euclidean ball, we have

N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})\leq\left(\frac{3\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}{{\epsilon}}\right)^{h}\text{ for }0<{\epsilon}<\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}}),

(33)

and
N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})=1
for
{\epsilon}\geq\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}\sqrt{\log N({\mathbb{T}},d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}\sqrt{h\log\left(\frac{3\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}{{\epsilon}}\right)}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}\sqrt{\frac{3h\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}})}{{\epsilon}}}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{3h}\mathrm{diam}({\mathbb{T}}).

∎

III-B

Estimation via Observations of Independent System

Equipped with these concepts and lemmas, we are now prepared to establish our main consistency results. We begin our analysis with the setting of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) nodes. While many real-world networks exhibit dependencies among nodes, this case provides a clear and essential baseline for our framework. It allows us to demonstrate the core proof strategy and establish a consistency guarantee that will serve as a stepping stone to the more general, weakly-dependent case.

Problem 2

(
Hyperparameter Estimation via Observation of Complex Network Dynamical System under i.i.d. Condition
)
.

Consider the nodes’ states in Problem
1
are independent and identically distributed, that is, the system
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t)=({\mathbf{s}}_{1}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{s}}_{n}(t))
, where
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)\sim\tilde{p}_{t}(s\mid{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})
are i.i.d. random variables. The observations
{\mathbf{Y}}_{n}({\mathbf{h}})=({\mathbf{y}}_{1}({\mathbf{h}}),\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}({\mathbf{h}}))
generated with the simulated hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}
, where
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
for
t=1,\dots,T
, are modeled under Assumption
3
as

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})=f_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}

(35)

and the recorded observation
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}=\left({\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{\star},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}^{\star}\right)
, is modeled with noisy observation operator
\tilde{f}_{t}
with the states generated with the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
as

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\star}=\tilde{f}_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{\star}(t),\omega_{t,n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\phi_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\star}(t))+\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\right],\text{ for }t=1,\dots,T,

(36)

where
\tilde{f}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{t}}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
is a noisy nonlinear mapping, the noise
\omega_{t,n}=(\omega_{t,n}^{(1)},\omega_{t,n}^{(2)},\dots,\omega_{t,n}^{(n)})
, where
\{\omega_{t,n}^{(s)}\}_{s=1,\dots,n}
are i.i.d. Gaussian random vectors with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{r_{t}}
.

Thus, the empirical objective function is defined as

L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\|\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[\phi_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t))-\phi_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\star}(t))-\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\right]\|^{2}.

(37)

And the corresponding deterministic objective function is taking the expectation form:

L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=\mathbb{E}[L_{n}({\mathbf{h}})].

(38)

As presented in section
II
, the nodes’ states
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)
can be expressed as a transformation of a underlying random variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)
of known distribution, such that
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)=\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
under mild conditions. We will show that the Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
guarantee the existence of a Lipschitz continuous transformation.

Lemma 5
.

Under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, the nodes’ states
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)
of dynamical system(
3
) can be expressed as a Lipschitz continuous bijective transformation of a uniformly distributed random variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)
and hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}
. That is, there exists a Lipschitz continuous bijective map
\tilde{\psi}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)=\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
, where
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([0,1])
.

Proof.

By Assumption
1
, we know that the solution
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)
of the dynamical system (
3
) depends on the initial condition and parameter Lipschitz continuously. Combined with Assumption
2
, we know that the distribution of
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)
at time
t
is also supported on a compact set and depend on the hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}=({\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{prob}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}})
Lipschitz continuously. Thus, by the Rosenblatt transformation
[
90
]
, there exists a continuous bijective map
\tilde{\psi}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)=\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
, where
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([0,1])
. More accurately, the transformation is given by the inverse of the probability cumulative distribution function of
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)
, which we denote as
F_{{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)}(s;{\mathbf{h}})
, that is,
\tilde{\psi}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})=F_{{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)}^{-1}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t);{\mathbf{h}})
. Since the initial distribution of nodes’ states,
p_{0}
, and the initial parameter distribution
q
are supported on compact sets and positive in their supports, the derivative of the transformation and its inverse are both bounded from below. Thus, the transformation
\tilde{\psi}_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to both the underlying random variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)
and the hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}
.
∎

For the identifiability condition of the objective function, we give a lemma with which we can verify through the second moment of observation difference under i.i.d. case.

Lemma 6
.

Denote
\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})
as the second moment of
\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})
, with

\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\triangleq(\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}),\dots,\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})),

(39)

that is,

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}))^{\intercal}(\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}))\right]

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\langle\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}),\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\rangle\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,k\leq n}\right].

If there exists some
t_{0},1\leq t_{0}\leq T
, such that
\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})
satisfying
\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})\mathbf{1}\neq 0
for all
{\mathbf{h}}\neq{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, then the deterministic objective
L^{\star}
function is uniquely minimized at
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
.

Proof.

Notice that the deterministic objective function can be expanded as

\displaystyle L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{t}({\mathbf{S}}_{n}(t),\omega_{t,n},{\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|^{2}\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{t,n}^{(i)},\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}),\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\rangle\right]+\frac{1}{Tn}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{Tn}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}.

Since all
\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})
are positively semi-definite, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})\mathbf{1}\geq 0
for all
{\mathbf{h}}\neq{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
. Thus
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
is a minimizer of
L^{\star}
that
L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\geq L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})=\frac{1}{Tn}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}
. For
{\mathbf{h}}\neq{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, we must have

III-C

Estimation via Observations of Weakly-Dependent Complex Network Dynamical System

The preceding subsection
III-B
established a nonasymptotic scaling guarantee for hyperparameter estimation in complex network dynamical systems with independent and identically distributed nodes’ states. However, the core premise of complex networks is interconnectivity, making the assumption of independence a significant limitation for real-world applications. The proof strategy in Theorem
1
, which leverages the properties of independent sums of random variables, is no longer applicable in the presence of dependencies among nodes.

We now turn to the central contribution of this work that extends the consistency results to the more realistic and challenging setting of weakly-dependent complex network dynamical systems. To overcome the technical challenges posed by dependencies, we employ the concept of
\beta
-mixing to formally quantify the degree of dependence among the nodes’ states. We then develop a proof based on an independent approximation technique, which allows us to control the deviation introduced by dependencies and establish consistency results analogous to those in the independent case.

Firstly, we give the definition of weakly dependence by
\beta
-mixing coefficient.

Definition 6

(
\beta
-mixing Coefficient)
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
be two
\sigma
-fields on the same probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{P}})
. Let the probability measure
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}}}
be defined on
(\Omega\times\Omega,{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}})
as the image of
{\mathcal{P}}
by the canonical injection
\pi
from
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{P}})
into
(\Omega\times\Omega,{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}})
defined by
\pi(\omega)=(\omega,\omega)
. Then

{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}}}(A\times B)={\mathcal{P}}(A\cap B),\text{ for }A\in{\mathscr{A}},B\in{\mathscr{B}}.

(59)

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{A}}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{B}}}
be the restriction of
{\mathcal{P}}
on
{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
respectively. The
\beta
-mixing coefficient, or the coefficient of absolute regularity, is defined by

\beta({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{B}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}}}\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{B}}}(C)-{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{A}}}\otimes{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathscr{B}}}(C)\right|.

(60)

Moreover, for a random process
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, the uniform
\beta
-mixing coefficient of
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is defined as

\beta_{k}=\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta\left(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{t}),\sigma(\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t+s}:s\geq k\})\right),\text{ for }k\geq 1.

(61)

The estimation problem of the weakly-dependent system is defined as follows.

Problem 3

(
Hyperparameter Estimation via Observation of Complex Network Dynamical System under Weakly-Dependent Condition
)
.

Consider the nodes’ states in Problem
1
are weakly-dependent with
\beta
-mixing coefficient
\beta_{k}
of polynomial decay rate such that
\beta_{k}\leq k^{-\lambda}
with
\lambda>0
, that is,
\beta_{k}=\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta\left(\sigma({\mathbf{s}}_{t}),\sigma(\{{\mathbf{s}}_{t+s}:s\geq k\})\right)\leq k^{-\lambda}
. And the other settings are the same as Problem
2
.

A core technical tool in our proof is the independent approximation lemma proposed by Rio
[
87
]
, which allows us to approximate dependent random variables with independent ones while controlling the approximation error using the
\beta
-mixing coefficient.

Lemma 7

(Independent Approximation
[
87
]
)

{\mathscr{A}}
be a
\sigma
-field on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{P}})

{\mathbf{x}}
be a random variable defined on this probability space with values in some Polish space. Then there exists a random variable
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
, with the same law as
{\mathbf{x}}
, independent of
{\mathscr{A}}
, such that
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\neq{\mathbf{x}})=\beta(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathscr{A}})
.

Theorem 2
.

For the hyperparameter estimation Problem
3

2

{\mathcal{A}}
used for finding the minimizer of empirical objective function satisfying Assumption
4
, moreover, if the following conditions:

1.

the estimation Problem
3
is identifiable,

2.

the nonlinear observation operator
\phi_{t}
is bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
for
t=1,\dots,T
,

3.

the hyperparameter space
{\mathcal{H}}
is compact

are satisfied, then, with
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}
being the estimator obtained by the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, for any
{\epsilon}>0
, under metric
d:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
, we have the following consistency guarantee result:

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\eta({\epsilon})}n^{-\frac{\lambda}{1+2\lambda}},

(62)

where
C
is a constant, and we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
, which is independent of the population size.

Analogous to Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
provides a nonasymptotic bound for weakly-dependent systems, demonstrating that the convergence rate is at least on the order of
n^{-\frac{\lambda}{1+2\lambda}}
. The exponent reflects the cost of dependencies, as a slower decay of correlation (smaller
\lambda
) reduces the effective amount of information and thus naturally leads to slower convergence. Notably, when
\lambda\to\infty
, the systems tends to be independent, the exponent
-\frac{\lambda}{1+2\lambda}
approaches
-1/2
, recovering the result in Theorem
1
. The identifiability condition required by this theorem is more direct than the injective condition required by Theorem
1
. This is because, in the weakly-dependent setting, the dependencies among nodes complicate the analysis, making it challenging to establish identifiability through injectivity alone by Lemma
6
. Assuming identifiability directly is a standard and practical approach in the analysis of complex system and is feasible to verify in practice.

Proof.

The brief proof routine is similar to the proof of Theorem
1
. The main difference is that the system
\{{\mathbf{s}}_{i}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
is weakly-dependent, thus we need to take care of the dependence by the mixing coefficient.

Firstly,
L^{\star}
is strictly identifiable by the identifiability and the continuity of
\varphi
and compactness of
{\mathcal{H}}
by Lemma
1
.

By the definition of
\beta
-mixing coefficient and Lemma
5
, we have
\beta(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)),\sigma(\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i+s}(t):s\geq k\}))\leq\beta_{k}\leq k^{-\lambda}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
k\geq 1
.

As the proof of Theorem
1
, it is sufficient to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})\right]
by the symmetric method. But now, the system
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
is weakly-dependent, if we took the same proof by estimating the sub-Gaussian property of the summation, the dependence would enlarge the variance proxy to order
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
so that the consistency failed. Thus, we need to take care of the dependence by the mixing coefficient. This will ensure that the variance does not grow uncontrollably and maintains the consistency of our estimates. We split the supremum over
{\mathbf{h}}
to two parts by the independent approximation method.

For
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, we divide this sequence in module manner into
\lfloor n^{\gamma}\rfloor
subsequences, where
0<\gamma<1
. Denote the gap as
\mathfrak{G}\triangleq\lfloor n^{\gamma}\rfloor
, while the length as
\mathfrak{L}\triangleq\lceil n/\mathfrak{G}\rceil
. The
k
-th subsequence is constructed as
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)}(t)\}_{1\leq r\leq\mathfrak{L}}
, where
1\leq k\leq\mathfrak{G}
, such that

{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)}(t)=\left\{\begin{matrix}&{\mathbf{x}}_{(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+k}(t),&\text{ for }(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+k\leq n,\\
&0,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{matrix}\right.

(63)

The length of each subsequence is at most
\mathfrak{L}
.

For each subsequence, we can recursively apply the independent approximation Lemma
7
to obtain an independent copy subsequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t)\}_{1\leq r\leq\mathfrak{L}}
of
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)}(t)\}_{1\leq r\leq\mathfrak{L}}
such that
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)}(t))\leq\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}
. To achieve this, we firstly consider
\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right)
, the
\sigma
-field generated by the random variables after index
2
. By Lemma
7
, there exists a random variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t)
, with the same law as
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)}(t)
, independent of
\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right)
, such that

{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)}(t))=\beta(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)}(t)),\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right))\leq\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}.

(64)

Having constructed
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t)
, we can repeat this process for the remaining random variables in the subsequence. Suppose we have constructed the independent copy till index
r
, for the
(r+1)
-th random variable, we consider the
\sigma
-field generated by the original random variables after index
r+2
and the independent copy random variables before index
r
:

\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t),{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right).

(65)

Lemma
7
yields that there exists a random variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)\prime}(t)
with the same law as
{\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)}(t)
, which is independent of the
\sigma
-field
\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t),{\mathbf{x}}_{r+2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right)
, such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)\prime}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)}(t))=

\displaystyle\beta(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)}(t)),\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t),{\mathbf{x}}_{r+2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right))

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(\sigma({\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)}(t)),\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{r+2}^{(k)}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{(k)}(t)\right))\leq\beta_{\mathfrak{G}},

the equality holds because
{\mathbf{x}}_{r+1}^{(k)}(t)
is independent of
\sigma\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k)\prime}(t),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t)\right)
by the construction. By induction, we completed the construction of the independent copy subsequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)\prime}(t)\}_{1\leq r\leq\mathfrak{L}}
. For each
1\leq k\leq\mathfrak{G}
, we constructe the corresponding independent copy subsequence as the above procedure. Then, the union of all independent copy subsequences together form a copy of the original sequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, denoted as
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, with
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)={\mathbf{x}}_{\lceil i/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}^{(i\mod\mathfrak{G})\prime}(t)
. And the copy sequence satisfies that

{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)={\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t))\leq\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n.

(67)

Now, we can split the supremum into two parts as

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\right],

(68)

where
Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\triangleq\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}{\epsilon}_{t,i,k}\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle
is the summation over the copy sequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, with
l_{t}
defined in the proof of Theorem
1
. The first is expressed as the approximation error, while the second is the independent approximation for the original supremum.

For the approximation error, we expand the difference as

\displaystyle Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}{\epsilon}_{t,i,k}\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle-\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}{\epsilon}_{t,i,k}\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}{\epsilon}_{t,i,k}\left\{\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right\}.

For each first inner product, we have

\displaystyle\left|\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right|

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\langle\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{t,n}^{(i)},\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-(\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}))\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\|_{2})(\|\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\|_{2}+\|\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4B_{t}(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\|_{2})\mathbb{I}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t)),

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. Similarly, for each second inner product, we have

\displaystyle\left|\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right|

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\langle\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-(\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})),\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\|_{2})(\|\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\|_{2}+\|\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\varphi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4B_{t}(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\|_{2})\mathbb{I}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)).

Notice that
\|\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\|_{2}
is of scaled chi-distribution. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]

(72)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left|\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right|+\left|\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(4B_{t}\left(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\|_{2}\right)\mathbb{I}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t))+4B_{t}\left(2B_{t}+\|\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\|_{2}\right)\mathbb{I}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t))\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(4B_{t}\left(2B_{t}+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\omega_{t,n}^{(i)}\|_{2}\right]\right){\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t))+4B_{t}\left(2B_{t}+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\omega_{t,n}^{(k)}\|_{2}\right]\right){\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}(t)\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Tn^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}8B_{t}\left(2B_{t}+\sqrt{2}\sigma_{t}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{r_{t}+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{r_{t}}{2})}\right)\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8B(2B+\sqrt{r+1}\sigma)\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}

where the notation
B\triangleq\max_{t}B_{t}
and
\sigma\triangleq\max_{t}\sigma_{t}
are defined as in the proof of Theorem
1
,
r=\max_{t}r_{t}
, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function. The last inequality is due to the maximality of
B,\sigma,r
, and the fact which is known as Gautschi’s inequality, that

\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+s)}\leq(x+1)^{1-s},\text{ for }s\in(0,1),

(73)

with assigning
x=\frac{r_{t}-1}{2}
and
s=\frac{1}{2}
.

Since
\beta_{\mathfrak{G}}\leq\mathfrak{G}^{-\lambda}\leq n^{-\gamma\lambda}
, therefore, we have for the approximation error that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]\leq 8B(2B+\sqrt{r+1}\sigma)n^{-\gamma\lambda}.

(74)

For the independent approximation part, we can apply the same proof as in Theorem
1
to obtain that
G_{t,i,k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\nu_{t}^{2}\triangleq 4\left[(2B_{t}+\sigma_{t})M_{t}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\right]^{2}
, as similarly defined in the proof of Theorem
1
, where
G_{t,i,k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\triangleq{\epsilon}_{t,i,k}\{\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\rangle-\langle l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{g}}),l_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{g}})\rangle\}
is replaced the original sequence with the independent copy. Then, concerning the constructed independent subsequences, we need to split the summation over
\left\{G_{t,i,k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\right\}_{t,i,k}
over the index
i,k
into several parts more carefully.

The first step is same to split the summation in diagonal manner as in (
55
). But now, notice that within each diagonal summation, the elements are no longer totally independent, because we constructed the independent subsequences with gap
\mathfrak{G}
. Therefore, we need to split each diagonal summation into
\mathfrak{G}
parts once more, where each part contains independent items. For the diagonal summation
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}G_{t,i,i+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
,
1\leq k\leq n
, we split it with the constructed independent subsequences of gap
\mathfrak{G}
, that is,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}G_{t,i,i+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{\mathfrak{G}}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{\lceil k/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}G_{t,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}).

(75)

For each
k
, the summation
\sum\limits_{r=1}^{\lceil k/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}G_{t,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is computed over totally independent terms, and all terms are sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\nu_{t}^{2}
. Therefore, we have that
\sum\limits_{r=1}^{\lceil k/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}G_{t,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s,(r-1)\cdot\mathfrak{G}+s+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\lceil k/\mathfrak{G}\rceil\nu_{t}^{2}
. Thus, the total diagonal summation
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}G_{t,i,i+n-k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\mathfrak{G}^{2}\lceil k/\mathfrak{G}\rceil\nu_{t}^{2}
. Then,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}G_{t,i,k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
(\sqrt{\lceil n/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\sqrt{\lceil(n-1)/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\cdots+\sqrt{1}+\sqrt{\lceil(n-1)/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\cdots+\sqrt{1})^{2}\mathfrak{G}^{2}\nu_{t}^{2}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and that
(n+1)/\mathfrak{G}\geq 1
, we have

(\sqrt{\lceil n/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\sqrt{\lceil(n-1)/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\cdots+\sqrt{1}+\sqrt{\lceil(n-1)/\mathfrak{G}\rceil}+\cdots+\sqrt{1})^{2}\mathfrak{G}^{2}\nu_{t}^{2}\leq 6(n+1)^{3}\mathfrak{G}\nu_{t}^{2}.

(76)

Therefore,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}G_{t,i,k}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
6(n+1)^{3}\mathfrak{G}\nu_{t}^{2}
, and
Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
\frac{6(n+1)^{3}\mathfrak{G}}{T^{2}n^{4}}(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t})^{2}
. With the same notation
M\triangleq\max_{t}M_{t}
defined in the proof of Theorem
1
, we have

\frac{6(n+1)^{3}\mathfrak{G}}{T^{2}n^{4}}(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t})^{2}\leq 6\nu^{2}\frac{(n+1)^{3}\lfloor n^{\gamma}\rfloor}{n^{4}}\leq 6\nu^{2}\frac{(n+1)^{3}}{n^{4-\gamma}}\leq\frac{48\nu^{2}}{n^{1-\gamma}},

(77)

where
\nu\triangleq 2\left[(2B+\sigma)Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\right]
. Then, as the same routine in the proof of Theorem
1
, Dudley’s inequality Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
yield that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}^{\prime}({\mathbf{h}})\right]\leq\frac{576\sqrt{h}(2B+\sigma)M\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})}{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}}}.

(78)

Finally, we combine the two parts together to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})\right]\leq 8B\left(2B+\sqrt{r+1}\sigma\right)n^{-\gamma\lambda}+\frac{576\sqrt{h}(2B+\sigma)M\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})}{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}}}.

(79)

By the choice of
\gamma=\frac{1}{1+2\lambda}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{n}({\mathbf{h}})\right]\leq\left(8B\left(2B+\sqrt{r+1}\sigma\right)+576\sqrt{h}(2B+\sigma)M\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})\right)n^{-\frac{\lambda}{1+2\lambda}}.

(80)

Therefore, Lemma
2
yields that

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\eta({\epsilon})}n^{-\frac{\lambda}{1+2\lambda}},

(81)

where
C=4\left(8B\left(2B+\sqrt{r+1}\sigma\right)+576\sqrt{h}(2B+\sigma)M\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})\right)
is a constant. As stated in Lemma
2
, we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
, which is independent of the data size.
∎

IV
Numerical Experiments

This section aims to bridge theory and practice by demonstrating our results on two distinct and widely-used models: a Susceptible-Infectious-Susceptible (SIS) model, which is a variant of Susceptible-Infected-Resistant (SIR) model

, representing continuous-state dynamics in neuroscience. For both systems, we will show that the empirical estimation error decreases as the network size grows, providing strong numerical evidence that aligns with our theoretical guarantees.

IV-A

Data Assimilation in SIS Models

Susceptible-Infectious-Susceptible model is a classical framework for describing the spread of infectious diseases in which previously infected individuals become susceptible again after recovery. Examples of diseases that fit this pattern include the common cold, seasonal influenza, and COVID-19
[

In the SIS framework, the population of interest is represented as a network, where each individual is modeled as a node
i
. For a network with
n
nodes, the connections between individuals are described by an adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}
, where
a_{i,j}=1
indicates that node
i
is connected to node
j
, and
a_{i,j}=0
if there is no connection between node
i
and node
j
. Let
\gamma_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}
be the infection rate and recovery rate of the concerning diseases, respectively. Since individuals may have different levels of immunity, these rates are not necessarily identical across nodes. However, once the disease is specified, the rates are assumed to follow certain probability distributions, that is,
\gamma_{i}\sim p(\gamma\mid{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}_{\gamma,\text{dyn}})
and similarly for the recovery rate
\lambda_{i}\sim p(\lambda\mid{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}_{\lambda,\text{dyn}})
, where
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}_{\gamma,\text{dyn}}

{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}_{\lambda,\text{dyn}}

{\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t)

{\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t)=0
representing that the individual is susceptible and
{\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t)=1
representing that the individual is infectious. For
i=1,\dots,n
and
t=1,\dots,T
, the SIS dynamical system can then be formulated as:

\begin{matrix}\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t)&=\left(1-{\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t-1)\right)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{i}(t)\leq I_{i}(t)\right]+{\mathbf{\xi}}_{i}(t-1)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)\leq S_{i}(t)\right],\\
S_{i}(t)&=\lambda_{i},\\
I_{i}(t)&=1-\prod\limits_{1\leq k\leq n,k\neq i}\left(1-\gamma_{i}a_{k,i}{\mathbf{\xi}}_{k}(t-1)\right),\\
\lambda_{i}&\sim p(\lambda\mid{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}_{\lambda,\text{dyn}}),\\

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have addressed a fundamental challenge in the statistical modeling of large-scale complex network dynamical systems: the reliability of parameter estimation in the face of high dimensionality and data scarcity. While the hierarchical Bayesian framework offers a powerful approach for modeling inhomogeneous systems by estimating hyperparameters that govern parameter distributions, its theoretical validity for networks of increasing size has remained an open question.

Our central contribution is the development of a rigorous theoretical framework that establishes the consistency of hyperparameter estimation with respect to the growing size of the network population. We have formally proven that as the population size increases, the estimates of hyperparameters, which govern both the system’s dynamics and its initial conditions, converge to their true values, even with a limited duration of observational data. By leveraging a measure transport perspective and focusing on common mean-type observations, we established this guarantee for a general class of optimization-based estimators.

Crucially, this consistency result is not confined to simplified systems with independent nodes; we have extended it to encompass systems with weak dependencies, which more accurately reflect the interconnected nature of most real-world phenomena. Our theoretical claims were substantiated through numerical experiments on representative models from epidemiology (the SIS model) and neuroscience (a spiking neuronal network model). In both cases, the empirical estimation error was shown to decrease as the network size increased, precisely as our theory predicts.

This research fills a significant void in the statistical theory of complex network dynamical systems. It provides the necessary justification for applying hierarchical inference and data assimilation techniques to large-scale, inhomogeneous problems, ensuring that the resulting models are not only computationally tractable but also statistically robust. The findings offer confidence that as we build larger and more complex models of phenomena like brain activity or global pandemics, our inference methods will remain reliable and will not be compromised by the scale of the system itself. By providing this theoretical guarantee, our work paves the way for more ambitious and realistic modeling of complex systems across science and engineering.

For Least Squares case

Although the ANN can be viewed as a dynamical system with respect to the layer forward process, in practice, most training methods of ANN only concern the last layer output, therefore no time perspective observations are available in this dynamical system construction. However, the training process of ANN can be modeled as a single node system with the whole forward process transformation as the state operator, and each time the node resamples an input sample and perform this transformation on it, and the observation is state transformed from the input sample. In the case of training process of neural networks, the dynamical system estimation task via observations degenerates to classical Least Squares Estimation (LSE) or Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

As described in the section
II
, the least squares estimation problem can be formulated as a single node network with a
resample and transform
dynamics (
12
). Thus, we can prove the consistency of least squares estimation within our framework by considering the sample size in general least squares estimation researches as the observation duration
T
in our dynamical system view. We formulate and proof the consistency of least squares estimation problem under i.i.d. condition as an demonstration in the following section. The Least Square Estimation problem under weakly-dependent condition can be similarly derived along the proof routine in the main text, thus we omit the part for simplicity. To integrate the Least Square estimation problem to our framework
1
, notice that the hyperparameter to be estimated is
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}={\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{H}}
while the probabilistic hyperparameter space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{prob}}
is null space. The whole observation till time
t
is
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\star}=({\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}^{\star})
, where
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\star}={\mathbf{s}}(t)=f({\mathbf{x}}(t);{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})
. Thus, the Assumption
3
is naturally satisfied. Therefore, we can formulate the least squares estimation within the framework
1
. We state the formulation for least squares estimation problem under i.i.d. condition as following:

Problem 4

(
Least Square Estimation Problem under Independent and identically Distributed Condition
)
.

Consider the resample and transform dynamics (
12
), the hyperparameter space is simply
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
while the probabilistic hyperparameter space in
1
is null space. The observation is defined by

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})={\mathbf{s}}(t)\triangleq f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}),\quad t=1,2,\dots,T,

(91)

and the true observation is generated with the true hyperparameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
with noise, that is,

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\star}={\mathbf{s}}^{\star}(t)\triangleq f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})+\omega_{(t)},\quad t=1,2,\dots,T,

(92)

where
f:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
is an transformation operator,
{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
are i.i.d. random vectors, the parameter
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{h}
and the noise
\omega_{T}=(\omega_{(1)},\omega_{(2)},\dots,\omega_{(T)})

\{\omega_{(t)}\}_{t=1,\dots,T}

\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{r}

3

We denote
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}=({\mathbf{x}}(1),\dots,{\mathbf{x}}(T))
and the loss function as
\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}({\mathbf{h}})-{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\triangleq l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
.

Then, the empirical objective function
L_{1}
merging the observation thus takes the form of

L_{1}({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}).

(93)

And the deterministic objective function is the expectation of the empirical objective function, that is

L_{1}^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=\mathbb{E}[L_{1}({\mathbf{h}})]=\mathbb{E}[l\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}}\right)].

(94)

The empirical objective function
L_{1}({\mathbf{h}})
and the estimator
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{n}(1)

1

and

for simplicity.

.

Lemma 8

(Rademacher Symmetry)

(95)

where

are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables independent of
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
, that is

{\epsilon}_{t}=\left\{\begin{matrix}1,&\text{ with probability of }\frac{1}{2}\\
-1,&\text{ with probability of }\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\right..

(96)

Proof.

be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables independent of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{L({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-\mathbb{E}[l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})]\right)\right\}\right]

(97)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{T}]\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right)\right\}\right].

Since
l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
and
{\epsilon}_{i}
are both symmetric, the term
l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
has the same law as
{\epsilon}_{i}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right)
. This implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right)\right\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right)\right\}\right].

(98)

Then, the triangle inequality yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}\left(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right)\right\}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}(-{\epsilon}_{t})l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right].

(99)

The symmetry of
{\epsilon}_{t}
once more yields that
(-{\epsilon}_{t})l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
has the same law as
{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
. Note that
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
, then
{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
has the same law as
{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{L({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}(-{\epsilon}_{t})l({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\right\}\right].

∎

Now we propose the consistency theorem for least squares estimation Problem
4
as following:

Theorem 3

(Consistency)
.

For the Least Square Estimation Problem
4
with independent and identically distributed nodes, assuming the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
used for finding the minimizer of empirical objective function satisfying Assumption
4
, moreover, if the following conditions:

1.

the LSE Problem
4
is identifiable,

2.

l(\cdot,{\mathbf{h}})
is continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
,

3.

with
{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,{\epsilon}_{n}
be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables independent of
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
,
\{{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is a sub-Gaussian process with variance proxy
M^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}
for some constant
M>0
,

4.

{\mathcal{H}}
is compact

are satisfied, then

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\eta({\epsilon})\sqrt{n}}.

(101)

where
C=96\sqrt{3h}\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})M
is a constant, and we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
, which is independent of the population size.

Proof.

Firstly, since the problem is identifiable and
{\mathcal{H}}
is compact and
l(x_{i},{\mathbf{h}})
is continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, by Lemma
1
, we have that the problem is strictly identifiable.

Combining this with Lemma
2
, we have

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\eta({\epsilon})}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\|L({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})\|\right].

(102)

To estimate the right hand of the above inequality
102
, by Lemma
8
, we only need to focus on the estimation of the symmetrized process
\left\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
, where

Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}).

(103)

Note that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})]=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}[{\epsilon}_{t}]\mathbb{E}[l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})]=0
, and

Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{T}({\mathbf{g}})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})).

(104)

Denote
g_{t}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})=\frac{1}{T}{\epsilon}_{t}(l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}}))
. Since
\{{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is a sub-Gaussian process with variance proxy
M^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}
, we have that
g_{t}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is a sub-Gaussian process with variance proxy
\frac{1}{T^{2}}M^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}
, consequently

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda g_{t}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{t-1}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}\frac{1}{T^{2}}M^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}/2}.

(105)

Therefore, the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality yields that

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})-Z_{T}({\mathbf{g}}))}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\lambda^{2}M^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}}{2T}}

(106)

Hence,
\left\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is a sub-Gaussian process on the metric space
({\mathbb{T}},\tilde{d})
, with the metric
\tilde{d}({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\triangleq\frac{M}{\sqrt{T}}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
. Moreover, since
l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
is continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}^{h}
, it shows that
\left\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is also separable. Therefore, the Dudley’s inequality Lemma
3
yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right]\leq

\displaystyle 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathcal{H}},\tilde{d},{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

(107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathcal{H}},\frac{M}{\sqrt{T}}d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathcal{H}},d,\frac{\sqrt{T}}{M}{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{12M}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N({\mathcal{H}},d,{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}{\epsilon}.

By Lemma
4
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right]\leq\frac{24\sqrt{3h}\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})M}{\sqrt{T}}.

(108)

At last, consider adding the zero random variable
Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}}_{0})=0
to the random process. The supremum of absolute value can be rewrite as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})|\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}|Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})|\right]

(109)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}\max\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}}),-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}}),\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right].

The last inequality is due to
\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\geq Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}}_{0})=0
and
\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\geq-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}}_{0})=0
. It is routine to verify that
\left\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}
and
\left\{-Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}\}}
are both separable sub-Gaussian process retaining the same metric as
\left\{Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})\right\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
. And notice that adding one point to
{\mathcal{H}}
would not affect the covering number, we finally result in that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}|Z_{T}({\mathbf{h}})|\right]\leq\frac{48\sqrt{3h}\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{H}})M}{\sqrt{T}}.

(110)

The proof is completed with assigning this result to the symmetrized version of inequality
102
.
∎

When the observation operator
f
in Problem
4
is nonlinear, then problem turns to a Nonlinear Least Square (NLS) estimation problem. The consistency of NLS estimation problem can be derived from the above consistency Theorem
3
with the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Consistency for Nonlinear Least Square Estimation)
.

For the Nonlinear Least Square estimation problem that the observation operator
f
is a nonlinear operator in Problem
4
, if the following conditions:

1.

the nonlinear mapping
f
is injective with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
,

2.

the nonlinear mapping
f
is bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
with some common constant
M>0
,

3.

{\mathcal{H}}
is compact

are satisfied, then

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\eta({\epsilon})\sqrt{T}}.

(111)

where
C
is a constant, and we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
, which is independent of the population size.

Proof.

Recall Theorem
3
, we need to verify the identifiability condition 1. and the sub-Gaussian condition 3. in the Theorem
3
.

The empirical objective function of Nonlinear Least Square is

L({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{(t)}\|^{2}.

(112)

Denote
l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})=\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{(t)}\|^{2}
. The deterministic objective function

L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=\mathbb{E}[L({\mathbf{h}})]=\mathbb{E}[l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})]=\mathbb{E}[\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|^{2}]+r\sigma^{2}.

(113)

Since
f
is injective with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
, then
L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})
is uniquely minimized by
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, which means that the problem is identifiable.

Let
{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,{\epsilon}_{T}
be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables independent of
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
. We aim to verify that
\{{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is a sub-Gaussian process. Note that

\displaystyle{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\epsilon}_{t}\langle f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}}),f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})+f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})-2f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-2\omega_{(t)}\rangle

(114)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})+\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}).

where we denote

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\triangleq

\displaystyle{\epsilon}_{t}\langle f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}}),f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})+f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})-2f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\rangle,

(115)

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\triangleq

\displaystyle-2{\epsilon}_{t}\langle f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}}),\omega_{(t)}\rangle.

(116)

For the first term
\Xi_{1}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
, suppose
f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})
is bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
with some common constant
M>0
, then by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

|\Xi_{1}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})|\leq\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})\|\cdot\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})+f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})-2f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\|\leq 4\|f\|_{\infty}Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}).

(117)

Thus, Hoeffding’s inequality yields that
\Xi_{1}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
(4\|f\|_{\infty}Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}))^{2}
.

As for the second term
\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
, notice that the gaussian random variable
\omega_{(t)}
and the Rademacher random variable
{\epsilon}_{t}
are both symmetric, thus the law of
\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is same as the law of
\Xi_{2}^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})\triangleq 2\langle f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}}),\omega_{(t)}\rangle
. Therefore, we can calculate the expectation via the properties of gaussian random variable.

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Xi_{2}^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Xi_{2}^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\mid{\mathbf{x}}(t)\right]\right].

(118)

Since
\omega_{(t)}
is gaussian with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{r}
, direct calculation yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Xi_{2}^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\mid{\mathbf{x}}(t)\right]=\mathrm{e}^{4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\|f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})\|^{2}/2}\leq\mathrm{e}^{4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}(Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}))^{2}/2}.

(119)

That implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{2}(2\sigma Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}))^{2}/2},

(120)

which means
\Xi_{2}({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
(2\sigma Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}}))^{2}
.

Combining the two estimations, we have that
{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})-{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{g}})
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of
[2(2\|f\|_{\infty}+\sigma)Md({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})]^{2}
.

This result means that
\{{\epsilon}_{t}l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})\}_{{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}}
is a sub-Gaussian process with variance proxy of
[2(2\|f\|_{\infty}+\sigma)M]^{2}d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{g}})^{2}
.

Then, Theorem
3
can be applied to the nonlinear least squares estimation problem to complete the proof.
∎

For Linear Least Square Estimation, it is a special case of Nonlinear Least Square Estimation where the observation operator
f
is linear, that is,
f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})={\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}{\mathbf{h}}
. The consistency of Linear Least Square Estimation can be derived from the above corollary
1
with the following corollary.

Corollary 2

(Consistency for Linear Least Square Estimation)
.

For the Linear Least Square estimation problem that the observation operator
f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})={\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}{\mathbf{h}}
in Problem
4
, if the following conditions:

1.

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}(t){\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}]
is positive definite

2.

the given random variables
\{{\mathbf{x}}(t)\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
is bounded with some constant
M>0
,

3.

{\mathcal{H}}
is compact,

are satisfied, then with the Linear Least Square estimator

\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}=\left({\mathbf{X}}_{T}{\mathbf{X}}_{T}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{T}{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star}),

(121)

we have that

{\mathcal{P}}\left(d(\hat{{\mathbf{h}}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\eta({\epsilon})\sqrt{T}}.

(122)

where
C
is a constant, and we can choose
\eta({\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{d({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\geq{\epsilon}}L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})-L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}}^{\star})\right)
, which is independent of the population size.

Proof.

The empirical objective function Linear Least Square problem is

L({\mathbf{h}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}({\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}({\mathbf{h}}-{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{(t)})^{2}.

(123)

Denote
l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})=({\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}({\mathbf{h}}-{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})-\omega_{(t)})^{2}
. The deterministic objective function is

L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})=\mathbb{E}[L({\mathbf{h}})]=\mathbb{E}[l({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})]=({\mathbf{h}}-{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}(t){\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}]({\mathbf{h}}-{\mathbf{h}}^{\star})+\sigma^{2}.

(124)

Since the non-central second moment
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}(t){\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}]
is positive definite,
L^{\star}({\mathbf{h}})
is uniquely minimized by
{\mathbf{h}}^{\star}
, which means that the problem is identifiable. This condition plays the same role as the condition 1. in corollary
1
.

For the second condition in corollary
1
, the boundness of
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
guarantees the uniformly Lipschitz continuity of
f({\mathbf{x}}(t),{\mathbf{h}})={\mathbf{x}}(t)^{\intercal}{\mathbf{h}}
with respect to
{\mathbf{h}}
, and the boundness of
f
is guaranteed by the boundness of
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
and the compactness of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Therefore, the second condition in corollary
1
is satisfied.

Moreover, the Linear Least Square estimator (
121
) is the minimizer of the empirical objective function, therefore the linear least squares algorithm satisfies Assumption
4
. Thus, the proof is completed by corollary
1
.

∎
