Surface Structure Dependent Activation of Hydrogen over Metal Oxides during Syngas Conversion
Authors: Bing Bai, Yihan Ye, Feng Jiao, Jianping Xiao, Yang Pan, Zehua Cai, Mingshu Chen, Xiulian Pan, Xinhe Bao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Despite the extensive studies on the adsorption and activation of hydrogen over metal oxides, it remains a challenge to investigate the structure-dependent activation of hydrogen and its selectivity mechanism in hydrogenation reactions. Herein we take spinel and solid solution MnGaOx with a similar bulk chemical composition and study the hydrogen activation mechanism and reactivity in syngas conversion. The results show that MnGaOx-Solid Solution (MnGaOx-SS) is a typical Mn-doped hexagonal close-packed (HCP) Ga2O3 with a Ga-rich surface. Upon exposure to hydrogen, Ga–H and O–H species are simultaneously generated. Ga–H species are highly active but unselective in CO activation, forming CHxO, and ethylene hydrogenation, forming ethane. In contrast, MnGaOx-Spinel is a face-centered-cubic (FCC) spinel phase featuring a Mn-rich surface, thus effectively suppressing the formation of Ga–H species. Interestingly, only part of the O–H species are active for CO activation while the O–H species are inert for olefin hydrogenation over MnGaOx-Spinel. Therefore, MnGaOx-Spinel exhibits a higher activity and higher light-olefin selectivity than MnGaOx-SS in combination with SAPO-18 during syngas conversion. These fundamental understandings are essential to guide the design and further optimization of metal oxide catalysts for selectivity control in hydrogenations.

Introduction

Hydrogen adsorption and activation is a topic of broad interest and has attracted increasing attention in hydrogen involving reactions over metal oxide catalysts,1−7 such as selective hydrogenation,8−11 semi-hydrogenation reactions, and propane dehydrogenation.12,13 Despite extensive research to identify different types of hydrogen species over metal oxides in recent years, it remains challenging to understand their roles in selective hydrogenation. CO hydrogenation to olefins using an oxide-zeolite (OXZEO) based bifunctional catalyst material is a representative selective hydrogenation reaction.14−18 In this reaction, metal oxide is responsible for the activation of CO and H2, and partial hydrogenation to form active intermediates such as ketene, CH2CO.15 Ketene is then converted into light olefins over the active sites of zeolites/zeotypes.15,19−24 Generally, a higher reducibility and hydrogenation activity of the metal oxide will promote the formation of ketene intermediates.25 For instance, we recently showed that MnGaOx with a spinel crystal phase (MnGaOx-Spinel), which was more reducible than MnGaOx solid solution with a similar chemical composition, has a higher syngas conversion activity and light olefins selectivity when they were combined with SAPO-18 as composite catalysts.26 However, metal oxides were reported to be active catalyzing over hydrogenation of the reactive olefins,10,27,28 such as InZrOx solid solution, leading to reduced selectivity of light olefins.25 Therefore, it is essential to investigate H2 activation and the selectivity origin in CO hydrogenation and olefin hydrogenation.29−31

It has been widely reported that H2 can dissociate and form various surface hydrogen species following either heterolytic or homolytic cleavage over metal oxides.1,3,7,12,13,32−34 For instance, Li et al. reported that H2 dissociated on the CeO2 surface forming hydride species accompanied by the oxidation of Ce3+ to Ce4+.1 Yang et al. identified that H2 undergoes homolytic cleavage to form Ga–H species on α-Ga2O3 with more coordinatively unsaturated Ga3+.35 Dong et al. observed Zn–H and O–H species upon H2 dissociation on the ZnO surface using in situ IR. The resulting Zn–H species can be stabilized by adding Cr into the ZnO catalyst.32 Feng et al. reported the formation of Zn–H and O–H species on ZnZrOx solid solution,36 and heterolytic cleavage of H2 over GaZrOx solid solution, generating Ga–H and O–H species.33 In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that metal–H species such as Zn–H species36−39 and Ga–H species10,13,33 over oxides could participate in CO and CO2 hydrogenation. For instance, Ling et al. pointed out a two-step mechanism of CO hydrogenation on ZnO; i.e., CO was first oxidized to CO2 at the step site of ZnO and then reacted with surface metal hydride to form formate.40 A solid-state NMR Spectroscopy study by Chen et al. showed that Ga–H species on Ga2O3 can react with CO2 to form formate species.13 An in situ IR study by Yang et al. showed that Ga–H species on α-Ga2O3 reacted with CO2 to form formate and further methoxy.35 However, the role of O–H species, which are abundant, has almost not been studied yet in selective hydrogenation. More importantly, the regulation of the types of hydrogen species, the chemical origin of different hydrogen species on metal oxides, and their catalytic roles in the selectivity control are yet to be elucidated.

Herein, we studied the effects of the crystal phase and surface composition of the MnGaOx oxide on the formation of the hydrogen species. Hydrogen easily dissociates on the MnGaOx-Solid Solution (MnGaOx-SS) with a Ga-rich surface to generate Ga–H and O–H species. We tune the crystal phase to form MnGaOx-Spinel featuring a Mn-rich surface to further effectively suppress the formation of Ga–H species. These hydrogen species exhibit different activities in the hydrogenation of C–O and C=C, leading to product selectivity control during syngas conversion via the OXZEO.

Results and Discussion

Structure and Surface Chemical Composition Analysis of MnGaOx

MnGaOx-SS and MnGaOx-Spinel have been synthesized using the coprecipitation and hydrothermal methods, respectively, as in our previous study.26 X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals that MnGaOx-Spinel is a face-centered cubic (FCC) spinel crystal phase, while MnGaOx-SS is a typical Mn-doped hexagonal close-packed (HCP) Ga2O3 crystal phase.26 They retain their original crystal phases after the syngas reaction (Figure 1A and 1B).

Figure 1C and 1D show the presence of Mn–O–Ga, Ga–O–Ga, and Mn–O–Mn structural units on the surface of both MnGaOx oxide. On the surface of MnGaOx-Spinel, Mn–O–Ga dominates. For MnGaOx-SS, Ga–O–Ga dominates, and Mn atoms are partially doped into the lattice of HCP Ga2O3 to form the Mn–O–Ga, consistent with the typical solid solution structure (Figure 1B). Although X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and scanning transmission electron microscopy-electron energy loss spectroscopy (STEM-EELS) mapping analysis indicate a similar bulk composition for these two oxides, their surface compositions differ significantly, as displayed in Figures 1E, S1, S2, and S3. The line scanning analysis across the surface of MnGaOx-Spinel at different regions, e.g., along arrows 1#, 2#, and 3# until the surface depth of 1.5 nm, reveals an average Ga/Mn molar ratio of 1.4, which is significantly lower than 2 in the bulk. Further study with low-energy ion scattering spectroscopy (LEIS), which allows elemental analysis at one atomic surface layer (∼0.3 nm),41−43 shows an even lower Ga/Mn of 0.4 for MnGaOx-Spinel. In comparison, the MnGaOx-SS surface has a Ga/Mn ratio of 1.0 (Figure 1F).

Theoretical calculations on the formation energies of different surfaces confirm that MnGaOx-Spinel most likely consists of the MnGa-terminated and Mn-terminated surfaces (117.6 and 127.6 meV/A2) (Figure 2A). DFT calculations on Mn-doped Ga2O3 (110) surfaces suggest that the MnGa-terminated and Ga-terminated surfaces are more energetically favorable, with surface energies of 142.2 and 154.0 meV/Å2, respectively, compared to other surface terminations (Figure 2B).

Hydrogen Activation over Metal Oxides

The metal oxide was first treated in a flowing Ar at 400 °C for 60 min in an in situ FTIR cell, followed by H2 exposure while the temperature increased stepwise from 200 to 400 °C. As displayed in Figure 3A, two weak bands appeared at 3669 and 1974 cm–1 at 300 °C over MnGaOx-SS, corresponding to Ga–OH and Ga–H species, respectively.3,12,13,33 Similar hydrogen species were reported during H2 activation over Ga2O3 and Zr-doped Ga2O3 solid solution (with HCP Ga2O3 crystal structure).12,13,33,35,44 Weak signals of Mn–OH and Ga–OH–Mn were also present at 3645 and 3580 cm–1, respectively.45,46 When the temperature was further increased to 400 °C, the Ga–OH and Ga–H bands significantly intensified but remained narrow, implying that they were spatially separated without interaction.34,47,48 Both the intensities of the O–H and Ga–H species reach a steady state after 60 min exposure to H2. By contrast, there was only a strong but broad IR band around 3500 cm–1 over MnGaOx-Spinel when the temperature went up beyond 300 °C (Figure 3B). The band intensified with temperature and reached a steady state after 60 min. It indicates the presence of a strong hydrogen bond interaction between hydroxyl groups (OH groups).34,47,48 It is worth noting that there were hardly any Ga–H species observed over MnGaOx-Spinel under studied conditions from 200–400 °C. Isotope D2 activation over MnGaOx-Spinel further confirmed the generation of only O–D species, whereas both Ga–D and O–D species formed over MnGaOx-SS (Figure S4).

In situ FTIR study and D2-TPD study on H2 activation over the oxides. IR spectra of (A) MnGaOx-SS and (B) MnGaOx-Spinel. Profiles of (C) D2 and (D) D2O during D2-TPD.

Temperature-programmed desorption of D2 (D2-TPD) profiles in Figure 3C and 3D show that the Ga–D and O–D species on the MnGaOx-SS primarily desorb as D2 and a small amount of D2O. In comparison, the O–D groups on the MnGaOx-Spinel prefer to desorb as D2O with almost no signal of D2.32,49

DFT calculations show that the heterolytic cleavage of H2 leads to the formation of O–H and Ga–H species over Mn-doped Ga2O3 (110) by overcoming an energy barrier of 1.01 eV at 400 °C (Figure S5). By contrast, H2 dissociation to form the O–H and Mn–H species is not favored, evidenced by a higher energy barrier of 1.37 eV (Figures S5 and S6), which is also reflected by the free energy difference of the final state of Ga–H and Mn–H. Mn-doped Ga2O3 (111) has a similar hydrogen dissociation pathway (Figure S7). These results validate the experimentally observed O–H and Ga–H species over MnGaOx-SS (Figures 3 and S4).

In comparison, over the MnGa-terminated surface of MnGaOx-Spinel, heterolytic cleavage of H2 to form the O–H and Ga–H species is unfavorable, as the energy barrier is as high as 2.20 eV (Figure S8). Instead, it is facile for H2 to cleave on the O sites and the adjacent Mn sites in the vicinity of the oxygen vacancy with a barrier of only 0.78 eV (Figures S8 and S9). However, Mn–H species are unstable and the H species would migrate toward the O sites with an energy barrier of only 0.64 and 0.41 eV, thus forming more stable O–H species (Figures S8 and S10). Similarly, H2 dissociation on the MnGaOx-Spinel surface with Mn-termination is very facile to form O–H and Mn–H species at the O site and the adjacent Mn site (an energy barrier of 0.75 eV). The H species on the Mn site migrate to the O site to form O–H species, which is thermodynamically favored with no additional barrier (Figure S11). Note that the oxygen vacancies are evidenced by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) as shown in Figure S12. The above results reveal unambiguously that the crystal phase and surface compositions of metal oxides play a key role in the formation of different hydrogen species.

The Reactivity of Hydrogen Species in CO Activation and Olefin Hydrogenation

To gain further insight into the reactivity of various surface H species in CO hydrogenation, we first treated MnGaOx in H2 or D2 atmosphere at 400 °C and then exposed them to CO. The differential IR spectra in Figure 4A show that the O–H species were gradually consumed over MnGaOx-Spinel. Formate species (HCOO*) formed at the same time, evidenced by the characteristic O–C–O vibration at 1587 and 1368 cm–1, and the gradually intensified characteristic C–H band at 2950–2700 cm–1. This evidences the reaction of CO with O–H species to form HCO* species, and further to HCOO*, in agreement with previous solid-state NMR observation on spinel ZnAl2O4 oxide.38 Thus, the change in the O–H intensity was normalized by referring to the steady state of the O–H intensity at 60 min of H2 exposure (Figure 3A and 3B). Figures 4B and S13 show that the intensity of the O–H species levels off around 80% after 20 min of exposure to CO, indicating that the remaining O–H species are inactive. These data further support the presence of two types of O–H species on MnGaOx-Spinel.

(B) The normalized intensity of the O–H band (centered at 3669 cm–1) as a function of reaction time over MnGaOx-Spinel. (C) Spectra recorded upon exposing the H2 pretreated MnGaOx-SS to CO. (D) The normalized intensity of the Ga–H (centered at 1974 cm–1) and O–H (centered at 3669 cm–1) bands as a function of reaction time over MnGaOx-SS.

The differential IR spectra in Figure 4C reveal that both Ga–H and the O–H species over MnGaOx-SS participate in CO hydrogenation. With the Ga–H and O–H species being consumed, formate species (HCOO*) form, evidenced by three characteristic vibrational bands of O–C–O (1587, 1368 cm–1) and C–H (2800–3000 cm–1). Notably, Ga–H species are almost completely consumed, whereas only part of the O–H species participates in CO hydrogenation at 60 min (Figures 4D and S14). CH4, C2H4, and CO2 are detected in the gas phase effluents by synchrotron-based vacuum ultraviolet photoionization mass spectrometry (SVUV-PIMS) (Figure S15).

As shown in Figure S16, Ga transfers 0.36 electrons to H, which results in negatively charged hydrogen species, while H transfers 0.67 electrons to O, resulting in positively charged hydrogen species over MnGaOx-SS. The fact that fewer electrons transferred between Ga and H (0.36 e–) indicates a weaker interaction compared to O and H (0.67 e–). The DFT calculations in Figure S17A confirm that the energy barrier for the reaction of Ga–H species with CO is much lower (0.61 eV) than that for the reaction of the O–H species with CO (1.10 eV). It validates the fast depletion of Ga–H species observed in Figure 4D. Figure S18 illustrates that upon adsorption on O, H donates electrons to O, resulting in the formation of positively charged hydrogen species over MnGaOx-Spinel. With the cooperation of oxygen vacancies in the vicinity, the energy barrier is only 0.92 eV for the reaction of CO with the O–H species by inserting O* into the vacancy and C* reacting with the O–H species, forming CHO*. By contrast, the energy barrier rises to as high as 2.15 eV for the reaction of CO with the O–H species without oxygen vacancies due to the steric hindrance (Figure S17B). This validates the experimental observation that part of the O–H species does not react with CO at 400 °C (Figure 4B). Interestingly, we find a similar phenomenon for CO2 activation, as metal oxide-zeolite catalysts have been applied to catalyze the hydrogenation of CO2 to a series of different chemicals. As displayed in Figure S19, both Ga–H and O–H species react with CO2 to form formate species (HCOO*). Furthermore, Ga–H species are almost completely consumed by reacting with CO2 whereas the O–H species partially participate in CO2 hydrogenation.

We further took ethylene as a model molecule for olefins and studied its hydrogenation over MnGaOx using in situ IR at 400 °C. The bands of Ga–H (1974 cm–1) and O–H (3669 cm–1) simultaneously appeared upon exposing MnGaOx-SS to 100% H2 (Figure S20A). When the feed gas was switched to 0.6% vol C2H4/Ar mixture at 400 °C, Ga–H species quickly depleted, as shown in Figure S20B. Correspondingly, C2H6 was detected in the effluents (Figure S20C). In comparison, there was hardly any consumption of the O–H species. It proves that the Ga–H species over MnGaOx-SS are highly reactive in C2H4 hydrogenation forming C2H6, but O–H species are inactive. Figure S21 shows that C2H6 appeared when the temperature increased beyond 270 °C during C2H4-TPSR experiments on the D2 pre-reduced MnGaOx-SS, evidenced by the strong m/z = 29 and 30 signals. By contrast, only a rather weak ethane signal was detected on MnGaOx-Spinel even at a temperature higher than 400 °C. As displayed in Figure S20D, with the consumption of O–D species (centered at 2714 cm–1), an equal amount of O–H species (centered at 3669 cm–1) was generated.

The above results demonstrate that Ga–H species are highly reactive in both CO activation and C2H4 hydrogenation whereas O–H species are not reactive for C2H4 hydrogenation, which is further supported by DFT calculations (Figures S17 and S22). Therefore, a highly reduced MnGaOx-Spinel without Ga–H can selectively activate CO and avoid olefin hydrogenation, thereby simultaneously achieving high activity and high selectivity in the direct syngas conversion via OXZEO. Although the presence of CO could inhibit the secondary C2H4 hydrogenation reactions (Figure S23), MnGaOx-SS still exhibits a much higher hydrogenation activity than its spinel counterpart. As a result, the light-olefin yield over MnGaOx-Spinel–SAPO-18 is nearly three times that over MnGaOx-SS–SAPO-18 (Figures S24, S25, and S26).

Conclusions

In this work, we presented a systematic study of H2 activation, the formation of different hydrogen species over MnGaOx, and their roles in selectivity control for hydrogenation. The results show that the formation of different hydrogen species strongly depends on their crystal phase and surface composition. MnGaOx-SS with a Ga-rich surface gives Ga–H and O–H species upon H2 activation. The Ga–H species are reactive not only for the targeted CO hydrogenation but also for the side reaction of C2H4 hydrogenation under syngas conversion conditions. In comparison, H2 is cleaved on MnGaOx-Spinel with a Mn-rich surface, ultimately generating thermodynamically stable O–H species. The O–H species are solely reactive in CO hydrogenation but inactive for C2H4 hydrogenation. Therefore, it is highly desirable to use the highly reducible MnGaOx-Spinel for OXZEO syngas conversion as it does not form Ga–H but instead forms O–H species instead. The fundamental understanding gained in this work could guide further optimization of metal oxides for syngas conversion and other hydrogenation reactions.
