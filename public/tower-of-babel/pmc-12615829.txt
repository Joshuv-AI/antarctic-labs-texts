Steering polarization toward consensus in signed majority-vote opinion models
Authors: Masaki Chujyo, Shu Liu, Fujio Toriumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSigned social networks, which encode both positive and negative relationships, frequently constrain opinion dynamics, leading to sustained polarization. We address this challenge in the signed majority-vote model by modifying the fast gradient-sign method to incorporate a uniform, sign-matched edge bias that dynamically evolves with the current opinion state. Experiments on synthetic graphs and several large real-world datasets demonstrate that a moderate level of this bias dissolves polarization and promotes broad consensus, whereas strength-matched random perturbations preserve divisions in opinion. The required bias rises with the density of antagonistic ties but is largely insensitive to network size. These results suggest that consensus in large signed networks can be restored through lightweight, structure-aware interventions, offering a feasible approach for mitigating online social division.Subject terms: Complex networks, Computational science

Introduction

Over the past decade, online social platforms have fundamentally transformed how individuals form and exchange opinions, often amplifying extreme viewpoints and reinforcing echo chambers that deepens societal polarization. As political discourse, public health initiatives, and misinformation campaigns increasingly unfold in these digital platforms, achieving a nuanced understanding of the underlying mechanisms of opinion polarization, and the factors for its prevention, has become critical.

Opinion dynamics models have provided a quantitative framework for studying consensus or polarization formation in social networks1–5. Among these, binary-state models offer a minimal yet powerful lens. In the voter model6, each node assume one of two opinions and asynchronously adopts the state of a randomly selected neighbor. The voter model converges to consensus in any finite, connected network and has been analyzed on regular lattices7, small-world topologies8,9, scale-free networks10 and multilayer networks11.

While the voter model captures consensus dynamics, it fails to generate the persistent polarization observed in real systems. Therefore, we consider the signed majority-vote model, a synchronous variant of the signed voter model12. The signed majority-vote model assigns positive and negative signs to edges to represent friendly and antagonistic relationships. Each node adopts the majority opinion among its positively linked neighbors and inverts the majority among its negatively linked neighbors. When every triangular cycle has a positive product of signs–i.e., the network is structurally balanced–the system converges to a stable polarized state characterized by two coexisting opposite opinions. However, unbalanced sign assignments induce perpetual opinion oscillations and prevent convergence. This model provides a minimal framework for exploring how antagonistic relationships generate and sustain opinion polarization. While Li et al.12 coined the term signed voter model to describe a copy-based update rule, our study focuses on a signed majority-vote model, i.e., synchronous sign-aware majority updating. To avoid ambiguity, we henceforth refer to the latter as the signed majority-vote model.

To influence opinion dynamics, researchers have adapted machine-learning-inspired adversarial-attack frameworks and simulated platform effects, including recommendation algorithms and homophilic network formation. Adversarial-attack frameworks were originally developed to mislead neural-network classifiers by introducing minimal perturbations to input data. A prominent example is the fast gradient sign method, which introduces carefully scaled noise to induce misclassification in image tasks13,14. These frameworks have since been adapted to complex networks by perturbing edges rather than pixels. In the unsigned voter model, small additions or removals of edges have been shown to alter consensus outcomes15–17, and similar methods can guide strategy evolution in games, such as the prisoner’s dilemma18. Building on this work, simulation studies of recommendation systems and homophilic structures demonstrate that isolating individuals from differing viewpoints can amplify polarization, while small attack-inspired edge perturbations can enable minority opinions to prevail even in unsigned models19. While these advances highlights ways to influence collective opinion, they assume uniform positive interactions and overlook the resistance created by antagonistic ties.

In this study, we extend adversarial-attack frameworks to the signed majority-vote model. We introduce an algorithm that determines the minimal edge perturbations required to transition the signed majority-vote model from a polarized state to global consensus, across varying levels of structural balance. We apply this method to both synthetic networks and empirical signed network datasets. Our simulations demonstrate that carefully targeted interventions can overcome structural frustration and restore high levels of consensus. These findings demonstrate how small, strategically chosen changes to network structure can control opinion dynamics in environments marked by antagonistic interactions.

Discussion and conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that a modest, sign matched perturbation can dismantle entrenched polarization in signed social networks. In both synthetic networks and seven empirical networks, the intervention increased the final consensus fraction by up to 35 percentage points with a perturbation strength of , whereas equivalently strong random edge noise left opinions trapped near the 50% baseline. These gains demonstrate that network wide agreement can be achieved without introducing stubborn agents or eliminating negative ties; a gentle, distributed adjustment of existing relationships is sufficient.

Contrarily to earlier adversarial attack studies that focused on unsigned networks, our work addresses the harder setting in which antagonistic edges actively resist alignment. The results indicate that networks containing more negative ties generally require stronger perturbations to achieve consensus; thus, hostile connections amplify the “cost” of control. Mechanistically, the intervention counters the opinion flipping effect of each negative edge by adding an opposing term, thereby making the effective interaction pattern increasingly similar to an unsigned graph as grows.

From an application standpoint, rewriting every edge weight is impractical. Nevertheless, the perturbation can be interpreted as a lightweight content ranking or recommendation signal that nudges users towards common ground. Such interventions raise ethical concerns: undisclosed manipulation can undermine trust, and malicious actors can potentially explore the same approach to entrench division. Thus, any deployment calls for transparency, opt in consent, and safeguards against abuse.

Several avenues remain open for future work. First, testing asynchronous or event driven update rules may reveal new dynamical regimes. Second, developing inference schemes to estimate optimal perturbations from partial network snapshots would enhance practical relevance. Third, extending the framework to multilayer or temporally evolving signed networks would capture complex social dynamics. Finally, controlled online experiments could validate the predicted thresholds in human populations.

In summary, this study provides the first empirical evidence that modest, sign aware perturbations can neutralise structural polarization in networks where antagonistic ties are pervasive. By outlining a principled route to consensus that neither suppresses dissent nor relies on heavy handed censorship, the results offer a new perspective on mitigating social fragmentation in digital communication platforms.

The classic voter model only considers friendships ; therefore, opinions are updated to match those of neighboring nodes. Conversely, this model considers repulsive behavior and updates opinions to reflect the influence of adopting the opposite stance from hostile nodes . Compared to the non-negative voter model15, our definition introduces the absolute value in the denominator of Eq. (5). This adjustment ensures that the probability is normalized by the total number of neighbors, regardless of whether the ties are balanced positively or negatively. Without this modification, the denominator could cancel out when the sum of signed connections vanishes, leading to ill-defined or unstable probabilities for nodes influenced by both friends and foes. Therefore, this extension allows the model to reliably capture opinion dynamics in general signed networks.

The convergence of node opinions depends on the structure of the signed network. Specifically, in numerous real-world networks, the structure is nearly sign-balanced, typically resulting in convergence to a state where two polarized opinions exist within the network. However, in cases where the network is not balanced, the dynamics can cause more complex patterns of opinion distribution that do not stabilize into a final state. Note that our method can be applied to both balanced and unbalanced structures.

Real-world dataset

We employ publicly available signed networks to assess the method of explicitly recording antagonism and to span heterogeneous conditions where negative ties hold significance. The selection covers distinct platforms and semantics of signed interaction (sentiment, friend/foe, trust/distrust), a broad range of sizes and sparsities, and different levels of structural balance (including temporal snapshots).

We employed seven publicly available signed networks from the Stanford Network Analysis Project (SNAP) repository20. The Reddit Hyperlink network joins subreddits whenever one post edges to another; each edge inherits the source post’s sentiment and is binarised to (neutral/positive) or (negative)21. Three Slashdot Zoo snapshots label user pairs as friend () or foe ()22. The Bitcoin OTC and Alpha networks store directed reputation scores between traders; ratings above zero are mapped to (trust) and non-positive ratings to (distrust)23,24. Finally, Epinions is a who-trusts-whom graph of reviewers, with for trust and for distrust22. For every data set we (i) ignore edge weights, (ii) collapse opposite directions to a single edge with the majority sign, and (iii) remove isolated nodes, yielding undirected signed adjacency matrices . The perturbation can be interpreted as a small algorithmic reweighting applied by platform systems (ranking, recommendation, or reputation aggregation), which slightly amplifies or attenuates neighbors’ influence when aggregating views or scores. Specifically, this reflects sentiment-sensitive ranking in Reddit-like settings, friend/foe filtering on social networks (Slashdot), and trust weighting in marketplaces or review platforms (Bitcoin, Epinions).
