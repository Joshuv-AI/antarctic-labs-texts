Case Report: Pembrolizumab-associated seronegative sicca syndrome during continued immunotherapy and after drug withdrawal in metastatic urothelial carcinoma
Authors: Yunjung Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pembrolizumab, an anti–programmed cell death protein 1 immune checkpoint inhibitor, has been associated with immune-related sicca manifestations, although its course during ongoing treatment and after cessation remains incompletely defined. We report a 78-year-old man with metastatic upper tract urothelial carcinoma who developed abrupt, severe xerostomia and xerophthalmia 45 days after pembrolizumab initiation, with no preceding sicca symptoms. Schirmer’s testing and salivary gland scintigraphy confirmed bilateral lacrimal and parotid-predominant salivary hypofunction. Autoimmune serology and IgG4 were unremarkable, other causes of sicca were excluded, and minor salivary gland biopsy was offered but declined. The findings supported a presumptive diagnosis of biopsy-unconfirmed pembrolizumab-associated seronegative sicca syndrome, without fulfilling the 2016 ACR-EULAR classification criteria for Sjögren’s disease. Causality was probable (Naranjo score 6, rising to 7 after drug withdrawal). Symptoms were moderate (CTCAE grade 2) but functionally burdensome, managed with artificial tears, saliva substitutes, and intermittent pilocarpine. Pembrolizumab was continued for 19 additional cycles with ongoing disease control; sicca persisted without escalating to a treatment-limiting grade, and treatment was eventually discontinued for cancer progression rather than sicca. Xerostomia improved substantially, and the patient regained the ability to swallow dry foods without added fluid, a patient-reported improvement consistent with a positive dechallenge. This case is informative less for its seronegativity than for its prolonged, manageable course during continued therapy and subsequent improvement after withdrawal. It suggests that, in carefully selected patients, moderate ICI-associated sicca syndrome may be managed symptomatically while immunotherapy continues, but this approach should not be generalized without close monitoring and further evidence.

Introduction

Sjögren’s syndrome is a chronic autoimmune disorder that mainly affects the exocrine glands, particularly the salivary and lacrimal glands, and produces xerostomia and xerophthalmia through lymphocytic infiltration and glandular inflammation (1). It affects an estimated 0.1–0.6% of the population and shows a marked female predominance in middle age (1). The pathogenesis of primary disease involves B-cell hyperactivity, autoreactive T-cell responses, and characteristic autoantibodies, most notably anti-Ro/SSA (1).

Distinct from primary Sjögren’s disease, sicca symptoms may also occur as treatment-associated exocrine immune-related adverse events in patients receiving cancer immunotherapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as pembrolizumab enhance antitumor immunity by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis (2). By disrupting peripheral immune tolerance, they can provoke immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in virtually any organ system (2). Sicca symptoms and Sjögren’s syndrome-like presentations are recognized but uncommon irAEs. Reported series indicate that ICI-associated disease differs from primary Sjögren’s syndrome in its abrupt onset, frequently seronegative profile, limited extraglandular involvement, and predominantly T cell–mediated pathology (3–5).

Seronegativity is itself common in ICI-associated disease, so a single seronegative case is not novel. What remains poorly characterized is the longitudinal behavior of such an exocrine irAE: existing reports are largely cross-sectional, capturing diagnosis and short-term management, with sparse data on its course during continued immunotherapy or after drug withdrawal. We describe a man followed for more than a year in whom a seronegative, biopsy-unconfirmed sicca syndrome was managed conservatively while pembrolizumab was continued for 19 further cycles, then observed after withdrawal for disease progression, addressing two under-documented questions: how such an irAE behaves when the ICI is not interrupted, and whether it improves after discontinuation. We also outline a pragmatic diagnostic approach for situations in which formal Sjögren’s syndrome classification criteria cannot be met.

Case description

A 78-year-old man was referred to the rheumatology clinic and first assessed on December 12, 2024 for evaluation of severe, rapidly progressive sicca symptoms. He had had no dry eye or dry mouth before starting immunotherapy.

Three years earlier he had been diagnosed with papillary, high-grade urothelial carcinoma of the left upper urinary tract. He underwent left nephroureterectomy (pT3Nx, high grade), followed by five cycles of adjuvant gemcitabine–cisplatin chemotherapy. About 18 months later, pulmonary metastases were detected on surveillance imaging, and he initially declined systemic therapy. After further progression of metastatic disease was documented (Figure 1), systemic treatment was reconsidered.

At the start of first-line treatment, the patient had renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate 47.7 mL/min/1.73 m2) consistent with his solitary-kidney status. This eGFR fell below the threshold for full-dose cisplatin, and together with his documented reluctance to receive cytotoxic chemotherapy, pembrolizumab monotherapy (200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks) was selected as first-line systemic therapy for metastatic disease; anti-CTLA-4 therapy was not part of the standard first-line strategy in this setting.

His medical history included hypertension and a previous pulmonary thromboembolism, for which he took a direct oral anticoagulant; none of his medications are recognized causes of xerostomia. He had no personal or family history of autoimmune disease, no prior head or neck irradiation, and no sicca symptoms at baseline.

He received his first pembrolizumab infusion on October 7, 2024, followed by a second cycle. The patient first reported sicca symptoms on November 21, 2024, at the visit for his third infusion, later stating that the dry mouth had seemed to begin around that time; using this date, onset was 45 days after pembrolizumab initiation and 24 days after the second infusion. He described disabling oral dryness (8/10 on a numeric rating scale), needed liquids to swallow solid food, had difficulty speaking, found previously tolerated dry foods such as crackers and rice cakes hard to swallow without water, and lost 2 kg over 3 weeks from reduced intake. On clinical oral examination by the treating rheumatologist, the mucosa and tongue were markedly dry with reduced saliva, without tongue atrophy or salivary or lacrimal gland enlargement; a formal dental or oral medicine assessment was not performed.

Diagnostic assessment, therapeutic intervention, and outcomes

Serological testing was negative for antinuclear antibody, anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB, anti-centromere antibody, antiphospholipid antibodies, and rheumatoid factor. Serum complement (C3 and C4) levels were normal, and inflammatory markers were not elevated (erythrocyte sedimentation rate 2 mm/h; C-reactive protein 0.8 mg/L).

Serum IgG4 was within normal limits (78 mg/dL; reference range <135 mg/dL), arguing against IgG4-related disease, an important mimic of bilateral salivary gland dysfunction in older men. Other recognized causes of sicca were considered and excluded clinically: there was no prior head or neck irradiation, no clinical or laboratory evidence of sarcoidosis or amyloidosis, no hepatitis C infection, and no xerogenic medication.

Technetium-99 m pertechnetate salivary gland scintigraphy assessed the bilateral parotid and submandibular glands. Perfusion was normal in all four evaluated glands. During the accumulation phase, radiotracer uptake was markedly reduced in both parotid glands, whereas submandibular uptake was preserved. Excretion was markedly delayed in both parotid and submandibular glands, with reduced response after orange-juice sialogogue stimulation. The parotid ejection fractions, calculated as the percentage decrease in parotid counts after stimulation, were 41% on the right and 34% on the left, below the institutional reference value of ≥50% (Figure 2). Schirmer’s test showed severely reduced tear production, measuring 2 mm in the right eye and 2 mm in the left eye over 5 min. Whole unstimulated and total stimulated salivary flow were not measured; salivary gland function was instead assessed objectively by scintigraphy. A labial (minor) salivary gland biopsy was offered but declined by the patient because of the burden of ongoing oncologic evaluation.

Causality was assessed formally at diagnosis using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale (range −4 to +13; ≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful). The score was 6, indicating a probable adverse drug reaction (item-by-item scoring in Supplementary Table S1), based on the temporal relationship, the absence of an alternative cause, previous reports of this reaction, and objective confirmation of glandular dysfunction; a dechallenge had not yet occurred. As described below, symptoms later improved after pembrolizumab was discontinued, raising the dechallenge item from 0 to +1 and the total to 7, still probable. The irAE was graded as moderate (CTCAE v5.0 grade 2): oral intake was maintained with dietary adaptation, weight loss was modest, and the patient required neither tube feeding nor parenteral nutrition—the criteria defining a grade 3 event under the “Dry Mouth” term—supporting the grade 2 classification.

Given the abrupt onset closely following pembrolizumab, the absence of an identifiable alternative cause, and objective confirmation of bilateral exocrine gland dysfunction, the presentation was attributed to pembrolizumab-associated sicca syndrome. Because autoimmune serology was negative and minor salivary gland biopsy was offered but declined by the patient, the 2016 ACR-EULAR classification criteria for Sjögren’s disease could not be applied. We therefore regarded the diagnosis as presumptive and describe the presentation as biopsy-unconfirmed pembrolizumab-associated seronegative sicca syndrome, recognizing that it lies within the reported spectrum of ICI-associated sicca syndrome, in which seronegative cases are well documented, and salivary-gland histopathology, when obtained, characteristically differs from that of primary Sjögren’s disease (4, 5).

Supportive therapy with artificial tears, saliva substitutes, and oral pilocarpine (5 mg twice daily) was initiated after the initial rheumatology assessment on December 12, 2024, while pembrolizumab treatment was ongoing; it was not started after ICI cessation. Because of excessive sweating, the pilocarpine dose was reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily, which was well tolerated. At a two-week follow-up he reported partial improvement, particularly in speech and in comfort during meals. Thereafter, however, he took pilocarpine only intermittently because of an aversion to the medication, and his sicca symptoms were subsequently managed largely with saliva substitutes, artificial tears, and dietary adaptation. Pembrolizumab was not stopped because of the sicca syndrome; it was continued with monitoring because the syndrome remained CTCAE grade 2 and the patient had ongoing disease control, and it was discontinued only after restaging demonstrated cancer progression after 21 cycles.

Restaging PET/CT in March and June 2025 showed stable disease, and the sicca symptoms persisted and remained burdensome but did not escalate beyond grade 2, with supportive care providing partial relief. The patient completed 21 cycles of pembrolizumab, with the final dose on December 4, 2025; subsequent restaging showed disease progression in the lungs, bone, and lymph nodes. After multidisciplinary discussion weighing disease control against renal risk, a reduced-dose cisplatin–gemcitabine regimen (80% of standard dose) was administered with close renal monitoring, an approach considered acceptable despite the borderline renal function that had precluded full-dose cisplatin at first line; subsequent restaging showed decreased metabolic activity in most osseous metastases. After pembrolizumab was stopped, xerostomia improved symptomatically over the following weeks, and by the time cisplatin–gemcitabine was initiated in January 2026 (approximately 4–6 weeks after the final dose) the patient reported that the dryness was much improved and that he could again swallow dry solid foods without supplementary liquid; he himself attributed the dryness to the immunotherapy. He was switched to enfortumab vedotin in May 2026 for further progression. Repeat Schirmer testing, salivary flow measurement, and scintigraphy were not performed, so the improvement was assessed clinically rather than confirmed objectively. Discussion

Diagnosing an exocrine irAE during ICI therapy is difficult when serological and histopathological confirmation is lacking. The diagnosis in this case remains presumptive because minor salivary gland biopsy was offered but declined and the 2016 ACR-EULAR classification criteria could not be applied. On the elements that were available—a compatible clinical presentation, objective functional deficits on Schirmer’s testing and salivary scintigraphy, an abrupt onset closely following pembrolizumab, a probable Naranjo causality score, and exclusion of alternative causes—the attribution to pembrolizumab is well supported; causality is therefore probable, even though the classification diagnosis is presumptive in the absence of histopathology. The differential diagnosis of sicca in this setting includes primary Sjögren’s disease, IgG4-related disease, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, hepatitis C infection, xerogenic medications, prior head and neck irradiation, and salivary gland dysfunction related to nonsurgical cancer therapies; as detailed in the Diagnostic Assessment, the non-treatment-related alternatives were excluded clinically and serologically, whereas therapy-related salivary dysfunction cannot be excluded on the same basis and is considered further below, where the abrupt onset during pembrolizumab monotherapy supports pembrolizumab as the most likely temporal trigger. We deliberately use the term sicca syndrome rather than Sjögren’s disease, because autoimmune markers and biopsy were lacking; this terminological caution reflects genuine diagnostic uncertainty.

Where autoimmune features were sought, the presentation was more consistent with ICI-associated sicca syndrome than with primary Sjögren’s disease: onset is typically abrupt (a median of 70 days from ICI initiation in one series (4), versus the insidious primary course; the 45-day onset in our patient was earlier than this median but within the reported range), anti-SSA/Ro positivity—a cornerstone of primary classification (1)—is frequently absent [2 of 20 positive in one series (4); all four seronegative in another (5), and extraglandular involvement tends to be limited (6)]. These features suggest a predominantly T-cell–mediated process rather than the B-cell–driven autoimmunity of primary disease: PD-1 blockade may enhance glandular T-cell activation, and cytokines such as interferon-γ and interleukin-17 may drive inflammation independently of autoantibody production (2, 3), potentially explaining our patient’s seronegative phenotype. This T-cell–predominant pathology is inferred from reported cases, as biopsy was not obtained here. Prior cancer therapy may also prime salivary tissue through epithelial injury, antigen release, or senescence, making later immune-mediated dysfunction more clinically apparent.

This report’s main strength is its extended follow-up, complementing the largely cross-sectional published literature. After diagnosis, the patient continued pembrolizumab for 19 additional cycles without escalation to a treatment-limiting grade, consistent with the ASCO guideline recommending symptomatic management with continued checkpoint inhibitor therapy for grade 1–2 toxicities, and reserving interruption and systemic immunomodulation for treatment-limiting (grade 3–4) disease (7); such immunosuppression has been reported beneficial in grade 3–4 or otherwise treatment-limiting ICI-associated sicca syndrome (4, 6), unlike the grade 2 course managed with supportive care alone here. This is a single case and should not be read as a general recommendation to continue ICI therapy without systemic treatment. Systemic corticosteroids were not used because the syndrome remained grade 2 with preserved oral intake, no gland enlargement or systemic inflammatory features, and no escalation during continued pembrolizumab, so their expected benefit was uncertain. In practice, this decision should weigh symptom timing and grade, oral intake, glandular findings, and systemic features. Because xerostomia raises the risk of dental caries and candidiasis, dental or oral medicine input should be sought early for preventive care (fluoride, saliva substitutes, oral hygiene, and surveillance). Continued ICI may be inappropriate when sicca progresses despite supportive care, becomes treatment-limiting, compromises oral intake, or shows glandular enlargement, systemic inflammation, worsening objective dysfunction, or active or granulomatous histology; the progressive granulomatous sialadenitis reported by Kulchar et al. (8) illustrates such a cautionary course. Nonsurgical cancer therapies can themselves cause xerostomia and salivary hypofunction (9), and therapy-related epithelial injury, antigen release, or senescence may prime glandular tissue for subsequent immune-mediated injury; prior gemcitabine–cisplatin exposure may have contributed to vulnerability here, although the patient had no sicca during or after that earlier chemotherapy. Kulchar et al. (8), in which prior BRAF/MEK inhibition preceded ICI-associated granulomatous sialadenitis, support a comparable two-step model of therapy-related priming followed by immune-mediated amplification. Continued immunotherapy did not prevent disease progression, so this observation concerns symptom management, not oncologic outcome.

The course after ICI discontinuation is equally informative. Sicca symptoms had persisted throughout pembrolizumab treatment but began to ease over the weeks following the final dose on December 4, 2025, and by the time cisplatin–gemcitabine was initiated (January 2026) the patient rated the dryness as much improved. We interpret this as consistent with a positive dechallenge. Two alternative explanations are unlikely: symptomatic treatment cannot account for the change, as pilocarpine was taken only intermittently, and although improvement coincided with the switch to cisplatin–gemcitabine, cytotoxic chemotherapy is a recognized contributor to xerostomia (9) and would be expected to aggravate rather than relieve oral dryness—so the improvement occurred despite, not because of, this regimen. Enfortumab vedotin was introduced later (May 2026), after xerostomia had already improved, and its recognized toxicity is predominantly ocular. The exact onset of improvement could not be determined, but xerostomia eased over the following weeks; this interval does not argue against a dechallenge, since improvement in an immune-mediated glandular adverse event may lag behind drug discontinuation. Reversibility after ICI cessation has been reported for some rheumatic irAEs, whereas others follow a chronic course (4); our patient appears to lie at the reversible end, and monitoring beyond ICI exposure remains advisable.

The reason for symptomatic improvement after withdrawal in this patient, when persistent sicca has been reported in others, remains uncertain: ICI-associated sicca syndrome may not follow a uniform course, reflecting a partly reversible functional disturbance in some patients versus persistent glandular inflammation or structural injury in others. The absence of gland enlargement or systemic inflammatory features, and persistence without escalation to a treatment-limiting grade over 19 additional cycles, are compatible with a partly reversible process in our patient; scintigraphy confirmed dysfunction but did not establish irreversible structural damage. This interpretation is clinically consistent with the subsequent improvement but remains limited by the absence of minor salivary gland biopsy and repeat objective testing after symptom improvement.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, histologic confirmation was lacking because the biopsy was offered but declined, so classification by the 2016 ACR–EULAR criteria was not possible; the diagnosis rests on clinical and functional findings and analogy with reported seronegative cases, which is why we favor the term sicca syndrome. Second, a formal oral medicine or dental evaluation was not performed, which would have better characterized the oral mucosa and salivary pooling. Third, a formal ophthalmologic assessment (ocular surface staining, tear break-up time) and measurement of whole salivary flow were not performed; quantitative sialometry would have permitted direct correlation of symptom severity with salivary output. Objective exocrine testing was also not repeated after pembrolizumab discontinuation, so the post-withdrawal improvement rests on the patient’s report of reduced xerostomia and restored ability to swallow dry foods rather than on repeat testing; such testing would have confirmed true exocrine recovery. Moreover, scintigraphy is not among the 2016 ACR–EULAR criteria items, and the functional reduction it showed was only mild to moderate despite the severity of reported symptoms—a symptom–sign discordance that further limits objective severity grading. Finally, enfortumab vedotin, started later, has dry eye as a recognized ocular toxicity (10), so ocular symptoms after this switch cannot be attributed to the original irAE, and the sicca follow-up window is best interpreted as ending at that switch. As a single case, the report is hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation in larger cohorts.

This case is clinically informative because it documents a prolonged, biopsy-unconfirmed pembrolizumab-associated sicca syndrome that remained manageable during continued treatment and improved after withdrawal. It should not be read as evidence that ICI therapy can generally be continued in ICI-associated sicca syndrome; rather, it illustrates one possible course in a selected patient with grade 2 symptoms, preserved oral intake, no escalation, and ongoing oncologic benefit at the time. Two practical points emerge. First, clinicians should consider an ICI-associated exocrine irAE in patients with abrupt sicca during checkpoint-inhibitor therapy even when serology is negative and biopsy unavailable, favoring the term sicca syndrome over Sjögren’s disease in these circumstances. Second, decisions about continuing or interrupting immunotherapy should be individualized—weighing cancer status, sicca severity, oral complications, objective glandular findings when available, and patient preference—with monitoring continuing beyond ICI exposure. Larger studies are needed to define which patients can be managed with supportive care alone and which require ICI interruption or immunosuppression.

Patient perspective

The patient described the dryness of his mouth as one of the most distressing problems he faced during cancer treatment. While receiving pembrolizumab, he found it hard to speak clearly and could not eat dry foods such as rice cakes without drinking liquid with every mouthful. After his treatment was changed in early 2026, the dryness improved markedly, and he could again manage dry, solid foods without sipping water. He agreed that the dryness had most likely been caused by the immunotherapy and was relieved that it eased once treatment stopped.
