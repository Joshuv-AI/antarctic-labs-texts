Associations between hearing and vision impairment and type 2 diabetes among middle-aged and older chinese adults: a nationwide longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Naixue Wang, Tiantian Wang, Xiaojuan Mao, Li Huang, Huajian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSensory impairments, including visual impairment (VI), hearing impairment (HI), and dual sensory impairment (DSI), are more common in middle-aged and older adults and are associated with poor health outcomes. However, the effect of VI, HI, and DSI on the risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) is unclear.MethodsThis study was based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which included a nationally representative sample of 13,484 middle-aged and older participants. HI and VI statuses were collected through self-reported questions. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) to analyze the associations of VI, HI, and DSI with the risk of T2D.ResultsDuring the 7-year follow-up period, 1267 participants developed new-onset T2D. Compared with normal hearing participants, individuals with HI had a higher risk of developing T2D (HR 1.19 [95% CI, 1.06–1.34]). Individuals with VI also had a higher risk of developing T2D than individuals with normal vision (HR 1.28 [95% CI, 1.11–1.49]). Individuals with DSI had a 42% increased risk of developing T2D compared with participants without sensory impairment (HR 1.42 [95% CI, 1.18–1.69]).ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that HI and VI have a combined effect on the incidence of T2D. Early identification of vision and hearing impairment may help identify individuals at higher risk of T2D and inform risk stratification and targeted prevention efforts. Individuals with DSI or more severe sensory impairment may warrant priority in diabetes risk assessment and closer monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01423-6.

Introduction

Diabetes is a serious chronic disease that is one of the leading causes of death and health problems. Of these, type 2 diabetes (T2D) is the most common type, accounting for more than 90% of cases [1, 2]. As of 2019, the global prevalence of diabetes has reached nearly 500 million, and it is projected to continue rising, potentially reaching 700 million by 2045 [1]. With the accelerating aging of the population, the prevalence of diabetes has increased significantly, further exacerbating the burden of cardiovascular and kidney diseases, reducing life expectancy, and posing a particularly serious threat to older people [3]. In China, the aging process is driving a rapid increase in the prevalence of adult diabetes, from 10.9% in 2013 to 12.4% in 2018 [4]. T2D not only entails huge healthcare costs but also increases the burden on individuals, thus its prevention has become one of the priorities in public health [5, 6].

Hearing impairment (HI) and visual impairment (VI) have become prevalent globally. Currently, hearing impairment (HI) affects approximately 20% of the global population [7]. According to the World Health Organization’s World Report on Hearing 2021, nearly 2.5 billion people are projected to experience varying degrees of hearing loss by 2050 [8]. Meanwhile, VI is also common, with moderate or severe visual impairment affecting nearly 300 million people by 2020 [9]. With global aging, the burden of coexistence of VI and HI, i.e. dual sensory impairment (DSI), is increasing [10, 11]. A study of middle-aged and older adults in China suggested that DSI significantly increased the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, and cardiac events [12]. In addition, previous studies have found that DSI is strongly associated with a higher risk of dementia [13–15]. Notably, sensory impairment and T2D may share some common pathophysiological pathways. Oxidative stress and vascular endothelial dysfunction, as key processes associated with aging, can promote insulin resistance and impair β-cell function, thereby driving the development of T2D [16, 17]. Simultaneously, it can impair microvascular perfusion and accelerate damage to neurosensory tissues, such as vision impairment associated with retinal microvascular damage and hearing loss linked to cochlear microvascular pathology/oxidative injury [18–21]. Therefore, sensory impairment may indicate underlying microvascular fragility and elevated oxidative stress burden, providing biological evidence for examining the longitudinal association between sensory impairment and new-onset T2D.

T2D, as a chronic metabolic disease, often leads to impairment of the cardiovascular system and is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease [22–24]. Meanwhile, some studies have suggested that diabetes significantly increases the risk of developing dementia [25–27]. Therefore, we hypothesize that sensory impairment may exacerbate the risk of cardiovascular disease and dementia through diabetes. However, studies on the effects of VI, HI, and DSI on the incidence of T2D are still limited and lack sufficient evidence to confirm them.

Based on national middle-aged and elderly participants in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this study explored the associations of VI, HI, and DSI with the incidence of T2D. In sensitivity analyses, we repeated the analyses after excluding incident T2D cases within 2 years and within 4 years after baseline to further address potential reverse causality. The results of this study may provide an important reference for public health work as a means of identifying high-risk groups for diabetes and providing a scientific basis for early intervention and prevention strategies for diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

CHARLS is a population-based prospective cohort study that included a nationally representative sample of community residents aged ≥ 45 years from China. CHARLS national baseline survey was conducted in 2011 using the multi-stage probability to proportional to size (PPS) sampling method [28]. Over 17,000 participants from 28 provinces in China were surveyed in 2011 (baseline, Wave 1) and followed up every 2 to 3 years using face-to-face computer technology-assisted personal interviews in their homes. Three subsequent follow-up surveys of participants were conducted in 2013–2014 (Wave 2), 2015–2016 (Wave 3), and 2017–2018 (Wave 4) [28, 29]. More detailed information on the CHARLS design has been posted elsewhere [30, 31]. This study was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol of CHARLS was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015). All participants provided written informed consent at the time of participation.

Among the 17,302 participants aged ≥ 45 at baseline, individuals meeting all the following criteria were included in the study: availability of sensory impairment information in Wave 1, no diabetes diagnosis in Wave 1, and at least one follow-up record regarding diabetes across the subsequent three follow-up surveys. A total of 13,484 individuals were ultimately eligible for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1). Missing covariates were filled in by multiple imputation of chained equations using the mice package in R [32, 33]. We generated 5 imputed datasets. The imputation model included the exposure (sensory impairment, i.e., HI/VI/DSI), the outcome (incident T2D), and covariates including sex, age, drinking status, smoking status, high blood pressure, stroke, heart disease, cancer, lung disease, arthritis, activity of daily living (ADL), and depressive symptoms.

Assessment of sensory impairment

HI and VI status were assessed through self-reported questions [34]. VI was assessed by first asking participants: “Do you usually wear glasses or corrective lenses?” The response options included “Yes”, “Legally blind”, and “No”. Participants who answered “Legally blind” were directly classified as having VI. In our analytic sample, 77 of 13,407 participants (0.57%) reported being “Legally blind”. These participants were included in the VI group and, when concomitant HI was present, were categorized as DSI. Given the small proportion, we did not treat “Legally blind” as a separate category to avoid unstable estimates. If participants answered “Yes” or “No”, they were asked to continue with the following two questions: (1) “How good is your vision for seeing things at a distance (with glasses or corrective lenses), like recognizing a friend from across the street?” and (2) “How good is your vision for seeing things up close (with glasses or corrective lenses), like reading ordinary newspaper print?”. For each question, response options included “excellent,” “very good,” “good,” “fair,” and “poor”. Participants were classified as having VI if they answered “fair” or “poor” to either question. HI was assessed with the following question, “Is your hearing excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor (with a hearing aid if you normally use it and without if you normally don’t)?”. The response options were also “excellent”, “very good”, “good”, “fair”, and “poor”. Participants were classified as having HI if they answered “fair” or “poor”. When HI and VI were both present, respondents were regarded as having DSI [34]. Therefore, based on the results of the VI and HI assessments, sensory impairments were classified into the following four groups: no sensory impairment (NSI), HI only, VI only, and DSI. The assessment and classification of sensory impairment aligns with previous studies [12, 34, 35].

Assessment of T2D

Diabetes was determined based on responses to the questions: “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes by a doctor?” and “Are you now taking any of the following treatments to treat or control your diabetes?”. Participants who provided an affirmative response to either question were classified as having a diabetes diagnosis [36]. Due to the age of the participants, all new cases of diabetes were considered new onset T2D rather than type 1 diabetes [37]. If the exact onset time of T2D was unavailable, the time to event was calculated as (the time of specific wave with T2D information - the time of interval wave)/2 + (the time of interval wave - the time of baseline investigation) [38].

Covariates

Baseline covariates were collected, including age, sex, residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, chronic conditions (HBP, stroke, lung disease, cancer, heart disease, and arthritis), ADL, and depression. Sex was categorized as male or female. The residence included both rural and urban. Education level was divided into two categories as high school and above and below high school. Marital status was classified as married or partnered and other marital status (unmarried, separated, divorced, or widowed). Smoking status was divided into three categories as current smokers, former smokers, and never smokers. Drinking status was assessed based on the frequency of drinking in the last 12 months and was categorized as ≥ 1 time per month, < 1 time per month, and never drinkers. Chronic diseases included the presence of HBP, stroke, lung disease, cancer, heart disease, and arthritis. In terms of ADL, participants were asked about difficulty performing six ADLs [39], such as dressing, bathing, eating, getting in or out of bed, toileting, and controlling urination and defecation. Participants were defined as having ADL disability if they had any difficulty with one or more activities [40]. Regarding the assessment of depression, the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) is a self-administered scale that measures depressive symptoms, with higher scores indicating greater experience of depression [41]. The ten-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) was used to assess participants’ level of depression in the past week [42]. The answers on the CES-D-10 were scored on a 4-level scale: 0 = little or no (< 1 day), 1 = not much (1–2 days), 2 = sometimes or half of the time (3–4 days), and 3 = most of the time (5–7 days). Positively worded items were reversed and scored. Participants’ depression scores ranged from 0 to 30. Using 10 as a criterion for determining depression, participants equal to or greater than this threshold were judged to be depression [43].

Statistical analysis

All participants were divided into 4 groups: NSI, HI only, VI only, and DSI. Participants’ baseline characteristics were presented as means (standard deviations [SD]) or medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]) for continuous variables and as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the 4 groups were performed by ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to assess the association between sensory impairment and the incidence risk of T2D. To control for potential confounding factors, we developed three models: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted, based on Model 2, for residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, HBP, stroke, heart disease, cancer, lung disease, arthritis, ADL, and depression. In subgroup analyses, the association between DSI and the incidence risk of T2D was assessed according to sex, age, residence, HBP, ADL, and depression subgroups.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) All cases of diabetes diagnosed within the first 2 years after baseline were removed (n = 13138) [37], and multivariate analyses were repeated to test whether reverse causality might explain the study outcome (undiagnosed diabetes at baseline causing sensory impairment); (2) Obesity is a recognized risk factor for diabetes [44]. Given that body mass index (BMI) was missing at baseline by more than 15%, BMI was not included in the main analysis to preserve the maximum sample size. However, in sensitivity analyses, BMI was added as a confounder and multivariate analyses were repeated (n = 11320) after removing participants with missing BMI and outliers [45] (defined as BMI < 15 kg/m² or BMI > 50 kg/m²); (3) After removing participants with missing and outlier BMI values at baseline and those with missing values on other covariates, BMI was added as a confounder and the multivariate analysis was repeated (n = 10025); (4) Multivariate analyses were repeated after removing all cases of diabetes diagnosed within the first 2 years after baseline and after removing participants with missing and outlier values for BMI at baseline (n = 11021); (5) After removing all cases of diabetes diagnosed within the first 2 years after baseline, participants with missing and outlier BMI at baseline, and participants with missing values for other covariates, BMI was added as a confounder and the multivariate analyses were repeated (n = 9763); (6) To further reduce the possibility of reverse causality bias, we excluded newly diagnosed T2D cases occurring within 4 years post-baseline (n = 12742) and repeated the analysis to assess the robustness of the results; (7) We further excluded participants who reported being “legally blind” at baseline (n = 77) and repeated the multivariable analyses to assess the robustness of the findings.

Data management and statistical analysis were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). All statistical tests were two-sided, with P less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

The number of participants in this study was 13,484 (6470 males and 7014 females). Among them, 2293 (17.0%) had no sensory impairment, 3804 (28.2%) had VI only, 755 (5.6%) had HI only, and 6632 (49.2%) had DSI. There were significant differences in baseline characteristics such as age, sex, residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, drinker status, chronic diseases (HBP, stroke, heart disease, lung disease, and arthritis), ADL, and depression between the 4 groups (Table 1).

Association between sensory impairment and T2D

During the 7-year follow-up period, 1267 participants were newly diagnosed with T2D. In terms of hearing, participants with HI had a significantly increased risk of T2D (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.34) compared to those with normal hearing (NH). In terms of vision, participants with VI were significantly associated with a higher risk of T2D (HR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.11–1.49) compared to participants with normal vision (NV). In the combined analysis of sensory impairment, participants with VI only had a 26% increased risk of T2D (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.04–1.53) compared with participants with NSI, whereas participants with DSI had a higher risk of T2D, with a 42% increase (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.18–1.69) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

In subgroup analyses (Table 3), the association between DSI and incident T2D was broadly consistent across subgroups defined by sex, age, residence, hypertension, ADL, and depression. No statistically significant interactions were detected (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating no evidence of effect modification and suggesting that the association did not materially differ across these subgroups.

Sensitivity analysis

The results of all sensitivity analyses are consistent with the main analysis, with no substantive changes in the direction of the associations. Whether removing cases of diabetes diagnosed within 2 years of baseline or adding BMI as a confounder to the model or removing participants with missing covariates, these analyses showed that VI, HI, and DSI were strongly associated with a higher risk of T2D (Supplementary Tables S1-S5). When applying a stricter exclusion window by removing incident T2D cases occurring within 4 years after baseline, the results were materially unchanged. VI, HI, and DSI remained associated with an increased risk of incident T2D (Supplementary Table S6). In addition, after excluding participants who reported being “legally blind” at baseline, the results remained materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This study, based on a nationally representative cohort of 13,484 middle-aged and older Chinese participants, explored the associations between HI, VI, and DSI with the incidence of T2D over a 7-year follow-up period. Our study results suggested that VI, HI, and DSI increase the risk of T2D and the diverse sensitivity analyses in this study were consistent with the main analysis. This study provides new insights into the association between sensory impairment and the risk of T2D in a Chinese population, further complementing research in related areas.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between sensory impairments (including hearing impairments and visual impairments) and the risk of T2D in a large-scale national longitudinal cohort study of middle-aged and elderly people in China. Much of the previous research on sensory impairment has centered around its impact on cardiovascular disease and dementia [12, 13, 15, 46–49]. For example, in a longitudinal study based on a Korean population, the co-existence of visual and hearing impairments was found to significantly increase the risk of myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality in T2D patients [50]. Another study of 90,893 UK Biobank (UKB) adults found significant associations between hearing and vision impairment and increased risk of all-cause and cause-specific dementia [48]. However, diabetes can cause vascular and neurological complications that can lead to a variety of impairments, which include an increase in cardiovascular disease and dementia [25, 26, 51]. During the 7-year follow-up, VI, HI, and DSI were associated with a higher risk of incident T2D. These findings suggest that assessing sensory impairment may help identify middle-aged and older adults at higher risk of T2D. Whether interventions to improve hearing or vision can reduce T2D incidence warrants confirmation in intervention studies.

Given the extensive systemic damage triggered by diabetes, it may also contribute to the development of HI and VI. In previous epidemiological studies, the proportions of patients with diabetes reporting visual impairment and hearing impairment were 5–17% and 21%, respectively [52–54]. Visual impairment is usually closely associated with diabetic retinopathy, a typical microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus [55]; Hearing impairment may be caused by microvascular lesions and neurological complications in the inner ear [54, 56]. Therefore, to reduce the possibility of reverse causality [37], the present study was reanalyzed after excluding T2D cases diagnosed within 2 years after baseline. The results showed that participants with VI, HI, and DSI had a higher risk of T2D, whether they controlled BMI. Therefore, this study differs from previous studies that have primarily examined the risk of hearing and visual impairment due to diabetes [54, 56–59], by focusing on whether hearing and visual impairment increase the risk of T2D.

We hypothesize that sensory impairment may increase the risk of T2D through the following potential mechanisms. First, physical activity is widely recognized as an effective measure to prevent T2D [60]. However, for individuals with VI or HI, engaging in traditional physical activity can be difficult. Several studies have shown that adults with VI are more likely to be physically inactive than sighted people [61], and that moderate or more severe HI in older people is strongly associated with lower levels of physical activity [62]. Therefore, lack of physical activity leads to a significant increase in the risk of T2D [37, 60]. Second, sensory impairment may adversely affect the daily communication and cognitive abilities of middle-aged and older adults, further leading to social isolation and increased psychological stress, thus harming mental health [63, 64]. This impairment may hinder information exchange and social participation, indirectly decreasing physical activity levels, which in turn alters physical functioning [65–67] and increases the incidence of T2D. Third, sensory impairments and T2D may also share common upstream biological mechanisms. Oxidative stress and vascular endothelial dysfunction, as key pathophysiological alterations associated with aging, can drive the onset and progression of T2D by inducing chronic low-grade inflammation, promoting insulin resistance, and impairing β-cell function [16, 17]. This includes vision loss associated with retinal degenerative changes and microvascular impairment, as well as hearing loss linked to damage to cochlear hair cells/vortices and microcirculatory dysfunction [18–21]. Taken together, sensory impairment may influence T2D risk through interconnected behavioral, social-psychological, and biological pathways. Sensory impairment may reduce physical activity and social participation, increase psychological stress and depressive symptoms, and interact with aging-related oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, thereby jointly contributing to metabolic dysregulation and the subsequent risk of T2D.

This study suggested that sensory impairment could be a modifiable risk factor for the prevention of T2D. Cataracts, uncorrected refractive errors, and myopic macular degeneration are the leading causes of low vision and blindness in adults [68–70]. Ophthalmological counseling should be recommended as soon as vision problems arise. VI due to cataracts can be restored surgically, VI due to uncorrected refractive error can be corrected with spectacles, and VI due to myopic macular degeneration requires individualized pharmacological and surgical interventions [69, 71, 72]. In addition, a randomized controlled trial of middle-aged and older adults in China showed that a hearing aid intervention not only improved hearing-related quality of life in socially active people but also significantly reduced depressive symptoms, mainly due to the ability of hearing aids to significantly reduce communication barriers and enhance interpersonal communication [73]. HI can be improved with hearing aids or alleviated by optimizing the living environment and making lifestyle changes, such as reducing environmental noise and communicating loudly and clearly. The risk of developing T2D can be reduced to some extent by targeted therapeutic measures (e.g., wearing glasses, and using hearing aids) and lifestyle interventions.

This study has several significant strengths. First, this study is based on a nationally representative prospective cohort of middle-aged and older adults with a large and broadly representative sample size. Second, to reduce the possibility of reverse causality, we excluded cases diagnosed with T2D within 2 years of baseline and reanalyzed them. Similarly, after conducting multivariate regression by maintaining the maximum sample size in the main analysis, we included BMI as a covariate and further conducted sensitivity analyses. The diverse sensitivity analyses further validated the robustness of the findings.

This study also has some limitations. First, the HI and VI in this study are based on self-reported assessments, which may introduce reporting bias and exposure misclassification due to subjectivity. Nevertheless, this criterion has been consistently adopted in numerous previous studies based on CHARLS [12, 34, 35], thereby helping to ensure methodological consistency in exposure definitions and comparability across studies. It should be noted that a broader self-reported threshold may increase sensitivity and include individuals with mild to moderate functional limitations. Therefore, compared to studies defining sensory impairment using objective measurements (such as visual acuity charts and audiometers), the prevalence of DSI estimated in this study may be relatively higher. Previous CHARLS studies employing comparable definitions have also reported similar levels of DSI prevalence [35, 74]. Furthermore, a broader definition of self-report may dilute effect size estimates and bring them closer to the null hypothesis level (where the HR approaches 1). Second, self-reported sensory impairment may be influenced by depressive symptoms, leading to differential reporting bias. Such bias may either exaggerate the observed associations due to negative health perceptions or attenuate the associations by introducing misclassification through overreporting, resulting in uncertainty in both the direction and magnitude of bias. Depression may act as a confounding factor or lie within underlying causal pathways, necessitating caution in its adjustment and interpretation of outcomes. We adjusted for depressive symptoms in the primary analysis and stratified by depressive status in subgroup analyses. Results demonstrated consistent associations across both groups, with no evidence of significant effect modification. Nevertheless, residual bias cannot be entirely ruled out, and future studies should incorporate objective hearing and vision measurements for further validation. Third, there are limitations to the assessment of T2D. Diagnoses of diabetes were made by questionnaire self-report and were not confirmed by subsequent medical records. The diabetes diagnosis questionnaire in the CHARLS database was administered every two years and did not ask for a specific date of diagnosis during follow-up. This design allows data on the time of the event to be presented only in even-numbered years at follow-up events, which may introduce bias in the analysis. Moreover, although incident diabetes was treated as incident T2D given the age of the cohort, information on diabetes subtype was unavailable in CHARLS. Thus, a small proportion of non-T2D cases such as late autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) cannot be excluded, which may have led to outcome misclassification. Finally, although we adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding may remain because some important lifestyle factors, such as physical activity, dietary patterns, and sleep characteristics, were not fully included in the analysis. Genetic susceptibility and other unmeasured factors may also have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provided evidence for the association of VI, HI, and DSI with an increased risk of developing T2D in middle-aged and older adults in China. Early identification of vision and hearing impairment may help identify individuals at higher risk of incident T2D and inform targeted prevention efforts. Individuals with DSI or more severe sensory impairment may warrant priority in diabetes risk assessment and closer monitoring.
