Microglia Attenuate Neuroinflammation After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage by Modulating Astrocyte Phenotypic Transformation via the RARα/Mafb/Msr1 Pathway
Authors: Yuxiao Meng, Yuanzhe Dong, Jiang Shao, Qiaowei Wu, Le Huang, Pei Wu, Shuai Lan, Zurong Yao, Huaizhang Shi, Yuchen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroinflammation mediated by microglia-astrocyte interactions significantly contributes to secondary brain injury after subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Retinoic acid receptor alpha (RARα) has emerged as a potential modulator of neuroinflammation. However, its specific mechanisms in the interaction between microglia and astrocytes after SAH are not yet known. This study investigated whether RARα activation might alleviate neuroinflammation post-SAH by regulating microglial-astrocytic crosstalk via the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt signaling axis. An in vivo SAH model in male Sprague-Dawley rats was established through endovascular perforation. In vitro, a microglia-astrocyte co-culture system was subjected to oxyhemoglobin treatment to mimic SAH conditions. The RARα-specific agonist Am80 was administered to activate RARα signaling. Key molecular markers (Mafb, Msr1, and PI3K/Akt), pro-/anti-inflammatory cytokines, and glial activation phenotypes were evaluated via western blotting and immunofluorescence. Functional recovery was assessed with neurobehavioral tests. Pathway-specific inhibitors were used for mechanistic validation. RARα activation markedly attenuated neuroinflammation, enhanced neurological outcomes in SAH rats, significantly decreased levels of pro-inflammatory factors, and inhibited neuroinflammation mediated by microglia. Mechanistically, Am80 was found to upregulate the transcription factor Mafb and its downstream target Msr1, thereby triggering PI3K/Akt phosphorylation. This cascade inhibited NF-κB-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine release and enhanced anti-inflammatory factors. Critically, blockade of PI3K/Akt or knockdown of Mafb/Msr1 abolished the anti-inflammatory effects of Am80. In addition, the inflammatory factor receptors TNF-αR and IL6R were demonstrated to be important mediators of microglia and astrocyte interaction. RARα activation mitigates SAH-induced neuroinflammation by modulating microglial-astrocyte interactions through the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt pathway. Our findings identified RARα as a promising therapeutic target for ameliorating neuroinflammation and improving recovery after SAH.Keywords: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Astrocyte, Microglial, Neuroinflammation, Retinoic acid receptor α

Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), characterized by bleeding into the subarachnoid space, is a devastating neurological emergency constituting approximately 5–10% of all strokes. Its primary etiology involves rupture of intracranial aneurysms [1, 2], although trauma and other vascular malformations also contribute. Despite major advancements over recent decades in neurocritical care, diagnostic imaging, particularly CT angiography and digital subtraction angiography, and endovascular and microsurgical techniques for securing ruptured aneurysms, SAH continues to pose an unacceptably high burden of morbidity and mortality [3]. Unfortunately, the overall prognosis for patients with SAH has not substantially improved in recent years [4]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiological mechanism of SAH is essential.

Among the multiple pathophysiological mechanisms underlying secondary brain injury after SAH, neuroinflammation has emerged as a critical and sustained driver of neuronal damage and functional impairment [5]. Central to this neuroinflammatory storm is the intricate and often maladaptive crosstalk between microglia and astrocytes. These microglial-derived signals, particularly involving TNF-α and IL-1α, potently activate neighboring astrocytes [6]. Activated astrocytes undergo an extensive phenotypic shift, transitioning from performing homeostatic roles in synaptic support, ion buffering, and metabolic regulation toward a reactive state [7, 8]. A1 astrocytes not only fail to support neurons but also actively contribute to damage by releasing neurotoxins, exacerbating BBB disruption, inhibiting synaptic function, and amplifying the inflammatory cascade through further cytokine and chemokine release. Consequently, a vicious, self-sustaining cycle of microglial activation drives astrocyte toxicity, which in turn leads to further microglial activation, and promotes neuronal apoptosis and synaptic loss [9].

Retinoic acid receptor alpha (RARα), a ligand-dependent nuclear transcription factor belonging to the steroid/thyroid hormone receptor superfamily, has recently garnered attention for its immunomodulatory functions within the CNS [10]. In the study of SAH, activation of the RARα receptor attenuated neuroinflammation by promoting M1(pro-inflammatory)-to-M2(anti-inflammatory) phenotypic polarization in microglia [11]. However, the ability of RARα signaling pathway to regulate the intercellular signaling pathway between microglia and astrocytes, thereby breaking the pathogenic cycle, remains a major but largely unexplored knowledge gap. Furthermore, the downstream molecular mediators through which RARα exerts its potential anti-inflammatory effects in SAH are entirely unknown [10, 12].

One compelling candidate pathway downstream of RARα involves the transcription factor v-maf musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog B (Mafb) [13]. Mafb is regulated by retinoid signaling and plays crucial roles in macrophage differentiation and regulation of the inflammatory response [14, 15]. A key downstream target of Mafb is the macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (Msr1). Msr1 signaling is intricately linked to activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, a central signaling hub integrating pro-survival and anti-inflammatory signals [16]. In microglia, PI3K/Akt pathway activation suppresses neurotoxicity driven by nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and promotes an M2-polarized state. Notably, the expression of Msr1 in macrophages affects microglia polarization, thus suggesting a potential role in mediating glial crosstalk [17].

Therefore, this study was designed to test the central hypothesis that pharmacological activation of RARα alleviates neuroinflammation and ameliorates functional outcomes after SAH by modulating microglia-astrocyte crosstalk via induction of the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. We used a multi-faceted approach combining an in vivo rat SAH model with an in vitro microglia-astrocyte co-culture system subjected to oxyhemoglobin (OxyHb) treatment to mimic SAH conditions. Using the specific RARα agonist Am80 (Am80), pathway inhibitors (LY294002 for PI3K), genetic knockdown (with siRNAs to Mafb and Msr1), and microglial depletion (with PLX3397), we aimed to elucidate the mechanistic cascade linking RARα activation to attenuated glial activation and neuroinflammation, and ultimately identify a novel therapeutic target for this devastating condition.

Materials and Methods

Single‑Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‑seq) Data Acquisition

Publicly available scRNA-seq data were retrieved from the China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix; accession no. OMIX006611) [18]. Detailed information regarding the experimental animals, SAH modeling, sample grouping, and time points is thoroughly documented in the repository’s metadata.

Downstream bioinformatic analysis was comprehensively executed using the R package Seurat (version 4.3.0). For stringent quality control (QC), low-quality cells were filtered based on standard criteria: cells with fewer than 500 detected genes (nFeature\_RNA < 500) or a mitochondrial gene percentage exceeding 5% (percent.mt > 5%) were excluded. To eliminate potential doublets, cells with excessively high UMI counts (nCount\_RNA > 25,000) were also removed. Genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were discarded from the initial matrix. To minimize potential batch effects across different sample sequencing libraries, data integration was performed using the standard Seurat integration workflow.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type were identified via Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function, with a negative binomial test. To ensure a robust identification of marker genes, the filtration thresholds were strictly set to a minimum detection rate of 25% and a minimum absolute log-transformed fold change of 0.25. The cell clusters were annotated to known lineages via marker genes from the CellMarker 2.0 database.

Cell Type Identification

The cells were classified into 14 major cell types (Fig. 1C–E) including B cells (Cd79A, Cd79B, Ms4α1) [19], NK cells (Ncr1, Klrb1c, Nkg7) [20], macrophages (C1qb, Cd68, Csf1r, Mrc1) [21], neutrophils (S100α9, IL1β, Csf3r) [22], monocytes (Ccr2, Ly6c2) [23], T cells (Cd3d, Cd3e, Cd247) [24], mast cells (Fcer1α, Kit, Ms4α2) [25], mesenchymal cells (Col1α1, Col1α2, Col3α1, DCN) [19], neural precursor cells (Cdh2, Pde6g, Gnb3) [26], platelet (pro-platelet basic protein) [27], forebrain neuron (Gng13, S100α5) [28], microglial cells (CD45, CD11b, Cx3cr1) [29], astrocytes cells (GFAP, AQP4) [30], and oligodendroglia cells (CSPG4, PTPRZ1) [30].

SAH Model and Bleeding Score

The experimental model of SAH was induced through the well-established endovascular perforation technique under aseptic surgical conditions. Briefly, rats were deeply anesthetized through intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40–50 mg/kg). Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of a pedal withdrawal reflex. Rats were placed supine on a thermostatically controlled heating pad to maintain the core body temperature at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C throughout the procedure. The ventral neck region was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine and 70% ethanol. Under an operating microscope, a midline incision was made to expose the left common carotid artery, external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery. The ECA was ligated distally and coagulated. A 4-0 monofilament nylon suture (diameter approximately 0.28–0.30 mm), with its tip rounded through brief exposure to a flame to minimize vascular tearing, was introduced into the ECA stump and gently advanced rostrally through the internal carotid artery approximately 18–20 mm until resistance, indicating passage beyond the origin of the middle cerebral artery, was felt. The filament was then advanced a further 2–3 mm with moderate force to perforate the bifurcation of the anterior cerebral artery and middle cerebral artery, thereby simulating aneurysm rupture. The filament was immediately withdrawn, thus allowing blood to extravasate into the subarachnoid space. The ECA stump was subsequently permanently ligated. The incision was closed in layers. Sham-operated control rats underwent identical surgical procedures, including vessel exposure and filament insertion into the ECA, but without perforation of the intracranial artery.

This study was aimed at objectively quantifying the extent of subarachnoid bleeding, while ensuring consistency across experimental groups. SAH severity was assessed 24 h post-surgery with the validated Sugawara grading scale. Rats were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove intravascular blood. The brain was carefully removed and placed ventral side up. The basal surface of the brain was divided into six distinct anatomical segments: (1) bilateral frontal lobes, (2) bilateral temporal lobes, (3) bilateral occipital lobes, (4) bilateral cerebellar lobes, (5) bilateral posterior cerebral artery territories, and (6) the ventral brainstem/pons region. Each segment was visually inspected and scored independently by two blinded investigators according to the following criteria: score 0, no visible blood; score 1, minimal, scattered subarachnoid blood clots; score 2, moderate bleeding covering less than 50% of the segment surface, with underlying vascular structures still discernible; and score 3, severe bleeding with a thick clot covering more than 50% of the segment surface, obscuring the underlying vasculature. The scores from all six segments were summed to yield a total hemorrhage score ranging from 0 to 18. Only rats with a total SAH grade score ≥ 7 were included in the SAH experimental groups, to ensure adequate and comparable hemorrhage severity. Rats with scores below 7 or sham-operated rats exhibiting any bleeding were excluded from further analysis. Representative photographs were taken for documentation (Fig. 1B).

Assessment of Neurological Outcomes

Neurological outcomes were assessed 24 h after SAH by modified Garcia scores and the beam balance test by two independent investigators. The modified Garcia score consists of six aspects (0–3 points each part), including autonomous activity, autonomous movement of the four limbs, forepaw outstretching, body proprioception, whisker proprioception, and climbing. Beam balance tests were used to evaluate the ability of rats to balance. The rats were allowed to walk on a narrow wooden beam for 1 min; the score ranged from 0 to 4 according to walking distance and the number of falls or slips. As previously described, the average scores of three consecutive trials were calculated for analysis. Higher scores indicated better neurological function.

Cell Culture

BV2 cells (murine microglials; Shanghai, China) and CTX cells (rat astrocytes; Wuhan, China) were cultured in endothelial cell medium (DMEM) (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days, and cells in logarithmic growth that had been passaged for three to eight generations were selected for experiments. A Transwell non-contact co-culture system was used, in which the lower layer comprised cells inoculated on the bottom of a 24-well plate and cultured for 48 h until complete adherence, and the upper layer comprised MGs Transwell in the small room. The chamber was inserted into plates containing ACs, serum-free/low serum (2% fetal bovine serum) co-culture medium (DMEM/F12) was added, and the cells were co-cultured under 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Drugs and Treatments

Am80 was dissolved in carboxymethyl cellulose solution and administered through intraperitoneal injection (5 mg/kg) 1 h after SAH. AGN196996 (a selective inhibitor of RARα; 10 mg/kg) was diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide and administered through intracerebroventricular injection 1 h before the operation. PLX3397 (a microglial inhibitor; 30 mg/kg; i.p.) was injected 15 days before the operation. We established a model of SAH in vitro by adding OxyHb (10 µM, 24 h) to the cell culture medium. LY294002 (a selective inhibitor of PI3K; 50 mmol/L) was injected (i.c.v.) 2 h prior to SAH.

Western Blotting

Brain tissue samples (ipsilateral cortex) or harvested cells were homogenized on ice in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant (total protein lysate) was collected. Protein concentration was determined with a bicinchoninic acid Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). Equal amounts of protein (typically 20–40 µg) were separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (on 8–15% gels, depending on the target protein size) and subsequently electrotransferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% non-fat dry milk dissolved in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer or 5% bovine serum albumin/TBST: anti-Mafb (1:1000, HPA005653, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-Msr1 (1:1000, ab123946, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-phospho-Akt (Ser473) (1:1000, #9271, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Akt (1:2000, #9272, Cell Signaling Tech), anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (1:1000, #3033, Cell Signaling Tech), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, #8242, Cell Signaling Tech), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, ab254360, Abcam), anti-TNF-α (1:1000, sc52746, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti-IL-6 (1:500, sc65327, Santa Cruz), anti-IL-10 (1:100, ab133575, Abcam), anti-complement component 3 (C3) (1:1000, ab200999, Abcam), anti-S100 Calcium-Binding Protein A10 (S100A10) (1:1000, ab187201, Abcam), anti-GFAP (1:5000, ab7260, Abcam), anti-β-actin (1:2500, ab8226, Abcam), and anti-GAPDH (1:2000, ab8245, Abcam). After extensive washing with TBST (3 × 10 min), the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit, anti-mouse, or anti-goat IgG; 1:5000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) diluted in blocking buffer. After further washing of the membranes, protein bands were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore) and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system (e.g., ChemiDoc XRS +, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensity (optical density) was quantified in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Target protein levels were normalized to the corresponding β-actin or GAPDH loading control.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected at the designated time points post-stimulation, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The levels of specific inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10) were quantified with commercially available rat-specific ELISA kits (Boster Biological Technology, Pleasanton, CA, USA) strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 96-well plates pre-coated with capture antibodies were used. Standards and samples (typically 100 µl) were added to the wells and incubated. After the plates were washed, biotinylated detection antibodies were added, and incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin was subsequently performed. Tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added for color development, and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid solution. The absorbance (optical density, OD) at 450 nm (with a reference wavelength of 570 nm) was measured immediately with a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Sample concentrations were interpolated from the standard curve generated with the provided recombinant cytokine standards.

Immunofluorescence Staining

For staining of in vivo tissue, rats were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h. Coronal brain sections (20 µm thickness) were cut with a cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and mounted onto Superfrost Plus slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For in vitro cell staining, cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. Both tissue sections and fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h at room temperature to avoid non-specific binding. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution: anti-C3 (1:200, ab200999, Abcam), anti-S100A10 (1:100, ab187201, Abcam), anti-Iba1 (1:500, 019–19741, Wako, Osaka, Japan; for microglia), and anti-GFAP (1:1000, ab7260, Abcam; for astrocytes). After being washed with PBS (3 × 5 min), samples were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, 594, or 647; 1:500; Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. After a final washing step, sections were coverslipped with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity and number of positive cells within defined regions of interest were quantified in ImageJ software by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

Morris Water Maze

The Morris water maze task was performed on the 15th day after SAH to evaluate spatial learning and memory ability. The experiment consisted of 5 days of hidden platform training (four times per day, with recording of the escape latency and swimming path length) and a space exploration test (removal of the platform, and recording the number of times in which the original platform was crossed within 120 s) on the sixth day. All behavioral experiments were conducted in a quiet environment with uniform lighting, and the data were recorded by experimenters who were blinded to the experimental groups. The water temperature in the pool was maintained at 23 ± 1 °C.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

At 24 h after the operation, the rats were deeply anesthetized and then perfused with 0.9% saline and 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and the white matter region containing axon tracts was collected. The tissue was immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight, fixed with paraformaldehyde for 2 h, and subjected to ultrathin sectioning (60 nm), dehydration with acetone, and staining with uranyl acetate. All specimens were examined under an electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700, Tokyo, JPN) at an operating voltage of 80 kV.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Potential key genes and pathways implicated in SAH pathogenesis, particularly in microglia, were identified in publicly available transcriptomic datasets retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Dataset GSE167957 provided transcriptomic profiles of purified microglia isolated from the damaged hemisphere in adult mice 3 days after SAH or sham surgery. Dataset GSE200224 contained transcriptomic data from BV2 microglial cells treated with OxyHb versus control. Raw data (CEL files) were downloaded. Data preprocessing, background correction, normalization (with RMA for Affymetrix arrays), and differential expression analysis were performed in R software (version 4.3.1) and relevant Bioconductor packages (limma and affy). Differentially expressed genes between the SAH/OxyHb and control groups were identified with thresholds of absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 and adjusted p-value (adj. P. val) < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method). Functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed with Gene Ontology terms (Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses via the clusterProfiler package.

Statistics

All data are expressed as the mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism (9.0) was used for statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among groups, and the Tukey method was used for back testing for homogeneous variance. Independent sample t tests were used for comparisons between the two groups. Repeated measurement data, such as water maze escape latency, were analyzed with repeated measurement variance. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Survival Rate and Bleeding Grade in the SAH Rat Model

A total of 135 SD rats were used in the in vivo animal experiments: 18 in the sham operation group and 117 in the SAH model group (27 died). Nine rats were excluded only because of a bleeding score below 7. The average mortality rate was 23.08% (27/117). Figure 1A shows the specific groups and deaths in each group. We observed no differences in SAH grade scores among rats in each group (Fig. 1C).

Microglial Activation Is Suppressed by RARα Signaling Following SAH

To regulate RARα, we used Am80 as an agonist and AGN196996 as an inhibitor, and subsequently detected the inflammatory levels in rat brain tissue. WB indicated significantly higher expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the SAH model group and the AGN196996 treatment group than in the sham group (Fig. 2A, B). However, this elevation was reversed by Am80 treatment. In addition, to explore the specific causes of these inflammatory changes, we used plx3397 to remove microglia in rat brain tissue. The plx3397 treatment group showed significantly lower inflammatory levels than the SAH model rats, thus, indicating similar effects from Am80 (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, we observed similar changes in the cell model constructed through OxyHb treatment (Fig. 2C, D). ELISA indicated that the changes in the pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in each group were consistent with the above, whereas the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 showed opposite changes (Fig. 2E).

Activation of Microglial RARα Ameliorates Neurological Function in SAH Rats

To explore the influence of inflammatory inhibition after RARα activation on neurological function and long-term cognitive dysfunction in SAH model rats, we conducted balance beam tests, Evans blue tests, and water maze tests. Nerve function (Fig. 3A), activity ability (Fig. 3B), and blood-brain barrier (BBB) function (Fig. 3C) were significantly lower in the SAH group than the sham group, whereas the Am80 treatment group showed significant amelioration of these changes. Water maze tests indicated that Am80 significantly ameliorated the spatial exploration and long-term memory cognitive dysfunction in rats in the SAH model group. However, the microglial scavenger PLX3397 did not ameliorate neurological function and long-term cognitive ability in the SAH rats (Fig. 3D–H). In the sham group, the mitochondria, myelin sheath, and microtubules were intact, and the myelinated fibers and non-myelinated fibers were evenly distributed. In the vehicle group, the myelin sheath layers were separated and disintegrated, the axons were diminished in number and irregular in shape, and severe swelling was observed. In the Am80 group, a small number of myelin lamellae were separated and disintegrated, the microfilaments were dense, and the distribution of the myelinated fibers and non-myelinated fibers was uniform (Fig. 3I).

Microglial Regulation of Inflammation Is Mediated by RARα Activation Through the Mafb-Msr1-PI3K-Akt Pathway

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were processed through a standardized pipeline with rigorous quality control procedures. Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were first subjected to primary quality assessment using the 10 × Cell Ranger platform to ensure data integrity and generate high-quality gene expression matrices. Subsequently, downstream analyses were conducted using the Seurat package following its standard work flow. After normalization and dimensionality reduction, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied to visualize the transcriptional heterogeneity among cells (Fig. 4A). Cells were clustered into 17 distinct groups (clusters 0–16) based on gene expression patterns. Through marker gene expression profiling and reference-based annotation, 19 major cell types were identified, including macrophages, microglial cells, monocytes, astrocytes, forebrain neurons, mast cells, neutrophils, neural precursor cells, B cells, T cells, NK cells, mesenchymal cells, oligodendroglial cells, and platelets. The expression profiles of canonical marker genes across these identified cell types are summarized in (Fig. 4B). Each cluster exhibited distinct marker gene signatures, confirming the accuracy of cell-type annotation. Further functional enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG pathway database (Fig. 4C). The results revealed that microglia and astrocytes were strongly enriched in immune and inflammation-related signaling pathways, suggesting potential cell–cell interactions in neuroinflammatory processes.

We explored the signaling pathways and detailed mechanism through which RARα regulates inflammation after SAH. From the NCBI gene expression database, we downloaded GSE167957 data on purified microglial samples in damaged hemispheres of adult mice 3 days after SAH or sham operation, and GSE200224 data on BV2 microglial samples in an OxyHb-induced group and control group. According to the differential gene expression results, a thermogram was drawn (Fig. 5A). Msr1 gene expression in vitro and in vivo was significantly higher in the SAH group than the control group (Fig. 5B, C). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that SAH was clearly associated with inflammation (Fig. 5D). WB indicated significantly greater expression of Mafb and Msr1, and lower levels of p-Akt, in the SAH group than the sham group. The expression of Mafb, Msr1, and p-Akt increased after Am80 treatment, whereas these responses were reversed after AGN196996 treatment. After PLX3397 treatment, the expression of Mafb and Msr1 was significantly lower (Fig. 5E, F), and the level of p-Akt was higher, than observed in the SAH group. Moreover, the expression of Mafb and Msr1 was significantly lower, and the level of p-Akt was higher, in the Am80 treatment group than the PLX3397 + Am80 group. The experimental results further indicated that decreasing the expression of Msr1 or inhibiting PI3K activity significantly exacerbated inflammatory levels after SAH. In addition, the changes in P-NF-κB were consistent with the above (Fig. 5H). WB indicated that, in the cell model, the use of siMsr1 and the inhibitor LY294002 significantly inhibited Am80’s resistance to the pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, thus significantly ameliorating inflammation levels (Fig. 5H–I). These findings indicated that microglial RARα has an anti-inflammatory role through the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt/NF-κB pathway.

Inhibition of Phenotypic Transformation of Astrocytes Caused by RARα Activation Through Microglia Mafb-Mrs1-PI3K-Akt Pathway

WB and immunofluorescence staining indicated that GFAP and C3 expression in the SAH group were significantly elevated, whereas Am80 mitigated these changes. In contrast, S100A10 resulted in opposite changes. In addition, PLX3397, a microglial scavenger, inhibited the effects of Am80 (Fig. 6A–F). Use of si-Msr1 and LY294002 to regulate microglial signaling pathways in the cell model demonstrated that inhibition of Msr1 and PI3K in microglia weakened the inhibitory effect of Am80 on the phenotypic activation of astrocytes (Fig. 6G, H). Brain tissue contains a variety of nerve cells, each with varying states and subtypes in different disease processes. Microglia, immune cells in brain tissue, often significantly influence other nerve cells. To explore the effects of RARα regulation on astrocytes, we co-cultured the two cells. After addition of microglial culture medium to astrocyte culture medium, WB indicated that the microglial culture medium significantly promoted transformation to the toxic astrocyte phenotype (Fig. 6I, J).

Discussion

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq to systematically delineate the cellular, molecular, and spatial alterations in white matter-enriched regions after SAH. This investigation elucidated the interplay between microglia and astrocytes, suggesting that the PI3K/Akt pathway may be involved in SAH-induced neuroinflammation.

This study revealed that the RARα and its downstream Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt signaling axis comprise a potent novel regulatory pathway that is capable of reprogramming this detrimental glial crosstalk and might offer a promising therapeutic strategy (Fig. 8). Our findings robustly demonstrated that pharmacological activation of RARα with the specific agonist Am80 significantly attenuated neuroinflammation and ameliorated both short-term neurological function and long-term cognitive outcomes in a rat model of SAH. Crucially, our mechanistic studies indicated that this neuroprotection hinges on RARα’s ability to modulate microglia-astrocyte communication. We provided compelling evidence that Am80, acting via RARα, upregulates the transcription factor Mafb in microglia. Mafb in turn drives expression of its downstream target, Msr1. This cascade culminates in the phosphorylation and activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. The activation of this Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt axis exerts a dual anti-inflammatory effect. First, it potently suppresses the NF-κB pathway, a master regulator of pro-inflammatory gene expression, thereby decreasing the microglial production and release of cytotoxic cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Second, it concurrently enhances the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators, including IL-10. The involvement of this pathway was supported by the marked reduction in the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of Am80, both in vivo and in vitro, following pharmacological blockade of PI3K with LY294002 or genetic knockdown of either Mafb or Msr1. Furthermore, microglial depletion with PLX3397 before SAH not only decreased baseline inflammation but also prevented Am80 from exerting its beneficial effects on astrocytes. These findings suggest that microglia may be the primary cellular target initiating this protective signaling cascade and mediating communication with astrocytes.

Mechanism diagram: RARα activation modulates microglia-astrocyte crosstalk via the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt pathway to attenuate neuroinflammation after subarachnoid hemorrhage

A critical novel discovery of this study was that RARα activation, regulated the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt pathway, effectively inhibits astrocyte polarization toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype and promotes a shift toward the neuroprotective A2 phenotype, as evidenced by the significant downregulation of the A1 marker C3 and the upregulation of the A2 marker S100A10 in brain tissue and in co-culture models after Am80 treatment. This modulation of astrocyte phenotype was found to be critically dependent on signals originating from microglia, because microglial depletion (PLX3397) or disruption of the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt axis in microglia (via siMsr1 or LY294002) blocked Am80’s ability to suppress toxic A1 astrocytosis. The transformation of astrocytes from a quiescent state to the neurotoxic A1 phenotype (C3 +) is increasingly recognized as a core pathological event in SAH and numerous other neurological disorders [31]. A1 astrocytes lose their essential supportive functions (synaptic maintenance, neurotrophic factor release, and glutamate uptake) and instead acquire detrimental properties. These properties include release of neurotoxins that directly damage neurons and oligodendrocytes, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that amplify microglial activation and recruit peripheral immune cells, upregulation of complement pathways leading to synaptic pruning and neuronal opsonization, and BBB disruption [32]. Our data demonstrated that interruption of the microglial signal driving this A1 transformation via RARα/Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt activation is a potent route to break this vicious cycle. Beyond complement, astrocytes contribute to SAH pathology through other mechanisms, such as aberrantly activating the RhoA/ROCK pathway via Sema4D-PlexinB2 signaling and subsequently disrupting the BBB, or undergoing metabolic reprogramming mediated by lactate transporters that fuel pro-inflammatory factor release [33, 34]. RARα activation might also plausibly ameliorate this astrocytic dysfunction, potentially through modulation of energy metabolism or other yet-to-be-defined pathways downstream of improved glial crosstalk [35].

The positive feedback inflammatory loop formed by reciprocally activating microglia and astrocytes might potentially be the key factor determining the extent of tissue damage and functional recovery after SAH. The breakthrough in the current study involved the identification that Msr1 is not only an intracellular signaling molecule within the PI3K/Akt pathway in microglia but also a crucial core mediator of the intercellular communication between microglia and astrocytes [36–38]. Our data indicated that Msr1 is a critical node, because its knockdown specifically in microglia disrupted the ability of Am80-activated microglia to beneficially influence astrocyte phenotype. Furthermore, we identified TNF-α and IL-6 receptors (TNF-αR and IL-6R) on astrocytes as essential mediators receiving the signals from activated microglia. Inhibition of these receptors on astrocytes (with siRNA) significantly attenuated the phenotypic shift toward the protective A2 state induced by Am80-conditioned microglial signals. This finding aligns with seminal work by Liddelow et al., which established that microglia-derived IL-1α, TNF-α, and C1q are necessary and sufficient to induce the neurotoxic A1 astrocyte phenotype [39]. Our results confirmed the centrality of TNF-α signaling in this process within the SAH context and implicated IL-6R as another important mediator. These cytokines signal through receptors that converge on the NF-κB pathway, a key driver of pro-inflammatory gene expression in astrocytes [6]. Other microglial-derived factors, including IFN-γ, reactive oxygen species, and matrix metalloproteinases, also influence astrocyte reactivity [5, 40–43]. In contrast, reactive astrocytes, particularly of the A1 type, release multiple factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL10, CCL2, and C3) that recruit peripheral immune cells, and further activate and sustain microglial pro-inflammatory responses, thus perpetuating the damaging cycle [44–46]. Although our study focused on interrupting the microglia-to-astrocyte arm driving toxicity, under certain conditions, astrocytes may importantly adopt states that release anti-inflammatory factors or express ligands that engage receptors on microglia, and subsequently suppress their activation and promote inflammation resolution. Targeting RARα appears to promote this beneficial crosstalk [47–49].

Astrocytes are a heterogeneous cell type in the CNS that play a role in CNS immunity, energy metabolism, and antioxidant functions to maintain brain homeostasis. However, understanding in this field is rapidly advancing beyond the simplified concept of A1/A2 dichotomy. Emerging technologies, particularly single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, have revealed that astrocyte reactivity is characterized by remarkable heterogeneity and context dependence [43, 50–52]. Astrocytes exhibit diverse molecular signatures and functional states not only across neurological diseases [37, 50], but also within the same lesion at different time points or even in different spatial locations relative to the injury core. Environmental changes or stimuli can trigger transcriptomic alterations, leading to the emergence of a reversible astrocyte substate potentially optimized for specific functional demands [53]. These states are likely to represent a continuum encompassing mixed pro-inflammatory/repair properties or dynamically evolving phenotypes during disease progression. A substantial limitation of our study, and indeed much of the current literature, was the reliance on the A1/A2 paradigm defined by C3 and S100A10 [54]. Although useful, this binary classification is likely to oversimplify the true complexity and heterogeneity of astrocyte responses in SAH. Future research must use more refined, disease-specific definitions of astrocyte states, with multi-omic approaches to identify comprehensive molecular signatures and functional correlates relevant to SAH progression and recovery. Furthermore, both astrocytes and microglia perform essential physiological functions. Systemic, non-selective inhibition of these cells carries inherent risks [55]. The runaway inflammatory response imbalances the immune crosstalk between microglia, astrocytes, and neurons, affecting regulatory mechanisms of synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and cognition [53]. Regulation of dysregulated crosstalk reduces inflammation in SAH.

In conclusion, the phenotypic transformation of astrocytes, particularly toward neurotoxic states, is a central mechanism in SAH pathology and other neurological disorders. The intricate, dynamic, and often pathogenic interactions between microglia and astrocytes form the critical axis driving neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Although the A1/A2 paradigm provides an essential foundational framework, the application of advanced technologies requires a more nuanced understanding of glial heterogeneity. This study suggests that targeting the RARα/Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt pathway may have therapeutic potential, possibly by regulating microglia–astrocyte crosstalk in SAH. By dampening microglial pro-inflammatory output and redirecting astrocyte reactivity toward a protective phenotype, RARα activation breaks the vicious cycle of neuroinflammation. The identification of TNF-αR and IL-6R on astrocytes as key conduits for microglial signals further refined the molecular targets within this interaction.

However, it was important to acknowledge the limitations of our research. Firstly, the side effects of the drugs used were not evaluated in this study. Second, our in vitro experiments used cell lines rather than primary cells. Lastly, the rats used in this experiment are all male, and gender may affect outcomes and inflammation in SAH. Our findings provide a robust scientific foundation for developing RARα agonists or strategies targeting this downstream axis as novel therapeutics to ameliorate neuroinflammation and improve neurological recovery after the devastating insult of SAH. Future work should focus on validating these mechanisms in higher-order models, exploring delivery strategies for CNS-penetrating RARα modulators, and dissecting the full spectrum of astrocyte states in SAH through cutting-edge single-cell methods.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that RARα activation regulates microglia-astrocyte interaction through the Mafb/Msr1/PI3K-Akt signaling axis, which is the key mechanism alleviating neuroinflammation after SAH. This pathway provides a new strategy for anti-inflammatory therapy targeting glial cell synergistic reactions. In addition, this study demonstrated that the TNF-α receptor and IL-6 receptor importantly influence the interaction between microglia and astrocytes, thus providing new strategies and potential targets for the treatment of SAH.
