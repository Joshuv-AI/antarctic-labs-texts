Isoperimetric inequality for nearly spherical domains in the Bergman ball in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n}
David Kalaj

Abstract
. We prove a quantitative isoperimetric inequality for the nearly spherical subset of the Bergman ball in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} . We prove the Fuglede theorem for such sets. This result is a counterpart of a similar result obtained for the hyperbolic unit ball and it makes the first result on the isoperimetric phenomenon in the Bergman ball.

1.
Introduction and the formula for the perimeter

The
isoperimetric inequality
states that among all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a given volume, the sphere minimizes the perimeter. More precisely:

P(\Omega)\geq C_{n}|\Omega|^{\frac{n-1}{n}},

where
P(\Omega)
is the perimeter,
|\Omega|
is the volume, and
C_{n}
is a dimensional constant. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is a sphere.

Fuglede’s Stability Theorem

[
3
]

While the classical inequality identifies spheres as optimal shapes,
Fuglede’s theorem
addresses the question of stability:

How close is a set
\Omega
to a sphere if its perimeter nearly minimizes the isoperimetric inequality?

Fuglede’s result provides a quantitative estimate linking the deficit:

\delta(\Omega)=P(\Omega)-C_{n}|\Omega|^{\frac{n-1}{n}},

to the geometric deviation of
\Omega
from a sphere. Specifically:

•

If
\delta(\Omega)
is small, then
\Omega
is close (in an appropriate metric) to a spherical domain with the same volume.

The paper
[
4
]
by Fusco, Maggi, and Pratelli is a substantial contribution to the study of isoperimetric inequalities. It advances the understanding of how the isoperimetric deficit, defined as

D(E)=\frac{P(E)}{m\omega_{m}^{1/m}|E|^{(m-1)/m}}-1,

and the Fraenkel asymmetry,

\lambda(E)=\min\left\{\frac{|E\Delta(x+rB)|}{r^{m}}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\ |rB|=|E|\right\},

are related. The results are pivotal in the field and significantly refine the classical isoperimetric inequality.

Extensions to hyperbolic space

The extension of isoperimetric to the hyperbolic space has been given by Schmidt in
[
16
]
.
In the
hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}
, the geometry is non-Euclidean, and the isoperimetric inequality takes a different form, reflecting the curvature of the space. Here, geodesic spheres minimize the isoperimetric ratio.

V. Bögelein, F. Duzaar, C. Scheven
[
1
]
extended stability results to hyperbolic settings. They showed that:

(1)

A quantitative stability result analogous to Fuglede’s holds in
\mathbb{H}^{m}
.

(2)

The deficit in the hyperbolic isoperimetric inequality controls the deviation of a domain from a geodesic sphere.

The Bergman ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
serves as a counterpart to hyperbolic space. In the case
n=1
(i.e., for the complex unit disk), these two spaces coincide, but for
n>1
, they exhibit significant differences. One of the key open problems in this setting is the isoperimetric inequality for subsets of the Bergman ball-that is, the unit ball in complex space equipped with the Bergman metric. This question remains an intriguing challenge in complex geometry and analysis.

While isoperimetric inequalities have been extensively studied in classical hyperbolic spaces and other standard geometric settings, the Bergman ball presents unique difficulties due to the intrinsic properties of the Bergman metric, which is neither isotropic nor conformally flat, unlike the hyperbolic metric. A particularly interesting question is whether the extremal sets for such an isoperimetric inequality are Euclidean balls centered at the origin and their holomorphic images. Evidence supporting this conjecture is provided in this paper. Establishing such an inequality would lead to the determination of the isoperimetric profile of the Bergman ball (for a discussion of isoperimetric profiles, see
[
8
]
).

A notable recent attempt at solving this problem appears in
[
7
]
. Moreover, proving this inequality would have implications for the Lieb–Wehrl entropy conjecture in the context of
SU(N,1)
(see
[
12
]
). In the one-dimensional case, the result was established by Kulikov in
[
11
]
using the isoperimetric inequality for the hyperbolic disk.

The stability framework unifies understanding across Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries, emphasizing the robustness of optimal shapes under perturbations. The Faber-Krahn inequality is essentially a spectral isoperimetric inequality. It emerges from the same principle as the isoperimetric inequality: balls are optimal shapes for fundamental optimization problems in geometry and analysis. The stability of Faber-Krahn inequality for the short-time
Fourier transform published in
[
6
]
also is based on isoperimetric inequality for the Euclidean space.

1.1.
Bergman unit ball

Let
\mathbb{B}_{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z|<1\}
be the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The boundary of
\mathbb{B}_{n}
will be denoted by
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}_{n}
. Let
dV
denote the Euclidean volume measure on
\mathbb{B}_{n}
.
The surface measure on
\mathbb{S}_{n}
will be denoted by
dH
. By
\mathbb{B}_{r}
we denote the ball
|z|<\tanh(r/2)
.

The Bergman metric in the unit complex ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z|<1\}
is defined as follows (
[
14
]
):
First, define the Bergman kernel on
\mathbb{B}_{n}
by

K(z,w)=\frac{1}{(1-\langle z,w\rangle)^{n+1}}

where
\langle z,w\rangle=z_{1}\overline{w_{1}}+z_{2}\overline{w_{2}}+\cdots+z_{n}\overline{w_{n}}
is the standard Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The real inner product is then defined by
\langle z,w\rangle_{R}=\Re\langle z,w\rangle
.
The Bergman metric is defined by the matrix
B(z)=\{g_{i\bar{j}}(z)\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
, where

g_{i\bar{j}}(z)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{i}\,\partial\overline{z_{j}}}\log K(z,z),

which provides the components of the metric tensor.

In the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}
, this can be computed explicitly as:

g_{i\bar{j}}(z)=\frac{(1-|z|^{2})\delta_{ij}+\overline{z_{i}}z_{j}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{2}}.

Its inverse is given by the following entries,

g^{i\bar{j}}(z)=(1-|z|^{2})(\delta_{ij}-\overline{z_{i}}z_{j}).

1.2.
The Bergman perimeter of a set in the Bergman ball

The aim of this section is to find the formula for the perimeter of a level set in the Bergman ball.
The measure (fundamental form) on the Bergman ball is defined by

d\mu(z)=(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}dV(z),

where
dV(z)
is the
2n
dimensional Lebesgue measure. So the Bergman measure of an set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{B}
is given by

\mu(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}d\mu(z).

Assume that
U
is a smooth real function defined in the unit ball and assume that

M=\{z\in\mathbb{B}:U(z)=c\}.

For a point
z\in M
let
N=\nabla_{b}U
be the induced normal by the Bergman metric at
M
. Assume
\sigma_{g}
is the volume form in
\mathbb{B}
and let
\sigma_{\tilde{g}}
be the induced area form in
M
. We use the formula

(1.1)

\tilde{\sigma}=\sigma_{\tilde{g}}=\iota^{\ast}_{M}(N\lrcorner\sigma_{g})

see
[
13
, Proposition 15.32]
.
Now we find the unit normal vector field. We first have

\nabla_{b}U=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{i\bar{j}}(z)\frac{\partial U}{\partial z_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}.

Then

\nabla_{b}U=(1-|z|^{2})((1-|z_{1}|^{2})U_{\overline{z}_{1}}-\sum_{k=2}^{n}z_{1}\overline{z_{k}}U_{\overline{z}_{k}},\dots,(1-|z_{n}|^{2})U_{\overline{z}_{n}}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}z_{n}\overline{z_{k}}U_{\overline{z}_{k}}).

We compute

\begin{split}\left<\nabla_{b}U,\nabla U\right>&=(1-|z|^{2})\left((1-|z|^{2})|\nabla U|^{2}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}|z_{k}U_{\bar{z}_{j}}-z_{j}U_{\bar{z}_{k}}|^{2}\right)\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})\left((1-|z|^{2})|\nabla U|^{2}+|z|^{2}|\nabla U|^{2}-|\left<z,\nabla U\right>|^{2}\right)\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})(|\nabla U|^{2}-|\left<z,\nabla U\right>|^{2}).\end{split}

We also have

|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}^{2}=\left<\nabla_{b}U,\nabla U\right>.

This implies the formula

(1.2)

\left<\frac{\nabla_{b}U}{|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}},\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|}\right>=\sqrt{1-|z|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\left|\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>\right|^{2}}.

The inner product between two vectors
V_{1},V_{2}
in the Bergman ball is given by

\left<V_{1},V_{2}\right>_{b}=\Re[\left<B(z)V_{1},V_{2}\right>.

Now for
V_{1}=\nabla_{b}U
, we have
B(z)V_{1}=c\nabla U,
with
c>0
. From this we see that,
V\in TM_{z}
w.r.t. Bergman structure if and only if
V\in TM_{z}
w.r.t. Euclidean structure of
\mathbb{B}_{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Then from (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), for vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1}
from
TM_{z}
we find that

\begin{split}\tilde{\sigma}(v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1})&=V_{g}\left(\frac{\nabla_{b}U}{|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}},v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1}\right)\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}V\left(\frac{\nabla_{b}U}{|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}},v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}\right)\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}\left<\frac{\nabla_{b}U}{|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}},\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|}\right>V\left(\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1}\right)\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}\left<\frac{\nabla_{b}U}{|\nabla_{b}U|_{b}},\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|}\right>\sigma(v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1})\\
&=(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1-\left|\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>\right|^{2}}\sigma(v_{1},\dots,v_{2n-1}).\end{split}

Then we get the following formula which is crucial for this paper

(1.3)

P(E)=\int_{U(z)=c}(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1-\left|\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>\right|^{2}}d\sigma(z).

Remark, that a special case of this formula for the balls
\mathbb{B}_{r}=\{z:|z|<t=\tanh(r/2)\}
, concentric to the Bergman ball has been calculated by Kehe Zhu in
[
17
, Sect. 1.6]
. His formula reads as follows

(1.4)

P(\mathbb{B}_{r})=\int_{\mathbb{S}}t^{2n-1}(1-t^{2})^{-n}dH(\omega),

which coincides with (
1.3
) for
U(z)=|z|=\tanh(r/2)
, because in this particular case

\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>=\left<\frac{z}{|z|},z\right>=|z|.

For the case of
n=1
the above formula coincides with the perimeter of a curve
\gamma
in the hyperbolic unit disk, and that formula is well-known (see
[
16
]
and its application in
[
11
]
). Namely in this case,
\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>=\frac{U_{\bar{z}}}{|U_{\bar{z}}|}\overline{z}
, and thus
|\left<\frac{\nabla U}{|\nabla U|},z\right>|^{2}=|z|^{2}.
Then

P(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\frac{|dz|}{(1-|z|^{2})}.

1.3.
Automorphisms of the unit ball
B\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}

Let
P_{a}
be the
orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the subspace
[a]
generated by
a
, and let

Q=Q_{a}=I-P_{a}

be the projection onto the orthogonal complement of
[a]
. Explicitly,
P_{0}=0
and
P=P_{a}(z)=\frac{\left<z,a\right>a}{\left<a,a\right>}
. Set
s_{a}=(1-|a|^{2})^{1/2}
and consider the map

(1.5)

p_{a}(z)=\frac{a-P_{a}z-s_{a}Q_{a}z}{1-\left<z,a\right>}.

Compositions of mappings of the form (
1.5
) and unitary linear mappings of the
\mathbb{C}^{n}
constitute the group of holomorphic automorphisms of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It is easy to verify that
p_{a}^{-1}=p_{a}
. Moreover, for any automorphism
q
of the Bergman ball onto itself there exists a unitary transformation
U
such that

(1.6)

p_{q(c)}\circ q=U\circ p_{c}.

By using the representation formula
[
17
, Proposition 1.21]
, we can introduce the Bergman metric as

(1.7)

d_{B}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+|p_{w}(z)|}{1-|p_{w}(z)|}.

If
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\left<z,a\right>\neq 1\}
, then the map
p_{a}
is holomorphic in
\Omega
. It is clear that
\overline{\mathbb{B}_{n}}\subset\Omega
for
|a|<1.

It is well-known that every automorphism
q
of the unit ball is an isometry w.r. to the Bergman metric, that is:
d_{B}(z,w)=d_{B}(q(z),q(w))
. In particular, every isometry preserves the volume and perimeter of any measurable subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{B}
of finite measure.

1.4.
Holomorphic barycenter of a set

We know from
[
5
, p. 54]
that the Bergman metric has negative (nonconstant) sectional curvature, which implies that the square of the corresponding distance function is strictly geodesically convex (see
[
10
, eq. 4.8.7]
). Consequently, the mapping

(1.8)

K(b)=\int_{E}\log\cosh^{2}(d_{B}(z,b))(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}\,dV(z)

is strictly geodesically convex. Therefore, it attains a unique minimum, which we refer to as the
barycenter
of
E
.

Let
a
be the barycenter of
E
. Then, the gradient of
K(b)
vanishes at
b=a
, leading to the equation

(1.9)

\int_{E}p_{a}(z)(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}\,dV(z)=0.

For further details, we refer to the recent paper
[
9
]
.

Alternatively, one could construct a barycenter using the strict convexity of the function

H(b)=\int_{E}d^{2}_{B}(z,b)(1-|z|^{2})^{-n-1}\,dV(z),

as in
[
1
]
. However, we choose to maintain our definition since the corresponding stationary equation takes a more convenient form.

Together with this introduction, the paper consists of three additional sections. In the second section, we formulate the main results and establish some auxiliary results necessary for their proof. The third section is dedicated to proving Theorem
2.1
. Finally, the last section serves as an appendix, where we provide formulas for spherical coordinates and the gradient of a function defined on the unit sphere in these coordinates. The appendix also includes some applications of our theorem.

2.
The main result

To formulate our main result, let us introduce some notation. Let
\mathbb{S}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
For a given point
z\in\mathbb{S}
, the tangent space
T_{z}\mathbb{S}
is the set of all vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
that are orthogonal to
z
, i.e.,

T_{z}\mathbb{S}=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\operatorname{Re}(\langle z,v\rangle)=0\},

where
\langle z,v\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}\overline{v_{j}}
is the Hermitian inner product.

Let
\tau=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{2n-1}\}
be an orthonormal frame for
T_{z}\mathbb{S}
, consisting of vectors that satisfy:

\langle\tau_{i},\tau_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij},\quad\text{and }\operatorname{Re}(\langle z,\tau_{i}\rangle)=0\text{ for all }i.

Let
\nabla
denote the Levi-Civita connection on the sphere
\mathbb{S}
. For any real valued differentiable function
U
on
\mathbb{S}
, the covariant derivative in the direction of
\tau_{i}
is defined as
\nabla_{\tau_{i}}U
. The notation
\nabla_{\tau}U
refers to the collection of these covariant derivatives:

\nabla_{\tau}U=\{\nabla_{\tau_{1}}U,\nabla_{\tau_{2}}U,\dots,\nabla_{\tau_{2n-1}}U\}.

The norm of
\nabla_{\tau}
is given by the sum of the squared norms of these directional derivatives:

|\nabla_{\tau}U|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}(\nabla_{\tau_{i}}U)^{2}.

Then we define

\|U\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}|\nabla_{\tau}U|^{2}d\sigma+\int_{\mathbb{S}}|U|^{2}d\sigma.

We also define

\|U\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=\max\{|\nabla_{\tau}U|_{\infty},|U|_{\infty}\}.

Theorem 2.1

(Fuglede’s theorem for the Bergman ball)
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\sqrt{1+n}}{\sqrt{2n}}\right)
, and any
r_{0}>0
, there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
, depending only on
n
,
r_{0}
, and
\theta
, such that the following holds:

Consider any nearly spherical set
E\subset\mathbb{B}_{n}
in the Bergman ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with its barycenter at the origin and satisfying the volume constraint

V(E)=V(\mathbb{B}_{r}),

for some
r\in(0,r_{0}]
.

Suppose the boundary of
E
is given by a graph over the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
, expressed as

Z:\mathbb{S}\to\partial E,\quad Z(\omega)=\omega\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right),

where
u:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function satisfying:

\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S})}\leq\varepsilon_{0},

and

(2.1)

\left\|\nabla_{\imath\omega}u(\omega)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq\theta\left\||\nabla_{\tau}u|\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}.

Then, the following estimate holds:

\frac{D(E)}{P(\mathbb{B}_{r})}=\frac{P(E)-P(\mathbb{B}_{r})}{P(\mathbb{B}_{r})}\geq c_{1}(n,r_{0},\theta)\|u\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2},

where
c_{1}(n,r_{0},\theta)>0
depends only on
n
,
r_{0}
, and
\theta
.

Remark 2.2
.

Our approach provides an explicit estimate for
c_{1}(n,r_{0},\theta)
, as indicated in (
3.9
).
We think the estimate (
2.1
) is redundant. Some applications of this theorem are given in the appendix below.

2.1.
Auxiliary results

2.1.1.
A formula for the volume of
E
.

A parametrization of
E
is given by

\tilde{Z}:\mathbb{S}\times[0,r]\to E,\quad\tilde{Z}(\omega,\rho)=\omega\tanh\left(\frac{\rho}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right).

For fixed
\omega\in\mathbb{S}
and
\rho\in[0,r]
, we introduce the notation
\mathbf{t}:=\tanh\left(\frac{\rho}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right)
. In order to compute the Jacobian of
\tilde{Z}
, we fix an orthonormal real basis
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{2n-1}
of
T_{\omega}\mathbb{S}
(w.r.t.
\left<w,z\right>_{R}:=\Re\left<w,z\right>
). Choose
\tau_{2n-1}=\imath\omega=\imath(\omega_{1}+\imath\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{2n-1}+\imath\omega_{2n})=(-\omega_{2},\omega_{1},\dots,-\omega_{2n},\omega_{2n-1})\in\mathbb{S}
. Let
\tau_{2n}=\omega
. (Then
\left<\omega,\imath\omega\right>_{R}=0
). Then we choose
(\tau_{1},0),\dots,(\tau_{2n-1},0),e_{2n}
as orthonormal basis of
T_{(\omega,\rho)}(\mathbb{S}\times(0,r))
and
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{2n-1},\omega
as orthonormal basis of
T_{X(\omega,\rho)}\mathbb{B}_{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

Concerning these choices, the Jacobi matrix of
\tilde{Z}
reads

D\tilde{Z}(\omega,\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{t}&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\mathbf{t}&0\\
\frac{\rho(1-\mathbf{t}^{2})\partial_{1}u}{2}&\cdots&\frac{\rho(1-\mathbf{t}^{2})\partial_{2n-1}u}{2}&\frac{1+u}{2}(1-\mathbf{t}^{2})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}.

Consequently, the Jacobian of
\tilde{Z}
is given by

(2.2)

J\tilde{Z}(\omega,\rho)=\frac{1+u}{2}\mathbf{t}^{2n-1}(1-\mathbf{t}^{2}).

The volume of
E
can thus be written as

V(E)=\int_{E}\left(1-|z|^{2}\right)^{-n-1}dV(z)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{1-\mathbf{t}^{2}}\right)^{n}\frac{1+u}{2}\mathbf{t}^{2n-1}\,d\rho\,dH(\omega).

Recalling the definition of
\mathbf{t}
, we compute

\left(\frac{1}{1-\mathbf{t}^{2}}\right)^{n}\frac{1+u(\omega)}{2}\mathbf{t}^{2n-1}=\frac{1+u}{2}\cosh\left[\frac{1}{2}\rho(1+u)\right]\sinh\left[\frac{1}{2}\rho(1+u(\omega))\right]^{2n-1}.

So

\begin{split}V(E)&=\int_{E}\frac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{n+1}}dV(z)\\
&=\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{1+u}{2}\cosh\left[\frac{1}{2}\rho(1+u(\omega))\right]\sinh\left[\frac{1}{2}\rho(1+u(\omega))\right]^{2n-1}dH(\omega)d\rho\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{\sinh^{2n}\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+u(\omega))\right]}{2n}dH(\omega).\end{split}

Here and in the sequel,
dH=dH^{2n-1}
is the
2n-1
dimensional measure.

2.1.2.
Consequences of the volume constraint
V(\mathbb{B}_{r})=V(E)

Let

F(u):=\frac{\sinh^{2n}\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+u)\right]}{2n}.

Then

\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}F(u(\omega))-F(0)dH(\omega)=0.

Thus

\begin{split}F(u)-F(0)&=\frac{1}{4}r\sinh^{2n-2}\left[\frac{r}{2}\right]\sinh(r)u\\
&+\frac{1}{8}r^{2}(-1+n+n\cosh r)\sinh^{2n-2}\left[\frac{r}{2}\right]u^{2}+\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(u_{1})}{3}u^{3},\end{split}

where

\begin{split}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(u_{1})&=\frac{1}{4}r^{3}\cosh\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+u_{1})\right]\left(1+(n-3)n+n^{2}\cosh[r(1+u_{1})]\right)\\
&\times\sinh^{2n-3}\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+u_{1})\right].\end{split}

Note that
u=u(\omega)
and
u_{1}=u_{1}(\omega)
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}u+\frac{r(-1+n+n\cosh r)}{2\sinh(r)}u^{2}+\frac{4F^{\prime\prime}(u_{1})}{3r\sinh(r)\sinh^{2n-2}\left[\frac{r}{2}\right]}u^{3}dH(\omega)=0.

Since
|u_{1}|\leq 1/2
,

\begin{split}\frac{4F^{\prime\prime}(u_{1})}{3r\sinh(r)\sinh^{2n-2}\left[\frac{r}{2}\right]}\leq C_{1}(n,r_{0}),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}C_{1}(n,r_{0})&=\cosh\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+1/2)\right]\left(1+(n-3)n+n^{2}\cosh[r_{0}(1+1/2)]\right)\\
&\times\max_{r\in[0,r_{0}]}\frac{r^{2}\sinh^{2n-3}\left[\frac{1}{2}r(1+1/2)\right]}{{3\sinh(r)\sinh^{2n-2}\left[\frac{r}{2}\right]}}.\end{split}

Let

c_{0}(n,r)=\frac{r}{2\sinh(r)}(-1+n+n\cosh r).

Then

It follows that

(2.3)

\lvert\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}udH(\omega)\rvert\leq c_{0}(n,r)\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}u^{2}dH(\omega)+\varepsilon_{0}C_{1}(n,r_{0})\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}u^{2}dH(\omega).

Since

\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}u^{2}dH(\omega)=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|u\|_{W^{1,2}}^{2}

we obtain that there is a constant
C_{2}(n,r_{0})
so that

(2.4)

|u_{0}|\leq\varepsilon_{0}C_{2}(n,r_{0})\|u\|_{W^{1,2}}.

2.1.3.
Consequence of barycenter constraint

If
z=0
is the barycenter, then it follows from (
1.8
) that

\int_{E}z(1-|z|^{2})^{-1-n}dV(z)=0.

Thus from (
2.2
) we have

\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{S}}(1-{\mathbf{t}}^{2})^{-n-1}\omega\frac{(1+u(\omega))}{2}{\mathbf{t}}^{2n-1}(1-\mathbf{t}^{2})\mathbf{t}d\rho dH(\omega)=0.

Hence

2.1.4.
Spherical harmonics and
W^{1,2}
norm of
u

For any integer
k\geq 0
, let us denote by
y_{k,i},\,i=1,\ldots,d_{k}
, the
spherical harmonics of order
k
, i.e., the restriction to
\mathbb{S}
of the homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
k
, normalized such that

\|y_{k,i}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=1,\quad\text{for all }k\text{ and }i\in\{1,\ldots,d_{k}\}.

First of all

\int_{\mathbb{S}}1\,d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1}=\omega_{n},

where
\omega_{n}
is the
(2n-1)
-dimensional measure of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, for
z=(x_{1}+iy_{1},\dots,x_{n}+iy_{n})
it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{S}}x_{i}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}y_{i}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1}=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{\mathbb{S}}|z|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{2n}.

Hence, we have

y_{0,0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{1,i}=\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}}},\quad y_{1,i+n}=\frac{y_{i}}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}}}

respectively.

The functions
y_{k,i}
are eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}
. Specifically, for all
k
and
i
,

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}y_{k,i}=k(k+2n-2)y_{k,i}.

Therefore if we write

u=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{k}}a_{k,i}y_{k,i},\quad\text{where}\quad a_{k,i}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}uy_{k,i}d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1},

we have

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{k}}a_{k,i}^{2}.

Further

\int_{S^{2n-1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2n-1}=-\int_{S^{2n-1}}u\,\Delta_{S^{2n-1}}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{k}}k(k+2n-2)a_{k,i}^{2}.

We therefore have

\|u\|_{W_{1,2}}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{k}}\big[k(k+2n-2)+1\big]a_{k,i}^{2}.

It follows from (
2.5
) that

(2.6)

\left|a_{1,i}\right|\leq C_{3}(n,r_{0})\varepsilon_{0}\|u\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})},i=1,\dots,2n.

c_{0}(n,r_{0})=\max_{0\leq r\leq r_{0}}c_{0}(n,r),

where

c(n,r)=\frac{r_{0}}{2\sinh(r)}(-1+n+n\cosh r).

To prove (

), denote by

. Then

\begin{split}\phi(s)&=\frac{d}{ds}\frac{r}{2\sinh(r)}(-1+n+n\cosh r)\\
&=\frac{(-1+n+ns)\sqrt{-1+s^{2}}-(n+(-1+n)s)\cosh^{-1}(s)}{2\left(-1+s^{2}\right)}.\end{split}

Let

\phi(s)=\frac{(-1+n+ns)\sqrt{-1+s^{2}}}{n+(-1+n)s}-\cosh^{-1}(s).

Then

\phi^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{s^{2}-1}(-1+n(2+n+(-1+n)s))}{(n+(-1+n)s)^{2}}

which is clearly positive for
s\geqslant 1
. Thus

(-1+n+ns)\sqrt{-1+s^{2}}-(n+(-1+n)s)\cosh^{-1}(s)

\geqslant(-1+n+n)\sqrt{-1+1^{2}}-(n+(-1+n))\cosh^{-1}(1)=0.

In particular
\phi(s)
is positive. Therefore the function
\frac{r}{2\sinh(r)}(-1+n+n\cosh r)
is increasing in
r
as claimed.

Thus for small enough
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, because
c(r,\theta)>c(r_{0},\theta)
for
r<r_{0}
, in view of (
3.7
) and (
3.8
)
we have that

I_{1}+I_{2}\geq c(r_{0},\theta)\min\{B_{1},B_{2}\}\|u\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}}.

We conclude that if
\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\varepsilon_{0}
, and if
\varepsilon_{0}
is small enough, then

\frac{D(E)}{P(\mathbb{B}_{r})}=\frac{I_{1}+I_{2}}{\omega_{n}}\geq C(n,r_{0},\theta)\|u\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}},

where

(3.9)

C(n,r_{0},\theta)=\frac{c(r_{0},\theta)\min\{B_{1},B_{2}\}}{\omega_{n}}.

Here
\omega_{n}=\frac{2\pi^{n}}{\Gamma(n)}
. Moreover
C(n,r_{0},\theta)>0
because
\theta<\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2n}}.

4.1.
Formula for the Jacobian

Assume that
E=\{z:U(z)<c\}\subset\mathbb{B}
and assume that
\partial E=\{z:U(z)=c\}
.
From the area formula we have that

(3.4)

P(\partial E)=\int_{S^{2n-1}}J_{2n-1}Z\,d\tilde{\sigma},

where
JZ
is the
(2n-1)
-dimensional Jacobian of the map

Z(\omega)=\omega\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right),

\omega\in S^{2n-1}.
Recall that, if
T_{\omega}S^{2n-1}
is the tangential plane to
S^{2n-1}
at
\omega
, then
JZ=\sqrt{{\rm det}((d_{\omega}Z)^{*}d_{\omega}Z)}
, where the linear map
d_{\omega}Z:T_{\omega}S^{2n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is the tangential differential of
Z
at
\omega
and
(d_{\omega}Z)^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to T_{\omega}S^{2n-1}
denotes the adjoint of the differential.

Since for any
\tau\in T_{\omega}S^{2n-1}
we have

d_{\omega}Z(\tau)=\tau\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right)+\frac{r\omega D_{\tau}u(\omega)}{1+\cosh(r(1+u(\omega)))},

the coefficients of the matrix
d_{\omega}Z
relative to an orthonormal base
\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{2n-1}\}
of
T_{\omega}S^{2n-1}
and to the standard base
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{2n}\}
are

\tau_{i}e_{h}\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right)+\frac{\omega_{h}rD_{\tau_{i}}u}{1+\cosh(r(1+u(\omega)))},i=1,\dots,2n-1,h=1,\dots,2n.

Thus, for all
i,j=1,\dots,2n-1
, the coefficients
a_{ij}
of the matrix
(d_{\omega}Z)^{*}d_{\omega}Z
, for

\mathbf{t}=\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}(1+u(\omega))\right),\ \ \mathbf{s}=\frac{r}{1+\cosh(r(1+u(\omega)))}

are given by

a_{ij}=\sum_{h=1}^{2n}\big(\tau_{i}\cdot e_{h}\mathbf{t}+\omega_{h}D_{\tau_{i}}u\cdot\mathbf{s}\big)\big(\tau_{j}\cdot e_{h}\mathbf{t}+\omega_{h}D_{\tau_{j}}u\cdot\mathbf{s}\big)=\delta_{ij}\mathbf{t}^{2}+\mathbf{s}^{2}D_{\tau}uD_{\tau}u.

In the last equality, we have used the fact that
\tau_{i}\cdot\tau_{j}=\delta_{ij}
and
\tau_{i}\cdot\omega=0
for all
i,j=1,\dots,2n-1
. Hence, recalling that for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
one has
{\rm det}(I+a\otimes b)=1+a\cdot b
, we have that

\begin{split}JZ&=\sqrt{{\rm det}(a_{ij})}\\
&=\sqrt{\mathbf{t}^{2(n-1)}+\mathbf{t}^{2(n-2)}\mathbf{s}^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}}\\
&=\mathbf{t}^{n-1}\sqrt{1+\frac{(1-\mathbf{t}^{2})^{2}}{4\mathbf{t}^{2}}r^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}}.\end{split}

One more Theorem application

Let us consider axially invariant functions or complex-invariant functions
u
, i.e. solutions of the equation
\nabla_{\imath z}u=0
. Let us consider the sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
For a smooth function
U:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, the operator
\nabla_{\imath z}
refers to the projection of the gradient of
U
onto the complex tangent directions associated with multiplication by
\imath
. In other words:

\nabla_{\imath z}U(z)=dU(\imath z),

where
\imath z=(\imath z_{1},\imath z_{2})
.

The condition
\nabla_{\imath z}U(z)=0
for every
z\in\mathbb{S}
implies that the directional derivative of
U
in the direction
\imath z
is zero at every point.

The tangent space of
\mathbb{S}
at a point
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
is given by:

T_{z}\mathbb{S}=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\mathrm{Re}(\langle z,v\rangle)=0\},

where
\langle z,v\rangle=z_{1}\overline{v_{1}}+z_{2}\overline{v_{2}}
is the Hermitian inner product.

The complex tangent space (denoted
T_{z}^{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{S}
) is a subspace of
T_{z}\mathbb{S}
consisting of vectors
v\in T_{z}\mathbb{S}
that are invariant under multiplication by
\imath
, i.e.:

T_{z}^{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{S}=T_{z}\mathbb{S}\cap\imath T_{z}\mathbb{S}.

This subspace has real dimension 2 (complex dimension 1).

The condition
\nabla_{\imath z}U(z)=0
means that the function
U
does not vary in the
\imath z
direction at any point
z\in\mathbb{S}
.

The vector
\imath z=(\imath z_{1},\imath z_{2})
lies in the tangent space
T_{z}\mathbb{S}
because:

\mathrm{Re}(\langle z,\imath z\rangle)=\mathrm{Re}(z_{1}\overline{\imath z_{1}}+z_{2}\overline{\imath z_{2}})=\mathrm{Re}(-\imath|z_{1}|^{2}-\imath|z_{2}|^{2})=0.

This indicates that
\imath z
is a valid tangent vector.

The direction defined by
\imath z
corresponds to the natural
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action (complex phase rotations) on
\mathbb{S}
, given by:

(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(e^{\imath\theta}z_{1},e^{\imath\theta}z_{2}),\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

This
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action generates the Hopf fibers of
\mathbb{S}
, with each point on
\mathbb{S}
lying on an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-orbit. The function
U
is constant along these Hopf fibers and thus depends only on the base space of the Hopf fibration, which is topologically
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

If
\nabla_{\imath z}U(z)=0
,
U
remains constant along the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-orbits, meaning
U
does not vary as
z
moves along the Hopf fibers. An example of such a function is

U(z_{1},z_{2})=f(|z_{1}|^{2},|z_{2}|^{2},\Re(z_{1}\overline{z_{2}}),\Im(z_{1}\overline{z_{2}})),

where
f
is any smooth function of four variables. Thus in particular, our theorem applies to the nearly spherical ellipsoids
a|z_{1}|^{2}+b|z_{2}|^{2}<1
for
a,b>1
and
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
