A Randomized Controlled Trial to Evaluate and Compare the Vertical Marginal Fit of Monolithic Zirconia Crowns Obtained Using Intraoral Scanning vs Monophase Impression Technique: An In Vivo Study
Authors: Rushikesh Narsing Kaknale, Rajiv Kumar Gupta, Akshay Bhargava, Bharti Dua, Manoj Kumar Goyat, Mahitosh Ashokrao Lakhe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The marginal fit of dental restorations is a critical factor influencing their longevity and clinical success. With the advent of digital dentistry, intraoral scanning has emerged as an alternative to conventional impression techniques.Methods:This in vivo study included 20 patients requiring mandibular first molar crowns. A total of 40 crowns were fabricated and divided into two groups: Group A (n = 20) using conventional monophase impression technique and Group B (n = 20) using the digital impression technique. All teeth were prepared with a chamfer finish line. The marginal adaptation was evaluated using the silicone replica technique, and measurements were taken at four points (mid-buccal, mid-lingual, mid-mesial, and mid-distal) under a stereomicroscope.Results:The mean marginal discrepancy for Group A (conventional) was 107.69 ± 32.67 µm, while Group B (digital) showed a mean of 100.41 ± 35.53 µm. No statistically significant difference was found between the two groups (P = 0.504). Regional analysis also revealed no significant differences at any measurement point (buccal: P = 0.929; lingual: P = 0.866; mesial: P = 0.355; distal: P = 0.445). Both groups demonstrated marginal discrepancies within the clinically acceptable range of 120 µm.Conclusion:Both conventional monophase impression and digital intraoral scanning techniques produce monolithic zirconia crowns with clinically acceptable marginal fit.

INTRODUCTION

Fixed prosthodontics, encompassing dental crowns and fixed partial dentures, represents a fundamental aspect of restorative dentistry, providing durable and aesthetically pleasing solutions for missing teeth.[1] These restorations not only restore form and function but also contribute significantly to patients’ psychological well-being by improving facial aesthetics, phonetics, and self-confidence.[2] With increasing awareness of biocompatibility and aesthetic outcomes among patients, the demand for fixed solutions that mimic natural dentition in function, appearance, and maintenance has grown considerably.[3]

The success of fixed prosthodontic restorations depends on multiple factors, with marginal fit being one of the most critical determinants of long-term success.[4] A well-adapted margin minimizes microleakage, reduces cement dissolution, and prevents plaque accumulation, thereby lowering the risk of secondary caries and gingival inflammation.[5] Numerous clinical studies have established a direct relationship between marginal integrity and prosthetic longevity.[6] The literature suggests that an ideal marginal discrepancy should not exceed 120 µm, as larger gaps facilitate bacterial colonization, plaque accumulation, and ingress of oral fluids, leading to chronic inflammation and eventual restoration failure.[7]

As material science has evolved, zirconia has become increasingly popular for fabricating all-ceramic restorations.[8] Monolithic zirconia crowns offer a promising combination of strength, longevity, and biocompatibility, making them suitable for both anterior and posterior regions.[9]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study involved 20 patients requiring single mandibular first molar crowns, with 40 crowns fabricated and divided into two groups: Group A (n = 20) received monolithic zirconia crowns fabricated using the conventional monophase impression technique, while Group B (n = 20) received crowns fabricated using the digital impression technique. The sample size was based on similar previous studies, ensuring adequate statistical power. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients over 18 years with endodontically treated mandibular molars requiring crowns, acceptable oral hygiene, and a visible finish line ≤1 mm below the gingival margin. Exclusion criteria included proximal/cervical caries, tooth mobility, periodontal surgery requirements, and parafunctional habits such as bruxism.

Tooth preparation was standardized by a single operator, with a chamfer finish line, 1.0 mm circumferential reduction, and 1.5 mm occlusal reduction. In Group B, digital impressions were obtained using a calibrated Shining 3D intraoral scanner, scanning the prepared and opposing quadrants, followed by a buccal scan for interocclusal registration. In Group A, conventional impressions were taken using polyether monophase material in custom trays, antagonist arch impressions with alginate, and occlusal records using modeling wax. All impressions were disinfected before being sent to the laboratory.

Crowns in both groups were designed by the same experienced technician using identical CAD/CAM software and manufacturing protocols. Marginal fit was evaluated using the silicone replica technique: A low-viscosity silicone layer was applied to the crown, seated with finger pressure, stabilized with high-viscosity silicone, and sectioned mesiodistally and buccolingually. Marginal gaps were measured at four points under a stereomicroscope with an image analyzer. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

DISCUSSION

The present study compared the marginal discrepancy of monolithic zirconia crowns fabricated using conventional monophase impressions and digital intraoral scanning. Mean values were 107.69 ± 32.67 µm for conventional and 100.41 ± 35.53 µm for digital impressions, both within the clinically acceptable threshold of 120 µm as proposed by Holmes et al.[1]. No statistically significant difference was observed, consistent with findings from previous research demonstrating similar outcomes between the two methods.[23] While the digital method showed a slightly lower mean value, this difference was not significant, likely due to the standardized protocols applied for both techniques.[4]

Factors influencing these results include material properties, technique accuracy, and operator proficiency. For the conventional method, monophase techniques reduce distortion and ensure dimensional accuracy,[5] though potential for shrinkage or setting distortion exists. Digital impressions depend on scanner resolution, software algorithms, and operator technique, with evidence showing operator experience can affect accuracy.[6] The use of standardized scanning protocols in this study likely minimized such variability, supporting findings that both workflows can yield comparable marginal fits when performed meticulously.[3]

Clinically, both techniques produced crowns with acceptable marginal fit, enabling selection based on patient comfort, workflow efficiency, and resource availability. Digital impressions offer advantages in comfort and speed, with patient preference documented in prior studies.[7] Conventional methods remain a viable, reliable option, especially in settings lacking access to digital equipment. Study limitations include the in vivo setting, which may introduce variables such as moisture or patient movement, and the exclusion of internal fit or long-term outcomes. Future research should evaluate different scanners, materials, and preparation designs, as well as assess both marginal and internal fits over extended follow-up periods.[89]

CONCLUSION

This in vivo study successfully evaluated and compared the marginal adaptation of monolithic zirconia crowns fabricated using conventional monophase impression and digital intraoral scanning techniques. The results demonstrated no statistically significant difference in marginal fit between the two methods, with both producing crowns within the clinically acceptable range of 120 µm. These findings suggest that both conventional and digital impression techniques are clinically viable for fabricating monolithic zirconia crowns, allowing clinicians to select the method based on practical considerations such as cost, patient comfort, and practice infrastructure without compromising restoration fit.
