Guess your neighbor’s input: Quantum advantage in Feige’s game
Simon Schmidt, Sigurd A. L. Storgaard, Michael Walter, Yuming Zhao

Abstract
. In this article, we study a nonlocal game with two questions and three answers per player, which was first considered by Feige in 1991, and show that there is quantum advantage in this game. We prove that the game is a robust self-test for the 3 3 -dimensional maximally entangled state. Furthermore, we show that the game can be seen as the ”or” of two games that each do not have quantum advantage. Lastly, we investigate the behavior of the game with respect to parallel repetition in the classical, quantum and non-signalling case and obtain perfect parallel repetition of the non-signalling value if Feige’s game is repeated an even amount of times.



1.
Introduction

A
nonlocal game

CHTW 04
consists of two (or more) cooperative players, often called Alice and Bob, that interact with a referee. In the game, the referee sends a question to each player, for which each player gives an answer. Based on the tuple of questions and answers, the referee decides if the players win or lose. Communication is not permitted between Alice and Bob, hence each player has no information about the questions given to the other players, nor do they know the answers provided to the referee by the other players. Nevertheless, the description of the game is known to the players ahead of time, allowing them to agree on a strategy beforehand and maximize their probability of winning the game. The
classical value

\omega_{c}(G)
of a nonlocal game
G
is the maximum winning probability of classical players, and the
quantum value

\omega_{q}(G)
denotes the maximum winning probability of quantum players sharing a (finite) amount of quantum resources (such as entangled quantum states, like EPR pairs). Furthermore, the
non-signalling value

\omega_{ns}(G)
is the maximum winning probability if one considers probability distributions such that the marginal distribution of either player’s answers must be independent of the other player’s question, without requiring the probability distribution to be physically realizable.

Feige’s game.
In this work, we revisit a game first studied by Feige
Fei 91
, where it was considered as counterexample in parallel repetition since its value does not decrease when played twice in parallel. The game is defined as follows. Alice and Bob both receive a bit as question (with uniform probability), and they are each allowed to choose an answer from the set
\{0,1,\perp\}
. To win the game, exactly one of the players has to answer with
\perp
, while the other player’s answer has to agree with the question the player who answered
\perp
received. More formally, the game
G_{F}

Fei 91
is defined via

X=Y=\{0,1\},\quad A=B=\{0,1,\perp\},\quad\pi(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{4}\quad\text{for all }(x,y)

and has the predicate

V(a,b,x,y)=\begin{cases}1\quad(a,b)=(\perp,x)\text{ or }(a,b)=(y,\perp),\\
0\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It has been conjectured in the literature that
\omega_{q}(G_{F})=\omega_{c}(G_{F})
(
Yue 16
), but so far no attempts for computing the quantum value of the game were made. Since there is no quantum advantage for other variants of ”guess your neighbors input” games (
ABB+ 10
,
AAAB 16
), it sounds plausible that there is also no quantum advantage in Feige’s game. We show that, surprisingly, there exists a quantum strategy for Feige’s game that exceeds the classical value and we compute the quantum value.

Theorem
.

Feige’s game
G_{F}
has quantum advantage with
\omega_{q}(G_{F})=\frac{9}{16}>\frac{1}{2}=\omega_{c}(G_{F})
.

Interestingly, the players use operators acting on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
in the optimal quantum strategy and they share the maximally entangled state
\mathinner{|\psi_{3}\rangle}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\mathinner{|i\rangle}\otimes\mathinner{|i\rangle}
. The strategy that achieves a winning probability of
\frac{9}{16}
is constructed from the operators

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}=Z\oplus 1\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{X}=X\oplus 1\quad\text{on}\quad\mathbb{C}^{3}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C},

where
X
and
Z
denote the usual Pauli matrices. The projective measurements are given by

\displaystyle P^{x}_{a}=\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\tilde{a}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{a}|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X},\quad a\in\{0,1,\bot\}\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle Q^{y}_{b}=\tilde{X}P^{y}_{b}\tilde{X},\quad b\in\{0,1,\bot\},

for a carefully chosen basis
\{\mathinner{|\tilde{a}\rangle}\}
of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. To obtain this basis, we crucially use that
\tilde{Z}
and
\tilde{X}
are two non-commuting observables such that
\tilde{Z}
and
\tilde{X}\tilde{Z}\tilde{X}
commute.

An approach to upper-bounding the quantum value of a nonlocal game is to associate a formal game polynomial
p
to a game
G
, whose variables are identified with abstract projective measurements
P^{x}_{a}
and
Q^{y}_{b}
such that
\mathinner{\langle\psi|}p(P^{x}_{a},Q^{y}_{b})\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}
is precisely the winning probability of a quantum (commuting) strategy for
G
. To prove that the quantum value of
G_{F}
is at most
\frac{9}{16}
, we find a
sums of square decomposition
of the game polynomial of Feige’s game by using the computer. More precisely, we obtain a vector of formal variables
V=V(P^{x}_{a},Q^{y}_{b})
and a positive complex matrix
Y
such that
V^{*}YV=\frac{9}{16}I-p(P^{x}_{a},Q^{y}_{b})
, which implies
\omega_{q}(G_{F})\leq\frac{9}{16}
.

Self-testing.
A nonlocal game is a
self-test
if its optimal quantum strategy is unique up to local isometries, as introduced in
MY 04
. Self-testing is a useful property of a nonlocal game as it can be used for certifying quantum devices. We have the following theorem for Feige’s game.

Theorem
.

The game
G_{F}
is a robust self-test for the maximally entangled state
\mathinner{|\psi_{3}\rangle}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\mathinner{|i\rangle}\otimes\mathinner{|i\rangle}
.

While self-tests for all maximally entangled states are known (
MPS 24
;
Fu 22
), we believe that this is the smallest game that self-tests
\mathinner{|\psi_{3}\rangle}
with just two questions and three answers per player.

In fact, we obtain the result by showing that a certain interesting algebra has a unique irreducible representation. We deduce the algebra from the sums of square decomposition we have seen before. Since it holds
V^{*}YV=0
in an optimal strategy, there are relations that the operators in the strategy have to fulfill. In our case we get the relations for Alice’s operators

\displaystyle P^{x}_{a}P^{1-x}_{a^{\prime}}P^{x}_{a}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}

\displaystyle=\nu_{aa^{\prime}}P^{x}_{a}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\text{ for all }x\in\{0,1\},a,a^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\bot\},\text{ where }\nu=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{9}{16}&\frac{1}{16}&\frac{3}{8}\\
\frac{1}{16}&\frac{9}{16}&\frac{3}{8}\\
\frac{3}{8}&\frac{3}{8}&\frac{1}{4}\end{bmatrix}.

The same relations hold for Bob’s operators. Note that the relations look similar to the relations obtained from mutually unbiased measurements
TFR+ 21
, where for the latter it holds
\nu=\frac{1}{d}I
. In case of
P^{x}_{a}=\mathinner{|\phi^{x}_{a}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\phi_{a}^{x}|}
being rank
1
projections, the transition matrix from the basis
\{\mathinner{|\phi^{x}_{a}\rangle}\}
to the basis
\{\mathinner{|\phi^{1-x}_{a}\rangle}\}
is given by a unitary matrix
H
with
|h_{aa^{\prime}}|=\sqrt{\nu_{aa^{\prime}}}
(which is a Hadamard matrix in the case of
\nu=\frac{1}{d}I
). Similar to some Hadamard matrices being unique up to equivalence
Haa 97
, we show that the same is true for unitaries with
|h_{aa^{\prime}}|=\sqrt{\nu_{aa^{\prime}}}
for the specific choice of
\nu
from above. More generally, we give simple criterion for when such a unitary is unique up to a certain kind of equivalence. Using this, and the operator-algebraic framework for self-testing developed in
Zha 24
, we obtain that Feige’s game is a self-test. Another ingredient we need in our proof is that in an optimal strategy, the actions of Bob’s projective measurements on the state are determined by actions of (self-adjoint polynomials in) Alice’s projective measurements.

Feige’s game as ”or”-game.
Feige’s game has another interesting property, it can be seen as the ”or” of two games (
MS 23
). For two nonlocal games
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, the
(G_{1}\lor G_{2})
-game is defined as follows. The referee sends Alice and Bob a pair of questions
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
, respectively, where
x_{i},y_{i}
are questions in
G_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Each of them chooses one of the questions they received and responds with an answer from the corresponding game. To win the game, two conditions have to be fulfilled:

(1)

Alice and Bob have to give answers from the same game,

(2)

their answers have to win the corresponding game.

Since in Feige’s game, one of the players has to guess the input of the other player, it can be realized as the ”or”-game of the following two games: In the first game, Alice always guesses the input of Bob, while he outputs
\perp
all the time and in the second game the roles are reversed. It is intriguing to note that the games for which a fixed player guesses the input of the other player do not have quantum advantage. We therefore have an example of an ”or”-game whose quantum value is strictly higher than the maximum of the quantum values of the games it is constructed from.

Theorem
.

There are nonlocal games
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that
G_{F}=G_{1}\lor G_{2}
and

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(G_{F})=\frac{9}{16}>\frac{1}{2}=\mathrm{max}\{\omega_{q}(G_{1}),\omega_{q}(G_{2})\}.

This theorem is in contrast to the case of perfect quantum strategies (
MS 23
). Here it is known that if the players give answers for one of the games in a perfect strategy of the ”or”-game, then this game has to have a perfect quantum strategy as well.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
A,B,X,Y
be finite sets,
\pi:X\times Y\to[0,1]
a probability distribution and
V:A\times B\times X\times Y\to\{0,1\}
a function.

Definition 2.1
.

A
(two-player) nonlocal game
is a tuple
G=(X,Y,A,B,\pi,V)
describing a scenario consisting of non-communicating players, Alice and Bob, interacting with a referee.
In the game, the referee samples a pair of questions
(x,y)\in X\times Y
according to
\pi
, sending question
x
to Alice and
y
to Bob.
Then, Alice (respectively Bob) returns answer
a
(respectively
b
) to the referee. The players win if
V(a,b|x,y)=1
, otherwise they lose.

The players are not allowed to communicate during the game, but they can agree on a strategy beforehand. For each strategy, we obtain probabilities
p(a,b|x,y)
of Alice and Bob answering
a,b
given
x,y
. The collection of numbers
\{p(a,b|x,y)\}
is called a
correlation
. The winning probability for the game, using this strategy, is given by

\displaystyle\omega(G,S)=\omega(G,p)=\sum_{a,b,x,y}\pi(x,y)V(a,b|x,y)p(a,b|x,y)

2.1.
Strategies for nonlocal games

There are different types of strategies, which we now describe. We start with classical strategies.

Definition 2.2
.

A
classical strategy
for a nonlocal game
G
consists of

(1)

a probability distribution
\gamma\colon\Omega\to[0,1]
on a (without loss of generality) finite probability space
\Omega
, along with

(2)

probability distributions
\{p_{\omega}(a|x):x\in X,\omega\in\Omega\}
with outcomes in
A
and
\{q_{\omega}(b|y):y\in Y,\omega\in\Omega\}
with outcomes in
B
.

In this case, we have

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\gamma(\omega)\,p_{\omega}(a|x)\,q_{\omega}(b|y).

A special case of classical strategies are
deterministic strategies
, in which the answers of the players are determined by the questions. In this case, the strategies are described by functions
f:X\to A
and
g:Y\to B
, and Alice and Bob answer with
f(x)
and
g(y)
given
x
and
y
, respectively.

In a quantum strategy, the players are allowed to share a quantum state and perform local measurements. A
(quantum) state

\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}
is a unit vector in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The measurements are described by
positive operator valued measures
(POVMs), which consist of a family of positive operators
\{M_{i}\in B(\mathcal{H})\,|\,i\in[m]\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}=1_{B(\mathcal{H})}
. If all positive operators are projections (
M_{i}=M_{i}^{*}=M_{i}^{2}
), then we call
\{M_{i}\in B(\mathcal{H})\,|\,i\in[m]\}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}=1_{B(\mathcal{H})}
a
projective measurement
(PVM).

Definition 2.3
.

A
quantum strategy
for a nonlocal game
G
consists of

(1)

a (without loss of generality) pure quantum state
\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, along with

(2)

POVMs
\{\{P_{a}^{x}:a\in A\}:x\in X\}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and POVMs
\{\{Q_{b}^{y}:b\in B\}:y\in Y\}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.

Then

p(a,b|x,y)=\mathinner{\langle\psi|}P_{a}^{x}\otimes Q_{b}^{y}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}.

Now, we will define non-signalling strategies. These strategies do not have to be physical, but the resulting probability distribution ensures that Alice does not use Bob’s input for her answer and vice versa.

Definition 2.4
.

A
non-signalling strategy
consists of a correlation
\{p(a,b|x,y)\}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}p(a,b|x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}p(a,b|\tilde{x},y):=p(b|y),

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in B}p(a,b|x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}p(a,b|x,\tilde{y}):=p(a|b)

for all
x,\tilde{x}\in X
and
y,\tilde{y}\in Y
.

It is natural to ask how well players can perform in a nonlocal game for a specific class of strategies. Therefore, one defines the value of a game as the highest winning probability for a given class of strategies.

Definition 2.5
.

For a nonlocal game
G
and we define

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup_{S\text{ classical strategy}}\omega(G,S),

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup_{S\text{ quantum strategy}}\omega(G,S),

\displaystyle\omega_{ns}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup_{S\text{ non-signalling strategy}}\omega(G,S).

We call
\omega_{c}(G)
the
classical value
,
\omega_{q}(G)
the
quantum value
and
\omega_{ns}(G)
the
non-signalling value
of the game.

From the definition, it is immediate that

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(G)\leq\omega_{q}(G)\leq\omega_{ns}(G).

Note that there exist games for which all these values are different, for example the CHSH game
CHSH 69
.

The proof of our main theorem follows an operator-algebraic framework for self-testing developed in
Zha 24
. For finite sets
X
and
A
, we use
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}
to denote the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{p^{x}_{a}:a\in A,x\in X\}
, subject to the relations

•

(p^{x}_{a})^{2}=(p^{x}_{a})^{*}=p^{x}_{a}\quad\forall a\in A,x\in X

•

\sum_{a\in A}p^{x}_{a}=1\quad\forall x\in X

Given a nonlocal game
G=(X,Y,A,B,\mu,V)
, for any strategy

(1)

S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\{P^{x}_{a}\},\{Q^{y}_{b}\}),

by the universal property, there are unique
*
-representations

\pi_{A}:\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\text{ and }\pi_{B}:\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{Y,B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

such that

(2)

\pi_{A}(p^{x}_{a})=P^{x}_{a}\text{ and }\pi_{B}(q^{y}_{b})=Q^{y}_{b}\quad\forall a\in A,b\in B,x\in X,y\in Y.

In the following we reserve the symbols
p^{x}_{a}
and
q^{y}_{b}
for the generators for
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}
and
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{Y,B}
respectively, whereas
P^{x}_{a}
and
Q^{y}_{b}
will denote representations given by operators on Hilbert spaces as in (
2
).
We call
\pi_{A},\pi_{B}
the representations associated with
S
as in (
1
), and sometimes write

(3)

S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\pi_{A},\pi_{B})

for a strategy.

Given a nonlocal game
G=(X,Y,A,B,\mu,V)
, we define the
game polynomial
as

\Phi_{G}:=\sum_{a,b,x,y}\mu(x,y)V(a,b|x,y)p^{x}_{a}\otimes q^{y}_{b}

which is an element of
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}\otimes\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{Y,B}
that encodes all the information of
G
. The value at the strategy (
3
) is then given by

\omega(G,S)=\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}(\Phi_{G})\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}.

3.
Feige’s game

In this section, we will discuss the classical, quantum and non-signalling value of a game that appeared in
Fei 91
. The game was considered as counterexample in parallel repetition, as its classical value does not decrease when played twice in parallel. As main result of this section, we will show that its quantum value exceeds the classical value.

We recall again the definition of the game from the introduction. Alice and Bob both receive a bit as question (with uniform probability), and they are each allowed to choose an answer from the set
\{0,1,\perp\}
. To win the game, exactly one of the players has to answer with
\perp
, while the second player has to guess the input of the first player, i.e. the answer is a bit that is equal to the question of first player. More formally, the game
G_{F}

Fei 91
is defined via

X=Y=\{0,1\},\quad A=B=\{0,1,\perp\},\quad\pi(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{4}\quad\forall(x,y)

and has the predicate

V(a,b,x,y)=\begin{cases}1\quad(a,b)=(\perp,x)\text{ or }(a,b)=(y,\perp),\\
0\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We will now argue that the classical value of this game is
\frac{1}{2}
. It is well-known that the classical value of a game is attained by a deterministic strategy. It is easy to see that the strategy in which Alice always answers
\perp
and Bob always answers
0
has winning probability
\frac{1}{2}
, since Alice receives the question
0
halve of the time.

Note that in a deterministic strategy, winning or losing just depends on the input of the players. We have the following observations.

(i)

Once a player answers a bit given a question (e.g. Alice answers
0
when receiving
0
), the game will always be lost if the other player receives a question different from this bit (the referee sends
1
to Bob), which happens halve of the time.

(ii)

In a deterministic strategy, the case that exactly one player answers
\perp
and the other player answers with a bit is only possible for zero, two or all four different input pairs
\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
. If this happens for zero or two input pairs, the winning probability is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{2}
. If it is true for all input pairs, we get by (i) that the winning probability of the strategy is
\frac{1}{2}
.

Summarizing, we obtain
\omega_{c}(G_{F})=\frac{1}{2}
. In the following, we will show that there exists a quantum strategy that allows the players to win the game with a higher probability. We will see that the winning probability of the quantum strategy is
\frac{9}{16}
. We show that this is an optimal quantum strategy for Feige’s game in
Appendix

A
and obtain a self-testing statement in
Section

4
.

3.1.
Construction of a quantum strategy exceeding the classical value

The game polynomial for
G_{F}
is given by

\beta(p^{x}_{a},q^{y}_{b})=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}(p^{x}_{\perp}\otimes q^{y}_{x}+p^{x}_{y}\otimes q^{y}_{\perp}).

Consider the operators

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}=Z\oplus 1\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{X}=X\oplus 1\quad\text{on}\quad\mathbb{C}^{3}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}

(i.e.,
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the defining and
\mathbb{C}
the trivial representation of the Pauli group).
Note that
\tilde{Z}
and
\tilde{X}
are two non-commuting observables such that
\tilde{Z}
and
\tilde{X}\tilde{Z}\tilde{X}
commute (and every such pair of projections essentially looks like this, up to multiplicities).
Choose
\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}\in\Fix(\tilde{Z}){\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\ =\mathinner{|1\rangle}^{\perp}}
, for now arbitrarily, and extend it by
\mathinner{|\tilde{0}\rangle},\mathinner{|\tilde{1}\rangle}
to a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
Alice’s and Bob’s strategy is then given by,

\tilde{S}_{F}=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3},\{P^{x}_{a}\},\{Q^{y}_{b}\})

where
\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\mathinner{|00\rangle}+\mathinner{|11\rangle}+\mathinner{|22\rangle}\right)
and

\displaystyle P^{x}_{a}

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\tilde{a}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{a}|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}

\displaystyle(a\in\{0,1\})

\displaystyle P^{x}_{\bot}

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\bot|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}

\displaystyle Q^{y}_{b}

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}P^{y}_{b}\tilde{X}

\displaystyle(b\in\{0,1,\bot\})

The winning probability is given by

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F},\tilde{S}_{F})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\frac{1}{3}\Tr[P^{x}_{y}Q^{y}_{\bot}+P^{x}_{\bot}Q^{y}_{x}]=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\Tr[P^{x}_{y}Q^{y}_{\bot}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\Tr[\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\tilde{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{y}|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{x}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\bot|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\bot|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\mathinner{|\tilde{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{y}|}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}

where the first line holds because

and
\tilde{Z}
commute, as well as that
\tilde{Z}\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}=\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}
.

\displaystyle\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}=\sqrt{p}\underbrace{\mathinner{|\text{nontriv}\rangle}}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}=\mathinner{|0\rangle}}+\sqrt{1-p}\underbrace{\mathinner{|\text{triv}\rangle}}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}=\mathinner{|2\rangle}},

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F},\tilde{S}_{F})

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{3}\underbrace{\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\text{nontriv}|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\mathinner{|\tilde{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{y}|}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\text{nontriv}\rangle}}_{=1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1-p}{3}\underbrace{\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\text{triv}|}\tilde{X}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\mathinner{|\tilde{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{y}|}{\tilde{Z}}^{y}\tilde{X}\mathinner{|\text{triv}\rangle}}_{=1-(1-p)=p}

R=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{y}\mathinner{|\tilde{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle\tilde{y}|}

is an arbitrary reflection supported on
\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}^{\perp}
.
Thus,
(\star)
is an arbitrary state in
\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}^{\perp}

It follows that the winning probability is maximized if we choose

\mathinner{|\text{triv}\rangle}

\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}^{\perp}

=&\lVert p\mathinner{|\text{triv}\rangle}+\sqrt{p(1-p)}\mathinner{|\text{nontriv}\rangle}\rVert\\

In this case, the winning probability is

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F},\tilde{S}_{F})=\frac{p}{3}\left\lparen 2-p+2\sqrt{1-p}\right\rparen.

This is maximized for

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F},\tilde{S}_{F})=\frac{9}{16}.

Concretely, this means that

\mathinner{|\text{triv}\rangle}=\mathinner{|2\rangle}

\displaystyle\mathinner{|\bot\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\mathinner{|0\rangle}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}}\mathinner{|2\rangle},

3.2.
Method for obtaining an upper bounds and algebraic relations

In order to prove that the above strategy is optimal, we obtain a sum-of-squares (SOS) decomposition using a method similar to that of the NPA hierarchy (
NPA 08
). Let us start by defining the following sets of monomials

A^{s}B^{t}=\{p^{x_{1}}_{a_{1}}...p^{x_{s}}_{a_{s}}q^{y_{1}}_{b_{1}}...q^{y_{t}}_{b_{t}}\mid x_{1},...,x_{s}\in X,a_{1},...,a_{s}\in A,y_{1},...,y_{t}\in Y,b_{1},...,b_{t}\in B\}

where
s,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We denote a level of the hierarchy by

k+A^{s_{1}}B^{t_{1}}+...+A^{s_{m}}B^{t_{m}}:=\bigcup_{s+t\leq k}A^{s}B^{t}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}A^{s_{i}}B^{t_{i}}

where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
s_{i}+t_{i}>k
for all
i\in\{1,...,m\}
.

The method can now be described as follows.

(1)

Specify a level as described above, denoted
L
, and a vector of polynomials,

F(p^{x}_{a},q^{y}_{b})=\left(f_{1}(p^{x}_{a},q^{y}_{b}),...f_{n}(p^{x}_{a},q^{y}_{b})\right)^{T}

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
i\in\{1,...,n\}
,
f_{i}(p^{x}_{a},q^{y}_{b})
contains monomials from the set
L
.

(2)

Specify an
n
-by-
n
positive definite real matrix,
Y
, such that

(4)

F^{*}YF=\mu_{L}-\beta.

where
\mu_{L}\geq 0
.

Notice that
n
may be strictly smaller than the cardinality of
L
. Notice also that we require
Y
to be positive definite and not just positive semi-definite.
Now, let

S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\pi_{A},\pi_{B})

be a quantum strategy. Since
Y
is positive definite it has a spectral decomposition

Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|

where
\lambda_{i}>0
and the
\mathinner{|\varphi_{i}\rangle}
’s constitute an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
\tilde{F}_{i}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\mathinner{\langle\varphi_{i}|}F
which are polynomials. Then (
4
) can be written as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{F}_{i}^{*}\tilde{F}_{i}=\mu_{L}-\beta.

so we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(\tilde{F}_{i}^{*}\tilde{F}_{i}\right)=\mu_{L}I-\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}(\beta)\succeq 0.

Thus, we obtain the upper bound

\omega(G_{F},S)=\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}(\beta)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\leq\mu_{L}.

We can also deduce that certain algebraic relations must hold if
S
in fact reaches the upper bound
\mu_{L}
. Suppose
\omega(G_{F},S)=\mu_{L}
. Then

\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(\tilde{F}_{i}^{*}\tilde{F}_{i}\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=0\quad\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}

which implies

\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(F^{*}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|F\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=0\quad\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}

since
\lambda_{i}>0
. Now, summing over
i
we obtain

\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(F^{*}F\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(f_{i}^{*}f_{i}\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(f_{i}\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\rVert^{2}=0.

Thus, we get

\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}\left(f_{i}\right)\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=0\quad\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}.

Our approach is as follows. First, we use standard NPA techniques to verify the upper bound. In the notation here, this means letting the coordinate functions
f_{i}
in
F
be the elements of
L
for some specific level of hierarchy and solving the following semi-definite program

(5)

\begin{split}\min&\text{ }\mu_{L}\\
\text{such that }&F^{*}\tilde{Y}F=\mu_{L}-\beta\\
&\tilde{Y}\succeq 0\end{split}

From the output of this we are able to guess the relations that hold at optimality. Concretely, this is done by performing Gaussian elimination on the matrix consisting of the eigenvectors of
\tilde{Y}
associated with nonzero eigenvalues. We then use these relations as in our polynomial basis
F
. Then we solve the following feasibility problem

(6)

\begin{split}\min&\text{ }0\\
\text{such that }&F^{*}\tilde{Y}F=\mu_{L}-\beta\\
&\tilde{Y}-\varepsilon I\succeq 0\end{split}

for a small
\varepsilon>0
where
\mu_{L}
is fixed as the output of (
5
). The output of (
6
) we then use as our matrix
Y
in (2) above.

In the case of Feige’s game, in the standard NPA approach, using level
1+AB
suffices to determine that
\frac{9}{16}
is an upper bound on
\omega(G_{F})
. However, in this work, in order to achieve the desired algebraic relations, we take
L
to be
1+AB+A^{2}+A^{3}
and
F
as specified in
Appendix

A
. Moreover we are also able to round the numerical output to an exact solution,
Y
, with rational entries satisfying

We have stored this exact solution in a Mathematica notebook in a GitHub repository
SoS
. Running the notebook verifies the claims made in this paper.

3.3.
The non-signalling value of Feige’s game

We have seen that the classical value of Feige’s game is
\frac{1}{2}
, and we can exceed this value with a quantum strategy that wins with probability
\frac{9}{16}
. In this section, we will study the non-signalling value of
G_{F}
and prove
\omega_{ns}(G_{F})=\frac{2}{3}
. Recall the definition of non-signalling strategies from
2.4
.

We start with the description of a non-signalling strategy that achieves the value
\frac{2}{3}
. Consider the non-signalling strategy
p
, where

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{3}\quad\text{ if }(a,b)=(\perp,x)\text{ or }(a,b)=(y,\perp),\\
\frac{1}{3}\quad\text{ if }a\neq y,b\neq x\text{ and }a,b\in\{0,1\},\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We see that
p
is a probability distribution and it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{a}p(a,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle=p(y,\perp|x,y)=\frac{1}{3},

\displaystyle\sum_{a}p(a,x|x,y)

\displaystyle=p(\perp,x|x,y)=\frac{1}{3},

\displaystyle\sum_{a}p(a,\overline{x}|x,y)

\displaystyle=p(\overline{y},\overline{x}|x,y)=\frac{1}{3},

where
\overline{x}
is the complement of the bit
x
. This shows
\sum_{a}p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{a}p(a,b|x^{\prime},y)
and similarly
\sum_{b}p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{b}p(a,b|x,y^{\prime})
holds. We conclude that
p
is a non-signalling strategy. We obtain

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F},p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(y,\perp|x,y)=\frac{2}{3},

since
p(\perp,x|x,y)=p(y,\perp|x,y)=\frac{1}{3}
.

For the upper bound of the winning probability of non-signalling strategies, consider any non-signalling strategy
p
for
G_{F}
. We first note that

\displaystyle p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(\perp,\overline{x}|\overline{x},y)+p(y,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}p(a,x|x,y)+p(a,\overline{x}|\overline{x},y)+p(a,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}p(a,x|x,y)+p(a,\overline{x}|x,y)+p(a,\perp|x,y)

(7)

\displaystyle=1,

by the non-signalling property of
p
and since it is a probability distribution. Similary, it holds

(8)

\displaystyle p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(\overline{y},\perp|x,\overline{y})+p(y,\perp|x,y)\leq 1.

We compute

\displaystyle 12\omega(G_{F},p)

\displaystyle=3\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(y,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(y,\perp|x,y)+\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,\overline{x}|\overline{x},y)+p(\overline{y},\perp|x,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(\perp,\overline{x}|\overline{x},y)+p(y,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}p(\perp,x|x,y)+p(\overline{y},\perp|x,\overline{y})+p(y,\perp|x,y)

\displaystyle\leq 8,

by rearranging terms and using
Sections

3.3
and
8
for the inequality. This yields
\omega(G_{F},p)\leq\frac{2}{3}
. Summarizing, we obtain
\omega_{ns}(G_{F})=\frac{2}{3}
.

4.
Feige’s game is a self-test

The goal of this section is to show that

given in

is in fact the unique optimal quantum strategy of

in the sense made precise in the following definition.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a nonlocal game, and let

be an optimal quantum strategy. We say that

robustly self-tests
\tilde{S}
if there exists a function

\delta:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\qquad\delta(\epsilon)\rightarrow 0\text{ as }\epsilon\rightarrow 0,

such that for any
\epsilon\geq 0
and any strategy

S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\{P^{x}_{a}\},\{Q^{y}_{b}\}),

with
\omega(G,S)=\omega_{q}(G)-\epsilon
,
there exist isometries

V_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A}\text{, }V_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{B}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}

and a unit vector
\mathinner{|\kappa\rangle}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
such that

(9)

\lVert V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\left(P^{x}_{a}\otimes Q^{y}_{b}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\right)-\left(\tilde{P}^{x}_{a}\otimes\tilde{Q}^{y}_{b}\mathinner{|\tilde{\psi}\rangle}\right)\otimes\mathinner{|\kappa\rangle}\rVert\leq\delta(\epsilon)

for all
x,y,a,b
.

Recall the following definition from
(
Zha 24
, Definitions 6.1 and 7.1)
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma=\{\gamma^{y}_{b}:b\in B,y\in Y\}
and
\mathcal{R}
be two sets of

-polynomials in
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}

is self-adjoint. We say

is a

if the following conditions hold.

(1)

For every

is a PVM in the quotient
\mathscr{A}_{PVM}^{X,A}/\langle\mathcal{R}\rangle

(2)

A strategy

5.

(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
, respectively, where
x_{i},y_{i}
are questions in
G_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Each of them chooses one of the questions they received and responds with an answer from the corresponding game. To win the game, two conditions have to be fulfilled:

(1)

Alice and Bob have to give answers from the same game,

(2)

their answers have to win the corresponding game.

More formally, suppose the nonlocal games
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have input sets
X_{i}
,
Y_{i}
, output sets
A_{i}
,
B_{i}

\pi_{i}

X_{i}\times Y_{i}

. Then the
(G_{1}\lor G_{2})
-game has input sets
X_{1}\times X_{2}
,
Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
, output sets
A_{1}\dot{\cup}A_{2}
,
B_{1}\dot{\cup}B_{2}
and verification function

\displaystyle V((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}),a,b)=\begin{cases}V_{G_{i}}(x_{i},y_{i},a,b)\,\text{ if }a\in A_{i}\text{ and }b\in B_{i}\text{ for some $i=1,2$,}\\
0\,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For the probability distribution, we take
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}
on
(X_{1}\times X_{2})\times(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
,
i.e.

\pi((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))=\pi_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\pi_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
. In this article, we assume that the probability distributions of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are uniform, so this will also be the case for the

-game.

We will now show that there exist
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that
G_{F}=G_{1}\lor G_{2}
, where
G_{F}
denotes the Feige game. We will see
\frac{9}{16}=\omega_{q}(G_{F})>\mathrm{max}\{\omega_{q}(G_{1}),\omega_{q}(G_{2})\}=\frac{1}{2}
. This is in contrast the case of perfect strategies
(
MS 23
, Lemma 4.3)
, where
\omega_{q}(G_{1}\lor G_{2})=1
implies
\omega_{q}(G_{i})=1
if the players give answers for
G_{i}
a perfect strategy of the ”or”-game.

Consider the games
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, where

(12)

\displaystyle X_{1}=\{*\},\quad Y_{1}=\{0,1\},\quad A_{1}=\{0,1\},\quad B_{1}=\{\perp\},\quad\pi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}

(13)

\displaystyle X_{2}=\{0,1\},\quad Y_{2}=\{*\},\quad A_{2}=\{\perp\},\quad B_{2}=\{0,1\},\quad\pi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2},

(14)

\displaystyle V_{1}(a,\perp,*,y)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }a=y\\
0\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}V_{2}(\perp,b,x,*)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }b=x\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

In the game

, Alice guesses the bit that Bob received and their roles are reversed in the game

.

Lemma 5.1
.

It holds
\omega_{q}(G_{i})=\frac{1}{2}
for

.

Proof.

We prove it for
G_{1}
, the proof for

is the same. Note that it holds
B^{y}_{\perp}=\mathrm{Id}
for each Bob POVM in any quantum strategy

, since he is only allowed to answer with

is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,G_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}A^{*}_{0}\otimes B^{0}_{\perp}+A^{*}_{1}\otimes B^{1}_{\perp}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}(A^{*}_{0}+A^{*}_{1})\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\frac{1}{2},

∎

Theorem 5.2
.

It holds

. We therefore have

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(G_{F})=\frac{9}{16}>\frac{1}{2}=\mathrm{max}\{\omega_{q}(G_{1}),\omega_{q}(G_{2})\}.

From

\displaystyle=\{(*,0),(*,1)\},

6.
Feige’s game and parallel repetition

In this section, we look at parallel repetition of Feige’s game. From
Table

1
, we see that Feige’s game has interesting behaviour with respect to parallel repetition. In the table, the classical, quantum and non-signalling values agree in the case of an even number of repetitions, but this is not the case for odd numbers. As we saw previously, all values are different for the original game. Furthermore, we get that at least the classical and non-signalling value are different for
n=3
, despite this not being the case for
n=2
. We do not know if there is quantum advantage for
n=3
. Note that the quantum strategy consisting of the optimal quantum strategy for one game and the optimal classical strategy for the
2
-fold parallel repetition performs worse than the optimal classical strategy for
n=3
, since
\frac{9}{16}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{9}{32}<\frac{5}{16}
.

We first recall the definition of parallel repetition. In the following, we define the product of two games, which then leads to the definition of the parallel repetition of a game.

Definition 6.1
.

Suppose

\begin{split}G_{1}=(X_{1},Y_{1},A_{1},B_{1},\pi_{1},V_{1}),\quad G_{2}=(X_{2},Y_{2},A_{2},B_{2},\pi_{2},V_{2})\end{split}

are nonlocal games. We define their
product
as the game

G_{1}\times G_{2}=\big(X_{1}\times X_{2},Y_{1}\times Y_{2},A_{1}\times A_{2},B_{1}\times B_{2},\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2},V_{1}\times V_{2}\big)

Where Alice and Bob receive pairs of questions
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
and
(y_{1},y_{2})\in Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
respectively, and provide answers
(a_{1},a_{2})\in A_{1}\times A_{2}
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in B_{1}\times B_{2}
respectively. The prior distribution is given by

\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\left((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\right):=\pi_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\pi_{2}(x_{2},y_{2}).

and
V_{1}\times V_{2}
likewise

V_{1}\times V_{2}\Big((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})|(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\Big)=V_{1}(a_{1},b_{1}|x_{1},y_{1})V_{2}(a_{2},b_{2}|x_{2},y_{2})

In words, Alice and Bob play the two games independently in parallel and they win if and only if they win both of them simultaneously.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
G=(X,Y,A,B,\pi,V)
be a nonlocal game. The game
G^{\times n}
is called the
n
-fold parallel repetition of
G
.

6.1.
Classical strategies for the
2m
-fold parallel repetition

The following strategy was already described by Feige
Fei 91
for the game
G_{F}^{\times 2}
. Receiving question
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
, Alice and Bob answer

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\perp,

\displaystyle b_{1}=y_{2},

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1},

\displaystyle b_{2}=\perp,

respectively. We see that the players win if and only if
x_{1}=y_{2}
, which happens with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. This may be surprising, since the classical winning probability of the
2
-fold parallel repetition does not decrease when compared with the original game
G_{F}
.

For the
2m
-fold parallel repetition
G_{F}^{\times 2m}
, we obtain a classical strategy with winning probability
\frac{1}{2^{m}}=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}
by repeating the strategy above
m
times. More precisely, given
(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{2m})
, the players answer
(a_{1},\dots,a_{2m})
and
(b_{1},\dots,b_{2m})
with

\displaystyle a_{2i-1}

\displaystyle=\perp,

\displaystyle b_{2i-1}=y_{2i},

\displaystyle a_{2i}

\displaystyle=x_{2i-1},

\displaystyle b_{2i}=\perp,

for
1\leq i\leq m
. We will see in the next section, that this probability coincides with the upper bound on the non-signalling value of

.

6.2.
The non-signalling upper bound for
2m
-fold parallel repetition

Let
n=2m
. Consider a set
I\subseteq[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and define

for each
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
by

\displaystyle(a_{I}(\mathbf{y}))_{i}=\begin{cases}\perp,i\in I\\
y_{i},i\notin I\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad(b_{I}(\mathbf{x}))_{i}=\begin{cases}\perp,i\notin I\\
x_{i},i\in I.\end{cases}

Then, we can write

\displaystyle\omega(G_{F}^{\times n},p)=\frac{1}{2^{4m}}\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}p(a_{I}(\mathbf{y}),b_{I}(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

7.
Feige’s game as synchronous game

A nonlocal game is called
synchronous
if the question and answer sets of the players coincide, and additionally they have to return the same answer if they receive the same question in order to win. More formally, it holds
X=Y
,
A=B
and
V(a,b\,|\,x,x)=0
for
a\neq b
. There is the following synchronous version of the Feige game, which we denote by
G_{F}^{s}
:

\displaystyle X=Y=\{0,1\},\quad A=B=\{A0,A1,B0,B1\},\quad\pi(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{4}

\displaystyle V(a,b,x,y)=\begin{cases}1\quad(a,b)=(Ax,Ax)\text{ or }(a,b)=(By,By)\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that Alice should never answer
A1
, when she receives
0
, since the players always lose in this case. The same is true for Bob, which shows that to achieve maximum winning probability, the players will restrict to three answers as in the original Feige game. The difference is that
G_{F}^{s}
is a synchronous game, whereas
G_{F}
is not synchronous.

We define a strategy
S
to be
synchronous
if
p(a,b\,|\,x,x)=0
for
a\neq b
, that is, the players never give different answers when receiving the same question. We can now define the
synchronous value

\omega_{q}^{s}(G)
of a nonlocal game
G
to be the supremum over all synchronous quantum strategies, i.e.

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{s}(G)=\mathrm{sup}\{\omega(S,G)\,|\,S\text{ synchronous quantum strategy}\}.

The goal of this section is to prove that

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{s}(G_{F}^{s})=\frac{1}{2}<\frac{9}{16}=\omega_{q}(G_{F}^{s}).

Therefore, we have a synchronous game for which the synchronous value is equal to the classical value, but the game has quantum advantage. Such games are known from
HMN+ 24
, where they considered certain graph coloring games. Note that
\omega_{q}(G_{F}^{s})\geq\frac{9}{16}
by using the optimal quantum strategy for the Feige game for the synchronous version, and
\omega_{q}(G_{F}^{s})\leq\frac{9}{16}
follows from the NPA hierarchy at level
1+AB
, in the same way as for
G_{F}
. In the following, we will show
\omega_{q}^{s}(G_{F}^{s})=\frac{1}{2}
. Recall the following lemma from
(
MPS 24
, Corollary 3.6 a))
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle},\{P^{x}_{a}\},\{Q^{y}_{b}\})
be a synchronous quantum strategy. Then

\displaystyle P^{x}_{a}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\mathrm{Id}\otimes Q^{x}_{a}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}

for any
x,a
.

With this we can prove our next result.

Proposition 7.2
.

It holds
\omega_{q}^{s}(G_{F}^{s})=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Consider a synchronous quantum strategy
S=(\mathinner{|\psi\rangle},\{P^{x}_{a}\},\{Q^{y}_{b}\})
for
G_{F}^{s}
. The winning probability of
S
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,G_{F}^{s})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}P^{x}_{Ax}\otimes Q^{y}_{Ax}+P^{x}_{By}\otimes Q^{y}_{By}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}.

Since
\mathrm{Id}-P^{x}_{Ax}
and
\mathrm{Id}-Q^{y}_{By}
are positive operators, we get
\mathinner{\langle\psi|}P^{x}_{Ax}\otimes Q^{y}_{Ax}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\leq\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\mathrm{Id}\otimes Q^{y}_{Ax}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}
and
\mathinner{\langle\psi|}P^{x}_{By}\otimes Q^{y}_{By}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}\leq\mathinner{\langle\psi|}P^{x}_{By}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,G_{F}^{s})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}\mathrm{Id}\otimes Q^{y}_{Ax}+P^{x}_{By}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}(P^{y}_{Ax}+P^{x}_{By})\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle},

where we used
7.1
with
Q^{y}_{Ax}
in the second step. We conclude

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(S,G_{F}^{s})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathinner{\langle\psi|}(P^{0}_{A0}+P^{0}_{B0}+P^{0}_{A1}+P^{0}_{B1})+(P^{1}_{A0}+P^{1}_{B0}+P^{1}_{A1}+P^{1}_{B1})\otimes\mathrm{Id}\mathinner{|\psi\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}.

∎

This implies that either all projections are finite rank and

for all

or none of them are.
Notice that for finite dimensional representations we immediately have that the dimension is a multiple of

which implies

and

Q_{b}P_{a}Q_{b}=\sum_{ij}X_{ia}^{(b)}X_{aj}^{(b)}\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|=\sum_{ij}\nu_{ab}X_{ij}^{(b)}\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|

which implies

(29)

We will use (
28
)-(
29
) several times in the following. We claim now that the operator,

UU^{*}=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}\sum_{j}\tfrac{1}{\nu_{1j}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{j1}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}\sum_{j}X_{11}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|=\sum_{j}I_{H^{\prime}}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|=I_{H}

and similarly

.
Then we have

UP_{a}U^{*}=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}\tfrac{1}{\nu_{1a}}X_{1a}^{(1)}X_{a1}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}X_{11}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|=P_{a}

and

In particular we find

\begin{split}Q_{1}^{\prime}=&\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}\sum_{jk}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\nu_{1j}\nu_{1k}}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{jk}^{(1)}X_{k1}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle k|=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}}\sum_{jk}\tfrac{\sqrt{\nu_{1j}}}{\sqrt{\nu_{1k}}}X_{1k}^{(1)}X_{k1}^{(1)}\otimes|j\rangle\langle k|\\

where
\mathinner{|v\rangle}:=\sum_{j}\sqrt{\nu_{1j}}\mathinner{|j\rangle}
is a unit vector.
Now we characterize the remaining
Q_{b}^{\prime}
. Let

Y_{jk}^{(b)}=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}\sqrt{\nu_{1j}\nu_{1k}}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{jk}^{(b)}X_{k1}^{(1)}

and notice that

(30)

\begin{split}Y_{ja}^{(b)}Y_{ak}^{(b)}=&\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}^{2}\nu_{1a}\sqrt{\nu_{1j}\nu_{1k}}}(X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{ja}^{(b)}X_{a1}^{(1)})(X_{1a}^{(1)}X_{ak}^{(b)}X_{k1}^{(1)})=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{11}\sqrt{\nu_{1j}\nu_{1k}}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{ja}^{(b)}X_{ak}^{(b)}X_{k1}^{(1)}\\

where

Moreover, we have

\begin{split}V_{j}^{(b)}(V_{j}^{(b)})^{*}=&\tfrac{1}{\nu_{1b}\nu_{1j}\nu_{11}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{j1}^{(b)}X_{1j}^{(b)}X_{j1}^{(1)}=\tfrac{1}{\nu_{1j}\nu_{11}}X_{1j}^{(1)}X_{jj}^{(b)}X_{j1}^{(1)}\\

\begin{split}Q_{b}^{\prime}Q_{b^{\prime}}^{\prime}&=\sum_{jk^{\prime}}V_{j}^{(b)}\left[\sum_{k}(V_{k}^{(b)})^{*}V_{k}^{(b^{\prime})}\right](V_{k^{\prime}}^{(b^{\prime})})^{*}\otimes|j\rangle\langle k^{\prime}|\\

&=\sqrt{\nu_{1b}\nu_{1b^{\prime}}}\left[\sum_{k}(V_{k}^{(b)})^{*}V_{k}^{(b^{\prime})}\right]\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|+...\\
&=0\end{split}

which implies

\sum_{k}(V_{k}^{(b)})^{*}V_{k}^{(b^{\prime})}=0,\quad\forall b\neq b^{\prime}

Completeness gives us

\begin{split}I_{H}=\sum_{jk}\left[\sum_{b}V_{j}^{(b)}(V_{k}^{(b)})^{*}\right]\otimes|j\rangle\langle k|=\sum_{j}I_{H^{\prime}}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|\end{split}

and hence

\sum_{b}V_{j}^{(b)}(V_{k}^{(b)})^{*}=\delta_{jk}I_{H^{\prime}}

Definition B.5

(Equivalence of
\nu
-biased measurements,
\sim
)
.

Let

(\{\mathinner{|\varphi_{i}^{(1)}\rangle}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\mathinner{|\psi_{j}^{(1)}\rangle}\}_{j=1}^{n})\text{ and }(\{\mathinner{|\varphi_{i}^{(2)}\rangle}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\mathinner{|\psi_{j}^{(2)}\rangle}\}_{j=1}^{n})

Then these pairs of

We also need the following definition.

Definition B.6

(Equivalence of unitaries,
\approx
)
.

Two unitary matrices

and
U_{2}
are equivalent, written
U_{1}\approx U_{2}
if and only if there exist permutation matrices
P,P^{\prime}
and diagonal unitaries

U_{2}=PDU_{1}D^{\prime}P^{\prime}.

This equivalence reflects the fact that permutation of basis elements as well as multiplication with phase factors leave the basis in question essentially unchanged. Now, it is clear that for any pair of
\nu
-biased bases

(M,N)\sim(I,M^{*}N)

It is also clear that since

\nu

where

Lemma B.7
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}>0
be strictly positive. If it holds
\sum_{k\neq l}a_{k}=a_{l}
for some
1\leq l\leq n
, then the equation

(31)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}e^{2\pi i\phi_{j}}a_{j}+a_{n}=0

has a unique solution for
\phi_{j}\in[0,1)
,
1\leq j\leq n-1

Proof.

Assume
\sum_{k\neq l}a_{k}=a_{l}
holds for some
l
. Multiplying
Equation

31

e^{-2\pi i\phi_{l}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq l}e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})}a_{j}+a_{l}=0,

where we
\phi_{n}=0
.
From
\sum_{k\neq l}a_{k}=a_{l}
, we get

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{j\neq l}e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})}a_{j}+a_{l}=\sum_{j\neq l}e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})}a_{j}+\sum_{j\neq l}a_{j}=\sum_{j\neq l}(e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})}+1)a_{j}.

Since
a_{j}>0
, we obtain
\mathrm{Re}(e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})})=-1
, which yields
e^{2\pi i(\phi_{j}-\phi_{l})}=-1
. Especially, we have

. This shows

and we deduce

∎
