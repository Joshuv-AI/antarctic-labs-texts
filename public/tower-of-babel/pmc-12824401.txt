Connecting algorithmic fairness and fair outcomes in a sociotechnical simulation case study of AI-assisted healthcare
Authors: Emma A M Stanley, Roger Y Tsang, Haley Gillett, Raissa Souza, Vibujithan Vigneshwaran, Chris Kang, Melissa D McCradden, Matthias Wilms, Nils D Forkert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) has vast potential for improving healthcare delivery, but concerns regarding biases in these systems have raised important questions regarding fairness when deployed clinically. Most prior studies on fairness in clinical AI focus solely on performance disparities between subpopulations, which often fall short of connecting the technical outputs of AI systems with sociotechnical outcomes. In this work, we present a simulation-based approach to explore how statistical definitions of algorithmic fairness translate to fairness in long-term outcomes, using AI-assisted breast cancer screening as a case example. We evaluate four fairness criteria and their impact on mortality rates and socioeconomic disparities, while also considering how clinical decision makers’ reliance on AI and patients’ access to healthcare affect outcomes. Our results highlight how algorithmic fairness does not directly translate into fair and equitable outcomes, underscoring the importance of integrating sociotechnical perspectives to gain a holistic understanding of fairness in healthcare AI.Subject terms: Medical ethics, Biomedical engineering, Ethics, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Widespread research and development of artificial intelligence (AI) systems in clinical settings has raised important questions regarding their implications on real-world fairness and health equity. Numerous prior studies to date have reported performance disparities between sociodemographic subgroups in a variety of use cases of AI systems developed for clinical decision support and medical image analysis1–6. Such systematic performance disparities between subpopulations on the basis of sex, gender, age, race, or socioeconomic status in clinical AI systems may pose a critical risk of introducing or further entrenching inequities in healthcare. Consequently, extensive research has focused on developing and evaluating bias mitigation methods7–15. These technical interventions into AI systems primarily aim to achieve mathematical formalizations of fairness (hereafter: “algorithmic fairness”), such as equal error rates across subgroups16. In many such studies, it is implied that after resolving performance disparities, the resulting AI systems will be fair. However, this framing focuses solely on the technical aspect of AI development, largely neglecting the reality of AI as a component of a sociotechnical system.

Sociotechnical systems are those that intrinsically contain relationships between technology, humans, and societal institutions17. AI systems designed for use in healthcare exemplify this concept particularly well — these technological systems are intended to be used by healthcare practitioners to assist in clinical decision-making, and the results of those decisions will have direct and tangible impacts on the lives of patients. Furthermore, the use of clinical AI can shape future interactions within the healthcare system, for example, by impacting human behaviors and diagnostic outcomes (which may also be captured in ensuing datasets used in the development of other AI systems). Selbst et al.17 describe that when failing to consider this broader sociotechnical scope, AI researchers pursuing algorithmic fairness lack the information necessary to understand how technical interventions translate to fair outcomes within the context of a deployed AI system. Moreover, methods for defining and achieving algorithmic fairness in the medical domain have largely been borrowed from AI research applications such as finance or college admissions18. Thus, applying these methods to clinical scenarios often fails to consider important nuances. For example, algorithmic fairness does not account for the complex relationships between biological factors, environmental variables, and social determinants of health in the manifestation of medical conditions, and overlooks the intrinsic heterogeneity within patient populations defined by broad subgroups such as race and gender19. The “leveling down” effect20 on subgroup performance that is often a result of certain bias mitigation strategies also may pose a salient ethical dilemma when considering the principle of non-maleficence in medical care21. In addition, the emphasis on neutrality that is inherent to algorithmic fairness (e.g., equal performance between subgroups) fails to consider existing inequities faced by underprivileged groups — an algorithmically fair AI system will not be effective in mitigating health inequity if a subpopulation experiences barriers to accessing adequate healthcare in the first place22.

McCradden et al.23 emphasized that while algorithmic fairness is an important aspect of responsible and ethical use of AI in healthcare, only satisfying technical definitions of fairness is generally not enough to ensure that patients will experience fair treatment and outcomes. However, investigating outcomes-based measures of fairness is a complex task that requires more than just quantifying subgroup performance. Studies that have attempted to explore and quantify outcome-based fairness have typically done so in simulation-based settings using dynamic models, since a temporal aspect is required to gain an understanding of long-term outcomes and possible feedback loops within a sociotechnical system. For instance, D’Amour et al.24 simulated long-term fairness outcomes in various non-healthcare scenarios that utilize algorithmic systems. They noted that a dynamic analysis is crucial for gaining insight into how algorithmic outcomes may impact future interactions within a sociotechnical system (for instance, algorithmic decisions in bank lending can affect credit scores, which can in turn impact future loan approvals). This study, along with several others25–27, framed their analyses around a single stage in which algorithmic decisions are made. In contrast, Scher et al.28 simulated various stages within a sociotechnical system using a compartmental modeling approach, which enabled them to investigate the impact that each component had on long-term fairness outcomes.

However, none of these prior simulation-based works have focused on medical applications of AI. Within this context, mammography screening for breast cancer is a relevant and interesting use case for studying the potential long-term fairness impacts of healthcare AI systems for a number of reasons. First, research and development of AI-based mammographic analysis systems have surged in recent years, with over 20 applications that have already been approved or cleared by the United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration (FDA)29. Second, however, is that these systems were rarely evaluated for subgroup-specific performance prior to FDA clearance30,31 (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the FDA-cleared Profound AI system was retrospectively found to exhibit false positive rate disparities across different categories of age and self-reported race32. This emphasizes that, although considerable progress has gone toward the development of recommendations for standards that ensure safe and ethical deployment of medical AI systems33–35, the current regulatory landscape makes it challenging to know if and how algorithmic bias could manifest during clinical deployment of these systems36. Finally, it is well established that considerable sociodemographic disparities exist in breast cancer screening, treatment, and mortality in the U.S.37–41 Taken together, these factors make breast cancer screening a compelling case study for an initial investigation into how algorithmic fairness in AI-assisted clinical decision-making systems could impact long-term health equity dynamics.

In this work, we develop an innovative sociotechnical system-inspired simulation and use it to investigate the relationship between algorithmic fairness and the fairness of long-term outcomes in a healthcare setting. Specifically, we propose a compartmental model to simulate the case example of AI-assisted breast cancer screening and its surrounding sociotechnical context. In contrast to studies that aim to accurately model real-world sociodemographic populations to simulate clinical outcomes, this work aims to gain a high-level understanding of how algorithmic fairness may interact with healthcare system components and existing disparities. To this end, we model a simplified version of the real-world breast cancer screening and care process in the U.S., which is not intended to exhaustively represent all nuanced aspects of disease pathology, medical institutions, socioeconomic factors, or treatment. Rather, we use clinically informed design choices to model relevant abstractions of different stages and patient characteristics. Importantly, we do not aim to explicitly represent any real-world sociodemographic groups in this work. Instead, we model two hypothetical groups, with “privileged” and “unprivileged” as pseudo-demographic labels, for which we draw assumptions largely from information gathered on socioeconomic disparities in breast cancer treatment, screening, and mortality in the U.S. Modeling these abstract representations of subpopulations which face differential barriers and degrees of access to healthcare facilitates our primary goal of exploring how technical definitions of fairness influence clinical populations in a society where challenges to health equity exist. In summary, we present a dynamic model to simulate and analyze long-term impacts of algorithmic fairness in a clinical AI system. We utilize our sociotechnical system-inspired simulation to investigate the extent to which mathematical formalizations of fairness in an AI-based computer-aided diagnosis system can translate to fair health outcomes.

Results

Overview

We developed a discrete-time compartmental model to simulate a population of women undergoing annual mammography screening for the detection of breast cancer as illustrated in Fig. 1. Within this population, we modeled two hypothetical subgroups, with the “privileged” group having a higher average socioeconomic index than the “unprivileged group”. The socioeconomic index of an individual in the population was a single value that represented an aggregate measure of factors related to financial accessibility, insurance status, healthcare policy impacts, income level, and access to healthcare-related resources. This value determined the probability of experiencing barriers to accessing care within the breast cancer screening and subsequent treatment process, including the opportunity to get screened, the likelihood of attending diagnostic follow-up appointments, the chance of experiencing treatment delays, and the ability to receive adjuvant treatment. Breast cancer screening was modeled as a double-reader process (radiologist and AI), with the radiologist rendering the final decision, influenced by how much importance was given to the AI decision compared to their own. Here, we assumed the radiologist to be “unbiased”, i.e., their diagnostic performance was equal between the privileged and unprivileged groups.

Schematic representing the breast cancer screening and treatment process used in this simulation study.

Each year (one time step), individuals in the population pool have the opportunity to undergo AI-assisted mammography screening for breast cancer, with those screened negative returning to the population pool and those screened positive going on to a stage simulating diagnostic confirmation with additional imaging and biopsy. Individuals initially determined to be false positives (after biopsy confirmation) are then returned to the population pool, while those initially screened as true positives and confirmed by biopsy proceed to receive treatment. Following treatment, individuals are either deemed to be cured or deceased. At the end of each year, breast cancer mortality is also computed for individuals who remained in the pool. Components in blue represent stages where the unprivileged group experiences disproportionate barriers to accessing care as a result of their lower socioeconomic status on average.

For the AI system, four different statistical definitions of algorithmic fairness were explored: (1) “Naive”, with disparities in both true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) between the privileged and unprivileged groups, (2) “equal opportunity” with TPR parity and disparity in FPR (EqOppTPR), (3) “equal opportunity” with FPR parity and disparity in TPR (EqOppFPR), and (4) “equalized odds” with both TPR and FPR parity (EqOdds). More specifically, in scenarios with TPR and/or FPR disparities, the privileged group had a higher TPR and/or FPR, respectively, compared to the unprivileged group. It was assumed that imposing error rate parity was achieved through “fair representation learning” methods, which resulted in increased performance for the initially lower-performing group (e.g., TPR increased, FPR decreased) and decreased performance for the initially higher-performing group (e.g., TPR decreased, FPR increased).

Individuals who received a breast cancer-negative imaging-based diagnosis returned to the population pool, while those who received an imaging-based positive diagnosis continued to the diagnostic confirmation stage, which represented follow-up imaging and biopsy analysis. If it was determined that a simulated individual received a false positive diagnosis from mammography screening, they returned to the population pool with a reduced probability of attending their screening appointment in the following year. All individuals with a true positive diagnosis went on to receive treatment, which consisted of a two-stage process, simulating surgical and adjuvant interventions. Undergoing treatment incurred a financial burden, which was modeled by a reduction in socioeconomic index; this financial burden was greater for the unprivileged group. It was assumed that all stages, from screening to treatment, were completed within one time step, assumed to be one year. Individuals were presumed deceased if the severity of their breast cancer exceeded a predefined threshold at the end of a given time step. Group-specific mortality rate and socioeconomic outcomes were computed at the end of each time step.

Using this simulation, we: (1) compared how each algorithmic fairness scenario impacted group-wise mortality and socioeconomic disparities over time, and (2) assessed how these outcomes were impacted by (a) various performance disparity ranges in the AI system, (b) the radiologist’s role in diagnostic decision-making, and (c) non-algorithmic interventions within the system. Modeling details for each stage illustrated in Fig. 1 are presented in the Methods section, along with justification for corresponding parameters.

Comparison of outcome disparities over time for different algorithmic fairness constraints

The simulation was run for 50 time steps (years) for each of the four algorithmic fairness scenarios (see Methods, Table 2), where it was assumed that the radiologist gave equal importance to their own diagnostic decision and the decision of the AI system, such that the final diagnosis had equal contribution from the two “readers”. Disparities in breast cancer mortality rates and average socioeconomic index between the privileged and unprivileged groups over time are visualized in Fig. 2. We observed stark differences in annual mortality rates between the privileged and unprivileged groups, regardless of the algorithmic fairness constraints imposed. The Naive and EqOppFPR scenarios led to very similar outcomes and likewise, the EqOppTPR and EqOdds scenarios also led to similar results (Fig. 2A). Specifically, the average mortality rate ratio for the EqOppFPR scenario was 3.82 (95% CI: 3.80, 3.83) compared to 3.77 (95% CI:3.76, 3.70) in the Naive scenario (Fig. 2B). This was reduced to 3.05 (95% CI:3.04, 3.06) and 3.07 (95% CI:3.06, 3.08) under the EqOppTPR and EqOdds algorithmic fairness scenarios, respectively. Comparing the Naive and EqOdds scenarios, this amounted to an absolute reduction in mortality rate disparity of 12.22 (95% CI:11.91,12.53) per 100k individuals. Although the reduced disparity in mortality rates implies a positive health equity outcome, it is notable that in the EqOppTPR and EqOdds scenarios, the privileged group's mortality rate increased (Fig. 2A). This was a result of the assumed “leveling down” effect that meant reduced sensitivity for the privileged group to meet algorithmic fairness targets. With respect to the average socioeconomic index of the two groups, the Naive and EqOppFPR systems reduced the inter-group disparity relative to EqOppTPR and EqOdds (Fig. 2C).

Assessment of the effect of AI system group-wise performance differences on outcomes

We also analyzed average mortality rate ratio and socioeconomic index disparities over 50 years for a range of values corresponding to AI system performance disparities between the privileged and unprivileged group. Here, higher values of ΔTPR represent reduced TPR for the unprivileged group relative to a static TPR for the privileged group, and higher values of ΔFPR represent increased FPR for the privileged group relative to a static FPR for the unprivileged group (See Methods, “Experiments”). These results are presented in Fig. 3, where it is observed that TPR disparities had a larger impact on determining outcomes compared to FPR disparities (Fig. 3A). For instance, at a given ΔFPR, lowering the TPR for the unprivileged group by 30% increased the mortality rate ratio by over 15%. This can be attributed to higher rates of false negatives (missed diagnoses) in the unprivileged group. In contrast, at a given ΔTPR, a 30% higher FPR for the privileged group led to less than a 5% reduction in the mortality rate ratio. However, this finding can be attributed to a higher mortality rate for the privileged group due to the false positives negatively impacting the likelihood of attending screening in the subsequent year. Regarding socioeconomic index disparities, higher values of ΔTPR reduced the inter-group socioeconomic index disparity with ΔFPR having minimal impact (Fig. 3B).

Evaluation of the impact of radiologist reliance on AI decisions

The simulation was also run for a range of values corresponding to how much weight, or “importance” the radiologist gave to the AI system when making a breast cancer diagnosis. More specifically, an AI reliance parameter value of one in our simulation meant that the diagnosis was based entirely on the decision of the AI system, while a value of zero meant that the diagnosis was based entirely on the radiologist’s decision. The average mortality rate ratio over 50 years for this analysis is visualized in Fig. 4. Here, it can be seen that the mortality rate disparity surged with a higher degree of reliance on the AI system under both the Naive and EqOppFPR scenarios. The mortality rate disparity was unaffected by AI reliance under the EqOdds scenario, and decreased slightly with full reliance under EqOppTPR compared to zero reliance. However, as can be seen from the actual misdiagnosis rates (FPR and FNR), presented for AI reliance parameter settings of 0.0, 0.5, and 1.0 in Table 1, the EqOppTPR and EqOdds scenarios resulted in increased FPR disparities when more weight was given to the AI model decision. Thus, the mortality rate disparity decreased under EqOppTPR scenario only due to increasing FPR for privileged group, leading to fewer of this group returning for screening in following years, and therefore dying at a rate more similar to that of the unprivileged group (Supplementary Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis of non-algorithmic healthcare system interventions

The screening, treatment delay, and adjuvant treatment stages of the simulation were modeled to have the largest discrepancies between the privileged and unprivileged group, with the unprivileged group being 1.5 times less likely to get screened, twice as likely to experience delays, and half as likely to obtain adjuvant treatment (see Methods). Therefore, we compared outcomes for the different algorithmic fairness scenarios over a range of values corresponding to how likely an individual of a specific socioeconomic index was to undergo each step, which practically corresponded to a range of proportions of each group getting screened, experiencing treatment delays, or receiving adjuvant treatment. The results of this analysis are illustrated in Fig. 5. Here, it can be seen that treatment delays and access to adjuvant treatment both had minor impacts on mortality rate disparities relative to screening access. In particular, when approximately 93% of the unprivileged group was able to be screened, the mortality rate disparity in the Naive scenario was reduced to 56.88 (95% CI:56.68, 57.08) per 100k, from 105.48 (95% CI:105.26, 105.71) per 100k in the baseline setting when approximately 65% of the unprivileged group was screened (illustrated in Fig. 2A). This corresponded to an absolute reduction in mortality rate per 100k for the unprivileged group from 144.06 (95% CI:143.86, 144.27) to 95.38 (95% CI:95.21, 95.54). On the other hand, reducing the proportion of the unprivileged group experiencing treatment delays to 0.05 reduced their mortality rate per 100k to 143.67 (95% CI:143.46, 143.88) and increasing the proportion of the unprivileged group receiving adjuvant treatment to 0.95 reduced their mortality rate per 100k to 143.89 (95% CI:143.68, 144.11) (Supplementary Tables 3–5). However, higher rates of screening and adjuvant treatment in the unprivileged group also correlated with an increase in inter-group socioeconomic index disparity, as a result of treatment costs.

Discussion

Fairness of clinical AI systems is the focus of a growing body of research, which primarily aims to ensure that algorithmic systems do not unjustly discriminate against patient subpopulations. However, it is not straightforward to determine how statistical definitions of algorithmic fairness translate into real-world outcomes. In this work, we took steps toward understanding the impacts of algorithmic fairness beyond the purely technical scope of AI system performance, specifically simulating mortality rate and socioeconomic disparities between a privileged and unprivileged group in a breast cancer screening setting over time.

The results of this simulation, which modeled clinically relevant abstractions of the breast cancer screening and treatment process, showed that equalizing TPR between groups was effective in reducing the mortality rate ratio of the unprivileged group to the privileged group from nearly four-fold to about three-fold. This intervention of algorithmically enforcing both groups to the same TPR was effective in reducing mortality rate disparities because it ensured that neither group had a higher rate of missed diagnoses (false negatives), although this was partially at the expense of a higher mortality rate in the privileged group (relative to the Naive scenario) due to leveled-down performance. Moreover, we observed that only equalizing FPR between groups actually led to a slightly higher mortality rate disparity compared to the Naive scenario. This can be attributed to the FPR for the unprivileged group being slightly higher under the EqOppFPR scenario, which meant that a larger proportion of this group did not attend screening in the following time step, leading to more cases of untreated breast cancer. However, the impact of enforcing FPR parity in the AI system was minimal compared to that of enforcing TPR parity. While the disproportionate impact of false negatives compared to false positives is intuitive from a clinical perspective, it points to a potential disconnect between current algorithmic fairness research and real-world health outcomes: Although FPR disparities and equalized odds are often targeted in bias mitigation studies as important measures to minimize15,43,44, their real-world impact in a healthcare setting is likely to be less dire than that of TPR disparities (which imply differential levels of missed diagnoses). While it is important to note that false positive diagnoses do have the potential to significantly impact the lives of individuals, for example through heightened stress and health anxiety45, this finding demonstrates that gaining an understanding of the sociotechnical context surrounding a clinical AI application can help to better prioritize targets and implementation strategies. For instance, tolerance of false positives may be much higher than that of false negatives in the development of a mammogram analysis system, provided that resources are available for other non-technical harm mitigation measures to be put in place (e.g., rapid follow-up for diagnostic confirmation to reduce the emotional impact of false positives).

Group-wise disparity in the average socioeconomic index of each group was also measured as an outcome. While socioeconomic status was highly simplified in this simulation, it was modeled to represent an important barrier to accessing various healthcare services. Although decreasing socioeconomic disparities appears desirable, this does not necessarily indicate the best outcomes for all groups. In particular, socioeconomic index disparity decreased under Naive and EqOppFPR scenarios relative to the others, but this was due to the fact that the higher TPR for the privileged group meant that this group was predominantly the one paying for treatment. This lowered their socioeconomic index on average, while many in the unprivileged group did not receive treatment — although their socioeconomic index was not negatively impacted, this simulated group was also dying at higher rates. This trade-off was also reflected in the sensitivity analysis, where reduced mortality disparities resulting from higher screening rates were observed at the expense of a higher socioeconomic index disparity, due to a higher assumed financial burden of breast cancer treatment for those in the unprivileged group46,47. In addition to existing economic barriers and hardships that may be faced by lower-socioeconomic status individuals (“Should I delay or forgo treatment, or go into medical debt?”), there is a risk of AI-assisted mammography analysis being associated with higher out-of-pocket costs not covered by insurance48. Thus, even if AI models are “algorithmically fair”, they may still pose a higher barrier to under-resourced populations, therefore directing benefits of these systems toward more socioeconomically privileged populations.

The analysis of the impact of the AI reliance parameter in determining mortality outcomes demonstrated the strong degree to which AI-assisted clinical decision support systems depend on the human decision maker. We observed that, assuming an “unbiased” radiologist (whose diagnostic performance was equal between groups), mortality rate disparities were highly affected by how much weight the radiologist gave to the AI decision versus their own, only when the AI system had TPR disparities. This exemplifies another way in which algorithmic fairness does not directly translate into fair outcomes, as relying exclusively on performance to determine whether an AI system is fair overlooks the role of human trust and reliance on that system in the context of shared decision-making processes. In the medical setting, automation bias (the tendency of humans to overly trust suggestions from automated systems) has been correlated with the experience level of the radiologist tasked with reading mammograms49. Going forward, this could mean that junior radiologists whose careers develop alongside the ongoing integration of AI in radiology may have a higher tendency to rely on AI decisions, even if those decisions have embedded bias. In this work, we included this radiologist reliance parameter to explore how factors external to the direct technical system can strongly influence outcomes associated with that system. However, we modeled a static level of AI reliance, which has limitations. Namely, this approach oversimplifies the relationship between human decision-makers and AI systems, where, in reality, it is likely to be more dynamic and evolving, leading to different outcomes. For instance, a radiologist may realize over time that an AI system has lower performance for specific subgroups, and consequently internally calibrate their diagnoses with this knowledge in mind3,23. On the other hand, humans could also introduce their own biases into clinical decision-making processes. Here, we model an “unbiased” radiologist, but the influence of cognitive racial and gender bias on patient care has also been widely documented50,51. While previous studies have shown that humans can be influenced by biased AI systems52,53, the degree to which AI systems could conversely temper human implicit biases has not been widely explored so far. Altogether, this demonstrates that further research into the interaction of biases between AI and human users in shared decision-making settings is warranted53.

The sensitivity analysis for parameters determining the likelihood of screening, treatment delays, and adjuvant treatment within the simulation showed that barriers to accessing appropriate healthcare had a greater impact on health disparities than AI-based interventions. In our simulation, the intervention with the most substantial impact on reducing mortality rate disparities between the two groups was ensuring that the unprivileged group had access to screening, regardless of the algorithmic fairness constraints on the AI system. Specifically, enforcing equalized odds only reduced the mortality rate disparity by approximately 12 individuals per 100k, while on the other hand, ensuring that 93% of the unprivileged group received annual screening reduced this value by approximately 57 individuals per 100k, without increasing the mortality rate for the privileged group. In practice, however, increasing access to screening, especially to the high levels simulated in our sensitivity analysis, is a highly complex task. Systemic challenges, including cultural barriers, geographical limitations, and financial accessibility, mean that there is no simple solution to achieving perfect screening uptake. As an example, in the largely privatized healthcare system of U.S., which our simulation was loosely modeled after, the Affordable Care Act introduced measures intended to reduce disparities in access to preventive screening, such as for breast cancer, by eliminating out-of-pocket costs for patients54. However, this legislation does not guarantee universal access to workup following mammography screening, meaning that disparities remain since some patients still must pay higher out-of-pocket costs for follow-up imaging and diagnostic procedures55. Thus, the interplay of health policy and medical economics plays a crucial role in ensuring that initial barriers to access — before interaction with an AI system even begins — are effective in reducing health disparities. This highlights that even if AI systems are “fair”, the role they play in impacting health equity can be largely influenced by external social, economic, and political factors.

We loosely modeled our simulation after the healthcare system in the U.S., where existing disparities in breast cancer screening access, treatment, and mortality are well documented37–41. However, even in universal healthcare systems, disparities can be prevalent, particularly in screening uptake. For instance, in Canada, factors associated with lower mammography usage also include lower socioeconomic status, geographical remoteness, ethnic minorities, and immigration status56. Studies from European countries also revealed lower rates of screening associated with area-level socioeconomic deprivation57. In a study from Taiwan, another universal healthcare system, top reasons for not getting screened included logistics, lack of family history, and discomfort58. Therefore, adapting our simulation framework to new contexts would require careful consideration of region-specific and patient-level characteristics, for example, differences in health policy environments, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural practices that shape and influence healthcare engagement. Equally important are features of the healthcare system itself, such as physician payment models (e.g., fee-for-service), referral pathways and waiting times, and the influence of public health initiatives or campaigns. Moreover, the landscape of AI governance, including the extent to which AI technologies are regulated, trusted, and integrated into clinical practice, would be a critical factor. These considerations become especially salient when extending the simulation to different medical institutions that may serve diverse populations with distinct healthcare needs and systemic constraints.

A primary limitation of this work is that various assumptions and simplifications were made, such that our simulation does not comprehensively model the real-world breast cancer screening and treatment processes. For instance, we modeled a simplified approximation of clinical decision-making based on a mortality risk factor that abstractly represented cancer severity, which did not consider specific patient-level physiological characteristics, such as tissue density, family history, or cancer subtype. We also assumed that all stages within the simulation occur within a single time step (one year), and computed population outcomes per time step. In reality, while all stages from screening to treatment could occur within one year, delays incurred can prolong this process and lead to worse outcomes59. In our simulation, we modeled the impact of treatment delays by increasing mortality risk by a factor corresponding to half of a year of disease progression, but assumed that all stages were completed in a year regardless of delays. We also assumed that individuals who were successfully treated became cancer-free (with the same likelihood of getting cancer again as the rest of the population), only women received screening, and the socioeconomic index, which was intended to encompass various complex socioeconomic-associated health risk factors, was only impacted by undergoing treatment. Importantly, the two groups we modeled were not intended to represent any particular demographic group in the U.S. or elsewhere, but were a broad abstraction of populations with differential levels of access to appropriate healthcare. The parameters we used were selected based on various literature sources (see Methods) that investigated disparities relevant to different factors, such as income level, race, and insurance status, which are subject to the limitations from those respective studies. These various assumptions and simplifications in our simulation, which do not exhaustively reflect the real world, mean that the specific numerical outcomes are unlikely to be directly applicable to data from real clinical populations. For instance, in our simulation, the absolute numbers for mortality rates are much higher than real-world values for the U.S. reported by SEER (simulation, Naive: 91.04 (95% CI:90.92, 91.16) per 100k, SEER: 19.2 per 100k42). However, we emphasize that the primary goal of this work was to compare the outcomes of algorithmic fairness scenarios relative to each other by modeling the healthcare process just well enough such that differences in outcomes between the two groups can be quantified. Therefore, we believe that this simulation did not need to capture every detail of the screening and treatment process, as we drew on clinical expertise and established literature to simplify the process into components that were pertinent to our analysis.

Additionally, the results of our simulation are closely tied to the assumption that fair representation learning methods were applied to achieve error rate parity across groups, which reduced group-specific performance. While this “leveling down” can be a side effect of methods commonly developed and applied within medical contexts60, reducing performance for a subpopulation represented in a dataset has been widely critiqued as being contradictory to the medical ethics principle of non-maleficence, which emphasizes avoiding harm to patients22,61. Our simulation further highlights this ethical conflict by demonstrating that lowering the TPR for a subgroup would practically translate to an increased mortality burden for that population. However, we assume here that the Naive model is able to faithfully capture features associated with malignant breast cancer in mammograms, when in reality, AI models trained on medical image data are susceptible to learning spurious correlations, which could result in artificially inflated performance62. Thus, leveling down may not always be inherently negative from a clinical perspective, if it means that the model is encouraged to learn generalizable and reliable representations of disease rather than exploiting dataset-specific artifacts63. Regardless, algorithmic fairness methods that aim to improve worst-group performance while keeping performance for initially higher-performing groups constant (e.g., distributionally robust optimization64, Pareto fairness65) may be a preferable strategy from a medical ethics standpoint. However, it should be highlighted that our simulation illustrated how specific algorithmic fairness interventions had a relatively small impact on outcomes relative to the absolute value of group-wise disparities that stemmed from structural barriers embedded in the healthcare system. This suggests that while algorithmic fairness methods are important for addressing statistical limitations of models, addressing systemic inequities more broadly remains central to reducing group-wise disparities in clinical outcomes.

Another limitation of this work is that we only quantified group-specific mortality rates and average socioeconomic index as outcomes. Patient-centered outcomes, such as quality-of-life, are also an important consideration in the context of health equity that should be studied within the context of outcomes-based fairness in future work. We also acknowledge that modeling this system in a simulation is still an inherently technical approach to understanding fairness in medical AI, which cannot capture important and ethically salient factors such as patient perspectives, clinician feedback, and human factors outcomes. However, we hope that by attempting to model broader aspects of the sociotechnical system in which AI-based clinical decision support systems exist, we shed further light on the importance of fairness considerations beyond just statistical definitions.

Finally, it should be emphasized that the results of this simulation work should not be used to form direct conclusions about the real-world impact of current AI-based mammographic analysis systems. Prospective studies have demonstrated improved recall in breast cancer screening when assisted by AI66,67, which can result in expedited access to care and treatment. Rather than attempting to simulate general public health impacts of AI-based breast cancer screening, the aim of this work was to compare how various algorithmic fairness scenarios impacted patient outcomes, using breast cancer screening only as an initial case example. Nevertheless, we believe that sociotechnical simulation setups like the one we developed in this study could be a valuable tool for researchers to investigate which algorithmic fairness strategies are most appropriate for a given clinical scenario, or to assist in educating clinicians on appropriate usage of AI systems.

In conclusion, this work aimed to explore the ways in which algorithmic fairness could translate to fair health outcomes, by considering interactions between an AI system, clinical users, and patient populations who face differential barriers to accessing healthcare. The results of our simulation study demonstrated how statistical definitions of fairness commonly applied to AI systems do not necessarily lead to equitable health outcomes when considering the sociotechnical context they exist in. However, AI as a technology is not unique in its potential to impact health disparities — “privileged” populations in society have historically been able to gain disproportionate benefits in health and longevity as a result of new medical technologies68. Although AI has the potential to improve access to healthcare across resource divides69, recent works have brought more attention to the potential of AI to introduce, perpetuate, or even amplify disparities in healthcare and patient outcomes3,70. Within technical communities, algorithmic fairness is often seen as a strategy to prevent or mitigate this problem. However, the results of our work exemplify how integrating algorithmic fairness into an AI system does not necessarily provide a simple solution to health equity challenges. This highlights the importance of avoiding “technological solutionism”17, which assumes that any and all problems can be solved with technological interventions like AI. Instead, AI, and algorithmic fairness specifically, should be viewed as one tool within an interdisciplinary toolbox that can complement other approaches for addressing large, complex societal challenges such as health disparities. Practical approaches for ensuring that AI improves patient outcomes and equity in healthcare rather than hindering it include translational trials71 and rigorous validation of these systems across diverse populations. Additionally, interdisciplinary research, development, and assessment throughout the entire AI life cycle can help to facilitate the implementation of holistically responsible healthcare AI72, as designing such systems with only technical goals in mind will inevitably limit applicability and responsible integration into clinical care. While algorithmic fairness has the potential to prevent AI-induced health disparities, technical approaches to fairness should be seen as only one tool among others in the pursuit of ethically and equitably integrating AI technologies into medical care.

Future screening attendance behavior has also been associated with prior false positive results. Although previous studies have shown variable effects77, a recent large-scale cohort-based study in the U.S. found that women who received a biopsy recommendation after a false positive screening result were significantly less likely to return for screening in the next 9-30 months78. Diagnosis

The diagnosis provided from the AI-assisted mammography screening is modeled as a shared decision-making process between a radiologist and an AI system. Practically, this means that the AI system and the radiologist each have a separate, independent decision function, and the output of each respective function represents the probability that a particular individual had breast cancer. These separate decision functions are then combined to represent the final AI-assisted diagnosis.

For both the radiologist and the AI system, the likelihood of an accurate breast cancer diagnosis is a function of the mortality risk factor, xR. Intuitively, more severe breast cancer (i.e., higher xR) should be easier to identify on a mammogram, while less severe cases may be more difficult to diagnose. Prior to receiving treatment, a portion of the individuals diagnosed with breast cancer are assumed to experience delays in receiving care. Numerous studies have demonstrated that racialized and lower socioeconomic status groups are more likely to experience delays in receiving breast cancer treatment41,80–82, and that delays in receiving surgical treatment are associated with increased breast cancer mortality39. This design choice arose from established literature, which showed that while the vast majority of women diagnosed with breast cancer undergo surgical treatment, there are disparities in receiving adjuvant treatments (such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or hormone therapy) between racial and socioeconomic groups40,81,83,84.

It is assumed that surgical treatment has the greatest effect in reducing mortality risk, while adjuvant treatment provides a smaller scale but nonetheless important benefit85,86. Therefore, the effect of the surgical treatment is modeled as a reduction in mortality risk score, xR, by a value of 1, while the effect of the adjuvant treatment is modeled as an additional reduction in xR by a value of 0.5.

Each treatment is also associated with a reduction in socioeconomic index, xSEI, to simulate the financial burden of breast cancer treatment, such as costs and loss of income87. The reduction in xSEI that results from treatment is modeled to be twice as much for the unprivileged group compared to the privileged group, based on the assumption that the unprivileged group would be more likely to have poorer insurance coverage, hourly wages versus yearly salary-based professions, and less of a financial safety net, resulting in a higher overall economic impact of undergoing treatment46,47.

After each individual completes their respective treatment(s), their mortality risk score xR is assessed against a threshold value xR,thresh defined here to be xR,thresh:=3. If xR exceeds xR,thresh, the individual is assumed to have died, while if xR was below xR,thresh, the individual is assumed to have been cured, and they return to the population pool as healthy (xBC = 0).

End of time step

In our simulation framework, one year corresponds to a single time step. At the end of each time step, which contains the screening, diagnosis, and treatment stages, mortality is also computed for individuals with breast cancer who remained in the population pool. These individuals either had false negative screenings or were never screened in the first place due to their socioeconomic status. It is assumed that the mortality risk scores for these individuals are increased by a value of xR = 1, representing the amount of disease progression over a single year without treatment. Then, it is assessed whether the mortality risk scores for these individuals exceeds xR,thresh. If so, they are assumed to be deceased.

Subsequently, the age of each individual is increased by one year, and those whose age exceeds 75 years, as well as those who died, are removed from the population pool. An influx of new individuals between the ages of 40−45, assumed to be newly eligible for annual breast cancer screening, is then added, such that the total population size remains at 500,000. Each year, the incidence of new breast cancer cases is computed for those in the population pool according to Eq. 3. It is assumed that every individual in the population pool has the same likelihood of getting breast cancer, regardless of whether they already had it and were successfully treated.

Algorithmic fairness scenarios

In the context of the simulation, we assumed that the “Naive” model (i.e., the model without any algorithmic fairness interventions applied to it) initially had group-wise disparities in both the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR), such that it tended to predict the positive class more often for the privileged group, resulting in higher sensitivity but lower specificity for this group relative to the unprivileged group. Three algorithmic fairness methods were simulated, corresponding to equal opportunity with TPR parity (EqOppTPR), equal opportunity with FPR parity (EqOppFPR), and equalized odds with both TPR and FPR parity (EqOdds). These criteria are generally used as algorithmic fairness constraints when groups under consideration may have different prevalence rates of positive instances16, making them a commonly used measure of algorithmic fairness in disease prediction tasks21. For the scenarios with TPR and/or FPR parity, it was assumed that the performance of the group with the more favorable TPR or FPR in the Naive model was “leveled down” to achieve equal performance between groups. This can be the case with fair representation learning/“in-processing” bias mitigation methods20,22,60, which have been commonly proposed in the literature for algorithmic fairness in healthcare setting8,11,15. Experiments

Each of the four algorithmic fairness scenarios was simulated for 50 time steps (years), with the first time step being discarded as a simulation “ramp-up” period. Parameters for the logistic functions were selected to reflect values reported in the literature of the relative differences in the rates that the privileged group would undergo screening, diagnostic follow-up, treatment delay, and adjuvant treatment compared to the unprivileged group. These corresponded to the unprivileged group being, on average, approximately 1.5 times less likely to get screened88 (as = 5, bs = −6.25), 0.95 times as likely to attend diagnostic follow-up appointments (af = 0.77, bf = −2)79, twice as likely to experience treatment delays39 (ad = 0.95, bd = −1.2), and half as likely to received adjuvant treatment40 (at = 0.45, bt = −1.8) in the Naive scenario. These parameters also reflected rates across the overall population that approximately corresponded to those reported in the literature for the various stages of the simulation (i.e., screening uptake: ≈80%89, lost to follow-up post-screening: ≈10%79, experiencing delays: ≈20%80, receiving adjuvant treatment: ≈80%40).

The baseline AI reliance parameter was set at α=0.5. The radiologist decision function, which models the likelihood of a true or false positive diagnosis given an individual’s mortality risk score, was defined according to Eqs. 15–17.

Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to assess the impact of several variables on average mortality and socioeconomic outcomes over the course of 50 in-simulation years. In each sensitivity analysis experiment, only the variable of interest was modified, with the rest of the parameters being equal to that in the baseline scenario (detailed in the previous paragraph).

In the first sensitivity analysis experiment, performance disparities in TPR and FPR between the privileged and unprivileged groups of the AI system were varied between values of 0.0 and 0.3 in increments of 0.05. Specifically, performance disparities in TPR amounted to lower TPR for the unprivileged group relative to a constant TPR for the privileged group (Eqs. 18–19).

Likewise, performance disparities for FPR amounted to increased FPR for the privileged group relative to a constant FPR for the unprivileged group (Eq. 20).

In the second sensitivity analysis experiment, the AI reliance parameter varied between no reliance (α=0) and full reliance on AI decision (α=1) in increments of 0.1. In the final sensitivity analysis experiment, the parameters defining the logistic functions for screening, treatment delay, and adjuvant treatment were also varied such that approximately 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 95% of the unprivileged group underwent screening, experienced treatment delays, and accessed adjuvant treatment. Additionally, group-specific mortality rates for a range of AI system TPR and FPR were computed, and reported in Supplementary Fig. 1 (Source data 8 file).

Statistics and reproducibility

Each experimental setup was run for 100 simulations with distinct random seeds, and 95% confidence intervals were computed for outcome metrics.
