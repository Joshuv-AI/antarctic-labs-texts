Transcriptomic evidence of remodeling in postpartum levator ani muscle associated with maternal age and recovery time after first vaginal delivery
Authors: Carolyn W Swenson, Nima Pouladi, Hannah A Zabriskie, Paul-Emile Bourrant, Jianrong Li, Liam S Wilson, Micah J Drummond, Yves A Lussier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND:Older maternal age at first vaginal delivery, defined in some studies as ≥30 years old, confers a substantial increased risk of pelvic organ prolapse. Age-related impairment of postpartum recovery of levator ani muscles may help explain this association; yet, little is known about the biomolecular changes that occur in the levator ani after vaginal delivery, or with aging.OBJECTIVE:To evaluate the transcriptional signature of the levator ani immediately after vaginal delivery and 6 to 8 weeks postpartum among young primiparas (18—25 years) and older primiparas (≥35 years).STUDY DESIGN:This study is a prospective study in which nulliparous women were recruited in the third trimester of pregnancy. Levator ani muscle biopsies were obtained immediately after vaginal delivery and at 6 to 8 weeks postpartum. Clinical measures of pelvic floor support and levator ani muscle function were obtained at the 6 to 8—week postpartum visit. Biopsies were analyzed using RNA sequencing methods and differentially expressed genes were compared with those reported in prior studies of pelvic organ prolapse patients. We used a moderated t test (false discovery rate <5%) to identify differentially expressed genes associated with postpartum delivery, maternal age, and the interaction between the 2. Principal component analysis confirmed segregation of clinical phenotype variables based on gene expression.RESULTS:Eight women (3 young and 5 older) underwent levator ani biopsies at both time points and had sufficient tissue for analyses. A total of 58 differentially expressed genes were identified when evaluating the interaction of age and recovery interval, including TP53BP1 (upregulated), PRR12 (upregulated), ZNF316 (upregulated), and PLK3 (downregulated). Between age groups, 309 differentially expressed genes were identified, while 159 were identified between time points. Both age groups were enriched for core tissue repair and homeostatic programs. Twenty of the differentially expressed genes identified in the separate analyses of age and time were previously identified in 4 case-control studies of pelvic organ prolapse. The correlation between the transcriptomic signature (principal component 3) and the overall clinical phenotype profile (principal component 1) was significant (ρ=0.88, false discovery rate <0.05), while the correlation observed with maternal age alone was not (ρ=−0.72).CONCLUSION:In agreement with clinical evidence, the effects of maternal age on levator ani muscle recovery can be seen at the transcriptional level. Age and recovery time share similar biological processes that, when disrupted, may predispose the levator ani to altered recovery and long-term susceptibility to pelvic organ prolapse. Specifically, elevated TP53BP1 combined with reduced PLK3 suggests a pattern of disrupted p53-regulated checkpoint and elevated cellular senescence which would impair healing. Results from the principal component analysis indicate that the transcriptomic signature captures clinical features beyond chronological age alone. Together, this study suggests a mechanistic pathway whereby aged levator ani may be at an increased risk of further dysfunction and disease and opens the possibility of targeted therapies to prevent pelvic organ prolapse.Keywords: inflammation, levator ani muscles, maternal age, postpartum pelvic floor recovery, senescence, vaginal delivery

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a burdensome and common disorder that results in surgery for up to 1 in 5 women.1,2 Vaginal delivery (VD) and aging are considered the 2 most significant risk factors for POP.2—4 Older maternal age at first VD has also been identified as an independent risk factor for POP suggesting a clinically meaningful interaction between the 2 risk factors.5 In a longitudinal study including more than 3700 women, those aged ≥35 years at first VD had a 3-fold increased odds of POP compared to women aged <25 years.6 Lowenstein et al found that women aged ≥30 years at first VD had over a 2-fold increased incidence of POP surgery compared to those aged <30 years.7 One potential explanation for the increased risk of POP with older maternal age at first VD could be age-related impairment of pelvic floor muscle recovery.

Skeletal muscle aging, which begins as early as age 308, is associated with increased inflammation leading to dysregulation of repair and regeneration and functional impairment.9—11 The levator ani muscles (LAMs) are the skeletal muscles responsible for maintaining normal pelvic floor function. While childbirth-induced injury to the LAM, visible on imaging, is a well-described risk factor for POP, only half of women with POP have this finding.12—14 Virtually nothing is known about molecular-level changes to the LAM which may contribute to POP risk. Previous human studies examining LAM transcriptomes have focused almost exclusively on women with established POP rather than on early postpartum changes. In a microarray study of the LAM, Visco et al15 found hundreds of genes differentially expressed in advanced POP. Substantial remodeling of the LAM was identified in POP cases suggesting a shift from robust, striated muscle fibers toward a smooth muscle—like phenotype. In other preclinical models of VD, analysis of skeletal muscle molecular events has exponentially expanded our understanding about tissue injury and regeneration.16—18 Yet, this type of muscle analysis following VD has never been performed on human LAM nor in 2 different maternal age cohorts.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to compare the transcriptional signature of LAM biopsies at 2 time points (immediately postpartum and 6—8 weeks postpartum) from 2 different age groups of women (18—25 years vs 35—45 years) after first VD. We sought to determine whether the interaction between maternal age and recovery time would identify potential molecular pathways that may provide insight into POP mechanisms. We hypothesize that unbiased functional analysis of LAM gene expression will reveal dysregulation of pathways associated with inflammation, repair, and regeneration in women who are older (35—45 years) at first VD which informs their heightened long-term susceptibility to POP.

Materials and methods

This is a prospective study aiming to assess the impact of maternal age at first VD on postpartum LAM recovery by comparing clinical parameters of LAM function as well as RNA transcriptomic data from LAM biopsy taken immediately following VD and at 6 to 8 weeks postpartum in 2 groups of age-disparate primiparas.

Human subjects cohort and tissue

Cohort

We recruited young (aged 18—25 years) and older (aged 35—45 years) nulliparous women in the third trimester from April 2023 through June 2024 from University of Utah outpatient clinics and labor and delivery. In addition to age, inclusion criteria for enrollment included a spontaneous VD of a term (≥37 weeks) singleton pregnancy with functional epidural anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included: bleeding disorder, need for postpartum anticoagulation, chronic inflammatory conditions (ie, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, anti-phospholipid syndrome), active infections (ie, upper respiratory infection, chorioamnionitis, pyelonephritis), use of corticosteroids in the last 2 weeks, known muscle or connective tissue disorders, obstetrical anal sphincter injury (third/fourth degree perineal lacerations), operative delivery, bilateral levator avulsion, postpartum emergency, or any concerns from the obstetrical team that would preclude a LAM biopsy. Informed consent was obtained either during third trimester clinic visit or on labor and delivery prior to active labor and/or administration of any opioid analgesia. Potential participants were enrolled once all inclusion/exclusion criteria were confirmed after delivery. This study was approved by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board #00157545. RNA-seq data will be deposited in database of genotypes and phenotypes and will be made publicly accessible through gene expression omnibus repository. The data are also available from the primary corresponding author upon reasonable request and with appropriate institutional review board approval and datause agreement.

Study procedures

Participants underwent 2 study visits.

Immediately postpartum: After VD and with continued adequate epidural anesthesia, the LAMs were palpated to ensure the absence of LAM avulsion. The vagina was prepped with betadine and an 18-gauge core biopsy needle was inserted through the vaginal tissue and into the mid-portion of the right LAM and a tissue specimen obtained. Up to 3 passes were performed to obtain an adequate specimen and each specimen was snap frozen in a separate vial in liquid nitrogen and immediately transported to the laboratory and stored at −80°C. Hemostasis was obtained using manual pressure. Author C.W.S. performed all LAM biopsies and examinations. Obstetrical data collected at the time of delivery included estimated gestational age, length of second stage, infant weight, and degree/type of perineal laceration.

Tissue processing and RNA sequencing

The LAM biopsies were stored at −80°C Zin cryovials and later processed in batches and submitted to the University of Utah’s High Throughput Genomics Core for RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis.

The frozen tissue specimens (~1 mg) were transferred to a 2-mL homogenization tube containing 0.9 mL of QIAzol lysis buffer and a 5-mm stainless steel ball. The tissue was immediately homogenized using a Qiagen Tissuelyser LT. Total RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Universal Kit (Cat #73404, Lot# 175019753) using the manufacturer’s protocol. Each RNA sample was eluted with 30 μL RNase-Free water. RNA was quantified using NanoDrop 8000 and Qubit RNA High Sensitivity Assay followed by DNase treatment with a Zymo Spin column. RNA-seq preprocessing and normalization are detailed in Supplemental Methods.

Analytical methods

Identification and prioritization of differentially expressed genes and differentially recovery-responsive genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are genes whose expression markedly differs between groups at a single point in time. Differentially recovery-responsive genes (DRGs) are genes that have markedly different levels of gene expression during a recovery phase reflecting the trajectory of change in gene expression over time. DEGs were identified and prioritized using Linear Models for Microarray Datas (LIMMA) moderated t test,21 followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction22 for multiple hypothesis testing. DRGs were defined as genes exhibiting a statistically significant maternal age—by—postpartum recovery interval interaction in the LIMMA model, indicating age-dependent differences in longitudinal expression change rather than simple between-group differences. Transcriptional “signatures” refer to patterns of gene expression. Comparisons were conducted to examine gene expression (ie, signature) differences across distinct phenotypic groups defined by mothers’ age group and biopsy timing (immediately and 6—8 weeks postpartum) among 8 patients. Specifically, the following statistical contrasts, which elucidate transcriptional signatures between defined groups, were analyzed (details in Supplemental Methods).

Maternal age DEGs (older vs young): independent of biopsy timing (pooling timing).

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a multivariate statistical approach that creates “summary” variables (ie, principal components [PCs]) which describe patterns of variation within high-dimensional datasets and identifies independent directions in which the data vary.23 The first component (PC1) is the vector that accounts for the greatest proportion of the variance. The second component (PC2) accounts for the second highest proportion of variance and is independent of PC1. This pattern continues for additional components of decreasing magnitude. Using data visualization methods, it is possible to ascertain which variables in the dataset parallel the identified PCs. We used PCA to summarize multivariate structure and avoid low-power gene—phenotype testing. PCA on the full transcriptome was used only for quality control (normalization performance and residual batch effects). For biological inference, PCA was performed separately on each LIMMA-derived contrast signature (maternal age DEGs, postpartum recovery DEGs, and age-by-recovery interaction DRGs), and on the clinical phenotype matrix. To control multiplicity, we specified 3 constraints a priori: (1) transcriptomic PCs were tested sequentially from highest to lowest variance explained (Supplemental Methods), (2) only the first clinical phenotype PC was considered, and (3) stepwise FDR correction was applied across the tested PC correlations with testing stopped at the first association achieving FDR <0.05 (Supplemental Methods). Spearman correlations using these constraints were calculated between the first clinical phenotype PC and transcriptomic PCs representing each of the 3 prioritized gene sets.

Concordance of maternal age and postpartum recovery differentially expressed genes from prior pelvic organ prolapse studies

We curated 4 POP case-control pelvictissue DEG datasets15,24—26 (Table 1). Gene symbols were harmonized to GENCODE GRCh38.27 Pairwise enrichment among these published sets and our primiparous DEGs were assessed by Fisher’s exact test in R, and statistically significant overlaps were visualized with the corrplot package.

Gene-set enrichment analysis

We characterized prioritized DEGs through gene-set enrichment analysis using 2636 curated Gene Ontology biological process gene-sets,28 applying Fisher’s exact test and Benjamini—Hochberg FDR adjustment (FDR <0.05)22; additional methodological details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Single protein interaction network analysis

To identify protein-protein interactions substantially enriched among DEGs, we used single protein interaction network analysis,29—32 a permutation-based method preserving network topology, applied to a high-confidence network derived from STRING version 12 downloaded on October 19, 2023.33 We further prioritized regulatory proteins by identifying hubs (top 20% by node degree—a measure of interaction or connectivity with other proteins) and bottlenecks (top 20% betweenness centrality—a measure of how often a protein lies on the shortest path between other proteins in the network), using hypergeometric testing to assess enrichment among DEGs. Hubs are central to a network’s function and are often tied to critical regulatory genes, transcription factors, or signaling proteins. Bottlenecks are functionally important proteins that regulate communication and connect pathways. The complete single protein interaction network analysis methods are detailed in the Supplemental Methods.

A flow diagram of the analytical methods is provided in the beginning of the Supplemental Methods.

Normalization and batch effects

Transcriptome-wide PCA confirmed effective RNA-seq data normalization, demonstrating that the first 2 PCs (PC1, 38%; PC2, 17%) together accounted for 55% of the transcriptomic variation (Supplemental Figure 1). PCA segregated samples into 4 clinical clusters defined by maternal age (young vs older) and recovery interval (immediately postpartum vs 6—8 weeks postpartum), with no observable clustering driven by sequencing or library-preparation batch effects. Thus, while the biopsy samples underwent RNA sequencing in 2 different batches, these results provide reassurance that group differences are due to real biological signals and not batch effects.

Maternal age×recovery interval statistical interaction DRGs

Intended to address whether maternal age impacted changes in regulation of gene expression between the 2 biopsies, 58 protein-coding DRGs across maternal age cohorts are illustrated as the fold-change in gene expression of recovery interval (6—8 weeks postpartum relative to immediately postpartum), compared across maternal age groups (Figures 4 and 5). Notably, comparing expression of genes TP53BP1, PRR12, and ZNF316 between the first and second biopsies revealed an antagonistic age-dependent “recovery trajectory” in which expression of these DRGs decreased between time points in the young group but increased in the older cohort. PLK3 displayed a similar pattern although in opposite direction, with PLK3 being decreased among older women at 6 to 8 weeks postpartum, while it remained elevated in younger women (Figure 5, Panel B). There was no enrichment between the maternal age×recovery interval DRGs and the 4 case-control POP studies shown in Figure 3.

Spearman correlations between clinical and transcriptomic PC scores identified a single significant association by design: PC1—capturing variance in clinical phenotype (Table 2)—correlated with transcriptomic PC3 derived from the 309 maternal age—associated DEGs (ρ=0.88, FDR <0.05) (Supplemental Figure 3). Comment

Principal findings

We found maternal age—associated differences in primiparous LAM gene expression during early postpartum recovery that substantially overlap with LAM transcriptomic signatures previously reported in postmenopausal women with POP compared with matched controls. This maternal age signature was significantly associated with the dominant PC derived from 19 quantitative maternal and obstetrical phenotypic variables, including 12 quantitative measures of maternal pelvic support. In addition, functional enrichment analysis of this maternal age signature implicated inflammatory and tissue repair—related biological processes. These alterations may lead to impaired recovery and remodeling of the LAM, which informs older primipara’s heightened long-term susceptibility to POP. Our study fills a mechanistic gap by profiling the immediate postpartum transcriptome of the LAM in primiparas, analyzing for the first time how acute gene-expression changes after delivery may predispose older women to later pelvic floor dysfunction. We also interrogated maternal age, an established epidemiologic risk factor for POP, to define its influence on these early repair programs.

Results in the context of what is known

Convergent transcriptomic signatures between maternal age and recovery interval implicate mechanisms underlying POP susceptibility. Both clinical and molecular evidence suggest that older maternal age (≥30 years) increases the risk of LAM injury at first VD, impairs recovery, and increases the risk of POP.5,34 When we isolate maternal age DEGs (both time points pooled) and recovery interval DEGs (all ages pooled), only 11 genes overlap; yet, their true convergence emerges at the pathway level. Both groups of DEGs were enriched for core tissue repair and homeostatic programs. Both groups of DEGs also tap into epithelial-related programs—skin and epidermis development through keratinocyte differentiation and columnar/cuboidal epithelial cell differentiation—and even ossification pathways, although this is likely attributable to capturing vaginal epithelium in the biopsy. Interestingly, 20 DEGs from the maternal age and recovery interval sets overlap with published POP case-control gene lists,15,24—26 and these shared genes are enriched for wound healing, cell—substrate adhesion, response to external stimulus, and cellular amide metabolic processes. In other words, although maternal age and recovery interval each perturb distinct gene cohorts, they trigger different yet likely interacting facets of the same reparative and remodeling mechanisms. Together, these data underscore how early postpartum and aging signals share biological processes that, when dysregulated, may predispose the LAM to long-term dysregulation and POP.

Antagonistic transcriptomic interactions between maternal age and recovery interval reveal a pattern of disrupted p53-regulated checkpoint and DNA repair signaling which may impair tissue healing and increase cellular senescence. Although these DRGs across maternal age genes did not overlap with DEGs from previous case-control studies,15,24—26 they showed substantial concordance with our 2 transcriptomic signatures based on maternal age. Unlike classical DEG analyses comparing static mean expression between groups, these DRGs capture divergent within-subject transcriptional responses across recovery intervals: revealing interaction effects that depend on maternal age rather than baseline expression levels. Prior POP case-control studies were not designed to detect such age-stratified response trajectories, as they compared prolapse vs control tissues without accounting for primiparous age—dependent recovery dynamics. Furthermore, a rigorous network analysis further prioritized 4 of these transcripts which were related to p53-checkpoint and repair signaling. When we compare immediately postpartum to the 6 to 8—week postpartum time point, young women downregulate TP53BP1, PRR12, and ZNF316, allowing damage sensing to resolve and repair programs to proceed while PLK3 remains stable, preserving checkpoint-to-repair signaling. In older women, however, TP53BP1, PRR12, and ZNF316 were upregulated and PLK3 was downregulated, effectively decoupling damage detection from kinase-mediated repair and locking cells into a senescent, proinflammatory state. This antagonistic expression pattern suggests that in older primipara, the damagesensing machinery in the LAM remains active but conformed into a senescent/inflammatory state instead of progressing into a productive healing phase. This pattern of elevated TP53BP1 alongside reduced PLK3 supports a model in which aging LAM accumulates DNA damage yet PLK3 fails to engage downstream p53-driven checkpoint and repair signaling, thereby impairing regenerative healing.35,36 Thus, it appears that “maternally aged” LAM similarly experiences greater cellular senescence after VD, whereas young LAM more readily enters a regenerative phase.

Clinical implications

From a clinical-translational perspective, these age-related differences reveal that “biological aging” at the transcriptional level is demonstrated in LAM of women aged ≥35 years immediately after first delivery. Attenuated activation of regenerative gene programs alongside elevated senescence and inflammatory signatures in older LAM possibly contributes to slower recovery and greater risk of chronic dysfunction and POP.

Research implications

Results from this study identify gene pathways and transcripts which may be implicated in POP pathomechanisms. Validation and further exploration of the identified transcriptional pathways are warranted. In addition, this study also offers insights into potential targeted POP interventions or regenerative strategies such as senolytic therapies or modulation of the postpartum inflammatory milieu focused on pelvic muscle repair in older primiparas. Indeed, animal model studies of senolytics demonstrate promise for clearing senescent cells and preventing POP.37,38

Strengths and limitations

All existing human studies of pelvicfloor gene expression, including ours and the 4 POP case-control analyses,15,24—26 are hampered by small patient cohorts and lack an independent validation group. Although we observe marked molecular concordance across prior datasets and our findings, these overlaps remain modest in number. To bolster confidence, we emphasize convergent biological processes and a conserved signaling axis validated by stringent network analysis. Importantly, our study’s paired design (LAM biopsies from the same women at 2 time points) provides a more robust internal comparison than cross-sectional case-control cohorts. Finally, the identification of keratinocyte and osteoblast differentiation transcriptional pathways may indicate that the biopsy also captured the vaginal epithelial tissue and not only skeletal muscle; thus, interpretation to only LAM is cautioned.

Conclusion

By linking older maternal age to dysre-gulated p53 signaling and immune response pathways, our findings provide a mechanistic basis for the well-documented epidemiologic association between older age at first VD and increased risk of POP.

Why was this study conducted?

To gain a better understanding of the molecular changes occurring in the levator ani of young and older primiparas immediately after vaginal delivery and at 6 to 8 weeks postpartum.

Key findings

Differential expression of genes revealed that older primiparas may have a disconnect between the molecular mechanisms that detect damage and those that initiate repair leading to cellular senescence.

What does this add to what is known?

This is the first study to conduct transcriptional analysis of the levator ani of human subjects after vaginal delivery and during postpartum recovery enabling the identification of potentially relevant mechanisms of pelvic floor disorders.
