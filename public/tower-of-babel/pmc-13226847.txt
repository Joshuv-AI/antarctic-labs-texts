Prolapsed ureterocele in a new-born female: A rare presentation of duplex kidney anomaly with diagnostic challenges
Authors: Fares Chaabouni, Rabiaa Ben Abdallah, Cyrine Saadi, Souha Laarif, Asma Jabloun, Fatma Trabelsi, Aida Daib, Youssef Hellal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUreterocele is a congenital dilatation of the distal ureter. Although typically diagnosed prenatally or incidentally, prolapsed ureterocele in a neonate is rare.We report a 13-day-old female infant referred for evaluation of a vulvar mass initially taken for hymeneal imperforation. Prenatal history was unremarkable. Physical examination revealed a reddish vulvar mass with whitish secretions. Pelvic ultrasound demonstrated a duplicated right renal system with a ureterocele, suggesting a prolapsed ureterocele. Incision then differed ureterocele resection was performed without complications.Prolapsed ureterocele in neonates is a rare but important differential diagnosis for interlabial masses. Early recognition and surgical management preserve renal function.

Introduction

Ureterocele is defined as a congenital cystic dilatation of the terminal intramural portion of the ureter. It is frequently associated with complete duplication of the collecting system (approximately 80% of cases), predominantly affecting the upper moiety. Most ureteroceles are identified antenatally through fetal ultrasonography (manifesting as hydronephrosis) or discovered incidentally during infancy or childhood.

Prolapse of the ureterocele through the urethra, presenting as an interlabial or vulvar mass in a new-born female, remains exceedingly uncommon. This presentation accounts for less than 5% of ureteroceles and is more frequently reported in girls due to the higher prevalence of duplicated systems in females. Prolapse may lead to urethral obstruction, recurrent urinary tract infections, or acute urinary retention. Differential diagnoses include hymeneal imperforation, urethral prolapse, paraurethral cysts, hydrocolpos, and botryoid sarcoma.

We report a case of an unusual prolapsed ureterocele in a 13-day-old female new-born, initially misdiagnosed as hymeneal imperforation, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the importance of prompt imaging and management.

Case report

A 13-day-old female infant, born at term following an uncomplicated prenatal course with normal antenatal ultrasonography, was urgently referred to the paediatric surgery department for evaluation of a protruding vulvar mass noticed by the parents shortly after birth. The infant was otherwise well, with normal feeding, weight gain, and voiding patterns reported by the family; however, mild irritability during micturition was noted retrospectively. No history of fever, haematuria, or urinary tract infection.

Physical examination confirmed a reddish, smooth, cystic-appearing mass protruding from the vulva, measuring approximately 2 × 1.5 cm, covered with whitish mucoid secretions on the surface. The mass was non-tender, irreducible on gentle manipulation, and appeared to emerge from the urethral meatus (Fig. 1).

A cystic-appearing mass protruding from the vulva.

No abdominal distension, palpable flank masses, or signs of systemic infection were observed. The initial clinical differential diagnosis included hymeneal imperforation, paraurethral cyst, and prolapsed ureterocele.

Pelvic and renal ultrasonography performed immediately revealed a complete duplication of the right renal collecting system. The upper pole moiety exhibited marked hydronephrosis, with a thin-walled cystic dilatation (ureterocele) projecting into the bladder lumen and prolapsing through the urethra to the external vulvar region (Fig. 2).

Ultrasonography showing a cystic dilatation into the bladder.

Urgent surgical intervention was undertaken under general anaesthesia to relieve potential obstruction and prevent complications such as infection or renal deterioration. An initial needle aspiration of the prolapsed mass yielded urine, thereby confirming the diagnosis of prolapsed ureterocele. Transurethral incision of the ureterocele were performed, allowing decompression and drainage of the dilated upper pole system. No intraoperative complications, including bleeding or perforation, occurred. A Foley catheter was left in place for 48 hours postoperatively (Fig. 3).

Post-operative result.

A urine sample analysis was performed, showing a urinary urea level of 3.7 mmol/L and a urinary creatinine of 3 mmol/L.

The prolapsed portion was resected after two months adequately to prevent recurrence while preserving sphincter function. The postoperative period was uneventful, with prompt resumption of normal voiding, resolution of the vulvar mass, and no signs of urinary tract infection. Follow-up ultrasonography at 4 weeks demonstrated significant improvement in upper pole hydronephrosis, with no residual prolapse or obstruction.

Discussion

Prolapsed ureterocele represents one of the rarest clinical manifestations of congenital ureteral anomalies.1 Ureterocele, defined as a cystic dilatation of the submucosal segment of the distal ureter, has an estimated incidence of 1 in 5000 to 12 000 live births and is strongly associated with complete ureteral duplication in up to 80 % of cases, predominantly affecting the upper moiety.2 Prolapse through the urethra occurs in fewer than 5 % of all ureteroceles and is encountered far more frequently in females (female-to-male ratio 4–7 : 1) because of the shorter urethra and the anatomical predisposition of ectopic ureteroceles. Although most ureteroceles are now detected antenatally, postnatal prolapse presenting as an interlabial or vulvar mass in the neonatal period remains exceptionally uncommon and is often initially mistaken for more frequent entities such as hymeneal imperforation, urethral prolapse, paraurethral (Skene's) cyst, hydrocolpos, or even botryoid rhabdomyosarcoma.3

In the neonatal period the prolapsed ureterocele typically appears as a smooth, reddish, cystic mass protruding from the urethral meatus, sometimes accompanied by mucoid or whitish secretions. Urinary obstruction may be intermittent, explaining why some infants void normally at presentation.4 The present case is illustrative: a 13-day-old female infant was referred with a vulvar mass initially attributed to hymeneal imperforation. Immediate pelvic ultrasonography demonstrated right-sided complete duplication with an upper-moiety ureterocele prolapsing through the urethra. Diagnostic confirmation was rapidly obtained by gentle needle aspiration of the mass, which yielded clear urine and thereby excluded solid or neoplastic lesions. This manoeuvre, rarely emphasised in the literature, proved decisive in guiding urgent surgical planning.

The first description of urethral prolapse of a ureterocele is attributed to Moore in 1982, but that report concerned an adult woman. True neonatal presentations were not published until the late 1990s and early 2000s. Robson et al.5 and Ilica et al.6 each described a new-born girl with a prolapsing ectopic ureterocele arising from a duplex system; both were diagnosed by ultrasonography and managed by endoscopic incision followed by upper-pole heminephrectomy because of a non-functioning moiety. Abdelgadir et al.7 reported a similar new-born with a vulvar mass that was reduced manually and later treated definitively. More recently, Fernández et al.2 documented a 7-month-old infant in whom the prolapsed ureterocele was initially reduced under sedation; magnetic resonance urography confirmed an ectopic ureterocele with moderate hydroureteronephrosis, and the child ultimately underwent upper-pole heminephroureterectomy. Abou-Bekr et al.8 described a 12-month-old girl whose prolapsed ureterocele required initial catheter decompression followed by heminephrectomy and subsequent ureterocelectomy with reimplantation because of recurrent prolapse. Kong et al.9 highlighted an incarcerated prolapsed ureterocele in a 4-month-old infant presenting with acute urinary retention; emergency endoscopic puncture relieved the obstruction, and imaging later revealed grade V vesicoureteral reflux in the lower moiety.

Diagnostic imaging has evolved. High-resolution ultrasonography remains the first-line modality, reliably demonstrating the duplex kidney, the intravesical cystic lesion, and its prolapsed component.1 When doubt persists, needle aspiration yielding urine offers immediate confirmation, as performed in our case. Voiding cystourethrography is mandatory to detect associated vesicoureteral reflux (present in >50 % of lower-moiety ureters and 25 % of contralateral systems). Magnetic resonance urography provides exquisite anatomical detail when renal function is equivocal or when prenatal diagnosis is unavailable.10

Management of prolapsed ureterocele in the neonate should be staged.11 The immediate goal is decompression to relieve obstruction and prevent ascending infection or further renal damage. Options include gentle manual reduction (often temporary), transurethral incision/marsupialisation, or simple needle puncture. In the present new-born, endoscopic incision and marsupialisation achieved complete decompression without complication.11 Definitive surgery is individualised according to upper-pole function: a non-functioning moiety is best treated by laparoscopic heminephroureterectomy, whereas a functioning moiety may be managed by ureterocele excision and ureteral reimplantation. Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended until reflux, if present, resolves or is corrected.4

The excellent outcome in our patient underscores the value of early recognition. Prolapsed ureterocele, although rare, must be included in the differential diagnosis of any interlabial mass in a new-born girl. Continued multidisciplinary follow-up with serial imaging and monitoring of renal function remains essential to optimise long-term preservation of the urinary tract.10

Conclusion

Prolapsed ureterocele in the neonatal period is a rare but important differential diagnosis for interlabial or vulvar masses in female newborns. This presentation, although accounting for fewer than 5% of ureteroceles, carries a risk of urethral obstruction and renal damage if not promptly recognized.

The present case highlights the diagnostic challenges, particularly when the prolapsed ureterocele mimics hymeneal imperforation. Immediate ultrasonography combined with bedside needle aspiration yielding urine allowed rapid confirmation and guided urgent endoscopic decompression. This approach achieved complete resolution of the prolapse while preserving upper pole function.

Early recognition, prompt imaging, and staged surgical management are essential to prevent complications and optimise long-term renal and urinary tract preservation. Prolapsed ureterocele should therefore be systematically considered in any newborn girl presenting with a cystic vulvar mass.
