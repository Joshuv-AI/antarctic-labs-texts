Parental knowledge, awareness, and intended healthcare-seeking responses toward congenital heart disease in the West Bank, Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Izzeddin Swidan, Nagham Noman, Lana Malhis, Omar Odeh, Shahd Saadeh, Sabaa Ahmad Massalha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital heart disease (CHD) is a major contributor to infant morbidity and mortality worldwide. Although prenatal screening, neonatal assessment, pulse oximetry screening, and routine pediatric evaluation contribute to CHD detection, some cases may remain unrecognized until after discharge or later in infancy. Parental recognition of warning signs may support timely healthcare-seeking, but it should be considered one component of a broader diagnostic and referral pathway. This study assessed parental knowledge, awareness, and intended healthcare-seeking responses toward CHD among parents in the West Bank, Palestine.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among parents and primary caregivers in the West Bank, Palestine, between February and April 2026. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling and completed a structured Arabic questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, prior awareness of CHD, knowledge of CHD warning signs and risk factors, and intended healthcare-seeking responses to CHD-related symptoms. Composite knowledge scores were calculated for warning signs and risk factors. Non-parametric tests, Spearman’s correlation, and multivariable linear regression were used to examine factors associated with knowledge scores and their associations with intended healthcare-seeking responses.ResultsA total of 1,003 parents participated. Knowledge of CHD warning signs was moderate, with a median score of 6.0 out of 10, while knowledge of CHD risk factors was relatively higher, with a median score of 6.0 out of 9. However, gaps were identified in recognition of several clinical warning signs, particularly the misconception that cardiac murmurs always indicate CHD. Warning-sign knowledge was significantly associated with correct emergency response to cyanosis, likelihood of consulting a physician for feeding-related fatigue, and confidence in recognizing abnormal symptoms. Risk-factor knowledge showed weaker associations with intended responses. Warning-sign knowledge showed stronger associations with intended healthcare-seeking responses than risk-factor knowledge. Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is among the most common congenital anomalies worldwide and remains an important contributor to infant morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Global estimates suggest that CHD affects approximately 8–9 per 1,000 live births, although reported prevalence varies across regions and health systems [2]. CHD includes a broad spectrum of structural cardiac abnormalities that develop during fetal life, ranging from mild lesions requiring follow-up to critical defects requiring urgent diagnosis and intervention [3, 4].

Timely recognition, referral, and management are important in the clinical pathway of children with suspected CHD. Although many cases can be detected through prenatal screening, neonatal assessment, pulse oximetry screening, or routine pediatric evaluation, delayed or missed diagnosis continues to occur, particularly in settings where screening coverage, referral pathways, and access to specialized pediatric cardiac services may be variable [5–7]. Delayed diagnosis of CHD has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality, and timing of diagnosis may affect outcomes in critical CHD [8, 9].

Prenatal detection can vary according to access to fetal cardiac imaging, quality of ultrasound assessment, timing of examination, and examiner expertise [10]. Pulse oximetry screening is highly specific but only moderately sensitive for critical CHD and may miss lesions that do not produce early oxygen desaturation [7]. In addition, some infants may appear clinically well at birth, with symptoms becoming more apparent only after discharge or as feeding demands increase [5, 9]. Other symptoms, such as feeding difficulty, sweating, poor weight gain, recurrent chest infections, or mild respiratory distress, may be nonspecific and mistaken for more common infant conditions [6, 9]. Health-system factors, including delayed clinical recognition, referral barriers, and variable access to specialized pediatric cardiac services, may also contribute to diagnostic delay [9, 11].

In this context, parents and caregivers may contribute to timely healthcare-seeking when they recognize concerning symptoms such as cyanosis, feeding difficulty, respiratory distress, poor weight gain, recurrent chest infections, or syncope [6]. However, parental recognition should be understood as one component of a broader healthcare pathway rather than the sole determinant of diagnostic timing.

Awareness of CHD risk factors may also support parental understanding of potential vulnerability and the need for medical consultation. Established or commonly reported risk factors include family history, chromosomal abnormalities, selected maternal conditions, maternal infections during pregnancy, exposure to certain medications or substances, and environmental factors [3, 12]. In Palestine, a population-based study from the Gaza Strip reported 598 CHD cases among 59,757 live births in 2010, corresponding to a birth prevalence of approximately 10 per 1,000 live births, supporting the relevance of CHD as a pediatric public health concern in this setting [13]. Consanguinity has also been associated with increased susceptibility to congenital anomalies, including CHD, in some populations [14]. This is relevant in the Palestinian context, where consanguinity remains socially and demographically important and has been described in recent Palestinian research [15].

Nevertheless, knowledge of risk factors alone may not translate into appropriate healthcare-seeking unless parents are also able to recognize clinically important warning signs.

Existing studies suggest that parental knowledge of CHD is often incomplete, even among parents of children already diagnosed with CHD [16–18]. Knowledge gaps have been linked to factors such as educational level, access to reliable health information, communication with healthcare professionals, and previous exposure to disease-specific counseling [16–18]. However, studies focusing on parents of affected children may overestimate awareness in the general parental population because these parents often receive repeated clinical explanations during diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. Less is known about baseline CHD awareness among parents without direct experience of caring for a child with CHD.

Despite the relevance of CHD as a pediatric public health issue in Palestine, evidence on parental awareness remains limited. Reports on Palestinian children requiring cardiac care have highlighted challenges related to treatment timing and follow-up [11] .

These contextual factors support the need to understand parental knowledge gaps and intended healthcare-seeking responses, particularly among parents without prior direct experience of CHD. Such information may help guide culturally appropriate health education through primary healthcare services, community programs, and digital platforms.

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess knowledge and awareness of CHD warning signs and risk factors among sampled parents in the West Bank, Palestine, and to examine associations between knowledge domains and intended healthcare-seeking responses. To our knowledge, this is one of the first cross-sectional assessments of parental knowledge, awareness, and intended healthcare-seeking responses toward CHD among parents of children without diagnosed CHD in Palestine.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted among parents and primary caregivers in the West Bank, Palestine, between February and April 2026. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from community and healthcare-related settings, as well as through online platforms.

Eligible participants were parents or primary caregivers of at least one child aged 0–14 years, aged 18 years or older, residing in the West Bank, able to understand Arabic, willing to provide informed consent, and willing to complete the questionnaire.

Healthcare professionals working specifically in pediatric or cardiology-related units, including pediatricians, cardiologists, pediatric nurses, and staff in related specialties, were excluded to reduce potential bias from direct clinical exposure to CHD. Healthcare workers from other departments or non-pediatric/non-cardiology settings were not excluded and were retained in the analysis. Therefore, employment in the healthcare sector was examined as a study variable.

Parents or caregivers of children previously diagnosed with CHD were excluded because direct experience with diagnosis, counseling, follow-up care, or treatment may substantially influence knowledge and intended healthcare-seeking responses. This exclusion was intended to assess baseline awareness among parents without direct personal experience of caring for a child with CHD.

Additional exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, inability to provide informed consent, incomplete eligibility information, and participation in a CHD-focused educational program within the previous three months, to minimize recent information-exposure bias.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the Raosoft sample size calculator [19], assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, 50% response distribution, and a population size of 100,000. This yielded a minimum required sample of 383 participants. To increase precision and account for incomplete responses, the final sample size was expanded to 1,003 participants.

Questionnaire development and validation

A structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study after reviewing relevant literature and authoritative health information sources addressing CHD symptoms, warning signs, risk factors, diagnosis, and parental awareness [1, 6, 12, 16, 17]. The questionnaire was prepared in Arabic, the native language of the target population, to ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness. An English version was prepared for reporting and publication purposes and is provided as Supplementary File 1. The Arabic version is provided as Supplementary File 2.

The questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of experts to assess content relevance, clarity, comprehensiveness, and alignment with the study objectives. Items were reviewed for coverage of the main domains, including sociodemographic characteristics, prior awareness of CHD, knowledge of CHD warning signs, knowledge of CHD risk factors, and intended healthcare-seeking responses. Expert review was conducted qualitatively; no formal content validity index was calculated. Based on expert feedback, unclear wording was revised before pilot testing. A pilot study was conducted among 20 parents to assess clarity, readability, completion time, and overall comprehensibility. Pilot participants were excluded from the final analysis. Feedback from the pilot phase was used to make minor wording refinements without changing the core questionnaire domains.

Following the informed consent screen, the questionnaire included five analytic sections. The first section collected sociodemographic and background characteristics, including age, gender, education level, employment status, healthcare-sector employment, previous diagnosis of CHD in any child, and number of children. The second section assessed self-rated CHD knowledge. The third section assessed prior awareness of CHD, primary source of CHD-related information, and general CHD knowledge. The fourth section assessed knowledge of CHD warning signs and related early-recognition concepts. The fifth section assessed intended healthcare-seeking responses to CHD-related symptoms and knowledge of CHD risk factors.

Two composite knowledge scores were calculated. The warning signs knowledge scale included 10 items covering clinical warning signs and early-recognition concepts, with scores ranging from 0 to 10. The risk factors knowledge scale included 9 items, with scores ranging from 0 to 9. Correct responses were assigned 1 point, while incorrect and “don’t know” responses were assigned 0 points. Higher scores indicated greater knowledge.

Internal consistency was acceptable for both knowledge scales. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.782 for the warning signs knowledge scale and 0.724 for the risk factors knowledge scale. One reverse-scored item, “cardiac murmur always indicates CHD,” showed a low corrected item–total correlation. This may reflect that the item assessed recognition of a common misconception rather than direct recognition of a clinical warning sign. The item was retained on content-validity grounds because it addressed a clinically relevant misconception and did not meaningfully reduce the internal consistency of the scale.

Data collection

Data were collected using paper-based and online questionnaires. Paper-based questionnaires were distributed in community and healthcare-related settings, while the online version was shared through social media platforms and messaging applications. No identifying personal information was collected, and all responses were stored securely and accessed only by the research team.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University (Med. Nov. 2025/161). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained, and data were accessible only to the research team. Data collection was conducted in private or secure settings to ensure participant comfort.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics and study variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Knowledge scores were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) because they were not normally distributed.

Normality of knowledge scores was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare knowledge scores between two groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess associations between knowledge scores, self-rated knowledge, and ordinal intended healthcare-seeking response variables.

Knowledge levels were categorized using modified Bloom’s cut-off points into poor, moderate, and good categories [20]. The association between warning signs knowledge categories and risk factors knowledge categories was assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test, and Cramér’s V was reported as a measure of association strength.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to identify factors associated with warning signs knowledge and risk factors knowledge. The two knowledge scores were analyzed as continuous outcomes. Independent variables included age group, gender, education level, employment status, healthcare-sector employment, number of children, prior awareness of CHD, self-rated CHD knowledge, and primary information source. Primary information source and employment status were treated as categorical variables using dummy coding. Doctor/nurse was used as the reference category for primary information source, and housewife was used as the reference category for employment status. Gender, healthcare-sector employment, and prior awareness of CHD were entered as binary variables, while age group, education level, number of children, and self-rated knowledge were entered according to their ordered categories.

All variables were entered simultaneously using the forced-entry method. Regression analyses were performed using listwise deletion, resulting in an analytic sample of 978 participants because of missing data for self-rated CHD knowledge and primary information source. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors and tolerance values. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and 95% confidence intervals were reported for regression estimates.

Residual histograms, normal probability plots, and plots of standardized residuals against predicted values were inspected to assess approximate normality of residuals, linearity, and homoscedasticity.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 1,003 parents participated in the study. The largest age group was 36–45 years (45.4%), followed by 26–35 years (36.5%), while participants aged ≤ 25 years represented 5.1% of the sample. Females accounted for 61.3% of participants.

Regarding education and employment, most participants held a bachelor’s degree (67.1%). Approximately half of the sample were employed (50.7%), while 40.9% were housewives. A small proportion (5.3%) were employed in the healthcare sector; however, healthcare professionals in pediatrics or cardiology were excluded, while other healthcare workers were included.

In terms of family characteristics and exposure to CHD information, most participants had three children (41.3%), and 73.4% reported prior awareness of congenital heart disease (CHD). The most common sources of information were family and friends (36.6%), followed by the internet and social media (30.5%), while only 15.4% reported healthcare professionals as their primary source (Table 1).

Knowledge of CHD warning signs

Overall, parental knowledge of CHD warning signs was moderate, with a median score of 6.0 (IQR: 4.0–8.0). Based on categorized scores, 43.2% of participants demonstrated moderate knowledge, 30.8% had poor knowledge, and 26.0% had good knowledge.

Regarding individual items, the most correctly identified warning sign was awareness that CHD can be detected at birth (72.9%). Recognition of symptoms such as cyanosis, feeding difficulties, and shortness of breath ranged between 59% and 62%. However, only 14.1% of participants correctly identified that not all cardiac murmurs indicate CHD (Table 2, Supplementary table S1).

Knowledge of CHD risk factors

Parental knowledge of CHD risk factors was relatively higher compared to warning signs, with a median score of 6.0 (IQR: 5.0–9.0). Based on categorized scores, 45.8% of participants demonstrated good knowledge, while 42.2% had moderate knowledge and 12.0% had poor knowledge.

Regarding specific risk factors, maternal smoking was the most frequently identified risk factor (77.6%), followed by advanced maternal age (74.9%) and maternal infections during pregnancy (74.8%). Awareness of genetic and environmental factors ranged between 63.8% and 72.7%. The least recognized risk factor was maternal obesity (59.3%) (Table 3, Supplementary table S2).

Factors associated with warning-signs knowledge

Significant differences in CHD warning signs knowledge scores were observed across several sociodemographic variables. Males had significantly higher scores compared to females (Mann–Whitney U = 88,690, p < 0.001).

Statistically significant differences were also found according to employment status (H = 76.803, p < 0.001), education level (H = 76.682, p < 0.001), and number of children (H = 17.281, p = 0.001). Participants working in the healthcare sector also demonstrated higher knowledge scores compared to non-healthcare workers (U = 19,157, p = 0.003).

Age group was not significantly associated with warning signs knowledge (H = 3.088, p = 0.378) (Supplementary table S3).

Factors associated with risk-factors knowledge

CHD risk factors knowledge scores differed significantly across several sociodemographic variables. Males had significantly higher scores compared to females (Mann–Whitney U = 95,794, p < 0.001).

Significant differences were observed according to employment status (H = 24.577, p < 0.001), age group (H = 12.797, p = 0.005), and number of children (H = 53.912, p < 0.001). However, education level was not significantly associated with risk factors knowledge (H = 6.856, p = 0.144).

In bivariate analysis, Participants working in the healthcare sector did not show a statistically significant difference compared to non-healthcare workers (p = 0.054) (Supplementary table S4).

Self-rated awareness and knowledge scores

Participants who had previously heard of CHD had significantly higher scores in both warning signs and risk factors knowledge (both p < 0.001).

Self-rated knowledge was positively correlated with actual knowledge scores. A moderate positive correlation was observed for warning signs knowledge (rs = 0.441, p < 0.001), while a weaker but significant correlation was found for risk factors knowledge (rs = 0.316, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

Associations between knowledge domains

Warning-sign and risk-factor knowledge categories were significantly associated (χ² = 118.189, p < 0.001), with a moderate effect size (Cramér’s V = 0.243), indicating that higher knowledge in one domain was associated with higher knowledge in the other.

Associations between knowledge scores and intended healthcare-seeking responses

Higher knowledge of CHD warning signs was significantly associated with more appropriate healthcare-seeking responses. Specifically, warning signs knowledge was positively correlated with correct emergency response to cyanosis (rs = 0.351, p < 0.001), likelihood of seeking medical consultation (rs = 0.227, p < 0.001), and confidence in recognizing abnormal symptoms (rs = 0.197, p < 0.001).

In contrast, knowledge of CHD risk factors showed weaker associations with healthcare-seeking responses. It was significantly correlated only with correct emergency response to cyanosis (rs = 0.216, p < 0.001), while no significant correlations were observed with likelihood of seeking medical consultation (p = 0.184) or confidence in recognizing symptoms (p = 0.115) (Table 6).

Multivariable analysis

Warning signs knowledge

In the multivariable linear regression model, higher warning-sign knowledge was independently associated with self-rated CHD knowledge, prior awareness of CHD, higher education level, number of children, being employed or a student compared with housewife, and gender. Compared with doctor/nurse as the primary information source, awareness campaigns and having no source of information were associated with lower warning-sign knowledge scores. Age group, healthcare-sector employment, internet/social media, and family/friends were not independently associated with warning-sign knowledge. The strongest standardized associations were observed for self-rated CHD knowledge (β = 0.276, p < 0.001) and prior awareness of CHD (β = 0.220, p < 0.001). The model explained 39.6% of the variance in warning-sign knowledge (R² = 0.396). Multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate problematic collinearity, with variance inflation factors ranging from 1.174 to 2.258 (Table 7).

Risk factors knowledge

In the multivariable linear regression model for risk-factor knowledge, scores were independently associated with self-rated CHD knowledge, number of children, prior awareness of CHD, unemployment compared with housewife, gender, education level, healthcare-sector employment, and awareness campaigns as a primary information source. Positive associations were observed for self-rated CHD knowledge, number of children, prior awareness of CHD, and being unemployed compared with housewife. In contrast, gender, education level, healthcare-sector employment, and awareness campaigns compared with doctor/nurse were negatively associated with risk-factor knowledge scores. Internet/social media, family/friends, and no source of information were not significantly different from doctor/nurse, and age group, being employed, and being a student were not independently associated with risk-factor knowledge. The strongest standardized associations were observed for self-rated CHD knowledge (β = 0.254, p < 0.001), number of children (β = 0.191, p < 0.001), and prior awareness of CHD (β = 0.171, p < 0.001). The model explained 26.1% of the variance in risk-factor knowledge (R² = 0.261). Multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate problematic collinearity, with variance inflation factors ranging from 1.174 to 2.258 (Table 8).

Discussion

This study assessed parental knowledge, awareness, and intended healthcare-seeking responses regarding congenital heart disease (CHD) among sampled parents in the West Bank, Palestine. Unlike many previous studies that focused on parents of children already diagnosed with CHD, this study examined parents of children without diagnosed CHD, providing insight into baseline awareness in a sampled West Bank parental population. This distinction is important because parents of affected children may have higher CHD-related knowledge due to repeated exposure to clinical counseling, follow-up, and disease-specific education [16, 21].

Parental awareness should be interpreted as one component of a broader CHD recognition and care pathway. Parents may notice concerning symptoms that appear after birth or after hospital discharge, such as cyanosis, feeding difficulty, poor growth, respiratory distress, or syncope. However, diagnostic timing is also strongly influenced by prenatal screening, neonatal examination, pulse oximetry screening, clinical referral, and access to specialized pediatric cardiac services [22, 23]. Therefore, the findings of this study should not be interpreted as evidence that parental awareness independently reduces diagnostic delay. Rather, they identify knowledge gaps and intended response patterns that may help inform supportive health-education strategies within maternal and child health services.

The findings showed that knowledge of CHD warning signs was moderate, whereas knowledge of risk factors was relatively higher. This suggests that parents may be more familiar with general or pregnancy-related risk factors than with the clinical signs that may appear in infants or children. Recognition of warning signs is particularly important because these signs may prompt parents to seek medical evaluation when symptoms arise. The low proportion of participants who correctly identified that cardiac murmurs do not always indicate CHD also suggests an important misconception. This item should be interpreted as measuring recognition of a common misconception rather than direct recognition of a clinical symptom. The observed knowledge gaps are consistent with previous studies showing that parental understanding of CHD is often incomplete, even among parents of affected children [16].

Previous research has also shown that parental knowledge may be shaped by education, access to information, communication with healthcare professionals, and prior exposure to CHD-related counseling [17, 18]. In the present study, gender, education level, number of children, prior awareness of CHD, and self-rated knowledge were associated with knowledge scores. These findings suggest that CHD awareness is related to both sociodemographic characteristics and previous exposure to information. However, the higher knowledge observed among males should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect differences in exposure to information sources, sampling characteristics, or unmeasured contextual factors rather than a direct gender-related difference in awareness. Some adjusted associations require cautious interpretation. Education level was positively associated with warning-sign knowledge but negatively associated with risk-factor knowledge after adjustment. Similarly, healthcare-sector employment was negatively associated with risk-factor knowledge but was not significantly associated with warning-sign knowledge. These findings may reflect overlap between covariates, such as prior awareness and self-rated knowledge, differences in subgroup composition, residual confounding, or suppression effects rather than true inverse relationships. Future studies with larger and more representative subgroup samples are needed to examine these associations more clearly.

Prior awareness of CHD and self-rated CHD knowledge were strongly associated with actual knowledge scores. However, the correlation between perceived and actual knowledge was only moderate, suggesting that subjective confidence does not necessarily reflect accurate understanding. This finding highlights the importance of providing clear and accessible CHD-related information rather than relying only on parents’ perceived familiarity with the condition [16] .

Information source also appeared relevant. Most participants reported family, friends, internet, or social media as their primary sources of CHD-related information, whereas a smaller proportion reported healthcare professionals as their primary source. After treating primary information source as a categorical variable, awareness campaigns were associated with lower knowledge scores compared with doctor/nurse as the reference category, and having no source of information was associated with lower warning-sign knowledge. However, internet/social media and family/friends were not significantly different from doctor/nurse after adjustment. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the cross-sectional design cannot determine whether information sources affect knowledge or whether parents with different knowledge levels seek different sources of information. Nevertheless, they suggest that CHD-related educational materials, including campaigns and digital content, should be developed or reviewed by healthcare professionals to improve accuracy and consistency.

Warning-sign knowledge showed stronger associations with intended healthcare-seeking responses than risk-factor knowledge. Parents with higher warning-sign knowledge were more likely to report appropriate intended responses to acute symptoms, including seeking emergency care for cyanosis and consulting a physician for feeding-related fatigue. In contrast, risk-factor knowledge showed weaker associations with intended responses. This distinction is important because knowledge of etiological risk factors may improve general awareness, but recognition of clinical warning signs may be more directly related to intended action when symptoms occur. However, because actual healthcare-seeking behavior was not measured, these findings should be interpreted as associations with intended responses rather than evidence of actual practice [17, 22].

Although most participants reported an appropriate intended response to severe cyanosis, gaps in recognition of other warning signs remain important. Delayed recognition of CHD has been associated with increased morbidity and poorer clinical outcomes in previous studies [9, 24, 25]. In addition, large epidemiological studies have highlighted the substantial global burden of CHD, underscoring its continuing public health relevance [26, 27]. However, early detection depends not only on parental awareness but also on healthcare-system factors, including screening coverage, professional recognition, referral pathways, and access to pediatric cardiac services [8, 28]. Therefore, parent-focused education through primary healthcare services, schools, community programs, and digital platforms may serve as a supportive component of broader CHD detection and referral strategies. Such education should emphasize recognition of clinically important warning signs while remaining integrated with screening, referral, and specialist cardiac-care pathways.

The practical contribution of this study is primarily implementation-oriented. Some associations, such as those involving prior awareness, self-rated knowledge, education, and actual knowledge scores, are consistent with expected health-literacy patterns. However, the study identifies context-specific gaps in warning-sign recognition, information sources, and intended healthcare-seeking responses among sampled parents in the West Bank. These findings may inform targeted, culturally appropriate CHD education through primary healthcare services, community programs, schools, and digital platforms, while remaining integrated with broader efforts to strengthen screening, referral, and specialist cardiac-care pathways.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it included a relatively large sample of 1,003 parents from different areas of the West Bank, which improved the precision of the estimates and provided useful insight into the sampled population. Second, the study assessed multiple relevant domains, including prior awareness, warning-sign knowledge, risk-factor knowledge, and intended healthcare-seeking responses. Third, the use of composite knowledge scores with acceptable internal consistency and multivariable regression analysis allowed a more structured assessment of factors associated with parental knowledge.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Online recruitment may have overrepresented educated and digitally connected parents; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the sampled West Bank population rather than all Palestinian parents. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and associations between knowledge and intended healthcare-seeking responses should not be interpreted as evidence that knowledge directly changes behavior, diagnostic timing, or clinical outcomes. Third, this study did not evaluate newborn screening, prenatal screening, pulse oximetry screening, referral pathways, access to pediatric cardiac care, or the prevalence of missed or delayed CHD diagnosis in Palestine. Fourth, although the questionnaire underwent expert review, pilot testing, and internal consistency assessment, formal psychometric validation was not performed. Fifth, excluding parents of children with CHD reduced information bias from direct disease experience but limited comparison with a subgroup likely to have higher CHD-specific knowledge. Finally, the study assessed intended healthcare-seeking responses rather than actual healthcare-seeking practices, and self-reported responses may be affected by recall and social desirability bias.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations can be proposed. First, future parental education efforts may benefit from emphasizing recognition of clinically important CHD warning signs, including cyanosis, feeding-related fatigue, excessive sweating during feeding or sleep, poor growth, and syncope. Second, primary healthcare centers may provide an appropriate setting for CHD-related education during antenatal visits, postnatal visits, routine child-health encounters, and vaccination visits. Third, schools and community-based programs may help extend health education to wider family and community networks. Fourth, digital health strategies, including social media campaigns and online educational materials, may be useful because many participants reported using informal or digital sources; however, such materials should be developed or reviewed by healthcare professionals to reduce misinformation. Finally, future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to evaluate whether awareness programs improve actual healthcare-seeking practices and to examine referral delays, screening coverage, and barriers to pediatric cardiac-care access.

Conclusion

This study found variable parental knowledge and awareness of congenital heart disease among sampled parents in the West Bank, Palestine. Knowledge of CHD warning signs was moderate, while knowledge of risk factors was relatively higher. Although many participants reported appropriate intended healthcare-seeking responses, important gaps remained in the recognition of key clinical warning signs. Several sociodemographic and informational factors were associated with knowledge scores, including prior awareness of CHD and self-rated knowledge. Warning-sign knowledge was more strongly associated with intended healthcare-seeking responses than risk-factor knowledge, suggesting that educational efforts may benefit from emphasizing recognition of clinically important symptoms. These findings suggest the need for targeted, multi-channel health education strategies involving primary healthcare services, community-based programs, and digital platforms. However, because this study was cross-sectional and measured intentions rather than actual healthcare-seeking behavior, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal effects on diagnostic delay or clinical outcomes.
