RefRef: A Dataset and Benchmark for Reconstructing Refractive and Reflective Objects
Yue Yin, Enze Tao, Weijian Deng, Dylan Campbell

Abstract
Modern 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis approaches have demonstrated strong performance on scenes with opaque, non-refractive objects. However, most assume straight light paths and therefore cannot properly handle refractive and reflective materials. The lack of datasets specialized for these effects has impeded efforts to fairly and thoroughly evaluate performance and thereby make progress in this domain. Most existing datasets focus on opaque scenes, while those targeting refractive objects are often limited to thin glass with negligible ray bending, untinted single-material objects, or backgrounds treated as infinitely distant, leaving key failure modes under strong refraction untested. To address this gap, we introduce the RefRef dataset. It contains 150 synthetic and 60 real scenes spanning varying levels of geometric complexity and diverse background types that expose the limitations of existing methods. For the purpose of benchmarking, we also provide an oracle method that, given the object geometry and refractive indices, calculates accurate light paths for neural rendering, and a simple two-stage baseline that relaxes these assumptions. We evaluate these against state-of-the-art methods and show that the task is far from solved.

Introduction

Refractive and reflective objects, such as glass and water, pose significant challenges for 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis due to the complex behavior of light as it passes through or reflects off these materials.
Accurate modeling of these optical phenomena is essential for precise 3D reconstruction and photorealistic rendering.
However, most existing neural radiance approaches
[
37
,
3
,
12
,

]
and Gaussian splatting methods
[
28

struggle with refractive or reflective objects
[

These methods typically assume that light travels in a straight path, which is valid for Lambertian objects, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
, but fail in scenarios with complex optical behavior.

A key obstacle hindering the development and evaluation of suitable methods is the lack of datasets that expose the full difficulty of reconstructing scenes with refractive and reflective objects.
Standard 3D reconstruction datasets contain mostly opaque scenes, whereas datasets for refractive objects typically simplify many of the challenges: thin glass produces negligible ray bending; single-material objects omit interfaces between different refractive media; a limited range of object geometries prevents evaluation across diverse shapes; untinted materials omit wavelength-dependent attenuation; and infinitely distant backgrounds mean that the observed color only depends on the direction of the outgoing refracted ray, not its origin.
These simplifications make the problem considerably easier and meet the baked-in assumptions of specialized methods.
Even general-purpose methods perform well since limited view-dependent modeling is sufficient for such scenes.
As a result, these datasets do not reveal how the methods perform under strong refraction, complex geometry, multiple materials, tinting, or diverse backgrounds.

To address this dataset limitation, we introduce RefRef, a dataset and benchmark designed for the task of reconstructing scenes with complex refractive and reflective objects, as shown in
Fig.

1
.
RefRef consists of 150 synthetic scenes with 50 objects and 60 real scenes with 15 objects.
Objects span three categories: convex single-material, nonconvex single-material, and nonconvex multi-material, with varied colors, opacities, and refractive indices.
These categories allow us to evaluate methods across diverse geometric and material configurations with different levels of complexity and varied combinations of refraction, reflection, total internal reflection, and absorption.
Each synthetic object is rendered against two bounded backgrounds and one unbounded background, while each real-world configuration is captured in two indoor and two outdoor environments with varied lighting and capture conditions.
Bounded backgrounds contain finite-depth geometry near the refractive object, so the observed color depends on both the origin and direction of the outgoing refracted ray.
Unbounded backgrounds use infinitely distant environment maps, so the color depends only on the ray direction, which simplifies the problem considerably.
Together, these scenes provide a diverse set of challenges for evaluating 3D reconstruction methods under complex optical effects.

For benchmarking, we also provide an oracle method that traces physically accurate light paths given the ground-truth object geometry and refractive indices, together with a simple two-stage baseline, R3F, that relaxes these requirements by estimating object geometry and properties.
We evaluate existing state-of-the-art methods against these references, and show that existing approaches are largely unable to handle scenes with strongly refractive objects.
Our contributions are:

1.

a dataset of 150 synthetic and 60 real scenes with strongly refractive and reflective objects;

2.

an oracle and a baseline that establish reference performance with and without ground-truth object geometry and refractive indices; and

3.

a benchmark evaluating state-of-the-art methods on this dataset, revealing the limits of existing approaches at handling complex optical phenomena.

Refractive and reflective object datasets.

Several datasets (

The Shiny dataset

Like these datasets, ours is designed to benchmark 3D reconstruction methods for handling refractive and reflective objects, but provides more challenging and diverse scene settings.
It contains 150 synthetic and 60 real scenes, featuring strongly refractive transparent objects, some with multiple materials or tinting, and with bounded backgrounds.
These properties appear in different combinations across scenes, supporting evaluation under varying levels of geometric, material, and optical complexity.

3
The Refractive–Reflective Object Dataset

In this section, we introduce RefRef, a dataset for reconstructing scenes with refractive and reflective objects from posed images, as shown in
Fig.

1
.
It contains 150 synthetic scenes rendered against three background types and 60 real scenes captured in diverse indoor and outdoor environments under different lighting conditions.
As summarized in
Tab.

1
, most existing 3D reconstruction datasets focus on opaque objects.
Those that target transparent objects either contain predominantly thin glass surfaces with negligible refraction or consider volumetric objects under simplified conditions, such as single-material configurations, low-transmittance materials, or distant backgrounds.
In contrast, RefRef includes objects with substantial refractive volumes, highly transparent and tinted materials, both single-material and multi-material configurations, and backgrounds ranging from nearby geometry to distant environments.
These properties appear in different combinations across scenes, introducing varying levels of optical complexity, such as significant light bending, multiple refractions, total internal reflections, and light absorption.

3.1
Dataset Overview and Categories

Geometry and material complexity.

RefRef is organized into three categories according to geometric complexity and the number of refractive materials:

1.

Convex single-material (27 synthetic, 8 real).

This category includes objects with convex geometries, each composed of a single refractive material, such as a glass sphere and a glass pyramid.
These relatively simple geometries provide a controlled setting for evaluating refraction and reflection through a single medium.

2.

Nonconvex single-material (60 synthetic, 4 real).

This category includes objects with nonconvex geometries, each composed of a single refractive material, such as glass sculptures, glass jars, and magnifiers.
Their nonconvex geometries introduce additional challenges, including self-occlusion, multiple surface intersections, and total internal reflection.

3.

Nonconvex multi-material (63 synthetic, 48 real).

This category includes objects with nonconvex geometries and multiple refractive materials, such as reed diffusers, wine bottles, and snow globes.
Differences in refractive indices and colors create nested interfaces, multiple refractions, and light absorption.

Background diversity.

The synthetic subset includes 100 bounded scenes with textured cube or sphere backgrounds and 50 unbounded scenes represented by different HDR environment maps.
The bounded backgrounds contain finite-distance geometry and high-frequency textures, producing parallax and spatially varying refracted patterns.
In contrast, the unbounded backgrounds evaluate methods under the assumption that the background of the scene is infinitely distant from the transparent object.
The real-world subset comprises 60 scenes captured across two indoor and two outdoor environments under different lighting conditions.
The indoor environments include one illuminated by ceiling lights and another captured in a darker room with illumination from a lamp.
The outdoor environments include one captured under direct sunlight with static shadows and another captured after sunset.
Together, these environments provide diverse backgrounds and lighting conditions for evaluating reconstruction methods in both controlled and real-world settings.

Material tinting.

To capture variations in material appearance, RefRef includes both colorless and tinted transparent objects, with 40% of scenes containing tinted materials.
Tinting occurs when the material absorbs part of the light passing through it, making the observed color dependent on both the material properties and the distance traveled within the object.
This creates a challenge for existing methods, many of which do not account for internal absorption.

3.2
Data Collection

The synthetic scenes

are rendered in Blender using the Cycles path tracer
[
6
]
, which models refraction, reflection, total internal reflection, and light absorption.
Each object is placed in one of three background environments:
a bounded cube textured with six high-frequency images randomly selected from a collection of 24;
a bounded (finite-radius) sphere textured with one high-frequency image selected from a collection of 13;
or an unbounded (infinite-radius) spherical environment map randomly selected from a collection of 5, which provides both the background appearance and illumination.
Refractive materials are assigned appropriate transparency properties and refractive indices from a set of six, with tint added where applicable.
Each scene contains 300 posed images at a resolution of
800\times 800
pixels, resulting in 45,000 synthetic images across 150 scenes.
The images are divided into training (used for per-scene fitting), validation, and test sets, each containing 100 views.
Training and validation views are sampled around the object at a fixed radius, while test views follow a separate trajectory with a different radius, preventing evaluation from relying on interpolation between nearby training views.
For each scene, we provide RGB images, camera parameters, object masks, depth maps, object geometry, and the refractive indices used during rendering.
Further preprocessing details, rendering configurations, and object licensing information are in the appendix (
Sec.

A
).

The real scenes

subset contains 60 scenes produced by capturing 15 object configurations across four environments, with each configuration consisting of either a single object or multiple objects, as detailed in the appendix (
Sec.

B
).
For each scene, we record two RGB videos using a DJI OSMO Pocket 4 at a resolution of
4096\times 2160
.
The camera moves around the object while the object and surrounding scene remain stationary.
The first video provides training and validation views, while the second follows a trajectory with a different radius and provides held-out test views.
We evenly sample 100 frames from each trajectory and jointly estimate camera intrinsics and poses for all 200 frames using Agisoft Metashape
[
1
]
, with foreground refractive objects masked out using SAM 3
[
9
]
.
Across all scenes, the estimated poses achieve a subpixel median epipolar error of 0.36 pixels on a set of 3.23M SIFT
[
34
]
feature matches from 25,934 image pairs, indicating high pose accuracy.
The images are downsampled and cropped to
800\times 800
pixels to match the resolution of the synthetic scenes, with camera intrinsics adjusted accordingly.
Of the 100 frames from the first trajectory, 90% are used for training and 10% for validation.

Outdoor scenes are captured under cloudless skies and windless conditions to ensure the scene is near-static throughout acquisition.
For scenes captured in direct sunlight, we ensure that shadows from the camera and operator do not enter the scene.
For liquid-filled objects, we remove visible bubbles and begin recording only after the liquid has completely settled.
Despite these controls, real-world captures still contain surface imperfections, camera reflections, sensor noise, and inaccuracies in the estimated camera poses.
All details are in the appendix (
Sec.

B
).

3.3
Supported Tasks and Applications

RefRef primarily supports 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis of scenes containing refractive and reflective objects.
Our benchmark evaluates novel view synthesis and depth estimation on test views from a held-out trajectory.
Additional annotations also support geometry evaluation, surface normal estimation, transparent object segmentation and detection, material estimation, and light transport modeling.
The synthetic and real scenes further enable research on physically plausible image and video generation, material editing, synthetic-to-real transfer, and robustness across capture conditions, with applications in robotics, augmented reality, and 3D content creation.

4
A Volume Rendering Oracle Method

To evaluate how well reconstruction methods perform on RefRef, we introduce an oracle method as a reference point for the benchmark.
Given posed RGB images, NeRF-based methods learn a volumetric representation of scene geometry and appearance that can render novel views.
However, most methods struggle with scenes involving refractive and reflective objects due to their assumption of straight light paths
[
17
]
.
Our oracle method instead uses the ground-truth geometry and refractive indices to trace refracted and reflected light paths, as shown in
Fig.

2
.
It assumes piecewise linear light paths (
i.e
.
, piecewise constant refractive indices), a single explicit reflection occurring at the first surface intersection, and a maximum of 10 refractions or total internal reflections.
Additional reflections may be modeled implicitly through NeRF’s view-dependent color.

NeRF preliminaries.

The Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) method
[
37
]

represents a scene with a coordinate network
\phi_{\theta}
that maps each 3D sample position
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
and view direction
{\mathbf{d}}
to density
\sigma_{i}
and view-dependent color
{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
.
For a camera ray
{\mathbf{r}}(t)={\mathbf{o}}+t{\mathbf{d}}
, the pixel color is approximated by integrating samples between the near and far planes,

{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{T_{i}(1-\exp(-\sigma_{i}\Delta t_{i}))}_{w_{i}}{\mathbf{c}}_{i},

(1)

where
T_{i}=\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sigma_{j}\Delta t_{j}\right)
denotes the accumulated transmittance up to sample
i
, and
\Delta t_{i}
denotes the distance between consecutive samples.

4.1
Modeling Refractions and Reflections

The oracle method extends Zip-NeRF
[
4
]
to handle refraction and explicit reflection, as shown in
Fig.

2
.
It represents light paths as piecewise linear functions parametrized by
K+1
points
\{{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}
and unit direction vectors
\{{\mathbf{d}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}

{\mathbf{r}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{K}\makebox[1.29167pt][l]{[}\makebox{[}t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})\makebox[1.29167pt][l]{]}\makebox{]}\left({\mathbf{p}}_{i}+(t-\tau_{i}){\mathbf{d}}_{i}\right),

(2)

where
\makebox[1.29167pt][l]{[}\makebox{[}\cdot\makebox[1.29167pt][l]{]}\makebox{]}
is an Iverson bracket,
{\mathbf{p}}_{0}={\mathbf{o}}
,
{\mathbf{d}}_{0}={\mathbf{d}}
and the cumulative distance is given by

\tau_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }i=0\\
\infty&\text{for }i=K+1\\
\sum_{j=1}^{i}\|{\mathbf{p}}_{j}-{\mathbf{p}}_{j-1}\|&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

The method considers two paths: a (multiple) refraction path
{\mathbf{r}}^{\text{{R}}}
and a (single) reflection path
{\mathbf{r}}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
.
We next show how to compute the parameters for each.

Refraction and reflection parameters.

For the refraction ray
{\mathbf{r}}^{\text{{R}}}

{\mathbf{p}}_{i+1}
from position
{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
along direction
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}
is computed using the ground-truth geometry.
Let

\alpha_{i}=\nu_{i}/\nu_{i+1}

\beta_{i}=-{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{p}}_{i+1})

\gamma_{i}^{2}=1-\alpha_{i}^{2}(1-\beta_{i}^{2})

\nu_{i}
be the refractive index of the
i
th
medium, and

be the ground-truth unit normal at location
{\mathbf{x}}

If

{\mathbf{d}}_{i+1}=\alpha_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}+\left(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}\right){\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{p}}_{i+1}).

(4)

{\mathbf{d}}_{i+1}={\mathbf{d}}_{i}-2({\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{p}}_{i+1})){\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{p}}_{i+1}).

(5)

For the reflection ray

4.2
Optimization

The parameters
\theta
of the coordinate network
\phi_{\theta}
are optimized with respect to a photometric loss
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{rgb}}
, an anti-aliased interlevel loss
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{int}}
, and a modified distortion loss
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{dist}}
.
The per-pixel photometric loss is given by

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{rgb}}(\hat{{\mathbf{c}}},{\mathbf{c}})=\frac{1}{3}\|\hat{{\mathbf{c}}}-{\mathbf{c}}\|^{2}_{2},

(7)

where
{\mathbf{c}}
is the ground-truth pixel color.
The interlevel loss
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{int}}

[
4
]
encourages consistency between the proposal network
and the main network.
The original Mip-NeRF 360 distortion loss
[
3
]
encourages a single compact weight peak, reducing floaters for opaque surfaces.
However, for transparent objects, it suppresses object density and proposal samples within the object.
We therefore exclude samples inside the refractive object for this loss,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{dist}}({{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{w}}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{out}}}w_{i}w_{j}\left|\frac{s_{i}+s_{i+1}}{2}-\frac{s_{j}+s_{j+1}}{2}\right|

(8)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{out}}}w_{i}^{2}(s_{i+1}-s_{i}),

where

is the normalized ray distance
[
3
]
to sample point

contains indices outside the refractive object.
This modification retains more high-weight samples within the object, improving reconstruction.
The overall loss is then

{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\text{rgb}}+\lambda_{1}{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{int}}+\lambda_{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{dist}},

(9)

where

4.3
R3F: A Simple Two-Stage Baseline

Alongside the oracle, we introduce a simple two-stage baseline that relaxes the requirements for ground-truth object geometry and refractive index, providing a second point of reference for the benchmark.
R3F (Refractive–Reflective Radiance Field) relaxes the oracle’s ground-truth requirements with a two-stage reconstruction pipeline.
First, the geometry and refractive index of the object are estimated using NU-NeRF
[
44
]
.
Then, a background radiance field is optimized using Zip-NeRF
[
4
]
with foreground rays masked out using object masks predicted by SAM 3
[
9
]
.
Finally, a separate in-object field is optimized using the oracle’s ray tracing, with the background field frozen, preventing artifacts like floaters outside the object, which are otherwise common when modeling refractive and reflective objects
[
4
,
16
,
17
,
63
]
.
While effective,
R3F’s performance entirely depends on the quality of the geometry prediction.

Metrics.

We evaluate novel view synthesis using the
peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), which measures the overall pixel-level fidelity,
masked PSNR (PSNR
M
), which restricts evaluation to the refractive object so that the background does not dominate,
structural similarity index measure (SSIM), which measures the structural consistency of refracted patterns,
and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS), which is more sensitive to blur than PSNR.
To assess geometric fidelity, we report the distance mean absolute error (DMAE) between the estimated and ground-truth distance maps, which reveals whether accurate appearance is accompanied by accurate geometry, since some methods may reproduce refracted appearances by deforming the geometry or introducing floaters.
Importantly, standard weighted-sum distance rendering techniques
[
37
]
are not reliable for refractive objects, since weights may be distributed across multiple refractive surfaces and the opaque background.
Therefore, we take the median distance of the weight samples along each ray, as a robust estimate of the distance to the nearest (potentially translucent) surface, avoiding an average over disconnected surfaces.

These consistent failures demonstrate that real-world refractive reconstruction remains far from solved.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we present RefRef, a dataset and benchmark comprising 150 synthetic and 60 real scenes for 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis of refractive and reflective objects.
It combines diverse geometries, material compositions, and tinting with bounded and unbounded synthetic backgrounds and indoor and outdoor real-world environments, allowing us to reveal the failure modes of existing methods across diverse challenges.
As reference points for the benchmark, we introduce an oracle method using ground-truth geometry and refractive indices and a simple two-stage baseline using estimated geometry. Benchmarking state-of-the-art methods, including those designed for nonlinear light paths, revealed significant performance gaps.
This shows that the task remains far from solved, highlighting the value of RefRef as a challenging benchmark against which to develop more reliable methods.

Synthetic Scene Rendering Details

We render all synthetic scenes using the Cycles path tracer in Blender
[
6
]
, which employs Monte Carlo integration to solve the rendering equation using BSDFs (Bidirectional Scattering Distribution Functions)
[
27
]
. Light transport is approximated by stochastically tracing paths of light rays and recursively evaluating their interactions with surfaces and transmissive materials:

\displaystyle L_{o}(x,\omega_{o})

\displaystyle=L_{e}(x,\omega_{o})

(10)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}f_{s}(x,\omega_{i},\omega_{o})\,L_{i}(x,\omega_{i})\,|\omega_{i}\cdot n|\,d\omega_{i},

where
L_{o}(x,\omega_{o})
is the radiance leaving point
x
in direction
\omega_{o}
,
L_{e}
is the emitted radiance,
f_{s}
is the BSDF,
L_{i}
is the incoming radiance from direction
\omega_{i}
,
n
is the surface normal at
x
, and
\Omega
is the unit sphere of incoming directions. The absolute cosine term
|\omega_{i}\cdot n|
accounts for the cosine falloff of incoming light. Unlike the BRDF, the BSDF
f_{s}
accounts for both surface reflection and transmission, which is necessary for modeling materials such as glass and transparent plastics used in our scenes.

We model the scenes using physically realistic materials provided by Blender’s Principled BSDF shader, specifying material parameters for glass, metal, and dielectric plastics. Object placements, lighting, and camera positions are randomized to encourage variation across samples. To ensure rendering quality:

•

Adaptive sampling
[
8
]
is enabled to improve rendering efficiency by dynamically adjusting the number of samples per pixel based on estimated noise. Pixels with low variance receive fewer samples, while high-frequency regions are sampled more densely. We set the noise threshold to 0.05, which defines the per-pixel error tolerance for terminating sampling. The minimum number of samples is set to 0, allowing Cycles to automatically determine this value from the threshold. The maximum number of samples per pixel is capped at 600.

•

The maximum number of light bounces is set to 12 to support complex global illumination. This includes up to 4 diffuse bounces for soft indirect light, 4 glossy bounces for specular reflections, 12 transmission bounces to handle multiple refractive interfaces, and 8 transparent bounces for rays passing through alpha-masked geometry. Volume scattering is disabled (volume bounces = 0) since no participating media are present in our scenes.

•

To reduce bright noise artifacts (“fireflies”), we clamp both direct and indirect light contributions to 1.0. We also enable reflective and refractive caustics to preserve high-frequency light transport effects such as focused reflections or refractions.

•

The Glossy Filter is set to 0.0 to retain sharp caustic and highlight details without blurring.

B
Real Scene Capture Details

Object configurations and environments.

The real-world subset contains 60 scenes, obtained by capturing 15 object configurations in four environments.
The configurations comprise a glass sphere, pyramid, rabbit, bulb-shaped bottle, dishwashing-liquid bottle, two different snow globes, cocktail shaker, measuring jug, perfume bottle, perfume set, glass of water, glass of water with a straw, wine bottle, and wine-bottle set.
The perfume set and wine-bottle set contain multiple objects, while the remaining configurations contain a single object.
The four environments include two outdoor and two indoor settings.
The outdoor settings are a courtyard under direct sunlight and a lookout deck captured after sunset, while the indoor settings are a bright living room illuminated by ceiling lights and a darker room containing a puzzle table illuminated by a lamp.
Each object configuration is captured once in every environment, yielding
15\times 4=60
scenes.

Image acquisition.

For each scene, we record two RGB videos using a DJI OSMO Pocket 4 at a resolution of
4096\times 2160
.
The camera moves around the object while the object and surrounding environment remain stationary.
The first video follows the training trajectory, while the second follows a trajectory with a different radius and is used for testing.
This separation reduces evaluation based on interpolation between nearby training views.
We uniformly sample 100 frames from each video.
Of the 100 frames from the first trajectory, 90 are used for training and 10 for validation, while all 100 frames from the second trajectory are used for testing.
The images are downsampled and center-cropped to
800\times 800
pixels to match the synthetic scenes, and the camera intrinsics are adjusted accordingly.

Camera calibration and pose estimation.

We jointly estimate the camera parameters of all 200 frames using Agisoft Metashape
[
1
]
.
Joint estimation places the training and test cameras in a common coordinate system.
During image alignment, the foreground refractive objects are excluded using masks generated by SAM 3
[
9
]
.
This prevents view-dependent refractions and reflections from being treated as stationary features during feature matching.

Capture controls.

Outdoor scenes are captured under cloudless skies and windless conditions to minimize changes in illumination and background motion.
For captures under direct sunlight, we ensure that shadows cast by the camera and operator do not enter the scene.
For liquid-filled objects, visible bubbles are removed and recording begins only after the liquid has completely settled.
Despite these controls, the real scenes retain realistic imperfections, including surface defects, reflections of the camera, sensor noise, and residual pose-estimation errors.

Pose-consistency evaluation.

We assess pose consistency using SIFT
[
34
]

correspondences from the static background.
Image pairs are selected using viewing-angle and camera-baseline filters, including a
60^{\circ}
viewing-angle criterion.
We exclude features within dilated foreground-object masks, match SIFT descriptors using bidirectional brute-force
L_{2}
search, and retain matches that pass Lowe’s ratio test and a mutual consistency check.
The matched points are undistorted using the estimated camera distortion parameters.
We then derive the fundamental matrix directly from the estimated intrinsics and poses
[
21
]
, after converting from the OpenGL to OpenCV camera convention, and compute the symmetric point-to-epipolar-line distance for every match.
We estimate fundamental matrices using USAC-MAGSAC
[
43
,
2
]
.
Across all scenes, we obtain 3.23 million valid correspondences from 25,934 image pairs, with a median epipolar error of 0.36 pixels.

C
Additional Methodological Details

In this section, we provide additional details on our sampling strategy and how updated sample points are processed for density and color prediction in our oracle method.
These extensions complement the core concepts outlined in the main paper and focus on implementation-specific details.

Sampling strategy.

We begin by generating an initial straight ray
{\mathbf{r}}(t)={\mathbf{o}}+t{\mathbf{d}}
, which is then split into two paths: a refraction path
{\mathbf{r}}^{\text{{R}}}
and a reflection path
{\mathbf{r}}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
.
To sample along these paths, we adopt the proposal sampling strategy introduced in Zip-NeRF
[
4
]
, which builds upon the hierarchical sampling approach of Mip-NeRF 360
[
3
]
. Zip-NeRF samples inside a cone following a spiral path, we update the center line of the cone while preserving the sampling pattern. It starts with a uniform sampler that generates
N
sample points
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
along the initial straight ray.
These points are then updated, resulting in new positions
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{{R}}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
with corresponding direction vectors
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\text{{R}}}
and
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
, which follow curved paths for refractions and reflections, respectively.
Next, the density
\sigma_{i}
and weight
w_{i}
of each sample point along these curved paths are computed.
These weights, which represent the contribution of each sample, are further passed through a probability density function sampler to concentrate samples in higher density regions to enhance the accuracy of rendering.

Processing updated sample points.

After each sampling stage, the updated sample point positions
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{{R}}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
are processed through a spatial encoding function
\xi
for efficient representation.
These encoded spatial points are then fed into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
f_{\theta}
, which predicts the scene density
\sigma_{i}
.
To obtain the predicted color at each 3D position, another MLP
g_{\theta}
processes the refined viewing directions
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\text{{R}}}
and
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\text{\reflectbox{R}}}
, encoded using spherical harmonics
\psi
, alongside the computed density
\sigma_{i}
.
This yields the emitted color
{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=(R,G,B)
for each sample point.

For optimizing the oracle method, we use an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of

,

E
Hyperparameters

In this section, we detail the hyperparameter settings used for training the models evaluated in our experiments.
The configurations, including learning rates, optimizers, and scheduler settings, were carefully chosen to ensure stable convergence and performance across different methods.
Specific hyperparameter choices for the evaluated approaches are described below.

For MS-NeRF
[
63
]
and RoseNeRF
[
32
]
, both the proposal networks and the field optimizer utilize an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
4\times 10^{-3}
,
\epsilon=1\times 10^{-15}
, and an exponential decay scheduler that reduces the learning rate to
1\times 10^{-4}
over
2\times 10^{5}
steps.
For Zip-NeRF
[
4
]
, the model optimizer utilizes a default configuration with an Adam optimizer set to an initial learning rate of
8\times 10^{-3}
,
\epsilon=1\times 10^{-15}
, and an exponential decay scheduler reducing the learning rate to
1\times 10^{-3}
over
2.5\times 10^{4}
steps, with 1000 warm-up steps.
For Ray Deformation Network
[
17
]
, both the proposal networks and the field optimizer utilize an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
2\times 10^{-3}
,
\epsilon=1\times 10^{-15}
, and an exponential decay scheduler reducing the learning rate to
1\times 10^{-4}
over
1\times 10^{5}
steps.
For NeuS
[
52
]
and TNSR
[
16
]
, the proposal networks utilize a default configuration with an Adam optimizer set to an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-2}
,
\epsilon=1\times 10^{-15}
, and a multi-step decay scheduler reducing the learning rate by a factor
\gamma=0.33
every milestone over
2\times 10^{4}
steps. The milestones are at 10000, 15000, and 18000 steps, respectively.
For the field optimizer, we use an initial learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
, and a cosine decay scheduler with 500 warm-up steps and learning rate peak value set to
5\times 10^{-2}
.
For NeRRF
[
13
]
, the optimization is conducted in two stages.
In the first stage, the model is optimized for 30 epochs with each epoch repeated 10 times.
In the second stage, the model is optimized for 8 epochs with each epoch repeated 100 times.
The gradient accumulation step is set to 1 in both stages.
For NU-NeRF
[
44
]
, we adopt the default configuration.
The optimization runs for
2\times 10^{5}
iterations in stage 1 and
1.5\times 10^{5}
iterations in stage 2.
We also include near and far planes implementation, which are set to 0.1 and 7.5, respectively, and apply a scene scale factor of 0.5.
We have also included a 3D Gaussian splatting approach, Splatfacto
[
28
]
, which is implemented in Nerfstudio
[
46
]
. For Splatfacto, we used the default configurations in Nerfstudio.

F
Extended Experimental Results

In this section, we present additional comparative results on both synthetic and real scenes from our RefRef test set, along with an ablation study of the oracle method.

Additional results.

We present comparative qualitative results on synthetic scenes in
Fig.

5
and
Fig.

6
, and show the results on real scenes in
Fig.

7
.
For synthetic scenes, the patterned backgrounds are more challenging due to the presence of nearby complex textures, where most methods struggle, especially in handling multiple refractions and total internal reflection.
These methods are unable to reconstruct the highly detailed patterns, either blurry or incorrect.
In contrast, the oracle and R3F methods are better able to capture these complex light interactions.
The HDR environment map background is relatively easier, and most methods perform well on simple geometries.
However, their performance fluctuates significantly on more complex shapes.
For real scenes, it is obvious to see that all methods struggle a lot compared to the synthetic scenes, which reveals that this task is far from solved.
Moreover, we include the full table that compares several state-of-the-art methods in
Tab.

5
, and geometry visualization in
Fig.

8
.

Ablation study.

An ablation study is presented in
Tab.

4
, comparing the full oracle method with four ablated versions: without the corrected distortion loss, first surface reflection, total internal reflection (TIR), and replacing the Zip-NeRF
[
4
]
backbone with Nerfacto
[
46
]
.
The full oracle method accurately models both reflections and TIR, producing highly detailed renderings and the best image quality across all metrics.
Removing the corrected distortion loss causes only a marginal drop in image quality.
Omitting the first surface reflection leads to the loss of subtle reflective details and lower image quality, although it yields a lower DMAE, while disabling TIR results in missing critical light interactions within refractive objects, and replacing the backbone causes the largest performance drop.
These results highlight the contribution of each component to rendering quality, with reflection, TIR, and the Zip-NeRF backbone being the most important.
