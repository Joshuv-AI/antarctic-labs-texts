Curcumin-loaded oil body emulsions prepared by an ultrasonic and pH-driven method: Fundamental properties, stability, and digestion characteristics
Authors: Jianyu Zhu, Huan Wang, Liming Miao, Ning Chen, Qing Zhang, Ziheng Wang, Fengying Xie, Baokun Qi, Lianzhou Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Oil bodies (OBs) are special tissues that store oil in plant cells and consist of triacylglycerols (TAG), phospholipids, proteins, and other trace components. TAG forms a hydrophobic core surrounded by an external phospholipid monolayer membrane embedded with surface proteins, and their particle sizes range from approximately 0.2 to 0.5 μm [1]. Based on their unique structure, OBs can be dispersed in a water phase to form a natural oil-in-water (O/W) emulsification system without using emulsifiers and high-pressure homogenization. Moreover, the system possesses better stability against oxidation and environmental pressure than artificial emulsions [2], [3]. Liu et al. [4] showed that the physical and chemical stability of OB emulsions could be further improved by modulating the interface properties of the OB droplet (including composition, structure, and charge distribution). Huang et al. [5] reported that the antioxidant groups in OB proteins had the ability to maintain oxidation stability. Natural OBs have attracted significant interest, and further development and utilization of OB emulsions warrant further research.

Soybean OBs can be designed as natural delivery systems for emerging bioactive compounds and have attracted increasing attention because of their unique membrane structure, selective permeability, and excellent carrier properties [6]. Acevedo et al. [7] developed an OB delivery system to protect bioactive substances from degradation by air and light and improve their stability. Liu et al. [8] prepared a soybean OB delivery system with excellent gastrointestinal digestion characteristics. Wu et al. [9] designed a soybean OB emulsion combined with carrageenan to control the gastrointestinal release of encapsulated compounds.

Curcumin (Cur) is a naturally active polyphenol widely used in food systems [10]. However, the application of Cur in food matrices is restricted by its low water solubility, chemical instability, and bioavailability [11]. Some studies have shown that OB emulsions can be used as simple templates to encapsulate hydrophobic Cur and improve its bioaccessibility and stability [10], [12]. Research has reported that the water solubility of Cur can be increased by a short-term strong alkaline treatment, with negligible degradation. Subsequently, as the pH decreases, the solubility of Cur decreases, and Cur is driven to the hydrophobic interior of the OB [13]. Thus, the pH-driven strategy is a simple, inexpensive, and solvent-free method to load Cur to OB [2], [14].

The emulsification method has an important influence on the properties of the resulting emulsion. Ultrasonication is a green, safe, and efficient technology widely used in food processing [15]. Unlike other high-energy methods, its special cavitation effect has many potential benefits for emulsions, such as small droplet size, narrow particle size distribution, and great emulsion stability [16]. Ultrasonic treatment can also be used to modify the structures of proteins, further enhancing their functional properties [17]. Wang et al. [18] found that ultrasonic treatment at a power of 400 W significantly improved emulsion stability. Li et al. [19] also showed that ultrasonic treatment improved the digestibility of casein/phosphatidylcholine complex emulsions. Sun et al. [20] explored the influence of different ultrasonic sequences on the structural characteristics of emulsions. However, the effect of ultrasonication and pH-driven on the OBs loaded with Cur has not been studied.

This study aimed to develop an efficient delivery system to improve the stability of Cur. The OB emulsions loaded with Cur were prepared by an ultrasonic and pH-driven method, and the effects of ultrasonic power on the physicochemical properties, microstructure, stability, and digestion characteristics of the OB-Cur emulsions were investigated. This study provides a new approach for loading hydrophobic compounds into OB delivery systems. It is of great significance to offer nutrients to OBs and expand their applications in food processing.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Mature soybeans (Dongnong 46) were provided by the Soybean Research Institute of Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China) for OB extraction. Curcumin (CAS No. 458–37-7) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sucrose was purchased from Tianjin Komiou Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China). All reagents were of analytical grade. Deionized (DI) water was used in all experiments.

Extraction of soybean OBs

Soybean OBs were extracted according to the method described by Zhu et al. [21]. Soaked soybeans were ground to collect the filtrate, which was mixed with sucrose at a ratio of 4:1 (w/w). The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 11.0. The mixture was then centrifuged at 25000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant OBs-rich layer was collected, redispersed in the 20 % (w/w) sucrose solution, and centrifuged for further purification. The cream layer was recovered and washed with DI water to obtain pure OB.

Preparation of Cur-loaded OBs and OB emulsions

Cur was loaded into OBs via ultrasonic treatment combined with pH-driven methods to produce a colloidal delivery system [2]. The pure OB system was obtained using a Scientz-IID ultrasonic homogenizer (NingBo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) at different ultrasonic power (0–250 W) for 10 min (2 s on/ 4 s off) at room temperature in the dark. An ultrasonic probe (6.36 mm) was placed 1 cm from the bottom and operated at a frequency of 20 kHz. Cur alkaline solution (5 mg/g) was added to the OBs (1:10, w/w), and the pH was immediately adjusted to 7.0. Finally, the samples were centrifuged to collect the supernatant yellow layer to obtain Cur-loaded OBs.

OB emulsions were prepared by dispersing 10 g of Cur-loaded OB samples (wet weight) in 90 g of DI water, followed by homogenization at 4000 rpm for 5 min.

Extraction of Soybean OB protein (OBP)

OBP was extracted using modified methods of Sun et al. [20]. Briefly, the collected Cur-loaded OBs were washed three times with acetone and chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v) to discard lipids and phospholipids, respectively, followed by purging nitrogen gas to remove residual organic solvents. The OBP was freeze-dried and stored for further analysis.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC)

EE and LC were determined referred to Zhang et al. [2]. Briefly, 80 % ethanol was mixed with OBs and centrifuged. The absorbance of Cur was measured at 426 nm on a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-5800, Shanghai Metash Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The Cur concentration was determined by the standard curve. The EE and LC were calculated using Eqs. (1), (2):(1)EE(%)=EncapsulatedCur/TotalCur×100(2)LC(%)=EncapsulatedCur/TotalmassofOB×100

Particle size and ζ-potential

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential of Cur-loaded OB emulsions were evaluated by a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern Instruments Co., Ltd., Worcestershire, UK).

Color properties

The color properties were evaluated using a colorimeter (CR-400. Konica Minolta, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The total color difference (ΔE) was quantified using Eq. (3) [14]:(3)ΔE=L∗-L2+a∗-a2+b∗-b2

where L (lightness), a (redness and greenness), and b (yellowness and blueness) represent the color indices of the white calibration plate (L = 94.26, a = 0.69, and b = 0.80).

Microstructure observations

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The droplets after ultrasonic treatment were observed using CLSM (FV 3000, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The samples were stained with fluorescent colorant solutions (Nile Red and Nile Blue). Microscopic images were recorded using selective excitation wavelengths at 488 nm and 633 nm.

Cryo-scanning electron microscopy (Cryo-SEM)

The microstructure of the emulsion droplets was observed on an SU8010 cryo-SEM (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at −140 °C with an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV [2].

Circular dichroism (CD)

Quantitative analysis of the secondary structure of OBP was carried out using a CD spectrometer (Chirascan V100, Applied Photophysics Ltd., Leatherhead, UK) referred to Deng et al. [15]. 0.4 mg/mL OBP solution was placed in a quartz CD cuvette (path length of 1 mm), and the spectrum was recorded with 1.0 nm bandwidth, 100 nm/min scanning speed, and 190–260 nm wavelength range.

Stability evaluation

Creaming index (CI)

The CI was used to evaluate storage stability of the emulsions. Cur-loaded OB emulsions were stored at 25 °C for 15 days, and the stratification of the emulsions was recorded. The CI was measured according to Surh et al. [22] and calculated by Eq. (4):(4)CI(%)=HS/HT×100

where HS represents the height of the serum layer, and HT represents the total height of the suspension.

Oxidative stability

Freshly prepared emulsions were stored at 25 °C for 2 weeks in the dark, and recorded the primary and secondary oxidation products every 2 days.

Peroxide value (POV) was calculated referred to Wang et al. [18] using a cumene hydroperoxide standard curve based on the absorbance at 510 nm.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) were measured according to Hu et al. [5]. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm, and 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane was used as the standard curve for calculations.

Photochemical stability

According to Han et al. [23], Cur content after the emulsion was exposed to ultraviolet (UV) irradiation (0.35 W/m2) for 120 min at 25 °C was evaluated. The Cur retention concentration was measured at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min, as described in Section 2.5. Cur dissolved in medium chain triglycerides (MCT) oil was set as MCT group. The retention rate and degradation rate constant (k) of Cur were calculated according to Eqs. (5), (6), respectively.(5)Retentionrate%=C/C0×100(6)Ln(C/C0)=-kt

where C0 and C are the Cur concentrations at time 0 and t, respectively, and k is the degradation rate constant.

Thermal stability

The thermal stability of the Cur-loaded emulsions was determined using the method described by Zheng et al. [14]. Briefly, OB emulsions loaded with Cur were stored in darkness for 15 days at 4, 20, 37, and 55 °C, and then were analyzed periodically, according to Section 2.10.3. The Arrhenius equation (Eq. (7)) was used to calculate the degradation kinetics of Cur in the emulsions during storage, and the Cur degradation activation energy (Ea) was estimated.(7)k=Ae-Ea/RT

where k is the rate coefficient, A is the Arrhenius constant, R is the universal gas constant (8.314 × 10-3 kJ•mol−1•K−1), and T is the temperature in Kelvin.

In vitro digestion

The INFOGEST protocol was used to simulate the in vitro gastrointestinal digestion behavior referred to Zhang et al. [2]. All simulated digestive fluids (saliva, stomach, and intestine) and digestive mixtures were incubated at 37 °C. For oral digestion, OB emulsions were mixed with simulated salivary fluids (SSF). The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 6.8, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 min. For gastric digestion, the oral samples were mixed with simulated gastric fluids (SGF) containing pepsin at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v). The mixture was adjusted to pH 3.0 and incubated for 2 h. For intestinal digestion, the gastric samples were mixed with simulated intestinal fluids (SIF) at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v). Pancreatin, lipase, and bile salts were added to the mixture at final concentrations of 100, 2000 U/mL, and 0.01 mol/L, respectively. Subsequently, the mixture was incubated for 2 h at pH 7.0.

Particle size and ζ-potential during in vitro digestion

The particle size and ζ-potential of the emulsion during digestion were measured, as shown in Section 2.6.

Fatty acid release rate

The released free fatty acids (FFA) were determined using the pH-stat method and Eq. (8) [24].(8)FFA%=VNaOH×MNaOH×Mlipid2×Wlipid

where VNaOH, MNaOH, Mlipid, and Wlipid are the volume of NaOH (mL), molarity of NaOH (mol/L), molecular weight of soy oil (0.874 kg/mol), and total mass of the oil (g), respectively.

The kinetic parameters for the initial release of FFA were determined using Eq. (9) [24].(9)Lnφmax-φt/φmax=-kt

where φmax, φt, t, and k are the maximum FFA release (%), the FFA release (%) at digestion time t (min), the intestinal digestion time (min), and the first-order rate constant for FFA release (min−1), respectively.

Bioaccessibility and stability

The simulated digestion samples were centrifuged at 12000 × g for 30 min and the micelle phase was collected. The Cur concentrations were calculated as described in Section 2.5. According to a modified method described by Zheng et al. [14], the bioaccessibility and stability of Cur were calculated using Eqs. (10), (11).(10)Bioaccessibility%=Cmicelle/Cdigest×100(11)Stability%=Cdigest/Cinitial×100

where Cmicelle, Cdigest, and Cinitial are the Cur concentrations in the micelle phase, after intestinal digestion, and in the initial stage, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times, and the data were expressed as mean value ± standard deviation. Means were compared using one-way analysis of variance and Duncan's test (P < 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results and discussion

EE and LC analysis

Fig. 1A shows the EE and LC of Cur-loaded OBs prepared by the ultrasonic-assisted pH-driven method. The EE and LC of the control group (no ultrasonic treatment) were only 68.96 % and 0.034 %, respectively. Considering the water solubility of Cur changed with pH, the pH-driven method could successfully load Cur into OBs [14]. The loaded Cur tended to diffuse to the hydrophobic core rather than interact with the OB membranes because it had a high affinity for TAG and a low steric hindrance effect [25]. With the increasing ultrasonic power, EE and LC were significantly (P < 0.05) enhanced and reached 88.27 % and 0.044 %, respectively, at 200 W. Ultrasound raised the temperature and the pressure in the OB systems, and the cavitation effect could unfold the protein structure in OBs and facilitate the diffusion of Cur into the oil phase [14], [26]. However, a further increase in ultrasonic power reduced the EE and LC because it caused structural damage to the OBs. These results indicated that an appropriate ultrasonic power level was beneficial for effective Cur encapsulation.Fig. 1Fundamental properties of OBs loaded with Cur. Particle size and ζ-potential analysis

Particle size and PDI are important parameters indicating the degree of droplet aggregation and the stability of the emulsion in the production process [15]. The correlation between ultrasonic power and average particle size, PDI, and particle size distribution of the OBs are shown in Fig. 1(B–C). With the increasing ultrasonic power, the average particle size and PDI first increased and then decreased, and the size distribution of all samples was unimodal. When the power reached 150 W and 200 W, the difference in average particle size and PDI became insignificant (P > 0.05), and the minimum values were achieved at 200 W (358.18 nm and 0.065, respectively). The cavitation effect, turbulence, and strong shear force induced by the ultrasonic treatment could destroy the aggregation and evenly disperse the OBs, enhancing the stability of the emulsion [27]. When the ultrasonic power was further increased to 250 W, the average particle size and PDI increased, and the particle size distribution was widened, likely due to the aggregation of small droplets under intense cavitation, as reported by Wang et al. [18]. Moreover, some OBs were damaged by the thermal effect of the ultrasonic treatment and aggregated, also resulting in large particle sizes and uneven distribution [18].

ζ-potential represents the degree of electrostatic attraction or repulsion between droplets, which is also related to the stability of the colloidal system. Fig. 1D shows the ζ-potentials of Cur-loaded OBs prepared with different ultrasonic power levels. The ζ-potential absolute value significantly increased and reached a maximum (-25.71 mV) at 200 W. Ultrasonic treatment unfolded the dense structure of OB proteins and changed the surface charge distribution, which further enhanced the electrostatic repulsion between droplets and limited aggregation. Thus, the stability of the emulsion was improved, consistent with the particle size results [27]. When the ultrasonic power was further increased to 250 W, the ζ-potential absolute value decreased because the structure of OBs was destroyed, and the surface charge on the droplet was reduced under the high ultrasonic power [28].

OB loaded with Cur had a lower a* value and a higher b* value, indicating that the yellow-green color was deepened. It has been reported that Cur crystals can selectively absorb more scattered light waves, showing more intense yellow and green colors [12]. Therefore, Cur is the most important color factor in the entire system, determining its appearance (Table 1). The appearance of the OBs could be associated with the EE and LC of Cur [29]. Moreover, all OB samples had relatively high L* values because of the strong light-scattering effect of the oil droplets. Microstructure observations

OBs loaded with Cur were observed using CLSM and cryo-SEM. The CLSM images are shown in Fig. 2(A–E). The neutral lipids inside the OBs were stained with Nile red and showed red fluorescence, and the surface proteins of the OBs were stained with Nile blue and showed green fluorescence [31]. CLSM observations revealed that all droplets were circular, smooth, and uniformly distributed, confirming the feasibility of loading Cur into OBs using the ultrasonic-assisted pH-driven method. The CLSM images showed significant differences for the samples prepared with different ultrasonic power. The most uniform OB distribution was observed at the optimal ultrasonic power (200 W), which agreed with the particle size results [32]. When the ultrasonic power was increased to 250 W (Fig. 2E), flocculation and aggregation occurred owing to the destruction of the interface by excessive ultrasonic treatment [33]. This result was confirmed by the size distribution (Fig. 1C).Fig. 2CLSM (A–E) and cryo-SEM (F–J) images of OBs loaded with Cur. The scales of A1–E1 and A2–E2 are 50 μm and 20 μm, respectively. Figrrr. 2(F–J) shows the cryo-SEM images of OBs prepared with different ultrasonic power. The OBs loaded with Cur exhibited sphere or ellipsoid morphologies, which were comparable with the OBs without ultrasonic treatment (Fig. 2F). The size of the OBs after ultrasonic treatment was relatively small and more uniform, and 200 W ultrasonic power was associated with the smallest particle size and the most uniform distribution (Fig. 2I), consistent with the particle size analysis results. Therefore, an appropriate ultrasonic power could generate nonlinear acoustic pressure pulsations to break bubbles, reduce droplet size, and narrow the particle size distribution, further stabilizing the OB emulsions [33]. When the ultrasonic power reached 250 W, the OB partially aggregated into a bigger particle size (Fig. 2J). The aggregation could be attributed to two reasons. First, excessive ultrasonic treatment reduced the protein distribution density at the oil–water interface, further altering the intermolecular force and inducing aggregation. Second, the strong shear force generated by the high ultrasonic power destroyed the OB structure, leading to aggregation and uneven distribution [34]. Moreover, it was observed that thick and dense surface protein interface coating on the surrounding OBs (Fig. 2F2), and still completely adsorbed on the interface and firmly coated the oil droplets under ultrasonic treatment, which could be helpful to form stable OB emulsions [35], consistent with above CLSM results (Fig. 2(A–E)).

CD analysis

Conformational changes in secondary protein structures depend on various factors, such as protein properties, denaturation degree, aggregation state, and ultrasonic conditions [36]. CD is a highly sensitive method to detect such changes. The secondary structures of OB surface proteins are composed of α-helices, β-sheets, β-turns and random coils, and their structures are closely related to the emulsifying properties [15]. The CD spectra and the relative contents of OB secondary structures subjected to various ultrasonic treatments are shown in Fig. 3A and Table 2, respectively. Nearly 60 % of the secondary protein structures were elastic ordered structures (α-helixs and β-sheets). Under ultrasonic treatment, the α-helix contents decreased, and the other secondary structures slightly increased, consistent with the results reported by Jiang et al. [37]. With the increase of ultrasonic power from 0 to 250 W, α-helix contents decreased by 1.7 % (P < 0.05), while β-sheet contents increased by 1.1 % (P < 0.05). Stability characteristic analysis

CI analysis

CI is used to illustrate emulsion stability during storage; the lower the CI, the better the storage stability. Fig. 3B shows the time-dependent CIs of OB emulsions prepared with different ultrasonic power. The CI tended to increase with time, with the control group showing the highest CI, suggesting the worst stability during storage. The stability was significantly improved in the samples subjected to ultrasonic treatment. When the power was 200 W, the lowest CI value indicated the highest storage stability. The correlation between CI and the ultrasonic power could be explained by the following three reasons. Firstly, the cavitation effects of ultrasonication caused strong repulsion between droplets, similar to the ζ-potential results, which effectively inhibited the coalescence and phase separation [38]. Secondly, according to Stokes' law, ultrasonic treatment reduced droplet size and enhanced emulsion stability [39]. Finally, appropriate ultrasonic treatment facilitated the formation of a interface layer, which provided sufficient steric hindrance for the OB emulsions to maintain stability [40]. After 14 days of storage, the CI increased significantly, suggesting that the stability of the emulsions declined. Notably, the CI of the OB emulsion treated with 250 W (21.51 %) was higher than that of the 200 W (14.49 %), suggesting that excessive ultrasonic treatment was unfavorable for the formation of stable oil–water interfaces.

Oxidative stability analysis

The oxidation stability of the OB emulsions was evaluated by monitoring the lipid oxidation of primary (POV) and secondary products (TBARS) during storage. The changes in POV and TBARS during storage are shown in Fig. 3(C–D). The POV and TBARS of all samples increased significantly (P < 0.05) with storage time. Initially, all samples had similar POV and TBARS values (P > 0.05). Lipid oxidation occurred at the fastest rate in the control group, especially after two days of storage. And ultrasonic treatment significantly reduced the formation rates of POV and TBARS, indicating better oxidation stability during storage. When the power was 200 W, the lowest POV and TBARS values were 3.43 mmol/kg and 1.04 μmol/kg, respectively, suggesting the best oxidation stability, which agreed with the findings reported by Li et al. [27]. The explanation for the inhibition of oil oxidation by ultrasonic treatment is as follows. First, ultrasonic treatments strengthened the steric hindrance between the aqueous phase and the lipid phase, further inhibiting lipid oxidation to a certain extent and improving oxidation stability [15]. Second, ultrasonic treatments changed the protein structure, as shown in the CD spectral analysis, and exposed more antioxidant groups, allowing more free radical scavenging to inhibit lipid oxidation [5]. Whereas, when the power was 250 W, POV and TBARS increased to 4.04 mmol/kg and 1.27 μmol/kg, respectively, because the strong ultrasonic power promoted the formation of highly active free radicals, decreasing the oxidative stability [27].

Photochemical stability analysis

Owing to the photosensitivity of Cur, it can be rapidly degraded into vanillin, vanillic acid, ferulic aldehyde, and ferulic acid, reducing its biological activity and limiting its application [41]. Therefore, the photochemical stability of Cur was evaluated by exposing the OB samples to ultraviolet radiation. As shown in Fig. 4A, the retention rate of Cur in all samples decreased significantly (P < 0.05) under UV irradiation. The retention rate of Cur was lowest in MCT oil (13.86 %) because free Cur molecules were directly exposed to UV irradiation without light-scattering effects [23]. In OB emulsions, the Cur retention rate increased because the interfacial layers of OBs could provide protective barriers against UV radiation. In addition, the protein molecules also had some free radical scavenging activity; therefore, the whole sample presented excellent photochemical stability [42], [43].Fig. 4Photochemical stability of OB emulsions. Thermal stability analysis

Heat treatment is commonly used in food processing and may cause Cur degradation and reduce its biological activity [23]. The dependence of the Cur retention rate on temperature is shown in Fig. 5(A1–D1). The degradation rate of Cur in all the samples increased with temperature, indicating that the thermal stability depended on the storage and processing temperatures. After 15 days of storage, less than 15 % of Cur in the control group was degraded at 4 °C and 20 °C; whereas approximately 40 % and 85 % degradation occurred at 37 °C and 55 °C, respectively. Moreover, the retention rate of Cur in the ultrasonically treated samples was higher than that in the control group, suggesting that ultrasonication was favorable for encapsulating and stabilizing Cur. This was mainly attributed to the unique structure of OBs. The surface protein layer separated the Cur from the water phase and formed a highly protective barrier against Cur degradation [44]. In addition, the increased steric hindrance and surface charge repulsion in the OB emulsions also helped inhibit the destruction or aggregation of droplets, further protecting Cur during heat treatment [41]. In vitro digestion

Particle size and ζ-potential during in vitro digestion analysis

The changes in particle size at different stages of in vitro digestion are shown in Fig. 6A. The overall trends in all samples were similar. Compared to the initial emulsion, the average particle size increased after the oral digestion stage, indicating that the mucin promoted bridging flocculation of a small number of droplets [19]. After the simulated gastric digestion, the particle size continued to increase because the complex gastric environment dramatically changed the OB structures. It is well-known that the highly acidic conditions and the pepsin hydrolysis in the stomach phase are related to the instability of the emulsion [45]. In the simulated intestinal digestion stage, the droplet size sharply decreased because the hydrolysis of the larger TAG clusters produced smaller fatty acids clusters, decreasing the particle size of the entire system [46]. Moreover, many relatively small colloidal particles were present in the intestinal digestion process, including micelles, vesicles, and insoluble calcium soaps. It is worth noting that at all digestion stages, the average particle size of the control group was significantly larger than that of the ultrasonically treated OBs, and the 200 W treated OBs had the smallest average particle size. Moreover, an appropriate ultrasonic cavitation can effectively alter protein structure, dissociate the protein subunits, and inhibit the formation of aggregates [19]. The cavitation effect commonly used in food industry induces sonomechanical and sonochemical process, causing unfolding in the native structure of proteins which is desirable for protein functionality, including improving solubility, emulsibility and digestibility [47]. Ultrasonic treatment improved emulsion aggregation during digestion, leading to smaller particle sizes and larger specific surface areas for better OB emulsion digestion [48].Fig. 6In vitro simulated digestive characteristics of OB emulsions. Fig. 6B shows the ζ-potential changes in different digestion fluids. At the initial stage, all samples showed ζ-potentials less than −20 mV, indicating that the OB emulsions had high negative charge and the systems were relatively stable. In oral conditions, the ζ-potentials of all samples increased because of the electrostatic screening effect caused by the counter ions in simulated saliva and the adsorption of mucin molecules onto the OB surfaces [45]. In the gastric digestion stage, the complex composition of the gastric environment (including low pH value and high ion strength) changed the ζ-potential from strongly negative to slightly positive (less than 10 mV). The pepsin in gastric fluids might hydrolyze OB surface proteins, thereby changing the interface composition and charge characteristics. At the same time, the reduced electrostatic repulsion could also cause droplets to aggregate [49]. The ζ-potential in the intestinal digestion stage became strongly negative again, as various anionic substances (including free fatty acids and bile salts) were dominated in the digestive fluid [50]. Ultrasonic treatment could enhance the ionization of the OB surface proteins, expose more negatively charged groups, and increase the effective charge on the protein surface. The higher surface charge fortified the electrostatic repulsion force between the droplets, which was beneficial for droplet dispersion and made the interface layer more susceptible to digestive enzymes under digestive conditions [51].

Fatty acid release rate analysis

To evaluate the effect of ultrasonic treatment on the lipid digestion of OB emulsions, the FFA release rate of the emulsions was analyzed using the pH-stat method, as shown in Fig. 6C. All emulsions showed similar lipid digestion processes; FFA was rapidly released at first and then gradually slowed down until stabilization due to the presence of digestive enzymes and calcium ions [45], [52]. Furthermore, the total FFA release rate of the ultrasonic treatment group was significantly higher than that of the control group (72.91 %), indicating that the ultrasonic treatment improved the digestive ability of the OB emulsion in the gastrointestinal tract. The OBs treated with 200 W ultrasonic had the highest rate (87.24 %), and this could also be related to its relatively small average particle size, uniform particle size distribution, and high ζ-potential absolute value. Smaller sample droplets resulted in less aggregation during the gastric phase, further leading to a larger surface area exposed to the small intestine lipase stage, which promoted the digestion rate and achieved a high final digestion degree [19], [53]. In addition, after ultrasonic treatment, the OB surface proteins were more easily displaced by bile salts; thus, more lipase was adsorbed at the oil–water interface to promote digestion [49].

As shown in Fig. 6D and Table 6, linear relationships of FFA initial release were obtained when Ln[(φmax-φt)/φmax] was plotted against digestion time (t) from 0 to 60 min. After ultrasonic treatment, the rate constant of the initial FFA release was significantly (P < 0.05) higher than that of the control group (37.40 × 10-3 min−1), indicating that ultrasonication could accelerate lipolysis [54]. The half times of φmax of all ultrasonic treated OBs were shorter (Table 6) than that of the control group (18.53 min). And the half time of φmax of OBs treated by 200 W was the lowest (13.52 min). High-concentration digestive enzymes might be absorbed on the OB surface at the beginning of the digestion stage. Furthermore, the interface layer consisted of a denser and more rigid structure of OB proteins that could resist lipolysis [24], where appropriate ultrasonic treatment loosened the protein structure and accelerated lipid digestion. Bioaccessibility and stability analysis

Owing to the chemical instability and low biological accessibility of Cur in the gastrointestinal tract, researchers have designed various delivery systems, such as emulsions, to prevent its degradation [11]. The influence of ultrasonic treatment at different power levels on the bioaccessibility and stability of Cur in emulsions is shown in Fig. 6E. The bioaccessibility of Cur in the ultrasonic samples was significantly (P < 0.05) higher than that in the control group (38.52 %), with the 200 W ultrasonic-treated sample showing the highest bioaccessibility (45.22 %). The ultrasonically treated OB emulsion with smaller particle size and larger surface area promoted lipid digestion and further increased the release of Cur into the micelles [40], consistent with the FFA release rate curve (Fig. 6C). Additionally, the stability and bioaccessibility of Cur followed the same trend, indicating that ultrasonic treatment increased the protective ability of OB emulsions during simulated gastrointestinal digestion. The stability of Cur in the OBs treated with 200 W ultrasonic was 57.72 % after digestion, which was significantly (P < 0.05) higher than that in the control group (38.51 %). This was attributed to the increased emulsion stability and the slow diffusion of oxygen, free radicals, and other substances on the surface of the oil droplets, which effectively delayed the degradation of Cur in the gastrointestinal tract [55], consistent with the oxidative stability results.

Conclusions

In this study, novel OBs loaded with Cur were successfully prepared as effective delivery systems by a simple, low-cost, and safe ultrasonic-assisted and pH-driven method. An appropriate ultrasonic power level (200 W) could improve EE and LC, decrease the average particle size, increase the surface charge and dispersibility, and effectively unfold protein structures. Thus, the OBs with 200 W ultrasonic treatment presented the best storage, oxidative, photochemical, and thermal stabilities. Furthermore, the appropriate ultrasonic treatment created OBs with small particle size and uniform distribution and loosened protein structure to encapsulate and load Cur, which were beneficial for its in vitro digestion. Therefore, the highest FFA rate, bioaccessibility, and stability were obtained. This study provides a novel approach to employing native emulsion systems as delivery vehicles in oral therapeutic agent formulations.
