Football clubs and their ‘hive mind’: distributed situation awareness (DSA) and its role in injury management
Authors: Scott Mclean, Paul M Salmon, Mitch Naughton, Tom Page, Barry Drust, Michael Brownlow
Published: 2025

Injury rates in elite football remain persistently high, despite significant investments in sports science, medical infrastructure and performance technology.1 This longstanding problem highlights potential limitations of the systems, processes and data currently involved in understanding and responding to the complexity associated with football injuries. Emerging qualitative investigations, particularly into the communication of performance teams, demonstrate important considerations for injury risk reduction frameworks.2 Shifting attention towards how awareness is shared, connected and acted on across the complex, interdisciplinary systems (ie, performance, medical, coaching, management) that define modern football clubs requires a pro-active approach.3 One concept offering explanatory and applied utility in this context is distributed situation awareness (DSA).4 5 DSA recognises that no single individual actor holds all relevant awareness and that effective decision-making requires the integration of disparate, yet complementary, awareness across a system of actors.5 This editorial introduces DSA as a critical concept to improve injury prevention and management within elite football clubs.

What is distributed situation awareness?

DSA has been extensively applied in numerous safety critical domains such as aviation, healthcare and defence, where teams, organisations and even entire sociotechnical systems must make time-sensitive, high-stakes decisions in complex and dynamic environments.5 6 In such contexts, the emphasis of the DSA model is not on an individual’s ability to perceive and interpret information in isolation, but on the system’s capacity to develop and maintain a coherent understanding of its operational environment.6 This involves the connection of mental models, timely communication, role clarity and compatible use of information and technologies. DSA emphasises the exchange and integration of awareness among team members and technology to develop a collective understanding of the situation. For detailed descriptions of the core tenets of DSA, we recommend the commentary by Salmon and Plant.7

Applying DSA in the Premier League

English Premier League (EPL) football clubs operate as complex sociotechnical systems characterised by numerous interacting components and stakeholders, operating under intense performance pressures.7 Within these systems, decisions regarding training and/or match load, rehabilitation, return-to-play, player selections and transitions between squads are determined across coaching teams, sport scientists, medical staff, analysts—and increasingly informed by data-driven technologies. As clubs adopt more sophisticated technologies for monitoring and forecasting, and as the number of internal and external actors increases, the risk also grows that specialists in different areas may not share information effectively or in a timely manner.8 For example, it is possible that coaching staff are unaware of a player’s injury risk profile, or that performance staff lack insight into the tactical requirements influencing selection decisions. These incompatibilities create failures in DSA that have potential implications for both player health and team performance.

Applying DSA to elite football offers a necessary reframing of common issues in player health and well-being management. Rather than focussing on individuals and their contribution, it shifts the emphasis to the club’s ‘hive mind’9 10 and how it maintains the awareness necessary to optimise player health and well-being. DSA transactions in which awareness is exchanged between various stakeholders are critical.6 Gaps in DSA, such as assumptions about physical readiness, an inaccurate collective understanding of injury status and fixture scheduling (ie, days prior to or following a match) or absence of feedback can enhance injury risk and hinder player development trajectories.

This misalignment highlights the importance of designing performance systems that explicitly support DSA. Such systems go beyond individual expertise, data availability and advanced technologies. They rely on processes and structures that ensure the right information reaches the right people at the right time. Practical features that promote DSA in elite football settings include:

Clarity in roles and responsibilities along with a clear understanding of information needs or ‘situation awareness requirements’ for each role.

Importantly, DSA is not achieved through more meetings or dashboards alone. It requires a systems-based mindset, in which the goal is not simply to inform others, but to exchange awareness to construct a system-level understanding of dynamic conditions. DSA is an emergent property of sociotechnical systems, and not merely a cognitive state in individuals.5

Advancing a culture of connected awareness

In the context of the EPL, where fixture congestion, international call-ups and commercial pressures compress decision timeframes, the need for robust mechanisms and processes to support DSA is crucial. Clubs that fail to align perspectives across performance domains may risk more than just injuries, including lack of tactical cohesion, low player morale, poor recruitment and long-term development outcomes. As artificial intelligence and advanced decision-support systems become more prevalent in football, new forms of ‘non-human agents’ will further complicate processes.11 12 These technologies will either enable or degrade DSA, depending on how they are designed and integrated into existing workflows and whether they enhance mutual understanding or create new silos.

To address these challenges, intentional design of communication and workflow systems, along with technologies that operationalise DSA principles, is required. The Event Analysis of Systemic Teamwork (EAST) framework13 provides a rigorous method for mapping and analysing the interactions of tasks, actors and information. Previous yet scarce applications of EAST in sport have demonstrated its utility in modelling DSA and identifying key nodes (ie, tasks, actors), information bottlenecks and pointing to opportunities to optimise DSA and performance.14

Improving player health and performance in the Premier League and elite football will not come solely from more data, better algorithms or increased specialisation. It will come from systems and processes in which relevant actors can see their own picture at the right time, and the club can see the whole picture. DSA offers a scientifically grounded framework for achieving this goal. Future performance enhancement and injury reduction strategies in football, and sport, must therefore move beyond individualised markers and metrics and embrace the broader sociotechnical system in which decisions and actions are made and carried out. To enhance football performance, injury prevention and management, it is not just about having more and better data, it is about having compatible awareness across the system.
