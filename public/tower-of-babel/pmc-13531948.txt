Clinical and public health economic impact of rapid strain-matched vaccination in influenza pandemic mitigation: A UK-like population dynamic transmission model
Authors: Sam Lovick, Malur Sudhanva, Alexander T Kennedy, Ray Longstaff, Emanuele Ciglia, Joaquin F Mould-Quevedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza A virus H5N1 has recently circulated at unprecedented levels in birds and mammals, causing sporadic human infections. Using a susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered (SEIR) dynamic transmission model of a UK-like population, this study aimed to quantify reductions in morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization attributable to rapid deployment of a strain-matched pandemic influenza vaccine; to compare outcomes across egg-based and cell-based vaccine platforms; to evaluate the economic impact of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) and their interaction with vaccination timing; and to estimate the impact of earlier or delayed vaccine availability across a full spectrum of pandemic transmissibility and severity scenarios. In the absence of NPIs, a 16-week vaccination campaign starting 16 weeks post-pandemic declaration reduced infections by 3.0 million and deaths by 28,000, with greater benefits observed in low-transmissibility scenarios. A cell-based vaccine with equivalent timing further reduced deaths by 5.0%, with additional gains in scenarios involving faster vaccine production. NPIs (average cost, £208 billion) enhanced vaccine effectiveness across all pandemic types, most markedly in high-transmissibility events; cell-based vaccine additionally reduced NPI costs by 0.9% relative to egg-based vaccine. Conversely, delays in vaccine availability increased both mortality and economic costs. These findings highlight the need for robust and agile vaccine development, registration, and distribution infrastructures, as well as flexible response strategies adaptable to varying pandemic severity and transmissibility. Policymakers should consider adopting a comprehensive framework integrating health outcome metrics and the economic impact of NPIs to inform vaccine procurement and deployment decisions.KEYWORDS: Pandemic influenza vaccine, cell-based vaccine, egg-based vaccine, health outcomes, bird flu, influenza A virus H5N1 infections, H5N1, public health outcomes

Introduction

Influenza has caused four pandemics in recorded history, and the next influenza pandemic is often viewed as a question of when, not if. One of the most closely followed influenza pandemic threats, but not the sole threat, is the highly pathogenic avian influenza A/H5N1. Since the beginning of 2021, H5N1 infections have become increasingly common in wild birds, domesticated poultry, and dairy cattle.1 While public health authorities consider the threat to the general public to be low, infections are on the rise in humans, particularly among people who may be occupationally exposed to sick animals.2–4 Cases of H5N1 have increased dramatically in recent years, rising from 21 confirmed human cases reported to the World Health Organization (WHO) between 2020 and 2023 to 29 confirmed human cases, including 11 deaths, in the first 10 months of 2025. Historically, H5N1 infections are associated with high mortality; out of 992 cases reported since 2003, 476 led to death.5

The potential threat posed by influenza calls for detailed pandemic preparedness planning, including development of strain-matched vaccines.6 Strain-matched vaccination is a cornerstone of influenza pandemic mitigation, and the faster a vaccine becomes available, the larger the benefits to society. Because multiple seasonal and pandemic influenza vaccines with well-established manufacturing platforms and safety profiles are already available, the response to influenza pandemics can be quicker than responses in scenarios requiring the development of vaccines against entirely novel pathogens, as occurred during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Nevertheless, development and manufacture of a pandemic influenza vaccine may take from 14 weeks to 6 months, depending upon clinical trial requirements, or even longer for a novel and previously unlicensed vaccine platform.7,8

Simulation modeling reveals a substantial disease burden during the initial period of influenza pandemics before strain-matched vaccines become widely available, particularly in higher transmissibility pandemics.9 Prior to widespread vaccine availability during the COVID-19 pandemic, nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) such as school closures and remote work mandates were widely implemented to reduce the spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). In future pandemics, including those potentially caused by H5N1, NPIs of some form will likely play a crucial role in early pandemic control, but at significant societal and economic costs.10

These observations underscore the need for rapid vaccine deployment to curtail early-stage morbidity and mortality. Licensed pandemic preparedness vaccines, which can be swiftly adapted to the pandemic strain, offer a promising approach to minimize the time gap between pandemic declaration and initial vaccine supply, as was evident in the response to the A/H1N1pdm09 pandemic, compared to the time required to develop and achieve regulatory approvals for a new vaccine to combat a newly emerged pathogen such as SARS-CoV-2. This strategy has the potential to significantly reduce the early-stage impact of a pandemic.7,11 Cell-based influenza vaccines, which are produced using mammalian cell lines rather than hen’s eggs, represent an advanced technology that avoids potential limitations associated with egg-based vaccines.7,12 Cell-based vaccines maintain greater antigenic stability during production, which can result in a better match between vaccine virus and the target circulating wild-type strains – characteristics that may be advantageous for a pandemic vaccine candidate.7,11–13

A comprehensive evaluation of pandemic mitigation strategies must consider both the health outcomes and the economic ramifications of interventions.

Materials and methods

Aims

This study modeled a range of hypothetical pandemic influenza scenarios spanning different strain characteristics in terms of transmissibility, severity, and the resulting shape of the mortality profile. Within this framework, this study had the following aims: (1) quantify the reductions in morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization (including infections, hospitalizations, intensive care unit [ICU] admissions, and deaths) attributable to rapid deployment of a strain-matched pandemic influenza vaccine in a population like that of the United Kingdom (UK); (2) compare those reductions across egg-based and cell-based vaccine platforms and assess the incremental benefit of a cell-based vaccine’s potential for superior antigenic match; (3) evaluate the clinical and economic impact of adjunctive NPIs and their interaction with vaccination timing; (4) assess the economic impact of vaccine interventions on health-related costs and NPI-associated costs as well as estimate cost-effectiveness; and (5) estimate the impact of earlier or delayed vaccine availability on health outcomes and economic burden across a full spectrum of pandemic transmissibility and severity scenarios.

Study design

A susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered (SEIR) dynamic transmission model was developed to simulate influenza pandemic spread in a UK-like population (Figure S1). The model represented estimates of the UK population structure at the time of the modeling (Office for National Statistics [ONS] mid-2022 estimates), POLYMOD UK contact rates, National Health Service (NHS) acute and ICU bed capacity, and UK economic data. The vaccination and NPI strategies evaluated are not established UK policy; the model therefore describes a UK-like setting rather than current UK practice. The model explored a full factorial of pandemic scenarios defined by two independent axes: transmissibility, characterized by the basic reproduction number (R0), and severity, characterized by the infection fatality rate (IFR), with four non-overlapping categories on each axis. All 16 transmissibility:severity combinations were modeled. These scenarios were intended to span a broad range of plausible pandemic influenza characteristics rather than represent any single virus: the model was not parameterized to a specific strain (such as H5N1), but instead explored how transmissibility, severity, and the resulting shape of the mortality profile jointly determine pandemic impact and the value of vaccination. Within each scenario, the model was used to evaluate the impact of rapid vaccine availability on infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, mortality, and the economic costs associated with NPIs and to compare the impact of egg-based vs. cell-based pandemic influenza vaccine campaigns across a range of campaign start times and durations. Ethical review and approval were not required for the retrospective analysis of the deidentified secondary data modeled in this study. Informed consent was not required for this category of study.

The model was implemented in R using the EpiModel package.14,15

Model inputs

Transmissibility was categorized as low (R0 ≥1.10 to <1.35), medium (≥1.35 to <1.60), high (≥1.60 to <1.85), or very high (≥1.85 to <2.10)16; severity was categorized as low (IFR ≥ 0.05% to <0.25%), medium (≥0.25% to <0.75%), high (≥0.75% to <1.50%), or very high (≥1.50% to <3.00%).17

UK-specific population characteristics, economic parameters, and health system capacities were derived from contemporaneous sources.18,19 Population contact rates were derived from the POLYMOD study20 and adapted to account for NPIs (Table S1). Demand for health resources was based on conditional likelihoods of presentation, hospitalization, and ICU admission as derived from the literature.21,22 Likewise, estimates of the health burden of pandemic influenza, including clinical and economic factors, were based on published studies.21,23,24 Tables S2–S5 list data and sources for all model inputs.

During the A/H1N1pdm09 pandemic, the first registrations in the European Union (EU) for strain-matched variations of the pandemic preparedness vaccine authorizations were achieved 111 d following the WHO pandemic declaration,25 which equates to approximately 16 weeks. Hence, the model assumed a pandemic influenza vaccine would be available 16 weeks after pandemic declaration and that the vaccination campaign would last another 16 weeks (in line with first product registrations in 2009), covering 80% of the population. Vaccine effectiveness for the egg-based vaccine was set at 56% across the whole population.26 Based on the seasonal influenza literature, the relative vaccine effectiveness (rVE) of the cell-based vaccine relative to the egg-based vaccine was conservatively assumed to be 9.5%.27,28 Key disease parameters include a latent period of 1.9 d, an infectious period of 4.1 d, and an asymptomatic fraction of 50–80%.16,22 Full details of all model inputs and sources are provided in Table S2.

NPIs were defined as social distancing measures including school closures and work-from-home and stay-at-home stipulations, each of which affect anticipated contact rates. They also included quarantine restrictions imposed on persons with confirmed infections and their households, each of which affect anticipated contact rates of infectious individuals within the community. Other measures that might reduce infectiousness, such as mask mandates, were not considered. The short-run economic impact of NPIs was estimated based on labor lost due to absenteeism related to work-from-home, stay-at-home, and quarantine mandates; childcare obligations under school closure mandates; and illness and death.24,29

The model endogenously determined NPIs to ensure that enough ICU beds would be available to meet the needs for influenza-related care. All pandemic deaths in the model were assumed to occur in an ICU setting if an ICU bed was available. Under this assumption, knowing the average duration of ICU treatment and the availability of ICU beds, the model determined the maximum number of deaths in each period that were consistent with demand for ICU beds not exceeding capacity. If the instantaneous (single period) mortality reached this level, the model implemented social distancing measures (other than quarantine mandates that are permanent if selected). The impact of social distancing on contact rates and hence the rate of spread was deterministic, which permitted the model to accurately predict the impact of NPIs on mortality given the instantaneous state of the pandemic. Based on this determination, the model instituted different measures in increasing order of effectiveness (school closures, work from home mandates, stay at home mandates) to seek to maintain mortality below the identified threshold level. The model also estimated ‘excess deaths,’ defined as the number of additional deaths that arise because no ICU beds are available, under the assumption that mortality is higher without ICU treatment.30–32

Data analyses

The analysis estimated the impact of each type of vaccine (egg-based or cell-based) on the burden of disease and the economic costs of NPIs implemented to help control the pandemic. To provide a broad assessment, sensitivities were modeled based on campaign commencements 2 weeks earlier and later than the 16-week start in the main analysis and on campaign durations that were 2 weeks shorter and longer than the 16-week duration in the main analysis.

The analysis was conducted from a combined health system and societal perspective. Economic costs included NPI-related labor losses, estimated by applying the UK labor share of gross domestic product (GDP) (58%) to working days lost through work-from-home, stay-at-home, and quarantine mandates; school closure costs estimated through childcare obligations and teaching labor; lost labor due to illness and death within the working age population; and direct healthcare costs (hospitalization and ICU utilization at UK unit costs). The monetary value of health outcomes was estimated using quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), valued at £37,000 per QALY. Lost years of life were estimated using cohort-specific life expectancy and age at death determined by the model. The time horizon was the first year of the pandemic. Cost-effectiveness was expressed as cost per life saved and cost per QALY gained, comparing vaccine strategies against no intervention and against NPIs alone. All costs are reported in pounds sterling at the time of analysis.

Results

The model simulations revealed substantial benefits of early vaccination across various pandemic scenarios, with and without the implementation of NPIs.

Impact of pandemic influenza vaccine without NPIs

The clinical and severity characteristics of a future pandemic strain remain unknown; this model therefore explored a full range of plausible transmissibility and severity combinations rather than assuming a single pandemic profile. The base case estimates without any intervention (either vaccine or NPI) averaged across all pandemic types were 38,694,000 infections, demand for 3,090,000 hospital admissions (with capacity for 1,166,400), demand for 761,000 ICU admissions (with capacity for 68,000), and 373,000 deaths (Table S6). Across the full range of pandemic transmissibility and severity scenarios in the absence of NPIs, promptly starting a vaccination campaign with an egg-based pandemic influenza vaccine 16 weeks after a pandemic declaration reduced infections by 3.0 million, demand for hospital admissions by 251,000, demand for ICU admissions by 62,000, and deaths by 28,000 compared with no intervention.

The effectiveness of vaccination without NPIs varied markedly across individual transmissibility and severity scenarios (Tables S7–22). In a high-transmissibility, high-severity scenario, the pandemic spread so rapidly that most infections and deaths occurred before a vaccine could be deployed: a 16-week vaccine reduced deaths by only 0.1% relative to no intervention (Table S17). By contrast, in a low-transmissibility, high-severity scenario, in which slower spread left more time for vaccination to take effect before the bulk of cases occurred, the same egg-based vaccine reduced deaths by 51.5% in the absence of NPIs (Table S9).

Use of a cell-based rather than an egg-based pandemic influenza vaccine further improved public health outcomes in the model. Delivering a cell-based vaccine in a 16-week campaign beginning 16 weeks after pandemic declaration further reduced the number of infections by 101,900 (a 3.4% improvement) and deaths by an additional 1,222 (a 4.3% improvement) (Table S6). Cell-based vaccine exhibited a similar profile of effectiveness as egg-based across different pandemic types (Figure S2). The relative advantage of cell-based over egg-based vaccine without NPIs also varied by transmissibility and severity scenario (Tables S7–S22).

Economic impact of NPIs alone over the first year

Without NPIs, overall disease burden was high across all scenarios (Table S6), especially in certain scenarios of high transmission (Tables S15–S22). Therefore, implementation of NPIs may be required to slow the spread of the pandemic prior to even rapid vaccine availability. NPIs instituted to reduce ICU admissions prior to vaccine availability carry a high economic cost due to labor losses, school closures, and reductions in economic activity when the population is required to stay at home. Across all pandemic scenarios, if no vaccine is available and NPIs alone are used to manage pandemic harm, the short-run economic cost over the first year would amount to £666 billion or 30% of GDP at the time of the analysis.

Impact of pandemic influenza vaccines with NPIs

When egg-based vaccine and NPIs are used together, the NPIs increased the effectiveness of pandemic influenza vaccine, resulting in 32.7 million fewer infections and 354,000 fewer deaths (Table S6). Because NPIs minimize harm until vaccines become available, campaign start time and duration had little impact on the number of averted deaths, but they do affect the cost of NPIs.

The average cost of implementing NPIs until egg-based vaccine can be administered was £208 billion, or 9% of UK GDP. This translated into a cost-effectiveness ratio of £0.6 million per additional life saved. The economic burden of NPIs varied by pandemic severity and transmissibility, with costs ranging from £6 billion in low-transmissibility, very high-severity scenarios to £372 billion in very high-transmissibility, high-severity scenarios (Table S23); transmissibility, rather than severity, was the dominant driver of NPI cost, since slower-spreading pandemics require less extensive social distancing regardless of their severity. Unlike the no-NPI case, vaccine effectiveness with NPIs in place was relatively stable across scenarios: egg-based vaccination reduced deaths by between 40% and 75% relative to NPIs alone across all 16 transmissibility and severity combinations (Tables S7–S22), confirming that NPIs substantially offset the timing disadvantage of a delayed vaccine even in the most challenging pandemic scenarios. Therefore, the characteristics of the vaccine are not the sole determinant of the impact of vaccine on pandemic outcomes. The timing of vaccine availability and the effectiveness of NPIs to delay pandemic spread while a vaccine is in preparation are significant factors. In severe (higher transmissibility, higher IFR) pandemics, earlier and higher rVE vaccines reduce the economic costs of NPIs needed to manage pandemic harm until vaccine becomes available. In lower transmissibility but higher IFR pandemics, where NPIs are less likely to be needed, higher rVE vaccines reduce the pandemic health burden. In addition, earlier vaccine availability broadens the range of pandemic types in which costly NPIs are not needed.

Use of cell-based vaccine reduced the costs of NPIs by approximately £4.1 billion (a 0.9% reduction compared to the cost of NPIs under the egg-based scenario), further reduced infections by 334,300 (4.3% improvement over egg-based vaccines), and deaths by 1,400 (5.0%) (Table S6).

Impact of accelerated and delayed vaccine delivery

In the absence of NPIs, commencing the egg-based vaccine campaign earlier with a shorter campaign further reduced the burden of disease. Without NPIs, commencing vaccination 2 weeks earlier and completing the campaign within 14 weeks resulted in 887,000 fewer infections (a 30% reduction) and 8,020 fewer deaths (28%). Delays resulted in commensurate increases in the burden of disease. Similar magnitude improvements in the burden of disease resulted from acceleration of cell-based vaccine, resulting in 841,000 fewer infections and 8,016 fewer deaths (Table 1).

As noted above, with NPIs in place, acceleration made no material difference to infections and deaths for either vaccine. However, it did affect the costs of NPIs; a 2-week acceleration reduced the cost of NPIs by approximately £15 billion (a 7.5% decrease). Savings from the acceleration in vaccine supply were approximately £6 billion greater with cell-based vaccine than the egg-based vaccine (Figure 1). On the other hand, delays gave rise to commensurate increases in NPI costs. Qualitatively similar variation in the impact of vaccine acceleration was observed across individual pandemic transmissibility and severity scenarios, consistent with the pattern described above for vaccine effectiveness without and with NPIs.

Discussion

This modeling study demonstrated that rapid vaccine availability substantially attenuates the multifaceted burdens associated with pandemic influenza. In this model of an influenza pandemic in a UK-like setting, earlier intervention (particularly in the absence of NPIs) markedly reduced infection rates, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, and mortality, while simultaneously alleviating the economic strain on healthcare systems and the broader economy. In lower-transmissibility pandemics, NPIs added little benefit over that of vaccines, and not requiring NPI provided cost-savings. In contrast, use of NPIs had a substantial impact in high-transmissibility events. Use of a cell-based vaccine was associated with further reductions in influenza infections and influenza-related mortality relative to an egg-based vaccine in scenarios involving NPI implementation. Cell-based vaccines also further reduced the economic impact of NPIs by 0.9%. These benefits were amplified in scenarios involving earlier availability of cell- and egg-based vaccines. In contrast, delays in vaccine availability contributed to both higher mortality and economic costs.

In severe pandemic scenarios, NPIs are likely to play a crucial role in disease containment prior to the availability of vaccines and achievement of widespread vaccine-induced immunity. NPI strategies can be classified as either suppression (reduce R to <1) or mitigation (reduce R, but not necessarily to <1) efforts. Suppression efforts, which involve severely reducing human-human contact through social distancing (i.e., closure of schools, places of worship, public spaces, etc), mask mandates, and similar measures, aim to stop pandemic spread and complications but must be maintained as long as the virus is circulating.6,33 These efforts are costly, often unpopular and divisive (contributing to low adherence), and may have long-lasting detrimental social and economic effects.10,33,34 Mitigation efforts (e.g., isolation of infected persons and their close contacts) are more limited in scope, aiming only to slow infection rates, but may not be sufficient to reduce the number of severe cases and the consequent healthcare burden, especially in high transmissibility or high severity pandemics.6,10,33 Hastening the availability of a pandemic vaccine mitigates the limitations of both types of NPI strategies. In suppression scenarios, the sooner a vaccine is available, the sooner strict NPIs can be relaxed. In mitigation scenarios, earlier vaccine availability will reduce pressure on the healthcare system sooner.

Because cell-based influenza vaccines can avoid egg adaptation (which can contribute to mismatches between circulating and vaccine strains), they have consistently demonstrated improved effectiveness compared to egg-based vaccines in studies of seasonal influenza, with rVEs of up to 19.8% in test-negative design studies.13,35,36 In a recent systematic meta-analysis of seasonal influenza vaccines, the rVE of cell-based vs. egg-based influenza vaccines was 8.4% against both A and B strains and 9.5% against A strains (which would be most similar to the avian H5N1 virus in our model; no influenza pandemics have involved B strains).27,37

Consistent with our sensitivity analyses on time to availability, a modeling analysis of early vaccination in the US by Nguyen and colleagues showed that the speed of campaign initiation was more important than vaccine effectiveness in terms of reducing adverse health outcomes from an influenza pandemic. In their model, a low effectiveness vaccine distributed 3 months after pandemic declaration reduced pandemic mortality by 38%, whereas a high effectiveness vaccine distributed at 6 months reduced deaths by 21%.38 A pre-reserved quantity of vaccine, aligned to the level of population coverage required, can ensure timely availability of vaccine supply and help reduce the duration and intensity of NPI usage, thereby mitigating the associated economic impact. Moreover, some mitigation strategies may include a stockpile of adjuvanted zoonotic pre-pandemic vaccines, which may provide cross-protection during an early vaccination campaign, prior to the availability of strain-matched pandemic vaccine.39,40

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The model is for pandemic influenza generally, and so is parameterized to plausible pandemic scenarios. It does not represent the specific virological or immunological characteristics of a known strain. Furthermore, the model treats transmissibility (R0) and severity (IFR) as unchanging across time and independent axes across the modeled scenarios; however, influenza virus virulence may change as transmissibility increases, and severity and transmissibility may change during the pandemic.

With respect to vaccination assumptions, the model applies a single population-wide vaccine effectiveness value (56% for egg-based vaccine) that does not capture heterogeneity by age, immune status, prior exposure history, or strain-specific characteristics. This simplifying assumption is consistent with licensed pandemic preparedness vaccines meeting CBER/former CHMP criteria in healthy children, adults, and older adults. The rVE of cell-based vs. egg-based vaccine (9.5%), which is applied to all cell-based scenarios in this analysis, is derived from seasonal influenza evidence and its applicability to a novel pandemic strain – against which the population would be largely immunologically naïve – is uncertain. The study could be adapted to investigate a wider range of rVEs, reflective of different factors affecting effectiveness.

In health and social distancing factors, the model assumes all pandemic deaths occur in an ICU setting; in reality, some deaths will occur in other settings. This assumption was adopted to estimate ICU and hospital bed demand, and because ICU capacity utilization is the endogenous trigger for NPIs in the model. The estimated number of deaths is, then, a direct function of IFR and the increase in mortality ascribed to deaths taking place outside of the ICU setting due to bed shortages.

Population contact rates are derived from the POLYMOD study,20 which predates the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-era and post-pandemic shifts in social mixing behavior are not captured, and no equivalently comprehensive UK contact survey with the same age resolution is currently available.

The NPI module did not model the effects of masks and contact tracing. By excluding these lower-cost interventions, the model may overstate reliance on costly social-distancing measures and the economic value attributed to vaccine acceleration, particularly in a less severe pandemic. However, in more severe pandemics, these measures are likely to decline in effectiveness. The model does not include voluntary changes in behavior, noting that evidence on the economic consequences of imposed and voluntary measures is lacking.

The analysis focuses on short-term economic consequences of pandemic interventions. High short-run costs can be mitigated over the longer-term, for example, by more than compensating rebounds in economic activity, government interventions, and the like. The longer-term economic impacts could be examined using suitable approaches such as computable general equilibrium (CGE) modeling. Additionally, the model assumes 80% coverage based on announced reservations; however, uptake can be challenging and variable, which may impact outcomes. The model also incorporated shorter delivery and campaign timelines, but achievement of shorter timeframes is limited by various factors related to manufacturing, regulatory agencies, supply chain, public policy, and health systems (ie, the ability to surge to peak vaccination rates).

Notwithstanding these limitations, it should be noted that the study compares two vaccine technologies under identical modeling assumptions. It is unlikely that the limitations above, if addressed, would differentially impact the two comparator arms. The comparative findings are therefore considered robust, even where absolute estimates carry greater uncertainty.

While the results of this analysis reflect a UK-like setting, the qualitative findings are likely to generalize across other settings. The core conclusions that earlier vaccine availability reduces disease burden, that cell-based vaccine confers an incremental advantage through improved antigenic match, and that the economic value of vaccine acceleration is amplified when NPIs are in place are not contingent on UK-specific inputs. In lower-income settings, where ICU capacity per capita is more limited and vaccine access during a pandemic may be more constrained, greater reliance on NPIs could further increase the relative value of rapid strain-matched vaccine supply, even where absolute mortality estimates would differ substantially from those reported here. Conversely, where vaccine uptake is lower or health system surge capacity is higher, the relative contributions of NPIs and vaccination to pandemic control may shift. Applying the model to other national contexts would require recalibration of population structure, contact rates, health system capacity, and economic parameters; the modeling framework presented here is designed to accommodate such adaptation.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the important role of a reserved and timely supply of pandemic influenza vaccine within a preparedness strategy. A shorter timeline from pandemic declaration to deployment of vaccines – even by as little as two weeks – would have a real benefit in terms of reduced disease burden and the potential for lower costs related to use of NPIs. The joint activity and investment of public health authorities and manufacturers should maintain a focus on surveillance, vaccine development, sustainable manufacturing capacity, scalable and ready distribution infrastructures, as well as exploration of flexible response strategies that can be adapted to varying levels of outbreak or pandemic severity and transmissibility. Our analysis also highlights the importance of adopting a comprehensive framework for demonstrating the ongoing value of preparedness and pandemic influenza vaccines. This framework should integrate both traditional health outcome metrics and the economic implications of NPIs. By considering these factors in tandem, scientific advisers and policymakers can make more informed decisions regarding vaccine reservation, procurement and deployment strategies.

Data availability statement

CSL will consider, on a case-by-case basis, requests from bona-fide, qualified scientific and medical researchers to share anonymized Individual Participant Data (IPD) and supporting documents from CSL-sponsored clinical studies completed after 27 September 2007 for the purposes of conducting legitimate, independent scientific research. Requests relating to discontinued or terminated programs may also be considered. IPD will generally be shared once primary study results are publicly available (eg., via publication or results posting) and relevant major regulatory (i.e. FDA, EMA) reviews are complete.

Requests must be supported by a research proposal and analysis plan and should address a meaningful and previously unanswered medical or scientific question. CSL will consider applicable privacy, data protection, consent, contractual and other legal requirements and may decline requests where these prevent sharing, where the risk of re-identification cannot be adequately mitigated, or where the research question can be reasonably answered without access to IPD (e.g., using information already in the public domain).

Each request will be reviewed by an internal CSL review committee against pre-defined criteria (including but not limited to scientific merit, researcher qualifications, feasibility, and patient privacy). Where appropriate, CSL may seek input from an independent reviewer. If a request is approved, the requesting institution must enter into a data sharing agreement setting out the terms and conditions for access before any data are released. Data will be provided in anonymized form and commercially confidential information will be redacted as appropriate. Supporting documents (e.g., study protocol and statistical analysis plan, and where available data dictionaries) may also be provided, subject to the same review and contractual protections.

For information on the process and requirements for submitting a voluntary data sharing request, please contact CSL at clinicaltrials@cslbehring.com or visit https://www.csl.com/research-and-development/clinical-studies/research-practices.
