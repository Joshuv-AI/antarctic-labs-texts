Investigation on the observational climate variables and the feedback of vegetation dynamics at Ergun City, Inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Guangyong You, Yan Xu, Minxia Zhang, Yuanyun Gao, Shawn McKenzie, Juan You, Hui Li, Dinghe Zhang, Naifeng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we compared the in-site ground-based climate variables (1982–2016) with a multiyear normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) dataset to characterize climate change and vegetation-climate interactions at Ergune, Inner Mongolia, China, using the time series analysis, the correlation analysis and the principal component analysis (PCA). To reveal the time lag effects in climate-vegetation relationship, vector auto regression (VAR) model was constructed and the impulse-response analysis, the causality analysis were conducted. We found that the regional climate change over the past decades could be summarized as climate warming and drying. And the regional climate warming was mostly contributed by summer warming, rather than the widely reported winter warming in the north hemisphere. Climate variables were highly correlated. The PCA analysis revealed that the 1st principal component represented the temperature related variables, and the 2nd principal component represented the humidity related variables. At seasonal scale, however, the humidity and temperature was the 1st principal component for summer and winter respectively. VAR analysis revealed that, the precipitation has higher impact on NDVI than the temperature. The feedback of NDVI to humidity was significant, but feedback of NDVI to Temperature was non-significant. VAR model had better performance in prediction of NDVI than the multi-linear regression approach. This study investigated the climate-vegetation relationship with full considerations on the co-linearity and the time lag effect in climate system. The cause-effect in climate-vegetation relationship indicated the feedback of NDVI to climate variability and the ecological function of vegetation in mitigating and regulating the regional climate.Keywords: Climate change, Climate-vegetation interactions, Principal component analysis, Vector autoregressive modelSubject terms: Climate-change ecology, Projection and prediction

Introduction

The complex climatic system interacts through mass, energy and momentum exchanges. These exchanges undergo time-lag effects and feedback loops, oscillations, resilience and fluctuation chains1, resulting in significant influences on ecological security and society’s functions2. These intangible interactions signify the changes in surface radiation budgets, heat exchange, hydro and carbon cycles3,4, recorded by ground observation of climate variables and satellite images5.

Vegetation dynamics played an import role in climate system6. Firstly, vegetation dynamics are largely driven by the availability of light, temperature and humidity7, and climate change and its variability on vegetation growth and dynamics8, vegetation phenological changes9,10, hydrologic and carbon cycles11 have been reported by global and regional studies4,12,13. Secondly, vegetation has a known influence on climate systems through transpiration and variations of surface albedo and radiation balance4. Thirdly, vegetation growth affects wind speed and direction through the changes in surface roughness10,14.

To clarify the climate-vegetation interactions, long-term observational records of climate variables and constructed indices of vegetation dynamics were collected to establish statistical relationships between climate variability and vegetation dynamics7,9,13,15. Correlation analysis, multi-regression and principal regression had been conducted7,8, but controversial conclusions were reported in some regional studies as a result of the differences in statistical approaches and spatial-temporal scales9,16–18. In cold and arid/semi-arid regions such as forest-steppe ecotone of the northeastern China, soil moisture and aridity were considered as the controlling factors for vegetation phenology and dynamics16, whereas, studies also reported the insignificant relationship between precipitation and vegetation dynamics17. To provide further controversy, You et al. (2020) reported the different trends of Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and NDVI above a threshold of 0.2, and highlighted the roles of temperature and the growing season warmth in controlling the vegetation dynamics and phenology9. Overall, the complexity of the natural processes in climatology, hydrology, and ecology and the negligence of memory effects hidden in the climate-vegetation relationship were accountable for the controversial conclusions13,16.

To reveal the hidden feed-backs of vegetation dynamics to climate change7, numerous studies on identifying the driving factor for vegetation dynamic were conducted using correlation analysis or multi-linear regression analysis. Due to the co-linearity and correlations among the climate variables, the statistical analyses would be insufficient in addressing the interactions of the related climate variables and concluded in pseudo driving factors7,13. Therefore, the principal component analysis was accountable in identifying the major components and understanding the characteristics of inter-annual variations of climate variables.

As a result of the negligence of the time lag effect or legacy effect of climate variables, pseudo driving factors or controversial conclusions were reported9,19. The time lag effect of vegetation growth to climate variation were accountable for the controversial conclusions and the residuals of linear regressions between climate variability and vegetation dynamics20.

To reveal the interaction between climate change and vegetation responses, one of the effective approaches is to employ a non-linear vector auto-regressive model (VAR) and conduct the Granger causal test21. For example, studies in Loess Plateau and northern China applied the Granger causality test to reveal the impacts of vegetation change on local surface climate22,23. In addition, Granger has also been used to identify the memory or legacy (lagging) temporal effects of climate anomalies on other climate variables24. Contrary to the previous studies using the correlation and regression analyses in understanding the climate-vegetation relationship, the VAR model examines the relationships between stimulus and response or between cause and effect by constructing the models with the time lagged time series and provided the time-lagged significance of the driving factors.

In this study, we integrate traditional correlation and causality analyses to identify climatic impacts on vegetation dynamics in a semi-arid region in northeastern China. We use long-term ground observations of climate variables and remote sensing datasets of vegetation dynamics to (1) investigate the changes in means and extremes of climate variables and vegetation dynamics at annual and seasonal scales over the past decades, (2) reveal the major components of climate variables at seasonal and annual scales, (3) identify the causality in climate-vegetation interactions and (4) compare the model prediction performances between a VAR model and a multi-linear approach.

Data and methodology

Site description

The Ergune City (50°14′33″N, 120°11′27″E) is located in the transition zone between conifer forest (e.g. Larix gmelinii, Pinus sylvestris var. mongolica, Betula platyphylla) and steppe (e.g. Aneurolepidium, Stipa baicalensis) in northeastern China (Fig. 1). Based on the long-term meteorological observations, this region has a continental monsoon climate, with an average annual mean temperature range from − 1.3 °C to 0.4 °C25. It belongs to a continental monsoon climate. The region experiences a 6-month period when temperatures are consistently below 0 °C. The average annual precipitation is 300 mm, with most of the precipitation falling as rain during the summer months (June-August). Due to low population density and the implementation of ecological protection strategies such as Three North Shelter Forest System Project (starting from 1979), the Grain for Green Program (starting from 1999), the Natural Forest Conservation Program (starting from 2000), the Live-stocks Loading Balance and Award Program (starting from 2005), and the Fencing Grassland and Moving Users project (enforced in 2002), the influence of human activity on climate-vegetation relationships was not considered in this study.

Time series analyses

To remove the impact of autocorrelation on trend analysis, we calculated the aotucorrelation coefficient of each climate variable with its lag-1 time series, then the significant autocorrelated time series was pre-whitened (MKTFPW)26 for trend analysis. To reveal the long-term trends of climate variables, the Mann–Kendall trend tests were conducted27,28. To reduce the influence of abnormal values and outliers, the non-parametric Sen’s estimator of slope29 was used. The prewhite refers to a procedure designed to eliminate the effects of serial correlation in time-series data, thereby improving the accuracy of statistical tests like the Mann-Kendall test for trend detection. The change point was detected by Mann–Whitney–Pettit test30. To reveal the seasonal pattern of regional climate change, all of the above variables in winter months (from November to February, the following year) and summer months (June to August) were collected and time series analyses were conducted. All variables were bootstrapped and Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to reveal temporal relationships among the variables. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to extract the major components of the climate datasets. The R package “greenbrown” was applied to extract the phenological characteristics such as SOS (start of season), EOS (end of season), LOS (length of season) Peak (peak of the curve) and POP (the date when the vegetation dynamic curve reaches the Peak)31.

Vector autoregressive model

To explore the time-lag effects of climate factors on vegetation dynamics, we constructed VAR model by using the decomposed time series of climate change and vegetation dynamics. Then, the impulse-response analyses were conducted to reveal the time lag effects of vegetation response to climate change. Meanwhile, we compared the performance of VAR model with the multi linear regression approach (MLR). Figure 2 demonstrated the flowchart of the developing and the applying of the VAR model.

Data preprocessing of the VAR model

All climate factors and NDVI data series passed the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, Johansen cointegration test and Granger causality test, and the VAR model was applicable in this study32. R package ‘vars’ was used to construct the VAR model33 and facilitate the impulse-response analyses.

Impulse response analysis

The impulse response analysis (IRF) was conducted to quantify the dynamic changes of NDVI by giving NDVI an impulse of climate force. Then, the NDVI responses in the simultaneous and the following periods were recorded. By using the ‘irf” function in ‘vars’ package, the IRF results showed the duration and intensity of vegetation response to the climate factors.

Granger cause analysis

The raw data in this study were decomposed into the trend, the seasonality and the random components. Then, the random components of the decomposed time series of TMP, P and NDVI were used to constructed the VAR model for Granger causality analysis. To reveal the cause-effect between the climate variables and the vegetation dynamic, VAR model was constructed by incorporating the decomposed NDVI with the decomposed TMP and P respectively.

Prediction and forecast error variance decomposition

We developed and trained the VAR model using NDVI data during 1982 and 2010, then we tested the performance of the model by comparing the predicted NDVI against the observed NDVI by using the data between 2010 and 2013. We used the root mean squared error (RMSE) to quantify the prediction accuracy, and compared the prediction accuracy of the multi-linear regression (MLR) model with that of the VAR model. Principal components analysis

Figure 3 displayed the correlation coefficients between different climate variables. TMP displayed positive correlations with TMNV, TMXV, TaMin, TaMax, St and Pa, but negative correlations with P, Pdays, PMax, Ws and WsMax. P was positively correlated with Pdays, PMax, VPA, RH, Ws and WsMax, but negatively correlated with Pa, TMXV, TaMax and St. Correlation analyses revealed that the TMNV were positively correlated with VPA, whereas, the TMXV were positively correlation with St but negatively correlated with P and Pdays, indicating that TMNV and TMXV were mostly influenced by humidity and solar radiation respectively. The WsMax displayed significant positive correlations with P and RH, indicating the WsMax was influenced by the occurrence of precipitation during convective weather. Air pressure (Pa), especially for the Pa during summer (Pa_Summer), displayed significant negative correlations with P and Pdays, suggesting the role of summer monsoon strength in controlling the regional humidity.

Principal component analysis on the climate variables revealed that, the 1st principal component represents the variations of temperature, the 2nd component represents the variations of humidity, suggesting the regional climate variability could be summarized by variations of temperature and humidity. These two components contributed 0.9086 of the cumulative proportion of variance.

At seasonal scale, the 1st principal was the variations of temperature and the 2nd component was the variations of humidity during winter (Fig. 4a). These 2 components account for 0.871 of the cumulative proportion of variance. The climate variables could be separated into 3 groups, namely, the temperature related variables (TMP, TMX, TMN, etc.), the humidity related variables (P, Pdays, Pmax, etc.) and the air pressure related variables (Pa, Ws, etc.). Therefore, the climate variability during winter could be represented by the variations of temperature, humidity and air pressure.

Causality analyses and impulse-response between climate and NDVI

As the regional climate variability could be summarized as the variations in temperature and humidity, we incorporated TMP, P and NDVI as a group and constituted a vector autoregressive model. The result of impact-response analysis showed that, TMP had a limited contribution on NDVI (Fig. 5a), P had a strong impact on NDVI (Fig. 5b), indicating the significant role of humidity in dominating the vegetation dynamics. In contrast, NDVI had a negative contribution on TMP (Fig. 5c) but positive contribution on P (Fig. 5d), suggesting the vegetation’s ecological function of regulating and mitigating the seasonal climate variations. The lagged effects of TMP on NDVI increased and reached their maximum at 2 months. Then the lagging effects decreased and disappeared within 6 months.

Impulse response analysis results of the NDVI impacted by the climate factors (a, TMP. b, P), and the climate factors (c, TMP. d, P) influenced by the NDVI. TMP (monthly mean temperature), P (monthly Precipitation), NDVI (normalized difference vegetation index).

The result show that, the variation of TMP did not Granger cause the fluctuation of the NDVI (P > 0.05), but the NDVI did Granger cause the change of TMP (P = 0.023). P did instantaneous cause the change of NDVI (P = 0.005) and NDVI did instantaneous cause the change of P as well (P = 0.005).

Records of TMP, P and NDVI from 1982 to 2010 were used for training and the records of the year 2011, 2012 and 2013 were used for model validation. The results showed that, the RMSE for MLR model is 0.145 and the RMSE for VAR model is 0.055, suggesting the VAR model had good performance than MLR approach in predicting the seasonal fluctuation of NDVI (Fig. 6).

Discussions

The characteristics of climate change

In this study, the observed temperature had an non-significant positive trend (0.237 °C per decade) which is comparable with You et al.4 who reported that the air temperature had a significant positive slope of 0.31 °C per decade (1982–2013) in eastern Mongolia. The regional climate warming was confirmed by studies in vicinity areas8–9. The significant trend of summer temperature complies with the significant positive trend of growing season warmth8. In contrast to the higher trend of minimum temperature than maximum temperature in southwestern China3,15, this study displayed higher trend of maximum temperature than minimum temperature, which signified the reduced cloud coverage and precipitation and the increased aridity8. With the significant positive trend of temperature and the negative trend of precipitation during summer, the regional aridity is strengthened over the past decades. The studies in the vicinity areas complies with the increased aridity in this region4,8–9. The change point around the year 2000 complies with the studies in Tibetan Plateau15, Yunnan Province3 and Notheastern China9, indicating the inter connections of climate variables and the remote connections resulting from the atmospheric circulations.

The regional climate change was influenced by the East Asian Summer Monsoon and the Siberian atmospheric circulation system34. Strong summer monsoon facilitates water-vapor transfer from the Pacific to the inland regions. In contrast, weak summer monsoon is usually accompanied with stagnation of water-vapor transfer, which brings droughts to the north and floods to the south35, causing great impacts on human society, agriculture, water crisis and ecological security. How East Asian Summer Monsoon is responding to climate change is a debatable issue. Some studies indicated that the East Asian Summer Monsoon has weakened over the pasted decades34. However, other studies showed the East Asian summer monsoon circulation and precipitation are projected to increase nonlinearly with warming, as a result of the increased vapor holding capacity of the troposphere4.

At the background of climate change and the increased mass from atmospheric carbon dioxide, the surface atmospheric pressure is considered to be increased. In this study, both of the Pa_Winter and Pa_Summer displayed comparable positive trends over the past decades. Specifically, the increase of Pa during summer suggests the weakened pacific monsoon effect and results in strengthened drought and heat wave36,37. The decreased P at the background of climate warming suggests that, energy that would be used to evaporate the surface water would be transferred into heating the air based on the theory of Budyko complementary relationship4. As a result, this study revealed the positive trend of TMP and negative trends of P, RH and VPA. Moreover, the increased PMax combined with decreased Pdays during summer suggests the increased intensity of flood and possibly results in aggravated soil erosion.

Whereas, the positive trend of Pa during winter, especially for the reversed trend of Pa_Winter after change point, suggests the recovery of the wintertime Siberian High intensity38. The non-significant winter warming revealed by ground observation at Ergune was different from the pronounced winter warming reported worldwide39,40. As high pressure is indicative of descending air, causing sunny weather and low cloud coverage, the decreased TaMin was possibly influenced by the increased radiation cooling effect during winter. Therefore, the increased TaMax and the decreased TaMin suggest the strengthened heat wave and coldness in this region41.

In conclusion, the changes in the atomospheric circulation were captured by the observed records of the climate variables and the frequency of climate extremes41. Studies in Mongolia showed that the inter-annual variation trends of summer days, warm days, and warm nights have significantly increased with a significant increase rate of 2.8 d decade− 1 for warm nights41, and a significant reduction of − 0.6 d decade− 1 for cool nights (days). The seasonal pattern of climate warming is different from the European grassland which is more pronounced in winter than summer at mid- and high latitudes40. The positive trend of air temperature was accompanied by the increases in frequency of drought and heatwave37. As a result of the increased aridity, summer warming has limited impacts on the vegetation growth, and this study displayed insignificant trend for the peak value of vegetation dynamics.

The interaction of climate variables

At global scale, the highly correlated climate variables could be explained by three principal components, i.e. temperature, humidity and radiation7. In this study, however, only 2 principal components, i.e. humidity and temperature, were extracted, suggesting the insignificant roles of the cloud coverage and the global radiation in controlling the regional climate variability8. Beyond the correlations among the climate variables, the physical processes were accountable for the climate variabilities. For example, the Clausius-Clapeyron equation provided the fundamental relationship between the vapor pressure of the temperature. The intensity of extreme precipitation events should be increased at the background of climate warming, according to the Clausius-Clapeyron equation42. In this study, however, Pdays and P had decreased at the background of climate warming (Table 2), suggesting the Clausius-Clapeyron equation was not applicable in estimating precipitation at Ergune. However, the PMax_Summer displayed positive trends, suggesting the atmospheric moisture holding capacity had increased along with the increased TMP and TMXV_Summer. Further studies are needed to reveal the driving factors for P and PMax and to clarify the preconditions for application of Clausius-Clapeyron equation in arid and semi-arid regions.

This study revealed that the Ws and WsMax were driven by different climate variables. The Ws was possibly influenced by changes of spatial and temporal distribution of air pressure, and the decreased Ws revealed in this study complied with the global declining rates of wind speed and evaporative demand43. Whereas, WsMax was influenced by convective weathers concurrent with heavy rains and strong storms, the decreased WsMax was possibly influenced by decreased Pdays. The different results of driving factors revealed by correlation and Granger causality approaches suggest the hidden links in climate systems.

The climate-vegetation relationship

Up to date, there are numerous studies concerning about the climate change and its impacts on vegetation dynamics2,7–9,15−18. For example, You et al. reported the non-significant positive trend of NDVI but the significant positive trend of NDVI above a threshold of 0.2 in the Upper Amur9. And the trend of NDVI was also influenced by the land use change8. In this study, the finding of decreased growing season length (Table 4) complies with the increased aridity but contradicts with the increased growing season length in the Upper Amur9.

In contrast, studies about the feedback of vegetation to climate variability is limited10,14,19. As vegetation dynamics change surface albedo and modulate the regional radiation and energy balance and thereby influence large-scale atmospheric circulation1, resulting in a complicated interplay between climate change and vegetation responses. The Granger causality approach captures a component of the atmospheric circulations and biophysical cycles. For example, vegetation models suggest that CO2 fertilization could be the main driver of greening, and greening in turn could mitigate climate warming by increasing the carbon sink on land and altering biogeophysical processes through the evaporative cooling effects10. Moreover, the precipitation facilitates vegetation growth, and vegetation growth provides moisture in the air and increases humidity through canopy transpiration. And the increased humidity facilitates the vapor condensation and precipitation through the fluctuation of air temperature4. Without consideration on the time lag effect, such loops, feedbacks, biogeophysical cycles could be concluded as the memory effect24 or the residuals of climate-vegetation interaction8.

Besides the previous climate-vegetation studies that concluded in the controlling factors for vegetation growth8,9, the application of VAR model in this study helps in understanding the climate-vegetation interactions. In contrast to the vegetation feedback on temperature14, the instantaneous interaction between P and NDVI though the application of VAR model (Table 5) suggests the direct impact of the humidity on the vegetation dynamics. As this study site is located in semi-arid climate zone with poor vegetation coverage, the ecological effect of vegetation growth on regulating the regional climate variability is non-significant, which complies with the conclusions in semi-arid region of Mexico44 but contrary to the conclusions of studies in highly vegetated regions19,23.

As the ground meteorological observation records captured the characteristics of the climate-vegetation interactions, this study used the ground observed meteorological data for causality analysis. Upscaling the site level results to the regional conclusions is challenging due to the accuracy and the spatial and temporal resolutions of the different source of datasets. For example, with the time series of climate variables extracted from ECMWF / Copernicus Climate Change Service (https://www.ecmwf.int/) and the NDVI from MODIS MYD13Q1, we found the ground observed temperature records agree with the ECMWF datasets. But the agreement between the ground observed precipitation and the ECMWF datasets was poor. The agreement between GIMMS NDVI datasets and the MODIS MYD13Q1 was poor. As the VAR model for the Granger causality effect analyzed the random component of the decomposed time series, different data source provided different results. As this study aims to provided a novel approach, further studies should strengthen the finding of the driving factors at local, regional continental and global scales, with full consideration of the time-lag effect hidden in climate-vegetation interactions.

Besides the climate variables, human activities/anthropogenic influences such as land management might be significant in driving the vegetation dynamics12. For example, expansions of residential area and impermeable surfaces might decrease the ecological function and reduce the effect of vegetation feedback to climate change10. Irrigation and crop land expansion might increase the residuals of climate-vegetation relationship8,11. Ecological protection might increase the resilience of vegetation to climate variability7.

Shortcomings of Granger causality

Unlike the previous approaches using correlation and principal regression analyses, Granger causality is particularly suited for empirical investigations of cause-effect relationships45. Granger causality analysis could provide meaningful results when there were time-lag effects hidden in the explanatory variables and mostly applicable in economical researches. One problem with the empirical notion of Granger causality is its dependence on the set of variables included in the VAR models. Therefore, spurious causalities would be concluded on condition the key variables that affect two or more of the rest variables was absent for causality analysis. Therefore, physical explanations of the cause-effect relationships among climate variables are implicit. Therefore, the physical explanations of the cause-effect relationship needs to be strengthened. Future studies are needed to include anthropogenic factors and to separate the causality relationships into direct causes and indirect causes.

Conclusions

In this study, the moisture availability during summer is decreasing and the winter warming was non-significant, suggesting the decreased strength of East Asia summer monsoon and the recovery of the Siberian High Pressure. Humidity dominated the climate variability in summer, whereas, temperature was the dominated role in winter. The regional moisture availability influences the vegetation dynamics instantly, whereas, the feedback of the vegetation dynamics to temperature has a time lag effect. Further studies are needed to reveal the physical explanation and implication of the time lag effects in climate systems.
