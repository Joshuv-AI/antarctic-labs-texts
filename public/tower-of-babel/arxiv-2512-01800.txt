Conservative Formulations of the Standard Enskog and Povzner Equations
Zhe Chen

Abstract
. This article introduces a conservative formulation of the Standard Enskog equation and the Povzner equation, both of which generalize the Boltzmann equation by incorporating the contribution of particle volume in collisions. The primary result expresses these collision integrals as the divergence with respect to the velocity variable v v of a mass current. Moreover, the terms v ​ C ​ [ f , f ] vC[f,f] and | v | 2 ​ C ​ [ f , f ] |v|^{2}C[f,f] , where C ⁡ [ f , f ] C[f,f] denotes the Standard Enskog or Povzner collision integral, are represented as phase-space divergences—i.e., divergences in both position and velocity—of corresponding momentum and energy currents. This work extends Villani’s earlier result [Math. Modelling Numer. Anal. M2AN 33 (1999), 209–227] for the classical Boltzmann equation to the case of dense gases.

Introduction

This paper focuses on collisional kinetic models designed to describe the dynamics of monatomic gases. The most classical example is the Boltzmann equation, introduced by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1872, which models the statistical behavior of dilute gas particles through binary collisions. The Standard Enskog equation, proposed by David Enskog in 1921, extends Boltzmann’s framework to dense gases by accounting for particle size and excluded volume. The Povzner collision integral, introduced in 1965, generalizes the model by allowing collisions between molecules to be elastic or soft. Roughly speaking, Enskog’s model permits collisions on a sphere, while Povzner’s model extends this to collisions within a spherical shell.

The kinetic equation is connected to the conservation laws of mass, momentum, and energy through the definition of macroscopic quantities via the particle distribution function; see Section 3.3 in
[
1
]
, for example. At the time of this writing, the direct verification of local conservation laws for mass, momentum, and energy by classical solutions of the Standard Enskog equation or the Povzner equation, as discussed in Section
1
, does not seem to have been studied systematically. To address this gap, we propose a systematic method for deriving local conservation laws in the framework of kinetic models for these two equations. Our approach is based on expressing the collision integrals as divergences in the position and velocity variables of mass, momentum, and energy currents, drawing inspiration from a lesser-known idea by Landau regarding the Boltzmann collision integral; see, for instance,
[
13
,
7
]
.

1.1.
The Boltzmann equation

We begin with considering the following collisional kinetic equation

(1)

(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})f(t,x,v)=C[f,f](t,x,v),

where
f(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a single particle distribution depending on time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
space
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and velocity
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The most classical example is the hard sphere Boltzmann collision operator defined by

(2)

B[f,g]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(f(x,v^{\prime})g(x,w^{\prime})-f(x,v)g(x,w))\langle v-w,n\rangle_{+}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}n\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product in Euclidean space, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{+}:=\max\{0,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\}
. The pre-collision velocities
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
are determined by the unit vector
n\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

(3)

\begin{cases}v^{\prime}(v,w,n)=v-n\langle n,v-w\rangle\\
w^{\prime}(v,w,n)=w+n\langle n,v-w\rangle,\end{cases}

Here we list some basic properties of the collision transform;

•

The collision transform is reversible:

(4)

v^{\prime\prime}(v^{\prime}(v,w,n),w^{\prime}(v,w,n),n)=v;\quad w^{\prime\prime}(v^{\prime}(v,w,n),w^{\prime}(v,w,n),n)=w.

•

The collision transform is symmetric:

(5)

v^{\prime}(w,v,n)=w^{\prime}(v,w,n);\quad w^{\prime}(w,v,n)=v^{\prime}(v,w,n).

•

The collision transform is even with respect to
n
:

(6)

v^{\prime}(w,v,-n)=v^{\prime}(v,w,n);\quad w^{\prime}(w,v,-n)=w^{\prime}(v,w,n).

•

collision transform has Jacobian determinant:

(7)

\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})}{\partial(v,w)}\right)=-1.

•

The collision transform conserves momentum and energy:

(8)

v^{\prime}(w,v,n)+w^{\prime}(v,w,n)=v+w;\quad|v^{\prime}(x,w,n)|^{2}+|w^{\prime}(v,w,n)|^{2}=|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}.

•

The collision transform reverses the direction with respect to
n
:

(9)

\langle v^{\prime}(v,w,n)-w^{\prime}(v,w,n),n\rangle=-\langle v-w,n\rangle

The two particles collide at the same position
x
. Due to the pre-to-post transformation (
3

v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
v_{3}\\
|v|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{B}[f,f]\begin{pmatrix}1\\
v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
v_{3}\\
|v|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}v=\mathbf{0}.

This result is given by the weak formula for the Boltzmann collision interal which can be found in every textbook in kinetic theory, such as section 3.1 in
[

]
.
If we generalize the model to dense gases, which means the molecular radius cannot be neglected.
In that case, the centers of two colliding particles are no longer at the same position in space.

1.2.
The Standard Enskog equation

We write the following so-called Standard Enskog equation

(13)

(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})f(t,x,v)=E[f,f](t,x,v),

where Enskog collision integral is defiend as

(14)

E[f,g](t,x,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bigg[\chi(x-\frac{1}{2}\sigma n)f(t,x,v^{\prime})g(t,x-\sigma n,w^{\prime})\\
-\chi(x+\frac{1}{2}\sigma n)f(t,x,v)g(t,x+\sigma n,w)\bigg]\sigma^{2}\langle v-w,n\rangle_{+}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}n\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w,

the pre-collision velocities
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
are given by (
3
).

We have to say that it seems nonsensical to define Standard Enskog collision integral as a bilinear functional of distribution functions, since one always assumes that the correlation function
\chi
depends on one of the distribution function
f
. In that case, the two functions don’t share the symmetry structure anymore. Here, we write the collision integral in this way only for mathematical convenience.

In addition to Standard Enskog equation, some people have also proposed a Revised Enskog equation. It has more mathematical structure. For example, it has the symmetry between two colliding particles, and it is more easily generalized to the case of mixtures. (The standard Enskog equation can also be generated to mixture case, see Chapter 16 of
[
2
]
for instance.)
The Revised Enskog collision operator is

\displaystyle\tilde{E}[f,g]:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bigg(Y\big(\rho_{f}(x),\rho_{g}(x-\sigma n)\big)f(x,v^{\prime})g(x-\sigma n,w^{\prime})

\displaystyle-Y\big(\rho_{f}(x),\rho_{g}(x+\sigma n)\big)f(x,v)g(x+\sigma n,w)\bigg)\langle n,v-w\rangle_{+}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w\penalty\ \mathrm{d}n,

where the correlation function
Y
satisfies
Y(x,y)=Y(y,x)
.
The Enskog equation is a generalized Boltzmann equation aimed at modelling dense gases. When we formally derive the Boltzmann equation from Liouville equation,
we no longer consider the particles as mass points. We distinguish between the collision point and the particle center. Its advantage is to describe two different kinds of particles collisions.
The Enskog equation was first proposed by David Enskog in 1922

1.3.
The Povzner equation

The Povzner collision integral was proposed to study the delocalized collision by Povzner in
[
8
]
. The collision integral is of the following form

(15)

P[f,g]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(f(x,v^{\prime})g(y,w^{\prime})-f(x,v)g(y,w)\bigg)J(x-y,v-w)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y,

where the pre-collision velocities
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
are also given by (
3
) with
n=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}
.
J(\xi,v)
is assumed to satisfy the following four assumptions as showed in
[
8
]

(i)

J
is continuous, and
|J(\xi,v)|\lesssim|v|+1
.

(ii)

Time reversibility:

(16)

J(-\xi,-v)=J(\xi,v).

(iii)

Invariance after collision:

(17)

J(\xi,v^{\prime}-w^{\prime})=J(\xi,v-w).

(iv)

Short-range interaction: There exists a constant
R
such that
J(\xi,v)=0
for
|\xi|>R
.

An example of Povzner kernel in
[
5
]
is

J(\xi,v):=\frac{1}{2\delta^{3}}\frac{1}{\theta}H(\delta-|\xi|)H(\theta-|v|)\left|v\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|,

where
H
is the Heaviside step function.
From this example, we can find out more clearly that the Povzner collision integral allows for the possibility of a spatial smearing process with range of
\theta
and
\delta
of collisions as well as for the classically assumed process in which the molecules collide at a point.
We point out here that if we set
J(\xi,v)=\tilde{J}(|v|,\frac{\langle\xi,v\rangle}{|\xi||v|})\delta(|\xi|)
in (
15
), then we can recover the Boltzmann collision kernel.
However, the Povzner collision integral generalizes the following form of the Boltzmann collision integral:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}[f(x,v^{\prime})f(x,w^{\prime})-f(x,v)f(x,w)]|\langle v-w,n\rangle|\penalty\ \mathrm{d}n\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w,

which corresponds to (
2
) after changing
n
into
-n
and symmetrizing (
2
). In the Boltzmann collision operator, the direction of the unit vector
n
is inconsequential, as it cancels out when taking the Boltzmann-Grad limit. However, in the case of dense gases, the locations of the gain term and the loss term differ. Consequently, a more physically meaningful assumption would be:

(18)

J(\xi,v^{\prime}-w^{\prime})=J(-\xi,v-w),

instead of (
17
). Nevertheless, in the following discussion, we adhere to Povzner’s original assumption (
17
). For results derived under the new assumption (
18
), we refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|u_{f}|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}P_{f}\right)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}|u_{f}|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}P_{f}\right)u_{f}+P_{f}\cdot u_{f}+q_{f}\right]=0.\end{cases}

However, the situation changes significantly when dealing with the Enskog equation.

Corollary 1
.

Let
f^{E}
be a classical solution of the kinetic equation (
1
) with collision integral (
14
). Moreover
f^{E}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}_{v})
rapidly decaying with respect to

(43)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{f^{E}}+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{f^{E}}u_{f^{E}})=0\\
\partial_{t}(\rho_{f^{E}}u_{f^{E}})+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{f^{E}}u_{f^{E}}^{\otimes 2})+\nabla_{x}\cdot(P_{f^{E}}-\mathbb{I}^{E})=0\\
\partial_{t}(\frac{1}{2}|u_{f^{E}}|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}P_{f^{E}})\\
\qquad+\nabla_{x}\cdot[(\frac{1}{2}|u_{f^{E}}|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}P_{f^{E}})u_{f^{E}}+P_{f^{E}}\cdot u_{f^{E}}+q_{f^{E}}-\mathcal{I}_{4}^{E}]=0,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{I}^{E}_{i},i=1,2,3
are components of
\mathbb{I}^{E}
, namely

\mathbb{I}^{E}(t,x,v)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{1}^{E}\\
\mathcal{I}_{2}^{E}\\
\mathcal{I}_{3}^{E}\end{pmatrix}

Correspondingly, we have the similar result for the Povzner equation.

To conclude the corollary, we only need to integrate (
21
)–(
25
) with respect to
v
over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, it is important to note that the equations in (

1
) and computing
\mathcal{I}^{E}_{4}
,
\mathbb{I}^{E}
, and
q_{f^{E}}
. Furthermore, a closure relation is necessary. For example, to derive the Euler system, the distribution function
f
should approximate a Maxwellian such that
q_{f^{E}}
and the anisotropic part of
P_{f^{E}}
become negligible, i.e.,

q_{f^{E}}=\mathbf{0},\qquad P_{f^{E}}=p\mathrm{Id}_{3}.

This reduction allows us to solve the equations with four unknowns (
\rho,u,p
), instead of handling ten unknowns with only four constraints.

The corollary not only clarifies the influence of molecular volume occupation on macroscopic equations but also highlights a key distinction: the Boltzmann collision integral does not contribute to the stress tensor
P_{f}
—even a free transport equation can generate a stress tensor. In contrast, the Enskog and Povzner collsion integrals have a direct impact on the pressure tensor. This distinction is significant because the coefficient of pressure depends on the adiabatic index, indicating that the gas governed by equations (
43
) no longer behaves as a perfect gas. This observation provides an alternative perspective on the limitations of the perfect gas assumption for such systems.

It is straightforward to establish the local entropy inequality, as demonstrated in
[
3
]
, due to the availability of a stronger weak formulation for the Povzner collision integral:

(P[f,f],\phi)_{x,v}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{4}}f(x,v)f(y,w)J(x-y,v-w)\\
\times\big[\phi(x,v^{\prime})+\phi(y,w^{\prime})-\phi(x,v)-\phi(y,w)\big]\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y\penalty\ \mathrm{d}v\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w,

as given in equation (10) of

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\mathrm{ln}f(t,x,v)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x\penalty\ \mathrm{d}v\\
=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{4}}f(x,v)f(y,w)J(x-y,v-w)\mathrm{ln}\left(\frac{f(x,v^{\prime})f(y,w^{\prime})}{f(x,v)f(y,w)}\right)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y\penalty\ \mathrm{d}v\penalty\ \mathrm{d}w\\

which follows from the basic inequality: for

However, this result is less remarkable for the Povzner equation because the entropy inequality is identical to the original Boltzmann’s entropy. As such, we do not discuss it further here.
As for the Standard Enskog equation, the local and global entropy inequality are studied in

6.
Conclusion

This paper serves as a complement to
[
3
]
. While the general framework developed there does not cover all delocalized collision models, we have here established conservative formulations for two cases that fall outside the framework given in
[
3
]
, namely the orginal Povzner and standard Enskog collision integrals. A key feature of our result is that it does not rely on asymptotic expansions around equilibrium: the conservative form remains valid for distributions far from equilibrium, thereby extending its mathematical and physical relevance. This provides new insight into the structure of kinetic models where standard perturbative approaches fail, and reinforces the role of conservation and entropy principles in non-equilibrium regimes.

As a perspective, and in analogy with
[
3
]
, we plan to extend these results to multispecies systems. In particular, we aim to investigate how the delocalized collision framework can be adapted to the study of dense gases.
