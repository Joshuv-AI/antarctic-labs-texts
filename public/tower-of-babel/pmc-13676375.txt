Investigation of Differences in the Firing Patterns of Paraspinal Muscle Electromyography in Healthy Subjects
Authors: Hirokazu Shitogishi, Daisuke Yamabe, Makoto Suzuki, Yusuke Chiba, Hirotaka Yan, Ryo Hatayama, Hideki Murakami, Yasushi Chida, Kenichiro Kakutani, Minoru Doita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Limited data exist regarding the contraction and relaxation patterns of paraspinal muscles (PSMs) during dynamic tasks like gait in healthy individuals. Our preliminary findings, as a cross-sectional study, suggested the existence of distinct, highly individualized activation patterns of PSMs. This study aimed to classify these patterns and to investigate their relationships with functional mobility parameters. Methods A total of 57 healthy volunteers participated in 10-m gait and Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) tests. Paraspinal electromyography (EMG), tri-axial acceleration, and angular velocity were simultaneously recorded using wireless sensors at the lumbar region. Participants were classified into four distinct types (Types A, B, C, and D) based on the synchronization of PSMs EMG with vertical acceleration and EMG amplitude during gait. Functional parameters were compared using analysis of variance and Dunnett's post hoc tests (vs. Type A). Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the predictability of Type C. Results Type C (n=20), characterized by extremely weak PSMs EMG firing (≤±1300 μV) during gait, showed several functional impairments compared with Type A: significantly longer TUG completion time (p<0.05), and shallower anteroposterior inclination angle and reduced pitch angular velocity during the sit-to-stand transition (both p<0.05). Logistic regression confirmed that Type C could be reliably distinguished from Type A (area under the curve ≤0.973) using a combination of EMG-root mean square (EMG-RMS) and kinematic parameters. Conclusions Healthy individuals exhibit characteristic individual differences in PSM activation during walking and rising. The group with extremely weak PSM activation (Type C) showed compromised physical function and altered postural control strategies, suggesting that these neuromuscular patterns may reflect subtle functional variations, potentially acting as preclinical indicators of future functional decline. Keywords: paraspinal muscle electromyography, standing movement, gait analysis, wireless multi-function sensor

Introduction

The human paraspinal muscles (PSMs) are generally known to be involved in maintaining lumbar spinal stability and postural control, functioning in coordination with the surrounding trunk muscles1,2). In the orthopedic field, electromyographic activity of the PSMs is often reported to be higher in patients with low back pain (LBP) and lumbar spine disorders than in healthy individuals, particularly showing insufficient muscle contraction during prolonged posture maintenance or dynamic movements3-6). Conversely, some studies indicate insufficient muscle relaxation in certain patients, with sustained muscle tension even at rest7,8). Such abnormal muscle activity may lead to muscle fatigue and reduced blood flow9), potentially contributing to the chronicity of LBP. It is hypothesized that coordination among spinal muscles is compromised in patients with LBP, leading to overactivity in some muscles and insufficient activity in others10). Specifically, delayed onset timing of the multifidus and erector spinae muscles has been suggested to lead to decreased spinal stability, implying that some changes might originate even before the onset of symptomatic disease4,11). However, reports of the characteristics and specific function of PSMs in healthy individuals are scarce, and knowledge regarding their contraction and relaxation patterns during gait is limited. PSMs are crucial for postural control and are presumed to undergo cycles of contraction and relaxation during walking, yet their specific dynamics remain largely unclarified. Preliminary measurements in this study, which simultaneously recorded and analyzed acceleration (ACC) and paraspinal electromyography (EMG), identified individuals showing extremely low EMG activity during walking (Figure 1). Therefore, we formulated a working hypothesis that distinct individual differences exist in paraspinal EMG patterns during walking in healthy individuals. The Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) test is widely used to assess a person's Quality of Life (QOL) and as an indicator of fall risk. The TUG test evaluates the sequence of rising from a chair, walking, and sitting down again, allowing for analysis of differences between healthy individuals and patients12,13). Since the sit-to-stand transition, in particular, involves contraction and relaxation of the PSMs14,15), we decided to analyze the characteristics of PSM activity during this movement to understand the differences in PSM activity patterns observed during walking in healthy individuals. The purpose of this cross-sectional study was to clarify the characteristic contraction and relaxation patterns of the PSMs during gait and QOL-related movements by simultaneously recording and analyzing ACC, angular velocity, and EMG data using wireless sensors during a 10-m straight-line walk and the TUG test.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

Healthy volunteers were recruited from among the staff at the Iwate Medical University Hospital and local residents. Exclusion criteria, confirmed by verbal screening, included any history of cerebrovascular, neurological, or spinal disorders, as well as symptoms (e.g., LBP and lower limb pain) that could affect walking or sit-to-stand movements. A total of 57 participants (30 males and 27 females) enrolled. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This research has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors' affiliated institutions, and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sensor device and data acquisition

A small, wireless, multi-function sensor (SWS) (TSND151, ATR-Promotions, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure ACC, angular velocity, and inclination angle during movement. Simultaneously, EMG was recorded using a biological signal measurement amplifier (AMP-151; ATR-Promotions, Kyoto, Japan) connected to the SWS, hook-type electrode cables (SE-C-AMP-H40; ATR-Promotions, Kyoto, Japan), and electrodes (SE-EXP-LEC60; ATR-Promotions, Kyoto, Japan). All data were stored in the SWS memory and transferred via Bluetooth to a personal computer (PC) (Microsoft Surface Laptop5 R8N00020; Redmond, WA, USA) immediately after measurement. The sampling frequency for all data was set to 1,000 Hz. The data, along with time information, were saved as comma-separated value (CSV) files. The preamplifier bandpass filter for EMG measurement was set from 10 to 500 Hz. Participant videos during walking were recorded digitally on the PC using an external webcam (Logicool C920N HD PRO; Logicool, Lausanne, Switzerland).

Recording protocol

Immediately before the walking test, participants were briefed on the protocol, and their height, weight, and maximum grip strength were measured. Grip strength was recorded for both sides, and the average of the left and right measurements was used as the individual data. The SWS sensor was fixed directly to the skin at the midline of the L3 level using an adhesive bandage (Kinopress 5.0 cm; Nitto Denko, Osaka, Japan). EMG electrodes were placed approximately 3 cm lateral to the midline at the L1 level (over the muscle belly) for the PSMs. The reference electrode was placed 2 cm superior to the recording electrode. To prevent noise, the skin was wiped with an alcohol swab before electrode placement. The ground electrode was placed approximately 10 cm ventral to the L1 level midline on a non-muscular area of the back. Participants performed the TUG test, followed by the 10-m straight-line walking test, each performed three times. Participants were instructed to perform both tests at their spontaneous pace. To avoid the influence of shoes during walking, a 2-cm thick mat was placed on the walkway, and participants were instructed to walk in socks without shoes. For the TUG test, measurement started with a verbal cue after confirming the participant was seated; the time until the participant stood up, walked around a cone placed 3 m away, and sat back down was recorded. For the 10-m straight-line walk, the participant started walking from a standing position upon a verbal cue, turned around before a cone placed 10 m away, and returned to the start line. Video and time measurements were recorded.

Data analysis

The individual TUG time data were calculated as the average of the second and third trials. The average velocity during the 10-m walk was calculated from the elapsed time of the second outward journey. Sensor data (CSV files) were imported into Excel (Excel 2019; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) for analysis. The central part of the second outward journey of the 10-m walk, where gait was stable, was sampled for analysis. The data section used for comparing ACC, angular velocity, inclination angle, and EMG was sampled for two complete gait cycles, starting from a vertical ACC peak where walking was stable. The root mean square (RMS) was calculated for the composite ACC vector (Formula 1), ACC data in each direction, and bilateral EMG data (Formula 2), and was used for group comparison.

Based on visual observation of distinct firing patterns of paraspinal EMG during walking, all subjects were classified into Type A, Type B, Type C, and Type D based on the criteria in Table 1. Paraspinal EMG firing patterns during gait were classified into four types (Types A-D) based on the synchronicity between vertical ACC and EMG amplitude, evaluated using a 2-sec window (2,000 data points) of straight-line walking. Specifically, synchronicity of EMG and ACC data set was defined by correlation coefficients, with values >0.76 categorized as Types A and B, <0.76 as Type C, and <0.60 as Type D. Type C was further defined by a lack of clear ACC-synchronized bursts and an EMG amplitude consistently within ±1,300 μV. Among the synchronized patterns, cases with 1-8 bursts per gait cycle were classified as Type A, while those with >9 bursts were classified as Type B. Finally, cases exhibiting random EMG bursts with no synchronicity to vertical ACC were assigned as Type D.

Statistical analysis

Differences in measured values among Types A, B, C, and D were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett's test, comparing Type A with each other group (Table 2). Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the ability to classify the types16). The independent variables included EMG-RMS, TUG time, anteroposterior (AP) angle of inclination, maximum grip strength, and pitch direction angular velocity. The dependent variable was defined as a binary variable representing the difference from Type A. The backward elimination method was used for variable selection, with the significance level set at p<0.05. An interaction model was used to evaluate the synergistic effects between EMG-RMS and other item variables (Table 3). The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to evaluate significance. Excel (Excel 2019; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and EZR (EZR Version 1.68; Jichi medical University, Omiya, Saitama, Japan)17) were used for basic statistics and all statistical analyses.

Results

Visual observation of the paraspinal EMG waveforms during walking showed distinct characteristics among participants, despite all being healthy (Figure 1). These patterns tended to include: types with extremely low firing frequency seemingly unrelated to walking motion (Type C), types with firing frequency synchronized with walking motion (Types A and B), and types with firing not synchronized with walking motion (Type D). Based on these characteristics, the paraspinal EMG and up-down ACC data were classified into four types using the criteria detailed in Table 1. The classification resulted in 13 participants in Type A, 13 in Type B, 20 in Type C, and 11 in Type D (Table 2). The average age for each group was: Type A, 33±13 years; Type B, 49±17 years; Type C, 44±15 years; and Type D, 41±21 years. Type B was significantly older than Type A (p<0.05). Body mass index (BMI) showed no significant difference among the groups. Maximum grip strength was: Type A, 39.4±8.0 kg; Type B, 34.3±10.2 kg; Type C, 30.7±10.9 kg; and Type D, 31.6±10.8 kg. Type C and Type D showed significantly lower values than Type A (p<0.05). TUG test completion time was prolonged in Type C (p<0.05). No significant differences were observed among the groups for the duration of sub-movements or the number of steps during the TUG test. The AP inclination angle during the initial sit-to-stand movement of the TUG test (with sitting angle set to 0 degrees) was 47.7±8.0, 44.6±8.6, 40.6±6.4, and 41.3±7.9 degrees for Types A, B, C, and D, respectively. Type C and Type D showed significantly shallower inclination angles than Type A (p<0.05). Furthermore, the AP (pitch) angular velocity during the sit-to-stand transition was 41.8±9.9, 40.0±9.4, 33.8±7.8, and 37.7±8.4 deg/sec for Types A, B, C, and D, respectively, with Type C showing a significant decrease in angular velocity. No significant differences were observed among the groups in the average velocity of the 10-m walk or the RMS of the 3-directional ACC composite vector during walking (Table 2). Regarding the EMG waveform patterns (Figure 1), Type A and Type B showed EMG waveforms coordinated with ACC. Type B tended to exhibit a ‘valley’ when the action potential was fired. Type C and Type D both showed EMG waveforms that were not coordinated with ACC; Type C was characterized by smaller EMG amplitudes, whereas Type D showed larger amplitudes. The results of the logistic regression analysis, using Type A as the reference, are presented in Table 3. For Type B, only the TUG time demonstrated statistical significance (p=0.039). In contrast, Type C exhibited statistical significance across almost all single-variable and combination models, with the highest area under the curve (AUC) reaching 0.973 in the model combining EMG-RMS and TUG time. Although no single variable reached statistical significance for Type D in the main effects models, introduction of an interaction term between EMG-RMS and grip strength revealed a significant association (p=0.048).

Discussion

This study was initiated by the discovery of participants (Type C), among healthy individuals, exhibiting extremely weak firing of paraspinal EMG during walking (Figure 1). Therefore, ACC, inclination angle, angular velocity, and paraspinal EMG during the initial phase of the TUG test (sit-to-stand transition) were compared to clarify the differences between healthy participants with reduced paraspinal EMG and other healthy participants. It is generally reported that patients with structural changes, such as lumbar spinal stenosis or osteoarthritis, or those with LBP or joint pain, exhibit changes in paraspinal EMG activity3,4,11). Consequently, we initially hypothesized that there are no significant differences in sit-to-stand movements among healthy individuals. However, characteristic differences in the magnitude of paraspinal EMG firing were observed among participants during both sit-to-stand and walking (Figure 1 and 2). This suggests the possibility that, even within the healthy population, there are groups who actively use their PSMs for postural control and walking, and groups that do not. The comparison of the average composite ACC vector calculated from the tri-axial ACC showed no significant difference among the groups (Table 2). This suggests that all participants possessed normal walking ability and were not affected by walking impairment due to orthopedic diseases, such as spinal degeneration or osteoarthritis. In the sit-to-stand movement, Type B showed a tendency for muscle activity to persist longer from the initial movement to the standing position than Type A (Figure 2A). Although the quantitative comparison using average EMG-RMS showed a slight decrease in Type B compared with Type A, the difference was not significant (Figure 2B). However, Type B exhibited increased EMG firing synchronized with the up-down ACC peak during walking, and the average age was also significantly higher. These findings suggest that the response of Type B PSMs may be affected by age-related changes18,19). It is known that type I muscle fibers (slow-twitch) are relatively maintained with age, whereas type II fibers (fast-twitch) decrease. In addition, decreased tendon stiffness and Young's modulus are reported age-related changes20-22). Therefore, to compensate for decreased tendon stiffness, an aged PSM may require a larger, possibly unconscious contraction of the muscle belly to perform the same movement as a younger adult, which could be reflected as increased EMG firing. Though some reports suggest no age effect on EMG waveforms23,24), the significant age difference observed in Type B makes it plausible that their PSM response is affected by aging. Type C and Type D showed a significantly lower AP inclination angle and AP angular velocity during the initial sit-to-stand movement compared with that of Type A (Table 2). This suggests a strategy of relying less on trunk flexion and potentially more on lower limb muscles, or simply performing the movement more slowly/cautiously. The EMG changes in Type C and Type D might be due to factors other than simple local lumbar paraspinal tension. Sit-to-stand movement usually involves trunk flexion followed by a lower extremity squatting action14,15); however, Type C appears to perform walking and sit-to-stand movements with minimal use of the PSMs. Conversely, Type D showed EMG waveforms unrelated to movement, possibly due to sustained muscle tension, though the exact cause remains unclear. Furthermore, grip strength, a marker of sarcopenia and frailty, was significantly lower in Type C and Type D (Table 2), suggesting a possible link between the distinct PSM patterns and overall physical function/muscle weakness25,26). The difference between the significant EMG reduction in Type C and the seemingly random firing in Type D suggests that their distinct patterns might stem from functional rather than structural differences, although the exact mechanism warrants further investigation. The logistic regression analysis confirmed the classification accuracy, particularly for Type C, which showed high AUC values (0.723-0.973) and significant p values across most parameters when compared with Type A. For Type D, the significant interaction between EMG-RMS and handgrip strength suggests a ‘neuromuscular mismatch.’ Despite having relatively preserved muscle strength, these individuals exhibit dysregulated paraspinal activity that is poorly synchronized with gait rhythm, potentially reflecting an early stage of impaired spinal motor control. This strongly suggests that reduction in paraspinal EMG in the Type C group is a reliable distinguishing feature associated with measurable differences in TUG performance and posture strategy. Although previous studies using MRI have demonstrated a correlation between handgrip strength and PSM volume, our results extend these findings by showing that low handgrip strength is also associated with reduced dynamic PSM activity (EMG-RMS) during gait.

This study is a cross-sectional study, and therefore has limitations. We believe that further longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the causal relationship. Although sagittal alignment is a key factor, whole-spine X-ray was omitted due to ethical concerns regarding invasive radiation exposure in healthy individuals, which remains one of the limitations of this study. In addition, measurements were taken only once, precluding the evaluation of changes over time or with aging. Future studies should include longitudinal observation to assess age-related waveform changes and the long-term impact on the incidence of orthopedic diseases in each group.

Conclusions

In healthy participants, two distinct groups were identified during walking and TUG sit-to-stand movements: those with active PSM firing and those with extremely weak firing. Participants exhibiting weak PSM firing (Type C) were characterized by a significantly prolonged TUG completion time, a shallower trunk inclination and slower AP angular velocity during rising, and a reduced grip strength. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the group with weak PSM firing could be reliably classified and distinguished from the actively firing group. This novel classification of paraspinal EMG patterns in healthy individuals suggests that differences in neuromuscular strategies are present even without symptoms, potentially indicating subtle variations in postural control ability.
