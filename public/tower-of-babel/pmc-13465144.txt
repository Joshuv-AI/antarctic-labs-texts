Measles challenges: global resurgence and factors affecting eradication
Authors: Eltayib H. Ahmed Mohamedosman, Mohammad Fahad Ullah, Tarig M. S. Alnour, Elmutuz H. Elssaig, Aadil Yousif, Mohamed I. Holie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Measles is one of the most highly contagious and inflictive human viral infections, and approximately 90% of exposed individuals become infected following the exposure. The infection is communicable from one day before the prodromal symptom onset until four days after rash onset. Up to 40% of people who attract measles will have other health problems arising during the course of infection. There is no specific treatment for measles. Recent reports highlight that measles infection erases the already existing immune memory of various pathogens. The latest measles outbreaks in sub-Saharan Africa and Europe show the ease with which measles virus can re-enter communities. However, measles outbreaks in recent years have been reported in all regions of the world, including countries that had previously declared the disease eliminated such as United Kingdom and the United States. The present review article provides an outlook of the current epidemiology and challenges to support the global efforts in controlling the disease through discussion on the recent resurgence and enumerating the factors hindering the eradication of measles. This constellation of factors contributing to measles resurgence and outbreak propagation is multifactorial. Key drivers include suboptimal vaccine coverage, stemming from either outright vaccine refusal or barriers to healthcare access leading to incomplete vaccination schedules. This results in a diminution of herd immunity, compromising community-wide protection. Furthermore, primary or secondary vaccine failure, while rare, contributes to a residual pool of susceptible individuals. Diagnostic challenges, including the misdiagnosis of measles as other exanthematous illnesses, can delay outbreak containment and facilitate silent transmission. An epidemiological consequence of prolonged low transmission is an upward shift in the age-specific susceptibility profile, exposing older children and adults who lack natural immunity. Finally, sociopolitical instability, conflict, and resultant internal displacement create conditions conducive to outbreaks by disrupting healthcare infrastructure, hampering vaccination campaigns, and fostering high-density living conditions among vulnerable populations. This review addresses a major gap in measles eradication by integrating scattered evidence on measles resurgence into an integrated comprehensive framework, encompassing health system, epidemiological, immunological, and sociopolitical factors.

Introduction

The highly contagious respiratory illness measles continues to generate substantial public health concerns globally even though effective vaccination already exists [1]. It has affected humans for centuries and primarily targets infants who usually have naïve immunity and individuals with compromised immune systems. Measles virus has a ssRNA which is negative sense and it is an enveloped pathogen classified within the family Paramyxoviridae and Morbillivirus genus [2]. As a single-stranded negative-sense RNA virus, the measles virus utilizes multiple host factors for its complex life cycle with 16,000 nucleotides in its genome [3]. This genetic material encodes for several structural proteins essential for the virus’s replication and pathogenesis. The virus uses two essential proteins including hemagglutinin (H) and fusion protein (F) to achieve efficient host cell infection. The hemagglutinin protein enables virus-cell attachment through its receptor-binding mechanism on cellular surfaces. The initial stage of infection occurs through this binding mechanism that enables the virus to penetrate the cell. The fusion protein enables the viral envelope to fuse with the host cell membrane so the viral genome can release into the cytoplasm of the cell [4]. The measles virus (MeV) shows 24 distinct genotypes that allow worldwide tracking of its transmission patterns. The classification of genotypes depends on genetic differences within virus genome sequences specifically in the H and N nucleoprotein genes [5].

Measles first emerged as a zoonotic virus through cattle interactions which occurred during the Neolithic era. The bovine rinderpest virus shares a direct evolutionary relationship with the measles virus according to recent phylogenetic investigations. This close relationship suggests that MeV likely evolved from a rinderpest-like virus that jumped from cattle to humans [6].

It is worth mentioning that countries like United States achieved measles elimination in 2000 yet the disease continues to resurface predominantly among populations that do not receive proper vaccination [7]. Data shows measles incidence and mortality rates have decreased substantially after the introduction of vaccine over the years, however in recent times there is a worldwide resurgence of measles [8].

The World Health organization (WHO) has classified the world into six regions: South-East Asia region, African region, Eastern Mediterranean region, Americas region, European region, and Western Pacific region. Four regions (Africa, Southeast Asia, Eastern Mediterranean, and Western Pacific) are endemic with measles [9]. The Global Vaccine Action Plan in 2011 was planned to eliminate measles infection in at least five WHO regions by 2020. Unlikely, since 2019, measles elimination efforts have not reflected a substantial outcome in any region. In 2020 the World Health Assembly (WHA), endorsed the immunization agenda 2030 which has been dedicated to eliminating measles viruses in at least five WHO regions by 2030. In 2023, the worldwide average for two-dose measles vaccination coverage stood at 65.3%, remaining below the ≥ 95% target in all regions, Plans- Rubió noted deterioration in all measles vaccination coverage indicators between 2019 and 2023 [10].

The recent measles epidemiology shows a marked post-pandemic resurgence in Europe alongside persistent wave-like transmission in Africa, driven largely by immunity gaps and under-vaccinated pockets. In 2024 more than 127,300 cases were reported (nearly double than 2023 and the highest since 1997) in European region; 40% of these cases were children under 5 years old [11]. While African region surveillance (Jan 2024–early Feb 2025) showed that West Africa had the highest case burden, it was the second wave in East and Southern Africa (around October 2024) in which transmission disproportionately affected young children (1–4 years). Globally, WHO noted that the cases surged in 2024 (estimated 11 million infections) and 59 countries reported outbreaks [13]. While measles elimination is technically feasible, recent resurgence highlight rapid re-establishment of transmission when immunity gaps accumulate. Existing evidence is dispersed across surveillance summaries, outbreak reports, and single factor analysis with rarely integrated synthesis that links the post-pandemic measles epidemiology especially in Europe and Africa to the interacting barriers that sustain transmission. This review consolidates these drivers into a unified framework and narrates how their combined effects hinder eradication.

Etymology & historical perspective

It is believed that both “misella” which translates to small illness in Latin and medieval English word “mesles” evolved into the modern English term “measles.” Historical writings identified measles as different from both smallpox and scarlet fever while specifying its specific conditions. The middle Ages medical community identified measles independently as “morbilli” through their use of this Latin terminology [14]. Throughout Europe together with certain Asian territories measles was a common illness during the medieval era. The records show that England and Scotland experienced measles outbreaks starting from the 17th century first becoming evident in 1670 and 1674. Measles maintained its endemic state throughout the eighteenth century and subsequently the cases increased during both the nineteenth and twentieth centuries [15]. The pathogen existed perpetually among affected communities although outbreaks happened occasionally. Measles maintained its endemic status because its continuous virus spread kept interacting with new groups of vulnerable individuals born into the population. It was through the work of Persian physician Rhazes the first complete clinical description of measles was documented in the 10th century while distinguishing this disease from smallpox [16]. His research established early understanding of disease symptoms together with their evolution. Thomas Sydenham established a contemporary description of measles in 1693 which produced advanced clinical insights about the condition [17]. Through his detailed observations Sydenham documented the defining signs of measles alongside fever and respiratory symptoms and the rash that appeared as the disease progressed. His observations highlighted the complexity of complications such as pneumonia and encephalitis which indicated that this illness could become serious for some patients. The scientific community confirmed measles as a viral disease during the early twentieth century when major epidemiological findings emerged from an 1846 Faroe Islands outbreak [15]. Further the measles virus research in 1954 initiated vaccine development and in 1958 scientists created a live attenuated vaccine that was first approved by the United States in 1963 [18]. The global use of this vaccine has played an essential role in decreasing measles incidence and rates of infection throughout the world [19]. The mortality risk from measles infections differed widely based on age groups and economic factors as well as overall health status of individuals. Severe outcomes and mortality rates from measles most strongly affected infants along with young children and individuals who had certain health conditions and weakened immune systems. The social economic divide significantly contributed to measles outcomes because poor living conditions combined with insufficient access to health care made people more susceptible to infections and limited their abilities to seek proper medical care [20].

Disease transmission and symptoms’ diagnostic utility

Measles shows high contagiousness as it spreads easily through the air (airborne disease) while infecting many potential patients who have not developed immunity against it. Since 2021, measles virus transmission is largely driven by just two predominant genotypes, B3 and D8, which have replaced the numerous genotypes that circulating in earlier years. The B3 is often linked to African origins whereas D8 is linked to Asia, both genotypes caused outbreaks worldwide even in highly vaccinated zones [21, 22]. The genotype seems to be a predictor for measles transmissibility. Ackley et al. conducted study entitled genotype specific measles transmissibility: a branching process analysis and reported that Genotype B3 is significantly associated with higher transmissible than other non B3 genotypes [23].

The measles disease ranks as a highly contagious, spreading rapidly among uninfected populations since it has a reproductive rate (R0) ranging from 12 to 18 in completely susceptible groups [24]. In a population consisting of only susceptible individuals the reproductive rate determines how many new infections result from one infected person. A measles patient remains contagious throughout a period spanning four days before symptom development and four days after the rash emerges [25]. Thus the virus has the ability for transmission before people recognize their infection status rendering effective control measures challenging to implement [26].

During the prodromal phase which represents the first stage of measles the symptoms show nonspecific patterns that delay the detection of possible infections. The illness presents symptoms such as fever combined with malaise together with headaches that frequently occur during viral infections. The progressive increase in body temperature can exceed 40 °C while patients experience non-specific discomfort and fatigue. The identification of Koplik’s spots in about 70% of cases represents the hallmark symptom for measles diagnosis [27]. Koplik’s spots exist as small bluish white lesions present on the buccal mucosa within the lining of the cheeks. They typically appear 1–2 days before the onset of the measles rash and are a valuable diagnostic clue for early identification of the disease. A maculopapular rash, the hallmark of measles, appears after the prodromal phase, typically 3–4 days after the onset of initial symptoms. The rash begins on the face and neck before spreading to the entire body in a cephalocaudal (head-to-toe) progression. The rash is characterized by small, raised, red bumps (papules) that merge together to form larger, flat, discolored areas (macules) [28].

In classic measles disease fever and maculopapular rash remain the cornerstone clinical predictors of measles and form the basis of widely used case-finding definitions [29]. The diagnostic utility of fever and rash combination is strongly context dependent. While the clinical sensitivity remains relatively high (approximately 76–88%), the positive predictive value (PPV) of clinical diagnosis declines sharply when measles incidence decrease, especially in regions where other causes of febrile rash illness such as rubella, human herpesvirus-6, parvovirus B19, drug eruptions are common [30]. Consistent with this finding, Husada et al. stated that adding rash characteristic details such as hyperpigmentation improves the sensitivity but often leaves specificity low; for example, the combination of fever, maculopapular rash and hyperpigmented rash achieved 90.7% sensitivity but only 28.6% specificity [31]. Other associated symptoms also showed variable diagnostic value, the “3 Cs” (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis) which usually present with fever during the prodromal period are nonspecific especially in low-incidence settings where multiple viral exanthems present similarly. In contrast, Koplik spots can markedly improve clinical confidence when present, numerous outbreak evaluations reports showed high specificity and PPV up to 100% for Koplik spots. Although sensitivity is limited because many patients present after rash is widespread, however the reported specificity and sensitivity vary across settings [31]. Atypical presentations further complicate clinical diagnosis. Secondary infection including secondary vaccine failure, may present with milder or absent respiratory symptoms, less extensive rash, or more prominent gastrointestinal manifestations, further reducing the reliability of symptoms based predication and illustrating why clinical criteria alone cannot reliably contribute to the effective diagnosis of measles without laboratory support [32].

A subset of patients has been observed to develop a severe clinical course requiring intensive care, including severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), encephalitis, and multi-organ involvement which reflect both host inflammatory responses and direct viral effects [33]. Measles inflammatory response is biphasic and complex in which the initial host responses include activation of innate immunity, cytokine, and subsequent acquired immune responses that contribute to both viral clearance and immune dysregulation including immune amnesia and transient immunosuppression that increases susceptibility to secondary infections and significantly contributes to overall morbidity and hospitalization burden [34]. Severe pneumonia involves a dysregulated immune response characterized by simultaneous immune suppression and hyperinflammation, reflected by raised inflammatory ratios. This imbalance drives impaired immune defense, tissue damage, and is connected with increased risk of intensive care admission, respiratory failure, and poor clinical outcomes [35].

Severe outcomes are more likely among young children, immunocompromised patients and malnourished individuals while hospitalization occur in up to 20% of cases as it has been reported that around one in every five unvaccinated measles cases require hospitalization [36]. The measles complications include diarrhea, dehydration, otitis media, pneumonia (about 1 in 20 children), laryngotracheobronchitis, encephalitis, blindness, keratitis. Pneumonia being the leading cause of measles-related death, acute encephalitis (neurological complications) occur in approximately one case for every 1,000 reported cases; two to three deaths occurred for every 1,000 measles reported cases [33].

The temporal dynamics of clinical presentation align closely with laboratory markers and should guide specimen collection. Rash onset coincides with the developing adaptive immune response and often parallels declining infectious virus level. Viral RNA is often most detectable when respiratory samples are collected early within the first three days after rash onset while delaying sampling increase the risk of false negative results [32]. Serology testing is also time dependent; measles IgM may be low or undetectable in the initial days following rash onset, rising thereafter with delayed or absent IgM responses in modified or secondary cases [37].

Global epidemiology and resurgence of measles

Despite the availability of vaccines, measles remains a significant public health issue, causing over 100,000 deaths annually, particularly in Africa, South America, and Asia. Earlier a report indicated a significant increase in measles cases globally in 2018, with a 167% increase compared to 2016 [38]. This resurgence reversed decades of progress toward elimination in many countries [39]. In 2019, the number of reported measles cases reached 869,770, the highest since 1996, with an estimated 207,500 deaths, marking a 50% increase since 2016 [40]. Between 2021 and 2022, estimated measles cases increased by 18%, from 7,802,000 to 9,232,300, and the number of countries experiencing large or disruptive outbreaks increased from 22 to 37. Estimated measles deaths also increased by 43% during this period, from 95,000 to 136,200. In 2023, global measles cases were 1.8 times higher than in 2022 [41, 42]. This represents a significant increase in measles incidence compared to previous years, and reflects the ongoing challenges in controlling the disease globally. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the situation by disrupting vaccination campaigns and surveillance efforts, leading to an increase in cases and outbreaks [43]. The WHO African region reported the most deaths (63%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean (29%) and South-East Asia regions (7%), these regions are characterized by weak healthcare systems, low vaccination coverage, and high rates of malnutrition, which contribute to the severity of measles and increase the risk of death. The disproportionate burden of measles in these regions highlights the need for targeted interventions to improve vaccination rates and address underlying health vulnerabilities [44]. The clinical implication of non-vaccination can be gauged from the emerging facts that measles outbreaks and high mortality rates are observed in under-vaccinated communities [45].

However, WHO measles surveillance displayed confirmed case into three groups; (i) laboratory-confirmed cases in which vaccine-associated illness has been excluded, (ii) epidemiologically linked cases that fulfill suspected case definition and are linked in time and place to a laboratory confirmed or previously epidemiological linked case, (iii) clinically compatible cases that meets clinical criteria but lacks adequate specimen and is not epidemiological linked [48]. The WHO European Region in 2024 reported 127,411measles cases, 85,472 (67.1%) of them were laboratory confirmed, 20,127 (15.8%) were epidemiological linked and 21,812 (17.1%) were clinically compatible. In contrast, the WHO African Region registered 86,127 cases, predominance of 51,811 (60.2%) of epidemiological linked, followed by 18,432 (21.4%) laboratory confirmed, and 15,884 (18.4%) were clinically compatible [49].

Measles outbreak can also be classified according to country status; that categorize countries with eliminated status; in these regions outbreaks are generally imported or import-related, frequently limited in size and affect under-vaccinated individuals. Countries near elimination which have high vaccine coverage (> 95% MCV2 coverage), where outbreaks often occur in pockets of susceptibility. Endemic countries with ongoing transmission and frequent outbreaks, reflecting persistent programmatic barriers [50].

However, the incidence of measles and clinical impact are strongly influence by age and the susceptibility pattern is shaped by the vaccination timing and accumulation of unvaccinated cohorts. In the European region in 2024, measles infection affected all age, with remarkable concentration (46.1% of cases) occurring in children less than 5 years (13%) of cases infants less than one year and 33.1% of cases children (1–4 years) [12]. Same pattern was reported in WHO African Region where the burden of measles concentrated in young children [51], highlighting persistent vulnerability in early childhood where immunity gaps exist [52]. Affected birth cohorts are predominantly under vaccinated or unvaccinated, especially in infants with reduced maternal immunity and older children who received early or less effective measles vaccine doses, indicating persisting gaps in vaccination coverage [53].

Factors hindering the eradication of measles

To date, among humans, smallpox is the only eradicated disease, while polio is the next eradication candidate. Measles is far more contagious than smallpox and Polio (both have R0 5–7), thus requiring much higher vaccination coverage (95%) for herd immunity, making it harder to eliminate entirely [54]. In 2012, the WHA endorsed a Global Vaccine Action Plan aimed at eliminating measles, rubella, and congenital rubella syndrome in all six World Health Organization (WHO) regions by 2020. However, these goals were not achieved, prompting the development of a new strategic framework targeting the elimination of measles and rubella by 2030. At present, none of WHO’s measles elimination objectives are being fulfilled. Nonetheless, efforts to address the challenges continue. The measles vaccine is currently administered as part of the trivalent MMR vaccine, which also protects against mumps and rubella. A major barrier to measles eradication lies in vaccination related issues. These include the high cost of the vaccine, wastage due to multi-dose vials, improper vaccine reconstitution, insufficient cold chain storage, and a shortage of trained healthcare professionals [55].

The factors which have negative effect on controlling the measles eradication could be categorized into five main groups as described in the sections ahead.

Health system factors

Suboptimal vaccination rates and incomplete vaccine dose

Failure to achieve and sustain required measles vaccination coverage (≥ 95% for two-dose) represents one of the principal barrier to measles eradication, as it weakens herd immunity, increases the susceptible individuals, facilitates transmission, drives recurrent outbreaks and immunity gaps.

The vaccination coverage remained under the required level through all WHO regions. This under coverage concurred with marked global resurface of measles in 2023 and geographically extended large and disruptive outbreaks which were mainly concentrated in African and Eastern Mediterranean regions. Further the European region experienced a major resurface in 2024 despite high regional average MCV1coverage, highlighting the influence of suboptimal vaccination and incomplete vaccine dose [56].

The global data showed that the immunization coverage for one dose of measles vaccine is ranged between 6% and 67% [57], worldwide with only 14.4% of countries had completed measles vaccine doses. Thus the level of anti-measles herd immunity was not sufficient against measles viruses [10]. Several reports showed that even high single dose coverage of measles vaccine is not sufficient to provide protection and prevent outbreaks [58]. A number of measles cases were described in United States in 2019 as a result of suboptimal vaccination [32]. Kurugöl et al. reported that, in the past, measles outbreaks in Turkey occurred every 3 to 4 years due to vaccination coverage being around 80% [59].

The interpretation of waning vaccine that enhanced measles immunity should be done cautiously with clear differentiation between the losses of clinical protection and drop of antibody titres. Depending on observational evidence and outbreak studies WHO stated that antibody production against clinical measles does not appear to be decreased substantially after the time following measles vaccination [60]. In low incidence or post elimination setting, secondary vaccination failure was reported in 2–10% of vaccinated people with evidence showing that secondary vaccination failure may arise after 6–26 years of the last dose depending on the context [61]. These patterns are more common in non-endemic settings where nonspecific immunity boosting is absent [60]. The cornerstone of measles outbreak prevention is achieving and sustaining two-dose coverage, however currently the CDC does not recommend further MMR dose [62].

Lack of vaccination and delay in immunization campaigns

The measles vaccine is highly effective; a single dose offering approximately 95% protection and two doses providing about 97% immunity significantly reducing the risk infection by 20–25 fold compared to unvaccinated individual [63]. The mean MCV1 coverage in the European region was about 92% in 2022 whereas the MCV2 for the same period in the same area was around 89%, demonstrating that program vulnerability may depend on dose completion and subnational gaps rather than initial uptake [64].

However, lack of vaccination is also linked to residential instability, including among travelers and individuals living in remote rural areas beyond the reach of vaccination programs where vaccinating children is particularly challenging. Moreover, routine vaccination has suffered enormous delays, the mass vaccine campaigns supposed to be conducted every 24–48 months in middle and low income countries [65]. While the Pan-American Health Organization recommends follow up campaigns every 36–48 months [66], in practice these campaigns are often suspended, due to logistical constraints and health system shocks; such as delayed in measles immunization campaigns in many countries in 2021 due to COVID-19 infections; at that time only 81% of children received their first measles vaccine dose, which is considered the lowest worldwide coverage rate since 2008 [67]. Failure to maintain high performance levels in vaccination campaigns over time has been a significant challenge. Inconsistent efforts, resource limitations, and shifting public health priorities often lead to declining vaccination coverage, undermining long term disease control and elimination goals. Masresha et al. selected African countries with high routine immunization coverage based on first-dose measles vaccination rates to assess their readiness for measles elimination verification. Among the 11 countries identified as high performing in measles vaccination, six (54.5%) had maintained a first-dose measles vaccine (MCV1) coverage of 95% or higher since 2011. However, in 2015, only three countries (27.3%) achieved 95% or higher coverage for the second dose (MCV2). They concluded that despite strong first dose coverage, all countries exhibited programmatic gaps, indicating that none fully met the criteria to undergo measles elimination verification at that time [68]. In early 2025 (February 26), the Texas Department of State Health Services reported the first death from measles of a school-aged child who was not vaccinated in the ongoing outbreak in the South Plains and Panhandle regions [45].

Low performing routine surveillance

A sensitive surveillance system is crucial for promptly detecting and confirming suspected measles cases. It plays a key role in guiding immunization programs to take timely and effective action to control and limit the spread of the disease [68]. Effective measles surveillance guidelines enable rapid detection of cases and prompt outbreaks response, thereby demonstrating their value through performance indicators such as high rates of discarded suspect cases displaying strong clinical vigilance and timely laboratory confirmation. These element limit onward transmission, facilitate the identification of imported and import-related cases, and assist in detecting immunity gap among vulnerable populations thereby supporting elimination goals [69].

The key performance indicators for measles surveillance emphasze on timeliness, completeness, and laboratory confirmation. Basic benchmarks comprise the annual discarding of at least two non-measles rash illness cases per 100.000 people, investigating of at least 80% of suspected cases within 48 h of notification, and collecting sufficient specimens from at least 80% of cases for laboratory investigations within 3 days of rash onset. Surveillance performance fluctuates among different regions, the African region has revealed improvements in certain indicators, but face continuing challenges, especially in timeliness, completeness; while Eastern Mediterranean region showed deterioration in numerous key indicators between 2019 and 2022, despite progress in certain areas like specimen submission [70].

Gaps in measles surveillance system indicate that the system might not be capable enough to respond and detect outbreaks immediately and the actual burden of disease is mostly underestimated [57]. Numerous countries faced financial problems to establish strong surveillance system; Peck et al. reported that most African countries used the flaccid paralysis surveillance infrastructure to establish Measles surveillance system. However, in developing countries and due to inaccessibility and instability the routine vaccination is mostly lead by nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), the presence of numerous NGOs operating in the same region can lead to inconsistencies in data collection, as different case investigation forms may be used. As a result, the measles vaccination status of case patients is often not consistently recorded [58]. Bashir et al. recently reported that the measles surveillance system in Kashmir is not functioning effectively, with case based surveillance falling short of WHO guidelines. The study highlighted significant gaps in planning, advocacy, awareness, and communication among key stakeholders. Major barriers to effective surveillance included inadequate training, insufficient logistical support, lack of incentives, and limited monitoring by both internal and external agencies [71].

Misdiagnosis of measles infection

The absence of epidemiologic risk factors such as travel to endemic areas, known outbreaks, or a history of measles exposure does not rule out the possibility of infection. Due to the limited presence of these risk indicators and indices, cases are particularly likely to be overlooked [32].

Primary measles infection in unvaccinated individuals typically presents with upper respiratory symptoms, as documented in prior studies, followed by the appearance of a widespread maculopapular rash [57]. In contrast, secondary measles infections often manifest with milder or no respiratory symptoms, a less extensive rash, or prominent gastrointestinal symptoms. Secondary vaccine failures are more prevalent than previously believed, accounting for approximately 11% of reported measles cases in 2019 [32].

Additionally, measles vaccine-associated rash illness (VARI) occurs in about 5–15% of vaccine recipients and can resemble measles clinically, leading to potential misdiagnosis. According to WHO criteria, VARI is characterized by a rash without accompanying respiratory symptoms. However, Cui et al. reported that some VARI cases did present with cough and coryza. Moreover, a few also exhibited Koplik spots which considered pathognomonic for measles making it challenging to clinically distinguish wild-type measles from VARI [72]. The identification of VARI relies on laboratory assays. However, the diagnosis of measles is based on clinical examinations and laboratory confirmation is rare. Kurugöl et al. determined the true reported measles cases in Turkey and showed that most of health care workers and clinicians do not have enough aware of measles case definition and most of the medical students will be graduated without seeing case of measles [59]. However, misdiagnosis (false negatives) hinders measles control efforts, causing delays in containment, leading to further transmission as well missing real cases owing to atypical presentations allowing measles virus to continue circulate undetected while false positives result can erode vaccines public trust. Consequently, misdiagnosis lowers the quality of surveillance information that is used to guide supplementary immunization activities, identification of population immunity gaps and accurate classification of cases [73].

Measles vaccination failure

Defective vaccines or failure in the development of immunity are among the most commonly cited factors contributing to measles outbreaks. Primary vaccine failure occurs when an individual fail to develop protective immunity after vaccination, while secondary vaccine failure refers to the fading of immunity over time in individuals who initially responded to the vaccine [74]. One theoretical reason for measles vaccination failure is the genetic variation between the vaccine strains and circulating wild type measles viruses. Ensuring close genetic alignment between the vaccine strains and the circulating wild type viruses is crucial for maintaining vaccine effectiveness and minimizing the risk of antigenic mismatch dissimilarity [75]. However, if the vaccine strain differs significantly from the circulating wild type strains, it may fail to adequately stimulate the immune system, resulting in reduced vaccine effectiveness and increased risk of infection [76].

Genetic mutations may alter the virus’s surface proteins, such as the hemagglutinin protein, potentially affecting how well the immune system can recognize and respond to the virus. This could diminish vaccine effectiveness, particularly if the vaccine is designed to target specific viral proteins [77–79]. While the measles virus has a relatively stable genome compared to other RNA viruses like influenza and SARS-CoV-2, genetic changes such as antigenic drift can still occur over time [80–82]. However, measles vaccine strains are live attenuated, the mutations within the vaccine virus can occur but they are rare, there is a theoretical possibility that the measles virus could mutate within a vaccinated individual and become more virulent [83]. The primary concern would be a mutation that enables the virus to evade immune recognition [84, 85]. If mutations alter the virus’s epitopes (the specific parts recognized by the immune system) it could reduce the effectiveness of the immune response triggered by the vaccine. This could potentially lead to decreased vaccine efficacy in preventing infection [86, 87]. As a result, even vaccinated individuals might become vulnerable to infection [86].

Current measles vaccine strains offer cross protection, meaning they can still trigger an effective immune response even when there are minor genetic differences between the vaccine strains and circulating wild type viruses. This ability to recognize and respond to slightly varied strains contributes to the continued effectiveness of the vaccine despite small mutations [88, 89].

Epidemiologic factors

Loss of herd immunity

Beyond the coverage gaps which is discussed in Sect. 5.1.1, measles persistence is gradually driven by the increasing burden of susceptible people over time even in regions previously declared free of measles infection. Primary vaccine failure, waning immunity and even small proportions of unvaccinated individuals, can progressively build up across successive cohorts, ultimately exceeding the threshold required for sustained transmission. Plans-Rubió carried out study to trace the reasons behind persistent measles infection in the European Union in 2017–2018; they found that population immunity remained below the required threshold over time from 2010 to 2017. In 22 countries (75.9%), the proportion of individuals with vaccine induced measles immunity within the target vaccination population was below the population immunity threshold [90]. However; absence of natural immune boosting in both post-elimination and low-incidence settings further amplifies this process and could be major contributing factor to the occurrence of epidemics [91]. Similarly, Sasaki et al. conducted a study to investigate the rise in sporadic measles cases in Japan in 2019. They examined the persistence of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody titers against the measles virus in participants aged 17 to 24 years and found that IgG titers declined over time following the last vaccination. This temporal decrease suggests that periodic measles vaccination is necessary to prevent sporadic infections among both young and older adults [92].

Moreover, multiple reports have documented measles infections in individuals who had received two doses of measles-containing vaccine [91], indicating that herd immunity is not a static threshold but a dynamic, time-dependent phenomenon influenced by immunological waning and heterogeneity in vaccine uptake [93–95].

Berry et al. published a study titled “Nosocomial outbreak of measles amongst a highly vaccinated population in an English hospital setting,” in which they confirmed five cases of measles in individuals had received two doses of the MMR vaccine [93]. Also, Zhang et al. documented a measles outbreak among immunized individuals [94]. Madi et al. assessed the immune status against measles, rubella, and mumps in young Kuwaitis and concluded that their findings support the need for a third dose of the MMR vaccine, as well as catch up vaccination campaigns. These measures are particularly important for boosting coverage and preventing waning immunity, especially among individuals who received only a single dose during childhood [95]. However, Park et al. reported that there is currently insufficient evidence to conclusively determine whether two doses of a measles-containing vaccine (MCV) ensure lifelong immunity in all individuals [91].

These findings highlight that persistence of measles reflects not only insufficient coverage, but also the continuous regeneration of susceptible individuals within populations over time, requiring continuing monitoring of individuals immunity and targeted immunization strategies.

Upward shifting of the susceptible age for measles infection

Before the introduction of vaccination programs, most individuals contracted measles during childhood. Measles occurrence is strongly affected by the growth of susceptible people within continuous birth cohorts. The European region surveillance 2024 shows that the individuals more than 14 years old represented 26% of reported cases, indicating persistent shortfalls over longer periods [11]. Other study conducted recently (2025) in Oman showed an increase in susceptible individuals as a result of an immunity gap over a period of time [96]. The classic epidemiologic studies have stated that vaccination tend to shift the epidemic curve, increasing the average age of infection compared to pre-vaccination era [97]. Accordingly, in high vaccination coverage regions the cases of measles are increasingly reported in adults and adolescents rather than in young children. One of the study carried out in USA (2001–2015) showed that 45% of measles cases were detected in adults (≥ 18 years) [98]. A recent study (2025) conducted in the same place showed that 29% of measles cases were among ≥ 20 years [99]. Moreover, the serological studies displayed declined seropositivity in age group of 15–29 compared to younger age groups, suggesting insufficient vaccination [100].

Subsequently in the vaccine era, due to incomplete coverage and occasional vaccine failure, the age distribution of measles cases has shifted upward, resulting in a higher proportion of infections occurring among adolescents and young adults [101]. The majority of cases (53%) during the 2018 measles outbreak in the European region, were reported among individuals aged 15 years and older, while 18% occurred in children aged 6–14 years, and 28% in children less than 5 years [90]. A similar upward shift in age distribution was observed during the 2022 outbreak in Europe, with measles infections reported among individuals over 20 years of age [67]. Chen and Tang conducted study entitled “Measles re-emerges and recommendation of vaccination” in which they stated that the average age of measles infection tends to shift toward adolescents and young adults as vaccination coverage increases. They noted that vaccine derived protective antibodies may gradually decline over time, leading to an increased risk of measles infection among young adults even in the absence of international travel [102].

Young infants who are not eligible for measles vaccination

The ideal age for the first dose of measles containing vaccine is 9 months in measles endemic region, whereas in the area where measles is not common the ideal age is12–15 months, while the second dose is administered at 12–15 months in endemic areas and at ages of 4–6 years in non-endemic areas [103]. Infants less than 6 months are not eligible for vaccination, the infants more than 6 months and in high risk may get early vaccine dose which do not replace the first dose [104]. The infant who do not reach the eligible age for vaccination remain susceptible to measles and they may act as source of spreading the measles infection. Zhao et al., carried out study in China to explain the reason of increasing vulnerability to measles infection in young infants and they concluded that the enhancement of immunity in young infants who are not eligible for measles vaccination could be important to interrupt the transmission. Same authors recommended offering booster vaccine dose to women before gestation so that the future newborns can receive more maternal measles IgG and may have elongated period of protection against measles [105]. Capasso et al. published a study titled “An old disease comes back”, in which they reported two cases of neonatal measles; one due to postnatal infection and the other congenital. The authors emphasized that these cases serve as a reminder to remain vigilant against measles and highlight the risks it poses to susceptible pregnant women and their newborns [106]. However, measles-specific antibodies passed from infected mothers are typically cleared from an infant’s bloodstream between 3 and 9 months of age [55].

Co-existence with other infectious diseases

Despite high immunization coverage, vaccine failure in Human immunodeficiency (HIV) infected children may permit measles transmission as they remain susceptible to measles infection [107]. Mutsaerts et al. reported that early initiation of antiretroviral therapy ART can considerably enhance measles vaccine immunogenicity and antibody persistence (108). Several studies have shown that children receiving effective ART with viral suppression can achieve serological responses comparable to HIV uninfected peers, whereas children with uncontrolled infection showed lower seroconversion rates [106, 108]. In high HIV-burden settings, HIV-infected children exhibit prolonged viral shedding, higher rates of vaccine failure, and increased risk of severe disease [107, 109], however the immune responses in HIV-infected children remain heterogeneous and dependent on immune reconstitution and viral suppression [110].

This immunodeficiency linked with more severe illness, reduced vaccine effectiveness, and prolonged measles virus shedding, essentially generating unidentified measles reservoirs that undermine herd immunity and necessitate more frequent vaccination efforts [107, 109].

Caregiver factors

Caregiver-related factors, particularly vaccine refusal and hesitancy exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and fears of rare but serious adverse events, now pose a major challenge to measles eradication efforts.

Vaccine refusal

Vaccine refusal and hesitancy were identified as the main drivers behind the rise in measles incidence [92]. Vaccine refusal is defined as any uncertainty or delay in acceptance of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services. Increasing vaccine refusal in the general population is one of the main problems facing eradication of measles. Ferrari et al., mentioned that reticence for COVID-19 vaccine has been expected to affect the acceptance of other vaccines. The National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups confirmed that the emergent of COVID-19 has worsened the vaccination coverage in various countries [67].

Adverse events can occasionally occur during vaccination campaigns, including serious incidents. For example, during a 2017 measles vaccination campaign in South Sudan, fifteen children died shortly after receiving the vaccine, leading to the immediate suspension of the campaign [57]. WHO safety summaries displayed that serious reactions to measles containing vaccines are rare but still drive refusal in the absence of trusted communication [111]. In Nigeria 6,214 suspected adverse event following immunization (AEFI) were reported during measles vaccination campaign 2017/2018; fever (38%), injection pain (30%), while 99.7% were non-serious, with most resolving without complication [112]. Malande et al. reported that experience to AEFI contributed to missed subsequent immunization visits and that 30% of vaccine-hesitant caregivers attributed hesitancy to AEFIs [113].

Socio-economic factors

Key socio-economic determinants such as limited parental awareness driven by low education, alongside population displacement in fragile settings, create critical barriers to achieving measles elimination.

Low parental education

Low parental education has negative affect on vaccination program. Khowaja et al., determined the parental awareness and participation in measles campaign in Pakistan and reported that only (28%) of parents had the knowledge about measles supplementary immunization activity and 38% of them refused measles vaccine. They concluded that low parental education were significant determinants for low coverage [9].

Conflict and internal displacement

Fragile and conflict affected countries are particularly vulnerable to measles outbreaks due to the presence of densely populated informal settlement camps, frequent population movement, and inadequate healthcare services, including limited access to vaccination. Conflict and internal displacement, which are prevalent in many parts of Africa and Asia, further exacerbate these challenges, creating conditions that facilitate the rapid spread of measles [57].

Geographical factors

Geographical factors, particularly the importation of measles infection through international travel and population mobility, pose persistent threats to elimination even in countries with high domestic vaccination coverage.

Importations of measles infection

Measles elimination is a fragile and unstable state that requires continuous vigilance, particularly against importations from regions with low routine immunization coverage [114]. The Republic of Korea (ROK) was certified measles free by the WHO in 2006; however, most current cases are linked to international travel from measles endemic areas [37]. Also, in the United States despite high vaccination coverage imported cases have triggered outbreaks, though these have not resulted in sustained transmission [115].

However, measles import plays important role in triggering outbreaks in non-endemic settings or in individual with suboptimal immunity. Worldwide travel may facilitate transmission of distinct genotypes like D8 into regions where other genotypes were endemic leading to transmission in susceptible individuals, particularly in unvaccinated children or adult indicating gap in vaccination. These imported cases enhance local chains of transmission which are amplified by virus’s high reproductive number R0 (12–18). In setting like migrant reception center, measles infection may coexist with other infection including chicken pox which complicated outbreak control, emphasizing that insufficient vaccination coverage may enables both endemic and imported strains to cause resurgence, with outbreaks linked to social determinants such as migration, population mobility and international travel [59].

Laboratory diagnosis

Laboratory diagnosis of measles is essential for eradication efforts, as it confirms the cases, differentiate vaccine-derived viruses from wild-type strains, thereby supporting rapid outbreak control. Molecular genotyping empowers the tracking of transmission chains, identification of infection sources, and monitoring the genetic evolution, confirming elimination. Sub-optimal policy is directed towards real circulating threats and preventing recurrence by noticing importations early. In near-elimination settings, genotyping surveillance is indispensable to link or unlink cases, determine whether outbreaks from imported strains or ongoing endemic trains, guiding targeted intervention and pinpoint transmission pathways [116].

The most commonly used laboratory method for diagnosing measles is the detection of measles virus-specific IgM antibodies using ELISA. However, IgM levels are typically very low or undetectable during the first four days after the rash appears, which can lead to false-negative results if testing is done too early. After four days of rash onset, nearly all individuals infected with measles have detectable levels of virus-specific IgM. Additionally, about 75% of infected individuals will already show detectable IgM levels within the first three days of rash development [55].

Clinically the diagnosis of measles mainly considered with the onset of a rash, IgM testing is usually performed to diagnose acute measles infection. A positive IgG and negative IgM result is mostly interpreted as evidence of old infection or immunity from vaccination. However, the IgG is usually very high in secondary vaccine failure patient. In secondary measles infections, IgM detection can be delayed or negative. Though IgG positive/IgM negative measles cases were earlier reported and they appear to be increasing, many physicians do not aware about the significance of this serologic pattern. The positive IgG for measles infection provides evidence of vaccination or previous infection and can be uses to differentiate primary infection from secondary vaccine failure. However, false-negative and false-positive measles IgM results pose significant challenges, especially in settings with low disease incidence [70].

The gold standard for diagnosing measles infection is the highly sensitive plaque reduction neutralization (PRN) assay. Research has shown that some individuals who test sero-negative for measles using ELISA may still test positive for sero-conversion when assessed with the PRN assay [117]. Therefore, individuals who receive negative ELISA results should be further evaluated using the PRN assay [55].

Up to date, measles RT-PCR is largely confined to public health laboratories. However, the RT-PCR tests may also be false negative in case of delayed collection as the viral shedding drops significantly after 4 days from rash onset [32]. RT-PCR can detect viral RNA in clinical specimens even before measles specific antibodies become detectable [55, 118].

Alter or changed clinical presentations, absence of obvious epidemiologic risk factors, positive measles IgG and false negative IgM results may misguide the clinicians, making delays in proper diagnosis and growing the risk of transmission [118], so throat swabs or Nasopharyngeal swab and blood samples for measles RT-PCR should be done to avoid the pitfalls of serology in diagnosing measles particularly in vaccinated persons. The physician should be aware that using serology tests alone may lead to miss diagnosis. Therefore, measles IgG and IgM testing along with PCR of should be performed [32]. However, the interpretation of serological results should be approached on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the patient’s vaccination status, clinical presentation, and travel history [119]. Measles laboratory surveillance is highly cost-effective as early detection avoid costly large scale outbreaks, protecting health care systems from huge costs per case, reduces expensive responses and enabling rapid vaccination responses and through sensible stools like rapid diagnostic tests minimizes preventable and economic losses, especially in limited resource.

Conclusion and recommendation

Measles eradication remains unachieved, with cases resurging across all WHO regions despite the availability of an effective vaccine and global initiatives such as the WHO’s 2012 Global Vaccine Action Plan and the 2030 measles elimination targets. Measles eradication hindered by heterogeneous immunity gaps, weak surveillance, and context specific epidemiological drivers, needs tiered, context adapted interventions rather than unified approaches. In near elimination settings, priorities include preventing re-establishment of transmission through targeted catch-up vaccination specifically for adult and adolescents with immunity gaps, timely detection of imported measles cases and strengthen molecular surveillance. In endemic or low coverage areas, the primary focus should be on attaining ≥ 95% two-dose coverage, implementing regular supplementary immunization activities (SIAs), robust cold chain systems, and addressing access barriers in remote and in conflict-affected areas. Across all settings, strengthen molecular laboratory-supported surveillance are essential for accurate case detection, early outbreak identification and differentiation between endemic from imported transmission. Vaccine hesitancy should be addressed through safety evidence and effective community engagement.
