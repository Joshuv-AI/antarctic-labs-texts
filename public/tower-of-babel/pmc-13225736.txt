Multidisciplinary interventions and outcomes of the Geriatric Faller Protocol in an emergency diagnostic treatment unit: an internal audit
Authors: Hwee Min Tan, Chen Yang, Xin Ying Tay, Angela Frances Hui Wen Yap, Kai Xin Low, Zi Yuan Ong, Chia Wei Jennifer Ting, Shu Fang Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This is a descriptive review of the implementation of the Geriatric Faller Protocol (GFP) in the Department of Emergency Medicine (DEM) Emergency Diagnostic Treatment Unit, Singapore General Hospital, along with a post-implementation internal audit over a 6-month period from 1 July 2024 to 31 December 2024.Methods:The clinical records of patients admitted to GFP during the 6-month period were retrospectively reviewed. Data of patients were collated under the categories: (a) sociodemographics and clinical profile, (b) medication profile, (c) injury profile, (d) non-pharmaceutical interventions, (e) pharmaceutical interventions and (f) intervention outcome measures.Results:Of the 115 patients admitted to GFP, 14 were excluded and 101 were included in the analysis (median age 81 years; 70.3% female). Most (79.2%) patients had a Clinical Frailty Scale of between 4 and 7. Acute hospital admission was avoided in 67.3% of cases; five reattended DEM within 30 days, of which only two were fall related. Falls were the primary reason for presentation (94.1%), with over half resulting in head or facial injuries. Polypharmacy was prevalent in 75% of patients (median number of medications: 9; interquartile range: 5–12), and 57.4% self-managed their medications.Conclusion:Front-loading of assessments and interventions by a multidisciplinary team for patients under GFP suggested possible hospital admission avoidance and a reduction in short-term adverse outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Emergency care utilisation and hospital admissions of older adults has risen over the years.[1] This may be due to increasing age-related comorbidities, polypharmacy, frailty and changes in sociodemographic factors.[23] In Singapore, those aged ≥65 years are projected to make up one-third of the population by 2035.[4] Hospital admissions for older adults are often associated with longer inpatient stay, higher costs, functional decline, emergency department (ED) reattendances and increased mortality.[256] Presentations to EDs often involve complex medical, functional, frailty-related and psychosocial issues,[7] offering opportunities for early, integrated intervention to reduce morbidity and mortality.

SUMMARY BOX

What is known?

Singapore’s ageing population increases demand for hospital care. Various geriatric emergency care models have shown improved outcomes for the geriatric population.

What is new?

An internal audit of a multidisciplinary intervention implemented under the Geriatric Faller Protocol (GFP) in the emergency department’s (ED) short-stay unit had suggested reduced hospital length of stay, ED reattendance for falls and medication-related issues.

What is the impact?

Front-loading of assessments and interventions by a multidisciplinary team for patients under GFP suggested possible hospital admission avoidance and reducing short-term adverse outcomes.

Falls are a major driver of ED visits among older adults. Around 30%–40% of those aged ≥65 years fall annually, with this rate rising to 50% in those aged ≥80 years.[2] Post-fall ED reattendance rates range from 2% within 3 days to 25% within a year.[8] A scoping review found that 36%–44% of older adults presenting with a fall experience adverse events within a year, including recurrent falls, repeat ED visits and death.[9] Falls are associated with significant morbidity, such as injuries, functional decline, recurrent falls, hospital admissions, caregiver burden and even mortality,[10] and are an independent risk factor for nursing home admission.[11] Therefore, ED visits for falls present an opportunity for early intervention and prevention of future falls, morbidity and mortality.

Despite the growing need for geriatric care, traditional ED practice often focuses on rapid diagnostics and treatments, which may not fully address the complex care needs of older patients.[12] Guidelines[13] have been developed to standardise staffing, training, equipment, transitions and quality measures in geriatric ED care. However, current practice frequently relies on outpatient follow-up, delaying timely risk identification and intervention.

There are four main geriatric ED care models: Geriatric ED Units, Geriatrics Practitioner Model, Geriatrics Champion and Geriatric-Focused Observation Unit.[12] These models use interdisciplinary patient-centred care, transition planning, outpatient referrals and geriatric assessments to identify older adults who may benefit from targeted interventions. No single model has demonstrated clear superiority.[14] A fall-prevention initiative in the ED for older patients presenting after a fall has been shown to reduce ED reattendance when ED clinicians, physiotherapists (PTs) and pharmacists collaborate.[15] Locally, an ED nurse-led fall screening and osteoporosis education programme showed a 4.3% reattendance rate at 6 months for older adults with fall-related fractures.[16]

Despite existing efforts, local ED practice still lacks a structured, multidisciplinary approach to assessing and managing older fallers in the acute setting, continuing to rely on outpatient follow-up. This delay in risk identification is increasingly concerning in Singapore’s ageing population. To address this, the Geriatric Faller Protocol (GFP) was developed to frontload multidisciplinary, patient-centred care by integrating geriatric medicine, physiotherapy, pharmacy and nursing at the point of care in ED, supported by structured screening and coordinated transition to community services.

METHODS

Study setting and design

Singapore General Hospital (SGH) is a 2000-bed acute tertiary hospital. Its Department of Emergency Medicine (DEM) operates a 24-h facility, handling over 135,000 visits annually, of which approximately 40% involve patients aged ≥ 65 years. The DEM also runs a 10-bedded Emergency Diagnostic and Treatment Unit (EDTU), a short-stay unit for patients requiring extended treatment and observation, typically between 8 and 23 h, without the need for full hospital admission.

Prior to the implementation of GFP in EDTU, geriatric patients presenting after a fall who were identified by ED physicians as having unaddressed fall risk factors were directly admitted to the Department of Internal Medicine. Those without such risk factors were discharged with follow-up appointments with the appropriate specialists. The introduction of GFP in EDTU provided an opportunity for intervention in a selected group of geriatric fallers (GFs) by the multidisciplinary Geriatric Care Champions (GCC) team, with the aim of providing holistic care while avoiding inpatient admission.

This study is a descriptive review of the implementation of GFP, along with a post-implementation internal audit over a 6-month period from 1 July 2024 to 31 December 2024. This internal audit was conducted as part of a service improvement initiative and was approved by the hospital’s clinical governance board; it was therefore exempt from review by the Centralised Institutional Review Board. Access to identifiable clinical records was limited to designated clinical team members involved in the internal audit process. Before analysis, all data were de-identified and extracted into a secure database. No patient-identifiable information was used in the analysis or included in any reporting, and all procedures adhered to the hospital’s data governance and confidentiality policies.

Geriatric Faller Protocol in EDTU

The EDTU currently manages musculoskeletal pain under the Minor Injury Protocol (MIP), which includes parenteral analgesia and observation. However, MIP lacks geriatric-specific interventions, prompting the development of GFP to address the complex needs of older patients.

The key innovation of GFP is a multidisciplinary GCC team comprising geriatricians, emergency physicians, geriatric-trained emergency nurses, pharmacists, PTs, occupational therapists (OTs) and medical social workers (MSWs). This team delivers frailty-centred care through comprehensive assessment of geriatric syndromes, functional status, multimorbidity, psychosocial needs and polypharmacy. Working collaboratively, risk stratification, targeted geriatric screening and coordinated disposition planning support admission avoidance and facilitate safe discharge or transfer to lower-acuity settings such as community hospitals.

In preparation for implementing geriatric-centric care, a core group of DEM nurses underwent specialised training. This included didactic sessions led by content experts and a 1-week clinical attachment featuring on-the-job training with geriatric emergency medicine nurses at Tan Tock Seng Hospital, qualifying them as geriatric-trained emergency nurses.

A pilot programme launched in April 2024 enhanced the existing EDTU MIP with geriatric-focused assessments supported by geriatricians and allied health professionals (AHPs). Under this model, ED physicians identify eligible patients (aged ≥65 years with Clinical Frailty Scale [CFS] of 4–7) admitted under EDTU MIP and refer them to the GCC-Nurse. The GCC-Nurse then performs structured fall risk assessment using a modified Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (mCGA) and collaborates with geriatricians and AHPs to develop individualised care plans. Following a successful 3-month trial, the EDTU GFP was formally launched on 1 July 2024, after approval from SGH’s medical board.

The EDTU GFP admits patients aged ≥65 years with a CFS of 4–7 who present with recurrent falls (≥2 in 12 months), near-falls or are at risk of fall. On admission, patients undergo a standardised assessment with a GCC-Nurse, followed by a multidisciplinary review with a geriatrician, GCC-Pharmacist and PT to identify and address modifiable fall risks. Support from OT and MSW is provided as needed for holistic fall prevention and care. Details on objectives, inclusion/exclusion criteria and interventions are provided in the Supplemental Digital Appendix 1.

Geriatric Care Champions-Nurse domain

An mCGA was developed as the foundation of GFP in EDTU [Supplemental Digital Appendix 1]. As geriatric-focused assessment is new to the department, a simplified and tailored version was essential for the initial launch. Designed primarily for DEM GCC nurses, the mCGA guides nurses in identifying key issues and suggest the next appropriate interventions based on assessment findings. It consists of ten main sections: (1) patient’s current follow-up, (2) CFS, (3) social assessment, (4) instrumental activities of daily living (iADL) and basic activities of daily living (bADL), (5) fall assessment, (6) cognitive screening, (7) hearing and visual assessment, (8) continence assessment, (9) bone health screening and (10) care plan.

The mCGA contains a mixture of ‘yes’ or ‘no’ questions, checkbox questions and open-ended questions. Key components of the validated tools include CFS, Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT), Osteoporosis Self-Assessment Tool for Asians (OSTA) for female menopausal patients and Creatinine Clearance. Revisions to the mCGA were conducted using a quality improvement approach.

Geriatric Care Champions-Geriatrician domain

A dedicated geriatrician provides weekday coverage for the GFP service. Upon notification of a GFP admission by the GCC-Nurse, the geriatrician conducts a comprehensive review of the patient’s electronic medical record (EMR), including medical history, presenting complaints, laboratory and electrocardiography results, radiological imaging and clinical progress in the DEM. This is followed by a case discussion with the GCC team at the EDTU, where the GCC-Nurse presents the mCGA. The geriatrician then performs a clinical assessment, including patient and caregiver interviews and a physical examination.

Based on the findings, the geriatrician provides recommendations on discharge suitability, need for inpatient admission for further investigations, outpatient follow-up for identified fall risk factors, bone health interventions and referral to community services. Physiotherapy and occupational therapy—either outpatient or home based—may also be advised. All recommendations are documented in the EMR and communicated to the GCC and EDTU teams.

Geriatric Care Champions-Pharmacist domain

Under the GFP, GCC-Pharmacists conduct comprehensive medication reviews to identify key issues in older adults, including polypharmacy, multiple prescribers and incomplete medication histories.

System medication reconciliation is first performed to generate a preliminary medication list, followed by patient or caregiver interviews to resolve discrepancies. This process allows for the evaluation of actual and potential drug-related problems (DRPs), identifies inappropriate medication use and reduces the risk of drug interactions. Subsequently, multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions with the geriatrician and GCC-Nurse are held to optimise pharmacotherapy. Patients identified to benefit from future pharmacist intervention (e.g. with non-adherence or multiple DRPs that cannot be resolved at the ED) are referred to the outpatient pharmacist-run medication management service to ensure care continuity.

Data collection and analysis

Clinical records of patients admitted to GFP during the study period were retrieved and reviewed. Data were manually coded and collated under the following six categories: (a) sociodemographic and clinical profile of GFP patients; (b) medication profile of GFP patients; (c) injury profile of GFP patients; (d) non-pharmaceutical interventions; (e) pharmaceutical interventions; and (f) intervention outcome measures of EDTU GFP. Descriptive statistics were generated using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA), and data were cross-checked by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy.

RESULTS

Clinical records of 115 patients admitted under GFP between 1 July 2024 and 31 December 2024 were reviewed. Fourteen patients were excluded, as they were not assessed by the GCC team [Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Appendix 2]. The reasons for non-review included admission over the weekend with no GCC coverage, conversion to another EDTU protocol and conversion to inpatient admission by the EDTU team before review by the GCC team. Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Appendix 2 summarises the internal audit results of the EDTU GFP.

Geriatric fallers’ profile

The median age of patients admitted to GFP was 81 years, with most aged 75–84 years (46.5%). More females (70.3%) than males were admitted, and Chinese ethnicity predominated (89.1%). Socially, only a minority (12.9%) lived alone. Most were functionally independent before presentation. Specifically, based on the Barthel Index for basic activities of daily living (ADLs), 57.4% were fully independent, while only 8.9% had severe dependence. For instrumental ADLs, the median independence level was four out of seven activities. These findings aligned with CFS, where most GFP patients (75.2%) fell within the 4–6 pre-frail to frail range.

Collateral history was obtained from patients and families to assess the cognitive function. Among those assessed, 48.5% scored ≥8 on AMT. However, AMT was not performed in 15.8% of patients, as it was administered only to patients with cognitive concerns to optimise ED resources. The median body mass index (BMI) was 22.8 kg/m², with 48.5% falling below the optimal range of 23 kg/m². Consistent with the low BMI findings, 54.5% of female patients were classified as high risk for osteoporosis based on the OSTA score.

In terms of medication profile, all patients were seen by pharmacists for medication review and reconciliation. Polypharmacy, which is traditionally described as being on ≥5 chronic medications,[17] was observed in 75% of patients, with the median number of medications being 9 (interquartile range 5–12). Over half (57.4%) of the patients self-managed their medications. One hundred and fifty-six DRPs were identified in 80% of patients, with avoidance of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), non-adherence and untreated indication being the most common (25.6% vs. 23.7% vs. 18.6%, respectively). Non-pharmaceutical interventions

Of the 101 patients reviewed by the GCC team, 18.8% were already under the care of geriatric medicine. An additional 37.6% were identified to benefit from continued geriatric medicine follow-up. It was noted that 39.6% had existing osteoporosis follow-up. Availability of recent bone mineral density (BMD) and vitamin D test results was considered before these investigations were ordered. Vitamin D and BMD tests were ordered for 63.4% and 40.6% of patients, respectively, and osteoporosis medications were prescribed for outpatients based on test results. Post-discharge, 14.9% required telecare follow-up, 15.8% required day rehabilitation centre referral and 9.9% were referred to the community nurse for follow-up. Pharmaceutical interventions

Medications with anticholinergic properties, including muscle relaxants (40%), first-generation antihistamines (15%) and codeine-containing cough suppressants (2.5%), were the most common drug classes involved in causing potential or actual ADRs. The GCC-Pharmacists most commonly recommended substituting (38.6%) or discontinuing (27.3%) the offending agents. One-third of affected patients received counselling on associated risks, and memos were provided to primary care physicians to prevent future inappropriate prescribing.

Patients identified to be non-adherent to their prescribed medications were mostly due to incorrect administration (64.9%) or omission (32.4%). This was contributed by unawareness (37.8%), difficulty in administration (10.8%) and ADRs experienced (5.4%). Reasons for non-adherence were not collected in 40.5% of patients.

With regards to untreated indications, pharmacists recommended screening and appropriate treatment for patients with vitamin deficiencies (37.9%), osteoporosis (24.1%) and constipation (17.2%). Deprescribing of vitamin B supplements occurred in 80% of cases identified to have lack of indication of the aforementioned medications. Geriatric Faller Protocol intervention outcomes

Of the 101 GFP patients, 50 (49.5%) were successfully discharged, while 18 (17.8%) required further rehabilitation in a community hospital. The remaining 33 (32.7%) were admitted as inpatients. Among those who required inpatient admission, 75.8% were admitted to the Department of Internal Medicine and 12.1% to orthopaedics. The primary reasons for inpatient conversion included newly diagnosed conditions identified during GCC team assessment (54.5%) and abnormal laboratory results detected post-admission to GFP (12.1%).

DISCUSSION

The study demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of implementing GFP in ED, with evidence suggesting potential benefits in patient outcomes. Notably, 67.3% of patients were managed without acute hospital admission. Only two of 50 discharged patients reattended the ED for fall-related complaints within 30 days, both with known Parkinson’s disease. Although more than half of the enrolled patients had head or facial injuries, nearly two-thirds were safely managed without admission. This highlights GFP’s potential role in avoiding hospitalisation for higher-risk cases, even in groups often thought to require hospitalisation. The GFP also revealed a high prevalence of underlying vulnerabilities, including sarcopenia, malnutrition, vitamin D insufficiency and DRPs, which are frequently under-recognised during routine ED care. Recognition of these factors during the ED encounter provides an opportunity for timely, targeted interventions to mitigate future fall risk.

The findings align with those of the local Emergency Department Interventions for Frailty study, which demonstrated that early geriatric assessment in ED can reduce admissions without increasing adverse outcomes.[18] It also underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration in fall prevention programmes, consistent with previous research.[19] Koh et al.[20] recommended including a ‘fall nurse specialist’ as a change champion to promote adherence to fall prevention guidelines. Pharmacists also play a pertinent role in fall prevention through the provision of medication reviews and therapy optimisation.[21] Interventions by pharmacists have been shown to improve health outcomes in older patients.[22]

Consistent with previous studies, older patients remain highly susceptible to polypharmacy and its complications, including increased fall risk and functional decline.[2324] Suboptimal medication regimens, along with inappropriate medication use, polypharmacy or under-prescribing also increase the risk of harm.[25] In this study, anticholinergic agents were the most frequent contributors to DRPs [Table 5], posing concerns due to the heightened anticholinergic burden in older adults.[26] These medications can cause peripheral effects such as dry eyes, dry mouth, constipation and urinary retention, as well as central effects such as drowsiness and impaired concentration, all of which increase fall risk.

Non-adherence was also observed in one-third of patients, lower than the 50% cited by the World Health Organization, though rates vary by condition and disease control.[27] The GCC team adopted strategies such as deprescribing, substitution and patient education to minimise the use of potentially inappropriate medications. Additionally, patient education and interventions were conducted to improve medication adherence to optimise health outcomes.

Sri-On et al.[28] reported a 21% rate of adverse events—including falls, ED revisits, hospitalisations and deaths—among older patients within 30 days of ED discharge. In comparison, the GFP group demonstrated a lower 30-day ED reattendance rate of 5.0%, suggesting that the protocol may be beneficial in reducing short-term adverse outcomes.

Most GFP patients had suboptimal BMI, consistent with evidence linking low BMI and sarcopenia to fall risk.[29] The high prevalence of sarcopenia, malnutrition and vitamin D deficiency underscores the need for systematic screening and supplementation in this population.

Following the observed trends in injury profiles, the inclusion criteria were expanded in May 2025 to encompass minor traumatic brain injuries, including minor intracranial haemorrhages suitable for 23-h care. Planned frailty-focused interventions include resistance training, nutritional support with adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D intake, and overall dietary optimisation. These measures aim to improve muscle strength and bone health and reduce fall risk, reinforcing a multidisciplinary strategy. Referrals to community services, such as community nurses and day rehabilitation centres, reflect the broad spectrum of support required to manage GFs effectively. These findings emphasise the need for a coordinated, multidisciplinary model integrating both acute and community care.

This internal audit has several limitations that may affect generalisability. First, as it was conducted in a single tertiary emergency department with a modest sample size, the findings may not be representative of broader clinical settings. Second, although the GFP inclusion criteria (CFS 4–7) were intended to target pre-frail to frail patients most likely to benefit from intervention, 20.8% of enrolled patients had a CFS ≤ 3. All had sustained a fall (except one identified as high fall risk) and were admitted at clinicians’ discretion due to pain-limited mobility. While these cases fell outside protocol criteria, they were reviewed by the multidisciplinary team to optimise function and prevent future falls; nonetheless, such exceptions may have introduced selection bias. Third, 14 patients were excluded from analysis due to inappropriate GFP admission, and AMT scores were unavailable for 16 of the 101 analysed patients owing to emergency department resource constraints, further limiting generalisability. Lastly, compliance with osteoporosis follow-up and treatment could not be fully assessed because post-discharge access to the National Electronic Health Record was restricted. Consequently, proxy measures such as BMD or vitamin D test orders may have overestimated actual treatment uptake. In addition, reasons for medication non-adherence were unidentified in 40.5% of patients. Understanding these barriers through longer interviews and rapport building may help improve adherence and support behavioural change.[30]

Long-term outcomes such as functional recovery, quality of life and caregiver burden, which may provide valuable insights into the wider impact of GFP, were not assessed in this audit. Further studies that evaluate these outcomes, including sustained reductions in falls, functional improvement and cost-effectiveness, are warranted.

In conclusion, front-loading multidisciplinary assessments under GFP enabled timely care planning and addressed medical, functional and social needs, reducing treatment delays and supporting appropriate decisions such as outpatient referrals and rehabilitation. This approach led to possible admission avoidance, especially preventable readmissions, and improved patient outcomes, including better functional recovery and reducing short-term adverse outcomes.
