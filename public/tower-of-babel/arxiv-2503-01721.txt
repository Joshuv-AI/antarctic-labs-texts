Diameter and girth of Representation Graphs of Quadratic Forms
Nico Lorenz, Marc Christian Zimmermann

Abstract
. Let q q be a non-degenerate quadratic form defined on an F F vector space V V and a ∈ F a\in F . We consider the Cayley graph on V V with generating set { x ∈ V ∣ q ⁡ ( x ) = a } \{x\in V\mid q(x)=a\} and study its diameter and girth. In particular, if F F is a finite field, we calculate these invariants and the number of cycles of minimal length in these graphs.

1.
Introduction

Quadratic forms are classical objects that appear in many different areas of mathematics.
Originating in number theory, the algebraic theory of quadratic forms initiated by the work of E. Witt in
[
Wit37
]
, has connections to Galois theory, central-simple algebras, and cohomology, among others.
Furthermore, quadratic forms induce combinatorial objects which are regularly studied in the literature and which will be investigated in this article.
Given a quadratic form
q
on a vector space
V
over a field
F
and a fixed element
a\in F
, one can consider the
representation graph
of
q
with respect to
a
, i.e. the graph with vertex set
V
in which two different vertices
x,y\in V
are connected if and only if
q(x-y)=a
.
In other words, we consider the Cayley graphs on
V
with generating set
V_{q,a}=\{x\in V\setminus\{0\}\mid q(x)=a\}
.
A special emphasis in the literature lies on the
sum of squares form
, i.e. the form defined on
F^{n}
which maps a vector
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in F^{n}
to
x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}
and
a=1
, since this situation can be interpreted as an analog of the Euclidean space of dimension
n
.
The articles mentioned in the following short survey all focus on the sum of squares form over different fields if not stated otherwise.

For the field of rational numbers
F=\mathbb{Q}
these graphs are studied in
[
Chi88
]
with respect to their connectedness and clique number.
From a combinatorial point of view, representation graphs are of particular interest in the case of a finite field (or even ring).
The spectral properties (also implying connectedness results) of representation graphs over finite fields were obtained in
[
Med+96
]
.
Formulated in the language of association schemes, generalizations of these results, also for arbitrary non-degenerate quadratic forms, can be found in
[
BST09
]
.

The connectedness in the case of finite fields was also derived using combinatorial techniques in a recent paper by M. Krebs in
[
Kre22
]
.
This article also deals with clique numbers and includes some results on the chromatic number.
In
[
LZ23
]
, the authors reprove the results of
[
Kre22
]
about the clique number using quadratic form theory.
Using this algebraic approach, the results could be generalized to the case of representation graphs of arbitrary non-degenerate quadratic forms and further, the number of maximal cliques instead of just their size could be computed.

Similarly the main goal of the present article is to generalize and quantify the results on connectedness.
While we focus on finite fields, some of our results are also valid for infinite fields.
We expand the results on the connectedness by calculating the
diameter
, i.e. the maximal length of minimal paths between two vertices is calculated in almost all cases over finite fields, see
Theorem

3.1
.
Similar techniques as for the diameter are used to compute the
girth
, i.e. the length of a shortest cycle, see
Theorem

4.2
.
Finally, the number of shortest cycles is calculate in
Proposition

4.3
and
Theorem

4.7
.

As noted above, our techniques differ from those in the literature in that we rely heavily on results from the algebraic theory of quadratic forms.
This theory provides results that can be used in combinatorial arguments.
Furthermore, the algebraic arguments can be used to reduce the general case of a given problem to a special case in which combinatorial arguments work more easily.
As one of the main tools, we use decompositions of quadratic forms.
Results on low-dimensional forms, which are naturally easier to establish, can then easily be used to cover the arbitrary case.
The final advantage of our approach is that some of our results also hold over infinite fields.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In
Section

2
, we recall some basic concepts about graphs, quadratic forms and their representation graphs.
In
Section

3
, we deal with connectedness and diameter of representation graphs over finite fields and even for arbitrary fields under some further conditions on the quadratic form.
Finally,
Section

4
is devoted to the length and the number of cycles of minimum length.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Quadratic Forms

We briefly recall some basic facts about and notation on quadratic forms that will be used frequently in this article.
We use standard terminology when working with quadratic forms, usually consistent with that used in
[
EKM08
]
and most of the results on quadratic forms we use can be found in
[

Readers familiar with the theory of quadratic forms may want to skip this section.

Let

be a field and

be a finite dimensional vector space over

A

(QF1)

and

(QF2)

b_{q}:V\times V\to F,(x,y)\mapsto q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y)

In particular we have

Throughout the text, when a quadratic form

is present, we refer to

as the underlying vector space of

and as usual in quadratic form theory, we write

for

For example, for

is given by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}

For

, the quadratic form

is denoted by
[a,b]
.
Orthogonal sums are denoted by
\perp
and isometries are denoted by
\cong
.

Our focus is on
non-degenerate
quadratic forms, i.e., forms with

\mathrm{rad}(b_{q})=\{x\in V\mid\text{ for all $y\in V$: }b_{q}(x,y)=0\}=\{0\}.

Note that this convention differs from our main reference
[

]
, in which non-degenerate quadratic forms are defined in a slightly more general way (including forms that are often called

The more general definition used there comes from a geometric approach to quadratic form theory, then a quadratic form is non-degenerate if and only if the underlying quadric is smooth (see
[

, Proposition 22.1]

However, since we are focussing on algebraic aspects, we use the more classical algebraic definition of non-degenerate

Extending the usual definition of represented elements

Recall that the

Since our combinatorial interest focuses on finite structures, we have a particular interest in quadratic forms over finite fields, which are well understood.

2.2.
Graph Theory

We now recall some standard notation for graphs and provide some basic properties of representation graphs of quadratic forms.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a (not necessarily finite, but simple) graph, where
V
is the
vertex set
and
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
the
edge set
of
G
.
We say that two vertices
u,v\in V
are
adjacent
in
G
if
\{u,v\}\in E
.
A
path of length
n
between two vertices
u,v\in V
is a sequence
(v_{0}=u,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}=v)
of vertices such that
v_{i}\neq v_{j}
for
i\neq j
and
\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}\in E
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Similarly, a
cycle
of length
n\geq 3
is a sequence
(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1},v_{0})
of vertices such that
v_{i}\neq v_{j}
for
i\neq j
and
\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}\in E
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

We define the
distance

\mathsf{d}(u,v)
of two vertices
u,v\in V
as the length of a shortest
u
-
v
-path in
G
and
\infty
if no such path exists, the
diameter
of
G
by

\mathsf{diam}(G)=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\text{there are }u,v\in V\text{ such that }d(u,v)=n\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\},

and the
girth
of
G
by

\mathsf{g}(G)=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\text{there is a cycle of length $n$ in $G$}\}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}\cup\{\infty\}.

Two graphs
G=(V,E),G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if and only if there exists a bijective map
\rho:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
such that
\{\rho(u),\rho(v)\}\in E^{\prime}
if and only if
\{u,v\}\in E
, we write
G\cong G^{\prime}
in this case.
It is clear that diameter and girth are invariants of the isomorphism class of a graph.
The group

\operatorname{aut}(G)=\{\sigma:V\to V\ \mid\sigma\text{ is an isomorphism of $G$}\}

is the
automorphism group
of the graph
G
.

We now introduce a family of graphs for a given quadratic form.
These graphs are the protagonists of this article.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form on an
F
vector space
V
and
a\in F
.
The
representation graph

\mathcal{G}_{q,a}=(V,E)
is defined by its set of edges

E:=\left\{\{x,y\}\in\binom{V}{2}\mid q(x-y)=a\right\}.

It is not hard to see that a representation graph
G_{q,a}=(V,E)
is the
Cayley graph

\operatorname{Cay}(V,V_{q,a})
for the group
(V,+)
and the symmetric set

V_{q,a}=\{x\in V\ \mid q(x)=a\},

i.e. we have
\{x,y\}\in E
if and only if
x-y\in V_{q,a}
.

The following lemma is well known.

Lemma 2.6
.

The number of connected components of a Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(V;S)
is equal to the index
[V:\langle S\rangle]
, where
\langle S\rangle
denotes the subgroup generated by the elements of
S
.
In particular
\operatorname{Cay}(V,S)
is connected if and only if
S
generates
V
as a group.

In the context of representation graphs it is natural to ask how isomorphy of graphs relates to isometry of quadratic forms.
We first note that it is clear that if
q\cong q^{\prime}
, then we also have
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}\cong\mathcal{G}_{q^{\prime},a}
for all
a\in F
.

We also consider the following extension of isometries.
The
translation
with
translation vector

v
is the map

\tau_{v}:V\to V,x\mapsto x+v.

We define the group of
affine isometries

\operatorname{iso}(q)
of
q
as the subgroup of the affine group in which the linear part is an isometry of
q
, i.e.

\operatorname{iso}(q)=\{\tau_{v}\mid v\in V\}\rtimes\mathsf{O}(q).

It is easy to see that every element of
\operatorname{iso}(q)
is a graph automorphism of
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}=(V,E)
.

Recall that an element
u\in F^{\ast}
is called a
similarity factor
for the quadratic form
q
if we have
uq\cong q
.
By
[
LZ23
, Proposition 3.1]
, we obtain the basic graph isomorphism
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}\cong\mathcal{G}_{q,\alpha a}
, whenever
\alpha\in F^{\ast}
is a similarity factor of
q
.
We will use this fact implicitly throughout the text, particularly for quadratic forms
q
of dimension
2
.
By
Remark

2.2

(d)

q\cong\mathbb{H}
or
q
is defined over a finite field, then each

is a similarity factor and so, for all

\mathcal{G}_{q,a}\cong\mathcal{G}_{q,1}
in this case.

Finally forms of dimension at least

∎

We used a computer algebra system to explicitly compute the diameter of representation graphs of the hyperbolic plane over finite fields with up to 2000 elements.

3.3.
Structural properties of the diameter

As a preparation for the proofs of the above theorems, we collect some structural information about the diameter and connectedness of representation graphs, where possible over arbitrary fields.

The action of the isometry group of a quadratic form on the vertex set of a representation graph, which is just the underlying vector space of the quadratic form, allows to restrict adjacency-related concepts to prototypical situations.
In particular, it is sufficient to consider distances and paths of vertices to the origin.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
F
be a field and
q
be a quadratic form defined on the vector space
V
and
a\in F
. Let
\operatorname{iso}(q)
be the isometry group of
q
acting on
V
.

(a)

The group
\operatorname{iso}(q)
acts on the set of paths in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
in the natural way, preserving lengths, i.e. if
P
is a
u
-
w
-path of length
\ell
in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
, then
\sigma(P)
is a
\sigma(u)
-
\sigma(w)
-path of length
\ell
.

(b)

Let
v_{1},v_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in V
such that
w_{1}-v_{1}\neq 0\neq w_{2}-v_{2}
and
q(w_{1}-v_{1})=q(w_{2}-v_{2})
.
Then
\mathsf{d}(v_{1},w_{1})=\mathsf{d}(v_{2},w_{2})
in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
.

(c)

For all
b\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)
, let
v_{b}\in V\setminus\{0\}
such that
q(v_{b})=b
.
We then have

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})=\sup\{\mathsf{d}(0,v_{b})\mid b\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)\}.

Proof.

(a)
follows from elementary considerations.
Then
(b)
follows from
(a)
, since
\operatorname{iso}(q)
acts transitively on

\left\{(u,w)\in V\times V\mid q(u-w)=b\right\}

for any
b\in F
.
Finally for
(c)
we observe that by translation invariance, we have

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})=\sup\{\mathsf{d}(0,v)\mid v\in V\}.

By
(b)
we have
\mathsf{d}(0,v)=\mathsf{d}(0,w)
if
q(v)=q(w)
and
v\neq 0\neq w
, so we can take the supremum over
\{v_{b}\mid b\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)\}
.
∎

In the remainder we will often reduce the study of arbitrary
u
-
w
-paths to such paths starting at the origin without explicitly referring to this lemma.

Next, we will show that for fixed
a
the diameter of
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
of an isotropic quadratic form
q
can be bounded by the diameter of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a}
.
This follows from the fact that every vector is contained in a subform isometric to the hyperbolic plane.
While this is not difficult to see we include an argument for the convenience of the reader, as we are not aware of a direct reference.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
q
be an isotropic quadratic form defined on a vector space
V
over a field
F
and
v\in V
.
Then there exists a
2
-dimensional subspace
W\subseteq V
with
v\in W
and
q|_{W}\cong\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Since
q
is isotropic, there is a subspace
W^{\prime}
with

q|_{W^{\prime}}\cong\mathbb{H}\subseteq q.

Since
\mathbb{H}
is universal, we find some
w\in W^{\prime}
with
q(w)=q(v)
.
By Witt’s Extension Theorem
[
EKM08
, Theorem 8.3]
, there is an isometry
\sigma\in\mathsf{O}(q)
with
\sigma(w)=v
and the claim follows by putting
W=\sigma(W^{\prime})
.
∎

We can now apply this to obtain a bound on the diameter of representation graphs of isotropic quadratic forms.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
q
be an isotropic quadratic form and
a\in F
.
We then have

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})\leq\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a}),

where we use the conventions
\infty\leq\infty
and
n\leq\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By translation invariance it suffices to consider
0
-
v
-paths in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
.
If
v
is an arbitrary element it is contained in a subform
q^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{H}
of
q
which is isomorphic to the hyperbolic plane (cf.
Lemma

3.5
).
Therefore we find a
0
-
v
-path in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
which is contained in the subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{q^{\prime},a}\cong\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a}
. In any case the length of a minimal
0
-
v
-path in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
is bounded by the diameter of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a}
.
∎

On a side note, it is also easy to see that the connectedness of representation graphs is related to structural aspects of the underlying quadratic forms.
To be precise, if
q
and
q^{\prime}
are quadratic forms over a field
F
and
a\in F
, such that
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
and
\mathcal{G}_{q^{\prime},a}
are connected, then
\mathcal{G}_{q\perp q^{\prime},a}
is also connected.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form from which we can split orthogonally a subform
q^{\prime}
which is universal and for which
\mathcal{G}_{q^{\prime},a}
is connected.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
is also connected.

Proof.

Write
q=q^{\prime}\perp q^{\prime\prime}
, where
q^{\prime}
is the universal subform.
Let
(v,w)\in V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime\prime}
be arbitrary.
Say
q^{\prime\prime}(w)=b
.
Then by the universality of
q^{\prime}
there exists
v^{\prime}
such that
q^{\prime}(v^{\prime})=a-b
.
Then

(q^{\prime}\perp q^{\prime\prime})((v,w)-(v-v^{\prime},0))=q^{\prime}(v^{\prime})+q^{\prime\prime}(w)=a-b+b=a.

So there is an edge between
(v,w)
and
(v-v^{\prime},0)
, and since
G_{q^{\prime},a}
is connected, there is a
(0,0)
-
(v,w)
-path in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
.
∎

3.4.
Representation graphs for

The connectedness of the representation graph
\mathcal{G}_{q,0}
depends only on whether

is isotropic or not.
Clearly, if

is anisotropic, the representation graph

consists only of isolated points.
We will show that if

is isotropic, the graph

Proposition 3.8
.

Let

be a field.
Let

be an isotropic quadratic form over

Then
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,0})=2

Proof.

First, by

we have
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,0})\leq\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},0})
.
So we only need to consider
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},0}
and it is enough to bound the length of a shortest
0
-
v
-path by translation invariance.

For
\mathbb{H}\cong[0,0]
the standard basis
e_{1},e_{2}
consists of isotropic vectors.
Let

.
If

is isotropic, i.e.

Otherwise we write

∎

3.5.
Hyperbolic planes

We collect some results specific to hyperbolic planes, not restricted to finite fields.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
F
be a field with at least
5
elements.
The set
\{(x,x^{-1})\ \mid x\in F^{\ast}\}
is a group generating set of
F^{2}
.

Proof.

We show that the set
\{(x,x^{-1})\ \mid x\in F^{\ast}\}
generates all vectors of the form
(0,f)
with
f\in F
. If
x,y,x+y\neq 0
then

(x,x^{-1})+(y,y^{-1})-(x+y,(x+y)^{-1})=(0,x^{-1}+y^{-1}+(x+y)^{-1})

represents every vector
(0,f)
with

f\in S=\{x^{-1}+y^{-1}-(x+y)^{-1}\ \mid x,y,x+y\neq 0\}.

We claim that
S\supseteq F^{\ast}
.
Note that
S
is closed under multiplication with nonzero scalars, so it is sufficient to show that
S\cap F^{\ast}\neq\emptyset
.
Note that for
x=1
and
y\notin\{0,-x\}=\{0,-1\}
we have

\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y}-\frac{1}{x+y}=\frac{y^{2}+y+1}{y+y^{2}},

which is zero if and only if
y^{2}+y+1=0
.
This quadratic equation has at most two solutions.
So if
|F|\geq 5
, we find some nonzero element in
S
.

Similarily, we get every vector
(f,0)
and thus every element of
F^{2}
.
∎

With this explicit generating set we can derive an upper bound on the diameter in the hyperbolic case.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be the hyperbolic plane over a field
F
and let

.

(a)

The graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a}
is connected if and only if
|F|\geq 5
.

(b)

If
|F|\geq 5
, then
\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq 3
for all isotropic

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})\leq 4
.

Proof.

We may assume
a=1
by the roundness of the hyperbolic plane, i.e.

for all

(see
Section

).
If
|F|\leq 4
, then
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
is disconnected as discussed in the examples in
Section

.

Now let
|F|\geq 5
.
Since
\mathbb{H}(x,y)=xy
, we have

V_{1}=\{(x,x^{-1})\ \mid x\in F^{\ast}\}

which is a group generating set of

by
Lemma

3.9
, so the graph is connected by
Lemma

2.6
.

For the bound on the diameter we observe that the proof of
Lemma

3.9
shows that there is a path of length at most

from the origin to

Concatenating this path with the edge

yields a path of length at most 4 from the origin to

.
The assertion now follows from choosing

in

.
∎

On the other hand, a simple argument gives a lower bound for the diameter in the hyperbolic case over arbitrary fields.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form of dimension 2.
For

, we have

(V_{a}+V_{a})\cap V_{0}=\{0\}.

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{H},a})\geq 3

Let

be such that

which implies

.
If

were a basis,

would be degenerate.
So

are linearly dependent and we have

for some

.
Finally we get

so

and

∎

From now on, we will restrict our attention to isotropic forms.
Quadratic forms over finite fields of dimension at least 3 are isotropic and by
Corollary

the diameter of an associated representation graph is bounded by that of the graph of the corresponding hyperbolic plane.
It will now turn out that this can be strengthened significantly.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form of dimension
3
over a finite field
F
and
a\in F^{\ast}
.
For

we have
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{G}_{q,a}}(0,v)>2
if and only if
v
is isotropic and
\det(q)\neq-a
.
In particular, if
|F|\geq 5
, we have

\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})=\begin{cases}2,&\text{ if $\det(q)=-a$}\\
3,&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let first
v
be an anisotropic vector, i.e.
b:=q(v)\neq 0
.
Since
\dim(q)=3
we necessarily have
\cha(F)\neq 2
, so
q|_{\operatorname{span}(v)}
is non-degenerate and therefore is a subform of
q
.
This means that we can extend
v
to a suitable basis of
V
in which
q
is isometric to
\langle b\rangle\perp\mathbb{H}
.
Since
\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(\mathbb{H})=F
, there exists some vector
w\in v^{\perp}
with
q(w)=a-\frac{b}{4}=q(-w)
.
Then
\tfrac{1}{2}v\pm w\in V_{a}
and

v=\left(\frac{1}{2}v+w\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}v-w\right)\in V_{a}+V_{a}.

Let now
v\neq 0
be an isotropic vector.
Similar to above, we extend
v
to a basis
(v,w,u)
of
V
such that in this basis,
q
is isometric to
\mathbb{H}\perp\langle c\rangle
for some

and such that
q(v)=0=q(w)
and

(i.e.

is a
hyperbolic pair
).
If

, we may assume that
c=a
.
In this case we can write
v=(v+u)-u\in V_{a}+V_{a}
.

Assume
c\not\in aF^{\ast 2}
and
v=x+y
with
x,y\in V_{a}
.
Since
v=v+0w+0u
we have

x=\alpha v+\lambda w+\mu u\quad\text{and}\quad y=\beta v-\lambda w-\mu u

with
\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\mu\in F
and
\alpha+\beta=1
.
Now
\lambda\neq 0

by assumption.
But

\alpha\lambda+\mu^{2}c=q(x)=a=q(y)=-\beta\lambda+\mu^{2}c

, a contradiction.

If
|F|\geq 5
we know
\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq 3
for isotropic vectors by

∎

Having dealt with forms of dimension at most

, we are left to consider forms of dimension at least

.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form of dimension at least 4 over a finite field and

.
Then
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})=2
.

Proof.

Since
V_{a}\subsetneq V
, we have
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{G}_{q,a})\geq 2
.
Now let
v\in V
with
q(v)=x\neq a
.
By
Theorem

2.1
,

3.5

As in the proof of

3.8

∎

Remark 3.18
.

In the proofs of
Lemma

3.16
and
Lemma

3.17
, we relied on the classification of quadratic forms over finite fields.
In fact, we only needed the property that over finite fields, every form of dimension at least 3 is isotropic, or equivalently, that every form of dimension
2
is universal (we used both characterizations in the proof).
In quadratic form theory, this behavior is expressed using the

-invariant
of a field

According to the above, the statements of

3.16
and
Lemma

3.17
apply to all fields

with
|F|\geq 5
and

.

4.1.

We will now determine the

We will see that, with few exceptions, this graph invariant is almost always given by 3.

Lemma 4.1

The vectors

4.2.
Cycles of Length 3

We complement the computation of the minimum length of a cycle in a representation graph by counting the number of such cycles, starting with cycles of length
3
.
Again linearizing, we first consider those cycles that include the origin, i.e. cycles of the form
(0,v,w,0)
.
The vector space spanned by the vertices of such a cycle has dimension
1
or
2
, and we will count them according to this distinction.

For fixed
q
,
a\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)
and
k\in\{1,2\}
, we will write

c_{k}=\text{number of cycles $(0,v,w,0)$ in $\mathcal{G}_{q,a}$ with $\dim(\operatorname{span}(v,w))=k$.}

While
c_{k}
clearly depends on
q
and
a
, we assume that this is clear from the context.
Then the number of all cycles of length
3
in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
can be related to
c_{1},c_{2}
as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
F
be a finite field with
f
elements,
q
be a quadratic form over
F
, and let
a\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)
.
The number of all cycles of length 3 in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
is given by

(c_{1}+c_{2})\cdot\frac{|V|}{3}.

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are as in
Lemma

4.4
and
Lemma

4.5
depending on
a
and
q
.

Proof.

Counting in two ways, we obtain

\displaystyle 3\cdot\#\text{ cycles $C$ of length 3}

\displaystyle=|\{(C,v)\mid C\text{ cycle of length 3, $C$ contains $v$}\}|

\displaystyle=|V|\cdot\#\text{ cycles $C$ containing $v$}

\displaystyle=|V|\cdot\#\text{ cycles $C$ containing $0$}.

and the claim follows.
∎

Now we are left to determine
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, we do this first in the case of adjacency induced by isotropic vectors, i.e.
a=0
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
q
be an isotropic quadratic form over a finite field
F
with
|F|=f
.
Let
t_{i}
be the number of totally isotropic subspaces of dimension
i
.
For the graph
\mathcal{G}_{q,0}
, we have

c_{1}=t_{1}\cdot\binom{f-1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{2}=t_{2}\cdot\frac{(f^{2}-1)\cdot(f^{2}-f)}{2}.

Proof.

Each cycle
(0,v,w,0)
with
v,w
linearly dependent uniquely determines a totally isotropic subspace of dimension 1 and for each such subspace, there are clearly
\binom{f-1}{2}
choices of
v,w
to construct a cycle.

If
C=(0,v,w,0)
is a cycle of length 3 with
v,w
linearly independent, then
W=\operatorname{span}(v,w)
is a totally isotropic subspace (see also
Lemma

4.1
).
For any totally isotropic subspace
W
of dimension
2
, vectors
x,y\in W
correspond to such a cycle if and only if
x,y
are a basis of
W
, giving the number in the assertion.
∎

To obtain explicit numbers one can use the well-known number of totally isotropic subspaces of fixed dimension, see for example
[
HT16
, Theorem 1.41 (i)]
for a modern exposition of this question, albeit in a geometric language.

Now we turn to the case
a\neq 0
.
Here we can base the calculation of the number of cycles of length 3 on suitable decompositions of
q
into lower dimensional subforms.
Over fields of characteristic not 2, we use subforms of the kind
\langle a\rangle
(non-degenerate only if
\cha(F)\neq 2
), and over fields of characteristic not 3, we use subforms of the kind
[a,a^{-1}]
(non-degenerate only if
\cha(F)\neq 3
).
Obviously, the respective scopes of these approaches have a huge overlap, but we know of no characteristic-free approach.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
F
be a finite field,
a\in\mathsf{D}(q)
.

(a)

We have
c_{1}=0
if
\cha(F)\neq 3
and
c_{1}=\tfrac{|V_{a}|}{2}
if
\cha(F)=3
.

(b)

Let
\cha(F)\neq 2
. If there exists a form
q^{\prime}
such that
q=\langle a\rangle\perp q^{\prime}
, then

c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot|V_{q,a}|\cdot|V_{q^{\prime},3a}|,

otherwise
c_{2}=0
.

(c)

Let
\cha(F)\neq 3
. If there exists a form
q^{\prime}
such that
q=[a,a^{-1}]\perp q^{\prime}
, then

c_{2}=\frac{|V_{[a,a^{-1}],3a}|\cdot|\mathsf{O}(q)|}{|\mathsf{O}([a,a^{-1}])|\cdot|\mathsf{O}(q^{\prime})|},

otherwise
c_{2}=0
.

Proof.

(a)
:
By
Lemma

4.1
, we see that
c_{1}=0
when
\cha(F)\neq 3
and when
\cha(F)=3
, for any cycle
(0,v,w,0)
with

linearly dependent, we have

and vice versa.

(b)
:
Let
v\in V_{a}
.
First we count the number of ways to extend

to a cycle
(0,v,w,0)
of length
3
.
Since
q(v)=a\neq 0
,
v
splits off orthogonally.
We write

with

and

.
By the proof of

, we have

a=b_{q}(v,w)=b_{q}(v,\lambda v+w_{0})=b_{q}(v,\lambda v)+b_{q}(v,w_{0})=\lambda b_{q}(v,v)=2a\lambda,

so
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
.
This implies

The number of possibilities to extend

|V_{q^{\prime},3a/4}|=|V_{q^{\prime},3a}|

This counts every cycle of length 3 exactly twice.

(c)

Each cycle of length 3 of the form

By Witt’s Extension Theorem

\frac{|\mathsf{O}(q)|}{|\mathsf{O}([a,a^{-1}])\times\mathsf{O}(q^{\prime})|}=\frac{|\mathsf{O}(q)|}{|\mathsf{O}([a,a^{-1}])|\cdot|\mathsf{O}(q^{\prime})|}

such subspaces

.

Now we fix

By the proof of

∎

\frac{|V_{a}|^{2}}{2}-|V_{a}|,\text{ if }\cha(F)\neq 2\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|V_{a}|\cdot(|V_{a}|-1)}{2},\text{ if }\cha(F)=2

We now turn to the case
a=0\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}(q)
, so necessarily
q\cong\mathbb{H}
.
If
C=(0,u,w,v,0)
is a
4
-cycle with
u,v
linearly dependent, then
\operatorname{span}(u,v,w)
is
1
-dimensional and necessarily totally isotropic.
To see this write
v=\lambda u
with
\lambda\in F\setminus\{0,1\}
.
With
Proposition

4.8
we have

0=b_{q}(w,u-v)=(1-\lambda)b_{q}(w,u)

and thus
b_{q}(w,u)=0
, which then implies

0=q(w-u)=q(w)+q(u)-b_{q}(w,u)=q(w).

If
u,w
were linearly independent, they would be an orthogonal basis consisting of isotropic vectors, so
q\cong\langle 0,0\rangle
would be degenerate, a contradiction.

Any three pairwise distinct non-zero elements in this subspace give rise to
3!/2=3
distinct
4
-cycles, so a total of
3\cdot\binom{f-1}{3}
distinct
4

Now there are
2

Let now
(0,u,w,v,0)
be a cycle with
u,v
linearly independent and let
\lambda,\mu\in F

.
Obviously, we have
b_{q}(u,v)\neq 0
.
We also have

0=q(w-u)=(\lambda-1)\mu b_{q}(u,v),

\lambda=1

\lambda=0
or
\mu=1

Since

We have
(f-1)^{2}

isotropic and linearly independent, and each choice gives a different cycle of length 4, so there are
(f-1)^{2}

The assertion finally follows from an argument analogous to

Besides the explicit computation of the number of
4

Some of this works in arbitrary dimension
\geq 2

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form of dimension
\geq 2
over an arbitrary field,

and

be a cycle of length 4 with diagonals

in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}

(a)

The diagonals
d_{1},d_{2}
are perpendicular, i.e.
b_{q}(d_{1},d_{2})=0
.

Now let

a\in F^{\ast}
. Then also

(b)

If
\cha(F)\neq 2
, we have
q\cong\langle q(d_{1}),q(d_{2})\rangle
.

(c)

If
\cha(F)=2
, we have
d_{1}=d_{2}

Proof.

After a translation, we can assume that
C=(0,u,w,v,0)
.
Then

a=q(w-v)=q(w)+q(v)-b_{q}(w,v)=q(w)+a-b_{q}(w,v)

and similarly for
w-u
.
Subtracting these equalities and using the bilinearity of
b_{q}
implies
b_{q}(w,u-v)=0
, proving
(a)
.

If
a\neq 0
and

w

3.11
.
This implies that

are linearly independent, which proves
(b)
.
For
(c)

5

∎

In general, computing the number of cycles of length 4 in higher dimensions seems to be a much harder task.
In dimension 2, (
5
) implies that a cycle of the form
(0,u,w,v,0)
is already determined by
w
, but in higher dimensions two different cycles can have the same diagonal vector.
This happens e.g. for
F=\mathbb{F}_{5},q=\langle 1,1,1\rangle,a=1
and
w=(1,4,0)
, then we have the cycles

C_{i}=(0,u_{i},w,v_{i},0)
where

\displaystyle u_{1}=(0,4,0),v_{1}=(2,1,1)\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{2}=(0,4,0),v_{2}=(1,0,0).

Nevertheless, we wanted to compute the number of cycles of length 4 at least in those cases where cycles of length 4 are the shortest cycles occurring in
\mathcal{G}_{q,a}
, and these cases are covered by our results with only one exception, the form
\mathbb{H}\perp[1,1]
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
a=1
.
We discuss this briefly as a concluding example to illustrate the greater complexity in higher dimensions.

First, we count the
4
-cycles containing the origin.
We observe (by explicit enumeration, which we omit), that there are
15
possible choices for the vertex
w
opposing the origin, fixing one of the diagonals.
For each of these
15
choices of
w
there are
6
distinct
0
-
w
-paths of length
2
in the graph.
This gives a total of
15\cdot\binom{6}{2}=225
cycles of length
4
containing the origin, and a total of
\frac{|\mathbb{F}_{2}|^{4}}{4}\cdot 225=900
cycles of length
4
.
