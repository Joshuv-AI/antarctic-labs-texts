Dietary adherence and overnutrition among Kuwaiti adolescents: a school-based assessment of anthropometry and dietary intake
Authors: Anwar H AlBaloul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a critical period for the development of dietary behaviours that influence obesity and non-communicable disease risk later in life. In Kuwait, evidence on adolescent nutritional status integrating dietary intake with anthropometric indicators remains limited. This study aimed to assess adherence to dietary recommendations and examine the association between dietary intake and overnutrition, as measured by BMI and waist circumference (WC), among adolescents in Kuwait.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 375 adolescents aged 13–17 years recruited from public high schools in Kuwait. Dietary intake was assessed using a 24-h dietary recall and evaluated against UK Dietary Reference Values. Anthropometric measurements included BMI and WC. Associations between dietary adherence and anthropometric outcomes were examined using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for relevant sociodemographic and behavioural covariates.ResultsOverall, 44.5% of participants were classified as overweight or obese based on BMI, and 34.7% had an elevated WC. Adherence to dietary recommendations was low, and almost all adolescents failed to meet recommendations for dietary fibre and fish intake. In contrast, excessive intake of free sugars (92.2%) and saturated fat (61.7%) was common. Protein intake was inversely associated with elevated WC, whereas excessive intake of total fat, carbohydrates, and red meat was positively associated with elevated WC.ConclusionsKuwaiti adolescents exhibit a high prevalence of overnutrition alongside widespread non-adherence to dietary reference values. Dietary intake showed stronger associations with WC than with BMI, suggesting that WC may better capture diet-related differences in adiposity among adolescents. These findings underscore the need for culturally appropriate dietary reference standards and targeted public health strategies to address poor diet quality and rising metabolic risk among adolescents in Kuwait.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07489-9.Keywords: Adolescent, Dietary behaviour, Diet quality, Kuwait

Introduction

Nutritional status reflects an individual’s ability to maintain a balance between dietary intake and physiological requirements, influenced by age, sex, health status, and environmental context [1]. Nutritional assessment, commonly based on dietary intake and anthropometric measurements in population-based studies, is essential for identifying individuals at risk of nutrition-related disorders and informing prevention strategies. The World Health Organisation (WHO) recognises poor nutrition as a major contributor to the global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), highlighting the importance of promoting healthy dietary behaviours throughout the life course [2].

Adolescence is a critical period characterised by rapid physical growth, hormonal changes, and the establishment of dietary behaviours that often persist into adulthood [3]. This period has therefore been recognised as a critical window for nutritional screening and intervention, with the potential to prevent or delay the onset of nutrition-related chronic diseases [4, 5]. Malnutrition during adolescence includes both undernutrition, such as inadequate growth and micronutrient deficiencies, and overnutrition, characterised by excessive energy intake leading to overweight and obesity [6]. However, in high-income countries, including those of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), overnutrition has become the predominant public health concern. Anthropometric indicators such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) are widely used in epidemiological studies to assess general and central adiposity, respectively.

Kuwait has one of the highest prevalences of obesity and NCDs worldwide. Approximately 72% of all deaths are attributable to NCDs, while recent evidence indicates that more than half of Kuwaiti adolescents are overweight or obese [7, 8]. This is particularly concerning because obesity established during adolescence frequently persists into adulthood and substantially increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension [9, 10]. Furthermore, studies from Kuwait have reported a high prevalence of prediabetes among adolescents with obesity, highlighting the urgent need for effective prevention strategies [11].

Despite the growing burden of adolescent obesity, evidence describing the relationship between dietary intake and overnutrition in Kuwait remains limited. Previous studies have largely relied on single anthropometric indicators, particularly BMI, with relatively little integration of detailed dietary assessment. Similar methodological limitations have been reported across the GCC and wider Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, where adolescent nutrition research remains fragmented and often relies on non-standardised dietary assessment methods. Appropriate evaluation of dietary intake alongside anthropometric measures may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of adolescents' nutritional status and obesity-related risk.

Accordingly, this cross-sectional study aimed to assess adherence to dietary recommendations among Kuwaiti adolescents and to examine the association between dietary intake and overnutrition, assessed using BMI and WC. By integrating dietary intake with complementary anthropometric indicators, this study seeks to provide evidence to inform adolescent nutrition policies and obesity prevention strategies in Kuwait.

Methods

Study design and population

This school-based cross-sectional study recruited a total of 375 adolescents aged 13–17 years from public high schools across all six governorates of the State of Kuwait. Kuwait’s public school system is gender-segregated, with separate schools for boys and girls, and comprises three academic grades at the high school level. Private schools operate in Kuwait; however, they follow heterogeneous curricula and were excluded to ensure comparability and representativeness of the national public-school adolescent population. The minimum required sample size (n = 350) was calculated to estimate population proportions with a precision of ± 0.05 at a 95% confidence level, assuming maximum variability (p = 0.5). The final sample was increased to 375 to account for potential non-response and incomplete data.

Eligibility criteria

Adolescents were eligible for inclusion if they were enrolled in a public high school, aged between 13 and 17 years, and able to provide informed assent with parental consent. Students with diagnosed chronic conditions affecting growth or dietary intake, or those unable to complete dietary assessment, were excluded.

A multistage stratified random sampling approach was employed. In the first stage, schools were stratified by sex (boys’ and girls’ schools) to reflect the gender-segregated structure of Kuwait’s public education system. Schools were then randomly selected from each stratum across the six governorates. The number of schools selected per governorate was proportionate to the distribution of public high schools and student population size, with larger samples drawn from more populous governorates (e.g. Al Ahmadi) and smaller samples from less populous governorates (e.g. Mubarak Al Kabeer). Due to higher female enrolment in public schools nationally, a greater proportion of female students was included to ensure population representativeness.

In the second stage, academic grades within selected schools were randomly chosen, followed by the random selection of students within those grades. Selected students were invited to participate and provided with study information sheets. Written parental consent and student assent were obtained before participation. Students who declined participation or did not return consent forms were not recruited.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements included body weight, height, and WC, performed by trained researchers. Body weight was measured to the nearest 100 g using calibrated portable scales (Omron BF511, Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), while wearing light clothes. Height was measured in a full standing position, without shoes, to the nearest centimetre using a measuring tape. Waist circumference was measured using a measuring tape, based on the WHO procedure, midway between the lowest ribs and the iliac crest [12].

Dietary data collection

Dietary intake was collected with a 24-h dietary recall, using the MyFood24 instrument. This instrument uses instructions from food portion photographs and common household measures to estimate the consumed food weight [13]. This study used the MyFood24 Middle East version, which considered food consumed in Kuwait, including cooking methods and the most consumed food brands.

Trained researchers conducted face-to-face interviews with participants in a designated private area within the school during school hours. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min and followed a standardised multiple-pass protocol to minimise recall bias. Researchers assisted participants with food identification, portion size estimation using photographic prompts and household measures, and data entry within MyFood24. Dietary output included detailed macro- and micronutrient intake for each participant.

Diet quality assessment

Due to the absence of national or regional dietary reference values for adolescents in Kuwait, dietary intake was assessed against the UK Dietary Reference Values (UK-DRV) for individuals aged 11–18 years [14]. The UK-DRV were selected because they are evidence-based, age-specific, and widely used in international adolescent nutrition research. Their compatibility with the Children’s Eatwell Guide (C-EWG) allowed for structured evaluation of both nutrient adequacy and overall diet quality.

While Kuwait is socially and culturally distinct from the UK, it has experienced a rapid nutrition and epidemiological transition characterised by increased availability of energy-dense foods and Westernised dietary patterns. The use of UK-DRV is therefore intended as a benchmark rather than a prescriptive standard, and the findings are interpreted with explicit consideration of sociocultural and environmental differences that influence dietary behaviours.

Dietary intake was assessed across ten food and nutrient components: carbohydrates, protein, total fat, saturated fat, dietary fibre, free sugars, salt, fruit and vegetables, fish (oily and non-oily), and red/processed meat. Age- and sex-specific portion size adjustments were applied in line with previously published methods [15–17].

Children’s eatwell guide score

Overall diet quality was assessed using the C-EWG score [17]. This score evaluates adherence to eight key dietary components: total fat, saturated fat, free sugars, dietary fibre, salt, fruit and vegetables, fish, and red/processed meat. Each component was scored as 1 if the recommendation was met and 0 if not met, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 8. Higher scores indicate greater adherence to dietary guidelines and better overall diet quality.

The C-EWG was selected due to its structured, evidence-based approach and its alignment with UK-DRV thresholds, enabling consistent interpretation of dietary adequacy in the absence of culturally specific adolescent diet quality indices for Kuwait [17].

Study variables

The primary exposure variables were the C-EWG score and adherence to ten food and nutrient components derived from the UK-DRV: carbohydrates, protein, total fat, saturated fat, dietary fibre, free sugars, salt, fruit and vegetables, fish, and red/processed meat. Participants were classified as adherent or non-adherent to each dietary recommendation according to the UK-DRV thresholds described above.

The primary outcomes were BMI and WC. BMI was calculated from measured height and weight and classified according to the 2022 CDC BMI-for-age and sex-specific percentiles as underweight (< 5th percentile), normal weight (5th–85th percentile), overweight (85th–95th percentile), or obese (≥ 95th percentile). WC was classified using the Kuwait age- and sex-specific WC percentiles, with values at or above the 90th percentile classified as elevated waist circumference [18].

Smoking status was self-reported by participants, reported as current smoker or non-smoker. Smoking was included as a covariate because it is a behavioural factor that may be associated with both dietary behaviours and adiposity and therefore could confound the association between dietary intake and anthropometric outcomes.

Statistical analysis

We used R statistical software version 3.6.1 for all analyses, with a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and were found to be normally distributed. The mean and standard deviation were determined for continuous variables, including age, and anthropometric measurements (weight, height, WC, and BMI). Two-sample t-tests were used to determine the difference in anthropometric measurements across sexes. Our descriptive analysis reported the prevalence of each categorical variable across our sample size, including nationality, governorate, smoking status, adherence to UK-DRV, WC, and BMI.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between anthropometric measures categories (WC and BMI) and dietary intake. For each anthropometric outcome, eleven separate regression models were constructed: one for the C-EWG score and ten for each food and nutrient. The primary exposure variables were levels of adherence to the UK-DRV, which were entered into the models as categorical variables [14]. Separate models were constructed for each exposure variable, and adjusted for age, sex, nationality, governorate, and smoking status.

Dietary assessment

The proportion of adolescents meeting the UK-DRV for selected foods and nutrients is presented in Fig. 1. Adherence varied substantially across dietary components. Approximately two-thirds of participants met the UK-DRV for protein intake, while fewer than half met the recommendations for carbohydrate intake. In contrast, the majority of adolescents did not meet the recommended intake for dietary fibre, free sugars, fruit and vegetables, and fish. Notably, almost all participants failed to achieve the recommended intake of fruit and vegetables.

The eatwell guide score

Overall diet quality, assessed using the C-EWG score, was low across the study population. The mean C-EWG score was 2.5 (SD = 1.0), with observed scores ranging from 0 to 5 out of a maximum of 8. None of the participants met six or more of the dietary recommendations. Approximately one-third of adolescents met two recommendations, while fewer than 20% met four or more recommendations.

Association between adherence to dietary recommendations and anthropometric measurements

Associations between dietary adherence and anthropometric outcomes are presented in Table 3. After adjustment for age, sex, nationality, governorate, and smoking status, adolescents who did not adhere to the recommendations for total fat (OR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.29–3.20), carbohydrates (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.08–2.66), and red/processed meat (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.07–3.26) had significantly higher odds of elevated WC. In contrast, adherence to the recommended protein intake was associated with lower odds of elevated WC (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.38–0.95). No associations were observed between the Children's Eatwell Guide score or the remaining dietary components and WC. Likewise, no associations were observed between dietary adherence and BMI categories.

Discussion

This study provides one of the first assessments of dietary intake alongside measures of both general and central adiposity among adolescents in Kuwait. We observed three principal findings. First, overweight, obesity, and elevated WC were highly prevalent, confirming that overnutrition remains a major public health concern among Kuwaiti adolescents. Second, adherence to dietary recommendations was consistently poor, with particularly low intakes of dietary fibre, fruit and vegetables, and fish, alongside excessive consumption of free sugars and saturated fat. Third, dietary intake was associated with WC but not BMI, suggesting that central adiposity may better reflect diet-related differences in body fat distribution during adolescence.

The dietary patterns observed in this study should be interpreted within the context of Kuwait's rapid nutrition transition. Economic development, urbanisation, and changes in food availability have been accompanied by increased consumption of energy-dense, highly processed foods and reduced adherence to more traditional dietary patterns. Adolescents in Kuwait are increasingly exposed to fast-food outlets, sugar-sweetened beverages, and sedentary lifestyles, which may contribute to positive energy balance. Although these factors were not directly measured in the present study, they provide plausible contextual explanations for the widespread dietary imbalance and high prevalence of overnutrition observed in our sample [14, 16, 17].

Nevertheless, the use of UK-DRVs in a socio-culturally distinct setting represents an important limitation of this study. Dietary practices, food availability, portion sizes, and meal structures in Kuwait differ substantially from those in the UK, and these contextual differences may influence the extent to which UK-DRV thresholds accurately reflect nutritional adequacy or risk among Kuwaiti [9, 20]. As such, observed levels of non-adherence should be interpreted as indicators of relative dietary imbalance rather than absolute measures of nutritional deficiency. These findings underscore the need for the development of culturally relevant, region-specific dietary reference standards for adolescent populations in Kuwait and the wider GCC region.

This study identified a high burden of overnutrition among Kuwaiti adolescents, with 44.5% of participants classified as overweight or obese and 34.7% exhibiting a WC indicative of elevated metabolic risk. Overall, the dietary profile of Kuwaiti adolescents was characterised by low consumption of fibre-rich foods, fruits, vegetables, and fish together with excessive intakes of saturated fat and free sugars, resulting in uniformly poor diet quality. Similar patterns have been reported among adolescents in other high-income countries, including the United Kingdom, the United States, and Australia, where adherence to dietary recommendations for fibre, fruit and vegetables, and fish is consistently low, while intakes of saturated fat and added sugars frequently exceed recommended levels [17, 21–23]. Evidence from neighbouring Gulf countries and the wider MENA region demonstrates comparable trends, with studies from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Lebanon reporting increasing consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods alongside declining adherence to healthy dietary patterns among adolescents [24–26]. Collectively, these findings suggest that poor diet quality among adolescents is a widespread consequence of ongoing nutrition transition, although the rapid pace of economic development and environmental change in Kuwait may further amplify these dietary challenges.

The widespread non-adherence to dietary recommendations observed in this study could be viewed as a reflection of an overall dietary imbalance pattern. The combination of low intakes of fibre-rich and nutrient-dense foods with high consumption of saturated fat and free sugars is consistent with dietary patterns that have been associated with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes during adolescence and may contribute to the growing burden of obesity and non-communicable diseases across the Gulf region [4, 27, 28]. While factors such as nutrition literacy, parental food practices, and the wider food environment are recognised determinants of adolescent dietary behaviour, these variables were not assessed in the present study and should therefore be considered as plausible contextual influences rather than direct explanations for the observed findings [15, 21, 29].

An important finding of this study was that several dietary components were independently associated with elevated WC but not with BMI after adjustment for potential confounders. Higher intakes of total fat, carbohydrates, and red and processed meat were associated with greater odds of abdominal obesity, whereas higher protein intake was inversely associated with elevated WC. These findings are consistent with previous studies from Canada and Northern Ireland demonstrating positive associations between dietary fat intake and WC among adolescents [26, 30]. The observed associations are also biologically plausible. Diets characterised by high intakes of energy-dense foods, saturated fat, and refined carbohydrates promote chronic positive energy balance and may contribute to insulin resistance and increased hepatic lipogenesis, processes that preferentially favour visceral fat accumulation [31]. In contrast, higher protein intake has been associated with enhanced satiety, improved appetite regulation, greater preservation of lean body mass during growth, and lower overall energy intake, which may contribute to reduced central adiposity [26, 29].

The absence of significant associations between dietary intake and BMI may highlight the limitations of BMI as a marker of adiposity during adolescence. BMI does not distinguish between fat mass and lean mass and is substantially influenced by pubertal growth and normal developmental changes in body composition [32]. Consequently, BMI may be relatively insensitive to early diet-related differences in fat distribution. In contrast, WC more directly reflects abdominal fat accumulation, which is may be associated with insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and other cardiometabolic abnormalities [18, 19]. The stronger associations observed between dietary intake and WC therefore support the use of central adiposity measures alongside BMI when evaluating nutrition-related health risks among adolescents, particularly in populations such as Kuwait where obesity prevalence is high and dietary patterns are rapidly changing.

Public health implications

The findings of this study have important implications for adolescent nutritional surveillance and public health practice in Kuwait. Using an established dietary reference framework as a benchmarking tool, this study identified widespread non-adherence to dietary recommendations among Kuwaiti adolescents, alongside a high prevalence of central adiposity as indicated by WC. Importantly, dietary adherence was associated with WC, a recognised marker of metabolic risk in adolescents, but not with BMI-based classifications. This distinction underscores the value of incorporating multiple anthropometric indicators when assessing nutrition-related health risk in adolescent populations.

From a public health perspective, these findings contribute to the limited evidence base on adolescent nutritional assessment in Kuwait and highlight key areas of dietary imbalance that may inform population-level monitoring and prevention efforts. Given evidence that cardiometabolic risk factors and adiposity patterns established during adolescence often persist into adulthood, the observed distribution of dietary non-adherence and central adiposity may signal increased future burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases.

Rather than advocating the direct adoption of UK-DRV, the application of this framework in the present study highlights the need for the development of culturally appropriate, locally validated dietary reference standards for adolescents in Kuwait. Such standards would support more accurate nutritional assessment, guide targeted interventions, and strengthen national strategies aimed at addressing adolescent overnutrition and related metabolic risk. Future longitudinal studies and intervention trials are warranted to examine how dietary patterns during adolescence influence metabolic health trajectories over time and to evaluate the effectiveness of context-specific dietary interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is among the first to integrate detailed dietary intake data with both BMI and WC measures in a school-based sample of Kuwaiti adolescents. The use of WC, alongside BMI, allowed for the identification of central adiposity and provided additional insight into obesity-related metabolic risk. Furthermore, the application of a standardised dietary assessment tool and a structured diet quality score enabled systematic evaluation of dietary intake across multiple components.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between dietary intake and anthropometric outcomes. Dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-h dietary recall, which captures short-term intake rather than habitual dietary behaviour and is subject to recall bias and potential underreporting. In addition, the use of UK-DRVs as a reference standard represents a key limitation, as these values were not developed for the Kuwaiti socio-cultural context and may not fully reflect local dietary practices or nutritional requirements. The lack of locally validated dietary reference standards constrained the interpretation of dietary adequacy and underscores the need for region-specific benchmarks. Although several demographic variables were included, the study did not collect detailed data on socio-economic status, household food environment, or broader environmental determinants of diet, which may influence adolescent dietary behaviours. As such, the findings should be interpreted primarily in terms of the distribution of nutritional risk rather than determinants of dietary intake. In addition, the very low prevalence of adherence to certain dietary recommendations (e.g., dietary fibre, fish, and fruit and vegetables) resulted in sparse data for some regression models. Consequently, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as they may be statistically unstable despite adjustment for potential confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights a high prevalence of overnutrition and widespread non-adherence to dietary reference recommendations among Kuwaiti adolescents, with central adiposity emerging as a key indicator of diet-related metabolic risk. By integrating dietary intake data with anthropometric measures, the study contributes valuable evidence to a limited body of adolescent nutrition research in Kuwait and the wider Gulf region.

While the use of UK-DRVs provided a pragmatic framework for dietary assessment in the absence of local standards, the findings emphasise the need for culturally relevant dietary reference values and validated nutritional assessment tools tailored to the Kuwaiti context. Strengthening adolescent nutrition surveillance and developing context-specific benchmarks will be essential for informing future public health strategies aimed at addressing the growing burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases in Kuwait.
