Reconstructing PTA measurements via early seeding of supermassive black holes
Sohan Ghodla, Cosmin Ilie

Abstract
The growing evidence of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) being present in the early Universe poses challenges to their traditional formation pathways. Separately, studies suggest that merging SMBH binaries with total masses ≳ 10 9 ​ M ⊙ \gtrsim 10^{9}M_{\odot} could be the primary sources of the nanohertz gravitational wave background detected by the Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTAs). Owing to their extreme masses, these SMBHs have a higher probability of forming earlier than their lower-mass counterparts. In this work, we provide a formalism to calculate the implications of early seeded SMBHs for the PTA signal. As an application, we explore the two most prominent scenarios of high- z z SMBHs seeding mechanisms: direct collapse black holes (DCBHs) and collapse of supermassive dark stars (SMDSs). We show that SMDS-seeded SMBHs, with comoving seed number density of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 − 3 ) ​ Mpc − 3 \mathcal{O}(10^{-3})\,{\rm Mpc}^{-3} can be the dominant contributor to the PTA signal, with mass > 10 9 ​ M ⊙ >10^{9}M_{\odot} binaries acquiring a peak merger rate at z < 1 z<1 . By contrast, SMBHs seeded via the DCBH channel may contribute only sub-dominantly unless produced with a much larger number density than implied by numerical simulations. Our analysis further suggests that the seed number density should be < 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 − 1 ) ​ Mpc − 3 <\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})\,{\rm Mpc}^{-3} in order to not over-predict the expected PTA signal.



I
Introduction

The global network of Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTAs) recently announced the detection of a stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) in the nano-Hertz frequency regime
(
Agazie et al. 2023a
;
Reardon et al. 2023
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
.
Although many sources have been considered (e.g.,
Ellis et al. 2023b
and reference therein), the conventional interpretation is that it primarily originated from the population of inspiraling supermassive black hole (SMBH) binaries distributed over the Universe.
The expected amplitude of the gravitational wave signal from such a population of SMBHs should depend on their masses and the rate at which these binaries inspiral into the PTA band throughout cosmic history. As most merging massive galaxies house an SMBH at their center
(
Saglia et al. 2016
)
, numerical studies employ specific recipes for the galaxy assembly and/or for the growth of SMBHs to generate such an SMBH population
(
Rajagopal & Romani 1995
;
Wyithe & Loeb 2003
;
Sesana et al. 2008
;
Rosado et al. 2015
;
Sesana 2013
;
Chen et al. 2019
;
Ellis et al. 2024a
;
Ellis et al. 2024b
;
Ellis et al. 2023a
;
Agazie et al. 2023a
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
)
.

The growing evidence of SMBHs being present in the early Universe
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Kovacs et al. 2024
)
poses challenges to the traditional means of SMBH formation (e.g.,
Volonteri & Bellovary 2012
;
Latif & Ferrara 2016
;
Volonteri et al. 2021
;
Tan et al. 2024
and references therein). Data suggests that at least a subset of present-day SMBHs were already very massive when the Universe was less than a billion years old. This includes candidates like UHZ1
(
Natarajan et al. 2023
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
)
, J
1342+0928

(
Bañados et al. 2017
)
, J
1120+0641

(
Mortlock et al. 2011
)
, J
0313-1806

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, GN
-1001830

(
Juodžbalis et al. 2024
)
among others.
Several SMBH seeding mechanisms have been put forward to explain these findings. Direct collapse black holes (DCBHs), formed through the collapse of pristine gas in atomically cooled halos (
M\gtrsim 10^{8}M_{\odot}
) at redshifts
z\approx 8-17

(
Eisenstein & Loeb 1995
;
Ferrara et al. 2014
;
Wise et al. 2019
;
Bhowmick et al. 2022
;
Latif et al. 2022
)
are a prominent example. Another possibility is that initially intermediate mass black holes formed in dense Pop III star clusters, which subsequently grew supermassive via rapid accretion of baryonic matter
(
Davies et al. 2011
;
Lupi et al. 2014
;
Schleicher et al. 2022
)
. More exotic mechanisms like the presence of massive primordial black holes
(
Kawasaki et al. 2012
;
Shinohara et al. 2023
;
Ziparo et al. 2024
;
Dayal 2024
)
and the gravothermal collapse of self-interacting dark matter halos
(
Feng et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2025
)
have also been postulated.
While these mechanisms could offer a solution to the presence of massive black holes in the early Universe, most of these seeding mechanisms may only account for a small fraction of the present-day SMBH population
(
Banik et al. 2019
;
Singh et al. 2023
)
. For instance, numerical simulations suggest that DCBHs could attain a comoving number density of only
n_{\rm BH}\approx 10^{-7}-10^{-6}
Mpc
-3

(
Habouzit et al. 2016
;
Wise et al. 2019
;
Latif et al. 2022
, e.g.)
which, as we discuss later, is
\mathcal{O}(10^{-4}-10^{-3})
times smaller than needed to reproduce the characteristics of the observed PTA data assuming that the latter primarily originates form an underlying population of SMBHs.

In addition, previous studies suggest that SMBH binaries with total mass
\gtrsim 10^{9}M_{\odot}
could be the dominant contributor to the observed PTA signal
(
Agazie et al. 2023a
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Ellis et al. 2023a
)
. Given their masses, these SMBHs are likelier to have assembled earlier than the less massive ones, and could be the descendants of first assembled SMBHs akin to those seen by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Chandra Observatory
(
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Kovacs et al. 2024
)
.
To quantify their contribution to the PTA signal, here we developed a formalism using which we calculate the SGWB resulting from the inspiral and merger of the descendants of these early seeded SMBHs, given their seed number density at some higher redshift. This enables us to estimate the abundance of such massive seeds that could make them a dominant contributor to the PTA signal.
As an application, in addition to the earlier discussed DCBH scenario, we explore an alternative mechanism of efficient early SMBH seeding, namely via the collapse of supermassive dark stars (SMDSs).

In the WIMP dark matter paradigm, dark stars are anticipated to form at the center of dark matter minihalos between
z\approx 10-30

(
Spolyar et al. 2008
;
Freese et al. 2008
)
. These stars are composed almost entirely of hydrogen and helium but powered primarily via heating from WIMPs annihilation rather than nuclear fusion (see
Freese et al. 2016
for a review). During their evolution, they remain cool - with surface temperature
T_{\rm eff}=\mathcal{O}(10^{4}\,K)
- and puffy with sizes
\mathcal{O}(10\,\text{AU})
. This allows them to efficiently accrete baryonic matter from their surroundings, allowing them to grow to masses
\mathcal{O}(10^{6}M_{\odot})
if a continuous gas supply could be maintained
(
Freese et al. 2010
)
. These stars could be as bright as early galaxies, and would be observable with JWST
(
Zackrisson et al. 2010
;
Ilie et al. 2012
)
or the upcoming Roman Space Telescope
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. In fact, recent JWST observations may be hinting at the presence of such objects in the early Universe
(
Ilie et al. 2023b
;
Ilie et al. 2025
)
. Unlike DCBHs, the conditions for forming SMDSs are less restrictive. The primary requirement is that the dark star hosting halo be sufficiently isolated to be undisturbed by chemical and radiative feedback from surrounding astrophysical sources
(
Banik et al. 2019
;
Singh et al. 2023
)
. Eventually, when the star runs out of dark matter fuel (or is dislodged from the center of its host halo during mergers), it will collapse, hence providing an efficient mechanism for SMBH seeding
(
Freese et al. 2010
;
Banik et al. 2019
;
Singh et al. 2023
;
Ilie et al. 2023a
)
with a seed number density much larger than DCBHs.

In this work, we begin with the assumption that potentially a fraction of present day SMBHs were seeded by collapse of SMDSs in the early Universe. We then calculate the expected SGWB resulting from the inspiral and merger of their descendants that could be observed on Earth, demonstrating that in principle they could
dominate
the PTA band in the low redshift Universe. Finally, using the pre-existing PTA data we derive upper limits on the number density of such seeds at high redshifts and hence an upper limit on the corresponding number density of DM halos able to SMDSs at
z\gtrsim 10
. Based on our analysis, we find that a comoving number density of
\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
Mpc
-3
could make SMBHs seeded by SMDSs a major contributor to PTA signal, and that a number density of
\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
Mpc
-3
would result in over-predicting the energy density of the observed SGWB. We contrast this to the case of DCBHs with their anticipated number density
\mathcal{O}(10^{-7}-10^{-6})
Mpc
-3

(
Habouzit et al. 2016
;
Wise et al. 2019
;
Latif et al. 2022
)
and show that the latter may only minimally contribute to the PTA signal.

II
Seeding probability and growth model

Fluctuations in the matter density field in the early Universe payed way for the formation of virialized dark matter halos. Here, we use the Extended Press-Schechter formalism
(
Press & Schechter 1974
;
Bond et al. 1991
;
Lacey & Cole 1994
)
to model the comoving number density
n(M,t)
of such dark matter halos with mass
M
at a given cosmic time
t
(more details in Appendix
A
).
Subsequently, a fraction of these halos could house SMBH seeds, of which seeds resulting from the collapse of SMDSs are our main interest. The formalism for calculating the SGWB spectrum resulting from the descendants SMBHs of SMDS and DCBH seeds is summarized in Fig.
1
where we assume that the underlying mechanism for constraining halo occupation and seed growth remains the same for both the seeding scenarios.

To relate the seed mass to its host halo, we assume that any halo in which a SMDSs can form has a mass
M
that can range from
10^{6}M_{\odot}-10^{8}M_{\odot}
, at the time the central Dark Star forms
(
Freese et al. 2008
;
Rindler-Daller et al. 2015
, e.g.)
. The exact mass of the resulting seed black hole would depend on the mass of the host halo
M
and is bounded from above by
f\times M
, with
f=0.15
, the latter being the fractional baryonic matter content of the halo. However, the halo might experience an early merger (with another halo) before the star could accrete most of the available baryonic matter. This could dislodge the star from the center
(
Ilie et al. 2023b
)
, hence losing its efficient dark matter supply. This will lead to its collapse, adding scatter to the seed‐masses. In this work, we assume that halo seeding happens between
z\approx 10-30
and that
z=10
marks the end of the seeding process, since SMDSs are unlikely to survive at lower redshifts
(
Ilie et al. 2012
)

II.1
Halo occupation probability

Before discussing the seed growth model, let us first calculate the probability of a halo housing an SMBH, seeded by the collapse of an SMDS. In this, we assume the usual that at most only one seed can form per halo. We assume that halo seeding happens between
z\approx 10-30
at the collapse of the SMDS and that
z=10
marks the end of the seeding process. At this point the number density of seeded halos, which is equivalent to the number density of black holes
n_{\rm BH}
should saturate and acquire a constant value. Owing to the variable initial halo seeding mass, seeding times and growth histories, these seed hosting halos at
z=10
should also carry a scatter in their masses.

We model the halo occupation probability via the cumulative distribution function:

p_{\rm{occ}}(z,M)=p_{\rm max}(z)\int_{M_{{\rm min}(z)}}^{M(z)}\mathcal{P}(M_{\rm low})dM_{\rm low},

(1)

which guarantees that
p_{\rm occ}
is a monotonically increasing function of the halo mass.
Above, a halo of mass
M
can only be seeded if
M\geq M_{\rm low}
, where
M_{\rm low}
is the
threshold
mass for a halo to host a seed at
z\leq 10
. We assume

to obeys a truncated (from below) log-normal probability density distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(M_{\rm low})=\frac{1}{M_{\rm low}\sigma_{H}\ln(10)\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\exp\left[-\delta(M_{\rm low})^{2}\right]}{\Bigl[1-\Phi\bigl(\sqrt{2}\delta_{\min}\bigr)\Bigr]};

(2)

\displaystyle\delta(M)=\frac{\log M-\log\mu_{H}}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{H}};\quad\delta_{\min}\equiv\delta(M_{\min})\,,

5

II.2
Accretion induced seed growth

Let us assume that the seed black hole grows in mass primarily via accretion at a rate
\dot{m}_{\rm acc}
. If
\epsilon
is the accretion efficiency then the rate at which mass is gained by the black hole is
\dot{m}=(1-\epsilon)\dot{m}_{\rm acc}
. We also know that
L=\epsilon\dot{m}_{\rm acc}c^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{t_{E}}mc^{2}
, with
\lambda=L/L_{\rm Edd}
. Thus we get

\displaystyle\dot{m}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}\frac{\lambda}{t_{E}}m\,,

(6)

where
t_{E}=\sigma_{T}c/4\pi Gm_{p}=4.5\times 10^{8}
yr is the Eddington time (
\sigma_{T},m_{p}
being the Thomson scattering cross-section and mass of proton respectively).
Integrating Eq. (
6
) then gives

m(M,z)=m(z_{\rm seed})e^{\alpha(M(t),z)}

(7)

with (note we use
m,M
for black hole and halo mass, respectively)

\alpha(M(t),z)=\int_{t_{\rm seed}}^{t}\frac{\lambda(M,z)(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon\,t_{\rm Edd}}dt\,,

(8)

where we take
\epsilon=0.1
. We assume that
\lambda
is a function of the halo mass,
M
and that
z
and takes the form:

\displaystyle\lambda(M,z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}(1+z)^{\beta}\left(\frac{m_{\rm seed}}{10^{4}M_{\odot}}\right)^{1/2}

(9)

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}\left(\frac{M(z)}{M_{*}(z)}\right)^{\frac{z\alpha_{1}}{z_{\rm seed}}},&{\rm if~}M(z)<M_{*}(z)\\
\left(\frac{M(z)}{M_{*}(z)}\right)^{\frac{z\alpha_{2}}{z_{\rm seed}}},&{\rm if~}M(z)>M_{*}(z)\end{cases},

where we adopt the following values for the fitting parameters:
\lambda_{0}=0.003,\beta=5/2,\alpha_{1}=1/2,\alpha_{2}=1/15
and
M_{*}=\frac{5\times 10^{11}M_{\odot}}{(1+z)^{3/2}}
. Assuming a broken power-law for
\lambda
in Eq. (
9
) allows us to change the black hole growth pattern once a threshold halo mass
M_{*}
is reached.
In the end, all these values are chosen such that the resulting black hole growth model remains in qualitative agreement with the empirically obtained black hole-halo mass relation at least at larger black hole masses and low
z
values, which is of primary relevance for our calculation of the SGWB spectrum.

For example, Fig.
4
shows the comparison between the empirical and the above accretion-induced growth model.
For this, we adopt the empirically obtained black-hole-stellar mass relation determined for inactive galaxies from Ref.
(
Girelli et al. 2020
)
which is also in fair agreement with the findings of the NANOGrav Collaboration
(
Agazie et al. 2023b
)
- e.g., see figure 7 in
Ellis et al. 2024a
. We then convert this to a black-hole-halo mass relation using
Reines & Volonteri 2015
.
One can immediately notice the deviation of the proposed relation from the empirical one at smaller SMBH masses. Indeed, at high redshifts, JWST observations suggest black holes tend to be more massive than that implied by the local data
(
Pacucci et al. 2023
)
. Separately, at lower redshifts, we expect the lower mass SMBH resulting from SMDSs to only account for a tiny subset of all low mass SMBHs, and thus the deviation could correspond to outliers in the empirical data.

III
Merger rate

III.1
Dark matter halo merger rate

Using the EPS formalism, one can estimate the comoving halo merger rate density
R_{h}
of mass
M_{1,2}
as
(M_{1}>M_{2})

\frac{d^{2}R_{h}(t)}{dM_{1}dM_{2}}=\frac{dn\left(M_{1},t\right)}{dM_{1}}\frac{dn\left(M_{2},t\right)}{dM_{2}}Q\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)\,,

(10)

where
Q\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)

is the halo merger rate kernel
(
Benson et al. 2005
)
and quantifies the rate at which halos with mass
M_{1}
merge with those with mass
M_{2}
at time
t
(see Appendix
B
for more information).

III.2
The central black hole merger rate

As previously discussed, only a fraction of dark matter halos might house a central black hole. Additionally, the merger of two halos might not result in the merger of their central black holes over some finite-time period. Let
p_{\rm occ}(m|M)dM
be the probability that a black hole with mass
m
occupies a halo with mass within the range
[M,M+dM]
and
p_{\rm merg}(m_{1},m_{2},\tau)
the probability that the merger of two halos with mass
M_{1},M_{2}
results in the merger of their central black holes with mass
m_{1},m_{2}
, with a delay time
\tau
. The resulting comoving black hole merger rate density
R_{\rm BH}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}R_{{\rm BH}}(t)}{dm_{1}dm_{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\iint dM_{1}dM_{2}\,p_{\rm merg}(m_{1},m_{2},t-t^{\prime})

(11)

\displaystyle\times p_{\rm occ}(m_{1}|M_{1},t^{\prime})p_{\rm occ}(m_{2}|M_{2},t^{\prime})\frac{d^{3}R_{h}(t^{\prime})}{{d}M_{1}{d}M_{2}dt^{\prime}}\,,

where
t_{i}
is the time when halos are first allowed to merge and
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
is the delay time between the merger of the halos and their central black holes. The integration over
t^{\prime}
ensures that we consider black holes that merge at time
t
due to all previous halo mergers and that over
M_{1},M_{2}
ensures that all possible combinations of halo masses that can house black holes with masses
m_{1},m_{2}
are considered.

For a given seed mass (
m_{\rm seed}
) the BH-halo mass relation provides a redshift dependent one to one mapping between
m
and
M
. Using this, and a change of variables, one can recast the central black hole merger rate density
R_{\rm BH}
as:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}R_{{\rm BH}}(t)}{dm_{1}dm_{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt^{\prime}p_{\rm{occ}}(m_{1},t^{\prime})p_{\rm{occ}}(m_{2},t^{\prime})

(12)

\displaystyle\times p_{{\rm merg}}(m_{1},m_{2},t-t^{\prime})\frac{dM_{1}}{dm_{1}}\frac{dM_{2}}{dm_{2}}\frac{d^{3}R_{h}(t^{\prime})}{dM_{1}dM_{2}dt^{\prime}}.

Above,
p_{{\rm merg}}(\tau)
remains a free parameter in our calculation and will depend on how efficiently the central black holes harden their orbit post-halo merger which is an unsettled issue (e.g., the final parsec problem,
Milosavljevic & Merritt 2003
). Studies suggest that the SMBH mergers can be swift, taking
\mathcal{O}(10)
Myr post galaxy merger in dense astrophysical environments
(
Khan et al. 2016
)
. Conversely, mergers could take multi giga-years when environmental effects are weak
(
Kelley et al. 2016
;
Tremmel et al. 2018
)
. On the other hand, a delay time
\mathcal{O}(10)
Gyr would also imply that SMBHs would seldom inspiral into the PTA band and thus may not be responsible for the detected PTA signal. As we make the assumption that the latter is not the case, here we sample
\tau
from a log-normal distribution with a mean merger time of
\tau_{0}=1\text{ Gyr},5
Gyr respectively and
\sigma_{\tau}=0.5
dex (see
Fang & Yang 2023
for a similar finding based on mock LISA dataset).
Eq. (
12
) is then integrated using Monte Carlo approach of importance sampling.

Fig.
5
demonstrates the merger rate of SMBHs resulting from such an analysis. One can see that the peak of the SMBH merger rate depends on the underlying masses of SMBH with more massive binaries experiencing a delayed merger peak. Moreover, this peak is shifted to further lower redshifts for larger values of mean merger delay time. We later show (see Section
IV
, Fig.
6
) that it is the binaries with
m_{1}+m_{2}\geq 10^{9}M_{\odot}
that produce the strongest PTA signal (cf.
Agazie et al. 2023a
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Ellis et al. 2023a
). As such one can conclude that most PTA featuring early seeded binaries should experience mergers at relatively lower values of
z
.

). The legend shows the total mass of the binary undergoing merger. The two lines styles represent the mean merger delay times.

IV
Gravitational wave background

So far, we started with a population of dark matter halos that were seeded at
z=10
with occupation probability given by Eq. (
3
). As the halos grew so did the central seeds where the mass evolution of the latter was computed using Eq (
7
). We then calculated the merger rate of these SMBHs as a function of
z
using Eq. (
12
). In this section we now calculate the SGWB that could result during their inspiral and merger which may ultimately be observed on Earth.

IV.1
Method

An observable SGWB results from the dynamics of a population of cosmological sources and hence should be unpolarized, stationary over the period of observation, and isotropically distributed over the sky. Thus, the characteristics of the SGWB primarily depend on its frequency spectrum.
A typical approach for quoting the gravitational wave energy density spectrum is by introducing the dimensionless parameter

\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}}\frac{d\rho_{\rm GW}}{d\ln f}

(13)

which characterizes the energy density
\rho_{\rm GW}
of the gravitational radiation contained in the logarithmic frequency bin
d\ln{f}
,
\rho_{c}=3H_{0}^{2}c^{2}/(8\pi G)
being the critical energy density. As we discuss in Appendix
C
, this yields

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}}\int_{0}^{z_{i}}dV(z)

\displaystyle\iint dm_{1}dm_{2}\frac{d^{2}R_{{\rm BH}}(z)}{dm_{1}dm_{2}}

(14)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\pi c^{2}f^{3}|\tilde{h}(f)|^{2}}{4G(1+z)}\,,

where
dV(z)
is the differential comoving volume element,
z_{i}=10
is redshift upto which mergers are considered and
|\tilde{h}(f)|
represents the optimal gravitational wave strain amplitude in the Fourier space. To compute
\Omega_{\rm GW}
, we need to determine the nature of
\tilde{h}(f)
for an inspiraling and merging binary. For this, we use the phenomenological waveforms provided in
Ajith et al. 2008
with further details relegated to Appendix
C.2
.

IV.2
Expected SGWB in the PTA band

The resulting
\Omega_{\rm GW}
for three different values of
\mu_{H}
(i.e. the mean of the threshold minimum seeded halo mass distribution at
z=10
, cf. Eq.
2
) is plotted in Fig.
6
. Here we also contrast the
\Omega_{\rm GW}
from DS seeded SMBHs to that from DCBHs. To calculate the
\Omega_{\rm GW}
resulting from DCBHs we keep everything the same as for the case of SMDSs and only vary the value of
n_{\rm BH}(z=10)
by setting it to
n_{\rm BH}=10^{-6}
Mpc
-3
, as suggested by simulations
(
Habouzit et al. 2016
;
Wise et al. 2019
;
Latif et al. 2022
)
. The low number density of DCBHs can be attributed to the conditions required for their formation which are quite rare
(
Natarajan et al. 2017
;
Habouzit et al. 2016
)
. This is because they require a very efficient star forming companion halo in
close
proximity, in order to destroy the
H_{2}
clouds inside the DCBH host halo
(
Begelman et al. 2006
;
Kiyuna et al. 2023
)
.
Owing to the low number density, consequently we find their contribution to the PTA signal to be minimal.

On the other hand, in the WIMP dark matter paradigm, SMDSs can form much more efficiently. For
n_{\rm BH}=\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
Mpc
-3
(e.g., Fig.
6
assumes
n_{\rm BH}=5\times 10^{-3}
Mpc
-3
based on
Singh et al. 2023
), their descendants could become the dominant contributor to the SGWB observed by PTA (see Fig.
6
). The latter depends on the masses of the halos hosting SMDSs-seeded SMBHs, with larger halo masses resulting in a stronger signal. This is because a larger halo mass provides a more favorable environment for the seed to grow to high masses. The same also holds for the case of DCBHs.
Additionally, we find that in the case of efficient seeding, SMBH binaries with total mass
>10^{9}M_{\odot}
could be the dominant contributors to the PTA band with binaries in mass range
10^{6}M_{\odot}<m_{1}+m_{2}<10^{9}M_{\odot}
playing a subdominant role. We note that the calculation in Fig.
6
considers gravitational radiation alone as the means of binary orbital hardening and thus follows the power law
\Omega_{\rm GW}\propto f^{2/3}
(
\tilde{h}\propto f^{-7/6}
), implying that most binaries are in their inspiral phase in the PTA band (cf. Appendix
C.2
). Although not considered here, including environmental interactions on the orbital evolution of the binary has been shown to produce an
\Omega_{\rm GW}
that is more aligned with PTA observations (e.g.,
Ellis et al. 2024a
;
Raidal et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
).

IV.3
Upper bound on seed number density

In our analysis, we kept
n_{\rm BH}(z)
constant under the anticipation that due to infrequency SMBH mergers its value would be impacted by
<10\%
to the present day (see Section
II.1
). This assumption allows us to pull
n_{\rm BH}
out of the integral in Eq. (
14
), yielding
\Omega_{\rm GW}\propto n_{\rm BH}^{2}
. Thus having calculated the form of
\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)
in Fig.
6
for
n_{\rm BH}=5\times 10^{-3}
Mpc
-3
we can readily estimate how a change in
n_{\rm BH}
would impact
\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)
. As an application, we use this to put upper limit on the number density of SMDSs (or any other efficient SMBH seeders) in the early Universe. Within the context of our analysis, for our fiducial case of
\mu_{H}=5\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}
, we anticipate that for SMDSs,
n_{\rm BH}>\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
Mpc
-3
is unlikely, as that could result in inconsistency with the PTA observations.

V
Conclusion

In this work we calculated the expected SGWB resulting from the inspiral and merger of the descendants of early (i.e.
z\gtrsim 10
) produced SMBHs. Depending on the masses of the halos in which these seeds were housed, we showed that these SMBHs could dominate the PTA band in the low redshift Universe, if produced with the number density of
n_{\rm BH}=\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
Mpc
-3
. Remarkably, this value for
n_{\rm BH}
is expected for SMBHs seeded by Dark Stars (a.k.a. Pop III.1 stars), as shown by
Singh et al. 2023
. Thus, in the WIMP dark matter paradigm, it is possible that a large part of the PTA signal is due to inspiral of SMBHs that are descendants of collapsed SMDSs. We also contrasted this seeding mechanism to Direct Collapse Black Holes (DCBHs) and find that DCBHs, in view of their rarity, may not contribute to any significant fraction of the PTA signal.
We also demonstrate that the observed PTA data sets an upper limit on the number density of high-
z
(i.e.
z\gtrsim 10
) seeded SMBHs to:
n_{\rm BH}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
Mpc
-3
. While the
apriori
expected values for
n_{\rm BH}
for the two mechanisms considered are well below this limit, i.e.
\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
Mpc
-3
for collapsed SMDSs and
\mathcal{O}(10^{-7}-10^{-6})
Mpc
-3
for DCBHs, our work provides means to observationally set an upper bound on their comoving number density.

Appendix A
Abundance of dark matter halos

A.1
Formation of dark matter halos

The matter in the early universe was uniformly distributed, albeit with small density fluctuations seeded during cosmic inflation are able to grow, and eventually collapse and form dark matter micro-halos. Let

\delta(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}{\bar{\rho}}-1

(A1)

be the overdensity field representing perturbations in the matter density
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
in comoving coordinates w.r.t. the mean matter density
\bar{\rho}
at some time
t
. In the linear regime, the overdenity field evolves as
\delta(\mathbf{x},t)=\delta_{0}(\mathbf{x})D(t)
, where
\delta_{0}(\mathbf{x})
is the
linear
extrapolation of
\delta(\mathbf{x},t)
to the present time
t_{0}
and
D(t)
is the linear growth function such that
D(t_{0})=1
. Then, it is the regions with
\delta(\mathbf{x},t_{0})\equiv\delta_{\rm crit}(t_{0})\gtrsim 1.686
that are able to collapse to form virialized halos implying that
\delta_{\rm crit}(t)=\delta_{\rm crit}(t_{0})/D(t)

(
Peebles 1993
;
Barkana & Loeb 2001
)
. Once formed, the subsequent evolution of such halos is then governed by accretion from their surroundings and hierarchical mergers.

A.2
The halo mass function

The comoving number density
n
of such dark matter halos with mass
M
at time
t
can be estimated by the halo mass function and takes the form

\frac{dn(M,t)}{dM}=\frac{\bar{\rho}_{0}}{M^{2}}f(\nu)\left|\frac{d\ln\nu}{d\ln M}\right|,

(A2)

where
\bar{\rho}_{0}
is mean matter density linearly extrapolated to
t_{0}
,
\sigma(M)
is the standard deviation of the matter overdensity field within a spherical volume containing mass
M
on average (again extrapolated to
t_{0}
) and
\nu=\delta_{\rm crit}(t)/\sigma(M)
is the number of standard deviations contained in
\delta_{\rm crit}(t)
on mass scale
M
.
Above,
\sigma(M)
takes the functional form
Eisenstein & Hu 1999
:

\sigma^{2}(M)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi^{2}}k^{2}P_{0}(k)\tilde{W}^{2}(kR)\,,

(A3)

where
M=4\pi\bar{\rho}_{0}R^{3}/3
,
R
is the radius of the sphere containing mass
M
and

\tilde{W}(kR)=\frac{3}{(kR)^{3}}(\sin kR-kR\cos kR)

(A4)

is the Fourier transform of the top hat window function such that in the position space

W(r)\propto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }r\leq R\,,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\,.\end{array}\right.

(A5)

Moreover, the primordial power spectrum extrapolated to
t_{0}
can be calculated as

P_{0}(k)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}\delta_{\rm crit}^{2}\left(\frac{ck}{H_{0}}\right)^{3+n_{s}}T_{0}^{2}(k)\,,

(A6)

where
n_{s}
is the initial power spectrum index and
T_{0}
is the transfer function, which we base on the fitting
function of
Eisenstein & Hu 1999
. Here, we adopt a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
n_{s}=0.9649
, the Hubble’s constant
H_{0}=100h
kms
-1
Mpc
-1
with
h=0.674
and the present day density parameters
\Omega_{M,0}=0.315
,
\Omega_{\Lambda,0}=1-\Omega_{M,0},\Omega_{b}h^{2}=0.0024
, where
\Omega_{b}
represents the baryon density
(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
. We set the matter fluctuation amplitude in a sphere of radius
8h^{-1}
Mpc to be
\sigma_{8}=0.811

(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
.

Thus, from Eq. (
A2
), we see that making a choice for the multiplicity function
f(\nu)
allows us to estimate the mass function of dark matter halos at a given time. Assuming spherical collapse of the density perturbations, here we consider
f(\nu)
to take the extended Press–Schechter (EPS) form
Press & Schechter 1974
;
Bond et al. 1991
;
Lacey & Cole 1994
, yielding

f(\nu)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\nu e^{-\nu^{2}/2}\,.

(A7)

Appendix B
Dark matter halo merger rate

Using the EPS formalism one can also calculate the probability
p
for halos of mass
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
(with
M_{1}>M_{2}
) to merge, thus forming a halo of mass
M_{f}=M_{1}+M_{2}
as
(
Lacey & Cole 1994
;
Benson et al. 2005
)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}p\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)}{dtdM_{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left|\frac{\dot{\delta}_{\rm crit}}{\delta_{\rm crit}}\right|\left|\frac{d\ln\sigma}{dM_{f}}\right|\left[1-\frac{\sigma^{2}\left(M_{f}\right)}{\sigma^{2}\left(M_{1}\right)}\right]^{-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\delta_{\rm crit}(t)}{\sigma\left(M_{f}\right)}\exp\left[-\frac{\delta_{\rm crit}(t)^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}\left(M_{f}\right)}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}\left(M_{1}\right)}\right)\right].

(B1)

where the dot represents derivative w.r.t.
t
. Thus, we can estimate the comoving halo merger rate density
R_{h}
as

\frac{d^{2}R_{h}(t)}{dM_{1}dM_{2}}=\frac{dn\left(M_{1},t\right)}{dM_{1}}\frac{dn\left(M_{2},t\right)}{dM_{2}}Q\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)\,,

(B2)

where

Q\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)\equiv\frac{d^{2}p\left(M_{1},M_{2},t\right)}{dtdM_{2}}\left[\frac{dn\left(M_{2},t\right)}{dM_{2}}\right]^{-1}

(B3)

is the halo merger rate kernel
(
Benson et al. 2005
)
that quantifies the rate at which halos with mass
M_{1}
merge with those with mass
M_{2}
at time
t
.

Appendix C
Stochastic gravitational wave background

In the following, for simplicity, we assume that the eccentricity of the binary is zero. Thus the source frame frequency
f_{r}
of the emitted gravitational wave is related to the binary’s orbital frequency as
f_{r}=2f_{\rm orb}
.
Additionally, the net orbital energy loss rate from the binary can be estimated as

\dot{E}=\dot{E}_{\rm GW}+\dot{E}_{\rm env}\,,

(C1)

where the first term corresponds to energy removed by gravitational waves and the second term accounts for orbital hardening due to environmental effects such as dynamical friction between the black holes and their surrounding matter or due to the occurrence of close three-body encounters.
Here, we also disregard the impact of
\dot{E}_{\rm env}
.

C.1
Energy density spectrum

At a fixed observer location
{x}_{0}
, the stochastic gravitational plane wave (in transverse traceless gauge) can be expanded into its Fourier components as

h_{ab}(t)=\sum_{A=+,\times}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\iint d^{2}\hat{\Omega}\,\tilde{h}_{A}(f,\hat{\Omega})e^{2\pi ift}e_{ab}^{A}(\hat{\Omega})\,,

(C2)

where
\hat{\Omega}
points in the direction of the propagation of the wave with
d^{2}\hat{\Omega}=d\cos{\theta}d\phi
being the differential surface area element,
f
is the frequency of the wave in the observer frame,
A
represents the two polarization of the wave and
e^{A}_{ab}
is the polarization tensor. The energy density of the gravitational wave averaged over many wavelengths can be written using the Isaacson formula as
(
Isaacson 1968
;
Phinney 2001
)

\rho_{\rm GW}=\frac{c^{2}}{32\pi G}\left\langle\dot{h}_{ab}\dot{h}^{ab}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi c^{2}f^{2}|\tilde{h}(f)|^{2}}{4G}df\,,

(C3)

where
|\tilde{h}(f)|
represents the optimal gravitational wave strain amplitude in the Fourier space.
Alternatively, for a source located at a fixed distance, we can also state
\rho_{\rm GW}
in terms of the energy received in gravitational radiation as

\rho_{\rm GW}=\frac{(1+z)}{4\pi D^{2}_{L}c}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dE_{\rm GW}}{df_{r}}df_{r}\,,

(C4)

where
E_{\rm GW}
is the total energy emitted in the source frame,
D_{L}
is the luminosity distance to the source and
f_{r}
is the frequency of the radiation in the source frame. Comparing Eq. (
C3
) and (
C4
), we find

\frac{dE_{\rm GW}}{df_{r}}=\frac{\pi^{2}c^{3}D^{2}_{L}f^{2}|\tilde{h}(f)|^{2}}{(1+z)^{2}G}\,,

(C5)

where we have used the relation
f_{r}=f(1+z)
. Thus, for an SGWB resulting from the inspiral of SMBH binaries over a cosmological scale, we can estimate the observed energy density by integrating

\displaystyle d\rho_{\rm GW}=\int_{0}^{z_{i}}dV(z)\iint dm_{1}dm_{2}\frac{d^{2}R_{{\rm BH}}(z)}{dm_{1}dm_{2}}\frac{(1+z)}{4\pi D^{2}_{L}c}\frac{dE_{\rm GW}}{df_{r}}df\,,

(C6)

where we set
z_{i}
to correspond to the time
t_{i}
in Eq. (
11
). The above equation calculates the energy radiated by a population of merging black hole binaries within a frequency interval
df
, located inside a comoving volume
dV(z)
at some distance
D_{L}(z)
. Integrating this over
z
then gives us the total energy density received on earth per unit time.
Additionally, we can recast the volume element as

dV(z)=\frac{4\pi c}{H_{0}}\frac{D_{c}^{2}(z)}{E(z)}dz\,,

(C7)

where
E(z)=\sqrt{\Omega_{M}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}
and

D_{c}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{E\left(z^{\prime}\right)}

(C8)

is the comoving distance such that
D_{L}=(1+z)D_{c}
. We note that although
R_{\rm BH}
was originally defined as the rate of binary SMBH merger but we can equally use it to model the rate at which the SMBHs inspiral into a given frequency band. Thus, this allows us to calculate not just the energy radiated during the merger but also during the inspiral phase.

A typical approach to quoting the gravitational wave energy density spectrum is by introducing the dimensionless parameter

\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}}\frac{d\rho_{\rm GW}}{d\ln f}

(C9)

which characterizes the energy density of the gravitational radiation contained in the logarithmic frequency bin
d\ln{f}
. To this end, using Eq. (
C5
) and (
C6
), we find that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}}\int_{0}^{z_{i}}dV(z)

\displaystyle\iint dm_{1}dm_{2}\frac{d^{2}R_{{\rm BH}}(z)}{dm_{1}dm_{2}}\frac{\pi c^{2}f^{3}|\tilde{h}(f)|^{2}}{4G(1+z)}\,.

(C10)

C.2
Phenomenological waveforms

To compute
\Omega_{\rm GW}
, we need to determine the nature of
\tilde{h}(f)
for an inspiraling and merging binary. For this, we use the phenomenological waveforms provided in
Ajith et al. 2008
. Depending on the stage of evolution of the binary,
\tilde{h}(f)
can be divided into three parts

\tilde{h}(f)=C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(f/f_{\text{merg }}\right)^{-7/6}&\text{ if }f<f_{\text{merg }}\,,\\
\left(f/f_{\text{merg }}\right)^{-2/3}&\text{ if }f_{\text{merg }}\leq f<f_{\text{ring }}\,,\\
w\mathcal{L}\left(f,f_{\text{ring }},\sigma\right)&\text{ if }f_{\text{ring }}\,,\leq f<f_{\text{cut }}\,.\end{array}\right.

(C11)

Here,
f_{\rm merg}
is the observed frequency at which the power law changes from
f^{-7/6}
to
f^{-2/3}
where the former corresponds to the inspiral and the latter to the ringdown stage and
f_{\rm cut}
is the cutoff frequency of the template. Moreover,

\mathcal{L}\left(f,f_{\text{ring }},\sigma\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)\frac{\sigma}{\left(f-f_{\text{ring }}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}/4}\,;

(C12a)

w=\frac{\pi\sigma}{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ring }}}{f_{\text{merg }}}\right)^{-2/3};

(C12b)

C=\frac{[(1+z)GMc^{-3}]^{5/6}f_{{\rm merg}}^{-7/6}}{\pi^{2/3}D_{L}/c}\left(\frac{5\eta}{24}\right)^{1/2},

(C12c)

where
M=m_{1}+m_{2}
is the total mass of the binary in the source frame and
\eta=m_{1}m_{2}/M^{2}
. The transition frequencies take the form

f_{i}=\frac{a_{i}\eta^{2}+b_{i}\eta+c_{i}}{\pi(1+z)GMc^{-3}};\quad i\in[1,3],

(C13)

where
f_{i}
is to be identified with
f_{\rm merg},f_{\rm ring}
, and
f_{\rm cut}
. The values of
\sigma
and other coefficients are given in Table 1 of
Ajith et al. 2008
. All relevant parameters are converted from geometric to SI units.

Appendix D
Halo mass evolution

Here, we are interested in calculating the growth of halo mass as a function of
z
. Following the median halo growth rate prescription of
Correa et al. 2015
, for
z<z_{i}
, where
z_{i}
is the initial redshift at which the halos has a mass
M(z_{i})
, we write

M(z)=M(z_{i})\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{\beta(z-z_{i})}\,,

(D1)

where the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are related to the power spectrum by

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f(M_{0}),

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1.686(2/\pi)^{1/2}\frac{dD}{dz}|_{z=0}+1\right]f(M_{0}),

\displaystyle f(M_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[S(M_{0}/q)-S(M_{0})]^{-1/2},

\displaystyle S(M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P(k)\tilde{W}^{2}(k;R)k^{2}dk\,.

Above,
D
is the linear growth factor,
