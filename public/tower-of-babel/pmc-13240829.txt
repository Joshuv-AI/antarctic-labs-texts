Protein–polysaccharide conjugation in food systems: Design principles, material and processing constraints, and functional outcomes
Authors: Naeem Ullah, Maduni Jayahansi Paththuwe Arachchi, Gafar Babatunde Bamigbade, Abdelmoneim Ali, Chaminda Senaka Ranadheera, Afaf Kamal-Eldin, Benu Adhikari, Mutamed Ayyash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein–polysaccharide conjugation is increasingly used to improve protein functionality in food systems, yet reported outcomes remain inconsistent because performance depends on protein conformation, polysaccharide structure, reaction pathway, and processing severity. This review critically examines Maillard-type glycation, enzymatic coupling, chemical cross-linking, and process-intensified approaches from a process–structure–function perspective. Emphasis is placed on measurable indicators that distinguish useful modification from over-modification, including degree of grafting, free amino group loss, browning development, particle size, ζ-potential, solubility, interfacial behavior, digestibility, and advanced glycation products. Moderate conjugation can enhance hydration, solubility, emulsion stability, bioactive protection, film formation, and printability, whereas excessive modification may promote aggregation, browning, lysine blockage, impaired digestion, and sensory deterioration. The review proposes application-specific design considerations and highlights real-food constraints, scalability barriers, and safety issues that must be addressed to enable predictable and industrially relevant use of protein–polysaccharide conjugates.

Introduction

Several comprehensive reviews during the past decade have examined the chemistry and functionality of protein–polysaccharide conjugates, with particular emphasis on Maillard-type glycation, enzymatic modification, and emerging processing aids aimed at improving solubility, emulsification, and thermal stability in food systems (Sedaghat Doost et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). Subsequent works have expanded this focus to include advances in plant protein modification, nature-inspired conjugation approaches, and growing interest in green, scalable processing methods for food and biopolymer applications (Li et al., 2025; Naik et al., 2022). In parallel, studies have explored the role of protein–polysaccharide interactions and conjugates in stabilizing emulsions and delivery systems, often employing model proteins and simplified formulations to demonstrate improvements in interfacial and bioactive performance (Gentile, 2020; Kan et al., 2021). More recent contributions have introduced rational design concepts and advanced delivery structures, particularly for encapsulating and protecting sensitive bioactive compounds in food matrices (Boostani et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2012). Collectively, this body of work provides a solid foundation for understanding current conjugation practices and their functional outcomes, while also highlighting the diversity of approaches—and the continuing need for clearer design rules and application boundaries in food systems.

Food proteins are widely used as structuring and surface-active ingredients because they contribute to solubility, emulsification, foaming, gelation, texture, and rheological performance in complex food matrices (Kusio et al., 2020; Małecki et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023). However, their functionality is often compromised during food processing by heat, pH shifts, ionic strength, shear, proteolysis, and interactions with other matrix components, leading to denaturation, aggregation, precipitation, or loss of interfacial activity (Gimenes et al., 2021; Kazlauskas, 2018; Salazar-Villanea et al., 2016). These limitations have encouraged the use of polysaccharides as modification partners because their molecular weight, charge density, chain flexibility, and hydration capacity can modulate protein stability, interfacial behavior, and resistance to environmental stresses (Liu et al., 2018; Paliya et al., 2023).

Protein–polysaccharide conjugation, particularly through Maillard-type glycation, offers a route to covalently attach hydrophilic carbohydrate chains to protein surfaces, thereby altering molecular size, surface charge, hydration, steric stabilization, and aggregation behavior (Kan et al., 2021; Nooshkam et al., 2020; Oliver, 2011). These structural changes can improve solubility, heat stability, antioxidant activity, emulsification, foaming, gelation, and bioactive protection, as shown in systems such as glycated ovalbumin, whey proteins, casein, and plant proteins (Gu et al., 2010; Huang et al., 2012; Jiang & Brodkorb, 2012; Naik et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2006). Nevertheless, functional improvement is not an automatic consequence of conjugation. Reaction conditions such as temperature, time, pH, water activity, protein-to-polysaccharide ratio, and carbohydrate structure determine whether the system remains within a useful modification range or progresses toward excessive browning, aggregation, lysine blockage, altered digestibility, or sensory deterioration (Kan et al., 2021; Kutzli et al., 2021; Nooshkam et al., 2020; Oliver, 2011).

The biological implications of conjugation also require careful interpretation. Several studies have reported reduced antigenicity after polysaccharide attachment, including soy protein–chitosan and whey protein–maltose systems (Li et al., 2011; Usui et al., 2004). This is relevant because proteins such as soy β-conglycinin and bovine β-lactoglobulin are recognized food allergens (Krishnan et al., 2009; Sélo et al., 1999). However, reduced antigenicity is not universal and depends on the protein, conjugation pathway, reaction intensity, and formation of new glycation-derived structures. Therefore, allergenicity, digestibility, nutrient retention, and advanced glycation product formation should be considered alongside techno-functional performance when evaluating the suitability of protein–polysaccharide conjugates for food applications.

Accordingly, the novelty of this review lies in shifting the discussion from a descriptive account of protein–polysaccharide conjugation toward an application-oriented framework for selecting, controlling, and evaluating conjugation strategies in food systems. While previous reviews have summarized reaction mechanisms, functional properties, or delivery applications, the present review specifically focuses on how processing route, reaction severity, protein conformation, and polysaccharide characteristics jointly determine whether conjugation improves functionality or causes failure. Particular emphasis is placed on measurable functional-window indicators, including degree of grafting, loss of free amino groups, browning development, particle-size growth, ζ-potential, solubility after stress treatment, interfacial performance, digestibility, and advanced glycation end-product formation. By integrating these indicators with method-selection guidance and real-food constraints, this review aims to provide practical design rules for researchers and food manufacturers seeking predictable, scalable, and safe use of protein–polysaccharide conjugates. This review was prepared through a targeted literature search using Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar. The search focused primarily on publications from 2020 to 2026, while earlier foundational studies were included when they provided essential mechanistic or methodological context. Search terms included combinations of “protein–polysaccharide conjugates,” “Maillard glycation,” “protein glycation,” “enzymatic conjugation,” “transglutaminase,” “laccase,” “protein–polysaccharide complexes,” “food emulsions,” “bioactive delivery,” “3D food printing,” “edible films,” “Pickering emulsions,” “digestibility,” “advanced glycation end-products,” and “industrial scalability.” Studies were included when they provided information on synthesis conditions, structural characterization, functional properties, food applications, or processing limitations of protein–polysaccharide conjugates or closely related protein–polysaccharide complexes. Priority was given to studies reporting measurable structural or functional outcomes such as degree of grafting, free amino group loss, browning intensity, particle size, ζ-potential, solubility, emulsion stability, rheological behavior, encapsulation efficiency, bioaccessibility, digestibility, or storage stability. Studies dealing only with unrelated protein modification, non-food biomedical systems, or polysaccharide-only formulations were excluded unless they provided transferable design principles relevant to food systems. Because this is a critical narrative review rather than a systematic meta-analysis, the literature was organized according to synthesis route, structural determinant, functional outcome, application type, and translation barrier.

Methods for synthesis of polysaccharide-protein conjugation

Protein–polysaccharide conjugation is typically achieved through non-enzymatic, enzymatic, or chemical routes, each offering distinct levels of control over reaction selectivity, structural modification, and functional outcomes. Among these approaches, non-enzymatic browning—most notably via Amadori-type reactions within the Maillard reaction pathway—has been widely reported as a practical route for modifying dietary proteins (Siddiquy, JiaoJiao, et al., 2024). Because this process does not require added chemicals or enzymes, it has been shown to improve physicochemical properties such as solubility, thermal stability, and antioxidant activity under food-relevant conditions (Kan et al., 2021; Nooshkam et al., 2020). However, the functional performance of the resulting conjugates depends more on precise control of reaction conditions than on the synthesis route alone. From a design standpoint, conjugation methods should not be seen as interchangeable options, but as processes that must be carefully selected and controlled to achieve specific outcomes. Factors such as the reaction environment, accessibility of reactive amino groups, and the balance between targeted modification and structural destabilization ultimately govern whether conjugation leads to functional enhancement or unintended losses. These considerations are discussed below in the context of the major synthesis routes.

Maillard reaction as a controllable conjugation route

The Maillard reaction, first described by Louis-Camille Maillard in 1912, is a non-enzymatic process involving the reaction between the carbonyl groups of reducing sugars and the free amino groups in proteins (El Hosry et al., 2025). In protein–polysaccharide systems, glycation typically occurs during the early Amadori rearrangement stage of the Maillard reaction (Uceda et al., 2024). The efficiency and outcome of this reaction are highly sensitive to processing variables such as temperature, time, pH, aw, the intrinsic structure of the protein, and the type of carbohydrate involved (Chen et al., 2020; El Hosry et al., 2025). The Maillard reaction is regarded as a suitable approach for food applications because it proceeds under relatively mild conditions without the need for added chemicals or enzymes. It has been reported to enhance protein solubility, foaming capacity, thermal stability, and antioxidant activity (Kan et al., 2021; Kutzli et al., 2021; Naik et al., 2022; Nooshkam et al., 2020). Both dry- and wet-heating approaches have been used, as demonstrated in mung bean protein isolate–peach gum, and spirulina protein-maltodextrin systems (Siddiquy, Ghamry, et al., 2024; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2023). However, from a functional standpoint, the Maillard reaction operates within a limited window: moderate glycation can enhance performance, but excessive reaction intensity may lead to undesirable browning and protein aggregation.

Plant-derived proteins should not be generalized as uniformly having lower Maillard reactivity than animal proteins, because their reactivity depends on protein type, extraction history, aggregation state, solubility, amino acid composition, lysine accessibility, surface hydrophobicity, and conformational flexibility (Kutzli et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025; Naik et al., 2022). For example, compact or aggregated plant protein structures may restrict the exposure of reactive amino groups and reduce glycation efficiency, whereas partial unfolding, improved dispersion, or increased molecular mobility can enhance access to glycation sites (Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, plant proteins should be evaluated using substrate-specific structural indicators rather than treated as a single category. Pretreatments and assisted technologies such as ultrasound, microwave heating, high-pressure processing, pulsed electric fields, electrospinning, and extrusion can promote protein unfolding, expose buried amino groups, and accelerate conjugation; however, excessive energy input may also cause irreversible aggregation, excessive browning, loss of solubility, or reduced techno-functional performance (Chen et al., 2024; Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2019). Thus, assisted processing should be optimized according to the structural state of each protein substrate and monitored using indicators such as free amino group loss, degree of grafting, browning intensity, particle size, solubility, and aggregation behavior.

Process-intensified and physical-field-assisted conjugation technologies

Physical-field-assisted technologies have been increasingly explored to accelerate protein–polysaccharide conjugation and overcome the long reaction times required by conventional dry- and wet-heating methods. These approaches are particularly relevant when the target is to increase glycation efficiency under milder thermal conditions or to improve functional properties without extensive heat exposure. Ultrasound is among the most frequently studied methods because cavitation can induce partial protein unfolding, expose buried amino groups, increase molecular collision frequency, and enhance mass transfer during glycation. For example, ultrasound-assisted glycation of ovalbumin with xylose decreased free amino groups and improved solubility, rheological behavior, and foaming properties, indicating that controlled conformational loosening can support functional enhancement. However, ultrasound must be optimized carefully because excessive intensity or duration can promote oxidation, fragmentation, aggregation, or excessive surface hydrophobicity (Chen et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2019).

Microwave-assisted conjugation offers rapid volumetric heating and can shorten reaction time compared with conventional heating. Its main advantage is fast energy transfer, but non-uniform heating and local overheating may accelerate browning or produce heterogeneous conjugates. High-pressure processing can modify protein conformation without severe thermal treatment, making it useful for substrates that require partial unfolding while limiting heat-induced denaturation (Zhang, Liu, et al., 2023). Nevertheless, pressure-induced structural changes are protein-specific and may either improve conjugation or produce compact aggregates with reduced reactivity. Pulsed electric fields and extrusion-based methods provide additional opportunities for continuous or semi-continuous processing. Extrusion is particularly attractive for industrial translation because it combines mixing, shear, and thermal input in a short residence time; however, its high solid content and shear intensity can complicate control of molecular structure and product uniformity.

From a design perspective, assisted technologies should not be viewed only as reaction accelerators. They should be selected according to the required structural change in the protein substrate. Ultrasound and pressure are useful when limited unfolding is needed to expose reactive groups; microwave heating is useful when rapid heating is desirable; extrusion is more relevant when continuous processing and low-moisture systems are targeted. In all cases, successful process intensification requires monitoring of free amino group loss, degree of grafting, browning intensity, particle size, solubility, and aggregation markers to ensure that acceleration does not push the system beyond the useful modification window.

Enzymatic conjugation: Specificity and accessibility constraints

Enzymatic conjugation provides a more selective and controlled alternative to non-enzymatic methods, with transglutaminase, laccase, and peroxidase being among the most widely used enzymes (Wu et al., 2022). Of these, transglutaminase (EC 2.3.2.13) catalyzes acyl-transfer reactions between the γ-carboxamide group of glutamine residues and primary amines, such as those found in lysine, forming ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine isopeptide bonds (Wu et al., 2022). This mechanism can facilitate covalent attachment between proteins and amino-containing polysaccharides, such as chitosan or polysaccharides that have been chemically aminated. However, not all polysaccharides are suitable direct substrates for transglutaminase-mediated conjugation. For example, polysaccharides with negligible primary amino groups, such as gum Arabic, are unlikely to participate in direct transglutaminase-mediated covalent coupling unless they contain reactive proteinaceous fractions, are chemically modified, or interact through an indirect non-covalent or mixed mechanism. Therefore, enzymatic conjugation should be discussed with clear distinction between true covalent grafting, protein–protein cross-linking in the presence of polysaccharides, and electrostatic complexation (Hu & Liu, 2023; Zhang, Holden, et al., 2023).

The effectiveness of enzymatic conjugation is influenced by the molecular conformation of the protein and the accessibility of reactive residues (Velazquez-Dominguez et al., 2023). Proteins with favorable spatial orientation of target amino acids tend to show higher reaction efficiency, whereas steric hindrance and limited enzyme affinity can impede conjugation (Niu et al., 2025; Velazquez-Dominguez et al., 2023). As a result, enzymatic approaches offer high specificity but are constrained by narrower substrate compatibility and structural requirements compared to non-enzymatic methods. From a design perspective, this makes enzymatic conjugation well-suited to applications requiring precise cross-linking, particularly under ambient or mild thermal conditions, but less adaptable for broad or heterogeneous protein modification. The designability of enzymatic conjugation is therefore narrower than that of Maillard-type glycation. Enzymes require accessible target residues, compatible substrate conformations, and reaction conditions that preserve enzyme activity. This limitation becomes especially important for complex plant-protein systems, where extraction-induced aggregation, low solubility, disulfide-linked networks, phenolic binding, or heterogeneous protein fractions can restrict enzyme access to glutamine, lysine, tyrosine, or other target residues. In such systems, poor enzymatic efficiency does not necessarily reflect enzyme failure, but rather limited substrate accessibility. Mild unfolding pretreatments may improve enzymatic modification, but excessive unfolding can induce aggregation and reduce processability. Thus, enzymatic conjugation is best suited to substrates with known reactive residues and controllable conformational states, rather than to poorly characterized protein mixtures.

Chemical and enzyme-mediated crosslinking: Functional benefits and limitations

Chemical crosslinking and enzyme-mediated oxidative crosslinking have also been used to modify protein systems and enhance food texture and stability (Ahmed et al., 2021; Sulaiman et al., 2022). These approaches primarily rely on the formation of covalent bonds that reinforce protein networks, although concurrent changes in pH and electrostatic interactions may also contribute to the observed functional improvements (Velazquez-Dominguez et al., 2023). Transglutaminase, a widely used enzyme in food processing, is valued for its strong cross-linking capacity and commercial availability (Miwa, 2020; Zheng et al., 2025). Its application has been shown to enhance the resilience of protein-based emulsions and support the formation of slowly solidifying gels (Tekle, 2025; Velazquez-Dominguez et al., 2023).

However, enzymatic crosslinking is limited by the specificity of enzymes toward certain amino acid residues, which restricts the diversity of potential cross-linking sites (Ahmed et al., 2021). To overcome this limitation, oxidative enzymes such as laccases, peroxidases, and tyrosinases have been investigated for their broader substrate selectivity and distinct reaction mechanisms (Ahmed et al., 2021; Li, Wu, et al., 2020). In these systems, enzymes typically generate reactive intermediates that initiate spontaneous polymerization, rather than directly forming protein–protein bonds, resulting in covalent cross-link formation (Fig. 2) (Li, Wu, et al., 2020). While these reactions can effectively modulate texture, they require careful control to avoid excessive network formation, which may compromise solubility and processability. Overall, no single conjugation route can be considered universally superior. Maillard-type conjugation is attractive for food systems because it avoids non-food chemical cross-linkers and can improve interfacial and delivery performance, but it requires strict control of browning, lysine loss, aggregation, and advanced Maillard products. Enzymatic approaches offer higher reaction selectivity and milder processing conditions, but their effectiveness depends on substrate accessibility and the availability of suitable reactive groups. Chemical cross-linking provides strong covalent stabilization but is less attractive for clean-label food applications unless the reagents and residuals are acceptable from regulatory and consumer perspectives. Therefore, method selection should be guided by the target function, the required degree of structural modification, the sensitivity of the protein substrate, and the processing constraints of the intended food matrix. Structure–function considerations in protein–polysaccharide conjugation

Key factors governing conjugation effectiveness

The effectiveness of protein–polysaccharide conjugation is influenced by processing parameters such as pH, temperature, water activity, and reactant concentration. These variables collectively determine protein conformation, the exposure of reactive groups, and the balance between controlled modification and undesired aggregation (Babu et al., 2024; Gentile, 2020). Importantly, these factors do not act independently; their combined effects ultimately determine whether conjugation preserves structural integrity or leads to destabilization, with corresponding impacts on functional performance. In practical terms, a functional window can be defined as the range of conjugation intensity in which structural modification is sufficient to improve hydration, solubility, interfacial adsorption, steric stabilization, or gel/network formation, but not severe enough to cause excessive browning, irreversible aggregation, lysine blockage, impaired digestibility, or loss of sensory acceptability. Because no universal numerical threshold applies to all protein–polysaccharide systems, functional windows should be defined using a panel of complementary indicators rather than a single value. Useful indicators include degree of grafting, reduction in free amino groups, browning index or absorbance at 420 nm, SDS-PAGE band shifts, molecular-weight distribution, particle size, ζ-potential, surface hydrophobicity, solubility after heat or pH stress, emulsion droplet size, creaming index, interfacial rheology, digestibility, and markers of advanced glycation end-products. A conjugate should be considered over-modified when additional reaction time or intensity no longer improves the target function and instead increases aggregation, browning, viscosity, nutritional loss, or instability. Practical indicators for identifying useful and excessive protein–polysaccharide conjugation in food systems.

Moderate thermal treatment is commonly used to promote conjugation by inducing partial protein unfolding, which exposes reactive amino acid side chains that are typically buried within the native structure (Babu et al., 2024). This controlled unfolding can facilitate covalent coupling via Maillard-type reactions and enhance non-covalent interactions between proteins and polysaccharides (Sun et al., 2022). However, excessive heating can push the system beyond its functional window, triggering protein aggregation and reducing the accessibility of reactive groups—outcomes that suppress effective conjugation (Yu et al., 2023). These opposing effects underscore the importance of maintaining a balance between molecular mobility and structural integrity during processing.

pH is a key control parameter that affects both protein charge and molecular flexibility (Sun et al., 2022). Conjugation reactions are typically most effective within a moderately acidic to neutral pH range, where amino groups are sufficiently deprotonated to act as nucleophiles, while proteins remain soluble and conformationally adaptable (Manzoor et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). Deviations from this optimal range can reduce reaction efficiency by limiting electrostatic attraction or promoting aggregation. Similarly, ionic strength modulates electrostatic interactions: moderate salt concentrations can reduce repulsive forces and facilitate protein–polysaccharide association, whereas high ionic strength often weakens these interactions and disrupts controlled conjugation (Sun et al., 2022).

The concentration of reacting protein and polysaccharide and their mixing ratios also influence conjugation outcomes (Sun et al., 2022). Higher concentrations can increase molecular collision frequency and reaction probability, but excessive levels often promote non-specific aggregation, resulting in heterogeneous products with diminished functionality (Babu et al., 2024). At the molecular level, conjugation behavior reflects the combined contributions of hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and covalent linkages (Benjakul et al., 2021; Gentile, 2020; Manzoor et al., 2024). The relative importance of these interactions depends on the structural characteristics of both components—including amino acid composition and conformational flexibility of the protein, as well as the molecular weight, substitution pattern, and charge density of the polysaccharide.

Influence of protein structure on conjugation outcomes

Protein structure plays a central role in determining the efficiency and uniformity of protein–polysaccharide conjugation. The amino acid composition and sequence of a protein influence key properties such as solubility, isoelectric point, surface hydrophobicity, and conformational flexibility, all of which govern protein behavior during processing (Kashung & Karuthapandian, 2025). In food systems, proteins can be broadly categorized based on their structural organization—such as compact globular proteins versus more flexible or disordered systems—and by their source, including dairy and plant proteins (van de Langerijt et al., 2023).

Globular proteins typically possess well-defined tertiary structures that limit the accessibility of reactive amino groups, reducing conjugation efficiency unless partial unfolding is induced during processing (Adamczyk et al., 2023; van de Langerijt et al., 2023). In contrast, proteins with more open or flexible conformations expose a greater number of reactive sites, which can facilitate polysaccharide attachment but may also increase the risk of uncontrolled aggregation under reaction-intense conditions (Wang et al., 2022). The spatial distribution of amino acid residues also affects conjugation behavior: hydrophobic and aromatic residues contribute to non-covalent stabilization, while lysine, arginine, histidine, and cysteine residues serve as key reactive sites for covalent attachment (Shahidi & Dissanayaka, 2023; Wang et al., 2022).

These structural differences help explain why proteins do not respond uniformly to identical conjugation conditions. Proteins with limited conformational flexibility may require assisted processing to enable effective modification, whereas highly flexible proteins often require tighter control to prevent excessive cross-linking or loss of solubility. As a result, classifying proteins based on structural features and amino acid composition offers a practical framework for predicting conjugation outcomes and guiding the selection of appropriate processing conditions.

However, protein classification should not rely only on broad categories such as globular, fibrous, flexible, or disordered proteins. A more useful approach is to describe protein substrates using measurable structural and colloidal indicators that are directly linked to conjugation efficiency and functional performance. These indicators include free amino group content, lysine accessibility, sulfhydryl and disulfide status, surface hydrophobicity, ζ-potential, particle size distribution, molecular-weight distribution, solubility profile, secondary-structure changes, and, where available, radius of gyration or molecular flexibility obtained from scattering or computational analyses (Adamczyk et al., 2023; Babu et al., 2024; van de Langerijt et al., 2023). For example, increased surface hydrophobicity and partial unfolding may improve exposure of reactive amino groups and promote glycation, but excessive unfolding can increase protein–protein interactions and aggregation, thereby reducing solubility and interfacial performance (Sun et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). Similarly, ζ-potential and particle-size changes can indicate whether conjugation improves colloidal stabilization or instead promotes aggregate formation under pH, salt, or thermal stress (McClements et al., 2022; Nooshkam et al., 2023). Therefore, protein structure should be interpreted through experimentally measurable descriptors rather than fixed structural labels, particularly when comparing dairy, egg, muscle, plant, and hydrolyzed protein systems.

Comparative evaluation of synthesis approaches and functional boundaries

Comparative studies on Maillard-type conjugation of food proteins, particularly whey proteins, demonstrate how synthesis methods influence functional performance under processing stresses such as heat, pH variation, and ionic strength (Sedaghat Doost et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023a). Native whey proteins are prone to denaturation and aggregation under such conditions, limiting their functional stability. Controlled conjugation has been used to improve solubility, thermal stability, and interfacial properties (Liu, Yuan, et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021).

Dry-heating of protein–carbohydrate powders at intermediate water activity remains one of the most established conjugation methods (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023). This method often yields a relatively high degree of glycation and produces conjugates with improved solubility near the isoelectric point, enhanced heat stability, and better emulsifying or gelling properties. However, it requires extended processing times and precise control of temperature, water activity, and duration to minimize excessive browning and potential nutritional losses (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023). Wet-heating methods, which are more compatible with liquid food processing, offer operational advantages but may promote extensive protein unfolding and aggregation if reaction conditions are not carefully optimized (Chaiwong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

Assisted technologies such as ultrasound, high-pressure processing, microwave heating, reactive extrusion, and electrospinning have been applied to accelerate conjugation and fine-tune protein–polysaccharide structures under milder processing conditions (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023a). While these methods can reduce reaction times and improve control over product attributes, they also introduce additional complexity and have yet to see widespread adoption at the industrial scale (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2025). In practice, the choice of synthesis route must balance process feasibility, desired extent of modification, and the risk of exceeding functional boundaries that compromise solubility, nutritional quality, or sensory attributes (Zhang et al., 2025).

Overall, the structure–function relationship of protein–polysaccharide conjugates is best interpreted as a balance between molecular exposure and structural preservation. Insufficient modification may leave proteins vulnerable to aggregation, precipitation, or weak interfacial behavior, whereas excessive modification may produce large aggregates, strong browning, poor digestibility, or reduced adsorption at interfaces. Therefore, the most useful conjugates are not necessarily those with the highest degree of grafting, but those in which the degree of modification matches the target application. For emulsion stabilization, rapid interfacial adsorption and steric stabilization are critical; for gel systems, controlled network formation is required; for delivery systems, particle stability and release behavior are more important; and for films or 3D printing, rheological and mechanical performance become the dominant design criteria.

Applications and functional performance of protein–polysaccharide conjugates in food systems

Protein–polysaccharide conjugates are increasingly applied in food formulations, as controlled conjugation can enhance techno-functional properties while also contributing to nutritional and bioactive functions (Zhang et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2024). Compared with native proteins, these conjugates often display improved heat stability, gelling and emulsifying capacity, and resistance to aggregation, along with enhanced antioxidant activity. In some systems, antimicrobial effects have also been reported, particularly when antimicrobial polysaccharides such as chitosan are involved (Nooshkam et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a). From a design perspective, these benefits are not universal but depend on maintaining conjugation within an optimal modification window. Polysaccharide grafting can enhance dispersion stability and texture by forming thicker interfacial layers or network structures in emulsions and gels, thereby improving tolerance to thermal, pH, and ionic stresses (Nooshkam et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). However, excessive conjugation severity or progression into advanced Maillard stages may compromise sensory attributes or nutritional quality, underscoring the importance of controlled processing. The following sections examine how these principles apply across key food applications.

Emulsification performance and stability constraints

Proteins are widely used to stabilize emulsions in soups, beverages, creams, and dairy-based products, but their performance is often limited by sensitivity to pH changes, temperature fluctuations, and ionic strength (McClements et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2021). Protein–polysaccharide conjugates (PPCs) can improve emulsion stability under these stresses by forming thicker, more cohesive interfacial films around oil droplets, which enhance steric repulsion and interfacial viscoelasticity (Nooshkam et al., 2023). As a result, PPC-stabilized emulsions tend to exhibit smaller droplet sizes, greater resistance to coalescence, and improved thermal stability during processing (Yao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023a).

Care is needed when interpreting examples from the emulsion literature because covalent conjugates, electrostatic complexes, coacervates, and protein–polysaccharide-coated particles are sometimes discussed together although their stabilization mechanisms differ. True Maillard conjugates involve covalent grafting between protein amino groups and carbohydrate carbonyl groups, whereas electrostatic complexes depend mainly on charge attraction and are more sensitive to pH and ionic strength (Kan et al., 2021; Nooshkam et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a). For example, whey protein–pectin systems may improve emulsion stability through electrostatic complexation, heat-induced aggregation control, or covalent glycation depending on the preparation conditions. Delivery of bioactive compounds: Benefits and translational limits

Many nutraceuticals incorporated into foods—such as carotenoids, curcumin, flavonoids, vitamins, and omega-3 lipids—exhibit low aqueous solubility and limited stability during processing, storage, and gastrointestinal (GI) digestion (Nooshkam et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022). Protein–polysaccharide conjugates offer a practical approach for improving delivery performance in food systems by combining the amphiphilic properties of proteins with the hydration and protective capabilities of polysaccharides (Nooshkam et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022). Covalent grafting allows PPCs to self-assemble into nanoparticles, nanogels, emulsions, or microcapsules, forming thicker and more hydrated interfacial or matrix layers that improve resistance to aggregation and chemical degradation across a broad range of pH, temperature, and ionic conditions (Du et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2022).

PPC-based carriers have been shown to enhance both the stability and bioaccessibility of lipophilic bioactive compounds. For example, curcumin-loaded nanoparticles prepared from glycated bovine serum albumin–dextran conjugates demonstrated improved thermal stability and antioxidant activity relative to free curcumin, with minimal particle size changes during incubation at elevated temperatures (Fan et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2019). Similarly, ovalbumin–dextran nanogels produced via Maillard conjugation and heat-induced gelation enhanced curcumin transfer into the micellar phase during in vitro GI digestion, indicating improved stability and bioaccessibility compared to unconjugated protein system (Feng et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022). In the case of carotenoids, whey protein isolate–xylo-oligosaccharide conjugates have been used to microencapsulate lycopene, achieving high encapsulation efficiency and significantly increased in vitro bioaccessibility compared with free lycopene (Jia et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022).

Beyond these specific cases, PPC-based delivery systems have been developed for a wide range of food bioactives, including vitamins, minerals, bioactive peptides, and probiotics (Sun et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). For instance, casein–maltodextrin conjugates have been used to form core–shell nanocarriers for hydrophobic nutraceuticals in clear beverages, while protein–polysaccharide complex coacervates have been applied as microcapsule matrices for essential oils, flavors, and probiotic cultures. These systems have shown improved retention, physicochemical stability, and survival during processing and gastrointestinal transit (Kord Heydari et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2021; Nagaraju, & P, S., Dubey, T., Chinnathambi, S., C. G, P. P., & Rao, P. J., 2021; Sánchez-Osorno et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2021; Worku et al., 2025).

Although many studies report improved delivery performance after protein–polysaccharide modification, direct comparison across systems remains difficult because encapsulation efficiency, particle size, digestion model, bioactive type, and release endpoint are not standardized. Nevertheless, several mechanistic trends can be identified. Maillard-type conjugates are particularly useful for lipophilic bioactives such as curcumin, β-carotene, and lycopene because covalent attachment of hydrophilic polysaccharide chains improves steric stabilization, reduces aggregation, and enhances resistance to pH, salt, and thermal stresses (Fan et al., 2017; Feng et al., 2016; Jia et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022). For example, ovalbumin–dextran Maillard nanogels improved curcumin stability and transfer into the micellar phase during simulated gastrointestinal digestion, while whey protein isolate–dextran or whey protein isolate–gum Arabic conjugates improved β-carotene emulsion stability and bioaccessibility (Chen et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2017; Feng et al., 2016). Electrostatic complexes and core–shell nanoparticles are generally easier to prepare and may provide high encapsulation efficiency, but their stability is more sensitive to pH, ionic strength, and competitive interactions in food matrices (Chen et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2022). Enzymatic conjugation and enzyme-mediated cross-linking may offer better structural specificity and controlled network formation, but their use in bioactive delivery is less broadly demonstrated than Maillard-based systems and is constrained by substrate accessibility, enzyme cost, and compatibility with the target food matrix (Hu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2022). Therefore, Maillard conjugates appear most suitable when environmental stability and digestion-resistant encapsulation are required, electrostatic complexes are attractive for mild and simple processing, and enzymatic approaches are more appropriate when controlled gelation or network reinforcement is the main design target rather than rapid carrier fabrication.

Despite these advantages, successful translation to real food systems depends on carefully controlling the degree of conjugation and resulting molecular structure. Over-modification can adversely affect solubility, sensory attributes, or release kinetics, indicating that PPC-based carriers are most effective when designed within defined functional windows tailored to the specific food matrix and processing conditions (Nooshkam et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). Emerging applications: 3D printing, Pickering systems, edible films, and structured foods

Beyond conventional emulsions and delivery systems, protein–polysaccharide conjugates and complexes are increasingly being explored as structuring agents for 3D food printing, Pickering emulsion gels, edible films, and advanced food packaging materials. In extrusion-based 3D printing, printable food inks require a balance between flowability during extrusion and shape retention after deposition. Protein–polysaccharide systems can contribute to this balance by increasing viscosity, yield stress, shear-thinning behavior, gel strength, and water-holding capacity. Recent work on corn oligopeptide/xanthan gum complexes demonstrated that edible Pickering emulsion gels could be prepared as promising inks for 3D printing, with xanthan gum concentration and oil–water ratio influencing the properties of both the complexes and the resulting emulsion gels (Zheng, Ma, et al., 2025). Similarly, abalone oligopeptide–xanthan gum complexes have been used to stabilize Pickering emulsions, showing that peptide–polysaccharide interactions can improve emulsion stability depending on biopolymer concentration and oil–water ratio (Li, Weng, et al., 2026). Myosin–κ-carrageenan Pickering emulsions have also been applied to bighead carp surimi gels, where the emulsion system influenced gel strength, hardness, and structured food quality (Ren et al., 2025). In addition, ovomucin–xanthan gum nanoparticles prepared by pH cycling have recently been evaluated as stabilizers for Pickering emulsions, further demonstrating the potential of protein–polysaccharide particles for designing food-grade emulsion systems with tunable microstructure, rheology, and stability (Zeng et al., 2025).

In edible films and coatings, protein–polysaccharide conjugation can improve film-forming ability, mechanical strength, barrier properties, water resistance, and the controlled release of active compounds. Proteins provide cohesive film networks, while polysaccharides contribute oxygen barrier capacity, hydration control, and matrix reinforcement. Polysaccharides such as pectin, starch, alginate, carrageenan, and xanthan gum are widely used as edible film and coating materials, while protein- and lipid-based polymers are also used to develop natural food-packaging systems (Mohamed et al., 2020). Conjugation or complexation can reduce phase separation between biopolymers and improve matrix homogeneity, which is important for packaging films carrying antioxidants, antimicrobials, pigments, or freshness indicators. However, film applications also expose a key design trade-off: increasing polysaccharide content or cross-linking density may improve strength and barrier performance, but excessive network formation can reduce flexibility, increase brittleness, or limit release of active compounds (Mohamed et al., 2020).

These emerging applications reinforce the need to define the target functionality before selecting the conjugation route. Emulsion delivery systems require colloidal stability and controlled digestion; 3D printing requires rheological precision and shape retention; edible films require mechanical integrity and barrier properties; and Pickering systems require particle wettability, interfacial anchoring, and resistance to coalescence. Therefore, future studies should report not only structural evidence of conjugation or complexation, but also application-specific performance indicators such as print resolution, yield stress, recovery after shear, tensile strength, elongation at break, water vapor permeability, oxygen permeability, active-compound release, and stability during storage.

Nutritional and safety implications: Digestibility, nutrient retention, allergenicity, and advanced glycation products

The nutritional consequences of protein–polysaccharide conjugation require balanced evaluation because structural modification can produce both beneficial and undesirable effects. Controlled conjugation may improve digestion-related performance indirectly by increasing protein dispersion, reducing aggregation, improving colloidal stability, or protecting bioactive compounds during gastric and intestinal digestion (Nooshkam et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022; Urango et al., 2024). In delivery systems, these effects may increase the bioaccessibility of encapsulated lipophilic compounds or protect sensitive ingredients from premature degradation; however, the same structural changes may also alter protease accessibility and peptide release during digestion (Sun et al., 2022; Urango et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023).

Conjugation can also influence allergenicity, but the outcome is protein- and process-dependent. Several food proteins, including soy β-conglycinin and bovine β-lactoglobulin, are known allergenic proteins, and previous studies have shown that conjugation with polysaccharides can reduce antigenicity or mask allergenic epitopes in some systems (Krishnan et al., 2009; Li et al., 2011; Sélo et al., 1999; Usui et al., 2004). However, Maillard reaction products may either reduce or increase immunoreactivity depending on the allergen, reaction conditions, degree of glycation, and formation of new glycation-derived epitopes (Teodorowicz et al., 2017). Therefore, reduced antibody binding in one system should not be generalized as evidence that conjugation universally lowers allergenicity.

Excessive Maillard reaction may compromise nutritional quality by blocking lysine residues, reducing essential amino acid availability, and decreasing protein digestibility. This is particularly important because lysine is one of the most reactive amino acids during glycation and is nutritionally indispensable. Therefore, when Maillard conjugation is used to improve techno-functional properties, available lysine and in vitro protein digestibility should be evaluated alongside conventional functional indicators such as solubility, emulsifying activity, particle size, and ζ-potential (Teodorowicz et al., 2017; Urango et al., 2024).

Potential health risks should also be considered when Maillard-based conjugation is applied in food systems. Early-stage glycation may improve functionality, but progression toward advanced Maillard stages can increase the formation of advanced glycation end-products, which are associated with reduced nutritional quality and possible adverse biological effects when present at high levels in thermally processed foods (Li et al., 2023; Patel et al., 2024). Therefore, future studies should not evaluate protein–polysaccharide conjugates only by solubility, emulsification, encapsulation efficiency, or storage stability. They should also include nutritional and safety-related markers such as available lysine, in vitro protein digestibility, peptide release profile, allergenicity assays where relevant, browning index, fluorescent Maillard products, and selected advanced glycation end-product markers (Li et al., 2023; Teodorowicz et al., 2017; Urango et al., 2024).

Challenges, failure mechanisms, and industrial translation

Despite strong laboratory evidence for improved functionality, translation of protein–polysaccharide conjugates into commercial food systems remains limited by several technical and engineering barriers. The first challenge is reaction control. Many conjugation methods, particularly dry-heating Maillard approaches, require extended reaction times and careful control of water activity, humidity, temperature, and storage conditions (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Kutzli et al., 2021). Small deviations in these variables can produce batch-to-batch differences in degree of grafting, browning, solubility, and molecular-weight distribution (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Wet-heating methods are easier to integrate into liquid processing lines, but they may increase aggregation and dilution-related downstream costs if protein concentration, pH, and heating intensity are not optimized (Chaiwong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021). Assisted technologies can shorten reaction time, but their industrial value depends on energy demand, equipment cost, scalability, cleaning requirements, and reproducibility at large volume (Dumitrașcu et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2025).

The second challenge is compatibility with real food matrices. Most studies evaluate conjugates in simplified buffer systems, but commercial foods contain salts, sugars, lipids, polyphenols, minerals, acids, stabilizers, and competing proteins that can modify electrostatic interactions, hydration, and interfacial adsorption (Gentile, 2020;McClements et al., 2022; Nooshkam et al., 2023). High salt concentrations may screen electrostatic repulsion and promote flocculation; low pH may cause precipitation near the protein isoelectric point; calcium and other multivalent ions may bridge anionic polysaccharides and induce network collapse; and thermal cycling may promote repeated unfolding, aggregation, or phase separation (Du et al., 2022; Nooshkam et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2022). In multi-component foods, conjugates may also compete with low-molecular-weight surfactants, phospholipids, or other proteins for interfacial adsorption, reducing their expected emulsifying advantage (McClements et al., 2022; Nooshkam et al., 2023).

The third challenge is defining application-specific failure mechanisms. In emulsions, failure may appear as large droplet size, creaming, coalescence, flocculation, or oxidative instability (Nooshkam et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2022). In delivery systems, failure may occur through low encapsulation efficiency, premature release, poor bioaccessibility, or instability during digestion (Sun et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). In gels and 3D-printed foods, failure may involve weak yield stress, poor shape retention, syneresis, brittle texture, or collapse during post-processing. In edible films, failure may involve brittleness, high water vapor permeability, poor sealing behavior, or uncontrolled active-compound release (Mohamed et al., 2020). Therefore, the success of a conjugation strategy should be judged against the specific failure mode of the target product, not only by evidence that conjugation occurred.

The fourth challenge is regulatory and consumer acceptance. Although Maillard conjugation can be considered label-friendly compared with synthetic chemical cross-linking, excessive browning, flavor changes, loss of available lysine, or formation of advanced glycation products may limit acceptability (Li et al., 2023; Nooshkam et al., 2023; Teodorowicz et al., 2017). Enzymatic approaches may be more acceptable when food-grade enzymes are used, but cost, enzyme specificity, and residual activity must be considered (Miwa, 2020; Wu et al., 2022). Chemical cross-linking requires particular caution because reagent residues, reaction by-products, and regulatory status may restrict food use. Overall, industrial translation will require integrated evaluation of functionality, safety, cost, processing throughput, reproducibility, and compatibility with existing food manufacturing equipment.

Concluding remarks and outlook

Protein–polysaccharide conjugation is a versatile strategy for improving the performance of food proteins, but its value depends on controlled modification rather than maximum reaction intensity. The main conclusion of this review is that functional improvement occurs within an application-specific window where protein unfolding, polysaccharide attachment, hydration, and interfacial or network stabilization are sufficient to enhance performance without causing excessive aggregation, browning, nutritional loss, or instability. This balance explains why similar conjugation approaches can produce different outcomes across protein sources, polysaccharide types, processing conditions, and food matrices.

Several practical design principles emerge from the reviewed literature. First, conjugation conditions should be selected according to the target function rather than applied uniformly across systems. Second, structural evidence of conjugation should be supported by functional markers such as solubility under stress, emulsion stability, rheological behavior, encapsulation efficiency, digestibility, or film and printing performance. Third, covalent conjugates should be clearly distinguished from electrostatic complexes and coacervates because they differ in preparation, stability, and response to pH and salt. Fourth, Maillard conjugation should be controlled to remain in early or moderate reaction stages, avoiding excessive browning, lysine loss, and advanced glycation product formation. Fifth, process-intensified technologies should be evaluated not only by their ability to accelerate conjugation, but also by their effects on aggregation, energy input, scalability, and batch reproducibility.

Future research should prioritize quantitative and semi-quantitative design maps for common protein–polysaccharide systems. Such maps should define useful ranges of degree of grafting, free amino group loss, browning index, particle size, ζ-potential, solubility, rheological behavior, and digestion-related outcomes for specific applications. Greater attention should also be given to real-food validation under high salt, acidic pH, thermal cycling, lipid oxidation, and multi-component conditions. Finally, industrial translation will require studies that integrate ingredient functionality with processing cost, equipment compatibility, regulatory acceptance, sensory quality, nutritional value, and consumer expectations. With this approach, protein–polysaccharide conjugation can move from empirical modification toward rational design of functional, scalable, and safe food ingredients.
