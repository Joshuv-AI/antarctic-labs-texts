Associations of Adherence to the 24hour Movement Guidelines with Depressive Symptoms and Anxiety: the Mediating Role of Physical Fitness
Authors: Qiang Sun, Danqing Zhang, Tao Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health problems such as depression and anxiety were increasingly prevalent among students. Emerging evidence links adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines—including adequate physical activity, limited screen time, and sufficient sleep—with improved mental well-being. However, the mechanisms underlying these associations remained poorly understood.ObjectivesThis study aimed to examine the association of adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines with symptoms of depression and anxiety among Chinese students, and to explore the mediating role of perceived fitness in these relationships.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1674 students (mean age = 11.4 ± 0.8 years; 47.6% boys) from Chinese elementary schools. 24-hour movement behaviours were assessed using validated self-report measures. Mental health outcomes were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). Perceived fitness was assessed using the International Fitness Scale. Structural equation modelling was used to evaluate direct and indirect pathways, adjusting for relevant covariates.ResultsOnly 1.4% of participants met all components of the 24-hour movement guidelines. Adherence to the guidelines was significantly associated with lower levels of depressive (β = -0.125) and anxiety symptoms (β = -0.107). Perceived fitness was also significantly associated with reduced symptoms of depression (β = -0.111) and anxiety (β = -0.049). However, the mediating effects of perceived fitness in both models were minimal or not statistically significant (depression symptoms: β = 0.002; anxiety symptoms: β = 0.001).ConclusionsBetter adherence to 24hour movement guidelines was associated with fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety in Chinese students. While perceived fitness was independently linked to improved mental health, its mediating role appears limited. Future studies should incorporate objective fitness assessments and explore additional mediating pathways to fully understand how movement behaviours impact students mental well-being.Keywords: Perceived fitness, Depression, Anxiety, 24-hour movement guidelines, Mediation model, Mental health

Introduction

A significant proportion (14%) of children and adolescents worldwide experienced mental disorders, including depressive symptoms and anxiety, as reported by the World Health Organization (2024) [1]. Moreover, an expanding body of literature highlighted the widespread prevalence and long-term impact of these disorders across the lifespan [2]. Recent studies further highlighted the increased prevalence of anxiety and depression among children and adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic [3], with data indicating that approximately 32% of Chinese adolescents reported anxiety symptoms and 4.4% experienced depressive symptoms [4]. Anxiety rose during adolescence and could continue into adulthood, affecting long-term outcomes [5]. Children and adolescents with anxiety and depression often reported more severe symptoms, including somatic complaints such as shakiness and headaches [6]. These physical symptoms were linked to increased school absenteeism and poorer academic performance, with autonomic symptoms like trembling and sweating being strongly associated with school refusal [7]. Chronic absenteeism due to anxiety and depressive symptoms could lead to long-term academic and social difficulties [8], underscoring the need for greater attention.

Research showed that adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines was linked to improved health outcomes in various populations [9–12]. These guidelines recommended at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily, limiting sitting time and recreational screen time to no more than 2 h per day to reduce sedentary behavior, and ensuring adequate sleep—9 to 11 h for children (5–13 years) and 8 to 10 h for adolescents (14–17 years). Children and adolescent movement behaviours were individually linked to various physical outcomes [13], such as adiposity [14–16]. In contrast, inactive lifestyles contributed to physical problems [17, 18] such as reduced fitness and poor weight status in youth. Several studies explored the relationship between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and physical fitness [19], showing that following these guidelines was linked to improved physical fitness level and indicators(e.g. aerobic fitness, muscle endurance) [20–22]. Furthermore, limiting screen time and sedentary behavior, as well as getting adequate sleep, were shown to benefit mental health by reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety and improving overall well-being [23–25].

Physical fitness in children and adolescents encompassed various components, including aerobic fitness, muscular strength, flexibility, and body composition [26], and played a crucial role in both physical and mental health [26]. A healthy level of physical fitness was associated with numerous benefits [27], including a reduced risk of cardiovascular diseases [28], obesity, musculoskeletal diseases, and mental health symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress [29]. Certain physical fitness components might also have explained the conceptual model and potential mechanisms linking fitness to mental health outcomes [30, 31]. Specific components of physical fitness, such as muscular and cardiorespiratory fitness, varied in their strength of association with different indicators of mental health in youth [32]. Muscular fitness was believed to directly influence mental health by linking to lower symptoms of anxiety and depression [33]. Research indicated that cardiorespiratory fitness was positively associated with psychological well-being [34], enhanced cognition and quality of life [35] and reduced depression- and anxiety-related outcomes [36].

Recent research showed that adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines was associated with improved mental health, particularly in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety [37, 38]. Although the beneficial effects of physical activity on mental health were well-established, the mechanisms underlying these relationship between 24-h movement behaviour and mental health remained poorly understood [39]. Numerous studies suggested that physical fitness served as a protective factor (e.g. resilience) against the onset of depressive and anxiety symptoms [40–42]. Physical fitness might acted as a mediator in this relationship, with regular physical activity contributing to improvements in both physical and mental health [43, 44]. These improvements were thought to occur through reductions in physiological markers associated with depression and anxiety [45], such as elevated cortisol levels and inflammation [46, 47]. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) examine the association of adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines with symptoms of depression and anxiety, and (2) explore the mediating role of perceived fitness in the relationship between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health outcomes.

Methods

Population and study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design and used convenience sampling from public elementary schools in a city of Eastern China from March to December 2024. For each school, five or six classes were randomly selected, and a contact person coordinated with the responsible teacher at each school. 1674 participants (47.6% boys; mean age ± SD: 11.4 ± 0.8 years) completed the entire questionnaire. The inclusion criteria for this study were elementary school students who were considered healthy. Students were excluded if they had diagnosed chronic physical illnesses (including epilepsy or congenital heart disease), diagnosed mental health disorders (including ADHD or autism spectrum disorders), physical disabilities or conditions that limited regular physical activity, or if they were absent during data collection.

Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, as all participants were minors. In addition, assent was obtained from the students themselves after they were informed about the purpose and voluntary nature of the study. During questionnaire administration, responsible teacher and researcher were available in the classroom to assist participants who had difficulty understanding any of the questions. This study procedure and protocol have been performed upon approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Nanjing Sport Institute (RT-2025-32).

Measurement

24-hour movement behaviour

The 24-h movement behaviours, included physical activity, screen time were measured using selected items derived from the Chinese version of the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC)- [48]which were reliable and validated in school-aged students [48, 49].

Physical activity was assessed with two items: (1) ‘How many days did you engage in moderate to vigorous physical activity for at least 60 min on weekdays during the past week? (0 = none, 1 = 1 day, 2 = 2 days, 3 = 3 days, 4 = 4 days, 5 = 5 days)’; and (2) ‘How many days did you engage in moderate to vigorous physical activity for at least 60 min on weekends during the past week? (0 = none, 1 = 1 day, 2 = 2 days)’. The two items were summed to produce a total number of days (0–7); participants reporting 7 days were considered as meeting the physical activity guideline. These items showed acceptable reliability in Chinese students [48].

Screen time was assessed with three items asking, for weekdays and weekend days separately, the number of hours spent in leisure time: (1) watching television or movies; (2) playing video games; and (3) using other electronic screen-based devices. Response options were 0 = none, 1 = 0.5 hour, 2 = 1 hour, 3 = 2 hours, and 4 = 3 hours or more. Participants whose total daily screen time did not exceed 2 hours were classified as meeting the screen time guideline [50]. These items also showed acceptable reliability in Chinese students [48].

Sleep duration was assessed using items were adapted from the Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [51]: (1) ‘What time do you usually go to bed and wake up on weekdays? and (2) ‘What time do you usually go to bed and wake up on weekends or holiday?’. Sleep duration was calculated separately for weekdays and weekends by subtracting bedtime from wake-up time, and an average daily sleep duration was then derived. Participants met sleep guideline if their average sleep duration was 9–11 h per night for children aged 5–13 years or 8–10 h per night for adolescents aged 14–17 years, in line with established recommendations [50]. The PSQI demostrated acceptable validity and reliability among Chinese students [52].

The number of 24-hour movement guidelines met was calculated by summing the total number of individual guidelines (physical activity, screen time, and sleep duration) for which the participant met the criteria. As a result, the possible number of guidelines met ranged from 0 to 3, where 0 indicated that no guideline was met, 1 indicated that one guideline was met, 2 indicated that two guidelines were met, and 3 indicated that all three guidelines were met.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). This instrument consists of nine items concerning experiences of depressive symptoms within the last 2 weeks. Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Total scores ranged between 0 and 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Severity categories were: 0–4 (minimal), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), as well as 20–27 (severe) [53]. The Chinese PHQ-9 had been tested among Chinese students and demonstrated adequate reliability and validity [54].

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety disorders were assessed using the Chinese version of 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale (from 0 to 3), yielding total scores for GAD-7 from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity. Severity categories were defined as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9) moderate (10–14) or severe (15–21) [55]. The Chinese GAD-7 was widely used among Chinese students and showed acceptable reliability and validity [54].

Perceived fitness

Perceived fitness was defined as an individual’s subjective assessment of their overall physical fitness, including aspects such as strength, endurance, agility, and flexibility. It was assessed using the International Fitness Scale (IFIS). The IFIS comprised five single-item domains (overall fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, speed/agility and flexibility), each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (“very poor”, “poor”, “average”, “good” and “very good”) [56]. In this study, the four component items (cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, speed/agility and flexibility) were summed to derive a total perceived fitness score ranging from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating better perceived fitness. Prior studies among children and adolesents in European and South American countries reported acceptable construct validity and reliabiltity [56–58]. The Chinese version (IFIS-C) showed moderate reliability in Chinese school-aged children and adolescents [59].

Covariates (Demographic Factors)

Demographic information included students’ gender (responses: 1 = boys, 2 = girls), age (years), siblings (Do you have siblings? responses: 1 = Yes, 2 = No), and living with parents (Do you live with your parents? responses: 1 = Yes, 2 = No).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS v26 to compute means, standard deviations, percentages, and bivariate correlations. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was performed in IBM AMOS v24. The mediation analysis followed a two-step approach: first, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for all measured variables to identify potential confounders; second, the SEM was estimated using the maximum likelihood method. Model fit indices included chi-square (χ²), χ²/degrees of freedom (df) < 5, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI > 0.9), Comparative Fit Index (CFI > 0.9), Root Mean Squared Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08) [60]. Mediation models were tested using the bias-corrected percentile method with 5,000 bootstrap samples. Robust estimation techniques (e.g., bootstrapping) were employed when using path analysis to estimate the regression coefficients and robust standard errors for the mediation model. Two models were examined: (1) the mediating role of perceived fitness in the relationship between the number of 24-hour movement guideline meetings and depression, and (2) the mediating role of perceived fitness in the relationship between the number of 24-hour movement guideline meetings and anxiety. All models were adjusted for age and sex, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 showed the demographic characteristics and key variables of the study participants. A total of 1,674 participants were included in the study, comprising 797 boys (47.6%) and 877 girls (52.4%). The participants had a mean age of 11.4 years (SD = 0.8). Only 1.4% of participants reported meeting all 24-hour movement guidelines, while 13.6% reported meeting none. The mean scores for perceived fitness, depression, and anxiety were 13.4 (SD = 2.6), 3.9 (SD = 5.0), and 2.9 (SD = 4.4), respectively. Additionally, 47.7% of participants reported having siblings, and 84.8% reported living with their parents.

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate that adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines was associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms and anxiety. Perceived fitness was significantly associated with lower levels of both depressive and anxiety symptoms. However, the direct relationship (mediator perceived fitness) between adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health was weak and not statistically significant. These findings suggested that improving adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines and perceived fitness may help improve mental health.

Evidence consistently supported the positive impact of combination of movement behaviours on mental health outcomes, underscoring the findings in this study of 24-hour movement guidelines in reducing anxiety and depression symptoms. Physical activity, in particular, had been widely accepted as an effective intervention for promoting mental health and preventing mental ill-health through mechanisms such as enhanced brain function, reduced inflammation, and development of coping skills [31, 61]. Numerous studies among adolescents demonstrated that higher levels of physical activity were associated with lower rates of depression and anxiety [62, 63]. For example, a meta-analysis found that physical activity interventions significantly reduced depressive symptoms in youth [62]. The physiological mechanisms underlying these associations included increased neurogenesis, the release of endorphins, improved regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and the promotion of neuroplasticity [31]. Sedentary behaviours were associated with adverse mental health effects, including increased exposure to stressors such as negative social comparisons and were linked to sleep disorders and reduced opportunities for physical or social engagement, all of which were detrimental to mental wellbeing [64–66]. Studies showed that higher levels of sedentary behavior, particularly excessive screen time, including use of smartphones, computers, and television, were consistently linked to increased risk of depression and anxiety [23, 67]. Mechanisms proposed for these associations included the displacement of physical activity, impaired sleep due to blue light exposure, and increased exposure to negative online content or cyberbullying. For example, Twenge and Campbell (2018) found that adolescents who spent more than three hours per day on electronic devices had significantly higher rates of depression and suicide-related outcomes [23]. In contrast, replacing sedentary behaviour with physical activity or adequate sleep had been shown to have beneficial effects on mental health indicators, which highlighted the importance of a holistic approach that included physical activity, sleep, and reduced screen time [31, 61]. Furthermore, evidence indicated that adequate sleep plays a crucial role in regulating emotional health by reducing inflammation and optimizing brain neurotransmitter activity, thereby mitigating stress and mental health disorders like anxiety and depression [68–70]. Sleep deprivation was linked to increased risk of depressive and anxiety symptoms among adolescents, with studies indicating that poor sleep duration and quality were significant predictors of mental health problems [71, 72]. These findings emphasized the need for public health interventions that promoted a combination of sleep, physical activity, and reduced sedentary behaviours as part of a comprehensive 24-hour movement framework to address mental health challenges. Collectively, these results highlighted the importance of integrating these behaviours into daily routines to support mental health and wellbeing.

Perceived fitness was associated with lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, but it might not have served as a strong explanatory mediator in this study. Our mediation analysis revealed that the effect sizes for perceived fitness as a mediator between 24-hour movement behaviors and mental health outcomes were very small. While perceived fitness was associated with lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, the mediation effect was limited and not statistically significant in most pathways. This suggested that, in the current sample, perceived fitness might not serve as a strong explanatory mediator for the relationship between movement behaviors and mental health. This finding was consistent with previous studies indicating that although perceived fitness was linked to mental health outcomes, its mediating role might have been overshadowed by other biological, psychosocial, or behavioral mechanisms [31, 63]. Possible reasons for the non-significant mediation included the multidimensional nature of both movement behaviors and mental health, as well as the use of self-reported measures for perceived fitness [26]. Although existing research suggested that perceived fitness and its associated indicators could be potential mechanisms [31], several factors might have contributed to the complexity of this relationship. Previous studies, especially those conducted among adolescents, found that higher perceived fitness was associated with better mental health outcomes, including reduced depression and anxiety [26, 63]. Perceived fitness might have influenced mental health through enhanced self-esteem, social acceptance, and positive body image [73].

The 24-hour movement behaviour, which encompassed physical activity, sedentary behaviour (including screen time), and sleep, was a set of complex variables. Recent meta-analyses and longitudinal studies also showed that while physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep each relate to mental health, the mediating role of perceived fitness was often modest [31, 63]. The relationship between overall movement patterns and mental health was likely mediated by various interrelated factors, beyond just perceived fitness. A systematic review had highlighted that the mechanisms linking physical activity, sedentary behaviours, and sleep with mental health were multifaceted, reflecting intricate interactions across biological, psychosocial, and behavioural domains [74]. Strong evidence suggested that physical health status or medical conditions mediate the relationship between physical activity and mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety [74]. Numerous studies had explored potential pathways between different physical activity domains (e.g., leisure-time physical activity) and mental health indicators (e.g. depression [75] and anxiety [76]), which contributed to a growing body of literature on the mechanisms through which each movement behaviour influenced mental health [39]. Secondly, the reliance on self-reported measures to assess physical fitness might have limited the study’s ability to capture the full scope of the relationship between physical fitness and mental health. Although perceived fitness had demonstrated good reliability and validity in existing research [59] and was widely utilized in physical fitness studies [45, 77], the majority of evidence linking physical fitness to mental health outcomes was based on objective measures [78]. While studies had examined various mediators within different physical fitness domains (e.g., muscular fitness) [79], perceived fitness as an overall measure in this study might have failed to represent the complex relationships between these domains and mental health. Therefore, future research was recommended to incorporate objective measures of physical fitness and to investigate the potential indirect effects of specific physical fitness indicators. Finally, sample characteristics might also have influenced the findings. This study was conducted with a subset of students from southeastern cities in China, which could have limited the generalizability of the results. Thus, further research was necessary to explore additional potential mediating variables and to replicate the findings in more diverse populations.

This study had several strengths, particularly its examination of the relationship between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health outcomes, as well as the role of perceived fitness as a mediator. However, several limitations were noted. First, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference. Second, reliance on self-reported measures for both movement behaviors and perceived fitness might have introduced bias and limited the accuracy of the findings. Third, the dichotomization of each 24-hour movement behavior (meeting vs. not meeting guidelines) might have oversimplified the relationships and lead to some loss of information, although the overall movement behavior variable was classified into four categories based on the number of guidelines met. Additionally, the sample was drawn from students in a single city, which might have limited the generalizability of the results. The mediation effects of perceived fitness were small and mostly non-significant, suggesting that other unmeasured mediators might have played a more substantial role. Future research was recommended to employ longitudinal designs, objective measures, and more diverse populations, and consider additional mediating factors to better capture the complexity of these relationships.

The findings have practical implications for public health and school-based interventions. Promoting adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines—encouraging regular physical activity, sufficient sleep, and reduced sedentary behavior, especially screen time—might have helped reduce depression and anxiety risk among students. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the study and the small mediation effects observed, recommendations for interventions should have been made cautiously, and further longitudinal research was needed to clarify causal pathways. Schools, families, and policymakers were encouraged to work together to foster environments that supported healthy movement behaviors, such as integrating physical activity into daily routines, setting limits on screen time, and prioritizing sleep hygiene. Adopting a comprehensive approach to movement behaviors might ultimately have improved the mental health and wellbeing of young people.

Conclusion

Only 1.4% of participants reported meeting all 24-hour movement guidelines. The study found that adherence to these guidelines was associated with lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, although the mediating role of perceived fitness was weak. Future research should further clarify the complex relationships between movement behaviours and mental health outcomes through targeted interventions.
