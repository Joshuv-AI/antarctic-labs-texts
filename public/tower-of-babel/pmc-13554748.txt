Chinese TEER Innovation
Authors: Yunpeng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: degenerative mitral regurgitation, GeminiOne, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

With the intensification of population aging, degenerative mitral regurgitation (DMR) has become a major cardiovascular disease threatening the Asian population. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), as an effective, minimally invasive and safe interventional therapy, is now guideline-recommended for older and high-risk patients with DMR (Class IIa in both American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery guidelines.1,2

Globally, 2 TEER systems have obtained Food and Drug Administration approval for DMR: the Abbott MitraClip series (mechanical locking) and the Edwards PASCAL system (elastic locking). Both are widely used. Now, a new player—the GeminiOne system (Peijia Medical)—has completed its pivotal trial in China. The 1-year results, published in this issue of the journal, provide the first rigorous look at a domestically developed fourth-generation TEER device with an entirely novel locking mechanism.

This prospective, multicenter, single-arm study enrolled 120 patients with symptomatic moderate-to-severe or severe DMR at prohibitive or high surgical risk (median Society of Thoracic Surgeons replacement score 8.40).3 The primary endpoint—clinical success at 1 year, defined as freedom from all-cause death, mitral reintervention, and residual mitral regurgitation (MR) >2+—was achieved in 89.84% (95% CI: 84.56%-95.46%), surpassing the prespecified performance goal. Notably, 92.24% of patients maintained MR ≤2+ at 1 year, and the proportion of NYHA functional class I/II improved from 23.33% at baseline to 94.74% (P < 0.001). The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score increased by a mean of 14.79 points.

Safety was also favorable: The Kaplan-Meier estimates for freedom from all-cause mortality, major adverse events, and heart failure hospitalization were 97.48%, 91.61%, and 94.00%, respectively, with corresponding crude event rates of 2.52% (3 of 120), 8.39% (10 of 120), and 6.00% (7 of 120).

These outcomes are comparable to those reported for MitraClip and PASCAL in CLASP IID (Edwards PASCAL TrAnScatheter Valve RePair System Pivotal Clinical Trial) and the EXPAND G4 (A Post-Market Study Assessment of the Safety and Performance of the MitraClip G4 System) trial.4,5 Importantly, 37.5% of patients had lesions outside the A2/P2 region. Further subgroup analysis revealed that the GeminiOne system achieved effective MR reduction in both anatomies, with the complex non-A2/P2 lesion subgroup demonstrating superior outcomes: 97.7% achieved MR ≤2+ at 1 year, compared with 89.0% in the standard A2/P2 lesion group. These results suggest that, even with limited TEER procedure experience among Chinese operators, GeminiOne provides robust and durable regurgitation reduction across a variety of lesion locations—and may offer particular advantages in treating complex leaflet anatomies.

Unlike the mechanical lock of MitraClip or the elastic lock of PASCAL, GeminiOne uses a groove-cam mechanism with a threaded central shaft and a U-shaped clip arm. This design allows automatic locking and release at any angle, enabling the operator to adjust grasping tension in real time. It combines the stability of a mechanical lock with the leaflet-protective features of an elastic lock. Additional ergonomic advantages include elimination of repetitive lock-unlock maneuvers and a single-step release, which appears to shorten the learning curve—device time decreased from a median of 72 minutes (first 60 cases) to 68 minutes (next 60 cases).

As noted in the accompanying editorial, China faces unique challenges in structural heart interventions. The regulatory pathway is separate and rigorous; annual TEER procedures remain <5,000, although the field is growing rapidly. In contrast to transcatheter aortic valve replacement, where multiple domestic valves (Taurus, VitaFlow, VenusA, J-valve) have reshaped the market, TEER is still in an early phase. GeminiOne and another Chinese system (DragonFly) represent the vanguard, but both have been tested only in single-arm, nonrandomized studies. Nevertheless, it cannot be overlooked that severe TEER-related severe complications have also emerged in this study, including TEER-associated valvular injury and gas embolism. For this novel device, standardized operational training in the early stage as well as further large-scale clinical research remain essential.

The GeminiOne-DMR (Safety and Efficacy of the GeminiOne Transcatheter Valve Edge-to-Edge Repair System in Patients With Moderate-severe or Severe Degenerative Mitral Regurgitation) trial is an encouraging beginning, but it also highlights how far China still has to go. The single-arm design, albeit appropriate for a first-in-pivotal study, cannot provide comparative effectiveness data against MitraClip or PASCAL. There is no large-scale, randomized TEER trial in China, no national DMR epidemiology study, and no prospective registry that captures real-world outcomes across different devices. Whether Chinese DMR patients differ from Western populations in leaflet anatomy, prolapse morphology, or calcification patterns remains unknown. Furthermore, the rapid emergence of multiple homegrown devices—each with unique locking mechanisms and clip geometries—demands standardized training and objective head-to-head evaluations.

The 1-year results of the GeminiOne system demonstrate that a novel, independently innovated Chinese TEER device can achieve clinical outcomes on par with international gold standards. This is a genuinely promising start. However, to move from innovation to evidence-based standard of care, China needs randomized controlled trials, national registries, and rigorous comparative effectiveness research. The road ahead remains long—but the first steps have been taken.
