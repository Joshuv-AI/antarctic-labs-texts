A polymorphism in the DOCK7 gene (rs1167998) is associated with salt restriction behavior in hypertensive patients
Authors: Hideo Nakane, Takashi Tamura, Mako Nagayoshi, Rieko Okada, Kenji Takeuchi, Yohko Nakamura, Sadao Suzuki, Naoyuki Takashima, Teruhide Koyama, Kokichi Arisawa, Hiroaki Ikezaki, Keitaro Tanaka, Daisaku Nishimoto, Yukihide Momozawa, Nobuyuki Hamajima, Hideo Tanaka, Kenji Wakai, Keitaro Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with hypertension are recommended to decrease their salt intake. However, some patients do not follow this recommendation. It is possible that some of these individuals cannot restrict their salt intake due to a genetic preference for salt. Thus, we attempted to identify genetic factors related to salt restriction behavior. The subjects were 817 patients with hypertension. Salt restriction status was determined by self-reported answers (yes/no) to the question “Do you restrict your salt intake in everyday meals?” We focused on 21 single-nucleotide polymorphisms related to genes involved in the regulation of blood pressure, salt metabolism, or neurotransmission, and analyzed the associations of the single-nucleotide polymorphisms with salt restriction behavior. Of the 817 subjects, 568 (69.5%) reported that they restricted their salt intake. Two single-nucleotide polymorphisms (rs1167998 and rs2970847) were found to be correlated with salt restriction behavior. Among the 21 single-nucleotide polymorphisms, rs1167998 in the intron region of the dedicator of cytokinesis 7 gene, which encodes a guanine nucleotide exchange factor that plays a role in axon formation and neuronal polarization, showed the strongest association (odds ratio per allele, 1.48; 95% confidence interval, 1.11 to 1.95). In conclusion, rs1167998, a single-nucleotide polymorphism in the dedicator of cytokinesis 7 gene that is associated with blood lipid levels and cardiovascular disease risk was associated with salt restriction behavior in a population of Japanese patients with hypertension. Further confirmatory studies are warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is one of the major risk factors for various cardiovascular diseases, such as stroke and myocardial infarction.1 Since excessive salt intake raises blood pressure, hypertensive patients are strongly recommended to reduce their dietary salt intake.2 Japanese people tend to have a high intake of salt from their diet, because many traditional Japanese foods, such as soy sauce, miso, and dried fish products, are rich in salt.3 In Japan, the average dietary salt intake was 13.7 g per day in 1976, but it has gradually decreased to 11.7 g per day in 1987, and 10.6 g per day in 2006.3 According to a more recent National Health and Nutrition Survey conducted in 2018, the value has reached 10.1 g per day.4 Nevertheless, considering that the World Health Organization recommends reducing salt consumption to less than 5 g per day,5 this level is still high, and further reduction of salt intake has thus been advocated in Health Japan 21 (the second term that started in 2013), a 10-year national health promotion plan of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan.

Unfortunately, even among patients with hypertension, some do not restrict their salt intake. It is thought that some of these patients may have difficulty in restricting their salt intake due to a genetic preference for salt. For such genetically predisposed people, a recommendation to reduce their salt intake based on a general theory might be ineffective, and instead, extensive antihypertensive strategies with consideration of the individual’s lifestyle and preferences would be needed. Most molecular epidemiological studies on salt intake and hypertension have focused on salt sensitivity. Only two reports have paid attention to genetic factors in salt restriction behavior.6,7 In these studies, however, the authors did not search for genetic factors related to salt restriction, but instead evaluated the effects of hypothetical genetic information regarding salt sensitivity on salt restriction behavior.

In the present study, we attempted to identify genetic factors associated with salt restriction behavior in hypertensive patients. We selected candidate single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to genes involved in blood pressure regulation, salt metabolism, and neurotransmission as genes that may be related to salt preference.

METHODS

Study population

The Japan Multi-Institutional Collaborative Cohort Study (J-MICC Study)8,9 is a large-scale cohort molecular epidemiological study in Japan that was launched in 2004. In the baseline survey, approximately 93,000 local residents aged 35 to 69 years were recruited from 14 areas. After giving written informed consent for participation, they provided blood samples and health checkup data, and responded to a questionnaire concerning their lifestyle habits. The study protocol of J-MICC Study was approved by ethical committees of Aichi Cancer Center, Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine, and other participating institutions. We used the existing dataset for a cross-sectional study that included 4,519 subjects selected from among the participants in the baseline survey.10 Approximately 500 to 600 respondents were recruited consecutively in eight study areas of the J-MICC Study and fewer participants were enrolled in additional two areas of the study. Details of the dataset have been described previously.10 The inclusion criteria of the present study were the following: i) both blood pressure data from the health checkup and SNP data were available; and ii) participants had hypertension (systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, and/or the use of antihypertensive drugs). Residents of Amami and Ishigaki islands were excluded, because they had genetic backgrounds that differed considerably from those of the participants in the other study areas. Participants from the Fukuoka area were also excluded because the area’s questionnaire did not include a question about salt restriction behavior. Consequently, 817 individuals who met these criteria were included in the present study.

SNPs

We selected candidate SNPs related to genes involved in blood pressure regulation, salt metabolism, and neurotransmission. The SNPs were genotyped using the multiplex polymerase chain reaction-based Invader assay11 (Third Wave Technologies, Madison, WI, USA) at the Laboratory for Genotyping Development, Center for Genomic Medicine, RIKEN. Twenty-one SNPs satisfying all of the following conditions were analyzed: i) related to genes involved in blood pressure regulation, salt metabolism, or neurotransmission; ii) minor allele frequency of 5% or more; iii) data available in the J-MICC cross-sectional study dataset (Table 1). We confirmed that all of the candidate SNPs were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium by the chi-square goodness-of-fit test.

Lifestyle factors

Salt restriction status was judged based on self-reported answers (yes/no) to the question “Do you restrict your salt intake in everyday meals?” Smoking and drinking habits were divided into three categories, ie, never, past, and current. Regarding the association between socio-economic status and salt restriction, Miyaki et al have reported that educational background and annual income were significantly associated with salt intake.12 Since information on annual income was not available in the J-MICC Study, we considered only the educational background, and divided the respondents into three categories in accordance with the study by Miyaki et al12: education of less than 12 years (elementary school or junior high school), 12 to 15 years (high school, vocational school, junior college, or technical college), and 16 years or more (university or graduate school). Total energy intake was estimated based on a validated food frequency questionnaire.13,14 The average blood pressure was calculated using the following equation:

Diastolic blood pressure + (Systolic blood pressure – Diastolic blood pressure)/3.

Statistical analysis

Differences in the distribution of continuous variables and categorical variables between groups of salt-restriction statuses were examined by unpaired t-test and chi-squared test for independence, respectively. Multiple logistic regression analyses were performed with per-allele or additive models in which the salt restriction status was the objective variable, and the minor allele number of each SNP was the explanatory variable. The age and average blood pressure (continuous variables) as well as sex, education level, smoking habit, drinking habit, study area, and four comorbid diseases (ie, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, angina/myocardial infarction, and stroke) (categorical variables) were adjusted as confounding factors. Regarding sex, in addition to the introduction of interaction term with SNP, a stratified analysis was also performed. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding individuals who had high blood pressure during the health checkup but were not diagnosed with hypertension and did not use antihypertensive drugs (336 subjects; Group A in Figure 1). We did not consider significance level correction for multiple comparisons, because we selected candidate SNPs based on mechanism-based hypotheses; thus, a P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical examinations were performed using STATA ver 17 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Conditions associated with hypertension among the study subjects

The subjects (total 817) were classified into the three conditions associated with hypertension: i) high blood pressure during the health checkup (Groups A, D, E, and G, 600 subjects); ii) diagnosis of hypertension (Groups B, D, F, and G, 452 subjects); and iii) antihypertensive drug use (Groups C, E, F, and G, 416 subjects).

RESULTS

General characteristics of the study subjects

Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the study subjects stratified by salt restriction status. Five hundred and sixty-eight patients (69.5%) reported that they restricted their salt intake (salt restriction group; 303 males and 265 females), whereas 249 patients reported that they did not (no salt restriction group; 173 males and 76 females). Females were significantly more likely to report their salt restriction behavior than males (P < 0.001). Body height, body weight, body mass index, and diastolic blood pressure were significantly higher in the no salt restriction group. The use of antihypertensive medicine was more common in the salt restriction group, which may be one of the reasons why the blood pressure was lower in this group (diastolic blood pressure, P = 0.011; systolic blood pressure, P = 0.139). Current smokers were more prevalent in the no salt restriction group. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of drinking habit and educational attainment.

Figure 1 shows the conditions associated with hypertension among the participants. High blood pressure during the health checkup was observed in 600 subjects. Among the participants, 336 had neither been diagnosed with hypertension nor had taken antihypertensive medication. Of the 452 individuals who had been diagnosed with hypertension, 387 were receiving antihypertensive medication, and 65 were not receiving the medication. All of the participants who were not taking antihypertensive medication had high blood pressure values in the baseline survey. Among those taking antihypertensive drugs (n = 416), 217 (52.2%) had normal blood pressure in the baseline survey.

Among the 21 SNPs, rs1167998 in the dedicator of cytokinesis 7 (DOCK7) gene showed the smallest P-value (P = 0.007 among the total patients; P = 0.032 among males). The ORs for the SNP were similar in males and females (OR = 1.46), although the OR in females was not statistically significant (P = 0.150). Also, rs2970847 in the PPARgamma coactivator 1 alfa gene showed a statistically significant association with salt restriction behavior.

Sensitivity analysis

This study also included participants who exhibited high blood pressure (ie, systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mm Hg) during the health checkup. Notably, people who have never been diagnosed with hypertension may be less likely to take daily blood pressure control measures, including restricting salt intake. Therefore, as a sensitivity analysis, we performed a similar investigation of the 481 participants who had been diagnosed with hypertension or were taking antihypertensive drugs (Figure 1, Groups B–G), excluding the 336 subjects who only had high blood pressure at the time of measurement (Figure 1, Group A). The results are shown in Table 4. The associations between salt restriction behavior and both rs1167998 and rs2970847 that were significant in the previous analysis remained significant in this analysis. In particular, the association between rs1167998 and salt restriction behavior strengthened. Additionally, associations with rs5186 and rs8192678, which were not significant in the previous analysis, became significant in this narrowed analysis. The minor allele frequency of rs5186 was low, and all of the females with one or more minor alleles (ie, heterozygote or minor homozygote) engaged in salt-restricting behavior. Therefore, the OR could not be calculated. The effect of rs5186 must be verified in a larger population with clinically diagnosed hypertension. On the other hand, rs8192678, which is located in the PPARgamma coactivator 1 alfa gene, showed a weak linkage disequilibrium with rs2970847, which had shown a significant association in the previous analysis. The linkage disequilibrium TAG SNP Selection tool of the SNPinfo Web Server15 (Genotype Data, HapMap; Population, JPT [Japanese in Tokyo]) indicated an r2 value of 0.558 between the two SNPs.

Characteristics of rs1167998

Of the 21 SNPs that examined an association with salt restriction behavior in Table 3, we focused on rs1167998, because it showed the smallest P-value. We investigated its characteristics using public databases. According to the Human Genetic Variation Database,16 rs1167998 is an intron SNP of the DOCK7 gene that is strongly associated with the expression of DOCK7 (P = 6.535 × 10–11 for information on expression quantitative trait locus). In the cerebellar hemisphere, the expression of DOCK7 is low in those with a minor allele (C) of rs1167998 (Figure 2).17 DOCK7 protein is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor that plays a role in axon formation and neuronal polarization (GeneCards—The Human Gene Database).18 Strong linkage disequilibrium relationships were observed between rs1167998 and most of the other SNPs present in the DOCK7 gene region.15

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we attempted to identify genetic factors related to salt preference in individuals with hypertension and found two SNPs that may be related to salt restriction behavior. Among the 21 SNPs evaluated, rs1167998, an SNP in the DOCK7 gene that has been reported to be related to blood lipid levels and cardiovascular disease risk, showed the strongest association.

The mechanism(s) underlying how DOCK7 affects salt restriction behavior is unknown at this time. One possibility is that decreased expression of DOCK7 reduces the ability of neurons to form axons, and decreased axon formation reduces synaptogenesis, resulting in reduced efficiency in processing signals from salt receptors. Thus, it is possible that individuals with a decreased expression level of the gene might become insensitive to salt deficiency, leading to a reduced salt preference and to salt restriction behavior. If DOCK7 polymorphism is associated with the efficiency of signal transduction in the brain, it may have a wide range of effects on brain function and development, not just on salt preference. Interestingly, an amino acid substitution in DOCK7 has been reported in patients with epileptic encephalopathy,19 suggesting that DOCK7 polymorphism may indeed affect brain function and development.

Several genome-wide association studies have reported that some SNPs in DOCK7, including rs1167998, are associated with the blood lipid level and/or cardiovascular disease risk (rs1168124 and rs6686331 with lipid levels [Varga et al]20; rs1748195 and rs12563308 with cardiovascular diseases [Li et al]21; and rs1167998 with lipid levels [Aulchenko et al]22). The r2 value between rs1748195 reported by Li et al21 and rs1167998 was 1.000 (calculated using the SNPinfo Web Server15 and HapMap genotype data [Tokyo]), which indicates a strong linkage disequilibrium relationship. In addition, regarding the association between salt intake and the blood lipid level, an excess intake of salt has been reported to increase the absorption of cholesterol in the intestine.23 An increased serum cholesterol level promotes lipid deposition in the artery walls as well as platelet aggregation via an increased intracellular calcium level in platelets.21 Aulchenko et al have reported a relationship between the minor allele of rs1167998 and a decreased blood triglyceride level.22 The correlation between the rs1167998 genotype and salt restriction behavior observed in this study may be consistent with the previous finding that the SNP is correlated with blood lipids and cardiovascular disease risk because an excess intake of salt may accelerate the absorption of cholesterol.23

One SNP (rs2970847) in the PPARgamma coactivator 1 alfa gene also showed a statistically significant association with salt restriction behavior. PPARgamma coactivator 1 alfa is thought to increase the flexibility of blood vessels,24 but the mechanism by which this or other functions lead to the behavior of refraining salt intake is currently unknown.

There are several limitations to this study. First, because salt restriction behavior was assessed based on a self-reported questionnaire, the possibility of some misclassification cannot be excluded. However, relationships between SNPs and misclassified salt restriction status are unlikely. Therefore, the proportion of participants who were misclassified on the outcome does not depend on the genotype; specifically, such misclassifications may be nondifferential. Except in extreme cases, most nondifferential misclassifications of outcome result in association estimates being biased toward the null.25 Thus, the actual association is likely stronger than that observed in this study. Second, no objective measurement data on salt intake, such as Na+ excretion in urine, were available, so we could not confirm whether the salt intake was actually lower in the salt restriction group when compared to the no salt restriction group. Third, because this study was a cross-sectional one, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded. However, since it is clear that the effects of polymorphisms temporally preceded the occurrence of outcomes, reverse causality is unlikely even in cross-sectional studies. Fourth, the candidate SNPs were selected based on previous reports that have supported associations with blood pressure regulation, salt metabolism, and neurotransmission. We cannot deny the possibility that SNPs other than those selected are associated with salt restriction behavior. In addition to these limitations, the relatively small number of participants in this study, and the possible presence of unmeasured confounding factors warrant the necessity for further research.

CONCLUSION

We identified two SNPs that correlated with salt restriction behavior in individuals with hypertension. Among the 21 SNPs evaluated, rs1167998, an SNP in the DOCK7 gene that has been reported to be associated with blood lipid levels and cardiovascular disease risk, showed the strongest association with salt restriction behavior.
