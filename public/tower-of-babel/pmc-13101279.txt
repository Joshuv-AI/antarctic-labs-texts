Cone-Beam Computed Tomography-Based Three-Dimensional Phenotypes of Skeletal Class II Malocclusion in Yemeni Adults: A Principal Component and Cluster Analysis
Authors: Salah M Ben Hafedh, Ghamdan Al-Harazi, Ramy Abdulrahman Ishaq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsSkeletal Class II malocclusion is heterogeneous, and conventional two-dimensional cephalometry may not fully capture relevant three-dimensional (3D) craniofacial variation. This study aimed to identify 3D skeletal phenotypes of Class II malocclusion in Yemeni adults using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and multivariate analysis.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included CBCT scans of 120 Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion (56 males, 64 females; age range 16–30 years; mean 23.1 ± 3.5 years) retrieved from the archives of the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Sana’a University. CBCT-derived cephalometric measurements were obtained from lateral reconstructions using Dolphin Imaging (version 12.0 Premium). Twenty prespecified skeletal variables were standardised and analysed by principal component analysis (PCA). Seven components were retained and entered into Ward’s hierarchical cluster analysis. Introduction

Skeletal Class II malocclusion is a prevalent sagittal discrepancy in orthodontic practice and encompasses a broad spectrum of craniofacial patterns involving the maxilla, mandible, and cranial base. Traditional classification systems (eg, Angle’s classification, Wits appraisal, A point–Nasion–B point [ANB] angle) provide useful but limited information because they predominantly capture the sagittal relationship and often ignore vertical, transverse, and dentoalveolar components.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 As a result, patients with similar ANB values may present with markedly different vertical patterns, ramus morphology, or dentoalveolar compensation, which have direct implications for treatment planning and stability.9, 10, 11

Conventional two-dimensional (2D) cephalometry, although well established, introduces geometric distortion and superimposition and cannot fully describe complex 3D structures.12, 13, 14, 15 CBCT overcomes many of these limitations by providing isotropic voxel data and enabling reconstruction of accurate 3D cephalometric measurements while maintaining acceptable radiation doses when used under appropriate indications.16, 17, 18, 19

Within the International Dental Journal (IDJ), recent CBCT-based work has emphasised the value of advanced morphologic analytics, including multilevel modelling of anatomical variation, reinforcing the role of standardised 3D imaging in contemporary craniofacial research.20 In parallel, recent IDJ cephalometric work has shown complex (including nonlinear) relationships between cephalometric parameters and perceived profile aesthetics, highlighting the need to interpret sagittal metrics in the broader facial context.21

In parallel, multivariate statistical techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering have been increasingly applied to craniofacial data to identify latent patterns and clinically meaningful phenotypes.11,22,23 PCA can reduce the dimensionality of multiple correlated measurements into a smaller set of orthogonal components that summarise key morphological trends, whereas cluster analysis groups patients into phenotypes sharing similar multivariate profiles. These techniques have been used to reframe malocclusion classification, to stratify treatment response, and to provide structured inputs or labels for emerging artificial intelligence (AI) systems.12,24, 25, 26, 27

Most existing craniofacial phenotyping studies have focused on Western or East Asian populations, leaving Middle Eastern populations under-represented despite known ethnic differences in craniofacial morphology and malocclusion distribution.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36 Yemen, in particular, has limited published CBCT-based craniofacial data, and population-specific normative information is scarce.34

Accordingly, this study aimed to:

1.Use CBCT-based cephalometric measurements to derive principal components summarising the 3D craniofacial morphology of Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion;

Use CBCT-based cephalometric measurements to derive principal components summarising the 3D craniofacial morphology of Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion;

2.Identify distinct multivariate phenotypic clusters using hierarchical cluster analysis; and

Identify distinct multivariate phenotypic clusters using hierarchical cluster analysis; and

3.Evaluate the discriminability of these phenotypes using canonical discriminant analysis.

Evaluate the discriminability of these phenotypes using canonical discriminant analysis.

By establishing 3D skeletal phenotypes in a Yemeni population, this work seeks to support individualised clinical decision-making and to provide structured, anatomically grounded labels that can be leveraged in future AI-based diagnostic workflows.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a retrospective observational study conducted in the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Sana’a University, Sana’a, Yemen. The research protocol entitled ‘Three-Dimensional Phenotypic Characterization of Class II Malocclusion in a Selected Group of Yemeni Population Using Cone-Beam Computed Tomography’ was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Sana’a University, Sana’a, Yemen (ethical approval reference number OR:19 November 2023; approval letter dated 27 November 2023). CBCT scans were obtained as part of routine diagnostic records. All participants (or their legal guardians, for subjects under 18 years) provided written informed consent for the use of their anonymised CBCT data for research purposes. Data were de-identified before analysis and handled in accordance with institutional policies and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample selection

CBCT scans were retrieved from the departmental archives. Records were screened according to the following inclusion criteria (as documented in the original protocol):

1.Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion based on ANB angle and Wits appraisal consistent with Class II sagittal discrepancy;

Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion based on ANB angle and Wits appraisal consistent with Class II sagittal discrepancy;

2.Availability of high-quality CBCT scans, including the entire craniofacial complex;

Availability of high-quality CBCT scans, including the entire craniofacial complex;

3.No previous orthodontic or orthognathic treatment;

No previous orthodontic or orthognathic treatment;

4.Complete cephalometric records suitable for 3D measurement.

Complete cephalometric records suitable for 3D measurement.

Exclusion criteria included craniofacial syndromes, cleft lip/palate, severe facial asymmetry, history of trauma or surgery affecting the craniofacial skeleton, and CBCT scans with motion artefacts or inadequate field of view (FOV).

After applying these criteria, 120 CBCT scans were retained for analysis (n = 120; 56 males, 64 females). All subjects were recorded as adults at the time of imaging; the precise age distribution (aged range: 16-30 years; mean aged: 23.1 ± 3.5 years).

CBCT acquisition protocol

All CBCT scans were acquired using a PaX-FleX 3D P2 system (version 1.0.0, VATECH Co., Ltd) following a standardised craniofacial protocol. The patient’s head was positioned with the Frankfort horizontal plane parallel to the floor and the midsagittal plane perpendicular to the floor. Subjects were instructed to occlude in maximum intercuspation and maintain relaxed lips and natural head position during exposure.

Exposure parameters (FOV, voxel size, kVp, mA, and exposure time) followed the manufacturer’s recommended craniofacial settings and were kept constant across patients to ensure comparability (FOV 16 × 9 cm, voxel size 0.2 mm, 90 kVp, 4 mA, 12 s).

CBCT-based cephalometric variables

3D cephalometric analysis was performed on CBCT-derived lateral reconstructions using Dolphin Imaging software (version 12.0 Premium; Dolphin Imaging & Management Solutions). Standardised craniofacial landmarks were identified according to established cephalometric definitions, including Sella, Nasion, A-point, B-point, Gnathion, Gonion, and other key skeletal and dental points described in classical cephalometric literature.2,3,5, 6, 7, 8

Data preprocessing and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp). The analytical workflow consisted of three main steps:

1.Principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction;

Principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction;

2.Hierarchical cluster analysis to derive craniofacial phenotypes; and

Hierarchical cluster analysis to derive craniofacial phenotypes; and

3.Canonical discriminant analysis (CDA) to evaluate cluster separation and classification performance.

Canonical discriminant analysis (CDA) to evaluate cluster separation and classification performance.

Continuous variables were inspected for outliers and data-entry errors. Measurements were standardised (z-scores) before PCA and clustering to remove scale effects and to ensure equal weighting of variables. No imputation was performed because scans with incomplete or unusable measurements were excluded during sample selection as part of the quality control process.

Reliability analysis

All landmarks and measurements were identified by a single calibrated examiner. To assess intraexaminer reliability, all CBCT scans (n = 120) were re-measured after a two-week interval under identical conditions. Reliability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC; two-way mixed-effects, absolute agreement, single measurement: ICC(A,1)) with 95% confidence intervals, and random method error was quantified using Dahlberg’s formula (ME = √(Σd²/(2n))).37,38

Principal component analysis

PCA was performed on the 20 prespecified skeletal variables (correlation matrix, z-standardised inputs). Component retention was guided by the scree plot (inflexion point), Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue >1), and clinical interpretability. Seven components were retained and carried forward as component scores for clustering.

Cluster analysis

Ward’s hierarchical clustering (squared Euclidean distance) was applied to the retained PCA component scores to reduce collinearity and cluster on the principal axes of skeletal variation. The target number of clusters was determined by triangulating dendrogram structure, agglomeration coefficients, average silhouette, and clinical interpretability; a five-cluster solution was selected.

Canonical discriminant analysis

Canonical discriminant analysis and linear discriminant classification were used for internal validation of the cluster solution. Retained PCA component scores served as predictors, and cluster membership served as the grouping variable. Overall classification accuracy was estimated using leave-one-out cross-validation.

Sex-based considerations: cluster distribution by sex was summarised using a cluster-by-sex frequency table and evaluated using chi-square testing (α = 0.05).

Sample size considerations

No formal a priori power calculation was performed because the study used an available CBCT archive. For multivariate modelling, sample adequacy was addressed by restricting PCA inputs to 20 skeletal variables (subject-to-variable ratio 120:20 = 6:1), consistent with commonly cited recommendations (≥5-10 subjects per variable) for stable factor extraction and downstream clustering.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

Using CBCT-based cephalometric measurements and a PCA–cluster analysis workflow, this study identified five distinct skeletal Class II phenotypes in Yemeni adults. Vertical patterns and sagittal jaw relationships emerged as the dominant dimensions of craniofacial variation, while dentoalveolar compensation played a secondary but important role. The identified clusters ranged from mild bimaxillary retrusion with balanced vertical proportions to more severe forms combining maxillary protrusion, mandibular retrusion, and hyperdivergent patterns.

These findings support the concept that skeletal Class II malocclusion is a heterogeneous entity that cannot be fully captured by single sagittal indices such as ANB or Wits alone and that multivariate, 3D approaches can reveal clinically meaningful subgroups.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous 2D and CBCT-based studies have described multiple craniofacial patterns within Class II malocclusion, often differentiating maxillary protrusion, mandibular retrusion, combined discrepancies, and various vertical profiles.7,9, 10, 11,28,39The phenotypes identified in this Yemeni cohort are broadly consistent with these reports, particularly the presence of clusters dominated by mandibular retrusion (Clusters 2 and 5), clusters with pronounced maxillary protrusion and vertical excess (Cluster 4), and more balanced but mildly retrusive patterns (Cluster 1).

The multivariate structure observed here, with vertical and sagittal components explaining the largest proportion of variance, mirrors findings from other PCA-based craniofacial analyses.11,22,23 However, the exact combination of skeletal and dentoalveolar features in each phenotype reflects the specific characteristics of this Yemeni population, in line with previous observations that ethnic background influences craniofacial norms and malocclusion patterns.34

2. Risk assessment and prognosis:

Hyperdivergent phenotypes (eg, Cluster 4) are often associated with increased treatment complexity, higher risk of open bite, and greater demands on anchorage and vertical control, whereas low-angle phenotypes may present different biomechanical challenges.

3. Patient communication:

Visualising and categorising complex 3D skeletal patterns into a limited number of clinically intuitive phenotypes can facilitate the explanation of the malocclusion to patients and improve shared decision-making.

Recent evidence also suggests that relationships between cephalometric measurements and perceived profile aesthetics can be nonlinear and assessor-dependent, reinforcing the need to interpret sagittal metrics within an overall facial context.21

4. Implications for future computational modelling

Although no AI or machine-learning model was developed in this study, the derived clusters provide a structured and clinically interpretable way to summarise heterogeneity within skeletal Class II malocclusion. Such phenotypic descriptors may be useful as outcome labels or stratification variables in future computational studies using CBCT datasets, provided that they are externally validated and their reliability is established.

Future work could evaluate whether automated systems (eg, landmark detection or direct volumetric feature learning) can reproduce these phenotypes across scanners, populations, and clinical settings, and whether phenotype-aware models improve treatment planning compared with traditional sagittal indices alone.

In the current manuscript, we therefore restrict our claims to the statistical phenotyping findings and their clinical interpretability.

Population-specific considerations and generalizability

The study focuses on Yemeni adults treated at a single academic centre. Prior research has shown that Middle Eastern populations may differ in craniofacial morphology from Western and East Asian norms.33, 34, 35 The phenotypes reported here therefore provide a population-specific reference that can inform diagnosis and treatment planning in Yemen and potentially in similar regional populations.44

At the same time, the absence of a control group (eg, Class I or other malocclusion classes) limits direct comparisons with “normal” or alternative skeletal patterns. Consequently, the phenotypes should be interpreted as relative subdivisions within Class II, rather than as absolute classifications across all malocclusions.

Recent articles in the International Dental Journal further highlight the value of CBCT-derived multivariate measurement frameworks. Pukkawanna et al.20 demonstrated that multilevel modelling of CBCT-derived morphology can identify predictors of anatomical variation, supporting the broader use of multivariable approaches when interpreting complex craniofacial datasets. Similarly, Pornsirianand et al.21 reported nonlinear (quadratic–linear) relationships between cephalometric variables and profile aesthetics, underscoring that clinically meaningful outcomes are rarely explained by single sagittal indices alone. Together, these findings align with our rationale for phenotype-based classification that integrates multiple skeletal dimensions.

Conclusions

Within a sample of Yemeni adults with skeletal Class II malocclusion, CBCT-based PCA and cluster analysis identified five distinct 3D skeletal phenotypes that differ in maxillomandibular sagittal relationships, vertical pattern, ramus morphology, and dentoalveolar compensation.

These findings confirm the heterogeneity of skeletal Class II malocclusion and highlight the limitations of relying solely on single sagittal indices such as ANB and Wits appraisal. Recognising phenotype-specific patterns may support more individualised treatment planning and risk assessment.

Moreover, the multivariate workflow presented here yields structured, anatomically meaningful phenotypes that can serve as high-quality labels for future AI-based diagnostic systems and for integrative research linking craniofacial morphology, genetics, and treatment outcomes.40, 41, 42, 43
