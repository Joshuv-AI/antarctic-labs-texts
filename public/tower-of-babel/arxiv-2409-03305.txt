Derangements in non-Frobenius groups
Daniele Garzoni

Abstract
. We prove that if G G is a transitive permutation group of sufficiently large degree n n , then either G G is primitive and Frobenius, or the proportion of derangements in G G is larger than 1 / ( 2 ​ n 1 / 2 ) 1/(2n^{1/2}) . This is sharp, generalizes substantially bounds of Cameron–Cohen and Guralnick–Wan, and settles conjectures of Guralnick–Tiep and Bailey–Cameron–Giudici–Royle in large degree. We also give an application to coverings of varieties over finite fields.



1.
Introduction

Given a finite transitive permutation group
G
on a set
\Omega
of size
n\geq 2
, an element
g\in G
is called a
derangement
if it acts without fixed points on
\Omega
.

Chebotarev density theorem establishes a direct link between derangements and rational points of varieties over finite fields. In particular, derangements arise naturally in arithmetic geometry and were apparent already in the work of Frobenius in the nineteenth century. Derangements have other applications, for example to problems of random generation (see
[
LP93
,
EFG16
,
Luc18
,
GM23
]
) and to the structure of Brauer groups (see
[
FKS81
]
).

Denote by
\delta(G,\Omega)
, or simply
\delta(G)
, the proportion of derangements of
G
on
\Omega
. A lemma going back to Jordan asserts that
\delta(G)>0
, and giving lower bounds for
\delta(G)
has been a central problem in group theory in the past three decades;
see
Corollary

1.3
,
Section

1.1
and
[
BG16
]
for context related to this problem.

Answering a question of Lenstra motivated by the number field sieve (see
[
BLP93
, Section 9]
), Cameron–Cohen
[
CC92
]
showed that
\delta(G)\geq 1/n
; more precisely, if
n\geq 3
then one of the following holds:

(i)

G
is a Frobenius group of order
n(n-1)
and
\delta(G)=1/n
.

(ii)

\delta(G)>1/n
.

The proof of this result is very short and elementary (see also
[
BDF+93
]
, which further simplifies the proof). See below for the definition of a Frobenius group. Later, Guralnick–Wan
[
GW97
]
showed that if
n\geq 7
then one of the following holds:

(i)

G
is a Frobenius group of order
n(n-1)/a
and
\delta(G)=a/n
, where
a\in\{1,2\}
.

(ii)

\delta(G)>2/n
.

The proof of this result is much harder than that of
[
CC92
]
– it requires, besides some nontrivial elementary arguments, the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.

The groups attaining the bounds
\delta(G)=1/n
and
2/n
are very special: they are primitive Frobenius groups. We recall that a transitive permutation group
G
is called
Frobenius
if some nontrivial element fixes a point, and only the identity fixes at least two points. A classical result of Frobenius, based on character theory, states that
G
contains a regular normal subgroup, which is furthermore nilpotent by a result of Thompson.

If
G
is Frobenius, then
|G|=n(n-1)/a
for a divisor
a
of
n-1
, and
\delta(G)=a/n
. There are infinitely many examples attaining this bound; for instance, for every prime power
n
and every divisor
a
of
n-1
, consider
G=\mathbf{F}_{n}\rtimes G_{0}
where
G_{0}\leq\mathbf{F}_{n}^{\times}
has order
(n-1)/a
.
In particular, we find infinitely many examples with

\delta(G)=\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\frac{3}{n},\frac{4}{n}\ldots

Given the exceptionality of Frobenius groups, it is natural to ask whether a considerably stronger lower bound holds in the other cases.
The purpose of this paper is to answer this question in large degree, by showing that a bound of approximately
1/(2n^{1/2})
holds. What is more, it is sufficient to exclude groups that are both primitive and Frobenius in order to get this improvement.
Let

(1.1)

g(n):=\frac{n^{1/2}+1}{2n}\sim\frac{1}{2n^{1/2}}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a finite transitive permutation group of sufficiently large degree
n
. Then one of the following holds:

(1)

G
is primitive and Frobenius.

(2)

\delta(G)\geq g(n)
.

The crux of this paper is the case of primitive affine groups; see
Section

1.1
and
Theorem

1.6
for details.

There are primitive non-Frobenius groups attaining equality in
Theorem

1.1
(2); take

G=\mathbf{F}_{n}\rtimes G_{0}
, where
n
is a square,
G_{0}=\mathrm{GL}_{1}(n)\rtimes\langle\sigma\rangle
and
\sigma
is the
n^{1/2}
-th power map (see
Remark

5.7
).

Theorem

1.1
generalizes substantially the aforementioned bounds of
[
CC92
,
GW97
]
. What is more, in
[
GT03
, p. 272]
, Guralnick–Tiep conjectured that
\delta(G)\geq 1/n^{1/2}
if
G
is primitive affine and non-Frobenius.
Theorem

1.1
, in particular, confirms (up to a necessary minor amendment) this conjecture in large degree, showing also that the primitivity assumption is not needed.

Let
D(G)
be the subgroup of
G
generated by all derangements. Bailey–Cameron–Giudici–Royle
[
BCGR21
, Conjecture 1]
conjectured that if
G
is not primitive Frobenius then
|G:D(G)|\leq n^{1/2}-1
, and reduced the statement to the case of primitive affine groups. We clearly have

(1.2)

|G:D(G)|\leq\frac{1}{\delta(G)},

hence by
Theorem

1.1
we get
|G:D(G)|\leq 1/g(n)\sim 2n^{1/2}
for
n
large. In fact, in most cases we will prove
\delta(G)>1/(n^{1/2}-1)
, so the conjecture will follow at once by
Equation

1.2
. In the remaining cases, the bound will be easily obtained by inspection of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, so we deduce
[
BCGR21
, Conjecture 1]
in large degree.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite transitive permutation group of sufficiently large degree
n
. Then one of the following holds:

(1)

G
is primitive and Frobenius.

(2)

|G:D(G)|\leq n^{1/2}-1
.

Next, we state a consequence of
Theorem

1.1
that highlights the aforementioned connection between derangements and arithmetic.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to Y
be a separable morphism of sufficiently large degree
n
between integral normal quasi-projective varieties of dimension
d
over a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}
. Assume that the Galois closure of
\pi
is geometrically integral, and that the monodromy group of
\pi
is not primitive and Frobenius. Then,

|\pi(X(\mathbf{F}_{\ell}))|\leq\big(1-g(n)\big)\ell^{d}+O_{\pi}(\ell^{d-1/2}).

If
\pi
is defined by a polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(Y)[x]
, then the condition that the monodromy group is primitive and Frobenius can be phrased field theoretically as follows:
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(X)/\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(Y)
is minimal, and the Galois closure of
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(X)/\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(Y)
is not
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(X)
, and is generated by any two roots of
f
. Therefore the bound in
Corollary

1.3
applies whenever this (very restrictive) condition does not hold.

If the Galois closure of
\pi
(namely, the normalization of
X
in the Galois closure of
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(X)/\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(Y)
) is not geometrically integral, then one must more generally count derangements in a coset (indeed this is one of the motivations of
[
GW97
]
, which addresses the case of curves).

With regard to
Theorem

1.1
, it seems worth noting that one gets almost the same bound (approximately
1/(60n^{1/2})
) just by excluding primitive Frobenius subgroups of
\mathrm{A\Gamma L}_{1}(n)
, as opposed to all primitive Frobenius groups. Let

(1.3)

f(n):=\frac{n^{1/2}+1}{60n}\sim\frac{1}{60n^{1/2}}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a finite transitive permutation group of sufficiently large degree
n
. Then one of the following holds:

(1)

G
is a primitive Frobenius subgroup of
\mathrm{A\Gamma L}_{1}(n)
.

(2)

\delta(G)\geq f(n)
.

There are primitive (Frobenius) groups that do not lie in (1) and that attain equality in (2), of the form
G=\mathbf{F}_{n}^{2}\rtimes G_{0}
, where
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(5)\trianglelefteq G_{0}
; see
Remark

6.2
.

1.1.
About the proof

We prove an elementary reduction of
Theorem

1.1
to primitive groups, see
Lemma

2.1
.

Luczak–Pyber
[
LP93
]
and Fulman–Guralnick

[
FG18
]
proved the so-called
Boston–Shalev conjecture
, asserting that if
G
is a finite simple transitive permutation group of degree
n
, then
\delta(G)\geq\epsilon
for an absolute constant
\epsilon>0
. See also
[
EG23
]
for a conjugacy-class version. A consequence of this result is the following (see
[
FG03
]
):

Theorem 1.5
.

(Luczak–Pyber, Fulman–Guralnick)

Let
G
be a primitive non-affine permutation group of degree
n
. Then
\delta(G)\geq\epsilon/\log(n)
for an absolute constant
\epsilon>0
.

Of course, if
n
is sufficiently large then
\epsilon/\log(n)>g(n)
, and so in view of
Theorem

1.5
and
Lemma

2.1
, it remains to prove
Theorem

1.1
in the case where
G
is primitive affine, so
G=V\rtimes G_{0}
where
V
is elementary abelian and
G_{0}\leq\mathrm{GL}(V)
is irreducible.

In this case, there is an amusing phenomenon –
G
has many elements fixing no point of
V=\Omega
if and only if
G_{0}
has many elements fixing a point of
V\smallsetminus\{0\}
(see
Lemma

3.2
).

In particular, denoting by
\alpha(G_{0},V)
, or simply
\alpha(G_{0})
, the proportion of elements of
G_{0}
having eigenvalue
1
on
V
, our task is to lower bound
\alpha(G_{0})
. This is a well-studied problem, especially for quasisimple groups; see for example
[
GT03
,
SZ07
,
CZ21
,
Spi17
,
NP98
]
and the references therein. Many of these references are concerned with the so-called
unisingular
representations, which is to say, representations where
\alpha(G_{0})=1
.

The following theorem about irreducible linear groups implies
Theorems

1.1
,
1.2
and
1.4
for primitive affine groups. We work over any finite field, not necessarily of prime order. Let
A(G_{0},V)
or
A(G_{0})
be the subgroup of
G_{0}
generated by all elements with eigenvalue
1
, and let

(1.4)

h(n):=\frac{n^{1/2}+2}{2(n-1)}\sim\frac{1}{2n^{1/2}}.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
q
be a prime power, with
qd
sufficiently large. Let
V\cong\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and
G\leq\mathrm{GL}_{d}(q)
be irreducible, and
r
be maximal so that
G\leq\mathrm{\Gamma L}_{d/r}(q^{r})
. Then one of the following holds:

(1)

d=r
and
G
acts semiregularly on
V\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

(2)

G
acts semiregularly on
V\smallsetminus\{0\}
, and
\alpha(G)\geq 1/(60(q^{d/2}-1))
and
\delta(V\rtimes G)\geq f(q^{d})
.

(3)

\alpha(G)\geq h(q^{d})
,
\delta(V\rtimes G)\geq g(q^{d})
, and
|A(G)|/|G|\geq 1/(q^{d/2}-1)
.

(In (2) and (3),
\delta(V\rtimes G)=\delta(V\rtimes G,V)
refers to the affine action on
V
.) We already remarked that the equalities in the bounds for
\delta(V\rtimes G)
can be attained. The same is true for the bounds for
\alpha(G)
, see
Remarks

5.7
and
6.2
.

In most cases (e.g., for
d/r\geq 3
) we will prove the bound for
\alpha(G)
, and the required bound for
\delta(V\rtimes G)
will follow immediately.

In order to prove
Theorem

1.6
, we will reduce the problem to the case of primitive linear groups and exploit the powerful structure theory of these groups, see
Section

4
. The reduction is elementary (
Lemma

4.4
), so assume
G
is primitive. At this point, after some further reductions, we will focus on the generalized Fitting subgroup
L
(whose index in
G
is relatively small), and we will count elements with eigenvalue
1
in
L
. Assuming for simplicity that
L
acts absolutely irreducibly, we have
L=G_{1}\circ\cdots\circ G_{t}
, where
G_{i}
is either central or extraspecial or a power of a quasisimple group, and
V=V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{t}
. This will essentially reduce the problem to lower bounding
\alpha(H)
when
H\leq\mathrm{\Gamma L}_{1}(q)
(
Section

5
), or
H\leq\mathrm{\Gamma L}_{2}(q)
(
Section

6
), or
H
extraspecial or quasisimple (
Sections

7
and
8
).

A nice feature of the proof is that we will be able to prove
Theorem

1.6
only by working with large extraspecial or quasisimple groups (and in many cases, bounds much stronger than we need hold). Note, however, that in the central product
L=G_{1}\circ\cdots\circ G_{t}
, there may certainly be factors that have small order, and to which our bounds do not apply. In order to handle this issue, we will group together all the factors of small order (thereby paying a constant); what we gain in the factors of large order will be enough to compensate.

We wonder whether, in the notation of
Theorem

1.6
, the bound
\alpha(G)\gg 1/q^{r}
holds. There are many examples where a matching upper bound holds, and the lower bound holds if
d/r\leq 2
(by
Theorem

1.6
) and for many quasisimple groups in defining characteristic, see
Lemma

8.7
and also
[
GT03
]
. This problem could be considered as an analogue of the Boston–Shalev conjecture for affine groups over fields of bounded size.

Notation

We write
f=O(g)
or
f\ll g
if there exists a positive absolute constant
C
such that
f\leq Cg
. We number equations by sections, so for example
Theorem

1.1
is not interchangeable with (
1.1

2.
Reduction to primitive groups

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
Theorem

1.1
holds for primitive groups. Then,
\delta(G)>g(n)
for every imprimitive permutation group
G
of sufficiently large degree
n
.

In fact, the proof will show that if
G
is imprimitive then
\delta(G)>1/(1.5n^{1/2})
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{t}
be a maximal system of imprimitivity, with
|\Omega_{i}|=m\geq 2
and
t\geq 2
, let
\Delta=\{\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{t}\}
and
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{Sym}(\Delta)\cong S_{t}
. Then,
G^{\rho}
is primitive.

By
[
CC92
]
, we have
\delta(G,\Omega)\geq\delta(G^{\rho},\Delta)\geq 1/t
. If
t
is bounded this is
\gg 1
and we are done, so suppose that
t
is large.

Case 1:

G^{\rho}
is not Frobenius. Then, by
Theorem

1.1

\delta(G,\Omega)\geq\delta(G^{\rho},\Delta)\geq g(t)
. Since
n=mt\geq 2t
, we have
g(t)>g(n)
and we are done.

Case 2:

G^{\rho}
is Frobenius. We have
|G^{\rho}|=t(t-1)/a
for a divisor
a
of
t-1
, and
\delta(G^{\rho},\Delta)=a/t
.

Assume first
t/a<1.5n^{1/2}
. Then
a/t>1/(1.5n^{1/2})>g(n)
and we are done. Assume then
t/a\geq 1.5n^{1/2}
, i.e.,
t\geq 2.25ma^{2}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,t
, let
H_{i}=\text{Stab}_{G}(\Omega_{i})
, so
H_{i}
acts transitively on
\Omega_{i}
. Since
G^{\rho}
is Frobenius, a nontrivial element of
G^{\rho}
fixes at most one point. In particular,

\delta(G,\Omega)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\delta(H_{i},\Omega_{i})}{|G:H_{i}|}-\frac{1}{|G^{\rho}|}\right)=\delta(H_{1},\Omega_{1})-\frac{a}{t-1}.

Now fix
0<\epsilon<1-1/1.1\leq 1-1/(1.1a)
(e.g.,
\epsilon=0.09
) and take
t
large so that
(t-1)/t>1-\epsilon
. By
[
CC92
]
, we have
\delta(H_{1},\Omega_{1})\geq 1/m
, and the assumption
t\geq 2.2ma^{2}
implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}-\frac{a}{t-1}

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{m}-\frac{a}{(1-\epsilon)t}\geq\frac{1}{m}\left(1-\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon)2.2a}\right)

\displaystyle\quad>\frac{1}{2m}

\displaystyle\quad\quad>\frac{1.4}{2n^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad>g(n),

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.
Preliminaries for affine groups

Let
G
be a finite group and let
V
be an
\mathbf{F}_{q}G
-module of dimension
d<\infty
. Denote by
\pi
the permutation character of
G
acting on
V
, and define
\eta(G)=\eta(G,V)
as the inverse of the harmonic mean of
\pi
, i.e.,

(3.1)

\eta(G)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\pi(g)}.

Lemma 3.1
.

\delta(V\rtimes G)=1-\eta(G)
.

Proof.

Let
gv\in V\rtimes G
. We have
u^{gv}=ug+v
, so
u^{gv}=u
if and only if
v=u-ug
. It follows that
gv
is a derangement if and only if
v\notin[g,V]
. Let
c(g):=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{C}_{V}(g))
, so
\pi(g)=q^{c(g)}
. Since
[g,V]
is a subspace of
V
of dimension
d-c(g)
, and counting derangements by summing over all
g\in G
and all
v\not\in[g,V]
, we have

\delta(V\rtimes G)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{q^{d}-q^{d-c(g)}}{q^{d}}=1-\eta(G)

as wanted.
∎

Recall that
\alpha(G)
denotes the proportion of elements of
G
with eigenvalue
1
on
V
, and

A(G)
denotes the subgroup of
G
generated by all elements with eigenvalue
1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\alpha(G)\leq\delta(V\rtimes G)\leq\alpha(G).

Proof.

Writing
\alpha=\alpha(G)
and
\eta=\eta(G)
, note that

\frac{1}{|G|}\left(1-\alpha\right)|G|\leq\eta\leq\frac{1}{|G|}\left(\frac{\alpha|G|}{q}+(1-\alpha)|G|\right)=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\alpha.

Now just apply Lemma
3.1
.
∎

In particular, as already noticed in the introduction, in most cases in order to prove
Theorem

1.6
it will be sufficient to bound
\alpha(G)
.

The following is
[
BCGR21
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

|V\rtimes G:D(V\rtimes G)|=|G:A(G)|
.

Proof.

We have
V\leq D(V\rtimes G)
, and as noted in the proof of
Lemma

3.1
,
gv
is a derangement if and only if
v\not\in[g,V]
; therefore for
g\in G
there exists
v
such that
gv
is a derangement if and only if
g\in A(G)
.
∎

in the inequality in

will be useful in the proof of

for technical reasons.

In

, we do not assume that

Lemma 4.4
.

If
Theorem

1.6
holds when
G
is a primitive linear group, then it holds when
G
is an imprimitive linear group.

Proof.

Let
G\leq\mathrm{GL}_{m}(q)\wr S_{t}
preserve the decomposition
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{t}
, with
t\geq 2
, permuting transitively the factors, and assume the stabilizer
H
of
V_{1}
acts primitively on
V_{1}
. Clearly
|A(G)|/|G|\geq\alpha(G)
, and in all but one cases the bound for
\alpha(G)
will be enough also for

; we will not repeat this every time.

Note
\alpha(G)\geq\alpha(H,V_{1})/t
.
If
H
does not induce a semiregular group on

, then
\alpha(H,V_{1})\geq h(q^{m})>1/(2q^{m/2})
and so
\alpha(G)>1/(2tq^{m/2})\gg 1/q^{d/3}
and we are done (recall

). Assume then that
H

; then

and
\alpha(H,V_{1})=1/s
, and so
\alpha(G)\geq 1/(t(q^{m}-1))
. If
m\leq d/3
this is
\gg q^{d/3}
, so assume
m=d/2
.

For
i=1,2
, let
N_{i}
be the subgroup of
H
acting trivially on
V_{i}
. We have
|G|=2|N_{1}|s
. The elements of
H
with eigenvalue

are those of

,
so

\alpha(G)\geq\frac{2|N_{1}|-1}{2|N_{1}|s}\geq\frac{1}{2s}\geq\frac{1}{2(q^{d/2}-1)}.

Moreover,

by

, so the case where
G
is semiregular on

is done. Assume then
G

. If
|N_{1}|\geq 2
, then we can improve the second inequality in (

) and get
\alpha(G)\geq 3/(4(q^{d/2}-1))>h(q^{d})
. What is more, the nontrivial elements of

\delta(V\rtimes G)\geq 3/(4(q^{d/2}-1))\cdot(1-1/q^{d/2})>g(q^{d})
.
Note also that

, so

, and so the case where
|N_{1}|\geq 2
is done. Assume finally
N_{1}=1
, so
|G|=2s
. If
G

Lemma 5.4

Let

be a prime and assume

\equiv ar^{i+1}+1\pmod{r^{i+2}}

∎

Note that for

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

be an odd prime, let

The case

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

and assume

, with

\gamma_{2}((p^{2^{i}}-1)/(p-1))=i+j-1

By induction on

. The case

is the hypothesis. For

6.3.
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(q_{0})

We begin by recording a simple estimate for the proportion of elements in a coset of

Lemma 6.4
.

Let

If

∎

We turn then to the general case. Let

, with

\mathrm{SL}_{2}(q_{0})\leq G\leq\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{\Gamma L}_{2}(q)}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(q_{0}))=\mathrm{GL}_{2}(q_{0})Z\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle

where

,

Lemma 6.5

We have
\alpha(G)>3/(4q)
,
\delta(V\rtimes G)>9/(16q)

|A(G)|/|G|\geq 1/(q-1)

Proof.

Let

Let

fixing a vector in

. Assume that

projects onto

and consider any coset

. Assume that

projects onto
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbf{F}_{q}/\mathbf{F}_{r^{i}})

. By Shintani descent (see

Since

Next, we prove the lower bound to

. Note that in a coset

where

(6.1)

\delta(V\rtimes G,V)\geq\frac{|N|}{|G|}\sum_{i\mid m}\frac{\varphi(m/i)(r^{i}-1)}{s(i)r^{i}}\geq\frac{q_{0}-1}{(q-1)m}\sum_{i\mid m}\frac{\varphi(m/i)(r^{i}-1)}{s(i)r^{i}},

Assume then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\mid m}\frac{\varphi(m/i)(2^{i}-1)}{s(i)2^{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4q_{0}}+\frac{\varphi(m)}{m}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{3}{4q_{0}}\right)>\frac{3}{4q_{0}}.

In particular, we get from (

Proof of

In this section,

8.1.
Alternating groups and groups of Lie type in non-defining characteristic

We first isolate the case of the fully deleted permutation module for alternating groups.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
G=A_{m}
with

, and let

denote the fully deleted permutation module. Then,
\alpha(G,V)\geq 1-2(1+\log(m))/m
.

Proof.

Let

. It is easy to see that if

has at least three cycles, then

The proportion of elements of

(8.1)

\frac{2\left[m~\text{odd}\right]}{m}+2\left[m~\text{even}\right]\left(\sum_{1\leq i<m/2}\frac{1}{i(m-i)}+\frac{2}{m^{2}}\right),

where

denotes the indicator function.
Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<m/2}\frac{1}{i(m-i)}+\frac{2}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i<m/2}\left(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{m-i}\right)+\frac{2}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m-1}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\log(m)}{m},

where in the last inequality we used a standard upper bound for the partial sum of the harmonic series. The lemma follows then from this estimate and (

).
∎

Assume in the following lemma that either

with

, or

Assume first

, we may assume that

Assume now

By

Assume now

If

Denote by

Lemma 8.3

.

Proof.

Since

Next, we recall a result of Steinberg (see

This also gives an interpretation of the sentence “

Theorem 8.4
.

(Steinberg)

Assume that

. Then, the proportion of regular unipotent elements of

Let

The following is well-known.

Lemma 8.6
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let

be a Steinberg endomorphism of

, and let

be an irreducible

-representation. Assume that there exists a weight

Note that

Let

be a

-stable maximal torus, such that

. By

. Note that, since

is

-stable. Denote by

, which is

-stable. It is known that

. Then

is

-stable, and consists of elements having eigenvalue

Assume finally that

8.3.
Natural module for classical groups

In this subsection
V
is the natural module for a classical group
G
.
For orthogonal groups we assume
n
even, since for
n
odd every element of
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(q)
has eigenvalue
1
.

For
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(s),\mathrm{GU}_{n}(s),\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(s),\mathrm{O}^{\pm}_{2n}(s)
, Neumann–Praeger
[
NP98
]
gave estimates for the proportion of elements of
G
having a given eigenvalue, and showed that its limit as
n\to\infty
with
s
fixed exists (and, for example, in non-orthogonal groups it is
1/s+O(1/s^{2})
). See also
[
FGG24
]
for recent related results.

We prove an easy result that holds in each coset of
[G,G]
in
G
. Our result and proof are similar to
[
GT03
, Proposition 3.3]
, and in particular are an application of the inequality

(8.2)

\Big|\bigcup_{g\in G}H^{g}\Big|\leq\frac{|G|}{|\mathrm{N}_{G}(H):H|}

for
H\leq G
. In
[
GT03
]
, in fact, the authors are interested in semisimple elements with eigenvalue
1
, and nontrivial estimates for the proportion of semisimple elements of
G
are required. Here we do not need to focus on semisimple elements, which makes the analysis easier and in some cases allows to handle some more value of
s
(for
s\geq 3
the bounds below are meaningful). We give exact bounds (rather than asymptotic), as the proof offers them naturally and they might be useful.

Lemma 8.8
.

(1)

If

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(s)\leq G\leq\mathrm{GL}_{n}(s)
with
n\geq 2
then

\frac{1-\frac{1}{s-1}}{s-1}\leq\alpha(G,V)\leq\frac{1}{s-1}.

(2)

If
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(s)
with
n\geq 2
then

\frac{1-\frac{1}{s-1}}{s}\leq\alpha(G,V)\leq\frac{1}{s-1}.

(3)

If
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(s)\leq G\leq\mathrm{GU}_{n}(s)
with
n\geq 3
then

\frac{1-\frac{s}{s^{2}-1}}{s+1}\leq\alpha(G,V)\leq\frac{s}{s^{2}-1}.

(4)

If
G=\Omega^{\pm}_{2n}(s)
with
n\geq 4
and
s
odd then

\frac{s(1-\frac{2s}{s^{2}-1})}{s^{2}-1}\leq\alpha(G,V)\leq\frac{2s}{s^{2}-1}.

Proof.

All proofs are similar; we first prove the upper bound, and deduce the lower bound. The upper bounds in (1)–(3) are contained in
[
GT03
, Remark 3.4]
. (This is an easy calculation using (
8.2
), applied to the centralizer
H
of a
1
-space, singular or nondegenerate; in (1) and (3) the bounds are stated only for
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(s)
and
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(s)
but the same proof works for any
G
as in the statement.)
For the upper bound in (4), note that if an element
g
of
\Omega^{\pm}_{2n}(s)
,
s
odd, has eigenvalue
1
, then it fixes either a singular vector, or it centralizes a nondegenerate
2
-space of minus type. (In order to see this, assume that
vg=v
with
v
nonsingular. Letting
W=\langle v\rangle^{\perp}
, since
\mathrm{dim}(W)
is odd and
\operatorname{det}(g)=1
, there exists
0\neq w\in W
with
wg=w
. If
w
is singular we are done; otherwise
\langle v,w\rangle
is nondegenerate. If it is of minus type we are done again, and otherwise it contains a singular vector, done for the third and final time.)
The contributions of the two subgroups in (
8.2
) are, respectively,
1/(s-1)
and
1/(2(s+1))
; bounding
1/(2(s+1))<1/(s+1)
and summing the two contributions we get the desired inequality.

Now let us prove the lower bounds. In (1), for
n=2
we have
\alpha(G,V)\geq 1/s
,
so assume
n\geq 3
. We count elements that centralize a vector
v
, stabilize a complement and act without eigenvalue
1
there. Each such element acts without eigenvalue
1
on exactly one hyperplane (namely
[V,g]
), hence there are no repetitions. Using the upper bound that we just proved, the statement follows from an easy calculation.

The bounds in (2), (3) and (4) are proved in the same way. In (2), we count elements that are regular unipotent on a nondegenerate
2
-space and act without eigenvalue
1
on the perpendicular complement. (The proportion of regular unipotent elements of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(s)
is
1/s
.) In (3) we count elements that fix a nondegenerate vector and act without eigenvalue
1
on the perpendicular complement. In (4) we count elements acting trivially on a nondegenerate
2
-space (of any type) and without eigenvalue
1
on the orthogonal complement.
∎

The previous result is empty for linear and symplectic groups when
s=2
, and does not address orthogonal groups in even characteristic. (This latter exclusion depends on the fact that the centralizer
H
of a nonsingular vector is a maximal subgroup, and so (
8.2
) is useless.) For these cases, we record estimates that can be extracted from
[
NP98
]
.

Lemma 8.9
.

(1)

For
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}(2)
and
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(2)
we have
\alpha(G,V)>0.5
.

(2)

For
G=\Omega^{\pm}_{2n}(s)
,
s
even,
n\geq 4
, we have
\alpha(G,V)>1/s-1/s^{2}-1/s^{16}
.

Proof.

(1) Let us start from

. By
[
NP98
, Theorem 8.1]
we get that

|1-\alpha(G,V)-G(2,1)|\leq c(2)\cdot 2^{-6},

where

and

are defined in that paper. We have

by
[
NP98
, Lemma 2.2]
, and so the error term is at most

. Moreover,

is estimated in
[
NP98
, Theorem 4.4]
. Looking at the proof, we see that

\log(G(2,1))=-1-\sum_{k\geq 2}\left(\sum_{j\geq 2;j\mid k}\frac{1}{j}\right)2^{-k}.

In order to upper bound

.
The case of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(2)
, as well as (2), are very similar, and we skip the details.
∎
