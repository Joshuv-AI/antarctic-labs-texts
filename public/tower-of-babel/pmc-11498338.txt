Acro-osteolysis in primary hyperparathyroidism
Authors: Rimesh Pal, Ashish Dua, Sanjay Kumar Bhadada
Published: 2024

A 56-year-old man presented to our clinic for follow-up evaluation of renal colic. On examination, we noted the patient had short fingers with rounding of the fingertips suggestive of pseudoclubbing (Figure 1A). Laboratory investigations showed the following: hypercalcemia (calcium 2.8 [normal 2.2–2.5] mmol/L), hypophosphatemia (phosphate 0.6 [normal 0.8–1.4] mmol/L), renal dysfunction (creatinine 159.1 [normal 44.2–106.1] μmol/L), elevated intact parathyroid hormone (parathyroid hormone 3112 [normal 5–65] ng/L), sufficient 25-hydroxyvitamin D (86.5 [normal > 75.0] nmol/L), and raised total alkaline phosphatase (25.2 [normal 0.7–2.1] μkat/L). Abdominal ultrasonography showed calculi in the bilateral renal pelvis with hydronephrosis. Radiography showed resorption of the midshaft of all terminal phalanges (acro-osteolysis), subperiosteal bone resorption, and brown tumour (osteitis fibrosa cystica) (Figure 1B). Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry showed osteoporosis. These findings were consistent with primary hyperparathyroidism, and imaging studies (ultrasonography and sestamibi scan) showed an enlarged left inferior parathyroid gland. The lesion was excised, with histopathology indicating parathyroid adenoma.

(A) The hands of a 56-year-old man with primary hyperparathyroidism showing short fingers with rounding of the fingertips suggestive of pseudoclubbing. (B) Radiograph of bilateral hands showing acroosteolysis, subperiosteal resorption (arrowheads), and brown tumour (osteitis fibrosa cystica) (arrow).

Acro-osteolysis refers to resorption of the distal phalanx. The most common causes include connective tissue disorders (scleroderma and psoriatic arthritis), occupational exposures (polyvinyl chloride), injury (thermal burn), and Hajdu–Cheney syndrome.1 Long-standing hyperparathyroidism (primary, secondary, or tertiary) can also result in acro-osteolysis,2 but this is more often seen in secondary or tertiary renal hyperparathyroidism than in primary hyperparathyroidism. Excessive parathyroid hormone binds to osteoblasts, leading to differentiation and activation of osteoclasts.3 Activated osteoclasts lead to subperiosteal bone resorption that starts in the radial aspect of the middle phalange of the second finger in the dominant hand. Increased bone resorption also leads to acro-osteolysis with collapse of the bony structure of the terminal phalanges resulting in foreshortening of the digit and telescoping of the soft tissues, clinically manifesting as pseudoclubbing as in this patient. Successful treatment of hyperparathyroidism will prevent further progression but does not reverse acro-osteolysis.
