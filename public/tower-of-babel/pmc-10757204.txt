Cross‐kingdom regulation by plant‐derived miRNAs in mammalian systems
Authors: Linpu Yang, Han Feng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small noncoding RNA molecules ubiquitously distributed across diverse organisms, serving as pivotal regulators of genetic expression. Notably, plant‐derived miRNAs have been demonstrated to have unique bioactivity and certain stability in mammalian systems, thereby facilitating their capacity for cross‐kingdom modulation of gene expression. While there is substantial evidence supporting the regulation of mammalian cells by plant‐derived miRNAs, several questions remain unanswered. Specifically, a comprehensive investigation of the mechanisms underlying the stability and transport of plant miRNAs and their cross‐kingdom regulation of gene expression in mammals remains to be done. In this review, we summarized the origin, processing, and functional mechanisms of plant miRNAs in mammalian tissues and circulation, emphasizing their greater resistance to mammalian digestion and circulation systems compared to animal miRNAs. Additionally, we introduce four well‐known plant miRNAs that have been extensively studied for their functions and mechanisms in mammalian systems. By delving into these aspects, we aim to offer a fundamental understanding of this intriguing field and shed light on the complex interactions between plant miRNAs and mammalian biology.Keywords: cross‐kingdom regulation, plant‐derived microRNAs

INTRODUCTION

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), initially discovered in C. elegans and later in all kingdoms and even viruses, are 21‐ to 24‐nt‐long, highly conserved, endogenous transcripts from genomic sequences, usually residing far from the annotated genes but also in introns or even exons. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Plant and animal‐derived miRNAs show similarities and differences in terms of their location in the genome, biosynthesis processes, targeting strategies and regulation mechanisms (Figure 1). 5 In animals, a higher proportion of miRNAs are located in introns compared to that in plants. 5 These miRNA‐encoding sequences are transcribed by RNA polymerase II or III, resulting in the production of primary miRNAs (pri‐miRNAs) that are more than 1 kb long and have a hairpin structure. 4 In animals, the pri‐miRNA duplex is cleaved at the nucleus by Drosha RNase III endonuclease, generating a miRNA precursor of 60 ~ 70 nt (pre‐miRNA) with a 2 nt overhang at the 3′ end and a 5′ phosphate. 6 , 7 The pre‐miRNA undergoes cleavage mediated by the nuclear class II RNase III Drosha‐DGCR8 complex and is conveyed to the cytoplasm via exportin‐5. Within the cytoplasmic milieu, the molecule is further cleaved by the class III RNase III enzyme Dicer, producing a miRNA duplex spanning approximately 22 nucleotides in length. 1 , 8 In contrast, plants employ a different processing mechanism for pri‐miRNAs. Both steps of pri‐miRNA processing, i.e. the formation of pre‐miRNA and the generation of mature miRNA, are catalyzed by a single enzyme called DICER‐LIKE 1 (DCL1), which is a homolog of animal Dicer, and both processes occur in the nucleus. 9 In plants, 2′‐O‐methylation at the 3′ end of both miRNA strands is processed by the small RNA methyltransferase HUA ENHANCER1 (HEN1), providing molecular stability. 10 However, HEN1 is only responsible for the methylation of siRNA in Drosophila and piRNA (Piwi‐interacting) in human germ cells. 10 The miRNAs are subsequently transported through the nuclear pore with the assistance of HST. 11 Within the realms of both animals and plants, the duplex RNA encompassing the miRNA, as well as its complementary strand, termed miRNA*, undergoes subsequent integration into the Argonaute protein in the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC), enabling the complex to search and bind to target mRNA sequences. 8 , 12 miRNA* (and the complement strand of siRNA) are rarely assembled to form RISC and easily degraded, and the strand loaded to RISC is always the one with less pairing stability with its complement. 1

RISC silences gene expression by one of three pathways: at the translational level by repressing protein translation, at the transcriptional level by degrading mRNA, or at the genome level by the formation of heterochromatin or by DNA elimination. 12 Slicing of the target mRNA, the best understood pathway, requires perfect or nearly perfect complementary binding of the miRNA and its target, and is achieved by the “slicer” protein Argonaute. 13 Plant miRNAs are reported to have higher complementarity to their target genes (0 ~ 3 mismatches) than animals. 5 Repression of translation by miRNA is the most prevalent form of gene regulation in mammalian organisms and is considered the prerequisite of mRNA degradation. 12 , 14 Unlike slicing of mRNA, repression of translation by RISC only requires 2–7 bp pairing of miRNA and its target and is achieved by blocking the interaction of eukaryotic initiation factor (eIFs) 4E and 4G or deadenylation of mRNA. 1 The yeast RITS complex (RNA‐induced transcriptional silencing) promotes the formation of heterochromatin via recruitment, which is also observed in plants and animals. 3

In terms of gene silencing, the distinction between miRNAs and siRNAs does not arise from differences in their chemical composition or operational mechanisms, as their fundamental biogenesis pathways overlap and their biochemical roles can be interchanged. The distinctions can only be made from their origin, structure, evolutionary conservation and target genes. 1 , 3 The absence of sequence homology and the divergence in miRNA biogenesis pathways point towards the independent evolutionary development of miRNA regulation within both kingdoms. This evolution appears to have originated from an ancestral siRNA mechanism that was already established in the most recent common ancestor of all eukaryotic organisms. 15

More than 60% of protein‐coding genes in humans are regulated by miRNAs, and in particular, a single miRNA could regulate a spectrum of targets or the whole gene pathway, highlighting its great potential as a target of therapeutic design and drug development. 16 Newly emerging evidence indicates that miRNAs can also relocate to the nucleus, where they engage in the modulation of transcriptional efficiency for particular genes, further augmenting their impact on the intricate networks of cellular signaling. 17

Plant miRNAs play a significant role in governing various aspects of plant development, responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, as well as processes related to parasitism. 9 Interestingly, evidence has demonstrated that these plant‐derived miRNAs can be transferred into mammalian systems and function as gene regulators. This review focuses on cross‐kingdom regulation by plant‐derived miRNAs in mammalian cells and tissues, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms by which miRNAs are absorbed and maintained stable in circulation, unraveling their roles in regulating gene expression and modifying metabolic pathways, and providing a comprehensive summary of recent studies based on some well‐known miRNAs (Table 1).

Databases and tools for miRNA studies

Studies on miRNA are greatly facilitated by comprehensive and publicly accessible databases and prediction tools. 18 miRBase serves as the primary repository for miRNA sequences and associated annotations, providing researchers with a valuable resource to explore and investigate miRNAs. 19 , 20 In addition, MiREDiBase offers valuable information for miRNA editing events and modifications, as well as the connection between specific physiopathological conditions. 21

Target prediction tools designed for individual miRNAs, notably TargetScan and miRanda, have been developed; however, these tools can sometimes exhibit partial deficiencies in terms of specificity or sensitivity. 22 , 23 To enhance the reliability of predictions and to better comprehend the reciprocal influence of miRNAs on genetic pathways, a more comprehensive approach involves the integration of miRNA data with mRNA expression information. Prominent examples of such integrated tools encompass miRTarVis, MIENTURNET, miRNet, and miRTrail. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 The inclusion of a priori knowledge sourced from pathway databases serves to refine the precision of miRNA target predictions. 28 In a complementary fashion, the tool miRTargetLink2 was conceived to facilitate systematic investigations involving miRNAs and their associated target genes. This is achieved by amalgamating both verified and predicted targets, alongside target pathways and pertinent insights from established databases like miRPathDB2 or mirDIP. 29 , 30 , 31 Through these cohesive strategies, a more holistic comprehension of miRNA functions within the context of target genes can be attained.

TRANSPORTATION AND RESISTANCE OF PLANT miRNAs

Plant miRNAs are transported in exosome‐like nanoparticles or incorporated into soluble proteins

Valadi et al. 32 were the first to report the presence of RNAs within mast cell exosomes and their ability to be transferred from mouse to human cells. Through microarray analysis, they identified these transferred RNAs as mRNAs and miRNAs and further demonstrated their functional activity in recipient cells. 32 Intercellular communication through exogenous miRNAs was confirmed by various studies in the following years, including the presence of maternal miRNAs transferred to the placenta and the detection of tumor miRNAs in human serum. 5 , 33

Plant miRNAs can be transferred to adjacent cells, and this is facilitated by passive diffusion through plasmodesmata or regulated processes via phloem and by exosomes or exosome‐like vesicles. 3 , 15 Various miRNAs have been detected and identified from 11 fruits and vegetables encapsulated in exosome‐like nanoparticles (ELNPs), which show potential regulatory effects on human genes. 34

Circulating miRNAs remain stable through encapsulation within extracellular vesicles (EVs) or binding with soluble proteins. 35 AGO2, a RISC component, coprecipitated with miRNAs in the serum. 36 Alternative candidates include high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), which has been shown to regulate the transportation and targeting of exogenous and endogenous miRNAs in mice via scavenger receptor class B type I. 37

Consistent with the transport processes, plant miRNAs target recipient cells via endocytosis and membrane fusion of miRNA‐containing vesicles with the help of exosomal receptors. 35 It has been suggested that nonvesicular miRNAs can be transported into target cells through their binding complexes, such as HDL and AGO2. 35 , 38

Plant‐derived miRNAs show resistance to culinary and digestive processes

Plant miRNAs exhibit higher stability than animal miRNAs or their synthetic counterparts lacking modification due to 3′ end 2′‐O‐methylation, nanoparticle encapsulation, association with other proteins and the intrinsic characteristics of miRNAs. 35 , 39 Recent studies have provided evidence that plant‐derived miRNAs can be detected in vitro and in vivo even after undergoing various processes, such as storage, processing, cooking, or digestion, although to a significantly lower degree. 40 , 41 2′‐O‐methylation at the 3′ ends, a feature of plant miRNAs but not animal‐derived miRNAs, prevents 3′ uridylation and 3′ truncation and improves the resistance of miRNAs in digestive processes and circulation. 42 ELNPs containing miRNAs are reserved to 20% after cooking and display an altered shape. 41 Various studies have demonstrated the coprecipitation of plant miRNAs with RISC components, especially AGO2, forming a complex resistant to RNase in circulation. 43

Intrinsic characteristics of certain miRNAs also contribute to their stability. MIR2911 possesses exceptional stability after digestion compared to other plant miRNAs, such as MIR168a and its 2′‐O‐methylated synthetic counterparts, and this bioavailability does not account for exosomes or complexes with soluble proteins. 44 It has been suggested that high GC content and the presence of the stem fold structure in this miRNA may contribute to its enhanced stability in the digestive system and serum. 45

MECHANISMS OF PLANT miRNA CROSS‐KINGDOM REGULATION

miRNA can be administered through injection, inhalation schemes and intake via food. 18 Exogenous miRNAs in human plasma with resistance to RNaseA have been detected. These miRNAs are mainly from dietary intake, including Solanum lycopersicum (tomato), Glycine max (soybean), and Vitis vinifera (grapes), and, predominantly, staples such as Zea mays (corn) and Oryza sativa (rice). 46 Sequencing results from Zhang et al. 47 indicate that MIR168a and MIR159 are the most abundant miRNAs in human serum. MIR168a

Rice microRNA168a (osa‐miR168a or MIR168a) engages in a diverse array of biological processes, including the regulation of flowering time, grain yield, and seed vigor. This multifaceted influence arises from its specific interaction with the Argonaute 1 (AGO1) component of the RNA‐induced silencing complex. 48 Zhang et al. 47 in 2012 notably demonstrated the stability of MIR168a within human serum. In this study, they further illuminated that MIR168a directs its regulatory attention to the mRNA of the low‐density lipoprotein receptor adaptor protein 1 (LDLRAP1). This interaction led to a reduction in LDLRAP1 protein levels within the blood and liver of mice that were fed rice, resulting in elevated low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) levels in their plasma. 47 Intriguingly, Zhang et al. 47 also revealed that a considerable proportion of circulating MIR168a predominantly resides within microvesicles (MV), utilizing the mammalian Argonaute 2 (AGO2) protein to assemble the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC), to execute its functional roles. This pioneering research provided initial substantiation that plant‐derived miRNAs can traverse the gastrointestinal tract, enter systemic circulation, and perform regulatory functions across the plant–animal kingdom divide, thereby extending the boundaries of our understanding in this field, notwithstanding some controversial opinions, as mentioned below.

MIR156a

Evidence from Arabidopsis and tomato (S. lycopersicum) reveals that MIR156a prolongs the juvenile phase before the juvenile–adult transition, modulates flowering time and determines inflorescences and lateral branches by targeting and silencing the gene of the master transcription factor, SPL (SQUAMOSA‐promoter binding protein‐like) protein. 49 , 50 In mammalian systems, it has been shown that MIR156a, the most abundant miRNA in broccoli, targets junctional adhesion molecule A (JAM‐A). 51 The upregulation of JAM‐A is related to epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), increased migratory abilities, and poor prognosis in various types of tumors such as breast cancer. 52 Tian and colleagues showed that MIR156a repressed EMT in nasopharyngeal cancer cells (NPC) and activated p‐Akt signaling. 51

MIR156a is less frequently detected in individuals with cardiovascular disease (CDV). 53 An independent group of researchers also identified JAM‐A as the target of MIR156a by integrating mRNA databases, with criteria including complementary binding sites at the 3′ UTR and the thermodynamic threshold. In vitro studies have indicated that MIR156a can repress the expression of JAM‐A on endothelial cells and thus reduce the adhesion of inflammatory monocytes, indicating a protective role of MIR156a against atherosclerosis. 53 , 54

MIR156a has also been shown to target Wnt10b and interfere with the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. 55 In vitro experiments using a porcine intestinal cell line demonstrated that MIR156 exerted a repressive effect on cell proliferation by inducing the downregulation of Wnt10b protein and enhancing the phosphorylation of β‐catenin. This effect was reversed by treatment with lithium chloride and an MIR156 inhibitor. 55

MIR159

The MIR159 family is involved in pathogen defense responses in plants, and comprises more than 60% of miRNAs isolated from broccoli. 56 , 57 These MIR159s are abundant in exosomes and exhibit 2′‐O‐methylation of the 3′ terminus, indicating their high biostability in circulation and digestive processes.

Studies demonstrated that artificially synthesized MIR159 exhibited the capacity to attenuate the proliferation of breast cancer cells by targeting a specific region within the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the mRNA encoding Transcription Factor 7 (TCF7), a pivotal transcription factor within the Wnt signaling pathway. In vivo studies showed that oral administration of synthetic MIR159 resulted in a significant inhibition of xerograph breast tumor growth in mice. 58 Encapsuled within broccoli‐derived EVs, these miRNAs offer a promising application for effective cancer treatment. 57

MIR2911

MIR2911 is abundant in various Chinese herbs, including sophora, honeysuckle and chamomile, and remains stable in the circulation through dietary intake. 43 Zhou and colleagues provided empirical evidence illustrating that the ingestion of a decoction derived from boiled honeysuckle (Lonicera japonica) led to elevated levels of MIR2911 in the bloodstream and lung tissues of mice. Through a combination of luciferase reporter assays and in vivo investigations, it was revealed that MIR2911 effectively targeted influenza A virus (IAV), specifically strains H1N1, H5N1, and H7N9 through viral PB2 and NS1 proteins. This targeted action resulted in the inhibition of viral replication and subsequently led to a reduction in mouse mortality. Notably, despite boiling, MIR2911 remained stable in honeysuckle, which was consistent with previous research, indicating the resilience of plant miRNAs in food sources and their survival through simulated digestion. 59

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, researchers have been harnessing their knowledge and experience to develop effective solutions to address this global health crisis. Continuing their study of MIR2911, Zhou et al. demonstrated the inhibitory effect of MIR2911 on the replication cycle of SARS‐CoV‐2 in the HEK293T cell line. A 75‐subject clinical trial revealed that MIR2911 increased the negative conversion rate on day 7 from 26.1% to 83.3%. Twenty‐eight binding sites for MIR2911 in the SARS‐CoV‐2 genome were first predicted by bioinformatics analysis and then verified via luciferase assay. 60 In addition, they showed that this inhibitory effect was not observed in SIDT1 (SID1 Transmembrane Family Member 1, a transmembrane dsRNA‐gated channel)‐deficient patients or SIDT1 KO mice, indicating a possible absorption pathway of this miRNA. 61

An independent study revealed the antitumor effect of MIR2911 by targeting and repressing TGF‐β1, and this research further confirms the absorption of MIR2199 through SIDT1. 62

miRNAs IN CHINESE HERBS

Du et al. 63 extracted miRNA from the roots of Rhodiola rosea for research purposes. Initially, they performed high‐throughput sequencing on the lung tissues of mice after administering Rhodiola rosea miRNA orally. The eight most abundant miRNA sequences derived from Rhodiola rosea were chemically synthesized and subjected to functional screening for anti‐pulmonary fibrosis in a cell model. The results revealed that HJT‐s RNA‐m7 exhibited the most significant anti‐fibrotic functionality among them. Subsequent studies demonstrated that HJT‐s RNA‐m7 directly targeted key proteins involved in the development of lung fibrosis, namely alpha‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), fibronectin, and collagen type III (COL3A1). Furthermore, it showed promising therapeutic effects in a bleomycin‐induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis. 63

More than 10 Chinese herbal medicines, 64 including Dandelion and Rhodiola, 63 , 65 were used to extract miRNA. The miRNA sequences from blood cells of individuals who consumed herbal decoctions and from mouse lung tissues administered with gastric herbal decoctions were subjected to extensive bioinformatic analysis. The calculations revealed a significant presence of miRNAs derived from Chinese herbs. Additionally, Du et al. 63 identified over 100 lipids in the broth prepared from Rhodiola, most of which were also present in three other Chinese herbal medicines – Dandelion, Polygonatum, and Lonicera japonica. Two phosphatidylcholines were selected and formed lipid complexes with the herbal miRNA, showing their ability to facilitate the uptake of miRNAs from Rhodiola and other herbs into human lung and gastrointestinal cells. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of heat‐stable components extracted from herbal decoctions was superior to decoctions itself, 63 , 65 revealing a potential pathway for the entry of herbal‐derived miRNAs into the human body through lipid complexes. The extensive research on miRNAs derived from various Chinese herbs, including Ophiocordyceps sinensis, 66 also proved that they serve as active components of herbal medicines. Unlike previous studies of Chinese herbs and the exploration of their effects on other molecular mechanisms upon entering the human body, the miRNAs from herbal medicines have been shown to directly regulate the expression of target genes' mRNA, providing a new direction for modern research on traditional Chinese medicine.

CONTROVERSY AND PROSPECTS

The function and activity of plant‐derived miRNAs in mammalian systems remains controversial despite the presence of abundant evidence. Some groups have reported that they failed to detect plant miRNAs in mammalian circulation and tissues following oral feeding. Dickinson et al. refuted Zhang's hypothesis by demonstrating the absence of MIR168a in mice fed rice. 47 , 67 They further argued that any observed increase in plasma LDL levels resulting from rice consumption was likely due to nutritional imbalances rather than a direct effect of consuming plant MIR168a. 67 Similarly, Snow et al. 68 cast doubt on the presence of plant miRNAs by failing to detect the reportedly abundant plant miRNAs (MIR156a, MIR159a, and MIR169a) following fruit ingestion. The extent to which plant miRNAs can reach biologically relevant levels within target tissues solely through dietary uptake remains unclear. Current understanding is limited, as a significant portion of the studies conducted have relied on in vitro tissue culture rather than in vivo evidence. To enhance our knowledge in this area, it is crucial to adopt more quantitative approaches that can accurately assess the levels of plant‐derived miRNAs in biological fluids and tissues, as well as standard methodology of comparing and integrating the results from different research groups. 39

Moreover, there exists a need for in‐depth exploration into several additional aspects, including the absorption mechanisms of miRNAs within the gastrointestinal tract, a question that has garnered recent attention, and the cellular processes delivering the package of plant‐derived miRNAs into mammalian exosomes subsequent to their absorption. Furthermore, a meticulous investigation of the extent to which plant miRNAs subvert the intracellular endosome‐lysosome trafficking cascade, facilitating their incorporation into the cellular cytoplasmic silencing complex, is imperative. At the same time, it is of equal importance to gain insights into how plant miRNAs withstand degradation kinetics and surmount diverse biological barriers to enter the blood stream. 39 These intricate inquiries will collectively lay the foundation for prospective studies in this field.

Although cross‐kingdom regulatory roles for plant miRNAs have been a subject of debate among scientists, we have compiled compelling evidence supporting the involvement of specific plant miRNAs, such as MIR168a, MIR156a, MIR159, and MIR2911, in regulatory processes with well‐defined molecular mechanisms. Furthermore, emerging evidence continues to support the role of miRNAs in reducing inflammation, including promotion of IL‐22 production by ginger miRNA MIR7267‐3p, and modulation of the TNF‐α signaling pathway by nut MIR159s and MIR156c. These findings, along with other relevant examples, have been extensively reviewed by Jia et al. and Li et al. 69 , 70 miRNAs are powerful regulatory tools with the ability to modulate complex cellular pathways through interactions with multiple target genes. 18 The versatility of miRNAs enables their potential therapeutic applications in human diseases.
