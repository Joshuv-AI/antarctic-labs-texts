Re-emerging syphilis among women of reproductive age: temporal dynamics, demographic drivers, inequalities and projections to 2046
Authors: Xue-Er Cheng, Xiao-Xiao Li, Yu-Tong Tan, Wei-Wei Wang, Yu-Wan Chang, Lei Li, Peng Wang, Hai-Feng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSyphilis is a re-emerging sexually transmitted infection and a major threat to reproductive and maternal health. Previous studies have described the burden among women of reproductive age (WRA), but have not quantified the demographic drivers of rising case numbers or compared changes in absolute and relative socioeconomic inequality. We examined these questions from 1990 to 2021 and projected trends to 2046.MethodsWe analyzed Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimates of syphilis incidence and prevalence among women aged 15-49 years in 204 countries and territories. Temporal patterns were assessed using Joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort analysis. Decomposition analysis quantified demographic and epidemiological drivers. Socio-demographic Index (SDI)-related inequalities were measured using the slope index of inequality and relative concentration index, and future burden was projected using Nordpred models.ResultsIn 2021, the global age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates were 279.2 and 1057.3 per 100,000 women, corresponding to 5.36 million incident and 20.5 million prevalent cases. Burden remained concentrated in lower-SDI regions and among women aged 20-29 years. Population growth accounted for 97.97% of the increase in incident cases and 99.90% of the increase in prevalent cases, while population aging partly offset both increases. Absolute socioeconomic gaps narrowed, whereas relative inequality changed little. Period rate ratios increased again during 2017-2021, and annual incident cases were projected to reach 6.95 million by 2046.ConclusionsStable age-standardized rates did not translate into fewer women affected. Demographic growth and persistent relative inequality should be considered in surveillance and service planning, particularly in lower-SDI settings.

Introduction

Syphilis, a long-standing but re-emerging sexually transmitted infection, remains a major threat to reproductive, maternal, and neonatal health [1,2]. Recent global surveillance reports have described renewed increases in syphilis infections in several regions, underscoring that syphilis is not only a persistent infection but also a re-emerging public health challenge [3]. Syphilis is preventable through health education, antenatal screening, and timely treatment. Nevertheless, it continues to be a leading cause of stillbirth, neonatal mortality, and long-term disability, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [4]. When maternal syphilis is untreated or inadequately treated, adverse pregnancy outcomes may occur in up to 80% of affected pregnancies [5]. Despite global prevention and control strategies, the burden among women of reproductive age (WRA) remains high and varies markedly by geography and socioeconomic development [6]. These patterns reveal persistent gaps in detection, treatment, and prevention, especially in resource-constrained settings, and may hinder progress toward global syphilis control, congenital syphilis elimination, and maternal-child health equity [7].

WRA are central to the pathway of mother-to-child transmission. The burden in this population therefore reflects gaps in timely detection and treatment, as well as their consequences for maternal and neonatal health. They are also important for assessing how transmission, demographic growth, socioeconomic inequality and access to prevention services shape the re-emergence of syphilis. Large-scale analyses based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data have provided important baseline information on the epidemiology of syphilis. Recent studies have already described the geographic, age-specific, and socioeconomic distribution of syphilis among women aged 15-49 years [8,9]. These studies show where the burden is concentrated, but they do not explain why case numbers continue to rise when age-standardized rates are stable or declining. Demographic growth may account for part of this divergence. The distinction also matters for inequality, because a narrower absolute gap may coexist with persistent relative concentration in lower-SDI countries. Population change, recent temporal shifts, and future case growth have yet to be assessed together in this population.

Against this background, we used GBD 2021 data to examine syphilis among WRA from 1990 to 2021 and to project the burden to 2046. Joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort (APC) analysis were first used to identify changes in temporal patterns and age-related variation. We then used decomposition analysis to quantify how changes in population size, age structure, and age-specific rates contributed to the observed case trends. We compared absolute and relative inequality measures and then projected how the observed patterns might affect future case numbers.

These analyses distinguish changes in underlying rates from changes in the number of women requiring testing and treatment. They also show whether a reduction in the absolute socioeconomic gap is accompanied by a comparable change in relative inequality. This distinction is important when progress in syphilis control is judged from a single summary measure.

Methods

Data source

GBD 2021 provides standardized estimates of disease burden by age, sex, year, location, and cause across 204 countries and territories. In this study, estimates of syphilis incidence and prevalence among WRA, defined as women aged 15-49 years, were extracted from GBD 2021 for the period 1990–2021. Estimates were reported as counts and rates with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Countries and territories were further classified according to the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), which summarizes socio-demographic development and categorizes locations into five groups: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high SDI [10].

Trend analysis

Joinpoint regression was used to identify temporal changes in the burden of syphilis among women of reproductive age. This method detects inflection points in time-series data and estimates the annual percentage change and average annual percentage change (AAPC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was assessed using Monte Carlo permutation tests based on 4499 randomly permuted datasets, with Bonferroni correction applied to maintain the overall asymptotic significance level. A trend was considered increasing when the AAPC estimate and the lower bound of its 95% CI were both greater than zero, decreasing when the AAPC estimate and the upper bound of its 95% CI were both less than zero, and stable otherwise [11].

To characterize age-specific patterns and temporal variation in syphilis incidence and prevalence, APC analysis was conducted for 1992-2021. This interval comprised six complete five-year calendar periods. Five-year age groups (15-19 years, 20-24 years, …, 45-49 years) and five-year calendar periods (1992-1996, 1997-2001, …, 2017-2021) were defined to construct consecutive birth cohorts. The analysis followed the estimable-function approach described for the National Cancer Institute Age–Period–Cohort Web Tool. The outputs included longitudinal age curves, local drifts, and period and cohort rate ratios with corresponding 95% CIs. Local drift represents the estimated annual percentage change in the fitted rate for each age group. Period and cohort rate ratios describe variation across calendar periods and birth cohorts relative to the model reference categories. This approach was selected because it focuses on identifiable age-related and temporal functions rather than a single constraint-dependent decomposition of the linear age, period, and cohort components [12].

Decomposition analysis

A decomposition analysis was conducted to quantify the contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change to changes in the numbers of incident and prevalent cases from 1990 to 2021. The number of cases in year y was expressed as Casey = ∑i=1n(ai,y*py*ri,y)100000, where Casey denotes the total number of incident or prevalent cases in year y, ai,y is the proportion of women in age group i, py is the total population of women aged 15-49 years, and ri,y is the age-specific incidence or prevalence rate per 100,000 women. The change between 1990 and 2021 was partitioned into contributions from population size, age structure, and age-specific rates by varying one component while holding the other components constant [13].

Cross-country inequality analysis

Cross-country socioeconomic inequalities in the burden of syphilis among women of reproductive age were assessed using the slope index of inequality (SII) and the relative concentration index (RCI). Countries and territories were ranked according to SDI, and a relative socioeconomic position was assigned to each location. The SII was estimated by regressing country-level incidence and prevalence rates on this relative SDI-based position, providing an absolute measure of inequality. The RCI was calculated from the concentration curve, which plots the cumulative distribution of syphilis burden against the cumulative population distribution ranked by SDI, providing a relative measure of inequality [14].

Future projection

To project global syphilis incidence and prevalence through 2046, we used the Nordpred age-period-cohort model with a power-5 link function. The model was fitted to age-specific case counts and population data grouped into five-year age and calendar periods. The prediction base was selected from the most recent three or four observed periods according to model fit. To reduce implausible extrapolation of long-term linear trends, the drift component was progressively attenuated by 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% across successive forecast periods. Projected age-specific rates were combined with GBD population projections to estimate the corresponding numbers of incident and prevalent cases. The projections were interpreted as a baseline scenario under continuation of recent trends [15].

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1).

Results

Overview of the global burden of syphilis among WRA

A positive AAPC was observed only in the high-middle SDI region (0.16, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.24), while the most significant decline occurred in the low SDI region (AAPC: −0.32, 95% CI: −0.38 to −0.27). These findings indicate substantial heterogeneity in temporal trends, with age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates declining in most low-SDI countries but remaining stable or increasing in some middle- and high-middle-SDI settings.

Age-period-cohort analysis

The APC analysis characterized age-related and temporal variation in the syphilis burden among WRA during 1992-2021.

The longitudinal age curves showed a clear unimodal pattern, with the highest incidence and prevalence observed among women aged 20-29 years. The incidence peaked at approximately 422 per 100,000 in the 20-24 year group, while the prevalence peaked at 1490 per 100,000 in the 25-29 age group. Thereafter, both measures declined steadily with age. Period rate ratios increased modestly during 1997-2001 (RRincidence = 1.03, RRprevalence = 1.02), followed by a steady decline until 2016 (RRincidence = 0.89, RRprevalence = 0.87) and a slight rebound in 2017-2021 (RRincidence = 0.97, RRprevalence = 0.92). High and high-middle SDI regions exhibited early increases followed by stabilization, while middle- and low-SDI regions experienced sustained declines across the study period, showing that the temporal patterns differed across SDI strata (Fig. 3C–Table A.6).

Cohort rate ratios generally declined across successive birth cohorts. Women born before 1960 had the highest rate ratios (RRincidence = 1.16; RRprevalence = 1.22), whereas those born after the early 1980s exhibited substantially lower rate ratios (RRincidence = 0.91; RRprevalence = 0.90). Collectively, the APC analysis showed lower rate ratios in successive birth cohorts, together with a partial increase in period rate ratios during 2017-2021. The recent period pattern indicates that the earlier decline in fitted rates has slowed.

Cross-national inequalities in syphilis burden

Globally, the SII for syphilis incidence decreased from −737.73 (95% CI: −855.25 to −620.21) in 1990 to −595.42 (95% CI: −688.91 to −501.92) in 2021, suggesting a modest reduction in incidence inequality across countries. However, the RCI remained stable (−0.34, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.25 in 1990; −0.35, 95% CI: −0.41 to −0.29 in 2021), indicating persistent disparities. For prevalence, the global SII decreased from −3035.71 (95% CI: −3543.57 to −2527.85) in 1990 to −2384.27 (95% CI: −2784.19 to −1984.36) in 2021, reflecting a reduction in absolute inequalities. However, the RCI showed minimal change (−0.36, 95% CI: −0.46 to −0.26 in 1990; −0.35, 95% CI: −0.41 to −0.29 in 2021), suggesting persistent relative disparities (Table A.9, Fig. 4C–D, Fig. B.4C-D).

Projection of syphilis burden up to 2046

Projections indicate a continued substantial global burden of syphilis among WRA. By 2046, the ASIR is projected to reach 331.22 per 100,000, corresponding to approximately 6,951,431 new cases annually. The projected ASPR is 1218.40 per 100,000, with an estimated 25,936,953 prevalent cases worldwide (Table A.10, Fig. 4B–B.4B).

Discussion

This study examined the global burden, long-term trends, inequalities, and future trajectory of syphilis among WRA. Over the study period, global age-standardized rates changed little, yet the number of women affected continued to rise. This divergence, together with marked regional variation and persistent relative inequality, provides the main context for interpreting the findings.

Global averages concealed divergent regional trajectories. Several low-SDI settings achieved sustained declines, while parts of the middle- and high-middle-SDI spectrum showed stagnation or resurgence. The longitudinal age curves peaked among women aged 20-29 years, while negative local drifts showed declining age-specific rates over time. Cohort rate ratios were lower in successive birth cohorts. Period rate ratios showed a modest rise in the late 1990s/early 2000s, followed by improvement and a partial rebound after 2017, indicating a deceleration of progress rather than a uniform global reversal.

Recent GBD 2021 studies have described the broad concentration of syphilis burden among WRA in lower-SDI settings and younger age groups [8,9]. The present analysis adds most by explaining two discrepancies that were not resolved in earlier reports. One concerns rates and case numbers. Although previous work noted that case numbers increased while age-standardized rates declined, population growth accounted for 97.97% of the increase in incident cases and 99.90% of the increase in prevalent cases in our decomposition analysis; population aging partly offset both increases. A stable or declining rate therefore did not translate into fewer women potentially requiring testing and treatment. The second discrepancy concerns inequality. Absolute gaps narrowed between 1990 and 2021, but the relative concentration of burden in lower-SDI countries remained largely unchanged. With case numbers projected to rise through 2046, improvement in a single rate or inequality measure would provide an incomplete account of progress.

The persistent relative concentration of burden in lower-SDI countries may be sustained by barriers to timely screening and treatment, including insufficient resources and staffing, weak health-system infrastructure, and gender-related or sociocultural constraints [16]. Broad improvements in service coverage can reduce the absolute difference between countries without removing the proportional disadvantage experienced by women in the least-resourced settings. This pattern suggests that improvements in overall coverage may be insufficient to close proportional gaps when the underlying structural determinants remain unaddressed [16].

Regional divergence underscores that syphilis control is context-dependent. Central Sub-Saharan Africa carried the highest incidence in 2021, and evidence from antenatal services helps explain why this burden has remained high. In a cluster trial conducted in Kinshasa and Lusaka, the provision of test kits and benzathine penicillin markedly increased screening, but supplies alone did not produce equally high treatment coverage. Treatment improved only after behavioral and service-delivery measures were added [17]. Thus, attendance at antenatal care did not always lead to prompt treatment. Tropical Latin America, particularly Brazil, showed a different weakness in the care pathway. An analysis of Brazilian state capitals found that prenatal care remained inadequate for the diagnosis and treatment of gestational syphilis despite the availability of relevant supplies [18]. At a large hospital in Porto Alegre, 61% of pregnant women with syphilis received ineffective treatment, and only 31.8% of their partners were treated [19]. In this setting, losses after diagnosis, including delayed treatment, incomplete follow-up, and limited partner management, may have allowed transmission to continue despite an established screening infrastructure.

Eastern Europe is less straightforward. The low modeled incidence followed the large syphilis epidemics of the post-Soviet period and may represent a real reduction in transmission. Yet earlier declines in reported cases coincided with less intensive active case finding and greater use of private or informal services that contributed little to routine notification [20]. More recent assessments still report under-ascertainment and uneven surveillance capacity in several non-EU/EEA countries [21]. Regional incidence estimates should therefore be read together with the completeness of local reporting. The immediate priority in Central Sub-Saharan Africa remains reliable same-visit testing and treatment. Evidence from Brazil instead places greater emphasis on completing maternal treatment and reaching sexual partners. For Eastern Europe, progress cannot be judged confidently without more complete case ascertainment.

The concentration of incidence and prevalence at ages 20-29 coincides with peak sexual activity and fertility, making this group central to interrupting both sexual transmission and congenital syphilis. Our results align with existing evidence [9]. Accordingly, age-tailored sexual health services and targeted testing remain important, while digital approaches may broaden prevention and testing outreach among younger populations [22,23]. Lower cohort rate ratios in more recent generations are consistent with gradual gains in prevention and access to reproductive health services. The recent period pattern, however, suggests that these gains may not be continuing at the same pace.

Demographic expansion changes how apparent epidemiological progress should be interpreted. In high-fertility, low-SDI settings, declining age-specific rates can coexist with increasing numbers of women requiring diagnosis and treatment. Program monitoring should therefore consider age-standardized rates, age-specific trends, and case volume together. The observed rate changes were not large enough to offset population growth during the study period.

The increase in period rate ratios during 2017-2021 raises concern about the resilience of syphilis control systems. Service disruptions, reduced political attention, and the COVID-19 pandemic likely delayed diagnosis and treatment; surveillance in several countries reported sharp increases in syphilis and congenital syphilis during this period [24]. Under the baseline projection, the burden remains substantial through 2046, with approximately 6.95 million incident cases and 25.94 million prevalent cases. These estimates indicate the scale of services that may be required if recent patterns persist.

These findings have two implications for monitoring and service planning. Progress should be assessed using standardized rates alongside projected case volumes, because demographic growth can increase demand for testing and treatment even when rates decline. Absolute and relative inequality measures should also be reported together. A narrower absolute gap does not necessarily mean that the burden has become less concentrated in lower-SDI countries. These considerations reinforce established priorities, including reliable access to benzathine penicillin, timely screening in reproductive and antenatal care, partner services, and focused outreach to women aged 20-29 years.

This study integrates GBD estimates with APC and decomposition analyses and inequality metrics to provide a comparable global overview of syphilis burden among WRA. Several limitations should be considered. The analysis relied on modeled GBD estimates, and uncertainty is greater where surveillance, diagnostic capacity, or reporting are limited. The aggregated data contained no individual-level behavioral or clinical information and did not distinguish syphilis stages; regional patterns could therefore not be linked to specific risk profiles or to changes in early, latent, or late disease. The APC framework summarizes estimable age-related and temporal patterns, but the exact linear relationship among age, period, and cohort prevents their independent linear effects from being uniquely separated. Projections to 2046 assume that recent trends continue with gradual attenuation. Future changes in screening, treatment, migration, reporting, or health-service delivery could alter these trajectories, so the estimates should be read as scenario-based projections rather than precise forecasts.

Conclusion

Syphilis remains a major and unequally distributed threat to WRA. The apparent stability of global age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates conceals a continuing rise in the number of women affected, driven largely by population growth, while the narrowing of absolute socioeconomic gaps has not reduced the relative concentration of burden in lower-SDI settings. Together with the projected growth in cases through 2046, these findings suggest that recent progress has been fragile and uneven. Control efforts should therefore be guided not only by changes in standardized rates, but also by case volume and the persistence of relative inequality, with sustained attention to Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and other high-burden settings. Strengthening surveillance, ensuring timely testing and treatment, and embedding syphilis services within reproductive and antenatal care remain central to reducing preventable maternal and neonatal harm.
