WF-1801: Single-arm pilot study of ramipril for prevention of radiation-induced cognitive decline in glioblastoma patients receiving chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Christina K Cramer, Sydney Smith, Emily V Dressler, Brandi Page, Jeffrey S Wefel, Stephen R Rapp, Edward H Ip, Stephanie L Pugh, Ashley Sumrall, Roy E Strowd, Zachary K Vaslow, Gena Volas-Redd, Kathryn E Weaver, Glenn J Lesser, Michael D Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo examine the feasibility of adding ramipril for prevention of cognitive decline to chemoradiation treatment of glioblastoma (GBM).MethodsThis prospective single-arm study (WF-1801) coordinated by the Wake Forest NCI Community Oncology Research Program Research Base (UG1CA189824) assessed feasibility, tolerability, and potential efficacy of ramipril to prevent treatment-induced cognitive decline in patients with GBM. Inclusion criteria and chemoradiotherapeutic paradigms were mirrored to the standard arm of NRG/RTOG 0825, such that cognitive outcomes could be compared. All patients were treated with ramipril during chemoradiation and for 4 weeks after completion of radiotherapy (RT). Major outcomes were retention and the Clinical Trial Battery Composite (CTB COMP) score of the cognitive tests. Results were compared to the corresponding outcomes from NRG/RTOG 0825.ResultsA total of 75 participants were accrued between March 25, 2019 and November 14, 2023: median age was 63 years. The NRG/RTOG 0825 cohort was younger (median 57 years, P < .0001). Overall retention rate at 1-month post-RT (defined as compliant with 75% of doses and completion of cognitive testing) was 48% (1-sided 95% CI: 38%-100%); 61% of patients completed more than 75% of doses and 57% had a CTB COMP score through week 10. The median (range) change in the CTB COMP score at 1 month after RT completion was 0.01 (−82.6, 13.0) versus NRG/RTOG 0825 median of 0.10 (−48.2, 8.6); P = .49.ConclusionsThe ramipril intervention did not meet the prespecified feasibility endpoint, neither did the cognitive scores differ significantly from the NRG/RTOG 0925 control group. We expect other agents will be the focus of future cytoprotective trials.

Importance of the Study

This prospective, single-arm, feasibility study of ramipril during chemoradiation of glioblastoma addresses treatment-induced cognitive decline in this at-risk population. Furthermore, by using available data from the NRG/RTOG 0825 trial, this design demonstrates the efficiency and caveats of using a historic control. The ramipril intervention did not meet prespecified feasibility endpoints or show a significant difference in participant cognitive scores. However, the study provides insight into glioblastoma trials in the community setting, particularly during the challenging COVID-19 pandemic era. While ramipril does not appear to be a neuroprotective agent in this population, ramipril may be better suited to other patient populations with longer life expectancies where greater retention is possible.

There are approximately 12 000 cases of glioblastoma (GBM) in the United States each year.1 The standard treatment approach includes maximum safe resection followed by radiotherapy (RT) with concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide chemotherapy. Approximately 30% of these patients will experience significant radiation-induced cognitive decline (RICD) within the first 4 months after completion of RT.2,3 As with other tumors for which RICD develops, the likelihood and severity of significant toxicity increase with survival time after brain RT.4 The past 2 decades have demonstrated significant therapeutic advances for GBM which have led to a corresponding increase in survival.5,6 As a result, a need has arisen to deal with the permanent cognitive toxicities of treatment, with a growing interest in the development of cytoprotective agents.

Preclinical data have shown that a blockade of the Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS) cascade in rats can produce changes in both the purported target structures of brain irradiation as well as in the functional capacity of the animal.7–10 The data also suggest that delivery of ramipril concurrently or soon after RT is critical to optimizing its cytoprotective effects, implying that once brain injury and functional loss has occurred, the cytoprotective window is lost. Lee et al demonstrated that use of ramipril prior to, concurrent with, and after whole brain RT was able to ameliorate RICD.7 The novel object recognition test, a test of hippocampal dependent memory, was used to assess for RICD in this study. A specific study of the timing of ramipril administration on its radioprotective ability in the CNS showed that earlier treatment with ramipril yields better functional neurologic outcomes.10

Advantages of ramipril as a potential neuroprotective agent are its known penetration across the blood-brain barrier and its established tolerability. In human clinical studies, ramipril has been reported to positively affect cerebral blood flow in elderly patients with carotid artery occlusive disease at a dose of 5 mg.11 Davies et al reported in a nested analysis that patients with Alzheimer’s Dementia were less likely to have been prescribed an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACE-I),12 suggesting that it is possible that there may be a lower rate of AD diagnoses in patients who had been prescribed an ARB or ACE-I. Furthermore, a pilot study showed that ramipril administered at a dose of 5mg inhibited cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) ACE activity, demonstrating that clinically relevant doses of ramipril cross the blood-brain barrier.13

Due to preclinical data and the ability of ramipril to cross the blood-brain barrier, we conducted a pilot study to determine the feasibility of administering ramipril as a cytoprotective agent against RICD in the GBM population. The aims of the WF-1801 study were to assess feasibility and tolerability and obtain efficacy estimates of a single daily dose of ramipril by comparing cognitive outcomes to the historical control population in the standard arm of the NRG/RTOG 0825 study. The NRG/RTOG 0825 was a 2-arm study; the standard arm utilized the current accepted approach of concurrent temozolomide treatment with radiation, followed by 6 months of adjuvant temozolomide. The treatment arm included this standard and added bevacizumab treatment.

Methods

Participants/Eligibility and Exclusion Criteria

This prospective single arm study, hereafter referred to as WF-1801 (NCI-2018-01807), was coordinated by the Wake Forest National Cancer Institute (NCI) Community Oncology Research Program (NCORP) Research Base (UG1CA189824). The study was approved by the NCI Central Institutional Review Board (cIRB) and then subsequently by the IRB at each participating site. Patients provided written informed consent prior to participating. The eligibility criteria were written to mirror the NRG/RTOG 0825 study.14 Patients were required to have adequate hematology and liver function and have the intention of receiving chemoradiotherapy to a total dose of 60 Gy with concurrent temozolomide. As the NRG/RTOG 0825 trial required central pathology review for 1 cc tumor tissue, it was mandated that patients have at least 1 cc tumor tissue resected (ie, stereotactic biopsy alone made patients ineligible). Additional ineligibility criteria were related to patient ability to take ramipril (hypotension, renal insufficiency, and current ACE/ARB use). Of note, tumor treating fields were allowed after publication of the EF-14 study demonstrated a survival benefit of this modality.6

Study Treatment and Trial Design

Patients were enrolled onto WF-1801 prior to the start of chemoradiotherapy. Fractionated RT was delivered in the same manner as was done on the NRG/RTOG 0825 study (60 Gy in 30 fractions with 3D conformal or intensity modulated radiation therapy allowed). Clinical Target Volume (CTV) margins also mirrored NRG/RTOG 0825 including a 2 cm margin beyond the residual disease, postoperative cavity and surrounding T2 or Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery abnormality to 46 Gy. A boost CTV to 60 Gy was delivered to 2 cm margin beyond residual disease and postoperative cavity. Planning Target Volume (PTV) margin of 3-5 mm beyond the CTV was used. Temozolomide was delivered concurrently with RT at a dose of 75 mg/m2. In the absence of myelosuppression, adjuvant temozolomide was administered on days 1-5 of a 28-day cycle for 6 cycles starting at a dose of 150 mg/m2 and titrating to 200 mg/m2 as tolerated. Patients were treated with ramipril which started at a dose of 1.25 mg/day and titrated upwards to a maximum dose of 5 mg/day as tolerated by blood pressure and renal function. Ramipril was subsequently given during chemoradiotherapy and for 4 weeks after RT. Blood pressure, serum creatinine, and serum potassium were monitored weekly during titration. If any of the following occurred, ramipril was not further titrated and the dose was decreased, or drug was discontinued if toxicity persisted: systolic blood pressure lower than 100 mmHg, elevated creatinine, or a serum potassium greater than 5.0 mEq/L. Cognitive testing and patient-reported outcomes (PRO) were acquired at baseline, at the completion of RT, 4 weeks post-RT (ie, 10 weeks), and 4 post-RT. All patients were evaluable for toxicity from the time of their first dose of Ramipril. Toxicity was assessed weekly during RT and at each follow-up and quantified using the CTCAE v5.0.

Study Instruments and Measures

Two major outcomes were analyzed for this study: retention and cognitive function. Our primary outcome, retention, was defined by the following 2 criteria: (1) compliance with drug therapy for at least 75% of doses during the 10-week period and (2) completion of cognitive testing permitting calculation of the CTB COMP through 4 weeks post-RT. Compliance with drug therapy was calculated as the ratio between doses taken and doses that a patient should have taken throughout the 10 weeks.

The co-primary outcome, cognitive function, was measured by the same standard battery that was used for the NRG/RTOG 0825. Tests included Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT-R), a test of verbal learning and memory15; Trail Making Test (TMT) Parts A and B,16 tests of attention and set shifting, respectively; and the Controlled Oral Word Association (COWA), a test of phonemic verbal fluency.17 PROs were the 30-item European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer-Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30)18 and the 20-item Brain Cancer Scale (BN20).19 The Clinical Trial Battery Composite (CTB COMP) cognitive score, comparable to the NRG/RTOG 0825 study,20 was defined as the arithmetic mean of the standardized scores from the HVLT-R Total Recall, HVLT-R Delayed Recall, HVLT-R Delayed Recognition Discriminability subscales, the TMT Parts A and B, and COWA. To ensure comparability, the standardization used the conventional normed values of mean and standard deviation values stratified by age/gender/education categories as was also used in the NRG/RTOG 0825 study.15–17 Specifically, HVLT-R normed values were age-adjusted, COWA were gender and education adjusted, and TMT were age and education-adjusted.

Statistical Analysis

The primary objective of the study was to assess the retention rate of patients at the time point at 4 weeks post-RT. Pretrial power analysis assumed a 1-sided test at the .05 significance level, the sample size of 75 provided a 90% power to detect a patient retention rate of 80% versus 65%. The co-primary objective, the effect of receiving ramipril on cognition, was calculated by comparing the CTB COMP score in the treatment group with the control arm in the NRG/RTOG 0825 study at the 4 weeks post-RT timepoint. Baseline values and changes through 4 weeks post-RT are presented for CTB scores and EORTC QLQ-C30/BN20 instruments. Unadjusted analyses assessed change in scores between arms from baseline to 4 weeks post-RT using rank-based Mann-Whitney tests. Based on baseline comparison, adjusted analyses accounted for imbalance between WF-1801 and NRG/RTOG 0825 cohorts. As CTB COMP and QOL data were highly skewed, comparison with NRG/RTOG 0825 used a nonparametric (rank-based) method for hypothesis testing. Intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis was applied to the co-primary and secondary outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R.21

Effect of Ramipril on Retention Rate

Retention was calculated as drug compliance and completion of cognitive assessments at 4 weeks post-RT. Thirty-six (48%) patients were compliant with drug therapy at 4 weeks post-RT and had CTB COMP scores at baseline and 4 weeks post-RT. The 95% confidence interval for retention was (36%,60%); retention thus was significantly less than the targeted 65%. The proportion of patients that were compliant with drug therapy ranged from 0 to 100%, with mean 73% (SD= 0.05). Forty-six (61%) patients were found to satisfy the definition of compliance and formed the compliant group. Of those in compliance with drug therapy, the mean of the compliant group is 98% (SD = 0.02, range 76-100%). For the non-compliant group, the mean is 33% (SD = 0.09, range 0-70%). Forty-three (57%) patients had CTB COMP scores at baseline and 4 weeks post-RT.

Toxicity of Ramipril

From time of enrollment to the 4-week post-RT timepoint, 29 of 70 patients who took at least one dose of ramipril reported at least one toxicity (Table S1). There were no grade 3 or greater toxicities possibly, probably, or definitely related to ramipril. There were 9 blood pressure toxicities (8 participants), 22 instances of rising creatinine (18 participants), 3 episodes of decreased glomerular filtration rate (3 participants), and 12 instances of hyperkalemia (7 participants). Of the 9 blood pressure toxicities, 2 (22%) led to a dose reduction in ramipril and 5 (56%) to a ramipril discontinuation. Of the 22 creatinine toxicities, 9 (41%) led to a dose reduction and 8 (36%) in drug discontinuation. One of 3 instances of decreased glomerular filtration rate led to each dose ­reduction (33%) and drug discontinuation (33%). In addition, 4 (33%) instances of hyperkalemia led to dose reduction and 3 (25%) led to drug discontinuation.

Discussion

Several attempts have been made to mitigate the adverse cognitive effects of brain RT. Technology has been developed to decrease the dose to structures of interest within the brain.23,24 Studies on decreasing the integral dose of brain radiation have been feasible and clinical trials have established early efficacy.25 Pharmacologic interventions which showed cognitive improvements in some dementias have been repurposed for use with brain RT and have exhibited promise.26–28 The present study represents a direct translation of preclinical work that demonstrated an available mechanism of inflammation and assessed a pharmacologic agent that both blocked this mechanism and could cross the blood-brain barrier.

The RAS is thought to mediate a proinflammatory cascade within the brain. Irradiation of the rodent brain leads to chronic increases in reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen oxide species, which leads to progenitor cell death as well as a phenotype of chronic inflammation and organ dysfunction. The high oxygen consumption within the brain as well as the presence of oxidizable unsaturated fatty acids and free iron provide an ideal environment for both acute and chronic inflammation.29,30 Jenrow et al described the use of ramipril in rats in the immediate post-radiation setting and then for 12 consecutive weeks after brain RT. Subsequent immunohistochemical studies revealed that ramipril mitigated the decrease in neurogenesis seen in the rat dentate gyrus of the hippocampus after whole brain RT and that it did so by preventing apoptosis in progenitor cells.8 Given that apoptosis is an acute response to RT, the findings implied that earlier treatment with ramipril after RT is critical to its effectiveness. In another study, Lee et al demonstrated that use of ramipril prior to, concurrent with, and after whole brain RT was able to ameliorate RICD in rats.7

Retention was chosen as a primary endpoint for this study given the unknown ability of normotensive GBM patients to tolerate and comply with a medication that could lower blood pressure. While ramipril has been previously found to be tolerable in normotensive patients,31 GBM patients have a greater comorbidity profile given usage of temozolomide, a higher hospitalization rate,32 and a greater fall risk that could be exacerbated by an anti-hypertensive.33 In the present study, 61% of patients were compliant to ≥75% of prescribed ramipril doses, which means that the trial did not meet this primary endpoint. Retention rates in this study may have been affected by the significantly older age and multiple clinical comorbidities in the enrolled population, as detailed in Figure 1. There may have also been differences in disease severity that led to patients’ withdrawal or from treating physicians’ recommendations to discontinue the study. The additional stressors and disruption created by the COVID-19 pandemic likely magnified and/or compounded these recruitment and retention challenges.

A component of retention was the willingness to complete brief (ie, 18-38 minutes) longitudinal cognitive testing. As shown in the CONSORT diagram (Figure 1), only 1 patient withdrew from participation citing unwillingness to complete testing/questionnaires. Prior studies have demonstrated that as patients with brain tumors progress, they are commonly less willing to complete a 4-h cognitive testing battery.34 As shown in the CONSORT diagram (Figure 1), 5 patients experienced disease progression or initiated hospice during the follow-up period. Fifty-seven percent of enrolled patients completed the neurocognitive battery through 4 weeks post-RT. This is compared to 65% of patients in the control arm of the NRG/RTOG 0825 study who completed the neurocognitive battery at the 4-week post-RT time point.20

Cognitive function was a co-primary endpoint of the WF-1801 trial. Cognitive outcomes of WF-1801 were compared to the historical population in the standard arm of the NRG/RTOG 0825 trial, because this study’s control arm represented a population for which modern chemotherapy and radiotherapy techniques and volumes were used. Importantly, radiation margins were standardized across the 2 studies as standard margins in the community setting can vary between institutions.35 Results of the comparative analysis did not show any clinical or statistical difference in cognitive performance between the 2 groups over time. Moreover, when the patients compliant with ramipril in the present study were compared to the control population, there was again no difference in cognitive outcomes when compared to the ITT analyses. One caveat of this analysis may be the early cognitive assessment timepoint at 10 weeks. Pragmatic considerations to maximize feasibility in this community-based pilot study led to this design, but the full impact of RICD may be delayed to 6 months or more post-RT.

Heterogeneity in the cognitive outcome as evidenced by the range in the CTB COMP scores, could be a factor that limits signal detection. While we used nonparametric methods to mitigate the influence of extreme values in the cognitive tests, the composite score still contains large variations in the component scores. Such large variation is likely due to differences in baseline function, disease heterogeneity, and intersite variation. This large cognitive variability reduced power to detect meaningful differences.

Using a single-arm study design with a historic control has advantages, including efficiency and allowing for a smaller study, but we acknowledge the study design has caveats and limitations. While recruitment challenges during the difficult COVID-19 pandemic did extend the enrollment period for this study to 4.5 years, the use of historic control was a principal reason for study completion.

However, the challenges of a historic control require examination. Specifically, there are several potential population differences between the NRG/RTOG 0825 cohort and ours. Although the inclusion criteria for the current study generally aligned with those of the NRG/RTOG 0825, our study did not exclude patients with a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) of <70%. The population in this study was also significantly older (Table 1).

Second, the NRG/RTOG study primarily enrolled participants from large academic centers (80%), whereas the current study enrolled only 20% from such centers, the rest coming from community-based cancer treatment centers. This difference in patient population may have introduced an unintentional bias in our cognitive data. For example, patients at presenting to an academic center may differ in disease severity. The characterizations of GBM may differ between academic and community centers with multiple confounding factors such management of comorbidities or financial concerns. It is possible that patients in our study were either removed or elected to stop the study earlier than patients on the NRG/RTOG study. This may have occurred for multiple reasons: their need for ongoing multidisciplinary care, lack of practical and caregiving supports for patients in community settings, or underestimation of the often dramatic changes in physical and cognitive functioning in patients with GBM undergoing initial therapy.

Additionally, the differences between NRG/RTOG 0825 and the current study may include other confounding and unmeasured factors such as socioeconomic status, social support, and health status. The difference in time between the 2 studies, that is, existence of time drift because of change in standard care, may further contribute to the bias of the study. Finally, it is conceivable that cognitive benefit due to ramipril may have been perceived as less critical than the NRG/RTOG treatment with bevacizumab for preservation of disease-free survival and patients may have been less motivated to remain on the present study than NRG/RTOG 0825.

In conclusion, although WF-1801 failed to meet either its primary or co-primary endpoints, the study illustrated challenges of quality-of-life research in patients with GBM, added insight into GBM trials in the community setting, and generated a cohort with prospective cognitive data. The accrual and retention of this population was a significant challenge (especially during the COVID-19 pandemic era). However, we designed it to utilize the historical RTOG dataset, and we were able to continue even during the difficult pandemic period.

Ramipril does not appear to be a compelling neuroprotective agent in this population as we could not obtain sufficient power to detect a signal for neuroprotection from ramipril co-treatment. Future studies in GBM would require more refined selection criteria such as a minimal KPS ≥ 70, a younger population, and more favorable tumor pathologies (ie, methylated MGMT promoter or complete resection). This could produce a healthier study population capable of longer follow-up periods and allow for the retention of a larger group. Retention in these longer surviving cohorts may further benefit from reducing the burden of assessments (streamlined neurocognitive battery or improved assessment alignment with clinical visit schedule) and providing increased practical supports to participants and clinic staff. Alternatively, ramipril may be better suited to populations of other diagnoses with longer life expectancies where greater retention is possible.
