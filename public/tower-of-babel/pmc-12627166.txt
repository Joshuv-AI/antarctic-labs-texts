Targeted Nanoparticles for Drug Delivery Across the Blood–Brain Barrier in Early and Late Stages of Alzheimer's Disease: A Review
Authors: Urszula Kochman, Hanna Sitka, Julia Kuźniar, Magdalena Czaja, Patrycja Kozubek, Jan Aleksander Beszłej, Jerzy Leszek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) presents important challenges for treatment. One significant factor that may reduce the effectiveness of therapy is the limited drug delivery to the brain due to the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Advancements in nanotechnology are offering innovative solutions to bypass this obstacle. This review highlights the role of targeted nanoparticles (NPs) as effective drug carriers across the BBB for both early and late stages of AD. The distinct pathophysiological traits of these stages—such as amyloid aggregation, abnormal accumulation of tau protein, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress—are examined for their impact on therapy. The analysis includes lipid-based, polymeric, and inorganic NPs, exploring their unique properties for drug delivery. Strategies to target NPs to brain tissues affected by AD are discussed, emphasizing surface modifications to enhance BBB permeability. Uptake mechanisms like receptor-mediated and adsorptive-mediated transcytosis are detailed alongside safety, toxicity, and biocompatibility evaluations to assess clinical feasibility. Key findings indicate that targeted NPs significantly improve brain drug bioavailability and enable stage-specific therapeutic interventions, addressing challenges unique to early and late AD. Future research should focus on optimizing NP design for enhanced targeting specificity and minimizing long-term toxicity, ultimately paving the way for personalized nanomedicine approaches in AD treatment.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Blood–brain barrier, Nanoparticles, Receptor-mediated transcytosis, Adsorptive-mediated transcytosis

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a major and growing neurodegenerative disorder, significantly impairing memory, cognition, and daily functioning [1]. In 2023, it ranked as the sixth leading cause of death in the United States, reflecting its increasing societal impact [2]. Current treatments can only slow disease progression and do not prevent neuronal loss. Additionally, many drugs have side effects and require frequent dosing due to short half-lives, complicating patient management [1].

A critical barrier to effective therapy is the blood–brain barrier (BBB), a selective interface that restricts the entry of most therapeutic agents into the brain [3, 4]. Its unique cellular composition and tight junctions (TJs) maintain brain homeostasis but also limit drug delivery, a challenge amplified in AD treatment [3]. Although some small molecules may transiently cross via paracellular routes, these are generally insufficient for therapeutic purposes in AD [5, 6]. Moreover, BBB integrity can vary during AD progression, influencing drug permeability and complicating treatment timing [7].

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems aim to overcome these challenges by exploiting specialized BBB transport mechanisms, primarily receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) and adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT) [8, 9]. Targeting receptors such as transferrin, insulin, and LDL-related proteins allows selective and efficient nanoparticle (NP) uptake, facilitating delivery of therapeutic agents directly to affected brain regions [10, 11]. This targeted approach is especially critical for AD, where precise delivery to amyloid plaques and inflamed tissue is necessary to maximize efficacy and minimize systemic toxicity [12].

Advances in NP surface modifications, including attachment of polyethylene glycol (PEGylation) and ligand conjugation, further enhance stability, circulation, and BBB penetration, though balancing these factors remains a key development challenge [13, 14]. These tailored nanocarriers hold promise to address the unique barriers imposed by the BBB in AD, potentially enabling therapies that effectively reach and modify disease pathology [15].

Pathophysiology of Alzheimer's Disease: Early vs. Late Stages

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a constellation of pathological features, including the accumulation of neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaques, heightened oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation, alongside a marked decline in cholinergic neurotransmission. The disease typically presents with recent memory deficits in its early stages, gradually progressing to severe cognitive impairment that significantly compromises the ability to perform essential activities of daily living in late stages [16].

Multiple hypotheses have been proposed to elucidate the pathophysiology underlying AD. One of them is the beta-amyloid (Aβ) hypothesis [17]. Physiologically, amyloid precursor protein (APP) can undergo either non-amyloidogenic or amyloidogenic processing. While the former precludes Aβ generation, the latter—via β- and γ-secretase cleavage—leads to the production of Aβ peptides, primarily Aβ40 and the more aggregation-prone Aβ42 isoform [17–19]. Aβ42, due to its hydrophobicity, more readily forms toxic oligomers and amyloid plaques, which are central to the amyloid cascade hypothesis of AD pathogenesis [20, 21]. Mutations in APP and presenilin genes can increase the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, accelerating plaque formation and downstream neurodegeneration, particularly in familial forms of AD [22].

Another key hypothesis in AD pathogenesis involves the tau protein, which normally stabilizes microtubules in neurons. In AD, tau undergoes hyperphosphorylation and other post-translational modifications, leading to its aggregation into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) and disruption of neuronal function [19, 23, 24]. Imbalances in tau isoforms and impaired microtubule binding further contribute to neurodegeneration. Numerous studies have linked tau pathology to cognitive decline, with some evidence suggesting it progresses more rapidly than Aβ accumulation and correlates with neuroinflammation, making it a valuable prognostic marker [20, 25–28].

Oxidative stress is another critical factor in AD pathophysiology [19]. It results from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses. ROS, including superoxide radicals, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide, are generated in mitochondria, peroxisomes, and the endoplasmic reticulum. At physiological levels, ROS aid signaling, but excess ROS cause oxidative damage, inflammation, and reactive nitrogen species formation [29]. Impaired Aβ clearance heightens ROS, damaging proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Tau pathology also contributes by disrupting nucleic acid integrity. Deficient antioxidants like glutathione and vitamin C worsen the damage, exacerbating oxidative stress. Metal ion dyshomeostasis (e.g., Cu, Zn) also impairs antioxidant enzyme activity [30, 31].

According to the neuroinflammation hypothesis, immune dysfunction drives neuronal impairment in AD. Microglia and astrocytes, the brain’s primary immune cells, are involved in homeostasis and response to injury. Microglia, constantly surveying the brain environment, play crucial roles in immune surveillance and neuroprotection by phagocytosing neurons. Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells, support neuronal function by releasing neurotransmitters and removing toxins. Under pathological conditions, both microglia and astrocytes undergo phenotypic changes, shifting towards a pro-inflammatory state [32].

Imbalances in immune cell activation—such as insufficient activation under mild conditions or excessive activation in severe cases—can significantly contribute to the development of various central nervous system (CNS) disorders, including AD, Parkinson’s disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [33].

Neuroinflammation is a key contributor to AD progression. While early immune responses may aid in the clearance of Aβ and tau aggregates, prolonged activation of microglia and astrocytes leads to chronic inflammation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, promoting neuronal damage in vulnerable brain regions like the neocortex and hippocampus [34–36]. In parallel, recruitment of adaptive immune cells—particularly T and B lymphocytes—can further exacerbate neurodegeneration through sustained cytokine production despite their initial roles in aggregate clearance [37–41]. These findings highlight neuroinflammatory pathways as promising therapeutic targets in AD.

Challenges in Drug Delivery Across the Blood–Brain Barrier

The BBB is defined by the specialized structure of brain microvasculature, characterized by TJs, limited vesicular transport, and minimal fenestration, all of which tightly regulate molecular exchange between blood and brain [42]. This unique vascular architecture eliminates the need for a well-defined lymphatic system within the CNS. Beyond this vascular core, elements such as the choroid plexus, circumventricular organs, and neurovascular unit further refine BBB function and drug permeability [37, 43, 44]. Factors including cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, binding proteins, and neuroinflammation critically influence drug transport and must be accounted for in AD therapy design [45, 46]. BBB dysfunction—whether structural or functional—can both result from and contribute to AD progression, making it a potential therapeutic target.

BBB disruption progresses alongside disease advancement, exhibiting differences between the early and late stages. Characteristic pathological features observed in the early phase of AD include, among others, pericyte damage—which may even precede Aβ deposition [47]. Another early-stage feature is transporter dysfunction, specifically reduced expression of GLUT1 and LRP1 prior to brain atrophy [48, 49]. This is accompanied by increased permeability, particularly in the hippocampus, which correlates with pericyte impairment [50, 51]. These alterations occur independently of classical AD biomarkers such as Aβ and tau [52]. In advanced AD, BBB alterations are marked by the progressive loss of TJ proteins—namely, decreased expression of occludin, claudin-5, and ZO-1 [48, 49]. Post-mortem studies of AD patients show extensive loss or delocalization of these proteins in brain capillaries, which strongly correlates with BBB leakage [53]. Furthermore, transporter impairment worsens, notably with decreased P-glycoprotein activity [49, 54]. There is also increased penetration of immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, and T lymphocytes, into the brain parenchyma [48, 49]. Simultaneously, widespread leakage and deposition of blood-derived proteins such as fibrinogen, thrombin, and albumin are observed [49, 55]. Interestingly, BBB disruption in early mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD is characterized by minimal gadolinium leakage on DCE-MRI, whereas in later stages, more focal lesions, such as microhemorrhages, are apparent on SWI-MRI [56].

Due to the limitations of in vivo models, such as low spatiotemporal resolution and limited translational relevance, in vitro human BBB models have gained prominence. These systems allow for high-throughput, reproducible studies of drug delivery mechanisms [57]. Although 2D and 3D models have advanced our understanding of Aβ transport and APOE-related effects [58, 59], they still fall short in replicating the full complexity of BBB–neuron interactions and the extent of BBB disruption seen in AD.

Advances in microfluidic technologies have enhanced in vitro BBB models by integrating multicellular interactions. 3D models with neurons, endothelial cells, or iPSC-derived vascular components have revealed features like Aβ deposition, increased permeability, and APOE4-associated dysfunction [41, 60]. However, their limited culture duration restricts modeling of long-term pathology. Cerebral organoids offer a more promising platform for studying chronic neurodegeneration.

Nanotechnology provides targeted strategies to enhance BBB permeability. Key NP properties—such as size, charge, and amphiphilicity—determine transport efficiency and uptake routes, which may vary with AD progression [61]. Functionalized NPs also enable theragnostic applications, though advanced BBB disruption may limit their efficacy [62]. Intranasal delivery, bypassing the BBB via neural pathways, has emerged as an alternative, particularly effective for peptide-modified or mucoadhesive NPs delivering hydrophilic or biologic agents [63].

Nanoparticles as Drug Carriers in Alzheimer's Disease Treatment

The BBB presents a significant obstacle to effective drug delivery. However, the use of innovative therapeutic nanotechnologies may offer a solution to this challenge [64]. Owing to their unique physicochemical properties and inherent ability to penetrate the BBB, engineered NPs with dimensions below 100 nm show considerable promise in addressing a range of biological and pharmacological challenges [65]. The ability of these NPs to traverse the BBB significantly enhances the prospects for successful therapeutic management of AD [66]. Achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes requires maintaining adequate drug bioavailability, pharmacodynamics, and pharmacokinetics. By incorporating drugs into polymeric or lipid-based NPs, it is possible to significantly enhance their efficacy. This approach improves drug solubility, extends half-life, and facilitates targeted delivery to the intended site of action [67]. Comparative Summary of Nanoparticle Types for Alzheimer’s Disease Therapy

Lipid-based NPs

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) represent a specialized class of nanocarriers composed of lipids—organic molecules including fats, oils, and related compounds.

Liposomes

Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of lipid bilayers, encapsulating a hydrophilic core for drug delivery [68]. Based on their size, they are classified as small unilamellar vesicles (< 100 nm), large unilamellar vesicles (100–1000 nm), and giant unilamellar vesicles (> 1000 nm) [69]. Typically, their size ranges between 50 and 500 nm, which significantly influences the delivery process. Liposomes can be readily functionalized for active targeting, thereby enhancing their ability to cross the BBB and improve pharmacokinetic profiles [70]. This strategy has demonstrated significant potential in enhancing the efficacy of approved drugs such as rivastigmine, galantamine, and donepezil for the treatment of AD [70–73]. By incorporating drugs into their nanostructure, liposomes enhance solubility and protect active substances against chemical and biological degradation.

Solid Lipid Nanoparticles (SLNs)

SLNs are lipid-based nanocarriers with sizes ranging from 10 to 1000 nm. High-pressure homogenization is a solvent-free manufacturing method for SLNs, offering several advantages, including improved biocompatibility [74]. This technique avoids the use of organic solvents, employing water as the solvent medium. Additionally, the widespread availability of raw materials enables cost-effective and scalable SLN production [75]. Despite their hydrophobic core, SLNs are capable of encapsulating both hydrophilic and lipophilic drug molecules [74]. Moreover, SLNs provide a stable environment that protects drug molecules from external degradation, thus enhancing their overall stability [76]. SLNs can effectively transport drugs across the BBB, enabling the delivery of compounds that are otherwise impermeable [74]. They increase drug retention in the bloodstream and create a drug gradient, potentially inducing the opening of tight junctions. Furthermore, SLNs utilize receptor-mediated and adsorptive-mediated transcytosis to cross the BBB and enable sustained drug release [74].

Nanostructured Lipid Carriers (NLCs)

NLCs, often referred to as the second generation of LNPs, represent a more advanced design approach. They are composed of aqueous and lipid phases, along with a surfactant. Their size varies depending on the concentration of surfactant used during synthesis [77]. NLCs exhibit high physical stability and encapsulation efficiency (EE) while requiring minimal surfactant concentrations, thereby reducing potential toxicity. They share with SLNs the ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs [78]. NLCs also enable the incorporation of larger drug quantities due to lower water content and reduced leakage during storage [77]. Moreover, the preparation of NLCs does not involve toxic solvents, resulting in a biocompatible drug delivery system. These NPs have been shown to improve the bioavailability of anti-AD medications [79].

Polymeric NPs

Polymeric NPs are solid colloidal particles composed of organic polymers [64]. Their dimensions typically range from 1 to 1000 nm [80]. These NPs are synthesized through the polymerization of a wide variety of monomers using different techniques, allowing for precise control over their physicochemical properties to meet specific application requirements [64]. The most commonly used polymeric NPs are derived from synthetic, natural, or hybrid materials [81]. For brain targeting, systems based on natural, synthetic, and hybrid materials are frequently employed; however, in the context of AD, synthetic polymers such as chitosan and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) are the most common choice [81]. Nevertheless, NPs may adversely affect the cerebral vasculature, potentially reducing endothelial permeability. The use of surfactants can enhance drug delivery across the BBB by increasing the fluidity of the endothelial cell membrane [82].

Nasal administration of NPs offers a promising route for enhancing drug absorption and facilitating targeted delivery to the brain [83]. To support nasal absorption, PEG polymers or antibodies, along with mucoadhesive coatings that prolong residence time in the nasal cavity, are often used [83]. NPs composed of poly(n-butyl cyanoacrylate) and coated with polysorbate 80 have been developed to deliver tacrine [84]. In murine models, these coated NPs were shown to be significantly more effective than free drug or uncoated NPs. The improved brain delivery is believed to result from interactions between the polysorbate 80 coating and endothelial cells of the brain microvasculature [84].

Inorganic NPs

In recent years, metallic NPs have emerged as valuable tools in the treatment of neurological disorders, including AD. Gold-based nanoparticles (AuNPs) are the most extensively studied, although NPs containing silver, iron oxide, platinum, and cerium oxide are also well-documented in the literature [64]. Metallic NPs offer high versatility, ease of fabrication, surface functionalization with diverse groups, and precise control over their size and morphology [85]. Recent research has focused on conjugating metallic NPs with various biomolecules, including therapeutic agents, DNA/RNA, peptides, antibodies, and aptamers [86].

Owing to their biocompatibility and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval, AuNPs have become a leading candidate for multiple medical applications. They offer several key advantages, including high stability, reliable synthetic methods, size-dependent optical properties, and significant potential for surface modification [86].

Iron oxide NPs (Fe₃O₄) possess favorable attributes such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, low toxicity, and chemical stability across a range of conditions, including variations in temperature and pH, making them well-suited for targeted drug delivery [87]. These NPs also exhibit magnetic properties, allowing external manipulation via magnetic fields [88]. Moreover, their surface—rich in hydroxyl groups—facilitates straightforward functionalization with other therapeutic or diagnostic molecules [86].

Exosomes and Other Biological Carriers

Exosomes are cell-derived nanocarriers that represent one of the most promising classes of biological vectors for drug delivery across the BBB in the treatment of AD. These vesicles, typically 30–150 nm in diameter, are composed of lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and other signaling molecules, and can be engineered to carry therapeutic agents such as drugs, peptides, or RNA. Exosomes exhibit low immunogenicity and high biocompatibility, thereby minimizing the risk of immune responses and facilitating efficient delivery to the brain [89, 90]. It has been demonstrated that exosomes are capable of transporting both pathogenic molecules (e.g., Aβ, tau) and therapeutic compounds. Their role in AD is dual: although they contribute to the disease’s pathogenesis, they also assist in the clearance of toxic protein aggregates and modulate neuroinflammation [91, 92].

In addition to exosomes, other biological carriers include liposomes (as discussed in Sect. "Liposomes"), polymeric NPs composed of natural materials, dendrimers, and a variety of other biomaterials. These systems, particularly when functionalized with surface ligands targeting BBB receptors, are capable of efficiently crossing the BBB and delivering therapeutics to brain regions affected by AD pathology [93, 94]. Dendrimers, in particular, are highly branched, monodisperse macromolecules with a well-defined structure, generally ranging from 10 to 100 nm in size [95]. Due to the high density of modifiable surface functional groups, dendrimers can be readily conjugated with therapeutic agents, BBB-targeting ligands, or diagnostic markers [96]. Several studies have confirmed that dendrimers are capable of penetrating the BBB and enabling targeted drug delivery to neurons affected by AD-related changes [97, 98]. Certain classes of dendrimers, such as poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) and poly-L-lysine-based structures, not only act as delivery platforms but also inhibit Aβ aggregation and may even disrupt existing amyloid plaques, thereby neutralizing their neurotoxicity [95, 99]. Moreover, dendrimers have been reported to exert anti-inflammatory effects and anti-tau activity, positioning them as multifunctional candidates for the complex therapeutic management of AD [95].

Targeting Strategies for Early and Late Alzheimer’s Disease

Drug development for AD has been hindered by numerous obstacles, including the selection of appropriate drug targets, the limitations of current biomarkers and animal models, and other factors such as delayed treatment initiation, dose-related side effects, and the challenge of overcoming the BBB. Furthermore, the heterogeneous presentation of the disease across patients significantly complicates drug development efforts [106]. The Aβ hypothesis, while extensively researched, faces significant challenges due to the diverse structural forms of Aβ and the existence of numerous pathogenic variants, complicating the identification of precise drug targets [107].

The unique features of NPs, such as their small size, modifiable surfaces, enhanced solubility, and multifunctionality, enable them to interact with biological systems in novel ways. Notably, their small size facilitates transport across the BBB via endocytosis, followed by biodegradation within the brain microenvironment, which results in the controlled release of the encapsulated drug [108]. Nano-based Approaches for Targeting Aβ Plaques

The development of Aβ-targeted nanotherapeutic strategies has centered on modulating Aβ production, inhibiting aggregation, and enhancing clearance, in alignment with the amyloid cascade hypothesis [109]. Clinical research has prioritized the reduction of Aβ production, mainly through inhibition of β- and γ-secretases or stimulation of α-secretase [110]. Although nanoformulations have demonstrated potential in these areas, γ-secretase inhibition has been associated with significant adverse effects due to its involvement in multiple physiological processes. Despite notable advancements in targeting α- and β-secretases using nanotechnology, further research is required to evaluate long-term safety [111].

Modulation of Aβ production

As the enzyme BACE1 plays a crucial role in the production of Aβ, a key factor in AD, inhibiting BACE1 activity represents a promising therapeutic strategy. However, the delivery of BACE1-targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to the brain remains a major challenge due to the BBB and the protective membranes surrounding neurons.

Researchers have investigated the use of fluorescent quantum dots (QDs) as carriers for BACE1 siRNAs. For instance, QDs coated with a polyethylene glycol (PEG) layer have been formulated into stable nanocomplexes capable of crossing the BBB. These complexes effectively suppressed BACE1 gene expression in human neuroblastoma cells, demonstrating their potential in AD therapy [112].

Furthermore, the unique optical properties of QDs enable real-time tracking and monitoring of siRNA delivery within the brain, providing valuable insights into the distribution and therapeutic efficacy of this approach [113].

Several nanoparticle-based strategies have also been explored for siRNA delivery to the brain in AD treatment. Among them are exosome-based systems and micellar nanoparticles. Shyam et al. developed micellar nanoparticles with various morphologies by encapsulating siRNA into carriers composed of linear polyethyleneimine grafted with PEG (LPEI-g-PEG). This system delivered siRNA targeting both BACE1 and APP into the brain’s ventricular space. Notably, rod-shaped nanoparticles showed the highest efficiency in reducing BACE1 levels following intracerebroventricular administration [114].

Another approach involves the inhibition of Rho-associated kinase II (ROCKII), a key regulator of neuronal function. The study by Liu and colleagues employed PEG-PEI NPs to deliver ROCKII-siRNA to neural stem cells in vitro, effectively downregulating ROCKII mRNA expression [115].

These findings underscore the potential of NP-based methods for siRNA delivery and the modulation of key pathways involved in AD pathogenesis.

Inhibition of Aβ aggregation

Inhibiting the aggregation of Aβ may serve as an effective therapeutic strategy for AD, given that Aβ oligomers are significantly more neurotoxic than monomers [116]. Nanotechnology provides promising means to suppress protein aggregation, particularly Aβ aggregation, through the functionalization of NPs with Aβ-binding peptides and other targeting ligands.

The KLVFF peptide, recognized for its ability to specifically bind Aβ and inhibit its aggregation, has been successfully incorporated into various nanoformulations [117].

For example, a multifunctional nanoformulation (Rapa@DAK/siRNA) was developed, combining rapamycin, BACE1 siRNA, and the Aβ-targeting KLVFF peptide. This system was further modified with aleuria aurantia lectin for intranasal delivery and utilized a PEGylated dendrigraft poly-L-lysine (DGL) backbone. In a transgenic mouse model, this approach significantly reduced Aβ deposition, enhanced autophagy, and improved cognitive performance [108].

Nanoformulation Promoting Aβ Clearance

Aβ clearance primarily occurs through two pathways: enzymatic degradation by microglia and astrocytes, and receptor-mediated clearance via low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein (LRP). In late-onset AD, these clearance mechanisms become impaired, leading to the accumulation of Aβ. Enhancing Aβ removal pathways, such as by promoting LRP-mediated efflux, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for AD [108].

Nanotechnology shows significant potential in enhancing Aβ clearance by supporting both major degradation pathways. Engineered NPs facilitate the enzymatic breakdown of Aβ by glial cells. A novel nano-cleaner was recently developed, comprising a rapamycin-loaded, ROS-responsive PLGA core (R@(ox-PLGA)-KcD). The surface of this system was functionalized with acid-cleavable DAG peptides to enhance cellular uptake, and KLVFF peptides to specifically target Aβ within AD lesions. KLVFF peptides promoted microglia-mediated Aβ clearance, mitigating Aβ-induced neurotoxicity. Additionally, the nano-cleaner responded to elevated ROS levels by releasing rapamycin, further enhancing Aβ degradation and regulating neuroinflammation [108].

Moreover, NPs support receptor-mediated Aβ efflux by strongly binding Aβ peptides and targeting LRP or its ligands. Enhancing peripheral Aβ clearance facilitates receptor-mediated Aβ transport from the brain. In APP/PS1 mice, mApoE-PA-LIP nanoparticles were shown to destabilize insoluble Aβ aggregates and convert them into soluble forms, promoting Aβ clearance from the brain to the periphery [118]. Recent studies demonstrate that this peripheral “sink” action of mApoE-PA-LIP substantially reduces cerebral Aβ burden without significant adverse effects [110].

Nano-based Approaches Targeting Tau Tangles

Given the limited success of amyloid-targeting therapies in AD, recent research has shifted focus to strategies that inhibit tau aggregation and promote tau degradation. A study conducted under the direction of Zhu described the development of self-assembling polymeric micelles coated with a tau-binding peptide, resulting in a multifunctional nanotherapeutic agent that specifically targets tau. This nano-inhibitor slowed tau aggregation, enabled its detection, and inhibited its ability to seed further aggregation in neurons. The multivalent interaction with aggregating tau significantly reduced tau-mediated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, the resulting nano-inhibitor–tau complexes were more susceptible to degradation than mature aggregates, thereby potentially increasing therapeutic efficacy [119].

In their study, Barracchia and colleagues functionalized dye-loaded silica nanoparticles by conjugating tau proteins to their surface, enabling interaction with tau aggregates or condensates. Although these modified NPs did not significantly affect tau aggregate morphology or formation, they associated with tau assemblies. Due to electrostatic interactions, the NPs were incorporated into tau-induced condensates, allowing real-time visualization of these dynamic liquid droplets using fluorescence microscopy. Notably, the NPs entered human neuroglioma cells without inducing cytotoxicity [120].

Approaches Targeting Oxidative Stress

Modifying the aberrant microenvironment within AD lesions by mitigating ROS cascades presents a novel therapeutic approach for early-stage intervention. The receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) exhibits elevated expression on cerebral vascular endothelial cells, astrocytes, and neurons within AD lesions [121].

Scientific evidence clearly indicates that targeting ROS and oxidative stress is more effective during the early stages of AD than in advanced stages. Oxidative stress markers increase even in the preclinical phase and during MCI, while antioxidant therapies in animal models have been shown to delay the disease course and improve cognitive functions [122, 123]. NPs enable early detection and effective suppression of oxidative stress and cognitive deficits in transgenic mice at disease onset, supporting the therapeutic potential of this mechanism in early AD [122].

In contrast, in advanced AD—characterized by widespread neuronal damage and complex pathological remodeling—the effectiveness of antioxidant therapy is significantly reduced. Numerous unsuccessful clinical trials have reported minimal cognitive improvements in patients at advanced disease stages [123–125]. One of the primary barriers to therapeutic efficacy in late-stage AD is the unidirectional nature of neurodegenerative processes and the emergence of secondary pathologies such as chronic neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction. In summary, ROS-targeting strategies exhibit the greatest efficacy in early AD, before irreversible neuronal damage occurs [122, 123].

In a recent study, researchers developed a novel drug delivery system (Ibu&FK@RNPs) for co-delivery of ibuprofen and the calcineurin inhibitor tacrolimus (FK506), specifically targeting RAGE and elevated ROS levels in AD. These nanoparticles were designed with a ROS-sensitive thioether bond (MAH-EDT), allowing for controlled drug release in response to heightened ROS conditions. By targeting RAGE, the delivery system preferentially released the drugs within astrocytes in AD lesions. This approach effectively reduced ROS-mediated Aβ accumulation and suppressed NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation [126].

In another study, researchers designed multi-targeting regulators of neuroinflammation (PDA@K) by conjugating KLVFF peptides to polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles. PDA, known for its metal-chelating and ROS-scavenging capabilities, formed the core of the nanoformulation. This strategy significantly reduced ROS levels, inhibited Aβ aggregation, alleviated neuroinflammation, and ultimately improved cognitive performance in mice [127].

Advancements of Nanotechnology in Alzheimer’s Disease Diagnostic and Theragnostic

Nanotechnology has substantially advanced diagnostic and theragnostic strategies for AD by enabling early detection of biomarkers and simultaneous targeted treatment. NPs, such as gold NPs, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), liposomes, and dendrimers, have been shown in recent studies to be functionalized with antibodies or ligands that specifically target Aβ and tau proteins. This allows for targeted imaging of pathological deposits and early brain alterations using modalities such as MRI, PET, or optical fluorescence-based techniques [128–130]. Electrochemical and optical biosensor-based nanodiagnostics also enable ultrasensitive detection of AD biomarkers in biological fluids, offering promising prospects for non-invasive disease monitoring [128].

Special emphasis is placed on theragnostic nanomaterials that integrate diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities within a single platform. For instance, magnetic NPs can act as both contrast agents and drug carriers, allowing real-time imaging alongside the treatment of neurodegenerative lesions [129, 131]. Preclinical and early clinical studies support the potential of these approaches to improve early AD detection and enhance the safety and efficacy of therapeutic interventions, thereby paving the way for breakthroughs in personalized neurodegenerative medicine [108, 131, 132].

Mechanisms of Nanoparticle Uptake Across the Blood–Brain Barrier

Crossing the BBB involves the use of specialized techniques which exploit the unique properties of brain endothelial cells. The endothelial layer, composed of tightly packed cells connected by tight junctions, forms a highly selective physical and biochemical barrier known as the BBB [3]. This barrier protects the central nervous system by restricting the entry of neurotoxic agents, while allowing essential nutrients and signaling molecules to pass through [133]. However, the delivery of therapeutic NPs across the BBB requires them to bypass or utilize specific cellular transport mechanisms. Two primary mechanisms utilized for this purpose are AMT and RMT, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Mechanisms of Nanoparticle Transcytosis across the Blood–brain Barrier in Early- and Late-stage Alzheimer’s Disease

AMT is based on electrostatic interactions between positively charged NPs and the negatively charged luminal surface of endothelial cell membranes, primarily due to the presence of heparan sulfate proteoglycans and other anionic residues [8]. This interaction promotes non-specific endocytosis, allowing NPs—particularly those under 200 nm—to enter endothelial cells via clathrin- or caveolin-independent pathways [134]. Once internalized, NPs are transported across the cell and released on the abluminal side into the brain parenchyma. Modifying the surface of NPs to carry a net positive charge enhances this mechanism. For example, cationic polymers like polyethylenimine (PEI) or cell-penetrating peptides (e.g., TAT peptide) increase AMT efficiency by improving NP uptake [135]. However, excessive cationic charge may lead to cytotoxicity, thus requiring careful optimization [136].

RMT, on the other hand, relies on the binding of NPs to specific transmembrane receptors abundantly expressed on BBB endothelial cells, such as the transferrin receptor (TfR), insulin receptor (IR), low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1), or nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) [137]. Ligand-functionalized NPs bind to these receptors and are internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, followed by vesicular trafficking across the endothelial cell [9]. Targeting TfR is particularly well-studied: for example, NPs functionalized with transferrin, anti-TfR antibodies, or peptides like T7 or OX26 exhibit enhanced brain uptake due to their ability to engage the endogenous transferrin transport system [10]. Similarly, insulin-conjugated NPs utilize the IR pathway, while LRP1-targeting ligands such as angiopep-2 enhance NP penetration and accumulation in brain tumors or neurodegenerative lesions [138].

Surface modifications like PEGylation play a dual role. PEG chains increase the colloidal stability of NPs, reduce opsonization and immune recognition, and prolong circulation half-life, thereby increasing the likelihood of BBB interaction [139]. However, PEGylation can also shield targeting ligands, decreasing their ability to bind receptors—an effect mitigated by optimizing PEG length and density or using cleavable PEG linkers [140]. Another critical surface modification strategy is ligand conjugation, which involves attaching targeting moieties (e.g., peptides, antibodies, or small molecules) to the NP surface. This improves selectivity and uptake via RMT pathways. For instance, ligands such as RVG29 peptide (targeting nAChR) or ApoE-mimetic peptides (targeting LRP1) have demonstrated enhanced brain delivery in AD models [141].

In diseases like Alzheimer’s, where targeted delivery to Aβ plaques or inflamed regions is critical, ligand-functionalized NPs allow site-specific delivery, reducing off-target effects and systemic toxicity [142]. Thus, combining PEGylation for stability and ligand targeting for selectivity represents a synergistic strategy for improving therapeutic outcomes in CNS diseases [143].

Comparative Analysis of Nanoparticle Efficiency in Early vs. Late Alzheimer’s Disease

The effectiveness of NPs in treating AD varies depending on the stage of the disease. In the early stages of AD, the BBB remains mostly intact, with only minor changes in its permeability [82]. This makes it easier for NPs to cross the barrier and deliver therapeutic agents to the brain. Even though the BBB’s function is slightly compromised at this stage, it does not significantly hinder the delivery of NPs.

During this phase, NPs designed to target Aβ plaques—a key feature of AD—as well as anti-inflammatory pathways, have shown higher bioavailability and effectiveness [80, 144]. Aβ plaques are known to build up in the brains of people with AD, leading to disrupted cell communication and the death of neurons [145]. A promising approach to treating this involves using NPs that specifically target these plaques. These tiny particles act as carriers for substances that bind to or break down amyloid, helping to neutralize these harmful clusters. Additionally, treatments that reduce inflammation have proven to be particularly effective in the early stages of AD, as inflammation worsens the damage to brain cells [146]. NPs can also help by targeting the activation of microglia or the signaling of cytokines, thereby reducing the harmful effects of neuroinflammation. This makes NPs a hopeful therapeutic option in the early phases of AD [145, 146].

Receptor-mediated targeting is a highly effective strategy, especially during specific stages of disease progression. By functionalizing NPs with ligands that bind to Aβ or microglial receptors, researchers can selectively deliver therapeutics to affected brain regions [147]. This approach minimizes off-target effects and enhances treatment specificity, ensuring that drugs or therapeutic agents reach the precise areas requiring intervention. Such receptor-specific targeting significantly improves the precision of therapy, offering a more effective and targeted treatment option [147].

As AD progresses to its late stages, the effectiveness of nanoparticle-based therapies often diminishes due to the increased disruption of the BBB [148]. In the advanced stages of AD, vascular degeneration, breakdown of the BBB, and heightened inflammation lead to significant changes in the brain’s microvascular structure [149]. These alterations can hinder the ability of NPs to cross the BBB and reach their intended targets. While the BBB becomes more permeable, this increased permeability is not necessarily beneficial. It can result in the non-specific distribution of NPs, reducing their therapeutic efficacy and potentially causing off-target side effects [150].

The focus of nanoparticle-based therapies for late-stage AD is increasingly shifting toward the creation of neuroprotective agents and strategies to address oxidative stress, which becomes more severe as neurodegeneration progresses [151]. These therapies aim to develop agents capable of neutralizing free radicals, reducing neuronal damage, and promoting cellular repair [151]. The strategies mentioned aim to slow the progression of AD and protect surviving neurons, as neuronal death and dysfunction become more severe in later stages [152]. To achieve this, NP designs must consider the altered BBB dynamics and focus on delivering treatments that target both neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation [152, 153].

The development of nanoparticle-based therapies should be tailored to the specific needs of each stage of AD. In the early stages, the focus should be on targeting amyloid plaques, modulating inflammation, and delivering agents that prevent plaque buildup [154]. In contrast, during the later stages, NPs should prioritize neuroprotective strategies, such as providing antioxidants or neurotrophic factors to help repair and preserve neurons [107]. This stage-specific customization ensures that NPs are optimized for the unique pathophysiological environment of each stage, offering the most effective treatment for patients at different points in the disease progression. This approach also helps minimize potential side effects and maximizes therapeutic benefits by addressing the most prominent disease features at each stage [107].

Strategies for AD based on NPs exhibit a wide range of clinical readiness and translational potential. The most advanced of these are lipid-based NPs (liposomes and SLNs), which have proven biocompatibility and the ability to carry both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. However, their clinical application has been hindered by rapid clearance via the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and stability issues, resulting in some trial failures [9, 100].

Polymeric NPs offer the advantages of controlled drug release and modifiable surface properties that enhance targeting and circulation time. However, concerns about polymer degradation, toxicity, and inconsistent BBB penetration limit their clinical application, despite some encouraging preclinical data [100, 155]. Metallic NPs are valuable for their theragnostic capabilities but face significant barriers to translation due to potential long-term toxicity and bioaccumulation, which limit clinical progress [102, 103]. Dendrimers offer a high degree of functionalization and size control, but their complex synthesis and potential toxicity limit their clinical application [104]. Emerging biomimetic NPs and exosomes, which have improved natural BBB penetration and biocompatibility, are hindered by the challenges of scalable production and a lack of clinical data [156].

While lipid and polymer NPs are showing the most promise in terms of clinical readiness, it is crucial to overcome challenges such as standardized NP characterization, long-term safety assessment, and reproducible manufacturing if we are to boost success rates and make effective AD nanomedicine therapies a reality.

Stage-Dependent Challenges in Drug Delivery for AD

AD progresses through distinct pathological stages, each presenting unique challenges for effective drug delivery. Early-stage AD is characterized primarily by the accumulation of Aβ plaques and subtle synaptic dysfunction, whereas late-stage AD involves extensive neurodegeneration, widespread neuroinflammation, and pronounced BBB disruption [157, 158]. These differences critically influence the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of therapeutic agents, necessitating stage-specific delivery strategies.

In early AD, the BBB remains largely intact, maintaining stringent control over molecular passage into the brain [7]. Consequently, drugs targeting amyloid aggregation or oxidative stress must traverse a highly selective barrier, limiting the efficacy of conventional therapeutics [159]. NPs designed for early-stage intervention require optimized surface properties to engage RMT or AMT pathways without eliciting immune activation [9, 160]. Additionally, early targeting demands precise delivery to discrete brain regions with minimal off-target effects to prevent interference with normal neural function [161].

Conversely, late-stage AD features significant BBB disruption due to chronic inflammation and endothelial damage, which may increase nonspecific permeability but also exacerbate neurotoxicity risks [162]. While enhanced BBB permeability could theoretically facilitate drug entry, it also leads to uncontrolled exposure of brain tissue to circulating toxins and immune cells, complicating therapeutic delivery [163]. Moreover, late-stage pathology involves extensive neuronal loss and gliosis, which impair cellular uptake mechanisms and reduce the efficacy of receptor-targeted NPs [164]. Multi-drug delivery systems encapsulated within NPs are being explored to simultaneously address neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and synaptic repair in this phase.

Both stages pose challenges related to NP clearance and long-term safety, especially considering the altered brain microenvironment and impaired clearance mechanisms in AD [165]. Therefore, tailoring NP properties such as size, charge, and ligand specificity to disease stage is crucial for maximizing therapeutic benefit while minimizing toxicity [166].

In summary, early-stage AD requires drug delivery systems that efficiently penetrate an intact BBB and target amyloidogenic and oxidative pathways with high specificity. Late-stage AD demands strategies accommodating BBB disruption, increased inflammation, and compromised cellular uptake, often necessitating multifunctional NPs with broader therapeutic payloads. Understanding these stage-dependent differences is essential for developing effective nanomedicine-based interventions in AD.

Safety and Toxicity Considerations in Nanoparticle-Based Therapies

The safety and toxicity of NPs used for drug delivery, especially in the context of AD, are of paramount importance. The brain is a highly sensitive organ, and any therapeutic intervention must be meticulously evaluated to ensure it does not cause harm [160]. NPs hold great promise for targeted drug delivery, but their safe use hinges on several factors: cellular toxicity (NPs must not harm brain cells or disrupt neural function—this requires careful design to ensure biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity), immunogenic responses (NPs should not trigger adverse immune reactions, which could lead to inflammation or other complications in the brain), and efficient clearance (it is crucial that NPs are effectively cleared from the body after delivering their therapeutic payload, because accumulation in organs or tissues could lead to long-term toxicity) [160].

Emerging evidence also highlights additional safety concerns, particularly the risk of nanoparticle-induced neuroinflammation and disruption of BBB integrity after repeated exposure. Certain NPs, especially those composed of metals or non-degradable synthetic materials, have been shown to activate microglia and astrocytes, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS [167, 168]. This immune activation can lead to chronic inflammation, contributing to neural tissue damage. Moreover, repeated NP exposure may compromise tight junctions within the endothelial lining of the BBB, increasing its permeability and potentially allowing peripheral toxins or pathogens to enter the brain [169]. These risks emphasize the need for long-term safety studies and the development of NP systems with minimal immunotoxicity.

Biocompatibility

One of the most critical factors in ensuring the safety of NPs is their biocompatibility. This means that the NPs should not trigger harmful immune responses or cause toxicity in the tissues they interact with. Biocompatible NPs are designed to avoid significant immune reactions and should not interfere with normal cellular or tissue functions [170]. To achieve biocompatibility, the use of biodegradable materials is essential.

Common materials like PLGA and chitosan are often used in NP formulations because they break down naturally in the body into non-toxic byproducts, making them safer for medical and therapeutic applications [171]. PLGA degrades into lactic acid and glycolic acid, naturally occurring metabolites that the body can safely process and eliminate. This biodegradation process significantly reduces the risk of long-term NP accumulation in tissues—a major concern with non-biodegradable NPs that could lead to chronic toxicity over time [171]. Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide derived from shellfish, is another example of a biodegradable material used in brain-targeted NP systems. Its low toxicity, controlled drug release capability, and enhanced biocompatibility make it particularly suitable for therapies in neurodegenerative diseases [172].

Surface Modifications and PEGylation

Surface modifications are critical for enhancing the safety profile of NPs. Among the most common modifications is PEGylation, which involves attaching PEG chains to the NP surface. This technique greatly improves NP stability in the bloodstream by reducing aggregation and minimizing immune clearance. PEGylated NPs are better able to evade recognition by macrophages, thereby reducing early removal and enhancing therapeutic bioavailability [173].

PEGylation also significantly reduces immunogenicity—the likelihood of triggering an immune response. Without PEGylation, NPs may prompt antibody production or activate innate immune cells, potentially resulting in neuroinflammation or systemic immune complications [174]. By mitigating these responses, PEGylation enhances the overall safety of treatment for neurological conditions such as AD. Furthermore, it facilitates effective NP clearance by enabling safe renal or hepatic excretion, reducing the risk of organ accumulation and long-term toxicity [151].

Long-Term Safety and Clearance

The long-term safety of NPs is a critical factor in their therapeutic application. Even biodegradable NPs may accumulate in neural tissues or peripheral organs, potentially causing toxicity if clearance mechanisms are inefficient [175]. Therefore, NPs must be carefully engineered to favor elimination through physiological pathways, such as renal filtration or liver metabolism.

Insufficient clearance could result in chronic exposure to NPs and cumulative tissue damage, including within the brain, particularly with repeated dosing regimens [175]. Another significant risk is cytotoxicity—damage to neurons or glial cells from prolonged NP presence—which may exacerbate neurodegenerative processes already present in AD [82]. This underscores the importance of integrating toxicity assessments, neuroinflammatory profiling, and BBB integrity studies in the early stages of NP development.

The long-term immunogenicity of NPs and their potential accumulation in the brains of people with AD are critical safety concerns that require thorough investigation. Chronic exposure to NPs, particularly non-biodegradable or metallic types, can lead to sustained activation of microglia and neuroinflammation, which could worsen AD pathology [176]. Several studies have reported that certain NPs can accumulate in brain tissue over extended periods, increasing the risk of toxicity and interference with normal cellular functions [177]. In contrast, biodegradable polymeric NPs tend to degrade and clear faster, reducing the risk of long-term accumulation [178]. However, there is currently a lack of comprehensive longitudinal in vivo studies assessing NP biodistribution, clearance kinetics, and immune responses in AD-relevant models. Closing these gaps through the use of advanced imaging techniques and immunohistochemical analyses is essential for establishing the safety profiles of NP-based therapeutics for chronic neurodegenerative conditions.

Comparison of NP Toxicity Profiles

The development of NP-based therapies for AD depends on a critical distinction in their biocompatibility and toxicity profiles, which vary significantly depending on material composition. Metallic NPs, such as those made of silver, gold, or iron oxide, have been shown to cause dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity in neural systems, primarily due to oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and pro-inflammatory responses. For example, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been reported to cause significant neurotoxicity at concentrations above 10 μg/ml in primary cortical neurons, resulting in increased production of ROS and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release [179]. In contrast, polymeric NPs, particularly those based on PLGA or chitosan, are generally more biocompatible and biodegradable. PLGA NPs have demonstrated minimal cytotoxic effects at concentrations up to 100 μg/ml in both neuronal and glial cell cultures, and in vivo studies suggest lower accumulation in brain tissue and faster systemic clearance compared to metallic analogues [180]. The importance of selecting materials rationally when designing safer and more effective products is highlighted by these quantitative differences [181].

Clinical Trials

Many nanoparticle-based AD therapies have failed in clinical trials, primarily due to poor BBB penetration and low brain delivery, as well as promiscuous peripheral accumulation, which limits efficacy and raises toxicity concerns [182]. Upon systemic injection, NPs often adsorb serum proteins to form a "protein corona" that alters pharmacokinetics, targeting specificity, and immunogenicity [183, 184]. In addition, many AD trials do not target the disease until it is already in a mild to moderate stage, and they do not use a patient stratification system based on biomarkers. Issues of suboptimal dosing and trial design have been highlighted by the failures of previous drugs such as semagacestat and solanezumab [185]. The complexity of aged human brains, including inflammation and biomarker heterogeneity, is poorly recapitulated by preclinical models (largely rodent amyloid-transgenic mice), limiting translational predictivity [186].

To overcome these barriers, strategies include functionalizing NPs with BBB-targeting ligands (e.g., transferrin, ApoE-mimetics, mannose-integrated or dual-peptide systems) to enable receptor-mediated transport across the BBB. Prolonging circulation and modulating protein corona formation can be achieved through PEGylation or zwitterionic coatings. Finally, physical BBB modulation techniques, such as low-intensity focused ultrasound with microbubbles or convection-enhanced delivery, can be employed. Furthermore, the use of biodegradable and biocompatible materials such as lipid NPs, PLGA, and chitosan enhances safety and clearance [187, 188].

Clinical translation may be improved by initiating trials in early-stage biomarker-positive patients (using amyloid/tau PET or cerebrospinal fluid) and by using combinatorial regimens that address neuroinflammation or vascular dysfunction alongside anti-amyloid therapies. Trials should also use human-relevant platforms, such as aged animal models, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons, or brain organoids [186, 189, 190]. Finally, consistent product quality and approval require scalable, reproducible manufacturing processes (e.g., microfluidic synthesis and rigorous physicochemical characterization), combined with early regulatory engagement [191]. A recent study demonstrated the successful delivery of IRAK4 inhibitors across the BBB in mice using dual peptide-functionalized polymeric NPs, highlighting their translational potential [192].

The clinical translation of nanoparticle-based therapies for AD is currently limited, with relatively few candidates progressing to advanced clinical stages. Several early-phase trials have investigated the use of polymeric or lipid-based nanocarriers to improve the delivery of small molecules or biologics that target Aβ or tau pathology. However, many have encountered challenges relating to safety, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy, resulting in trial termination or limited progression [9, 100]. For instance, a phase 2 trial investigating a liposomal formulation of curcumin was halted due to suboptimal pharmacokinetics and inadequate clinical benefits [193]. Meanwhile, newer strategies incorporating multifunctional NPs with BBB-targeting ligands are entering early clinical testing, emphasizing the need for optimized design, patient stratification, and biomarker-based outcome measures [194]. Discussion

NPs offer significant potential as a therapeutic approach for AD, particularly in overcoming challenges posed by the BBB. The BBB is a major obstacle to delivering many drugs to the brain, highlighting the importance of developing NP systems capable of traversing this barrier to advance AD treatments [25]. Beyond their ability to cross the BBB, NPs can be tailored to deliver therapeutic agents precisely to affected brain regions, enabling highly targeted interventions [195]. This specificity is crucial in AD, where neuronal damage is closely linked to the buildup of toxic Aβ plaques and tau protein tangles. However, the complexity of Alzheimer's, particularly as it progresses from early to late stages, requires careful design and optimization of NP systems to ensure efficacy across all phases of the disease [196].

In the early stages of AD, the BBB remains mostly intact, though its permeability shows some changes. This presents an opportunity for nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems since the barrier's integrity is relatively preserved [197]. NPs can be engineered to target Aβ plaques, neuroinflammation, and other early indicators of the disease. For instance, NPs functionalized with ligands targeting specific endothelial cell receptors, such as transferrin or insulin, can enable RMT, facilitating their efficient passage through the BBB [197]. Additionally, adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT), which relies on electrostatic interactions between positively charged NPs and negatively charged cell membranes, may enhance the penetration of smaller NPs into the brain. These strategies allow for the targeted delivery of anti-inflammatory agents or plaque-disrupting molecules, potentially slowing disease progression during its early stages [198].

As AD progresses to later stages, the integrity of the BBB becomes significantly compromised. This creates new challenges for NP delivery. While a more permeable BBB might make it easier for NPs to reach the brain, it also raises concerns about off-target effects [199]. In advanced stages of AD, the brain's vasculature often shows severe damage, and the increased BBB permeability can result in NPs being distributed to unintended areas. This could enhance drug delivery to regions with reduced BBB integrity but also heightens the risk of toxicity and immunogenicity, as NPs may accumulate in non-target tissues and cause side effects [200].

Designing stage-specific NP therapies is essential to address these challenges effectively. For early-stage AD, NPs optimized for relatively intact BBB function can deliver drugs with high precision. In contrast, for late-stage AD, NPs may need to account for the compromised BBB's altered properties [201]. Modifications such as PEGylation can help reduce immune system recognition, prolong circulation times, and improve NP stability in the bloodstream, increasing their ability to reach the brain [202]. Furthermore, advanced multifunctional NPs that combine therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities (theragnostics) could play a critical role. These NPs could not only target Aβ plaques or tau tangles but also provide real-time monitoring of disease progression, enabling a personalized and dynamic approach to treatment [203].

Safety and toxicity are critical considerations when developing nanoparticle-based therapies for AD. Many NPs are composed of synthetic materials like polymers, lipids, or metals, which could pose toxicological risks if not properly designed or cleared from the body. Ensuring that NPs are biocompatible and biodegradable can minimize long-term accumulation and reduce cytotoxicity in neural tissues. Commonly used materials such as PLGA and chitosan degrade into non-toxic byproducts, reducing these risks [201]. Additionally, surface functionalization with biomolecules like peptides or antibodies can improve targeting specificity and biocompatibility, reducing the likelihood of immune reactions or damage to healthy tissues. Nonetheless, rigorous toxicological evaluations are necessary to assess long-term effects, especially given the potential for NP accumulation in sensitive tissues like the brain [202].

Scalability and regulatory approval are other significant challenges for nanoparticle-based treatments. Large-scale production of NPs that comply with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards is essential for clinical applications. Manufacturing processes must be optimized to ensure consistency, safety, and quality while addressing cost barriers to make these treatments accessible to patients. Additionally, clinical trials must rigorously assess the safety and efficacy of NP formulations in humans, considering the complexity of AD and variability in patient responses [203].

Despite promising preclinical results, the clinical translation of nanomedicine-based AD therapies remains in its early stages. Only a few NP formulations have entered clinical trials, with the main focus being on improving drug delivery across the BBB and targeting Aβ or tau pathology. While some trials have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of these therapies, many face challenges relating to therapeutic efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and long-term safety. This has led to the discontinuation of some trials or slow progression. Emerging strategies involving multifunctional NPs and personalized treatment approaches are being investigated to overcome these barriers and accelerate clinical success [9, 100, 193, 194].

Conclusions

The development of targeted NPs is transforming the treatment landscape for AD. The BBB, a selective membrane protecting the brain, has historically impeded the delivery of therapeutic agents. NPs are addressing this challenge by employing mechanisms such as RMT and AMT to facilitate drug transport across the BBB [201].

NPs offer distinct advantages in targeting AD pathology. Their nanoscale dimensions and modifiable surfaces enable precise delivery to affected brain regions. Various NP types contribute uniquely to treatment strategies:

This adaptability underscores the potential of NPs in providing stage-specific interventions for AD. Despite their promise, several challenges must be addressed before NPs can be widely adopted in clinical settings. Ensuring safety and biocompatibility is paramount, as the long-term effects of NPs within the human body remain under investigation. Additionally, scaling up production while maintaining consistent quality and performance poses significant hurdles [81].

Future research is directed toward enhancing NP targeting capabilities through innovations like ligand engineering and stimuli-responsive systems that release drugs under specific conditions. The integration of therapeutic and diagnostic functionalities (theragnostics) holds promise for real-time treatment monitoring, paving the way for personalized medicine approaches [205].

The implications of targeted NPs extend beyond AD. The principles of BBB penetration, precise targeting, and controlled drug delivery are applicable to other neurological disorders, including PD, multiple sclerosis, and brain cancers. Advancements in this field have the potential to fundamentally change the treatment paradigms for various CNS disorders [81].

In summary, NPs represent a significant advancement in the treatment of AD, offering innovative solutions to longstanding challenges in drug delivery. While obstacles remain, ongoing research and interdisciplinary collaboration are laying the groundwork for future breakthroughs. By uniting experts across nanotechnology, pharmacology, and neuroscience, the full potential of targeted NPs can be realized, potentially redefining care for millions of patients worldwide and establishing new standards in the treatment of AD.

This review provides novel insights into the application of NPs in the treatment of AD by offering an integrative and translational perspective that goes beyond previously published literature. While prior reviews have discussed the general potential of NPs in crossing the BBB, this article emphasizes the molecular and cellular mechanisms—such as RMT and AMT—in a detailed and mechanistic manner, highlighting how surface modifications like PEGylation and ligand conjugation influence NP transport, biocompatibility, and therapeutic efficacy [9].

Furthermore, this review introduces a stage-specific framework for nanoparticle-based therapy in AD, aligning NP types and delivery strategies with distinct disease phases (early vs. late-stage AD). This nuanced approach is rarely addressed in existing literature, and it contributes a new dimension to therapeutic planning [206]. The integration of theragnostic applications, which combine imaging and treatment functions within a single NP system, is also discussed in the context of real-time monitoring and personalized medicine—an area gaining momentum but still underrepresented in mainstream AD reviews [207].

Importantly, the review identifies emerging safety concerns such as nanoparticle-induced neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and altered BBB integrity upon repeated exposure. It critically analyzes these challenges and outlines strategies to overcome them, including the use of biodegradable polymers (e.g., PLGA, chitosan) and advanced surface engineering [208, 209].

By combining detailed mechanistic insight, safety profiling, disease-stage targeting, and future directions for clinical translation, this review adds meaningful depth to the existing body of literature and serves as a comprehensive guide for researchers developing nanoparticle-based therapies for AD and related neurodegenerative conditions [210–212].

In conclusion, nanoparticle-based therapies offer great potential for treating AD by facilitating targeted drug delivery across the BBB and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Lipid-based and polymeric NPs have emerged as the most promising platforms due to their favorable biocompatibility, versatility, and potential for surface functionalization to improve brain targeting [9]. However, there are still significant gaps in the research, including the need for a comprehensive evaluation of long-term safety, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics. There is also a need to develop standardized protocols for assessing NP biodistribution and therapeutic outcomes in clinical trials [156]. Furthermore, significant regulatory challenges persist, such as establishing consistent characterization methods for NPs, ensuring reproducible manufacturing processes, and implementing rigorous toxicity assessments to meet approval standards [213]. Overcoming these scientific and regulatory obstacles through interdisciplinary collaboration and the integration of advanced nanotechnology with personalized medicine is essential to realize the full clinical potential of nanomedicine in the treatment of AD.
