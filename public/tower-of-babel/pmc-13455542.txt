Meta‐Analysis of Imatinib Resistance in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia due to BIM Gene Deletion and Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms Reveals Ethnic Variations in Prevalence
Authors: Abhinav Bhattarai, Dimitri Kountouris, Sanjit Sah, Rachana Mehta, Ranjit Sah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimDeletion and single nucleotide polymorphisms in the BIM gene have been proposed as a potential contributor of intrinsic resistance to imatinib in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). Individual studies, particularly in East Asian population, have reported higher frequency of these gene variants and have suggested higher resistance rates, however, results remain varied across other non‐East Asian cohorts. We aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of BIM deletion and polymorphisms across East Asian and non‐East Asian populations and to evaluate their pooled association with imatinib resistance in CML.MethodsA meta‐analysis was performed using R Studio 2024.12.0. PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched through October 2025. Studies reporting BIM deletion or polymorphisms and imatinib treatment response outcomes in CML patients were included. Random‐effects models were applied to estimate pooled prevalence and odds ratios (ORs) for resistance. Subgroup analyses were performed by ethnicity: East Asians versus non‐East Asians to compare the differences in prevalence of gene variants.ResultsTen studies comprising 1,614 CML patients were included. The pooled prevalence of BIM deletion was significantly higher in East Asians (12%, 95% CI: 10%–15%) compared to non‐East Asians (4%, 95% CI: 0%–11%, p = 0.025). Despite ethnic variability in frequency, BIM deletion was not significantly associated with imatinib resistance (OR 1.43, 95% CI 0.65–3.15, p = 0.37).ConclusionAlthough BIM deletion has a significantly higher prevalence in East Asian populations, pooled evidence does not support a statistically significant association with imatinib resistance. However, large, prospective, multi‐ethnic studies incorporating standardized response criteria and integrated molecular profiling will be essential to clarify whether BIM genotyping holds additive value in the risk stratification for TKI resistance.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not applicable for this submission.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm and involves multilineage expansion of myeloid clones. The underlying genetic disorder in CML is a chromosomal translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11) also called Philadelphia chromosome and is formed by the fusion of genes BCR and ABL1. This fusion gene produces a BCR‐ABL1 oncoprotein, which is a tyrosine kinase enzyme that promotes proliferative pathways and drives CML development [1]. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (allo‐HSCT) is a curative intervention in CML, however, carries a risk of graft‐versus‐host disease (in ∼63% recipients), and is a life‐threatening complication [2]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are drugs that target specific domains on BCR‐ABL1 protein, inhibits its oncogenicity, and has significantly better survival benefits than transplant. Imatinib was the first TKI approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and drastically lowered the CML death rate from previously 10%–20% to 1%–2% [3].

However, one of the biggest challenges today is allo‐HSCT after resistance to revolutionary imatinib [4]. Patients with intrinsic imatinib or TKI resistance have poor prognosis and allo‐HSCT is the curative option of choice [5]. It is challenging to identify at diagnosis, which patients are resistant to imatinib, and patients may often receive sequential TKIs given their known efficacy and less toxicity. Later when resistance develops, allo‐HSCT is considered and by that time the disease is usually progressed which dramatically worsens transplant outcomes [6]. It is therefore important to identify key genetic alterations and mutations that lead to TKI resistance to identify patients who would not benefit from TKIs, and allo‐HSCT can be considered timely to achieve the highest response [7]. Upon literature review, it came into observation that few studies report BIM (BCL2‐Interacting Mediator of cell death) gene deletion to be one of the genetic alterations behind TKI resistance [8]. BCL2 protein family has both pro‐apoptotic and anti‐apoptotic function. BIM gene encodes the BH3 domain of the BCL2 protein that can interact with other proteins of BCL2 family to activate apoptosis, a programmed cell death pathway [9]. Deletion polymorphism of the BIM gene leads to alternative splicing of the BIM exon generating a BH3 domain‐deficient non‐apoptotic isoform and may render TKIs, including imatinib, ineffective [10]. In non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells, the BH3 domain has been shown to facilitate EGFR TKI mediated cancer cell killing via promoting apoptotic pathways [11].

In addition, it was found that East Asian CML patients had lower complete cytogenetic response to TKIs as compared to Western (50% vs. 80%) and uniquely harbor BIM deletion [12]. Given that East Asians represent a significant proportion of the world's population (20.07% as of November 2025) and have lower response rates, a possible interplay of BIM gene could exist in this population [13]. Despite huge disparity in TKI response in East Asians, there is no population wide data on BIM gene deletion prevalences and moreover, the pre‐clinical investigation of BIM gene deletion and TKI resistance in CML are limited. Investigating the mechanism of BIM deletion‐mediated TKI resistance in CML and establishing population‐specific prevalences can help understand why East Asians have lower rates of non‐response and identify other populations with BIM deletion/variants. In addition, BIM deletion specific genetic testing could be developed to identify potential non‐responders and maximize the probabilities of complete response through timely allo‐HSCT.

This meta‐analysis aimed to pool the prevalence of the BIM gene deletion and polymorphisms across various ethnic groups and obtain a group‐specific prevalence. Furthermore, we aimed to pool the association of BIM gene deletion and polymorphism with imatinib resistance to obtain an effect size that can guide decisions regarding TKI vs. allo‐HSCT treatment in CML.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This meta‐analysis has been performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The completed PRISMA checklist has been uploaded in the Supporting Information. The protocol for this meta‐analysis has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) as CRD420251207469.

Study Search

To review the literature on the association of BIM deletion and polymorphism with imatinib resistance, a comprehensive literature search was performed by two reviewers AB and SS on three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Embase for studies published from inception to October 15, 2025. A search term was constructed with relevant keywords and appropriate Boolean operators. The following search term was used across all databases: ([“chronic myeloid leukemia” OR “chronic myelogenous leukemia” OR “CML”] AND [“BIM” OR “BCL2L11” OR “BCL2‐like 11”] AND [“deletion polymorphism” OR “intron 2 deletion” OR “genetic polymorphism” OR “BIM deletion”] AND [“imatinib” OR “tyrosine kinase inhibitor” OR “TKI” OR “dasatinib” OR “nilotinib”] AND [“resistance” OR “treatment failure” OR “response” OR “molecular response” OR “cytogenetic response”])

Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria

As per PRISMA guidelines, we performed the study selection from the retrieved search results. All results were exported as .csv files from each database and merged into one where duplicates were removed and screening was done. We initially screened for potentially relevant studies by title and abstract and sorted them to full‐text screening. Following criteria were evaluated during the full‐text screening for a study to be included in our meta‐analysis: 1. Original studies performed in CML patients.2. Investigated deletion and/or polymorphism in the BIM gene.3. Reported number of patients that did/didn't respond to imatinib, in the context of BIM gene deletion/polymorphism.4. Criteria for resistance/non‐response was elucidated.5. Published in English language or other that could be conveniently translated.

When studies were found eligible, their bibliographies were further screened similarly for fetching additionally relevant studies. We excluded studies performed solely in cell line/animal models of CML/BCR‐ABL1 translocation, non‐CML population, drugs other than imatinib, case studies, other meta‐analyses, reviews, and non‐English studies that could not be translated. Abstracts, posters, and correspondences were considered for inclusion if the population, condition, and outcome were clear and complete.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (AB and RM) extracted data referring to the study characteristics and patient demographics for qualitative synthesis and numerical data on CML patients with BIM gene alterations and imatinib resistance for quantitative synthesis. Data extracted for qualitative synthesis included study author and published year, study country, study design, number of CML patients, ethnicity, imatinib dose, type of genetic alteration (deletion/polymorphism) reported, specific alleles, genotyping method, definition for resistance, and results. Data extracted for quantitative synthesis were frequency of BIM deletion/polymorphism/wild‐type, and number of responders/non‐responders.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was employed by two reviewers (AB and RM) to evaluate the potential risk of bias in the included studies. This tool assesses methodological quality across three domains: selection of participants, comparability of study groups, and assessment of outcomes, with a maximum achievable score of nine points. Studies scoring between 7 and 9 were interpreted as having a low risk of bias, those with scores of 4–6 were considered at higher risk, and those receiving 0–3 points were categorized as very high risk. In brief, the evaluation process involved scrutiny of elements such as case definitions, control group designation, biomarker assessment methods, and the measurement of study outcomes.

Meta‐Analytic Methods

The R studio version 2024.12.0 was used to perform this meta‐analysis. The “meta” and “metafor” packages were used. Imatinib resistance was considered as “event.” A random‐effects model was applied due to differences in the population ethnicity, genotyping methods, resistance definitions, and wide sample size distribution. Such differences indicate that the true effect size may differ across studies rather than being identical and random‐effects model conserves this possibility. Heterogeneity, a measure that informs the percentage of variation in the effect estimates due to real differences between studies was measured and quantified using the I2 and the absolute variation was quantified using DerSimonian and Laird estimated τ 2 statistics. A single‐arm proportion meta‐analysis was performed to pool the proportion of BIM gene deletion and polymorphism across studies. Proportions were pooled using Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) for polymorphism. However, for BIM gene deletion, a continuity correction of 0.5 with the inverse variance method was used to overcome a false wide confidence interval associated with zero‐events. The proportion data was sub‐grouped by ethnicity: East Asians and non‐East Asians. For this analysis, ethnicity was assigned according to the self‐reported population descriptors provided by each primary study rather than by independent genetic ancestry analysis. A dichotomous outcome meta‐analysis was performed to pool the odds ratio (OR) of imatinib resistance in BIM deletion/polymorphism versus wild type. Inverse variance method was used to obtain the pooled OR. For polymorphism analysis, a subgroup analysis was performed by allele type.

In all analysis, the test of subgroup differences was represented by Chi‐square statistics and p‐values. Forest plots were generated to visualize the results. To determine if the pooled estimates were robust and not influenced by specific study, a sensitivity analysis was performed by omitting each study from the analysis, one at a time. A leave‐one‐out forest plot was generated to visualize the differences in the pooled estimate and statistical significance following omission. Finally, publication bias was analyzed to assess the bias associated with negative or non‐significant results. This was done by visual inspection of funnel plots as well as statistical inspection by Egger's regression test and Begg's rank correlation test. These tests were only done if the analysis had at least 10 studies for statistical validity [14]. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To investigate heterogeneity, a meta‐regression was only performed for more than 10 studies and sufficient testable covariates, to avoid overfitting. Conversely, heterogeneity was explored qualitatively from within subgroup analyses and sensitivity checks.

Results

Search Results and Study Selection

The PRISMA‐style flow diagram in Figure 1 outlines the study selection process for this systematic review. A total of 66 records were identified through database searches (PubMed n = 17, Scopus n = 7, Embase n = 42), with 20 duplicates removed electronically before screening. After this, 46 titles were screened, resulting in 21 exclusions. Twenty‐five abstracts were assessed, out of which 9 were included. In addition, 20 records were identified through citation searching and one among them was included, making altogether 10 studies in our analysis.

PRISMA diagram showing the study selection process.

Study Characteristics and Patient Demographic

Altogether, 10 studies that were included were published between 2012 and 2018 and performed at different countries, with most of them being from East Asia followed by Southeast Asia, South Asia, Europe, and Australia. The study designs varied from retrospective, prospective cohorts, case‐controls, observational, and clinical trials. There were altogether 1614 CML patients and all studies had male patients more than the females. The age ranges reported were between 5 and 92 years. Studies performed in populations with East Asian ethnicities outnumbered those with non‐East Asian ethnicities. A majority of the East Asian ethnicities represented were Japanese, followed by Chinese. Non‐East Asian ethnicities were diverse and represented Singaporeans, Malaysians, and Indians. Two studies’ population were Caucasians. Since Ng et al.’s study represented two cohorts of patients, East Asians and non‐East Asians, we split the study into two cohorts. In almost all studies, a 400 mg/day dose of imatinib was administered to CML patients. Few studies had patients with higher doses being given (600 and 800 mg/day). BIM deletions reported in most studies were on Intron 2 whereas BIM gene polymorphism was varied. Three studies reported the rs724710 SNP also named c.465C>T while one study reported a distinct SNP, the rs686952. Techniques used for genotyping were varied and performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), or next‐generation sequencing (NGS). One study utilized a unique methodology called Q‐invader assay. The frequency of BIM deletion ranged between 0% and 18.4% across populations. On the other hand, prevalences for polymorphisms were higher and ranged from 23% to 51%. The detailed differences in prevalence of BIM deletion and polymorphisms across populations and ethnicities are summarized in the next section.

The overall results for each study varied from other as expected from the differences in ethnicities. For instance, Ng et al.’s study didn't find significantly higher OR for Imatinib non‐response in both East Asian and non‐East Asian populations (OR 2.73, p = 0.09 and OR 3.52, p = 0.13 respectively). Katagiri et al. A and B studies both observed higher proportion of CML patients with BIM deletion and SNP in non‐responders which was statistically significant (p = 0.0055). The complete molecular response (CMR) was slightly higher in patients without deletion as compared to patients with deletion (11% vs. 9.7%) in Shinohara et al.’s study in East Asians. However, a statistical difference was not found (p = 0.785). Miyamura et al. reported that higher number CML patients with BIM deletion and/or SNP achieved major molecular response (MMR) after a longer duration (24 months). In Than et al.’s study, higher proportions of East Asians with BIM deletion were found in 10‐year CML related death cohort as compared to those with BIM wild‐type (16% vs. 4%). Likewise, in non‐East Asians, when evaluated for 10‐years overall survival, there were significantly lower number of CML patients with BIM deletion than wild‐type (p = 0.014).

Similar findings were observed for SNPs. Katagiri et al. A showed that 18.2% of CML patients with SNP c.465C>T had to switch from imatinib to a second TKI due to non‐response, whereas none in patients without any variant had to. Augis et al. observed that the same SNP was present in non‐responders in higher frequency and was associated with significantly lengthened time to achieve an MMR. However, CML patients with the same SNP in Miyamura et al.’s study had lower median time to reach MMR than those without the SNP. Interestingly, Marum et al. reported a unique variant in the Australian population, rs686952 which was an exchange of C > A. They classified patients with homozygous (A/A) and A/C allele as good risk and compared for non‐response with patients having normal (C/C) allele (poor risk). They reported significantly lower odds and risk of early molecular response (EMR) and MMR in patients with poor risk. Table 1 summarizes the study characteristics and patient demographics in detail.

Meta‐Analysis of the Prevalence of BIM Gene Deletion

We pooled the prevalence of BIM gene deletion across all studies using single‐arm proportion meta‐analysis and found that it was present in 9% of all CML patients (0.09 [0.05–0.13]). When we sub‐grouped the population into East Asian and non‐East Asian, a unique trend was observed. We found that the pooled prevalence of BIM deletion was significantly higher in East Asians as compared to non‐East Asians (12% vs. 4% respectively, p = 0.0252). It was interesting to note that two studies reported zero prevalence in Non‐East Asians. The ethnicities in these two studies were Malaysians, Indians, or mixed. One study (Ng et al. A) reported an 11% prevalence and their non‐East Asian population included Singaporean and Malaysian ethnicities. Since most Singaporean population belong to Chinese ethnicity, that is, East Asians, we propose that this resulted in increased prevalence in this study alone. Figure 2 shows the forest plot of the pooled prevalence of BIM gene deletion in CML patients, sub‐grouped as East Asians and non‐East Asians. The overall heterogeneity was high (I2 = 91.3%). When investigating this, we found that heterogeneity was negligible for the East Asian subgroup (I2 = 0%), whereas it was high among non‐East Asians (I 2 = 87.7%). We caution that the low within‐subgroup heterogeneity among East Asian cohorts should not be over‐interpreted as evidence of genetic uniformity. The East Asian studies in this analysis comprised only Han Chinese and Japanese populations, they were few and used broadly similar genotyping approaches. The high heterogeneity among non‐East Asians likely reflects the greater diversity of those cohorts (Singaporean, Malaysian, Indian, and mixed ancestry).

Forest plot of the pooled prevalence of BIM gene deletion in CML patients, sub‐grouped as East Asians and non‐East Asians.

Meta‐Analysis of the Prevalence of SNPs in BIM Gene

Single‐arm proportion meta‐analysis for pooling the prevalence of SNP in CML patients (Figure 3) showed a 37% prevalence. BIM c.465C>T SNP in CML patients showed a relatively higher prevalence than BIM gene deletion (32%, 95% CI = 20%–46%; pooled estimates after omitting Marum et al. rs686952). We observed that this prevalence was significantly different between East Asians and Caucasians (p < 0.001). Almost half of the Caucasian (French) ethnicities had a higher prevalence of BIM c.465C>T SNP (47%) as compared to East Asians (24%). The heterogeneity for both subgroups was 0%. However, the overall heterogeneity was 82.7%, possibly due to one different variant type included.

Forest plot of the pooled prevalence of BIM gene polymorphism in CML patients, sub‐grouped as East Asians and non‐East Asians.

Meta‐Analysis of the Association of BIM Deletion With Imatinib Resistance

Altogether seven studies were included in the analysis. There were 101 CML patients with BIM deletion and 695 patients with BIM wild type. The pooled OR of imatinib resistance for patients with BIM deletion was 1.43 (95% CI 0.65–3.15) which was statistically insignificant (p = 0.3687). Figure 4 shows the forest plot of the directionality of the OR in each study. The heterogeneity observed was moderate (I2 = 50%). Figure 4 displays the forest plot of the pooled OR of the association of BIM deletion with Imatinib resistance.

Pooled odds ratio of the association of BIM deletion with Imatinib resistance.

Meta‐Analysis of the Association of BIM c.465C>T SNP With Imatinib Resistance

Three studies were included to evaluate the association of BIM c.465C>T SNP with imatinib resistance and consisted of 61 and 124 CML patients with and without the SNP respectively. We found no significantly high OR of imatinib resistance in patients with the SNP (OR = 1.76 [95% CI 0.43–7.14], p = 0.6337). Sub‐group analysis was performed to compare the association between (1) heterozygous (C/T) versus wild‐type (C/C) alleles, and (2) homozygous (T/T) versus wild‐type (C/C) alleles.

The OR for C/T versus C/C was 2.28 (95% CI 0.39–13.44). Likewise, the OR for T/T versus C/C was 0.71 (95% CI 0.07–7.16). We observed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 54.9%). Figure 5 displays the forest plot of the pooled OR of the association of BIM c.465C>T SNP with imatinib resistance.

Pooled odds ratio of the association of BIM SNP with Imatinib resistance.

Sensitivity Analysis

To investigate heterogeneity and see the influence of each study in the pooled outcome, sensitivity analysis was performed which revealed no significant deviations from the pooled estimates in all analyses. In the prevalence pooling of BIM deletion, exclusion of each study retained the pooled proportion between 9% and 11% (Figure 6A). However, we noticed that the heterogeneity dropped from high to moderate (I2 = 44.6%) when Kagita et al. was removed, indicating source of heterogeneity. Likewise, in case of BIM gene polymorphism, the proportion was retained between 33% and 42% and there was no drastic shift in heterogeneity (Figure 6B). In case of the analysis of imatinib resistance and BIM deletion, the OR was retained between 1.20 and 1.88 with heterogeneity remaining within moderate (Figure 6C). Finally, in case of the analysis of imatinib resistance and SNPs, slight deviations in the pooled OR were noted, however were insignificant (Figure 6D).

Leave‐One‐Out Forest plots assessing the sensitivity of the meta‐analysis.

Publication Bias

In the analysis of BIM deletion prevalence, we notice two outlier studies indicating potential publication bias. The Egger's regression test revealed a significant publication bias (p = 0.0013), however, the Begg's rank test was insignificant (p = 0.7884). The statistical tests for publication bias could not be performed due to less than 10 studies in rest of the analysis, therefore, only visualized in funnel plots. All except the analysis of SNP with resistance, had at least one outlier study indicating a potential publication bias (Figure 7).

Discussion

As the accessibility of radical new biotechnologies begins to improve in the field of oncology, personalized medicine has entered a new era of clinical integration [25]. With access to “big data” computational tools, the utilization of personalized genetic data into CML treatments seems increasingly actionable as a screening strategy. Specific genetic variants, notably the BIM Intron 2 deletion and various single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), have been identified as possible drivers of intrinsic resistance to imatinib. We highlight some of the possible roles these markers may play in disparities, response, progression, and resistance. Our systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to pool the differences in the frequency of BIM deletion/polymorphism across populations and test the pooled association of these alterations with resistance to imatinib in CML. We did find that the BIM deletion is significantly higher and polymorphism c.465C>T is significantly lower in East Asians compared to non‐East Asians.

Data from previous studies reveals a notable ethnic variation in the prevalence of these genetic markers. In East Asian populations, studies by Ng et al. and Katagiri et al. reported high‐deletion frequencies of 18.4% and 12.8% in Japanese cohorts. Chen et al. similarly noted a 13.6% prevalence in Chinese patients. Conversely, the deletion was found to be 0% in Indian patients according to Kagita et al. and among the Caucasian population in France (Augis et al.). East Asian patients seem to uniquely harbor this deletion, potentially explaining their historically lower response rates to TKIs [12, 15]. The functional impact of these variants is primarily observed through delayed or failed molecular response. Augis et al. demonstrated that the c.465C>T SNP (rs724710) was associated with a significantly lengthened time to achieve MMR. In addition, Miyamura et al. observed that while all patients with the BIM deletion achieved MMR, it required a much longer treatment duration (sometimes up to 24 months). In an Australian cohort, Marum et al. identified a unique variant (rs686952), where “poor risk” genotypes (C/C) had lower odds of achieving EMR and MMR compared to “good risk” (A/A, A/C) genotypes. Finally, Katagiri et al. noted that East Asian patients with BIM variants were far more likely to require a switch to newer, second‐generation TKIs (18.2% vs. 0% for wild‐type). These findings suggest that inheritance of these variants may be associated with delayed treatment response.

The presence of these variants seems to have long‐term implications for patient mortality and overall survival. Than et al. have published longitudinal data showing that East Asian patients with the BIM deletion had a 16% risk of 10‐year CML related death, compared to only 4% for wild‐type individuals. The study also found that non‐East Asian patients with this deletion had significantly lower 10‐year overall survival (75% vs. 89% for wild‐type), highlighting a possible universal risk of this deletion [23].

The resistance observed in patients with BIM genetic variants is characterized by a failure to undergo apoptosis, rather than a failure of the drug to inhibit its target. BIM is known as a pro‐apoptotic protein, acting as the primary trigger for cell death once BCR‐ABL1 signaling is blocked by TKIs. Normally, TKI therapy leads to an accumulation of the functional BIM‐alpha isoform, which contains the BCL2‐homology domain 3 (BH3). This domain allows BIM to antagonize anti‐apoptotic proteins, allowing for apoptosis to proceed. The East Asian intron 2 deletion seems to disrupt this process by altering the pre‐mRNA splicing pattern. As established by Ng et al., this deletion pushes splicing toward the production of the BIM‐gamma in informs. These isoforms do not contain BH3 domains, leaving them unable to antagonize the key anti‐apoptotic proteins. Consequently, even when Imatinib successfully inhibits the BCR‐ABL1 kinase, these cells can evade death because the resulting BIM isoforms lack the functional BH3 domain. This results in a state of intrinsic resistance where cells remain viable despite successful TK inhibition. Further research by Ng et al. has demonstrated that this splicing defect is not permanent; the imbalance can be reversed using histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors. By shifting the splicing machinery back toward the production of the BIM‐alpha isoform, these drugs can re‐sensitize leukemic cells to TKIs, highlighting a clear molecular rationale for combination therapy in deletion cohorts [15, 19, 22, 26].

Despite these biologically plausible findings and cohort‐level observations, our meta‐analysis which pooled the findings from multiple studies in this context, did not yield a significant association of BIM gene deletion/c.465C>T SNP with imatinib resistance in CML patients. There could be various factors resulting in this discrepancy. First, early studies that showed the association of gene deletion with imatinib resistance were conducted in relatively smaller yet ethnically homogeneous populations. Second, the heterogeneity in the definitions for categorizing responders and non‐responders has widely varied between studies. For example, few studies adhered to ELN criteria on failure to achieve complete cytogenetic response (CcyR) while others considered specific molecular response thresholds such as BCR‐ABL1>10% at 3 months. Failure to achieve BCR‐ABL1 values of ≤ 0.01%/0.0032%, failure to reach MR3, or failure to achieve deeper responses (MR4–MR4.5) on consecutive measurements, and also fluctuating non‐sustained responses. Some analyses combined multiple endpoints, including treatment milestones at 3, 6, and 12 months, or long‐term outcomes such as progression or death. This diversity in the resistance definitions likely has contributed to the statistically insignificant pooled association of Imatinib resistance in CML.

Although these confounders in our pooled results for resistance exist, the ethnic variation in prevalence of BIM variants is epidemiologically meaningful. It represents the essence of conducting pharmacogenomics studies within diverse populations and heightened molecular monitoring of East Asian population during early TKI therapy. Although the therapeutic landscape of CML has evolved beyond the sole reliance upon Imatinib and other alternative choices of second‐generation TKIs such as Dasatinib and Nilotinib and third‐generation TKIs like Ponatinib and Asciminib have demonstrated efficacy in resistant mutations, in resource‐limited settings where imatinib remains the primary available therapy, understanding modifiers of delayed response may carry greater practical importance [21].

Our study is not the first one to demonstrate the insignificant association of BIM variants with Imatinib resistance. Another meta‐analysis has also shown similar association [26]. However, addressing the confounders such as varied resistance definitions has been limited. In contrast, we have compared the pooled frequency of BIM deletion and c.465C>T SNP frequency between East Asians and non‐East Asians for the first time and this clearly shows an existing difference in prevalence. However, there were limited number of studies and there is an unmet need of epidemiological study of BIM variants across more ethnicities and ancestries. Future large, prospective, multi‐ethnic studies incorporating standardized response criteria and integrated molecular profiling will be essential to clarify whether BIM genotyping holds additive value in the modern TKI era.

One last limitation concerns the genetic basis of our ancestry grouping. As noted, our “East Asian” subgroup reflects the population grouping of the included studies rather than a genetically homogeneous category. Whole‐genome studies have shown that the major East Asian populations have distinct genetic architectures despite their shared physical features, and deep ancestral roots [27, 28]. East Asian populations harbor considerable “substructures” including distinct Northern and Southern Han Chinese groups and the dual Jomon–Yayoi ancestry of mainland Japanese populations [27], alongside numerous related and indigenous groups (Korean, Mongolian, Tibetan, Ryukyuan, and Ainu populations), that form genetically distinguishable clusters yet were absent from our available data [28]. The three best‐characterized East Asian populations (Han Chinese, Japanese, and Korean) share a recent common ancestor dated to only 3000–3600 years ago and have since undergone substantial admixture and gene flow from surrounding populations [28]. Because our pooled East Asian estimate derives solely from Han Chinese and Japanese cohorts, it should not be extrapolated to East Asians as a whole. The BIM intron 2 deletion is best described as enriched in East and Southeast Asian populations sharing the relevant ancestral lineage rather than as a uniform “East Asian” trait. The behavior of the Chinese‐ancestry Singaporean cohorts, which grouped with East Asian rather than other Southeast Asian populations, shows that geographic labels and genetic ancestry are not interchangeable, and that admixture must be considered when interpreting gene prevalences. Future (adequately) powered studies in currently unrepresented ancestries are needed before population‐specific conclusions can be generalized.

Conclusion

Our meta‐analysis revealed that BIM deletion is significantly higher in East Asian populations, while SNP distributions vary by ancestry. Although multiple studies show that these variants are associated with higher resistance rates with imatinib in CML, the pooled association was insignificant, however, masked by the heterogeneous resistance criteria used. While routine universal screening cannot be justified based on current evidence, the observed ethnic variability in prevalence highlights the need for continued population‐specific pharmacogenomics research for TKI resistance in CML. If a subset of “poor‐risk” BIM genotypes does prove to be reliably associated with resistance, failing to identify these patients at diagnosis may prolong ineffective TKI therapy and permit disease progression, which in turn compromises the outcomes of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Clarifying the prognostic value of these genotypes in adequately powered, ancestry‐specific studies should therefore be a priority, as it may ultimately allow earlier and more rational treatment decisions for patients who would otherwise receive a sub‐optimal TKI regimen.
