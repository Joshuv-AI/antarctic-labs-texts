Extrinsic contribution to bosonic thermal Hall transport
Léo Mangeolle, Johannes Knolle

Abstract
Bosonic excitations like phonons and magnons dominate the low-temperature transport of magnetic insulators. Similar to electronic Hall responses, the thermal Hall effect (THE) of charge neutral bosons has been proposed as a powerful tool for probing topological properties of their wavefunctions. For example, the intrinsic contribution of the THE of a perfectly clean system is directly governed by the distribution of Berry curvature, and many experiments on topological magnon and phonon insulators have been interpreted in this way. However, disorder is inevitably present in any material and its contribution to the THE has remained poorly understood. Here we develop a rigorous kinetic theory of the extrinsic side-jump contribution to the THE of bosons. We show that the extrinsic THE can be of the same order as the intrinsic one but sensitively depends on the type of local imperfection. We study different types of impurities and show that a THE can even arise as a pure impurity-induced effect in a system with a vanishing intrinsic contribution. As a side product, we also generalize existing results for the electronic AHE to general types of impurities beyond the standard assumption of local potential scattering. We discuss the importance of our results for the correct interpretation of THE measurements.



I
Introduction

For charged particles, such as electrons, the origin of classical Hall transport from the Lorentz force has been known since the end of the 19th century. After the discovery of the quantum Hall effect (QHE) a century later
Klitzing et al. 1980
, its origin from the intrinsic Berry curvature of the electron’s wavefunctions was swiftly established
Thouless et al. 1982
. Understanding the nonquantized anomalous Hall effect (AHE) in metallic materials with broken time reversal (TR) symmetry has proven much more complicated, because it is both difficult experimentally and theoretically cumbersome to disentangle contributions from
intrinsic
Berry phase effects and
extrinsic
scattering contributions
Nagaosa et al.
. Charge neutral excitations in solids, for example phonons, magnons or exotic fractionalized spinons, can also exhibit Hall transport but now in response to gradients in temperature (or energy density
Luttinger 1964
). A quantized thermal Hall effect (THE) has been put forward as a powerful probe of exotic fractionalized excitations from intrinsic topological order of Mott insulators
Kitaev 2006
;
Kane and Fisher 1997
, signatures of which have been arguably observed experimentally
Kasahara et al. 2018
;
Paul et al. 2024
. Unquantized THE in insulators can also originate from Berry phase effects of more mundane phonon and magnon excitations, as recently reported in many experiments on magnets
Onose et al. 2010
;
Ideue et al. 2012
;
Hirschberger et al. 2015a
;
Hirschberger et al. 2015b
;
Watanabe et al. 2016
;
Doki et al. 2018
;
Hentrich et al. 2019
;
Akazawa et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Lefrançois et al. 2022
;
Bruin et al. 2022
;
Czajka et al. 2023
;
Sharma et al. 2024
– for a recent review see
Zhang et al. 2024
. While the theory of the
intrinsic
(Berry curvature) contribution to this bosonic THE has been firmly established over the last decade
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Qin et al.
;
Matsumoto et al.
;
Shitade 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Mangeolle et al.
, much less is known about the
extrinsic
contributions. Concretely, scattering contributions from disorder inevitably present in any material are not rigorously understood and the subject of the present paper.

The established way of calculating the THE in insulating magnets is to derive an effective quadratic magnon Hamiltonian (various ways of including effects of interactions, or not, have been explored)
and to derive from it the magnon band structure and especially the Berry curvature.
The same procedure can also be used for other types of neutral bosonic excitations, e.g. phonons.
In both cases, knowing the Berry curvature one may compute the thermal Hall conductivity,

\displaystyle\kappa_{xy}^{\rm clean}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T}{\rm Tr}_{+}\int_{p}\int\text{d}\varepsilon\,\Theta(\varepsilon-\underline{{\sf K}}_{\rm d}(p))

(1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{\varepsilon}n_{\rm B}\left(\varepsilon,T\right)\,\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{x}p_{y}}(p),

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function,
{\rm Tr}_{+}
is a sum over boson bands,

n_{\rm B}(\varepsilon,T)=1/(e^{\varepsilon/T}-1)
is the Bose function,
\underline{{\sf K}}_{\rm d}(p)
is the bosonic energy, and crucially
\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{x}p_{y}}(p)
is the bosons’ Berry curvature.
This general formula for the
intrinsic
thermal Hall conductivity of neutral bosons was first derived rigorously using the Kubo-Streda formalism
for phonons
Qin et al.
and magnons
Matsumoto et al.
.
Its intuitive picture as a semiclassical current carried by particles obeying the semiclassical equations of motion
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011

was recently provided rigorous support in Ref.
Mangeolle et al.
, where one of the authors took part in deriving Eq.(
1
) directly from the inhomogeneous kinetic equation.
Except for the now well-known complications introduced by the energy magnetization
Qin et al. 2011
, this development is very similar to that of the intrinsic anomalous Hall effect (AHE) of electrons,
which was first obtained from the Kubo formula
Karplus and Luttinger
before it was given a semiclassical picture using the semiclassical equations of motion of the electron wavepacket
Sundaram and Niu
,
later derived rigorously within the kinetic equation using the Wigner-Weyl formalism
Wickles and Belzig
.

In both the (bosonic) thermal and (fermionic) electrical cases, the intrinsic contribution is but part of the total Hall conductivity.
Other phenomena contribute, that are usually classified as skew-scattering
Mori et al. 2014
;
Flebus and MacDonald 2022
;
Sun et al. 2022
;
Mangeolle et al. 2022a
;
Mangeolle et al. 2022b
;
Chatzichrysafis and Mook 2025
and side-jump mechanisms: for a review in the electronic case see
Nagaosa et al.
.
The effect of disorder on the
electronic
AHE, and especially the side-jump effect, was originally computed in specific electron models using Kubo’s formula

;
Fivaz
;
Nozières and Lewiner
,
but can also be derived in the language of semiclassical dynamics. This was shown much later, based on the wavepacket analysis

;
Sinitsyn et al. 2006
,
and was also derived rigorously in a particular model within the Schwinger-Keldysh kinetic formalism
König and Levchenko
. At least in one particular case, a formal connection can be drawn between both approaches
Sinitsyn et al. 2007

The general expectation is that the electronic side-jump effect is
non-perturbative

Yet, for electronic systems, the side-jump effect is actually not so commonly seen experimentally

In this paper, we derive rigorously the side-jump effect in the thermal Hall conductivity of neutral bosons. Employing the kinetic equation formalism for systems of non-interacting bosons we include the presence of generic disorder fields, i.e. not only
potential
but a fully-fledged local deformation of the Hamiltonian as the most genereal form of disorder.
Within the semiclassical approximations which we discuss later, we find that disorder scattering induces new contributions in the boson thermal Hall conductivity which are carried by
bulk
currents.
As our main result we derive the corresponding disorder-induced thermal Hall conductivity reading

\displaystyle\kappa_{xy}^{\rm dis}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T}{\rm Tr}_{+}\int_{p}\int\text{d}\varepsilon\,\delta(\varepsilon-\underline{\sf K}_{\rm d}(p))\,\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{\varepsilon}n_{\rm B}\left(\varepsilon,T\right)

(2)

\displaystyle\qquad\times p_{\lambda}\left[{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}}(p)\,\partial_{p_{x}}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(p)-{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}}(p)\,\partial_{p_{y}}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(p)\right],

where
{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\mu}}(p)
is a band-dependent and disorder-dependent quantity, generally distinct from the Berry curvature (and which reduces to it in specific cases).
We find that the side-jump Hall effect is comparable in magnitude to the intrinsic Hall effect; because
{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\mu}}(p)
depends on the instance of disorder considered,
the above formula shows that
\kappa_{xy}^{\rm dis}
is non-universal and depends on the specific instance of disorder that we consider, as is illustrated in Fig.
1
.
We highlight in particular case (c), which shows that even with vanishing intrinsic Berry curvature
\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{x}p_{y}}
there may still exist sizeable disorder-induced thermal Hall conductivity.

Interestingly, our derivation translates with very little change to the case of electrical Hall transport by electrons. This provides a generalized formula for the side-jump AHE where impurities, as local random Hamiltonians, have a general matrix form in the space of fermion flavors and a general momentum dependence. We provide this electronic AHE contribution, together with a simple derivation, in Eq.(
IV.5
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II

Mangeolle et al.
of the inhomogeneous kinetic theory without disorder, which forms the basis for the inclusion of
disorder in a real-space, real-time picture. In Section
III

and show how disorder-induced coordinate shifts naturally appear in the full gauge-invariant kinetic equation. In Section
IV
we show how these coordinate shifts
lead to a side-jump current operator, which we derive, and to a displaced equilibrium distribution, which together lead to Eq. (
2
).
Finally, in Section
V

II
Setup

II.1
Reminders about the clean case

We start from the general formulation introduced in Ref.
Mangeolle et al.
,
of a free boson system described by
2N
hermitian fields
\Phi_{a}(r)
,
r\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a=1,..,2N
,
with commutation relation
[{\Phi}_{a}(r_{1}),{\Phi}_{b}(r_{2})]\equiv\hbar\,\hat{\sf\Gamma}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})

matrix with pure imaginary

-number entries.
The free theory

is defined by an arbitrary quadratic hamiltonian,

\textrm{H}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[\Phi\circ\hat{\sf H}\circ\Phi\right]

\textrm{H}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1},r_{2}}\sum_{a,b}\Phi_{a}(r_{1})\hat{\sf H}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})\Phi_{b}(r_{2}),

(3)

where
\hat{\sf H}
is a
2N\times 2N
matrix of pure real
c
-number entries.
The kinetic theory describes the evolution of the (real) density matrix

\hat{\sf F}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\left\{\Phi_{a}(r_{1}),\Phi_{b}(r_{2})\right\}\right\rangle.

(4)

All three
\hat{\sf H},\hat{\sf F},\hat{\sf\Gamma}
, regarded as matrices in the joint
flavor (

) and coordinate (

) space, are hermitian.

The theory can be formulated in phase space, with
(X,p)
coordinates, by means of the Wigner transform,

\displaystyle{\sf O}(X,p)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{x}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}px}\;\hat{\sf O}\left(X+\tfrac{x}{2},X-\tfrac{x}{2}\right),

(5)

where

and
\int_{x}\equiv\int d^{d}x
.
Then
\mathsf{F},\mathsf{H},\mathsf{\Gamma}
are functions on phase
space (
X,p
) and are all hermitian as
2N\times 2N
matrices at fixed
(X,p)
.
The equation of motion for the density matrix is

\partial_{t}{\sf F}(X,p)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left({\sf K}\star{\sf F}-{\sf F}\star{\sf K}^{\dagger}\right),

(6)

where
\star
is the Moyal product (with sign convention as in
Mangeolle et al.
;
Kamenev 2011
)
and
{\sf K}(X,p)=\hbar\,{\sf\Gamma}\star{\sf H}
is the dynamical matrix.

{At this stage, it is worth noticing that the same procedure applied to fermions instead of bosons,
with
\textrm{H}={\rm Tr}\left[\Psi^{\dagger}\circ\hat{\sf H}\circ\Psi\right]
,
yields the very same kinetic equation Eq.(
6

\hat{\sf F}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})\equiv\left\langle\Psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r_{1})\Psi_{b}(r_{2})\right\rangle

{\sf K}=\hbar\,{\sf H}

Back to bosonic systems, the energy current density, obtained from the continuity equation

reads (to the first order in

\displaystyle J_{\mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left(\partial_{k_{\mu}}{\sf K}({\sf F}\star{\sf H})\right),

II.2
Introducing disorder

We now consider the similar problem of an inhomogeneous theory (allowing for a possibly spatially-dependent lagrangean) of free bosons with disorder.
While, in principle, frozen disorder may be treated as a specific instance of spatial dependence in the hamiltonian Eq.(
3
),
we will instead consider disorder as a distinct
random
contribution to the hamiltonian.
This means replacing Eq.(
3
) with

\displaystyle{\rm H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[\Phi\circ(\hat{\sf H}+\hat{V})\circ\Phi\right]

(8)

where
\hat{V}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})
is a random matrix variable.
Since
{\rm H}
, together with the commutation relation, defines a free theory for the fields
\Phi

one can simply use the classical equations of motion to derive the kinetic equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\sf F}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big((\hat{\sf K}+\hat{\sf V})\circ\hat{\sf F}-\hat{\sf F}\circ(\hat{\sf K}+\hat{\sf V})^{\dagger}\big),

(9)

where now
\hat{\sf K}=\hbar\,\hat{\sf\Gamma}\circ\hat{\sf H}
and
\hat{\sf V}=\hbar\,\hat{\sf\Gamma}\circ\hat{V}
.
{ In the fermionic case,
\hat{\sf K}=\hbar\,\hat{\sf H}
and
\hat{\sf V}=\hbar\,\hat{V}
. }

The new ingredient here is
\hat{V}
, accounting for disorder.
Generally,
\hat{V}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})
has an arbitrary matrix structure, only constrained by the same symmetry properties as
\hat{\sf H}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})
.
In the existing treatments of disorder in (fermionic) anomalous Hall effects

;
Fivaz
;
Nozières and Lewiner
;
Sinitsyn et al. 2005
;
Sinitsyn et al. 2006
;
Sinitsyn et al. 2007
;
König and Levchenko
,
a diagonal scalar potential
\hat{V}_{ab}(r_{1},r_{2})=\delta_{a,b}\,\delta(r_{1}-r_{2})\,V(r)
is used,
which corresponds effectively to an on-site shift of the electronic chemical potential

3

3

In
König and Levchenko
an extra valley index is introduced, but the
(a,b)
and
(r_{1},r_{2})
indices remain decoupled,
the spatial dependence is still of the

\hat{V}\propto\text{\textbb{1}}

in the following we will refer to this as the “scalar fermionic case”.
For real bosons this would be unreasonable in general: because of the relation
\hat{\sf V}=\hbar\,\hat{\sf\Gamma}\circ\hat{V}

an on-site chemical potential (i.e. a term
\hat{\sf V}\propto\text{\textbb{1}}

We also drop the assumption

This allows us to describe structural disorder, bond disorder, impurities interacting or mediating interactions at longer range, and more generally any local disturbance of

II.3
Equal-time formalism

The next step is to diagonalize the clean problem: here we just outline in two sentences the method described in
Mangeolle et al.
.
Explicitly the diagonalization is realized by solving for a matrix
\mathsf{S}
such that
\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}=\mathsf{S}^{-1}\circ\mathsf{K}\circ\mathsf{S}
is diagonal (one can also prove that its entries are real):
this defines the basis of bosonic bands.
Then, we defime
\mathsf{F}=\mathsf{S}\circ\mathsf{F}_{\rm d}\circ\mathsf{S}^{\dagger}
and
\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}=\mathsf{S}^{-1}\circ\mathsf{V}\circ\mathsf{S}
–
the index “d” indicates that a quantity is expressed in the basis where
\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}
is made diagonal, but note that neither
\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}
nor
\mathsf{F}_{\rm d}
is diagonal a priori.
Then the kinetic equation reads

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}{\sf F}_{\rm d}-[{\sf K}_{\rm d}\,\overset{\circ}{,}\,{\sf F}_{\rm d}]=\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}\circ{\sf F}_{\rm d}-{\sf F}_{\rm d}\circ\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}^{\dagger},

(11)

with the shorthand
[{\sf K}_{\rm d}\,\overset{\circ}{,}\,{\sf F}_{\rm d}]={\sf K}_{\rm d}\circ{\sf F}_{\rm d}-{\sf F}_{\rm d}\circ{\sf K}_{\rm d}
.
Note that
\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}
is a random variable, and consequently
{\sf F}_{\rm d}
, as a solution of Eq.(
11

to extract physical quantities, a disorder average of Eq.(
11

Because the disorder we consider is correlated in space but not in time, scattering is elastic and an equal-time formalism
(as opposed to the full Schwinger-Baym-Kadanoff-Keldysh treatment) is sufficient. We write

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\rm d}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{U}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,\mathsf{F}_{\rm d}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(y,t|y^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle x|{\mathbb{T}}\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\text{d}\tau(\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}+\mathsf{V}_{\rm d})(\tau)\right]|x^{\prime}\rangle,

where

12

), disorder now enters solely in the form of statistical correlations

\displaystyle\langle(\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}^{s})_{n,n^{\prime}}(z_{1},z_{2})(\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}^{s^{\prime}})_{n^{\prime},n}(z_{3},z_{4})\rangle=\int_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}w\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4})\Big)

(16)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(z_{1},\lambda_{1})\mathsf{W}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mathsf{S}(\lambda_{2},z_{2})\right]^{s}_{n,n^{\prime}}\;\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(z_{3},\lambda_{3})\mathsf{W}(\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})\mathsf{S}(\lambda_{4},z_{4})\right]^{s^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime},n},

where the integral is over all four
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,4
and the upper index
s,s^{\prime}=-
(resp.
+
) on an operator means hermitian conjugation (resp. absence thereof).

Taking
t^{\prime}\rightarrow-\infty
, Eq.(
15
) gives
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(t)
as a function of
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(\tau)
at all earlier times
\tau<t
.
Considering
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(-\infty)
to be disorder-independent, then
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(\tau)
on the right-hand side of Eq.(
15
),
as a solution to the equation at an earlier time, is no longer a random variable but a deterministic function.
Therefore we can drop the disorder average
\langle\cdot\rangle
on the left-hand side,
and henceforth treat Eq.(
15
) as a
deterministic
integro-differential equation for
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(t)
.

III
Derivation of the kinetic equation

III.1
Phase space formulation and semiclassical approximations

In order to obtain the kinetic equation in terms of phase space variables
(X,p)
,
we now perform the Wigner transformation (see Eq.(
5
)) on the kinetic equation.
We provide some technical details about the intermediate steps in Appendix
A
,
and here we only outline a few salient aspects of the calculation.

First, our semiclassical derivation relies on an expansion in powers of
\hbar
.
Scattering of bosons on disorder affects the phase-space coordinates
X
and
p
of the wavepackets, which by convention are
O(\hbar^{0})
in the free case.
Upon scattering, the particle momentum is modified,
p\rightarrow p^{\prime}
, by an amount
p^{\prime}-p=O(\hbar^{0})

governed by the Fourier-transformed disorder correlation
\tilde{w}(k)=\int_{\xi}e^{-ik\xi/\hbar}w(\xi)
: since the latter can hardly be known with sufficient
accuracy, any
O(\hbar^{1})
semiclassical correction is irrelevant, and we neglect scattering shift effects on the
momentum
of excitations.
Meanwhile, we focus on the
position
shift effects induced by disorder which will occur at order
O(\hbar^{1})
– in other words,
we consider semiclassical corrections on the
X
but not the
p
variable of
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(X,p),{\sf K}_{\rm d}(X,p)
in our derivation.

Second, we use the classical limit of the time evolution, given by the limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
and the identity

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\hbar}\theta(t)e^{-it(E-i0)/\hbar}

\displaystyle=-i\delta(t)\frac{1}{E-i0}

(17)

\displaystyle=-i\delta(t)\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{E}+\pi\delta(t)\delta(E),

where
\mathcal{P}
stands for principal value.
Physically, this amounts to assuming destructive intereferences between all contributions in Eq.(
15
) at times
\tau
earlier than
t
,
yielding an
equal-time
differential equation for the time evolution of the density matrix.
Equivalently, the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit selects the classical path in the functional integral representation of time evolution.
In the following we also discard the principal value term, which accounts for the perturbative effect of disorder on the bosonic quasiparticle energies,
and keep only the dirac delta term, which accounts for elastic scattering of bosonic waves.

Third, we assume adiabaticity in the sense that the disorder correlation length
\lambda_{\rm dis}
is much larger than the typical wavelength of bosonic excitations

\lambda_{\rm bos}
(at a given temperature
T
),
\lambda_{\rm dis}\gg\lambda_{\rm bos}
, equivalently that the disorder correlation function

\tilde{w}(k)
is a narrow enough peak centered around zero momentum: the momentum transfer
k
during a single scattering event is then small
in comparison to a typical bosonic excitation’s momentum
p
.
Based on this, as a crucial step we perform a small-
k
expansion

\displaystyle(\mathsf{S}^{-1})_{n,a}(p+k/2)\,\mathsf{W}_{ab}(\tfrac{p+p^{\prime}}{2}+k/2)\,\mathsf{S}_{b,n^{\prime}}(p^{\prime}+k/2)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\exp\left[{i(k/2)\,\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})/\hbar}\right],

(18)

where we defined

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{S}^{-1})_{n,a}(p)\mathsf{W}_{ab}(\tfrac{p+p^{\prime}}{2})\mathsf{S}_{b,n^{\prime}}(p^{\prime}),

(19a)

\displaystyle\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hbar\,(\partial_{p}+\partial_{p^{\prime}})\,{\rm Arg}\left[\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right].

(19b)

Contrary to the previous two approximations, Eq.(
III.1
) is in principle an uncontrolled assumption,
which amounts to neglecting variations of the moduli of eigenvectors, focussing on their
phase
variations.
{ In the scalar fermionic case, because
\mathsf{W}\propto\text{\textbb{1}}
and
\mathsf{S}^{\dagger}\circ\mathsf{S}=\text{\textbb{1}}
,
Eq.(
III.1
) is strictly equivalent to
k\ll p{}^{(}{}^{\prime}{}^{)}
; in our general bosonic case there is no such equivalence. }
In the wavepacket interpretation of phase-space quantum dynamics, this means we are focussing on the position and momentum of particle wavepackets (contained in the phase),
neglecting variations of their extension or spreading (contained in the amplitude): this is again a semiclassical approximation in the traditional sense
Gutzwiller 1990
.
Note also that in Eqs.(
III.1
),(
19
) we dropped the
X
variable of all quantities (
\mathsf{S},\mathsf{M},\delta\mathsf{r}
, etc):
while these may indeed vary spatially in our inhomogeneous theory,
this dependence is irrelevant to the order of
\hbar
considered here,
and we will keep omitting the
X
variable in the following wherever this cannot possibly cause any confusion.

The upshot of the procedure just described is Eq.(
63
) in Appendix
A
,
which, after one extra (minor) simplification discussed there, becomes

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}({\sf F}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p,t)-[{\sf K}_{\rm d}\,\overset{\star}{,}\,{\sf F}_{\rm d}]_{n,n}(X,p,t)

(20)

\displaystyle=-i2\pi\sum_{n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\,\delta\left((\mathsf{K}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{-}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime})-(\mathsf{K}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg\{(\mathsf{F}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p,t)\;{\rm Re}\left[\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime},n}(p^{\prime},p)\right]-(\mathsf{F}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{-}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime},t)\;{\rm Re}\left[\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})^{*}\right]\bigg\},

where
[\mathsf{O}_{1}\overset{\star}{,}\mathsf{O}_{2}]=\mathsf{O}_{1}\star\mathsf{O}_{2}-\mathsf{O}_{2}\star\mathsf{O}_{1}
is the Moyal bracket,
and
\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{-}(p,p^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{2}[\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})-\delta\mathsf{r}_{n^{\prime},n}(p^{\prime},p)]
appears as a coordinate shift. We note that
\tilde{w}^{*}(k)=\tilde{w}(k)=\tilde{w}(-k)
.

III.2
Gauge-invariant coordinates and shift

When defining eigenfunctions of a given operator (here,
\mathsf{K}
), one is faced with a gauge choice:
after fixing the eigenvector normalization
\mathsf{S}^{\dagger}\star\mathsf{H}\star\mathsf{S}=\text{\textbb{1}}

there remains a
U(1)
phase redundancy

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\mathsf{S}\star e^{i\stheta}

(21)

at any point
(X,p)
in phase space (
\stheta
a diagonal matrix of phases, function of
X
and
p
).
As explained e.g. in
Mangeolle et al.
,
{\sf K}_{\rm d}(X,p)
and
{\sf F}_{\rm d}(X,p)
themselves are not invariant under such a gauge transformation Eq.(
21
).
Instead,
\underline{\sf K}_{\rm d}(X,p)={\sf K}_{\rm d}(\underline{X},\underline{p})
and
\underline{\sf F}_{\rm d}(X,p)={\sf F}_{\rm d}(\underline{X},\underline{p})
,
functions of the “kinetic” coordinates

\displaystyle\underline{X}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=X_{\mu}+\hbar\mathsf{A}_{p_{\mu}},

(22a)

\displaystyle\underline{p}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=p_{\mu}-\hbar\mathsf{A}_{X_{\mu}},

(22b)

are gauge-invariant (to the first order in
\hbar
).

Here
\mathsf{A}_{\gamma}={\rm Im}\left(\mathsf{S}^{-1}\partial_{\gamma}\mathsf{S}\right)^{(\rm d)}
, for
\gamma=X_{\mu},p_{\mu}
,
where Im selects the imaginary part and (d) the diagonal part,
is the generalized Berry connection in phase space (up to
O(\hbar^{1})
corrections).

In terms of these new coordinates and physical quantities, one can show (see
Wickles and Belzig
;
König and Levchenko
;
Mangeolle et al.
)
that the time derivative operator appearing on the left-hand side of the gauge-invariant kinetic equation is

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{t}=\partial_{t}+\dot{\mathsf{X}}_{\mu}\partial_{X_{\mu}}+\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mu}\partial_{p_{\mu}},

(23)

with what can be identified as the Hamilton equations,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{X}}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\partial_{p_{\mu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}-\hbar\left({\sf\Omega}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}\partial_{X_{\nu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}-{\sf\Omega}_{p_{\mu}X_{\nu}}\partial_{p_{\nu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}\right),

(24a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{X_{\mu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}+\hbar\left({\sf\Omega}_{X_{\mu}p_{\nu}}\partial_{X_{\nu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}-{\sf\Omega}_{X_{\mu}X_{\nu}}\partial_{p_{\nu}}\underline{\sf K}_{d}\right),

(24b)

in terms of the generalized Berry curvature in phase space,

\displaystyle{\sf\Omega}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\equiv\partial_{\alpha}\mathsf{A}_{\beta}-\partial_{\beta}\mathsf{A}_{\alpha},

(25)

with
\alpha,\beta\in\{X_{\mu},p_{\nu}\}
, and always up to
O(\hbar^{2})
corrections.
We note that all these quantities
{\sf\Omega}_{\alpha\beta},\mathsf{A}_{\gamma},\dot{\mathsf{X}}_{\mu},\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mu},{\rm D}_{t}

must be understood as diagonal matrices in the eigenbasis of energy bands (where
\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}
is diagonal).
{ We also note that this discussion translates straightforwardly to the language of fermions
Wickles and Belzig
. }

The above results hold for a general inhomogeneous theory.
In the following, we focus on physical problems where the mixed Berry curvatures
\mathsf{\Omega}_{X,X},\mathsf{\Omega}_{X,p},\mathsf{\Omega}_{p,X}

and the real-space Berry connection
\mathsf{A}_{X_{\mu}}
play no role (which includes not only homogeneous theories but also,
for instance, the “gravitational potential” or “separable” case
Luttinger 1964
;
Mangeolle et al.
).

Then, performing the change of coordinates Eq.(
22
) in Eq.(
20
), one obtains the gauge-invariant kinetic equation Eq.(
27
),
whose left-hand side is
{\rm D}_{t}\,\underline{\sf F}_{\rm d}(X,p)

and whose right-hand side is now expressed fully in terms of
\underline{\sf K}_{\rm d}(X,p),\underline{\sf F}_{\rm d}(X,p)
,
with the corresponding position coordinates

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad(\mathsf{K}_{\rm d})_{n,n},(\mathsf{F}_{\rm d})_{n,n}\quad

\displaystyle:\quad X_{\mu},

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad(\mathsf{K}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}},(\mathsf{F}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}\quad

\displaystyle:\quad X_{\mu}+\delta{\sf X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),

where we have defined the gauge-invariant position shift

\displaystyle\delta{\sf X}^{\mu}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{-,\mu}(p,p^{\prime})

(26)

\displaystyle\;+\hbar(\mathsf{A}_{p_{\mu}})_{n,n}(p)-\hbar(\mathsf{A}_{p_{\mu}})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(p^{\prime}).

We note that all three terms in Eq.(
26
) are order
O(\hbar^{1})
,
and that although
\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{-}(p,p^{\prime})
is not gauge-invariant, the full position shift
\delta{\sf X}^{\mu}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
is.

We have obtained:

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}

\displaystyle+\dot{\mathsf{X}}_{\mu}\partial_{X_{\mu}}+\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mu}\partial_{p_{\mu}}\big)\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p,t)\big|_{n,n}

(27)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\sum_{n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\times\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta{\sf X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big)

\displaystyle\times\bigg\{(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p,t)\;{\rm Re}\left[\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime},n}(p^{\prime},p)\right]-(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta{\sf X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime},t)\;{\rm Re}\left[\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})^{*}\right]\bigg\}.

Note that the position arguments of the functions
\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}
and
\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}
in Eq.(
27
) are derived systematically
to semiclassical order
O(\hbar^{1}
), and they are crucial in the following.

III.3
Corrected kinetic equation: practical implementation and further assumptions

In order to make quantitative predictions in specific applications, it is possible to use Eq.(
27
) as a starting point,
and to solve it for instance numerically, using variational methods
Ziman 1960
and/or decomposing the distribution function onto a basis of harmonics
Allen 1978
.
Here, we will remain general and proceed with further approximations to make analytical progress.

First we will replace
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
by its modulus: while it is clear that this does not affect the semiclassical physics of coordinate shifts but merely modifies the scattering rates, whose precise form ultimately
does not
affect our final results, it is also possible to justify this assumption more rigorously based on a small-momentum expansion (see Appendix
C.3
).
Second, contrary to the scalar fermionic case, where it is always true,

\big|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\big|\overset{?}{=}\big|\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime},n}(p^{\prime},p)\big|

(28)

does not hold
a priori
for the problem at hand. We discuss the validity of this assumption in Appendix
C.4
,
where we show that it is valid in a particular (yet still reasonably general) case.
Henceforth we will assume Eq.(
28
) to hold, so that
\big|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\big|^{2}
may be factorized in the right-hand side of Eq.(
27
).
The latter then takes a simplified form,

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{t}\,\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p,t)\big|_{n,n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\sum_{n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\times\delta\big(\text{energy}\big)

\displaystyle\times\big|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\big|^{2}\times\delta F_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),

(29)

where one defined the distribution difference

\displaystyle\delta F_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)

\displaystyle-(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta{\sf X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime})

(30)

and the energy conservation delta-function is the same as in Eq.(
27
).
Besides, in the following we neglect
n\neq n^{\prime}
contibutions to the collision integral.
An argument based on matrix elements
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
is given in App.
C.2
,
but here we simply notice that for non-degenerate bands and small deviations
p^{\prime}-p
,
the energy conservation delta strongly favors
n^{\prime}=n
terms, which we henceforth focus on.

III.4
The disorder-induced curvature

We now consider the band-diagonal position shift
\delta{\sf X}_{n,n}
.
An expansion at small deviations
p^{\prime}-p
(see details in Appendix
B
) yields

\displaystyle\delta\mathsf{X}^{\mu}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx(p^{\prime}-p)_{\nu}\,(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n},

(31)

where we defined a gauge-invariant, disorder-dependent quantity,

\displaystyle\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv-\partial_{p_{\nu}}\,{\rm Im}\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{S}(p)\right]^{(\rm d)}

(32)

\displaystyle+\partial_{p_{\mu}}\,{\rm Im}\left\{[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\partial_{p_{\nu}}\mathsf{S}(p)]^{(\rm d)}{\mathsf{D}}_{\rm d}^{-1}(p)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{p_{\mu}}\,{\rm Im}\left\{[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\partial_{p_{\nu}}\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)]^{(\rm d)}{\mathsf{D}}_{\rm d}^{-1}(p)\right\},

where we used the shorthand

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}}_{\rm d}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)]^{(\rm d)},

(33)

and we note that a necessary condition for this small-deviation expansion to be well grounded
is that
({\mathsf{D}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\neq 0\;\forall(n,p)
, i.e. that the diagonal elements of
\mathsf{S}^{-1}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{S}
be non-vanishing.
To the order we consider in
\lambda_{\rm bos}/\lambda_{\rm dis}\ll 1
(small deviation expansion),
in Eq.(
31
)
\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}
can be evaluated equivalently at
p
or
p^{\prime}
.
The quantity Eq.(
32
) may be dubbed for convenience the “disorder-induced curvature”, although it does not
a priori
qualify as a curvature in the strict mathematical sense.
{ In the scalar fermionic case, with
\mathsf{W}(p)\propto\text{\textbb{1}}
, the two curvatures coincide: then one just has

\delta\mathsf{X}^{\mu}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})\approx(p^{\prime}-p)_{\nu}\,(\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n},

where
\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}=\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{A}_{p_{\nu}}-\partial_{p_{\nu}}\mathsf{A}_{p_{\mu}}
is the usual Berry curvature.
This recovers the familiar result of
Sinitsyn et al. 2005
;
Sinitsyn et al. 2006
. }

We notice that in general
\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}\neq-\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\nu}p_{\mu}}
, at odds with the usual Berry curvature.
Also, in Eq.(
32
) the sum of the first and second terms is gauge-invariant,
while the third term by itself is gauge-invariant.

Besides our rigorous formal proof of Eq.(
27
) (and of the energy current below) including semiclassical disorder corrections,
equations Eqs. (
31
), (
32
) are the main new result of this work,
displaying both qualitative and quantitative differences with the known theory for the scalar fermionic case.

IV
Coordinate shift effects in the thermal Hall current

In the presence of disorder, there arise two new contributions to the thermal Hall current,
originating (1) from a displacement of the equilibrium distribution due to the
O(\hbar^{1})
position shift,
and (2) from a new term of order
O(\hbar^{1})
in the current operator itself.

IV.1
Solving the kinetic equation

We now look for a distribution
\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p)
, solution of the kinetic equation Eq.(
III.3
), in the form

\displaystyle\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p)

\displaystyle=f\left(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p),T(X)\right)+\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}(X,p),

(34)

where the first term is the clean equilibrium solution, with
f(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\left[n_{\rm B}(\varepsilon)+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]
,
and the second term contains both the out-of-equilibrium distribution and the “compensation term” to the local equilibrium distribution due to the position shift.

Details are given in Appendix
E
. One finds

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}(X,p)+\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}(X,p),

(35a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}(X,p)

\displaystyle=-p_{\nu}\,\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\lambda}p_{\nu}}(p)\;\partial_{\lambda}T\;\partial_{T}f\!\left(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}\right),

(35b)

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\partial_{k_{\lambda}}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}\;\partial_{\lambda}T\;\partial_{T}f(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})\,\stau(X,p),

(35c)

where
f
is still implicitly a function of
T(X)
, all objects
\mathsf{g}_{\rm d},\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}},\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}
are diagonal matrices in the basis of energy bands,
the dependence
\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p)
remains implicit,
Eq.(
35c
) relies on the same assumptions as the relaxation time approximation, and

\displaystyle\stau^{-1}_{n,n}(X,p)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\,\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\,\left(1-\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big),

(36)

where
\hat{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}/|{\mathbf{p}}|
.

IV.2
The “jump-forward” term

Plugging Eq.(
35b
) into the
O(\hbar^{0})
formula for the energy current density
Mangeolle et al.
,
one obtains the first disorder contribution to the thermal Hall current:

\displaystyle J_{\mu}^{\rm gv,sj}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p}\sum_{n}\partial_{p_{\mu}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}\,(\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj})_{n,n}(X,p).

(37)

Since we assumed disorder is the only source of scattering,
J_{\mu}^{\rm gv,sj}
does not depend on the disorder strength,
in the sense that under a rescaling
\tilde{w}(k)\rightarrow\zeta\tilde{w}(k)
, with
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}
and thus
J_{\mu}^{\rm gv,sj}
are invariant.

IV.3
The “side-jump” accumulation term

We now derive the “side-jump” contribution, corresponding intuitively to the accumulation of position shifts over the course of a trajectory.
In the case of charge transport by electrons, it was originally postulated
Sinitsyn et al. 2005
;
Sinitsyn et al. 2006
on a phenomenological basis.
The charge current
operator
may also be derived rigorously using a Ward identity
König and Levchenko
,
but its expectation value still required a phenomenological input.

4
4

4

Namely, one needs to fix whether the distribution function (against which the current operator is integrated)
should be evaluated before or after the scattering event.

In the present case of a thermal current carried by nonconserved bosons, we cannot resort to such an approach.
Instead we use the local conservation equation of energy to identify the energy current,
similar to the derivation of Eq.(
7
) in the case without disorder.
For details of the derivation in the presence of disorder, we refer the reader to Appendix
D.1
.
The result is identical to Eq.(
7
) with the replacements
\mathsf{H}\rightarrow\mathsf{H}+V
and
\mathsf{K}\rightarrow\mathsf{K}+\mathsf{V}
.

Expanding the Moyal product, we find to the order
O(\hbar^{1})
two new contributions in the energy current density:
one which we argue does not have a semiclassical interpretation and will henceforth be neglected,
and one which we identify as the side-jump current,

\displaystyle J_{\mu}^{\rm sj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{V})\mathsf{S}{\sf F}_{\rm d}\right].

(38)

After some lengthy algebra, very similar to that involved in deriving the kinetic equation Eq. (
27
) and detailed in Appendix
D.2
,
this can be recast into the form

\displaystyle J_{\mu}^{\rm sj}(X)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int_{p,p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\,\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}

(39)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times(p^{\prime}-p)_{\nu}\,(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n}(p)

\displaystyle\times\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\Big)\,(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime}).

In this expression, only the out-of-equilibrium part
\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}
of
\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}
may yield a finite current:
this follows from basic thermodynamic principles and may also be easily proven directly (see Appendix
D.3
).
Plugging Eq.(
35c
) into Eq.(
39
), and again under the usual assumptions of the relaxation time approximation (see App.
E
),
we obtain
J_{\mu}^{\rm sj}\rightarrow J_{\mu}^{\rm sj,rta}

V.4
Results and discussion

These three examples show that the side-jump contribution to
\kappa_{xy}
gives an order one correction to the intrinsic part. Since both its amplitude and its sign depend on the specifics of the disorder hamiltonian, it can enhance or reduce
\kappa_{xy}
and may even lead to sign changes. In addition, even in the case of a trivial (i.e. TR preserving) clean system, special forms of disorder like local viscosity terms can lead to a non-vanishing THE.
In these examples,
\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{x},p_{y}}
and
\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x},p_{y}}
both are concentrated close to the gamma-point
k=0
. It might be interesting, as another direct application, to study cases, for instance hybridized magnon-phonon bands, where one or both of these quantities is largest at a finite momentum, e.g. around the avoided-crossing point.

That the contribution we derived is non-perturbative in the disorder strength (i.e. it scales as
n_{\rm imp}^{0}
) is an intriguing, yet well-known
Nagaosa et al.
property of coordinate shift effects in anomalous Hall transport, and may be given a very intuitive explanation. Whenever heat is carried by well-defined quasiparticles, one may write
\kappa\sim c\,v\,l
with
c
the specific heat,
v
the velocity and
l
the mean-free path. This holds for any contribution independently, so focussing on the side-jump Hall conductivity,

\kappa_{xy}^{\rm sj}\sim c\,v_{\perp}^{\rm sj}\,l,

(55)

where
v_{\perp}^{\rm sj}
is now the side-jump transverse velocity,
v_{\perp}^{\rm sj}\propto n_{\rm imp}
.
Here we have assumed that
l
is governed by disorder scattering,
l^{-1}\propto n_{\rm imp}
, which entails that
\kappa_{xy}^{\rm sj}
is independent of
n_{\rm imp}
.

VI
Conclusion

We have shown that the side-jump contribution gives a sizable non-perturbative contribution to the bosonic THE. As a side product, we also find a generalized formula of the side-jump contribution to the electronic AHE from generic types of impurities (e.g., any local deformation of the Hamiltonian), see Eq.
IV.5
. This contribution is an order one correction to the intrinsic part of the Hall response, that is independent of the density of impurities
only if
the carriers’ mean free path is governed by disorder scattering. For sufficiently strong interactions, such that the mean free path is independent of impurity density, the side-jump contribution becomes perturbative (
\propto n_{\rm imp}
) and might be negligible – the price to pay is that interactions, in turn, will play a role in the Hall conductivity, e.g. via many-body skew-scattering mechanisms. In particular, should there be a near-“universal” mechanism for THE in trivial non-magnetic insulators, interactions should be involved to some extent.

Beyond thermal Hall transport, our gauge invariant formulation of a kinetic equation for bosons including disorder scattering promises many useful applications. For instance it naturally allows for the calculation of other transport coefficients, such as the spin Nernst effect. It also points to several concrete directions for future research: (1) The kinetic formalism should be extended for calculating nonlinear response functions – for a recent treatment of the clean case see
Park et al. 2025
. (2) It can also be generalized to include time-dependent (random) terms in the Hamiltonian, allowing in particular to study in a minimal version the dissipative quantum dynamics of systems with nontrivial band geometry. (3) Our results indicate that quantitative predictions for the THE in the intrinsic regime will require knowledge of the operatorial form of disorder. Thus, it will be useful to interface our theory with quantitative ab-initio efforts to obtain not only the clean features (energies, wavefunctions) of the phonons or magnons, but also input for the effective disorder Hamiltonian. (4) A challenging next step will be the inclusion of interaction effects between bosons, which will not only provide yet another contribution to the thermal Hall effect from skew-scattering, but will also alter the side-jump contribution from impurities, and may even generate a side-jump-like contribution of their own by mixing many-body dynamics with band geometrical properties.

The promise of THE measurements in magnetic insulators is to learn about the properties of charge neutral low energy excitations, most commonly of bosonic nature. Disentangling the different intrinsic and extrinsic contributions will be crucial for a proper understanding. In this sense our work,
by providing means to compute extrinsic, disorder-dependent contributions that might otherwise obscure some more “universal” features, serves as a rigorous step towards a full quantitative theory.

Appendix C
Collection of technical arguments

C.1
Neglecting
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}
:

We first note that
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}
is gauge-invariant, therefore taking
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}\rightarrow 0
is a well-defined approximation.
Note that in the scalar fermionic case,
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}=0
is rigorous.
We now argue why it can also be neglected in the cases we are considering in this paper.

In Eq.(
63
), one sees that
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}
appears with the same sign in the position coordinate of both
({\sf F}_{\rm d})_{n,n}
and
({\sf F}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
on the right-hand side,
so that it can be absorbed in a global shift of
X
without any physical consequences (to the order
\hbar^{1}
).

By contrast, its occurrences in the position coordinate of
{\sf K}_{\rm d}
on the right-hand side cannot be absorbed in such a shift.
However, their contribution to the collision integral is proportional to
\hbar^{1}\partial_{X}\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}
, which can be safely neglected in the homogeneous limit of the bulk theory.
This justifies the assumption
\delta\mathsf{r}^{+}\rightarrow 0
.

C.2
Neglecting interband
(n\neq n^{\prime})
terms:

Here we argue, based on the matrix elements
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}
, that interband effects can be neglected,
at least in the particular case
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{K}
– for a more generally valid proof based on elastic scattering
and the small-deviation approximation, see the main text.

For
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{K}
, one sees from Eqs.(
19a
),(
70
) that within the small-deviation approximation,

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})=(\mathsf{K}_{\rm d})_{n,n^{\prime}}(\tfrac{p+p^{\prime}}{2})+O(p^{\prime}-p),

(71)

where we recall that
\mathsf{K}_{\rm d}
is a diagonal matrix.
Therefore, the moduli of off-band-diagonal elements
\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}
with
n\neq n^{\prime}

are smaller by a factor of the order
|p^{\prime}-p|
than those of band-diagonal elements.
Now, the important quantity whose diagonal and off-diagonal elements need be compared is

\displaystyle\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2},

(72)

as is visible e.g. in Eqs.(
III.3
),(
D.2
).
From Eq.(
31
) one finds
\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n}=O(p^{\prime}-p)
while a priori
\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}=O(1)
for
n\neq n^{\prime}
.
Thus, ultimately Eq.(
72
) is smaller by one power of
|p^{\prime}-p|
for off-diagonal elements than for diagonal ones.
This concludes the argument.

C.3
Replacing
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\rightarrow\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|
:

From the small-deviation expansion Eq.(
B.1
), it is clear that replacing
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
by its modulus will be justified, to the leading order, provided that

\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\;\overset{?}{\in}\;\mathbb{R},

(73)

at least for
n=n^{\prime}
(see App.
C.2
) and for all
p
. We note that because matrix elements
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
appear pairwise in the collision integral, the condition “
\in\mathbb{R}
” (instead of “
>0
”) is sufficient.

In the particular case
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{K}
, the validity of Eq.(
73
) follows directly from Eq.(
71
). More generally, all the cases we consider for applications (see Appendix
G
) satisfy this constraint, although this is not
a priori
a general truth. This justifies the replacement here.

C.4
Validity of the approximation Eq.(
28
):

In the particular case
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{K}
, the approximation Eq.(
28
) is justified.
Indeed, focusing on band-diagonal terms
n=n^{\prime}
as justified above,
it follows immediately from Eq.(
71

Appendix D
Derivation of the accumulation term in the energy current

D.1
Identifying the side-jump current

We derive the energy current from the conservation equation in phase space, following the same steps as
Mangeolle et al.
.
The phase-space energy density in the presence of disorder
V
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(X,p)=\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\sf H}+V\,\overset{\star}{,}\,{\sf F}\right\},

(74)

whose time evolution is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2\hbar}{\rm Im}{\rm Tr}\left([{\sf K}+{\mathsf{V}}\,\overset{\star}{,}\,{\sf F}\star({\sf H}+V)]\right).

(75)

Then after a very short calculation, the phase space energy current, up to a total phase-space derivative and to the order
O(\hbar^{1})
, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(X,p)=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\big(\partial_{\beta}({\sf K}+{\mathsf{V}})\big)\big({\sf F}\star({\sf H}+{V})\big)\right),

(76)

where
\alpha,\beta=X_{\mu},p_{\nu}
and
\omega_{X_{\mu},p_{\nu}}=\delta_{\mu,\nu}=-\omega_{p_{\nu},X_{\mu}}
.

Then to the first order in
\hbar
, it will be sufficient to replace the star-product by a “normal” product
in those terms of the current that contain disorder operators
\mathsf{V}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\alpha}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[(\partial_{\beta}{\sf K})\big({\sf F}\star{\sf H}\big)\right]

(77a)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[(\partial_{\beta}\mathsf{V})\big({\sf F}{\sf H}\big)\right]

(77b)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\partial_{\beta}({\sf K}+\mathsf{V})\big({\sf F}V\big)\right]+\dots.

(77c)

These three terms can be understood as follows:

–

The first term Eq.(
77a
) contains both the clean classical energy current (order
\hbar^{0}
) and its anomalous velocity correction (order
\hbar^{1}
),
responsible for the clean anomalous thermal Hall effect Eq.(
1
), as shown in
Mangeolle et al.
;

–

The second term Eq.(
77b
), as we will show, has the interpretation of the side-jump accumulation current;

–

The third term Eq.(
77c
) features
V
where
{\sf H}
was in Eq.(
77a
), whose interpretation was the normalization of eigenvectors or, physically and more loosely speaking,
the amount of energy carried by a bosonic excitation. Thus Eq.(
77c
) corresponds to a local modulation of the normalization of wavepackets,
or “equivalently” to a renormalization of the bosonic energies by disorder. In both cases, as argued in Sec.
III.1
,
this falls outside of the semiclassical picture as it is usually defined: thus we will discard this contribution in the following.

Introducing
\mathsf{K}_{\rm d},\mathsf{F}_{\rm d}
defined in Sec.
II.3
and using the normalization choice
\mathsf{S}^{\dagger}\star\mathsf{H}\star\mathsf{S}=\text{\textbb{1}}
,
which as shown in
Mangeolle et al.
guarantees that the clean anomalous thermal Hall current density is band-diagonal, Eq.(
77b
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{V})\mathsf{S}{\sf F}_{\rm d}\right],

(78)

and its integrated version Eq.(
38
) in the main text.

D.2
Semiclassical expansion

We can now use the fact that in real-space notations

\displaystyle(\partial_{p_{\mu}}{\mathsf{V}})(x,y)\equiv-i(x_{\mu}-y_{\mu}){\mathsf{V}}(x,y),

(79)

then carry out the same technical derivation as in Sec.
II.3
and App.
A
.
We do not reproduce the intermediate steps here, since they are very similar. The upshot is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)=-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\,\delta\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right)

\displaystyle\times\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\;(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime},t)\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\;\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime}),

(80)

In Eq.(
D.2
) one already took
\delta{\mathsf{r}}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{+}(p,p^{\prime})\rightarrow 0
, as justified in App.
C.1
, for notational clarity.
Now focusing on band-diagonal processes (
n=n^{\prime}
), as justified in App.
C.2
and in the main text,
and dropping
\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})
within the position arguments of energies and distribution functions since this is irrelevant to the order
O(\hbar^{1})
,
one obtains the result Eq.(
39
) in the main text.

It might not be obvious why the same technical steps appear in the derivation of the
kinetic equation
and the
current
. A proof based on a Ward identity is given in
König and Levchenko
.
In the present case, here is an intuitive explanation at the technical level. One can rewrite Eq.(
78
) as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left((\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}){\sf F}_{\rm d}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}\,{\rm Tr}\left(\mathsf{\Lambda}_{p_{\mu}}\left[\mathsf{V}_{\rm d},{\sf F}_{\rm d}\right]\right),

(81)

with
\mathsf{\Lambda}_{p_{\mu}}=\mathsf{S}^{-1}\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{S}
. In the case where interband processes are neglected (meaning only diagonal entries of
\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}
are considered),
the commutator in the second term vanishes and
{\mathcal{J}}_{\mu}^{\rm sj}
consists only of the first term.
Using eq.(
79
) and the fact that
x-\xi=2\left(\tfrac{x+y}{2}-\tfrac{y+\xi}{2}\right)
, one gets

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(x,y)\approx{\rm Im}\,{\rm Tr}\int_{\xi}\left[\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}(x,\xi){\sf F}_{\rm d}(\xi,y)\right]\,(\tfrac{x+y}{2}-\tfrac{y+\xi}{2}).

(82)

Now it is key to notice (e.g. from Eq.(
11
)) that Appendix
A
is precisely devoted to computing

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}\circ{\sf F}_{\rm d}-{\sf F}_{\rm d}\circ\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}^{\dagger}=2i\,{\rm Im}\,\left[\mathsf{V}_{\rm d}\circ{\sf F}_{\rm d}\right],

(83)

which is the right-hand side of the kinetic equation.

Thus
{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}
(Eq.(
82
)) can be easily obtained from the calculation of App.
A
,
by in Eq.(
A
) or Eq.(
A
) replacing

{\sf F}_{\rm d}(K)\rightarrow(X_{\mu}-i\partial_{K_{\mu}})\,{\sf F}_{\rm d}(K),

and dividing by
2i
. Assuming
\delta\mathsf{r}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{+}(p,p^{\prime})\rightarrow 0
as always, one thus obtains (cf e.g. Eq.(
63
))

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}(-i)\,\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\,\delta\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta X_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right)

\displaystyle\times\bigg[\big(X-X\big)\times(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p,t)\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|\,\left|\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime},n}(p^{\prime},p)\right|

\displaystyle-\big(X-X-\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\big)\times(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X+\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}),p^{\prime},t)\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|\,\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|\,\bigg],

(84)

which is the correct result Eq.(
D.2
).

D.3
Formal proof that the side-jump current vanishes in thermodynamic equilibrium

We now consider its value at equilibrium,
{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)\big|_{\rm eq}
, obtained by replacing

(\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})\rightarrow f\big(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime}),T(X)\big)

within Eq.(
39
), where
f(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\left[n_{\rm B}(\varepsilon)+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]
.
The deviation
{\mathtt{g}}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}=O(\hbar^{1})
, which yields the corrected equilibrium distribution,
need not be included to this order: we will only verify that

\displaystyle J_{\mu}^{\rm sj}(X)\big|_{\rm eq}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{p}{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)\big|_{\rm eq}=0+O(\hbar^{2}).

(85)

We know on general grounds (see
Mangeolle et al.
especially Appendix B.3 therein) that the boson spectrum is symmetric,
in the sense that there exists a bijection between bands
n\leftrightarrow\overline{n}
such that

\displaystyle(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(-p)

\displaystyle=-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{\overline{n},\overline{n}}(p),

(86)

\displaystyle(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n}(-p)

\displaystyle=-(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{\overline{n},\overline{n}}(p).

(87)

Since
f(-\varepsilon)=-f(\varepsilon)
, one finds the relation

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,p)\big|_{\rm eq}

\displaystyle=-{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj}(X,-p)\big|_{\rm eq},

(88)

therefore its momentum integral vanishes, which proves Eq.(
85
).

Appendix E
Solving Boltzmann’s equation and the displaced local equilibrium current

Here we derive the solution Eq.(
34
) to the equation Eq.(
III.3
), to the first order in
\hbar
, equivalently in coordinate shifts
\delta\mathsf{X}
.
To this order the “compensation term”
\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}(X,p)
and out-of-equilibrium part
\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}(X,p)
can be solved for independently,
which we do following the steps of Ref.
Sinitsyn et al. 2005
adapted to our generalized bosonic problem and notations.

E.1
Displaced local equilibrium current

We write the time-independent
local equilibrium
solution to the kinetic equation as

\displaystyle\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p)

\displaystyle=f\left(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p),T(X)\right)+\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm sj}(X,p)

(89)

where
\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}
is assumed to satisfy local equilibrium,
{\rm D}_{t}\,\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p)=0
.
In the collision integral of Eq.(
III.3
), we expand to the order of linear response,

\displaystyle f\!\left(E,T(X)\right)-f\!\left(E,T(X+\delta\mathsf{X}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime}))\right)

(90)

\displaystyle=-\delta X_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\;\partial_{X}T\;\partial_{T}f\!\left(E,T(X)\right),

where

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\simeq(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X,p^{\prime}).

(91)

Here the “
\simeq
” approximate equality is enforced by the energy conservation delta-function in Eq.(
27
);
below, this is enforced only to the order
O(\hbar^{0})
which is sufficient in that context. Then Eq.(
III.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\forall n,\quad 0

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\sum_{n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\;\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X,p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big)

(92)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\bigg[(\mathsf{g}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)-(\mathsf{g}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X,p^{\prime})-\delta\mathsf{X}^{\mu}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\,\mathsf{\partial}_{X_{\mu}}T\;\partial_{T}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{(}{}^{\prime}{}^{)},n^{(}{}^{\prime}{}^{)}}(X,p^{(}{}^{\prime}{}^{)})\right)\bigg]+\dots

where “
+\dots
” contains terms higher-order in powers of
\hbar
or beyond linear response,
and the optional set of apostrophes in
\partial_{T}f
follows from Eq.(
91
).

We now use the fact that Eq.(
26
), up to the approximations discussed elsewhere, takes the form Eq.(
34
).
Then, a solution to the collision integral is given by Eq.(
35b
) in the main text, which is clearly gauge invariant.

E.2
Response in the relaxation time approximation (RTA)

We now consider the
O(\hbar^{0})
out-of-(local-)equilibrium distribution
\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}
, by again defining

\displaystyle\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}(X,p)=f(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p),T(X))+\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta}(X,p)

(93)

but now only requiring that both the left-hand side and the right-hand side of Eq.(
III.3
) be
O(\hbar^{0})
.
We focus on the bulk currents of the homogeneous theory, so that spatial gradients
\partial_{X}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p)
are neglected, and the left-hand side reads

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{t}\,\underline{\mathsf{F}}_{\rm d}\Big|_{n,n}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\partial_{p_{\mu}}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}\;\partial_{X_{\mu}}T\;\partial_{T}f(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p),T(X))\Big|_{n,n}

(94)

Meanwhile, in the right-hand side, all the position shifts can be discarded at
O(\hbar^{0})
, and one obtains

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{\mu}}\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}\;\partial_{X_{\mu}}T\;\partial_{T}f(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(X,p))\Big|_{n,n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\sum_{n^{\prime}}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\times\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X,p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big)

(95)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\times\bigg[(\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta})_{n,n}(X,p)-(\mathsf{g}_{\rm d}^{\rm rta})_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(X,p^{\prime})\bigg].

While this is still in principle a hard problem, it reduces to the relaxation time approximation
Mahan 2000
,
provided that scattering is elastic (which is the case here), that dispersion is isotropic (meaning
\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(|p|)
only) and non-degenerate,
and that scattering is isotropic i.e.
\mathsf{M}_{n,n^{\prime}}(p,p^{\prime})
only depends on the angle between
p
and
p^{\prime}

(i.e. on
\hat{\rm p}\cdot\hat{\rm p}^{\prime}
where we define
\hat{\rm p}\equiv p/|p|
).

Assuming all this for the sake of analytical tractability, one obtains Eqs.(
35c
),(
IV.1
) in the main text.

E.3
Explicit writing of the side-jump current in the RTA

Inserting Eq.(
35c
) into Eq.(
39
), one obtains

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{X_{\mu}}^{\rm sj,rta}(X,p)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int_{p^{\prime}}\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\,\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big)\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times(p^{\prime}-p)_{\nu}\;(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n}(p)\times\partial_{k_{\rho}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})\;\partial_{X_{\rho}}T\;\partial_{T}f((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime}))\,\stau_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}})_{n,n}(p)\,\partial_{k_{\rho}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\;\partial_{X_{\rho}}T\;\partial_{T}f((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p))\;\stau_{n,n}(X,p)\;\Xi^{\nu}_{n}(X,p).

(96)

In going to the second line we used the fact that
\stau_{n,n}
, defined in Eq.(
IV.1

|p|=|p^{\prime}|

and we used the temporary notation

\displaystyle\Xi^{\nu}_{n}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\int_{p^{\prime}}(p^{\prime}-p)_{\nu}\;\tilde{w}(p-p^{\prime})\;\left|\mathsf{M}_{n,n}(p,p^{\prime})\right|^{2}\,\delta\Big((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p^{\prime})-(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\Big)

Appendix F
Other calculatory details on Hall currents

F.1
Thermal Hall conductivity

We consider for concreteness the case where a temperature gradient is imposed along
x
and the (local) energy current is measured along
y
.
Then, explicitly, Equations (
37
) and (
40
) read

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm sj,rta}(X)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}T\times\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\,\partial_{p_{x}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\;(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(X,p)\,\partial_{T}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right),

(98)

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm gv,sj}(X)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}T\times\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\,\partial_{p_{y}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\;(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(X,p)\,\partial_{T}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right).

(99)

Contrarily to the scalar fermionic case, because
\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}
is not antisymmetric a priori, these two currents are not identical a priori.
The local current due to disorder,
{J}_{y}^{\rm dis}\equiv{J}_{y}^{\rm sj,rta}+{J}_{y}^{\rm gv,sj}
, is thus

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm dis}(X)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}T\times\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\;\left[(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(p)\,\partial_{p_{x}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)-(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(p)\,\partial_{p_{y}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\right]\;\partial_{T}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\right).

(100)

Now one can use the spectrum symmetry relations Eqs.(
86
),(
87
) to replace
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\rightarrow{\rm Tr}_{+}
, which is
the sum over positive-eigenvalue eigenspaces of the dynamical matrix i.e. over bosonic
bands
in the strict physical sense.

We obtain the disorder-induced thermal Hall conductivity

\displaystyle\kappa_{xy}^{\rm dis}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{+}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\;\left[{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}}(p)\,\partial_{p_{x}}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(p)-{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}}(p)\,\partial_{p_{y}}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(p)\right]\;\partial_{T}f\!\left({\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d}(p),T\right),

(101)

and using
\partial_{T}f(\varepsilon,T)=-(\varepsilon^{2}/T)\,\partial_{\varepsilon}n_{\rm B}(\varepsilon,T)
we obtain the final result Eq.(
2
) in the main text.

F.2
Electrical Hall effect

The two disorder contributions to the
charge
current are

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm sj,rta}(X)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{e}^{2}E_{x}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\,\partial_{p_{x}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\;(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(X,p)\,\partial_{\varepsilon}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right),

(102)

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm gv,sj}(X)

\displaystyle=+\mathsf{e}^{2}E_{x}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\,\partial_{p_{y}}(\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\;(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(X,p)\,\partial_{\varepsilon}f\!\left((\underline{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(X,p)\right).

(103)

The local
charge
current due to disorder,
{J}_{y}^{\rm dis}\equiv{J}_{y}^{\rm sj,rta}+{J}_{y}^{\rm gv,sj}
, is

\displaystyle{J}_{y}^{\rm dis}(X)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{e}^{2}E_{x}\sum_{n}\int_{p}p_{\lambda}\;\left[(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{y}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(p)\,\partial_{p_{x}}({\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)-(\mathsf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{W}}_{p_{x}p_{\lambda}})_{n,n}(p)\,\partial_{p_{y}}({\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\right]\;\partial_{\varepsilon}f\!\left(({\mathsf{K}}_{\rm d})_{n,n}(p)\right).

(104)

At odds with the bosonic case, here one simply has
\sum_{n}\rightarrow{\rm Tr}
, which is
the sum over fermionic bands.
We obtain the disorder-induced electrical Hall conductivity, Eq.(
IV.5
) in the main text.

Appendix G
Applications – Details

G.1
Effective low-energy theory for magnons in an antiferromagnet

For an antiferromagnet on a bipartite lattice, the spin operator may be expressed locally as

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=x,y,z}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}\left[(-1)^{\mathbf{r}}{\rm n}_{a}(\mathbf{r})+{\rm m}_{a}(\mathbf{r})\right]

(105)

where the vectors
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}
define an orthonormal basis,
(-1)^{\mathbf{r}}=\pm
alternates between the two sublattices,
and
{\rm n}_{a},{\rm m}_{a}
are the staggered and the net magnetization fluctuations, respectively,
constrained by

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{2}+\mathbf{n}^{2}=1,\qquad\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0.

(106)

Assuming collinear Néel order along the
z
axis, to the cubic order in the transverse components one may replace

\displaystyle{\rm m}_{z}

\displaystyle\simeq-{\rm m}_{x}{\rm n}_{x}-{\rm m}_{y}{\rm n}_{y},

(107a)

\displaystyle{\rm n}_{z}

\displaystyle\simeq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\rm m}_{x}^{2}+{\rm n}_{x}^{2}+{\rm m}_{y}^{2}+{\rm n}_{y}^{2}\right).

(107b)

This yields an effective theory in terms of the low-energy fields
n_{x},n_{y},m_{x},m_{y}
,
defined in the collinear case simply as
n_{a}={\rm n}_{a},m_{a}={\rm m}_{a}
for
a\in\{x,y\}
.
These low-energy fields satisfy the commutation relation

\displaystyle[m_{y},n_{x}]=i\hbar=-[m_{x},n_{y}]

(108)

which is Eq.(
45
).

In terms of
m_{i},n_{i}
with
i=1,2
, and writing
\partial_{1}\equiv\partial_{x},\partial_{2}\equiv\partial_{y}
, the hamiltonian Eq.(
V.1
) reads

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\chi}m^{i}m^{i}+\Delta n_{i}n_{i}

(109)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}c_{1}n^{i}\partial_{\mu\mu}^{2}n^{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}c_{2}\partial_{i}n^{i}\partial_{j}n^{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}d_{4}m^{i}\partial_{\mu\mu}^{2}n^{i}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}d_{1}\epsilon_{ij}m^{i}\partial_{\mu\mu}^{2}n^{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}d_{2}\epsilon_{i\overline{i}}\partial_{i}m^{\overline{i}}\partial_{j}n^{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}d_{3}\epsilon_{ij}m^{i}n^{j}.

Because to leading order in spatial gradients

m^{i}\approx\chi\partial_{t}n^{i},

up to integration by parts the
d_{4}
term is a total time derivative and does not appear in the effective theory.
It is then possible to write

\epsilon_{i\overline{i}}\partial_{i}m^{\overline{i}}\partial_{j}n^{j}\rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ij}m^{i}\partial^{2}_{\mu\mu}n^{j},

so that an equivalent effective hamiltonian density is

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\chi}m^{i}m^{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}c_{1}n^{i}\partial_{\mu\mu}^{2}n^{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}c_{2}\partial_{i}n^{i}\partial_{j}n^{j}

(110)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}d_{3}\epsilon_{ij}m^{i}n^{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}(d_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}d_{2})\epsilon_{ij}m^{i}\partial_{\mu\mu}^{2}n^{j}+\Delta n_{i}n_{i}.

One can rewrite this, to the first order in
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
, as Eq.(
46
) in the main text.

G.2
Diagonalization of the clean problem

The dynamical matrix is

\mathsf{K}=i\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-p_{\mu}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{\chi}\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{i,j}+\frac{c_{2}}{\chi}\delta_{i,\mu}\delta_{\nu,j}\right)p_{\nu}&-\eta\epsilon_{ij}p^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(111)

One finds the energy bands

\displaystyle{\sf K}_{\rm d}

\displaystyle={\rm diag}\left(\varepsilon_{1},-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},-\varepsilon_{2}\right),

(112)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=[(c_{1}+c_{2})\bm{p}^{2}/\chi]^{\frac{1}{2}},\;\quad\varepsilon_{2}=[c_{1}\bm{p}^{2}/\chi]^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the Berry curvatures

\displaystyle{\mathsf{\Omega}}_{p_{x}p_{y}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\eta\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{4c_{1}+3c_{2}}{2c_{2}}\quad+O(\eta^{3}),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{\Omega}}_{p_{x}p_{y}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\eta\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{4c_{1}+c_{2}}{2c_{2}}\quad+O(\eta^{3}).

(113)

The normalized eigenvectors are
\Psi_{i},\Psi_{i}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\;\vline\;\Psi_{1}^{*}\;\vline\;\Psi_{2}\;\vline\;\Psi_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

(114)

A suitable choice of properly normalized eigenvectors (in the sense
\mathsf{S}^{\dagger}\mathsf{H}\mathsf{S}=\text{\textbb{1}}
) is

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{\chi}\frac{\eta}{c_{2}}\frac{|\bm{k}|}{|k_{2}|}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\frac{|k_{2}|}{|\bm{k}|},\frac{i}{\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{|k_{2}|}{|\bm{k}|},\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\frac{|k_{2}|}{|\bm{k}|}-i\frac{\eta}{c_{2}}\frac{|\bm{k}|}{|k_{2}|}\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{1},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\frac{|k_{2}|}{|\bm{k}|}\right)^{\top}\;+O(\eta^{2}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{\chi}\frac{\eta}{c_{2}}\frac{|\bm{k}|}{|k_{1}|}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}\frac{|k_{1}|}{|\bm{k}|},-\frac{i}{\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{|k_{1}|}{|\bm{k}|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}},\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\frac{|k_{1}|}{|\bm{k}|}-i\frac{\eta}{c_{2}}\frac{|\bm{k}|}{|k_{1}|}\sqrt{\chi}\varepsilon_{2},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\frac{|k_{1}|}{|\bm{k}|}\right)^{\top}\;+O(\eta^{2}).

(115)

Here we provide
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}
to the first order in
\eta
only.
This is sufficient to compute the leading contribution to the Berry curvature
\mathsf{\Omega}_{p_{x}p_{y}}
given above, Eq.(
G.2
),
which is odd under time reversal, and thus odd in powers of
\eta
.

[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)]_{n,n}=0,

implying that the small-momentum expansion in App.
B.1
would be ill-defined.
Here instead we consider
\hat{W}
as given in Eq.(
54
), whence

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}={\sf\Gamma}\big|_{\eta=0}\hat{W}={\sf K}\big|_{\eta=0}-\tilde{\eta}p^{2}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{y}&0\\
0&\sigma^{y}\end{bmatrix}.

(122)

We obtain

\displaystyle\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)\right]_{n,n}=\left(\varepsilon_{1}\;;\;-\varepsilon_{1}\;;\;\varepsilon_{2}\;;\;-\varepsilon_{2}\right),

(123)

and

\displaystyle\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{W}(p)\mathsf{S}(p)\right]_{n,n}

(124)

\displaystyle\quad=-\tilde{\eta}p^{2}(p_{\mu}/p^{2})\left(\varepsilon_{1}\;;\;-\varepsilon_{1}\;;\;\varepsilon_{2}\;;\;-\varepsilon_{2}\right)_{n,n},

which is real so the third term in Eq.(
32
) vanishes. There only subsists the second term, which is determined by

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\mathsf{W}(p)\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{S}(p)\right]_{n,n}

\displaystyle\quad=-\tilde{\eta}p^{2}\,{\rm Im}\left[\mathsf{S}^{-1}(p)\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{y}&0\\
0&\sigma^{y}\end{bmatrix}\partial_{p_{\mu}}\mathsf{S}(p)\right]_{n,n}

\displaystyle\quad=+\tilde{\eta}p^{2}\left(\epsilon_{\mu,\overline{\mu}}(p_{\overline{\mu}}/p^{2})+O(\eta^{2})\right).

(125)

G.4
Concrete microscopic spin model: details

Here we show how to derive
\chi,c_{1},c_{2}
from the hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\rm spin}

\displaystyle=J\sum_{\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\rangle}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}+K\sum_{\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\rangle_{a}}S^{a}_{\mathbf{r}}S^{a}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}.

(126)

Performing the expansion Eqs. (
105
)–(
108
) and assuming smooth variations of the emergent
\bm{m},\bm{n}
fields to derive a continuous low-energy theory,
Eq.(
126
) becomes, on the square lattice,

\displaystyle H_{\rm mag}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\sum_{\bm{\eta}}\bigg(2m_{a}(\mathbf{r})m_{a}(\mathbf{r})+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu}\eta^{\nu}\partial_{\mu}n_{a}(\mathbf{r})\partial_{\nu}n_{a}(\mathbf{r})\bigg)+K\sum_{\mathbf{r}}m_{a}(\mathbf{r})m_{a}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{K}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}n_{a}(\mathbf{r})n_{a}(\mathbf{r})+\frac{K}{4}{{\mathtt{a}}}^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bigg(\partial_{x}n_{x}(\mathbf{r})\partial_{x}n_{x}(\mathbf{r})+\partial_{y}n_{y}(\mathbf{r})\partial_{y}n_{y}(\mathbf{r})\bigg)+\dots,

(127)

where
{\mathtt{a}}
is the lattice parameter and “
+\dots
” contains terms that are higher order in gradients or beyond quadratic order in
\bm{m},\bm{n}
.
Since the purpose of this appendix is to derive the magnon velocities, determined by parameters
\chi,c_{1},c_{2}
, and not the magnon gap which will depend sensitively on other (anisotropic) magnetic exchanges not included in Eq.(
126
), we henceforth drop the mass term in Eq.(
G.4
).

The last term is not rotationally invariant, therefore to recover the symmetry of Eq.(
V.1
) we select its rotation-invariant component.
Technically this is simply done by writing
\bm{n}=R_{\theta}\tilde{\bm{n}}
and
\bm{\partial}=R_{\theta}\tilde{\bm{\partial}}
, where
R_{\theta}
is the 2D rotation matrix by an angle
\theta

and
\tilde{\bm{n}},\tilde{\bm{\partial}}
are the expression of
{\bm{n}},{\bm{\partial}}
in a rotated orthogonal frame, coinciding with
(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{y}})
at
\theta=0
.
We then average over the angle,
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\text{d}\theta
. This yields

\displaystyle\partial_{x}n_{x}\partial_{x}n_{x}+\partial_{y}n_{y}\partial_{y}n_{y}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{n})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\bm{n}\cdot\nabla^{2}\bm{n}.

(128)

One thus obtains

\displaystyle H_{\rm mag}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}(8J+2K)m_{a}(\mathbf{r})m_{a}(\mathbf{r})-\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{a}}^{2}J\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bigg(n_{a}(\mathbf{r})\partial^{2}_{xx}n_{a}(\mathbf{r})+n_{a}(\mathbf{r})\partial^{2}_{yy}n_{a}(\mathbf{r})\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{K}{4}{{\mathtt{a}}}^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{n})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bm{n}\cdot\nabla^{2}\bm{n}\bigg)+\dots,

(129)

allowing for the identification
\chi^{-1}=8J+2K,c_{1}={\mathtt{a}}^{2}(J+K/8),c_{2}={\mathtt{a}}^{2}K/4
as reported in the main text.
