A Shotgun Proteomic-Based Approach with a Q-Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap High-Resolution Mass Spectrometer for the Assessment of Pesticide Mixture-Induced Neurotoxicity on a 3D-Developed Neurospheroid Model from Human Brain Meningiomas: Identification of Trityl-Post-Translational Modification
Authors: Kaouthar Louati, Amina Maalej, Fatma Kolsi, Rim Kallel, Yassine Gdoura, Mahdi Borni, Leila Sellami Hakim, Rania Zribi, Sirine Choura, Sami Sayadi, Mohamed Chamkha, Basma Mnif, Zouheir Khemakhem, Tahya Sellami Boudawara, Mohamed Zaher Boudawara, Abderrahman Bouraoui, Jamil Kraiem, Fathi Safta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread use of pesticides, particularly in combinations, has resulted in enhanced hazardous health effects. However, little is known about their molecular mechanism of interactions. The aim of this study was to assess the neurotoxicity effect of pesticides in mixtures by adopting a 3D in vitro developed neurospheroid model, followed by treatment by increased concentrations of pesticides for 24 h and analysis by a shotgun proteomic-based approach with high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry. Three proteins, namely, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate-dehydrogenase (GAPDH), α-enolase, and phosphoglycerate-kinase-1, were selected as key targets in the metabolic process. Only high doses of pesticides mitigated cell-density proliferation with the occurrence of apoptotic cells, which unlikely makes any neurological alterations in environmental regulatory exposures. The proteomic analysis showed that majority of altered proteins were implicated in cell metabolism. De novo peptide sequencing revealed ion losses and adduct formation, namely, a trityl-post-translational modification in the active site of 201-GAPDH protein. The study also highlights the plausible role of pyrethroids to be implicated in the deleterious effects of pesticides in a mixture. To the best of our knowledge, our finding is the first in toxicoproteomics to deeply elucidate pesticides’ molecular interactions and their ability to adduct proteins as a pivotal role in the neurotoxicity mechanism.Keywords: brain tumors, 3D neurospheroids model, neurotoxicity, tandem mass spectrometry, pesticides, proteomics, oxidative stress, post-translational modification, toxicity biomarkers

Introduction

In agricultural areas, pesticides are the most widely used chemicals, especially in mixtures, due to the necessity to increase efficacy and reduce the cost of crop pest treatment. However, because of lack of safe management in developing countries, various environmental compartments are contaminated and pesticide residues are found in a wide range of everyday foods resulting in enhanced toxicity for various organisms,1−10 especially if they are simultaneously exposed to more than two chemical substances that may interact and have synergistic effects, leading to many health problems such as cancer, chronic inflammatory diseases, and neurological disorders.11−15

The use of synthetic pyrethroids in combination with neonicotinoid insecticides is common in modern agricultural practices, given that diverse methylcarbamate and organophosphorus pesticides have been now subjected to restrictions as they are highly toxic to mammals and ineffective against pest resistance.16,17 In our Tunisian market, mixtures based on imidacloprid (IMD), a member of the neonicotinoid class, and Lambda-cyhalothrin (λ-CYH), a new generation type II synthetic pyrethroid, are most widely used owing to their high efficacy and broad spectrum practices18 with lowest toxicity to mammals and birds.11,19−22 Previous toxicological studies had been conducted to examine each pesticide’s possible neurotoxic effects on nontargeted organisms, including animals,23−30 aquatic creatures (fish, carps),31,32 or in mixtures,33−35 and to assess their oxidative damage, metabolic impairment, genotoxicity, and endocrine-disrupting activity17,36−41· Nevertheless, little is known about the corresponding neurotoxicity molecular mechanisms, and even studies on their plausible interacting effects on human neuroglial cells are lacking.

Pesticides are known to induce toxicity at the cellular level through alteration in redox homeostasis by the generation of oxidative stress following the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS)42 either by products of detoxification pathways, altering the functioning of mitochondrial transport and endoplasmic reticulum electron chains, or by entering redox cycles.43−48 Disruption of the normal cell state can deregulate several signaling pathways,49 impair neuroeffector junctions,50−54 or harm all cell components such as proteins, DNA, and lipids, resulting in lipid peroxidation, metabolism disruption due to variation in the expression of antioxidants, inactivation of glycolytic enzymes, and ultimately to apoptotic or necrotic cell death.55−59

Proteins as one of the major targets of oxygen-free radicals and other reactive species may encounter post-translational modifications (PTMs) or be covalently modified by adduct formation on its key amino acid side chains to initiate toxicity via modification of their structure and/or cellular function (e.g., catalytic activity) to secondarily damage the cytological processes.60 These modifications are considered pivotal predictors of toxicity or biomarkers to diagnose chemical poisoning or examine exposure to toxicants which allows detailed understanding of the underlying mechanisms involved in toxicity.60

Characterization of protein adducts and/or PTMs that symbolizes changes in the polypeptide chain has received great research interest as a potential diagnostic biomarker related to toxic response for an in-depth identification of their role in pathogenesis, which helps answer clinical questions on novel strategies of diagnosis and therapy. However, their analysis is challenging which highlights the need for computational and sensitive methods for their detection.61

Cultured cell lines in toxicology studies have been well recognized and widely used, especially the 3D proliferation of primary cells, which is considered as the most rigorous and representative model for in vitro cytotoxicity investigations that could bridge the gap between animal models, which require the use of a high number without being a true representative of the human body,28 and conventional 2D monolayer cell cultures, which cannot mimic the intricacy and diversity of clinical tumors developed according to a particular structure and architecture.

The use of in vitro models with proteomics approaches has recently grown in cancer-biology science due to the advancement in modern mass spectrometry (MS) including new ionization techniques, fragmentation methods, and more sensitive and accurate analyzers, as well as improvement of enrichment strategies, development of more powerful PTM workflows by targeted or semitargeted scanning procedures, bioinformatics tools, and powerful algorithms of protein search databases. Hence, it is now possible to perform the identification of various PTMs and protein-xenobiotic adducts with high sensitivity and accuracy into a single measurement62,63·

Hence, the current study was designed to explore whether low or high doses of IMD and λ-CYH used in the mixture could elicit neural cell apoptosis in order to assess their potential neurotoxicity and identify novel molecular mechanisms. Our strategy first involved development of human stem-cell-derived 3D-neuronal spheroids for in vitro neurotoxicity analysis after short-term treatments with pesticides in binary mixtures. Afterward, we adopted a shotgun-proteomic-based approach for the identification of protein-PTMs and prediction of alteration sites. The full workflow adaptive methodogy is described in Figure 1.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

All reagents and substances used during this research were of HPLC analytical grade (purity >95%). Our selected pesticide molecules, namely, IMD, C9H10ClN5O2, and λ-CYH, C22H19Cl2NO3, were those adapted in our previous investigations.64,65 Their corresponding molecular structures are shown in Figure 1. Standard solutions based on these pesticide mixtures, were prepared as described in our previously published papers64,65 and serially diluted; then, prior cytotoxicity analysis was conducted.

All cell culture reagents and materials essential for the growth and maintenance of brain tumor spheroids, such as culture media, serum, and growth factors, were those described in our prior studies.64,65 All necessary solvents for MS/MS analyses were also those adapted in our previous investigations.64,65

Ethical Consideration

The Declaration of Helsinki was followed in this study process that was approved under the same number of our previously published works64,65 by our Ethics Committee in Habib Bourguiba University Hospital where this research was conducted.

The target participants consented to participate in this study. Before undergoing any neurosurgery, each patient gave his written informed consent for any risk of paralysis or death. In addition, each individual participant whose identifying information appears in this work gave his additional informed consent.

Sample Collection and Culture Conditions

Preliminary experiments have been conducted on diverse meningiomas [human brain meningiomas (MNGs)] histological subtypes including meningiomal metastasis in order to access the neurotoxicity effect of our selected pesticide molecules and determine the inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the binary mixture. Nine surgical specimens were used for the generation of neurospheroids that were grown in triplicate by applying enzymatic and nonenzymatic dissociation techniques as detailed in the Supporting Information file.

Afterward, to evaluate the MNG self-renewal ability to form secondary subspheres, primary spheres were gently dissociated into single cells, plated into a single-cell ultralow attachment plate, and maintained in the neural stem cell culture medium.

We visually inspected spheroid health and growth under an inverted phase contrast bright-field microscope (MOTIC AE21, Motic Incorporation Ltd. Hong Kong, China) equipped with an Axiocam camera 208, the main characteristics of which are described in our previously published studies.64,65

Immunohistochemical Staining

Primary cell identity was confirmed prior to use by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, namely, for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) (Bio-Rad cat. no. MCA1908, RRID: AB_322557) and vimentin (VIM) (Abcam catalog no. 4211–1, RRID: AB_765097), which are the most widely used markers of MNG diagnosis, and keratin (RayBiotech cat. no. DS-MB-01725, RRID: AB_854575) and GATA3 (trans-acting T-cell-specific transcription factor) (BD Biosciences catalog no. 563349, RRID: AB_2738152) for the diagnosis of meningiomal metastasis.

Neurospheroid expression of “Stemness” markers was also analyzed for CD133, VIM, Nestin, SOX2, GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) and βIII-tubulin using the same panels of mouse-anti-human monoclonal primary antibodies as performed in our previous studies on gliomas and medulloblastoma brain tumors.64,65

Following standard fixation and embedding techniques, both primary cells and 3D spheroids were extracted and processed into histological blocks and then sectioned into very thin slides for IHC staining. The detailed experimental protocol is presented in the Supporting Information file.

Pesticide Treatment

We have adopted the same method described in our previous published works64−66 for in vitro neurotoxicity analysis of pesticide mixtures on human stem-cell-derived 3D-neuronal spheroids. We daily assessed the neurospheroid health and growth size by microscopy, and after 7 days, once the diameter reached the ideal range of 250 to 350 μm, we measured the coefficient of variation (CV) (Appendices eqs A and B) in spheroid volumes to confirm growth uniformity and size reproducibility prior pesticide treatment.

Since tumor cells are typically less sensitive in 3D compounds compared to 2D compounds, many trials have been performed to check the appraite inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of our pesticide molecules used in a binary mixture.

Five serial diluted concentrations were used ranging from 2.95 μM (754 ng/g) to 0.59 mM (151 × 103 ng/g) of IMD and 0.55 μM (247 ng/g) to 0.11 mM (49.5 ng/g) of λ-CYH. After careful removal of 100 μL of neurosphere medium from each plate and adding 100 μL of pesticide mixture, we kept the plates in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2/37 °C for 24 h. For each concentration, three replicates were analyzed. Each analysis also included untreated control cells, obtained by substituting 100 μL of media with 100 μL of 0.01% DMSO. At the end of the exposure period, toxicity end points were established for both exposed and control neurospheroids.

Spheroid Viability Tracking

The MTT assay and microscopy were used for the assessment of the histopathological profile and morphological alterations of tumor tissues.

MTT Assay Viability

After 24 h of pesticide treatment, the MTT assay viability was performed following the protocol described in our previous published works.64,65 A dose–response curve was created, and a nonlinear regression analysis was used to determine the IC50 value of the mixture of IMD and λ-CYH (concentrations needed for 50% growth inhibition).

Assessing Morphological Changes

The morphological changes in the spheroid shape and density after pesticide exposure were evaluated by microscopy.

Histopathological Profile Analysis

This method was adopted for assessment of the cytotoxic response based on cell proliferation of tumor neurospheroids following pesticide treatment. The collected pieces of tumor tissues were manipulated following the protocol described in our previous published studies.64,65

Measurement of Glycolytic Activity

Brain tumor samples from the control and treated spheroids were collected and washed with cold PBS solution, then suspended in ice-cold 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm at 4 °C using a cold microcentrifuge. The supernatant homogenates were collected for the estimation of the glycolytic activity by colorimetric methods using standard commercial kits such as human glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate-dehydrogenase (GAPDH) activity assay kit (ab204732), Enolase assay kit (ab241024), and phosphoglycerate kinase activity assay kit (ab252890), which are all from BioVision (Abcam, USA). Assays for each enzyme were performed by following the kit protocol. Enzyme activity is expressed as U/mg of total protein in the sample.

Sample Preparation and Protein Extraction

Following a 24 h pesticide treatment period, three samples of each treated and nontretaed brain tumor spheroids were processed in the same way to the detailed protocol described in our published studies64,65 for protein extraction, reduction, alkylation, and trypsin digestion. The resulting extracted peptides were lyophilized to near-dryness to be analyzed by the nano-ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC)-MS/MS.

Analysis by Nanoflow UHPLC-MS/MS

Samples were analyzed on the same day by high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry consisting of an Ultimate 3000 nano-UHPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), an ESI nanospray source, a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and a higher energy collision induced dissociation (HCD) fragmentation mode. The nanocolumn comprised a trapping column (PepMap C18, 100 Å, 100 μm × 2 cm, 5 μm) and an analytical column (PepMap C18, 100 Å, 75 μm × 50 cm, 2 μm). We adopted the same analytical conditions and the same mobile phase as performed in our previous works on glioma and medulloblastoma human brain tumors,64,65 which are described in this associated data repository.

Data-MS Analysis and Protein Identification

Based on the species of our investigated samples, we have applied the same MaxQuant research engine and the same setting parameters, as performed in our previous works on glioma and medulloblastoma human brain tumors64,65 and supplied in the associated data repository.

Proteins containing at least one identified peptide were detected at a high confidence interval (95%). A threshold of a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤0.05 was applied to compare the proteome profiles between the two groups and judge the significance of the differences in protein expression maps. Only proteins identified three times were taken into account.

Based on log 2 FC criteria and paired t test with a P value threshold of 0.05, the proteins were filtered into two groups: “up-regulation” with positive values and “down-regulation” with negative values.

Screening of Protein-Adduct Formation by De Novo Sequencing

Protein adductomics was performed by the same strategy adopted in our previously published work on medulloblastoma human brain tumors.65

The procedure of manual verification was applied for the validation of adduct compounds based on their added mass shift (ΔM).

The full workflow schematic illustration of our adaptive strategy for protein adductomics and/or PTMs is shown in Figure 2.

Schematic illustration of the workflow by an untargeted bottom-up proteomic-approach-based MS analysis for the identification of protein-PTMs. DDA, data-dependent acquisition mode; HCD, higher energy collisional dissociation; MS1/MS2, tandem mass spectrometry; FDR, false discovery rate; FC, fold change.

Bioinformatic Analysis

As performed in our previous study on glioma brain tumors,64 we adopted the same databases for the analysis of our differentially expressed proteins, namely, PANTHER (version 17.0) for the gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis,67 METASCAPE68,69 for pathway enrichment analysis, and STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) database (version 11.0)70 for the identification of the most encoding hub genes by Protein–protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis.

Other Statistical Analyses

Data by GraphPad Prism Software (Version.8, San Diego, CA, USA) were expressed as mean values with ± standard deviation (SD), and the statistical significance of differences between counts was determined by the unpaired Student’s t test. Three duplicates were conducted for each distinct experiment. P values ≤0.05 were statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Selected Patients

MNGs derived from arachnoid cells were the most common intracranial tumor for our patients hospitalized in the neurosurgery department of Habib Bourguiba Medium Hospital. Although, grade I represents the most frequent type of MNGs, we have tried in our analysis to conduct a homogenate study by selecting all histopathological grades based on the WHO classification: grade I (benign), II (atypical), and III (malignant and the most aggressive forms associated with the highest risk of recurrence and shortest survival times).

Thus, all patients who participated in this study were exposed to image examinations, such as magnetic resonance images pre- and postsurgery for the identification and evaluation of tumor recurrence.

Nine freshly resected human specimens were collected from patients diagnosed with MNGs grade I, II, and III. Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

For the semiadherent culture type, the nonenzymatic protocol was adopted, and the dissociated cells derived from freshly human surgical specimens were grown as colonies. After 3-day incubation, primary homogeneous tumor cells appeared in a fusiform shape and were arranged in multidirectional cultural bundles. Then, growth factors were added to the medium. Two days later, these primary cells gave rise to primary sphere formation (Figure 5A, left panels).

Additionally, we carefully dissociated these primary cells into single cells to test their ability of self-renewal. Five days later, we saw the formation of secondary subspheres (Figure 5A, middle panels), confirming the self-renewal capability of MNG stemlike cells. Following 5 other days of culture, daughter MNG adherent cells could be developed (Figure 5A, right panels). Similarly, the enzymatic cell dissociation protocol produced spheroids more swiftly, resulting in the development of a heterogeneous population of proliferating cells within 10 days.

These two procedures draw our attention to the fact that unlike neurospheres that were isolated straight from the fresh tumor, the primary tumor sphere culture could generate neurospheres with a more robust and developed morphology, (Figure 5B). Thus, we applied 3D spheroids isolated from nonenzymatically dissociated cells for our subsequent experiments.

Neurospheres’ growth and changes were assessed for several days with regard to modification in shape and size evolution. Spheroid images were imported into Image-Pro-Plus Analyzer software and manually manipulated for data analysis. The curve illustrated in the supplementary Figure S1 shows the spheroid volume growth kinetics (in μm3) per day. Spheroids grew to diameters of 250–350 μm on day 7. The calculated CV (<20%) proved the reproducibility of the spheroid size over three different batches of plates.

By a meticulous analysis of stemness characteristics, the developed spheroids have shown positivity expression for nestin, VIM, GFAP, and βIII-tubulin but lack CD133+ and SOX2 genes. Our results were also confirmed by the proteomic analysis (described in supplementary Table S2).

Proteomic Analysis of Differentially Expressed Proteins in MNGs

The proteomic analysis revealed a total of 2906 proteins expressed in MNG brain tumors, which was reduced to 1292 by pesticide treatment. The volcano plot presented in Figure 9 showed 319 differentially expressed proteins at up-regulation, 791 differentially expressed proteins at down-regulation, and 182 not differently expressed proteins. Following mapping to their enriched GO, all of these differentially expressed proteins were classified into three different groups, namely, biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) as illustrated in Figure 10 and supplementary Figure S3A,B. In terms of MF, the majority of enriched GO terms were involved in binding and catalytic activities. For the BP, genes were found relevant to cellular, metabolic process and biological regulation. For the CC, most proteins were distributed in the cellular part. For the class of proteins, the majority were related to metabolite interconversion enzymes (presented also in the Supplementary Figure S3A,B). The biological significance of enriched pathways for the annotated genes involved in MNGs was also identified. The up-regulated genes were mainly involved in the TCA cycle, redox mitochondrial metabolic process, and cytoskeleton structure (shown inSupplementary Figure S4A). For the down-regulated genes, the most abundant pathways include inflammatory mediation, cellular response to stress, cytoskeleton structure, and neurogenesis (shown in Supplementary Figure S4B).

Measurement of Glycolytic Activity

We examined the biochemical changes in terms of the glycolytic activity for three enzymes, namely, GAPDH, α-enolase (ENO1), and phosphoglycerate-kinase-1 (PGK1), in order to validate and make evidence of the cytotoxicity effect of pesticides to induce metabolic perturbation.

The adopted commercial activity assay kit provides simple and sensitive colorimetric methods for monitoring enzymes’ glycolytic activity in various samples. In this assay, GAPDH catalyzes the conversion of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 1,3-bisphosphate glycerate and an intermediate, which reacts with a developer to form a colored product that absorbs maximally at 450 nm. The enolase activity is determined following the consumption of NADH in an assay buffer that includes pyruvate kinase, lactate dehydrogenase, and required substrates. The consumption of NADH was monitored as a decrease in absorbance at 340 nm. For the PGK1 activity, in the first step of the enzymatic assay, PGK converts 3-phosphoglycerate and ATP to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and ADP. The nascent intermediate was detected via a series of enzymatic reactions that lead to the oxidation of NADH to NAD+, which can be easily detected.

Our obtained results as presented in Figure 11 revealed a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in the glycolytic activity of each selected enzyme between treated and nontreated samples, which make evidence of the neurotoxicity effect of pesticides to induce metabolic perturbation.

Estimation of enzyme glycolytic activity in the treated spheroids after 24 h of pesticide exposure. Assays for each enzyme were performed following the kit protocol. Enzyme activity is expressed as U/mg of total protein in the sample. Data are means ± S.D., n = 3, ***, p < 0.001.

Discussion

Isolation of Tumor Stemlike Cells from Human Meningiomas and Characterization of Sphere Formation and Stemness Markers

Cancer stemlike cells, often referred as tumor-initiating cells,71−80 are issued from cultured human brain tumor that could proliferate and generate clusters of multipotent floating cells called neurospheres.80 These developed 3D spheroids constitute an advanced and exciting in vitro model for cytotoxicity studies and drug discovery since they yield more biologically meaningful data than the traditional 2D monolayer cultures. In fact, spheroids contain a mixture of neuronal, astrocytes, and endothelia cells, which set up the vascular network structures and mimic the brain tissue.80

In our study, we have begun setting up primary cell cultures by using a serum-free neurosphere medium in order to maintain the same properties as the primary tumor and avoiding spontaneous cell differentiation.66,81 Then, we developed 3D stem-cell-derived neuronal spheroids (primary spheroids) to yield finally to secondary neurospheres or subspheres.

The cytotoxicity studies were achieved after 7 days of neurospheroids’ growth once they reached size around 250–350 μm of diameter, which is reported as the optimal size than 500 μm to enable drug perfusion and avoid necrotic centers and hypoxic regions.78,79 Furthermore, as performed in our previous studies,64,65 since the spheroids’ volume and shape are among the critical parameters that influence data variability, we accurately measured the CV of spheroids’ volume on day 7 by using the Image-Pro Analyzer software as an improved substituted method to cytometers79 in order to select homogeneous spheroids, minimizing difference in cells’ responses to chemicals, and hence to acquire reliable results.

Tumor stemlike cells are characterized by their spherical shape, unlimited proliferation, or self-renewal to secondary neurospheres, as well as the expression patterns of stemlike markers specific to their parent tumor.82 Hence, we have elected to examine their expression of CD133, Nestin, VIM, SOX2, GFAP, and βIII-tubulin.

Interestingly, CD133 is the most commonly used marker for cancer stemlike cells that is related to cell proliferation and differentiation of various malignancies.83,84 Nestin, a type VI intermediate filament, is an important cancer stem cells marker for central nervous system (CNS) tumor growth, migration, and invasion,85,86 detected at higher levels in grades II and III MNGs compared to grade I.87 The transcription factor SOX2 has been observed in several CNS tumors cancer stem cells.84,86,88 VIM is highly associated with MNGs. It is a class III intermediate protein expressed in mesenchymal cells and involved in neuritogenesis and the cytoskeleton structure.86,89 GFAP is a class III intermediate filament and a cell-specific marker that, during the development of the CNS, distinguishes astrocytes from other glial cells.90 βIII-Tubulin is a neuron-specific microtubule required for neuronal axon guidance, maintenance, and development,91 which is considered as a neuronal differentiated cell marker.

The obtained results for our developed MNG spheroids are similar to some studies reported in the literature,92,93 showing that the aggressive forms of MNGs are characterized by higher positivity levels of Nestin, CD133, and Sox2 expression.83,86

Effects of Pesticide Mixture on Tumor Stemlike Cells Proliferation and Viability

Histological staining performed at multiple time points on days 0, 4, and 10 before treatment (Figure 5B) enabled target validation through inspecting tumor spheroid viability and growth. This protocol was also appropriate for evaluating the cytotoxic response following chemical treatment (Figure 5B).

Based on our data analysis process, pesticide mixtures may interact to prompt oxidative stress response and mitigate neural cell density proliferation, leading to the gradual emergence of apoptotic and necrotic cells. This result, thus, may confirm prior studies, proving the effect of a pyrethroid on pesticide mixtures to cause increased toxicity in shorter amounts of time.37,94 Moreover, these pesticides applied in ternary and quaternary mixtures95 have shown notable synergistic reactions.

Neurotoxicity response was detected at greater doses than the average daily intake reported for IMD and λ-CYH.96,97 According to the repeated dose toxicity studies reviewed by JMPR (Joint FAO/WHO Meeting on Pesticide Residues; 2002), two NOAELs (no observed effect levels) were notably established for the risk assessment of IMD98 and λ-CYH on the basis of neurotoxic signs.99 Therefore, we can conclude that the selected highly neurotoxic doses of pesticides make unlikely any detrimental impact on human health in regulatory environmental exposures.

Proteomic Analysis

Assessment of Pesticides-Induced Alteration on Key Protein Targets in the Metabolic Process

The proteomic analysis revealed that most of the identified proteins were involved in the MNG cancer process, exhibiting critical roles in biological homeostasis and cell metabolism, especially related to binding and catalytic activities.

According to functional networks and enrichment analysis, most signaling pathways implicated in the mechanism of cell apoptosis were predominantly associated with mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox homeostasis, protein folding and degradation, cytoskeleton structure and neurogenesis, signal transduction, wound healing, blood coagulation and hemostasis, and inflammatory mediation by chemokine and cytokine signaling pathways.

Our results are similar to our previous study on human high-grade gliomas for the main identified altered signaling pathways involved in neuronal apoptosis as well as the significance of protein GO enrichment analysis in terms of BP and MF.64

From literature review, one of the major effects of pesticide-induced toxicity is the metabolic perturbation by inactivation of glycolytic enzymes, fall in the cellular ATP level, and therefore the rate at which oxygen is consumed, which may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis.59 The most commonly described proteins involved in the metabolic oxidative stress response are enolases (ENO1, ENO2), GAPDH, aldolase (ALDOC), and PGK1 associated with glycolysis, aconitases (ACO1, ACO2) related to TCA (Krebs) cycle, and maleate dehydrogenase (MDH2) involved in both glycolysis and TCA cycle.56 In our case study, many key regulatory proteins involved in energy metabolism were found altered in their expression profile by down-regulation, namely, aldehyde dehydrogenase family (ALDH1A1; ALDH2; ALDH1B1.), glycogen phosphorylase (PYGB), enolases (ENO1, ENO2), aconitases (ACO1, ACO2), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and aldolases (ALDOC).

To further scrutinize molecular changes induced by pesticide treatement with the predicted alteration sites, we have focused our analysis on three key target proteins, namely, GAPDH, ENO, and PGK1, selected on the basis of PPI network analysis which allowed investigating all classes of expressed proteins as well as their degree of relationships, which helped to highlight the significance of each protein/gene with its meaningful relationship and regulation.

Following literature, GAPDH enzymes are a family of largely expressed oxido-reductases expressed in all prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms, known for their key role in glucose metabolism, catalyzing the phosphorylation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) to 1,3-bisphospho-glycerate, and as a multifunctional protein involved in many cellular processes such as membrane trafficking, microtubule formation as a catalyst, cytoskeleton organization and assembly, nuclear processes of ribosome interaction, RNA export, DNA repair and replication, transcription regulation, phosphotransferase activity, and response to ROS such as a trigger of neuronal apoptosis.59 ENOA is a metabolic enzyme, which catalyzes the interconversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate during glycolysis. It is involved in cell morphology and proliferation by regulation of the cytoskeletal filament100 and gene transcription101 and also known to act as a plasminogen receptor and a heat shock protein, implicated in many transcriptional and pathological functions, the alterations of which have been demonstrated in many neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease.102−104 However, its overexpression was found in more than 20 types of human cancer105 explained by the Warburg effect.106 PGK1 is a glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of 1,3-diphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate. Deficiency of this protein is also described in many neurological diseases107 in humans, whereas its overexpression is involved in cancer cell invasiveness.

Analytical Validation of the Identified Protein-Trityl Post-Translational Modification

PTMs are involved not only in most signaling cellular processes but also in cellular recognition events and morphology changes that they symbolize changes in the polypeptide chain as a result of either the addition or removal of chemical moieties from amino acid residues, the proteolytic processing of the protein termini, or the covalent cross-links between protein domains.

The research of PTMs and/or xenebiotic adducts has received widespread attention for progress in their analysis as putative predictors of toxicity since they exhibit tremendous potential in assessing the relationship between exposure to environmental pollutants and human health risks. In fact, the high-throughput screening and quantification of these modifications can help clarify the toxicity and pathogenic mechanisms as well as their roles in human pathogenesis which enable the early diagnosis of related diseases.108

However, their analysis is challenging which highlights the need for computational and sensitive methods for their detection.61,109

Several research studies in the literature have identified sites and type of modifications induced by oxidative stress in the GAPDH protein, e.g., identification of Tyr-255–adduct induced by organophosphate Dichlorvos treatment alters the GAPDH function, molecular structure, and trafficking between the nucleus and cytoplasm, which consequently leads to accumulation of the altered protein in the nucleus and thus to apoptosis. Various cysteine modifications were also described, including S-nitrosilation by nitric oxide, nitroalkylation by nitroalkene derivatives, thiol oxidation, sulfenic acid formation, and glutathionylation in response to various oxidative stresses. Oxidized GAPDH on its active site cysteine residue showed an enhanced binding activity to nucleic acids.59 Nevertheless, there are only few reports on the use of proteomic approaches for the identification of environmental pollutant-inducing PTM alteration in PGK1 and ENO proteins.

Thus, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first conducted research in “Protein Adductomics” concept which is based on the “Exposome”110,111 concept which has emerged as a new science field that reflects environmental exposures and extensively identifies pesticide molecular integration and their mechanistic role to induce neurotoxicity.

Based on traditional methods, PTMs had been identified by Edman degradation, amino acid analysis, immunochemistry, and gel-based proteomics approaches.109 Recently, several analytical methods have been deeply developed in various biological matrices such as DNA damage sequencing, chemical isotope labeling, and MS imaging.108,112 MS represents a powerful tool for the high-throughput screening of new prognostic biomarkers. It has been steadily emerged in “omics” studies to qualitatively and quantitatively characterize PTMs with several advantages, namely, (i) good sensitivity and high efficiency, (ii) identification of the predicted sites of PTMs, (iii) discovery of novel PTMs, and iv) capability to identify multiple PTMs in complex mixtures of proteins, which no other analytical technique could provide.113

In fact, the recent examples of MS-based strategies for PTM mapping include the introduction of robust high-performance hybrid tandem mass spectrometery (MS/MS) which provide very high mass accuracy (mass error <5 ppm) and multistage MS capabilities at a chromatographic time scale, thereby contributing to detailed insights of post-translationally modified amino acid residues via peptide sequencing. Moreover, the multistage MS and efficient fragmentation methods have also facilitated the analysis of large polypeptides.114

Moreover, Orpitrap has gained widespread use and played a prominent role in proteomics and in the PTM analysis due to its high resolution and accurate MS data.115 The Q-Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer is a newly developed technology that provides new levels of speed and resolution for every scan while maintaining high spectral quality for sensitive detection and confident quantitation of peptides. This type of mass spectrometer is amenable to untargeted and targeted proteomics, lipidomics, metabolomics, drug discovery, pharmacokinetic analysis, clinical research, and forensic toxicology.116 Consequently, there is no doubt that the MS-based analysis of protein-PTMs and/or xenobiotic adducts is very exciting from which it is now possible to to ascertain PTM sites’ stoichiometry or the absolute occupancy on a broad scale.117,118

Following the detection of protein adducts by the bottom-up approach, identifying the most mechanistically important adducts is a challenging task. In fact, hypotheses on adduct identities were set based only on their molecular weights and retention times.110,119

The process of ″de novo peptide sequencing″, or manual identification requires more time analysis than the newly developed bioinformatics research engines for protein-PTMs and data analysis, e.g., UniNovo, that is reported as the best tool to facilitate the manual validation of Orbitrap MS data.120

Despite the fact that proteomics analysis has been simplified and accelerated in view of MS potential pitfalls by powerful algorithms of protein sequences’ research databases, developed robust workflows via targeted or semitargeted scanning procedures, and improved enrichment strategies with chemical labels, manual interpretation is rather truthful and always needed for validation. This helps prevent false positive data and gather accurate details on the position and specificity of modified amino acid within peptides such as the identification of erroneously assigned oxidation to proline using a probability-based research engine, revealed by Verrastro et al.121 For this reason, our study focused on the identification of protein adducts manually.

From an analytical perspective, validation of the suggested elemental compositions for unknown adducts could be based not only on their physicochemical properties but also upon the microenvironment (steric factors) around the nucleophile, affecting the approach of the reactive electrophilic sites to make covalent bonds.119

In our adaptive strategy, the reliability of the adduct masses depends not only on the use of high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry but also on the database search engine performance which is the “MaxQuant” software, a well-known platform for LC-MS/MS shotgun proteomics that uses sophisticated nonlinear recalibration algorithms to achieve peptide mass accuracies at high sequencing coverage.122

After the elemental composition was proposed, the identity of trityl-adduct formation was validated by a colorimetric assay method. Although the previously described methods for trityl ethers either gravimetrically by precipitation as trityl chloride or triphenyl-carbinol or by ultraviolet absorption have shown deficiencies and limitations of use, such as lack of sensitivity and the loss of materials in each assay of the former method or absorbance of several functional groups in the ultraviolet region,123 we have searched from the literature review for a very simple and highly sensitive assay method for the determination of the triphenyl-methyl (trityl) group and confirm the identity of our adduct. It was reported that samples containing trityl ether or N-trityl-amino group are soluble in organic solvents, and their analysis depends on acid treatment with subsequent colorimetric determination of the yellow-produced coloration that could be measured at any time. Concentrations ranging from 1 to 35 pg (0.004 to 0.14 mmol) are sufficient, which is our case study in using pesticides at the CI50 to yield, following the described protocol,123 to a yellow color measured at 420 nm that proves the trityl-adduct formation.

Overall, in our case study, the colorimetric methods have only proved the neurtotoxocity effect of pesticides to induce metabolic perturbation in the glycolytic activity, which was further confirmed by the proteomic analysis adapting the recent development of MS high-throughput technology for the characterization of protein-PTMs and the prediction of alteration sites with high sensitivity and accuracy.

Hence, structural modifications have been identified in the active site of GAPDH, PGK1, and ENO1 by de novo peptide sequencing such as ion losses and adduct formation, particularly a mass shift of (+242.0 Da) on the C-terminal of 206-Ile residue assigned as a trityl-PTM and confirmed by manual validation with a colorimetric assay method.

Elucidation of the Neurotoxicity Mechanism Induced by Pesticide Treatment

In humans and animals, apoptosis or programmed cell death can be triggered by a number of environmental contaminants.124−126 Xenobiotic’s toxicity is mostly determined based on some defined physicochemical characteristics, which predict its potential to interact and penetrate cell membranes. According to literature review, our selected pesticide molecules have the power to target brain tissues since they exhibit lipophilic characteristics with low molecular weight which are able to pass through the blood brain barrier.127

Although cells have evolved antioxidant defenses protecting againt stress factors, proteins could be altered and modified by several ways, either by reaction of reducing sugars or their oxidation products or via direct attack by oxygen reactive species or by backbone oxidative cleavage or by end-products of lipid peroxidation.127−129 Besides, a number of interrelated mechanisms can initiate oxidative stress such as increase of intracellular free Ca2+ as well as release of excitatory amino acids.57,130

Direct and indirect interactions of proteins with ROS or reactive nitrogen species can result in not only a massive number of PTMs, such as oxidation, phosphorylation, glycosylation, acetylation, methylation, sumoylation, and ubiquitination, but also both reversible or irreversible modifications in their primary structure, biological functions, cellular location, DNA binding, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, or dynamic interactions with other proteins.131 Some modifications, like carbonylation, nitration, and protein–protein cross-linking, are mostly related to functional loss leading to unfolding and degradation of damaged proteins or aggregation and accumulation as cytoplasmic inclusions associated with age-related neurodegenerative troubles132−134 or metabolic disorders like type-2 diabetes owing to the long-term chronic effects of protein modifications.135

Following our discovery, we present here for the first time pesticides’ molecular interactions on a human neurospheroid model by adapting a proteomic-based approach. Our results revealed that the neurotoxicity effect was strongly related to xenebiotic-adduct formation, which constitute potential biomarkers of the toxicity mechanism.

Typically, protein adducts are steady modifications issued by covalent link between the protein nucleophilic site and the electrophilic form of chemically reactive drugs’ metabolites issued in vivo from either endogenous sources (such as oxidative stress) or exogenous (such as food) ones. Nucleophilic atoms (mainly O, N, and S) can be targeted by these reactive species, including reactive functional groups, at the side chains of amino acid residues.

In our case study, as illustrated in Figure 15 for the suggested molecular mechanism, the added mass shift (+242.0 Da) predicted as triphenyl-methyl compound acts like a Michael acceptor to covalently bind with nucleophilic moiety on the C-terminal of 206-Ile (Isoleucine) residue in the active site of GAPDH. Hence, proteins post-translationally modified accumulate and reach a sufficient level for pathway failure, ultimately causing cell damage and neurotoxicity.

Predicted mechanistic molecular interaction between λ-CYH and IMD to form a triphenyl-methyl adduct which reacts as Michael acceptor to covalently bind with the nucleophilic moiety on the C-terminal of the 206-Ile (isoleucine) residue. The trityl-adduct could pursue metabolic activation via P450 oxidation and then rearrangement into active-oxidized metabolites as quinone compounds.

Furthemere, the triphenyl-methyl or trityl (Trt) adduct could pursue metabolic activation via P450 oxidation and then rearrangement into active-oxidized metabolites as quinone compounds (presented in Figure 15) that are able to form DNA adducts and/or affect DNA topoisomerases, which are enzymes implicated in disentangling the topological issues from double-stranded DNA. This circumstance leads consequently to DNA damage.126 Indeed, a recent study conducted by Sule, RO (2022) reported the causative role of pyrethroids to induce genotoxicity via production of semiquinones and quinones.47

Based on the literature review focusing on each molecule target to induce neurotoxicity, pyrethroids possess the capability to hinder mammalian voltage-gated channels,136,137 as well as to negatively affect hormone receptors, particularly γ-aminobutyric acid and glutamate receptors,138,139 and drug transporters that are key players for drug detoxification, including ATP-binding cassette and solute carrier transporters, which were reported to be inhibited by both allethrin and tetramethrin.94

Nitric oxide, induced by pyrethroids, interacts with the superoxide radical to produce very reactive peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which interacts with guanine leading to DNA damage such as as 8-NO2-Gua (nitrative lesion) and 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) (oxidative lesion).94 Interestingly, CYH was shown to interact with nucleotide and nucleoside-converting enzymes as modulators of purinergic signaling system.140

Pyrethroids are metabolized by oxidation mediated by cytochrome enzymes. CYP2E1 that acts against environmental chemicals and plays a crucial role in neuronal detoxification was reported at overexpression in the brain tissue after deltamethrin treatment, which could increase the risk of neurotoxicity.141

For the neonicotinoids, IMD exhibits the same toxicological mechanism as the tobacco toxin nicotine that is linked to the alteration of neurotransmission in the nicotinic cholinergic nervous system. IMD binds to the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuronal and neuromuscular junctions due to its hydrophobic character. This causes prolonged stimulation, desensitization, receptor blockage, and ultimately cell death.21,142

Overall, we have attempted in our study to identify a novel neurotoxicity mechanism with the predicted alteration sites induced by a pesticide mixture by examining their potential to adduct proteins. The resulted neurotoxicity effect could be assigned to the additional cytotoxicity effect of each molecule target and/or to their molecular interaction to yield to a trytl-adduct formation as protein-PTM. This type of modification plays a crucial role in the neurotoxicity mechanism since it impairs proteins’ 3D structure, consequently affects their physiological function mainly for those endowed with a crucial role such as our case study.

Conclusions

Pesticide-induced toxicity in both in vitro and in vivo models were reported in many published works seeking to ascertain their levels of safety for general population and occupation hazard. However, few studies reported the effect of pesticides in mixtures, especially on neural and glial cells.

Hence, as far as we know, this is the first finding combining the use of a human 3D-neuronal spheroid array with an untargeted bottom-up proteomic-based approach in order to elucidate in-depth the molecular mechanisms involved in the neurotoxicity effect of pesticides used in the mixture and to give insights on alteration sites.

Advancment in proteomics-based approaches with high-throughput MS technology has helped to investigate in-depth xenobiotic-adduct formation or protein-PTMs as potential diagnostic biomarkers that may aid in understanding the pathophysiologic molecular mechanisms and better plan therapeutic strategies with minimum side effects.

In our study, the proteomic analysis has proved the neurotoxicity effect of pesticides to induce alteration on sevral proteins involved in cascading regulatory pathways, mainly on the mitochondrial metabolic process, which was confirmed by colorimetric methods for the glycolytic activity. Furthermore, our adaptative strategy on protein adductomics by the use of a shotgun proteomic-based approach has enabled the characterization of protein-PTMs and prediction of alteration sites with high sensitivity and accuracy. Hence, several modifications have been identified in the active site of GAPDH, PGK1, and ENO1 by de novo peptide sequencing such as ion losses and adduct formation, particularly a mass shift of (+242.0 Da) on the C-terminal of 206-Ile residue assigned as a trityl-PTM and confirmed by manual validation and a colorimetric assay method.

Thus, our finding provides the first proof that the neurotoxicity effect of IMD and CYH used in mixture was linked to their molecular interaction to yield protein-PTMs that play a pivotal role in the neurotoxicity mechanism since they impair the molecular structure, which in turn affects protein function particularly for those endowed with a crucial biological role such as our case study of GAPDH, ENO, and PGK1. The study also offers a broad perspective on how pyrethroids might significantly increase the harmful effects of pesticides used in combination.

Finally, given the widespread use of pesticides and our investigation on human health hazardous effects, we support our public health authorities to be vigilant in the postmarket surveillance of these agrochemicals and for taking preventive measures with their regular use without polluting the environment and contaminating food in order to decrease human exposure risks and provide safer life.
