Impact of SARS-CoV-2 Breakthrough Infection on Short-Term CD4 Count Among Adults With HIV in the United States From 2020 to 2022
Authors: Lincoln Hopkins, Elizabeth Humes, Kirsha S Gordon, Vincent C Marconi, Raynell Lang, Michael A Horberg, Sonia Napravnik, Michael J Silverberg, Lindsay E Browne, Lesley S Park, Kelly A Gebo, Deana M Agil, Keri N Althoff, Sally B Coburn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCD4 T cells are critical for reducing disease severity and viral clearance of SARS-CoV-2, which is particularly important among populations that are immune compromised, including people with HIV (PWH). While vaccines are effective, risk and severity of COVID-19 breakthrough infections are higher in people with vs without HIV. Little is known about the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on CD4 counts among PWH after a breakthrough infection.MethodsWe evaluated the impact of COVID-19 breakthrough infection on immediate (0–60 days) and short-term (61–180 days) CD4 count from 2020 to 2022 among PWH vaccinated for COVID-19 in the CIVETs-II study (Corona-Infectious-Virus Epidemiology Team). We utilized paired t tests to test for statistically significant changes in CD4 count before and after breakthrough infection and linear mixed effect models to quantify changes in CD4 count following SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection.ResultsAmong 152 PWH, 37% were White and 40% were ≥55 years of age. Median CD4 count prebreakthrough was 614 cells/mm3, while median postbreakthrough CD4 count was 612 cells/mm3. Most patients had an undetectable HIV viral load (62%). Paired t tests indicated no change in CD4 count prior to breakthrough infection as compared with CD4 count 0 to 60 days (−40 cells/mm3; 95% CI, −86 to 5), or ≥61 days (25 cells/mm3; 95% CI, −11 to 61). Longitudinal trajectories indicated CD4 count rebound ≥61 days following breakthrough infection (112-cell/mm3 increase per 60-day increase, P < .05).ConclusionsWhile changes in CD4 counts were observed after breakthrough infection, these results are likely not clinically significant, suggesting that among PWH vaccinated for COVD-19, CD4 count is not substantially affected by breakthrough infections.Keywords: breakthrough, CD4, COVID-19, HIV, vaccine

CD4+ T cells are a crucial part of our immune system to combat disease, including COVID-19 [1]. Epidemiologic studies have shown that CD4+ T cells are integral for viral clearance of SARS-CoV-2, even more so than other immune cells, such as CD8+ T cells [2, 3]. Beyond viral clearance, CD4 counts are strongly associated with disease severity among people who become infected with SARS-CoV-2 and may even be used to predict the severity of disease [4, 5]. SARS-CoV-2 infection may also cause lymphopenia, and this may largely be attributed to CD4 T cells [6]. A decline in CD4 count following COVID-19 infection is particularly concerning for people with HIV (PWH), who are already experiencing varying levels of immune compromise. Prior evidence has shown that even with antiretroviral therapy, PWH can have residual immune dysfunction despite having recovered CD4 counts and viral suppression [7]. Two studies that assessed the interaction of HIV and SARS-CoV-2 coinfections noted lower CD4 counts in cases of coinfection [8, 9].

The burden of disease caused by SARS-CoV-2 was drastically reduced due to vaccines, to the extent that they are projected to have prevented 2.5 million deaths [10]. While vaccines still are incredibly effective at protecting from severe COVID-19, breakthrough infections can still occur [11, 12]. Waning antibody levels after about 6 months postvaccination and the rise of new variants of concern further reduce vaccine efficacy [13, 14]. Recent studies have also indicated that PWH are at higher risk of breakthrough infection, especially if they are immunocompromised as defined by a low CD4 count, and they may not have as robust of an immune response to COVID-19 vaccines [15, 16].

Despite the importance of maintaining a high CD4 count among PWH, little has been studied on whether breakthrough infections of SARS-CoV-2 are associated with changes in CD4 count among this population. The objective of this study was to assess whether and to what extent SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infections were associated with transient or sustained changes in CD4 counts among PWH from initial breakthrough up to 6 months after COVID-19 infection.

METHODS

Study Population

We conducted a secondary analysis using data from the Corona-Infectious-Virus Epidemiology Team (CIVETs-II), a part of the North American AIDS Cohort Collaboration on Research and Design. CIVETs-II consists of data from 2020 to 2022 from 4 health centers: Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States (Maryland, District of Columbia, and northern Virginia), Kaiser Permanente Northern California, University of North Carolina Chapel Hill HIV Clinic, and the Veterans Aging Cohort Study [15, 17]. Cohorts received approval from their local institutional review boards, including waivers and/or exemptions of consent when necessary. Overall approval was obtained from the institutional review board of the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

We included PWH diagnosed with COVID-19 after being fully vaccinated (ie, had a SARS-COV-2 breakthrough infection). PWH were identified by HIV registries or ICD-10 diagnosis codes for HIV [18]. Full vaccination was reached 2 weeks after receiving 2 doses of BNT162 or mRNA-1273 (the Pfizer and Moderna vaccines, respectively) or 2 weeks after receiving 1 dose of Ad26.COV2.S (the Johnson & Johnson vaccine) [19–21]. Patients had to be fully vaccinated by June 2021 for inclusion in the study. For inclusion in this nested study, patients needed to be >18 years of age and have at least 1 CD4 count measured at a minimum 30 days before the breakthrough infection (median, 127 days; IQR, 84–235) and at least 1 CD4 count within 6 months after breakthrough infection.

Exposure: Breakthrough Infection

Breakthrough infection was confirmed by laboratory diagnosis through SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid amplification assays, antigen tests, or ICD-10 codes using the same methods as prior CIVETs-II analyses [15, 22]. If a participant had multiple diagnoses for breakthrough infections, we utilized the first occurrence following full vaccination.

Outcome and Other Covariates

Our outcome of interest was absolute change in CD4 count (cells/mm3) before and after a breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection. History of COVID-19 infection before full vaccination (yes/no) and vaccine type were also evaluated. We included factors such as period (1 January 2020–31 March 2021 for pre-Alpha variants; 1 April–31 May 2021 for Alpha; 1 June–30 November 2021 for Delta; 1 December 2021–28 February 2022 for Omicron) [23] to account for secular changes in the dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant, as well as demographic factors (sex, age group, race and ethnicity, and health center). We evaluated days from full vaccination to breakthrough infection and whether patients received an additional vaccine beyond the primary series.

HIV viral load was dichotomized as detectable vs undetectable. PWH were classified as having an undetectable viral load if their viral load measurement was less than or equal to the lower limit of quantification for the given assay (range, 19–47 copies/mL). The viral load measurement closest to breakthrough infection within a window of 6 months prior to and 3 months after was utilized to measure this covariate. If patients did not have a viral load measured within this period, they were classified as “unknown.”

Statistical Analysis

First, we characterized the distribution of clinical and demographics factors among our study population. Then, we evaluated longitudinal CD4 count among our study patients from the earliest time point recorded (20 months prior to breakthrough) up to 180 days postbreakthrough infection of SARS-CoV-2. CD4 measurements in the 30 days preceding breakthrough were excluded because they could be part of the causal pathway from COVID-19 breakthrough infection to diagnosis.

We fit a linear mixed effects model to evaluate longitudinal change in CD4 count from at least 1 month prior to breakthrough infection up to 6 months after breakthrough infection scaled per 60-day increase. Adjustment factors included history of COVID-19 infection, vaccine type, period, sex, age, race and ethnicity, health center, receipt of additional vaccine doses beyond the primary series, and days since full vaccination as described in the Outcome and Other Covariates section.

To assess the immediate and short-term effects, we added a piecewise linear spline with knots at 0 and 61 days postbreakthrough infection to evaluate change in CD4 prior to breakthrough infection, 0 to 60 days following breakthrough infection, and ≥61 days following breakthrough infection. As a secondary analysis, we dichotomized time as prior to vs after breakthrough infection.

We then conducted 2 pre- and posttest analyses: 1 for immediate and 1 for short-term change in CD4 count following breakthrough infection. The immediate postbreakthrough measurements were taken 0 to 60 days after breakthrough, and the short-term postbreakthrough measurements were taken after 61 days up to 180 days. Differences were assessed by paired t tests. If there were multiple prebreakthrough measurements, we selected the value closest to the 30-day cutoff. If there were multiple postbreakthrough measurements within a single pre- and posttest analysis, we selected the measurement furthest away from the initial breakthrough date.

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate how robust our primary analysis was to evaluate (1) the removal of CD4 count values on the causal pathway to COVID-19 breakthrough diagnosis and (2) differences in HIV viral load detection status. We estimated longitudinal changes in CD4 count using the linear mixed effects models, as in our main analysis, but ran separate models (1) removing CD4 counts 14 days prior to breakthrough, (2) removing CD4 counts ±7 days from breakthrough, and (3) retaining all CD4 count values. We conducted another sensitivity analysis stratifying our linear mixed effects models by viral detection status at breakthrough infection, among those with viral load values available.

RESULTS

Among 31 298 PWH in the CIVETs-II study with complete data, 1235 had a breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection. An additional 1083 were excluded because they did not have a CD4 count before and following their breakthrough infection. After these exclusions, 152 patients remained (89 contributed to the immediate assessment and 79 to the short-term assessment), with 16 contributing to both assessments (Figure 1). Of the 152 included patients vs the 1085 excluded patients with a breakthrough infection, a larger proportion of Hispanic PWH met the inclusion criteria (24% vs 14%) and received the Ad26.COV2.S vaccine (16% vs 7%). There were fewer Black patients (30% vs 48%) and fewer patients aged ≥65 years (14% vs 25%). A higher percentage of people with an undetectable viral load were included rather than excluded (62% vs 24%) whereas a lower percentage of people with an unknown viral load met our inclusion criteria (25% vs 72%). In total, 602 CD4 counts were recorded: 426 before breakthrough, 92 up to 60 days after breakthrough, and 84 at >60 days after breakthrough (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1).

Patients were predominantly male (88%) and <55 years old at the time of first recorded CD4 count (60%), with no evidence of prior SARS-CoV-2 infection (86%). Most were either non-Hispanic White (37%) or non-Hispanic Black (30%). Most received the BNT162 vaccine for their full vaccination (57%), as their additional dose (50%), or both (45%), with 27% receiving 2 doses of mRNA-1273 for full vaccination and 16% receiving the Ad26.COV2.S vaccine. No patients received the Ad26.COV2.S as a booster and 28% received no additional vaccine. Most patients were also virally undetectable near the time of breakthrough infection (62%), or a measurement was not available (25%). The median CD4 count prior to breakthrough infection was 614 cells/mm3 (IQR, 450–801) and that for the postbreakthrough infection was 612 cells/mm3 (IQR, 434–821).

The linear mixed effects model indicated that CD4 count changed modestly over time following a breakthrough infection. Prior to breakthrough infection, there was nonsignificant increase in CD4 count by 26 cells/mm3 (95% CI, −36 to 89) per 60-day increase. Within 60 days of breakthrough, CD4 cell count declined nonsignificantly by 25 cells/mm3 (95% CI, −110 to 59) at >60 days. More than 60 days following breakthrough infection, the CD4 count significantly increased by 112 cells/mm3 (95% CI, 32–192) per 60-day increase (Table 2). Our within-patient assessment—using a before-and-after breakthrough infection variable in our linear mixed effects model rather than a continuous time-varying variable—yielded a statistically significant increase in CD4 count of 70 cells/mm3 (95% CI, 16–124; Supplementary Figure 1).

Our sensitivity analyses modifying the window for including CD4 counts yielded little difference in results as compared with the main analysis. Our findings among those with an undetectable viral load were consistent with the main analysis. Among those with an undetectable viral load, there was a decline in CD4 count ≥61 days after breakthrough, although this estimate was not statistically significant: (−49 cells/mm3; 95% CI, −417 to 318; Table 2).

DISCUSSION

In a geographically diverse sample of PWH engaged in care in the United States, CD4 count declined in the first 2 months following breakthrough infection and rebounded within the following 3 months. This indicates that the impact of breakthrough infection on CD4 count within 6 months was minimal. PWH and their clinicians should be encouraged by the rebound in CD4 count observed and that this decrease after a breakthrough infection was not significant.

Our findings are congruent with a prior study reporting an insignificant increase in CD4 count within the first 3 months of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the general population (n = 22) [24]; however, other studies have reported conflicting information on changes in lymphocyte counts or ratios [25–27]. A meta-analysis also established a significant decrease following COVID-19 progression in lymphocytes but not specifically with CD4 T cells [28]. This variation likely has limited clinical impact in healthy individuals, as a significant change requires an absolute CD4 count to change by at least 30% [29]. For those with current lymphopenia or lower-than-average cell counts, this variation could have clinical significance if it were to drop below 350 cells/µL with an increased risk of opportunistic illnesses occurring at 200 cells/µL [30]. Given that PWH may be immunologic nonresponders and never have CD4 levels similar to their pre-HIV baseline, this variation may be relevant [31, 32].

In Figure 2, we notice an increase between days 0 and 60 postbreakthrough infection from the LOESS curve in contradiction to our paired t tests and piecewise linear spline. This is due to the LOESS curve giving more weight to data points closer to the calculated curve and is heavily influenced by smoothing parameters. The intention of this curve was for visual aesthetics, while the splines were addressing our biological question. As such, more weight should be given to our linear mixed effects model than to the visuals of the LOESS curve.

Our sensitivity analyses indicated that our model was robust in terms of addressing whether a breakthrough infection affected CD4 counts [33]. Our analysis among those with a detectable viral load at breakthrough infection were indicative of CD4 decline ≥61 days following breakthrough, although our sample size was limited (n = 20). This may be attributed to a lower initial CD4 count and poorer health during the breakthrough infection. Further investigation into the impact of breakthrough infection among those with uncontrolled HIV viral load may be considered.

The study has a few limitations. Foremost, it has a small sample size and is subsequently underpowered to detect significant changes in CD4 count. Accordingly, we were unable to stratify analyses by COVID-19 disease severity, which is associated with CD4 count, and estimates stratified by viral detection status should be interpreted with caution. CD4 counts were collected in the context of clinical care among those seeking medical care. PWH receiving CD4 count testing more than once a year may reflect a subgroup of patients engaged in care but experiencing health issues. Moreover, most patients came from health centers with comprehensive health insurance plans and may receive more consistent care than other populations of PWH. As such, our study population does not reflect those who are not engaged in care or those who are healthy enough to receive minimal testing in the course of their HIV management. While we included the dominant variants at the time in our model [23], specific coronavirus variants were not documented. The dominant variants at the time may not be generalizable to other variants. When the Delta and Omicron variants were the most prevalent, there was an increase in at-home testing. This may affect our results as more diagnoses may have gone unreported [34]. The CIVETs-II project was designed to collect real-time data for the analysis of vaccine effectiveness. Because of this, we lacked information that could affect our results, such as treatments for COVID-19 that affect the immune system (eg, steroids), duration of HIV positivity, and immunosuppressive treatments for other comorbidities.

Overall, CD4 counts did not vary in a clinically meaningful way following a breakthrough infection of SARS-CoV-2 among PWH. While there is a potential for a slight decrease in CD4 counts immediately following infection, this was temporary and appeared to rebound to the prebreakthrough infection CD4 count. Although our findings are reassuring among people with treated HIV, further studies should evaluate the impact of breakthrough infection among PWH with chronic comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [35]. These subgroups increasingly represent the multimorbidity that PWH face, and they may be at heightened risk for poor outcomes following a COVID-19 breakthrough infection.
