Assessing UML Models by ChatGPT: Implications for Education Journal: Elsevier
Chong Wang, Beian Wang, Peng Liang, Jie Liang

Abstract
In software engineering (SE) research and practice, UML is well known as an essential modeling methodology for requirements analysis and software modeling in both academia and industry. In particular, fundamental knowledge of UML modeling and practice in creating high-quality UML models are included in SE-relevant courses in the undergraduate programs of many universities. This leads to a time-consuming and labor-intensive task for educators to review and grade a large number of UML models created by the students. Recent advancements in generative AI techniques, such as ChatGPT, have paved new ways to automate many SE tasks. However, current research or tools seldom explore the capabilities of ChatGPT in evaluating the quality of UML models. This paper aims to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of ChatGPT in assessing the quality of UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. First, 11 evaluation criteria with grading details were proposed for these UML models. Next, a series of experiments were designed and conducted on 40 students’ UML modeling reports to explore the performance of ChatGPT in evaluating and grading these UML diagrams. The research findings reveal that ChatGPT performed well in this assessing task because the scores that ChatGPT gives to the UML models are similar to the ones by human experts, and there are three evaluation discrepancies between ChatGPT and human experts, but varying in different evaluation criteria used in different types of UML models.

Introduction

In software engineering (SE) research and practice, Unified Modeling Language (UML) has been widely used in requirements engineering, software design, and system modeling by providing a set of standard graphic modeling notations

1

For software engineers and developers, well-designed and structured UML models can effectively bridge the gap between business requirements and technical implementation in software analysis and design. To help our students develop their competencies for future SE-related jobs, essential knowledge of UML modeling and practice on how to create high-quality UML models are included in the SE-relevant courses of the undergraduate programs of many universities, such as

Generally, the UML diagrams created by the students are manually reviewed and evaluated by the teachers or teaching assistants to examine whether the students can correctly understand and use UML. However, it is a time-consuming and labor-intensive task for educators.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence techniques, especially the development of generative AI techniques, have paved new ways to automate many tasks in the software development life cycle.
As a typical and widely used type of generative AI model, ChatGPT has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in requirements analysis and modeling

Related Work

The recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have ushered in significant transformation in how we tackle numerous computational tasks, extending their impact from natural language processing to diverse domains. In the domain of SE, LLMs are increasingly proving their potential, particularly in automating routine tasks and enhancing both the precision and speed of software development processes
[
9
]
.

Modeling has been a major task and provides a communication channel in requirements engineering and software design.
In the context of Requirements Engineering (RE), LLMs have been applied to aid in the elicitation, documentation, and verification of software requirements. The ability of these models to understand complex specifications and provide coherent summaries or improvements has been highlighted in various studies. Cámara et al. proposed a conceptual framework to pave the way for standardization of the benchmarking process, ensuring consistent and objective evaluation of LLMs in software modeling
[
10

. Ruan et al. presented an automated framework for requirement model generation that incorporates ChatGPT-based zero-shot learning to extract requirement models from requirement texts and subsequently compose them using predefined rules
[
11

[
12
]
. In
[
13

, LLMs were used to extract problem diagrams from natural language documents to facilitate requirements extraction and modeling for cyber-physical systems. Tabassum et al. investigated the capabilities of OpenAI’s GPT-4 and Google’s Gemini for generating standard and UCM4IoT textual use cases by carrying out a comparative study using four IoT applications
[
14
]
. Chen et al. proposed to create class diagrams using LLMs with two sequential steps, including class generation and relationship generation, and the results show that their proposed approach outperforms the single-prompt-based prompt by improving recall values and F1 scores in most systems for modeling the classes, attributes, and relationships
[
15
]
. Chen et al. conducted a comprehensive comparative study on automated domain modeling with LLMs (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4), and they found that LLMs demonstrate impressive capabilities on domain understanding, but are still impractical for fully automated domain modeling
[
16

. Chaaben et al. evaluated the usefulness of a novel approach utilizing LLMs and few-shot prompt learning to assist in domain modeling, and the results show its usability and effectiveness
[
17

. Netz et al. developed MAGDA, a user-friendly tool, through which they conduct a user study and assess the real-world applicability of our approach in the context of domain modeling, offering valuable insights into its usability and effectiveness
[
18

. For architecture design, Ahmad et al. conducted a preliminary study on using ChatGPT to support a human-bot collaborative software architecting process
[
5

.

Several researchers exploited the use of LLMs for education. Cipriano et al. experimented with three prominent LLMs to solve real-world object-oriented programming (OOP) exercises used in educational settings, subsequently validating their solutions using an Automatic Assessment Tool (AAT) and found that while the models frequently achieved mostly working solutions to the exercises, they often overlooked the best practices of OOP
[
19
]
. In particular, our previous work explored how LLMs help undergraduate students create three types of UML models, i.e., use case models, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams, and synthesized the potential factors affecting their performance
[
4
]
.

However, the application of LLMs specifically in evaluating and scoring software models, such as those represented in the Unified Modeling Language (UML), has not received comparable attention. Ferrari et al. investigated the capability of ChatGPT to generate a specific type of model, i.e., UML sequence diagrams, from NL requirements by conducting a qualitative study in which they examine the sequence diagrams generated by ChatGPT for 28 requirements documents of various types and from different domains
[
20
]
. Nevertheless, the limitations of this paper include a small sample size (only 28 requirement documents) and the subjective nature of manual evaluations, which may introduce bias. Additionally, the study only assessed UML sequence diagrams, leaving the generalizability to other modeling tasks in question. To evaluate the capability of LLM agents in correctly generating UML class diagrams, De Bari et al. collected 20 exercises from a diverse set of web sources and compared the UML class diagrams generated by a human and an LLM solver in terms of syntactic, semantic, pragmatic correctness, and the distance from a provided reference solution. They also suggested that future research should focus on evaluating the ability of LLMs to generate natural language modeling tasks and assess students’ answers
[
21
]
. Our study is a direct implementation of that future direction, focusing on LLMs’ performance in scoring UML class diagrams created by students.

Although the potential benefits of using LLMs to evaluate the quality and completeness of UML models are clear, there is a significant gap in the existing literature that addresses this application. This highlights the need for further exploration of the methods that could take advantage of LLMs to enhance the evaluation process of UML models, especially for the education and training in SE.

3
Research Design

The main research objective of this work is to explore the feasibility and capability of ChatGPT in assessing three types of UML models, as well as their implications for education of UML modeling. For this purpose, we designed and conducted a series of experiments, as detailed in the following subsections.

3.1
Research Data

The research data of this work is UML models created by the students for their course reports. To standardize the assessment process and results, we design and conduct a specified UML modeling task to get the UML models to be graded. In this task, the participants are required to create three types of UML models, i.e., a use case diagram, a class diagram, and a sequence diagram, for the given case study: Order Processing System (see the case study description in [6]).

The participants in this study are undergraduate students majoring in Software Engineering (SE) from the School of Computer Science of Wuhan University. Before participating in this task, they had undergone a three-month training program in their
Requirements Analysis and Modeling
course. During this course, they not only acquired knowledge but also honed their skills in crafting UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams for a designated case study. All participants submitted their project reports including the three UML models they had created at the end of this course.

We initially collected 60 project reports. After a careful review, 20 reports were excluded because the UML models were either hand-drawn or represented in Chinese because these formats are difficult for ChatGPT to understand and analyze in our pilot study. Finally, UML models created by 40 students were selected as the research data of this work. These 120 UML models (

3.3
ChatGPT-based Assessment

In this work, GPT-4o was employed to grade the three types of UML models created by the 40 students. To give ChatGPT enough background knowledge of grading UML models, the prompt is designed to integrate the description of the UML modeling task, the role that ChatGPT would like to play in this task, the suggested answers to this modeling task, and the grading criteria defined in Section
3.2

Prompt for assessing UML models:

Requirements Analysis and Modeling
. I made an assignment on the creation of UML models. Now I need some help in grading these assignments submitted by the students.

The assignment is provided as follows: There is a textual description of a case study, i.e. an Order Processing System, followed by three questions on designing a use case diagram, a class diagram, and a sequence diagram for this case study. Firstly, I will send you the textual description and three questions of this assignment, followed by the reference solutions. Then, the evaluation criteria for grading these UML models will be provided. Finally, I will give you the UML models created by the students, and you need to grade them reasonably based on your knowledge of UML models and the following information I provide.

Here is the problem statement:

Case Study: Order Processing System

Consider the following problem description: A mail-order company wants to automate its order processing. The initial version of the order processing system should be accessible to customers via the web. Customers can also call the company by phone and interact with the system via a customer representative. It is highly likely that the company will enhance this system in upcoming years with new features.

The system allows customers to place orders, check the status of their orders, cancel an existing order, and request a catalog. Customers may also return a product, but this is only possible through the phone, not available on the web. When placing an order, the customer identifies himself either by means of a customer number (for existing registered customers) or by providing his name and address. He then selects a number of products by giving the product number or by selecting products from the online catalog. For each product, information such as price, a description, and a picture (only on demand as they are usually high-resolution images of large size) is presented to the customer. Also, the availability of the product is obtained from the inventory. The customer indicates whether he wants to buy the product and in what quantity. When all desired products have been selected, the customer provides a shipping address and a credit card number and a billing address (if different from the shipping address). Then an overview of the ordered products and the total cost are presented. If the customer approves, the order is submitted. Credit card number, billing address, and a specification of the cost of the order are used on the invoice, which is forwarded to the accounting system (an existing software module). Orders are forwarded to the shipping company, where they are filled and shipped.

Customers who spent over a certain amount within the past year are promoted to be gold customers. Gold customers have additional rights such as being able to return products in an extended time period as well as earning more bonus points with each purchase. In addition, in cases where a product is on back order, gold customers have the option to sign up for an email notification for when the particular product becomes available.

3.4
Human-based Evaluation

To further evaluate the performance of ChatGPT in grading UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams, we conduct a human-based evaluation on the UML models created by the students in Section
3.1
and graded by ChatGPT in Section
3.3
.

First, the first two authors randomly selected four students’ project reports and graded their UML models independently, based on their understanding of the evaluation criteria defined in Section
3.2
. Then, all four authors had a short meeting to resolve the unclear issues and inconsistent understanding when grading these models. After achieving consistency in grading UML models, the second author evaluated UML models in the project reports of all 40 students. Finally, the first author randomly reviewed the scores of four project reports to ensure consistency in grading, according to the proposed evaluation criteria.

4
Results

In this section, the experimental results intend to answer the following two research questions (RQs).

RQ1:

Can ChatGPT be used to assess UML models?
This RQ is designed to investigate the practicality of ChatGPT in assessing UML models, compared with manually grading the UML models by professionals and/or educators.

RQ2:

How competent is ChatGPT in assessing UML models?
This RQ is designed to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of ChatGPT in assessing different types of UML models.

4.1
Answer to RQ1

Table

To explore whether ChatGPT ‘thinks’ like human experts when grading UML models, we zoom in on the difference between the scores that ChatGPT and human experts gave to the same models, as listed in the last row of Table
4
. Specifically, we manually reviewed the chat history of human-ChatGPT conversations of grading these models, especially the returned reasons that ChatGPT adheres to their scores. It is reported that the scores of 21 of the 40 use case diagrams, 19 of the 40 class diagrams, and 22 of the 40 sequence diagrams provided by ChatGPT are the same as those by human experts. Here, the word ‘same’ means that not only the total score of each UML model but its sub-scores after applying each of the 11 evaluation criteria proposed in Section
3.2
are the same. Regarding the remaining UML models with inconsistent total scores and/or sub-scores, we summarize the following three types of assessment discrepancies that bring differences in the scores generated by ChatGPT.

1.

Misunderstanding.
It indicates the case where ChatGPT misunderstands the grading instruction of the evaluation criteria defined in Section
3.2
or uses inappropriate evaluation criteria for the models to be graded. Taking CC1 as an example, ChatGPT sometimes deducts points when the total number of the identified classes is between 8 and 10. In this case, ChatGPT misunderstood the implied meaning of CC1, i.e., “
Deduct 0.2 points for each missed class if the number of identified classes is less than eight
”. This implies that the class diagram containing 8 to 10 classes is reasonable.

2.

Overstrictness.
It means that when grading UML models, ChatGPT is too strict by absolutely following the suggested answers, rather than allowing alternative answers with similar expressions. One typical example is the enumerated values of the class ‘OrderStatus’ in the suggested class diagram. Considering the case without specifying the enumerated values for ‘OrderStatus’, ChatGPT deducted points by strictly following the suggested answer, but human experts will not deduct points because they agree that enumerated values are not mandatory for class design.

3.

Wrong Identification.
It reflects the errors made in identifying the modeling elements in the specified UML models. For example, ChatGPT did not identify the generalization between two use cases, but this relationship was explicitly represented in the specified use case diagram.

Key Findings of RQ1:
We found that based on the detailed evaluation criteria, ChatGPT works in assessing UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. Especially, ChatGPT always generates lower scores than the authors did, but the grading difference between ChatGPT and human experts is not great. In addition, we identified three types of assessment discrepancies when ChatGPT assesses UML models, compared to the assessment performed by human experts.

Discussion

In this section, we discuss the experimental results presented in Section
4
and analyze the potential impacts and significance that ChatGPT may bring to the education of SE in universities.

5.1
Interpretation of Results

Interpretation of RQ1 Results
: The answers to RQ1 reveal that, by feeding ChatGPT the role (e.g., a computer science lecturer in this paper) it will play in this task and detailed evaluation criteria, the scores automatically given to the specified UML models were similar to those provided by human experts. This indicates that ChatGPT can work as AI educators to grade course assignments on UML modeling with well-designed and structured prompts, instead of human teachers or teaching assistants. Furthermore, three types of evaluation discrepancies, i.e.,
Misunderstanding
,
Overstrictness
, and
Wrong Identification
, were reported by zooming in on UML models with lower scores given by ChatGPT. This implies the weakness of ChatGPT in assessing UML models and suggests the potential directions to improve ChatGPT’s capabilities in this task.

Interpretation of RQ2 Results
: The findings of RQ2 further elaborate on the differences in both subscores and total scores of each UML model created by the students reported in the answer to RQ1. Referring to the scores provided by human experts, these differences reveal ChatGPT’s competency in using specified evaluation criteria to assess various types of UML models. The results in Table
5
reflect the overall capability of ChatGPT in evaluating different types of UML models. On one hand, 37 out of 120 UML models (3 models per student
\times
40 students)) were evaluated too rigidly by ChatGPT. The higher percentage of this type of evaluation discrepancies (i.e., 30.83%) implies that ‘Overstrictness’ could be the main reason why ChatGPT gave lower scores to the UML models created by students. On the other hand, the proficiency of ChatGPT in evaluating these three types of UML diagrams exhibits slight differences, according to the lowest occurrence (19 instances of ‘Perfect Match’, see Table
5
). The reason could be that essential modeling elements (e.g., class, attributes, and operations) and the topology structure of class diagrams are more complicated than the other two types of UML models, i.e., class diagram and sequence diagram. The results in Section
4.2.1
, Section
4.2.2
, and Section
4.2.3
report detailed evaluation discrepancies of ChatGPT in the task of assessing three types of UML models, respectively.

1.

Considering UML use case diagrams, the findings indicate that ChatGPT relatively struggles more to identify necessary roles (i.e., actors by following the evaluation criterion UC1) and functional requirements (i.e., use cases by following UC2) from the textual descriptions. The reason could be that it is not easy for ChatGPT to extract candidate actors from some texts in the case study, taking ‘the accounting system (an existing software module)’ as an example. Meanwhile, the frequent overstrictness of using UC1 indicates that ChatGPT has limited flexibility in accepting and processing synonymous or differently phrased actor names.

2.

As for UML class diagrams, it seems more challenging for ChatGPT to correctly identify classes from the textual description because more instances fall into the three types of evaluation discrepancies in the column ‘CC1’ of Table
7
. One possible reason could be that as the first and foremost element in class diagrams, classes represent an abstraction formed by comprehending and distilling key information in relevant text, rather than simply reusing the nouns explicitly listed in the sentences. Another reason is that ChatGPT may struggle with processing synonymous or similar terms, and this limitation is also evident in its performance in evaluating UML use case diagrams. Surprisingly, ChatGPT works better to grade attributes and operations if the classes are identified, seeing the ‘0’s in the third and fourth columns of Table
7
. The main reason could be that grading attributes and operations is conducted after the corresponding class is confirmed, which matches the regular steps followed by human experts to extract elements in class diagrams, i.e., to identify the class before its attributes and operations.

3.

When evaluating UML sequence diagrams, the employment of SC1 had fewer instances of ‘Overstrictness’ (3 instances), as shown in Table
8
. This implies that ChatGPT is more flexible to recognize primary objects. Whereas, the higher occurrence of ‘Overstrictness’ in SC2 and SC3 highlights the limited tolerance of ChatGPT for alternative valid answers when evaluating messages and their order in sequence diagrams. One potential reason could be that sequence diagrams mainly represent the interactions between objects from a dynamic perspective, posing challenges for both ChatGPT and human beings.

5.2
Implications

The answers to RQ1 and RQ2 reveal the feasibility and effectiveness of ChatGPT in evaluating and giving scores to UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. Although the results expose some limitations of ChatGPT in assessing UML models, it is promising to employ ChatGPT as AI educators in the field of SE education.

For teachers:
The research findings inspire new directions for SE education, especially teaching activities, in the era of AI. First, the employment of ChatGPT makes it possible to review and grade students’ course projects or assignments automatically and efficiently. This allows teachers to devote more time and energy to teaching courses. Second, ChatGPT can mitigate biases that may arise from human experts who are fatigued after reviewing a large number of UML models. This ensures the objectivity of the grading process and the fairness of scores to some degree. Third, the rationale provided by ChatGPT can serve as valuable information to help teachers understand students’ learning situations and identify their weaknesses. Based on such information, teachers can tailor their teaching plans to better meet the needs of students.

For students:
Instead of manually reviewing their UML models, ChatGPT enables students to quickly receive scores and personalized feedback. This enables students to efficiently and effectively understand their learning status for improvement. However, due to the weaknesses and limitations of ChatGPT reported in Section
4.2
, these students need to strengthen their ability to identify and correct the errors made by ChatGPT. Moreover, this work opens the door for students to improve and polish their UML models before submitting. In this situation, ChatGPT can help them create UML models with higher quality and then get higher final scores for relevant courses.

For researchers:
This study paves the way to apply other generative AI techniques to aid in evaluating the three types of UML models created by the students. Therefore, researchers are expected to explore the performance of diverse generative AI techniques in this task or other similar tasks in SE education. In addition, more research is necessary to improve ChatGPT’s proficiency in assessing UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. For example, prompt engineering can be utilized to refine and polish the prompts to evaluate these UML models. Further improvements on the grading specifications of the proposed evaluation criteria are also expected to mitigate the ‘Overstrictness’ issue that ChatGPT brings to the automatic evaluation of UML models.

Threats to Validity

This section follows the guidelines in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
to evaluate the potential threats to the validity of our research results.

Construct validity:
Due to the inherent randomness exhibited by current generative AI techniques, including ChatGPT used in this study, identical inputs (e.g., the picture of UML models) may lead to different outputs (e.g., scores of the input UML models). Therefore, the performance of ChatGPT on the evaluation of UML models cannot be fully controlled with precision by formatting the prompts. Accordingly, this may impact the analysis and assessment of ChatGPT’s capabilities. In the next step, we plan to execute the evaluation of UML models by GPT multiple times to partially mitigate this threat.

Internal validity:
Since the analysis and evaluation of the UML models are based on the evaluation criteria proposed for the three types of UML models, the threats to internal validity introduced by the subjectivity and bias of the scorers are minimized. In addition, the scoring method adopted in this study may not fully capture the accuracy and completeness of UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. In this paper, the scoring details of each evaluation criterion were first defined based on the UML teaching and grading experience of the authors, and then polished by pilot evaluation and discussion between the authors. This measure can partially reduce this threat.

External validity:
There is an external threat to validity with respect to the limited generalizability of the research findings because ChatGPT is the only generative AI model used in this study. The results may vary if other generative AI models are employed to grade the three types of UML models. Thus, the performance of more generative AI models in evaluating UML models remains to be studied in our future work. In addition, the UML models created by students from other universities or practitioners may help improve the external validity of the research results.

Conclusion validity:
Due to time and resource limitations, the research data of this work, i.e., the UML models to be graded, were created by 40 undergraduate students at our university and collected from their course project reports. The research findings may be affected by the import of the UML models created by the practical modelers, varying in educational background and modeling experience, for the same case study used in this study. Another threat to the conclusion validity could be caused by the UML models created for different application domains or modeled with different levels of complexity and size, which will be further investigated in our future work.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

In this exploratory study, we quantitatively explored the capability of ChatGPT in evaluating and grading three types of UML models, i.e., use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams. More specifically, we first collected UML use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams provided in the course project reports of 40 undergraduate students. Then, we defined the evaluation criteria and the grading details for these three types of UML models, respectively. These evaluation criteria not only guided the grading process and results conducted by human UML experts but also were integrated into the prompt feeding to ChatGPT to get grading scores of input UML models. By comparing and analyzing the scores provided by human experts and ChatGPT, it is concluded that ChatGPT is competent in evaluating specified types of UML models (e.g., use case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams), as the automatically grading scores by ChatGPT are only slightly lower than those given by human experts. These differences were further studied by analyzing the evaluation discrepancies that ChatGPT brought by using each evaluation criterion. We found that ChatGPT often evaluates the UML models too rigidly, and its performance slightly differs in the assessment of three types of UML models.

The next steps of our work are the following: (1) to refine and elaborate the grading specifications of the proposed evaluation criteria in order to mitigate the ‘Overstrictness’ issue in ChatGPT-based evaluation. (2) to expand the sample size of the study by collecting UML models created by students from other universities or practical modelers, (3) to extend the experiments to more types of UML models, such as UML state diagram and activity diagram, and (4) to evaluate the capabilities of other generative AI models in grading UML models, as well as in requirements analysis and software modeling tasks in software engineering education.
