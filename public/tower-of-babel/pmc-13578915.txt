Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices of Nurses on Antimicrobial Resistance
Authors: Abilba Frieda Ayinema, Jamal‐deen I. Tiiba, Cynthia Amaning Danquah, Kwame Ohene Buabeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntimicrobial Background: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health challenge driven largely by inappropriate antimicrobial use. Nurses play a critical role in antimicrobial stewardship, infection prevention, and patient education, yet gaps between knowledge and practice persist, particularly in low‐resource settings.ObjectiveThis study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of nurses regarding AMR in selected hospitals within the Tamale metropolis, Ghana.MethodsA descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted among nurses across three hospitals using a convenience sampling approach. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables, and inferential analysis (χ 2, t‐test, and regression) was used to assess associations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 610 nurses approached, 384 completed the questionnaire (response rate: 73.3%). All respondents (100%) had heard of AMR, with 78% identifying the hospital as their main source of information. A majority demonstrated good knowledge of AMR, with key contributors identified as poor infection control practices (67%), improper antibiotic prescribing (59%), and use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics (76%). Overall, 83% of respondents demonstrated good knowledge (mean score: 11.9 ± 0.11). Positive attitudes toward antimicrobial stewardship were observed in 78% of participants. Educational level was significantly associated with attitudes toward antibiotic use (p = 0.022), although the correlation was weak. In practice, 57% of respondents reported that patient demand contributes to inappropriate antibiotic use, while 19% did not recognize that unnecessary antibiotic administration promotes resistance.ConclusionNurses demonstrated good knowledge and generally positive attitudes toward AMR; however, important gaps remain in stewardship‐related practices. Strengthening institutional antimicrobial stewardship programs and continuous professional education is essential to improve practice and mitigate the growing threat of AMR.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most pressing global health challenges of our time. The widespread and often inappropriate use of antibiotics is steadily undermining the effectiveness of these life‐saving medicines, threatening to reverse decades of medical progress. Globally, resistant infections are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, with thousands of deaths reported annually and an increasing burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries [1, 2]. Projections suggest that, if left unaddressed, AMR could account for up to 10 million deaths each year by 2050, alongside significant economic losses [2].

The problem is particularly concerning in resource‐limited settings, where infectious diseases remain highly prevalent and access to diagnostic and stewardship systems is often limited. Studies have shown increasing resistance among common pathogens, including gastrointestinal organisms such as Salmonella species, which are capable of causing severe systemic infections [3]. At the same time, the development of new antibiotics has not kept pace with the emergence of resistant organisms, further compounding the challenge [4].

However, this advantage is now being eroded by inappropriate use, including over‐prescription, misuse, and poor adherence to treatment. In many developing countries, weak regulatory systems and easy access to antibiotics without prescription further accelerate the emergence of resistance [5].

Healthcare workers, particularly nurses, are central to the fight against AMR. Nurses are directly involved in administering medications, monitoring patient responses, ensuring infection prevention and control, and educating patients on appropriate antibiotic use. Their daily interactions with patients place them in a unique position to influence antimicrobial use and adherence. However, improving antimicrobial prescribing and usage patterns requires not only awareness but also meaningful changes in behavior and practice [6].

Evidence from previous studies suggests that while healthcare workers often have adequate awareness of AMR, gaps persist in translating this knowledge into practice. For instance, a cross‐sectional study in Ethiopia found that although most participants had heard of AMR and demonstrated reasonable knowledge, this did not always translate into appropriate antimicrobial practices [7]. Similarly, misconceptions about antibiotic use and resistance have been reported among both healthcare professionals and the general public, highlighting the complexity of the issue [8].

In addition to individual knowledge, systemic factors such as workload, lack of institutional guidelines, limited access to diagnostic services, and patient expectations play a significant role in shaping antimicrobial use. In many settings, antibiotics are prescribed or administered inappropriately, with estimates suggesting that more than half of all medications in developing countries are used incorrectly [9]. Practices such as self‐medication, over‐the‐counter access to antibiotics, and patient demand further contribute to the problem [10, 11].

Moreover, AMR places a significant economic burden on healthcare systems due to prolonged hospital stays, increased treatment costs, and loss of productivity [12].

Addressing AMR therefore requires a multifaceted approach, including strengthening antimicrobial stewardship programs, improving infection prevention and control practices, and enhancing education for healthcare workers. Understanding the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of nurses is a critical first step in designing effective interventions. Assessing these factors helps identify gaps and informs targeted strategies to promote rational antibiotic use [13].

Given the central role of nurses in patient care and antimicrobial use, this study aimed to assess their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding AMR in selected hospitals within the Tamale metropolis. The findings are expected to provide insight into existing gaps and guide interventions aimed at improving antimicrobial stewardship in this setting.

Materials and Methods

Study Site

This study was conducted in three major healthcare facilities within the Tamale metropolis in Northern Ghana, namely Tamale West Hospital, Tamale Central Hospital, and Tamale Teaching Hospital. These facilities serve as key referral and secondary care center's within the region, providing a wide range of clinical services and hosting a substantial nursing workforce.

Study Design

This was a descriptive cross‐sectional study design using nurses from selected three hospitals in the Tamale metropolis in 2019.

Study Population and Sample Size

The study population included professional nurses from the Tamale Regional Hospital, the Tamale Teaching Hospital and Tamale West Hospital who were available at the time of the study and were willing to take part.

Sampling Technique

A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit participants. Nurses who were readily available during the data collection period and met the inclusion criteria were approached and invited to participate. Only those who provided informed consent were enrolled in the study.

Data Collection Tool

Data were collected using a structured, self‐administered questionnaire, which was distributed by trained research assistants. Participants were approached during their shifts and invited to complete the questionnaire voluntarily.

The questionnaire was divided into four main sections:

Section A: Sociodemographic (biographic) characteristics of respondents.

Section B: Knowledge of antimicrobial resistance.

Section C: Attitudes toward antimicrobial resistance.

Section D: Practices influencing antimicrobial resistance.

The tool was designed to capture both theoretical understanding and real‐world clinical behaviors related to antimicrobial use.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered using Microsoft Word and Excel 2013 but analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences Windows Version 21.0. Descriptive statistics summarized the respondents' characteristics and responses, and inferential statistics were used to assess the association between demographic variables and KAP variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the School of Medical Sciences/Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital Committee on Human Research, Publication and Ethics (CHRPE/AP/369/18).

Permission to conduct the study was also sought and granted by the management of the participating hospitals prior to data collection.

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained, as no identifying information such as names was included in the questionnaires. All data were handled securely and used solely for research purposes.

Discussions

The findings from the study demonstrate that awareness of AMR was universal (100%), with the majority of respondents (78%) identifying the hospital as their primary source of information. This high level of awareness is consistent with studies conducted in similar low‐ and middle‐income settings, where healthcare professionals are increasingly exposed to AMR‐related information through institutional training and clinical practice [7, 15]. However, awareness alone does not necessarily translate into optimal antimicrobial stewardship practices.

With reference to knowledge, respondents demonstrated a strong understanding of major drivers of AMR, including poor infection control practices, inappropriate antibiotic use, and the use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics. Notably, 76% strongly agreed that broad‐spectrum antibiotic use contributes to AMR, while 67% identified poor isolation precautions as a key factor. These findings suggest that nurses possess adequate theoretical knowledge of AMR determinants. Similar trends have been reported in studies across sub‐Saharan Africa, where healthcare workers show good conceptual knowledge but struggle with implementation [16].

Despite this, the study found no statistically significant association between work experience and knowledge (p = 0.06), indicating that knowledge acquisition may not be solely dependent on years of practice but could be influenced by access to training and continuing professional education. However, regression analysis revealed that age and work experience were significant predictors of knowledge, suggesting that cumulative exposure may still play a role in strengthening understanding.

Attitudinal assessment revealed that 78% of respondents demonstrated a positive attitude toward antimicrobial stewardship, indicating general acceptance of the importance of AMR control. However, a concerning finding was that 70% of nurses reported administering antibiotics frequently, and 56% indicated only moderate commitment to antibiotic stewardship practices. This reflects a critical disconnect between knowledge and practice, which has been widely documented in the literature [1].

The relationship between educational level and antibiotic use was found to be statistically significant (p = 0.022), although the correlation was weak. This suggests that while higher education may influence attitudes and practices, it is not a strong independent determinant. This aligns with findings from previous studies indicating that structural and systemic factors such as hospital protocols, workload, and resource availability play a more substantial role in shaping clinical practice than individual educational attainment alone [2].

These findings highlight systemic challenges within healthcare delivery, where patient expectations and institutional dynamics may override clinical guidelines. Furthermore, only 38% of respondents considered likely infecting organisms when selecting antibiotics, and less than half relied on laboratory results (46%), indicating limited integration of microbiological evidence into clinical decision‐making. This is a major gap in antimicrobial stewardship and has been identified as a key driver of resistance globally [17].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Second, the use of self‐reported data may introduce response bias, as participants may overestimate their knowledge or practices. Third, the study was conducted in selected hospitals within the Tamale metropolis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions.

Conclusion

Nurses in the Tamale metropolis demonstrated good knowledge and generally positive attitudes toward AMR. However, significant gaps persist in clinical practice, particularly in antibiotic administration and evidence‐based decision‐making. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to bridge the knowledge–practice gap and strengthen antimicrobial stewardship efforts in healthcare settings.

Recommendations

1.Implement regular antimicrobial stewardship training programs for nurses to reinforce the practical application of knowledge.2.Strengthen institutional antibiotic guidelines and protocols to guide clinical decision‐making.3.Promote multidisciplinary collaboration, including pharmacists and microbiologists, in antibiotic management.4.Enhance access to diagnostic microbiology services to support evidence‐based prescribing.5.Establish monitoring and evaluation systems to track antibiotic use and adherence to guidelines.
