Capnocytophaga sputigena bacteremia in a nasopharyngeal carcinoma patient with severe neutropenia: a case report
Authors: Zhi-Ying Deng, Li-Hua Tong, Zhan-Tao Lu, Chun-Lin Liu, Jie Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCapnocytophaga sputigena, a fastidious Gram-negative commensal of the human oral flora, rarely causes bloodstream infections but poses significant risks to immunocompromised hosts, particularly those with hematologic malignancies or mucosal barrier injury.Case presentationWe report a rare case of C. sputigena bacteremia in a 51-year-old man with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-positive nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) Stage IV (cT3N2M1), as confirmed by immunohistochemistry. The patient, who was undergoing chemotherapy, presented with severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count 0.04 × 109/L), fever (38.9°C), and oral mucositis. Blood cultures identified slender Gram-negative bacilli, which were confirmed as C. sputigena using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and 16S rDNA sequencing. Initial empirical therapy with meropenem led to clinical improvement, and antimicrobial therapy was subsequently de-escalated to ceftazidime. The patient was eventually discharged in stable condition.ConclusionsThis case underscores that C. sputigena can act as an opportunistic pathogen in solid tumor patients with therapy-induced mucosal damage and highlights the critical importance of meticulous oral care and advanced microbiological diagnostics in managing immunocompromised individuals.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12331-1.

Background

Capnocytophaga spp. are facultative anaerobic, Gram-negative bacilli belonging to the Flavobacteriaceae family, currently comprising nine species phylogenetically classified into two distinct groups: (1) human-adapted species (e.g., C. sputigena, C. ochracea), which colonize the oropharyngeal mucosa as commensals, and (2) zoonotic species (e.g., C. canimorsus, C. canis), primarily associated with animal reservoirs [1]. Among human-associated strains, C. sputigena recognized as a fastidious, capnophilic organism and a well-established member of the oral microbiome, primarily colonizing the oropharyngeal mucosa [2]. C. sputigena primarily colonizes the subgingival plaque, where it is implicated in the development of periodontal disease. However, in immunocompromised hosts, it can function as an opportunistic pathogen, potentially leading to severe systemic infections including bacteremia, endocarditis, or disseminated abscesses [3, 4]. Notably, invasive C. sputigena infections are exceedingly rare, with most reported cases occurring in patients with hematologic malignancies or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) recipients [5, 6]. Here, this report presents a rare case of C. sputigena bacteremia in a patient with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), a solid tumor characterized by mucosal barrier disruption. This finding underscores an underappreciated clinical risk in non-hematologic immunocompromised populations and highlights the need for heightened microbiological vigilance when managing such patients.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old male presented to the oncology department with a 2-day history of fever, oral ulcers, and a scattered skin rash. He had a 1-year history of NPC with liver metastases. One year prior, the patient had been diagnosed with Stage IV (cT3N2M1) undifferentiated non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma of the nasopharynx. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirmed EGFR positivity. The first-line therapy with camrelizumab (200 mg) plus chemotherapy (nab-paclitaxel 400 mg and nedaplatin 100 mg) had been administered. Eight months later, he underwent radical radiotherapy with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) but discontinued it after five sessions and was lost to follow-up. One month prior to admission, he developed fatigue, headache, and diarrhea. A PET-CT scan revealed multiple hepatic lesions suggestive of progressive metastases, along with abnormal liver function tests. Subsequently, second-line therapy was initiated, consisting of camrelizumab (200 mg) and the TP regimen (nab-paclitaxel 400 mg plus nedaplatin 90 mg). Following chemotherapy, efbemalenograstim alfa and eltrombopag were administered to support hematologic recovery.

On admission (day 1), the patient reported a two-day history of recurrent fever, fatigue, and oral ulcers. Vital signs included a fever of 38.9 °C, tachycardia (132 bpm), and tachypnea (24 breaths/min). Physical examination revealed diffusely congested oral mucosa. A 1 cm, deep ulcer with irregular borders and a rough base was present in the right mandibular retromolar area, which was tender and bled on palpation, consistent with Grade III mucositis. A scattered, erythematous maculopapular rash on the trunk and enlarged cervical lymph nodes were observed. His Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score was 65, and his pain score on the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) was 4. Laboratory investigations revealed severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count [ANC] 0.04 × 109/L), leukopenia (white blood cell [WBC] count 0.3 × 109/L), and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 98 × 109/L). Marked elevation of inflammatory biomarkers was observed, with a procalcitonin (PCT) level of 7.123 ng/mL and an interleukin-6 (IL-6) level of 2201.20 pg/mL, suggestive of a severe bacterial infection. To identify the causative pathogen, two sets of blood cultures (each set comprising one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle) were collected aseptically from separate venipuncture sites (left and right arms). Concurrently, specimens for bacterial culture were collected from oral ulcer swabs and skin lesions.The management strategy included local care for oral mucositis with a topical compound chlorhexidine gargle and diclofenac sodium spray. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with meropenem (1 g every 8 hours) was promptly initiated to cover potential pathogens in the context of profound neutropenia.

On hospital day 3, additional microbiological culture results were reported. An oral ulcer swab, cultured on blood agar plates (BAPs) and chocolate agar plates under 5% CO2, yielded normal flora. A skin secretion sample showed no growth on BAPs, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar plates (MACs) incubated under aerobic conditions. In contrast, two aerobic blood culture bottles (BacT/ALERT 3D system, bioMérieux) signaled positive. Gram staining of the positive broth revealed medium-length to long spindle-shaped Gram-negative bacilli (Fig. 1A). Subcultures from the positive bottles were inoculated onto BAPs and MACs. After 3 days of incubation at 35℃under 5% CO2, gray, spreading colonies with irregular margins were observed on the BAPs (Fig. 1B), whereas no growth occurred on the MACs. The remaining two anaerobic blood culture bottles showed no signals after 5 days of incubation. Colonies from the BAPs were analyzed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; EXS3000, Zybio, China) with formic acid extraction [7]. The analysis identified the isolate as C. sputigena with a score of 1.86 (Fig. 1C). The reference database (version V3.3.4.8) comprised 5,225 bacterial species. Per the manufacturer’s criteria, a score of ≥2.0 indicates secure species-level identification, whereas scores between 1.7 and 1.99 suggest probable genus-level identification. Consequently, the isolate was presumptively identified as C. sputigena with a score of 1.86. A biochemical profile consistent with C. sputigena was observed. The isolate tested positive for esculin hydrolysis, sucrose fermentation, lactose fermentation, and ONPG hydrolysis, but negative for catalase, oxidase, indole production, arginine dihydrolase, nitrate reduction, starch hydrolysis, gelatin liquefaction, and xylose fermentation.

To further confirm the bacterial species, 16S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequencing was performed.Genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial colonies using the PrepMan™ Ultra Sample Preparation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nearly full-length 16S rDNA amplicons encompassing the V1–V9 hypervariable regions were generated by PCR using universal primers 27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492 R (5’-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’). The PCR protocol comprised an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. The PCR amplicons were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer. The resulting electropherograms were assembled, edited, and aligned using BioEdit software (version 7.0.9). Taxonomic assignment was performed by comparing the consensus sequence to the NCBI non-redundant nucleotide database using the BLASTN algorithm (version 2.15.0+). The 16S rDNA sequence of the clinical isolate exhibited > 99% identity with the reference sequence of C. sputigena (Accession No.NR_113564.1), thereby confirming the species-level identification.

By hospital day 5 following the initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy, the patient exhibited significant clinical improvement, characterized by the resolution of fever and a marked reduction in systemic inflammatory biomarkers (procalcitonin decreased to 0.330 ng/mL and interleukin-6 to 23.46 pg/mL). Based on this favorable clinical response, coupled with supportive evidence from the disk diffusion assay (which demonstrated a 28-mm zone of inhibition for ceftazidime), the antimicrobial regimen was de-escalated from meropenem to ceftazidime (1 g every 8 hours) in accordance with antimicrobial stewardship principles. He was subsequently discharged on hospital day 7 and transitioned to an oral regimen of cefixime to complete the antibiotic course. Discussion

This patient’s clinical course represents a convergence of key risk factors for invasive C. sputigena infection. The patient was from Southern China, a region with a high global incidence of NPC [8]. The treatment for NPC, which often involves radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, can induce a significant state of immunosuppression [9]. In this context, the patient’s age (over 40 years), combined with chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression and severe neutropenia, likely acted synergistically to create a permissive environment for bacteremia. The presence of chemotherapy-related oral mucositis represents a biologically plausible mechanism for bacterial translocation, although this has not been definitively proven. It must be noted that the oropharyngeal route as the source of infection in this case remains speculative. Direct microbiological evidence linking the oral cavity to the bacteremia is currently limited, as the patient did not undergo periodontal surgery, and swab cultures from the oral ulcers yielded only normal flora. This negative result may be attributed to technical factors such as delays in sample inoculation, low bacterial density, or insufficient culture duration, which are known limitations of routine swab cultures in capturing fastidious organisms [10]. Although the oropharyngeal route is the most plausible clinical presumption, other possibilities were considered, such as transient bacteremia from minor skin breaches; however, skin secretion cultures in this case were negative. Despite these diagnostic limitations, this case highlights the critical importance of maintaining rigorous oral hygiene and implementing multidisciplinary care (e.g., measures like laser therapy for mucositis prevention) in high-risk patients with a NPC background to mitigate such risks.

Laboratory identification of C. sputigena poses significant challenges. Gram staining reveals medium-length to long spindle-shaped, weakly staining Gram-negative bacilli, which may be readily overlooked without meticulous examination by experienced personnel [11, 12]. According to our laboratory protocols, a dual-person verification process is implemented for positive blood culture bottles when reporting positive blood culture smears. This process not only helps reduce missed diagnoses to some extent but also ensures that smear results are promptly transmitted to clinicians through an electronic reporting system. Additionally, C. sputigena exhibits slow growth characteristics, requiring 48–72 hours of incubation at 35 °C under 5% CO₂ to form Gray-colored, spreading colonies with irregular margins on blood agar. These growth requirements, coupled with the limitations of traditional biochemical and morphological methods, render the latter insufficient for species-level identification [13, 14]. In our case, C. sputigena was promptly identified using MALDI-TOF MS (EXS3000, Zybio, China), which yielded a reliable identification score of 1.86, meeting the confidence threshold for genus-level identification. This result was further validated by 16S rDNA sequencing. Such advanced techniques are critical for achieving accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate clinical management.

Currently, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) have not established standardized susceptibility breakpoints for C. sputigena, which poses significant challenges for antimicrobial stewardship. Prior studies report that a high proportion of C. sputigena isolates are β-lactamase-positive, with β-lactam-resistant strains harboring the β-lactamase genes blaCfxA or blaCSP-1, leading to resistance against amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and third-generation cephalosporins [15–17]. Recent advances in genotypic antibiograms have further elucidated the resistance mechanisms in Capnocytophaga species. Whole-genome sequencing has not only confirmed the distribution of known resistance genes (such as blaCfxA and blaCSP-1) among different species but has also uncovered novel resistance determinants. For instance, analysis of Capnocytophaga spp. from animal oral cavities revealed, in addition to known genes like blaCfxA2, blaOXA-347, ermF, and tetQ, a previously unreported chromosomally encoded class A β-lactamase gene, CST-1. This enzyme demonstrates hydrolytic activity against penicillins and first- and second-generation cephalosporins, although its activity is inhibited by sulbactam [18]. These findings underscore that integrating phenotypic susceptibility data with genotypic analysis is crucial for the rapid and accurate delineation of the resistance profile of Capnocytophaga spp. In such scenarios, carbapenems or β-lactam drugs combined with β-lactamase inhibitors are recommended for bacteremia management [19, 20].

In the present case, standardized antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was not feasible due to the fastidious growth characteristics of the Capnocytophaga isolate and the unavailability of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)-based methods such as Etest. However, the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method performed on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood demonstrated a zone of inhibition of 28 mm for ceftazidime. Although this finding cannot be interpreted using established breakpoints in the absence of validated criteria for Capnocytophaga species, it suggests potential intrinsic in vitro activity of cephalosporins against this isolate. The initial empirical choice of meropenem was driven by the severity of the bacteremia and the need for reliable broad-spectrum coverage against potentially resistant pathogens, aligning with guideline recommendations [2, 21]. The decision to de-escalate to ceftazidime was subsequently guided by a combination of factors: (1) marked clinical improvement in the patient (including resolution of fever), (2) a significant decrease in inflammatory biomarkers (procalcitonin and IL-6 levels), and (3) the supportive, albeit non-definitive, indication of susceptibility from the disk diffusion test. This rationale for de-escalation, which integrates clinical response with available supportive microbiological data when standardized AST is unattainable, is consistent with antimicrobial stewardship principles and represents a valuable approach for managing C. sputigena infections in the absence of conclusive susceptibility data [22].

Conclusions

This report of a rare C. sputigena bacteremia secondary to chemotherapy in a nasopharyngeal carcinoma patient underscores the necessity for rapid pathogen identification via advanced diagnostics and heightened clinical awareness in immunocompromised oncology populations.
