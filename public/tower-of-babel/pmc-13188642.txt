ATG5 overexpression enhances the therapeutic efficacy of mesenchymal stem cells in a mouse colitis model by augmenting anti-inflammatory and antioxidative mechanisms
Authors: Yang Sun, Huiyan Sun, Tengfei Zhao, Yanyun Zhao, Fengjun Xiao, Lisheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe therapeutic efficacy of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can be improved by enhancing their adaptation to the inflammatory microenvironment. Autophagy maintains MSCs functionality, and autophagy-related gene 5 (ATG5) mediates autophagy and regulates the biological functions and therapeutic efficacy of these cells. The aim of this study was to investigate the role of ATG5 in the antioxidant capacity and evaluate the therapeutic effect of ATG5-engineered MSCs for colitis treatment.MethodsCell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8. The mRNA expression of autophagy-, antioxidant-, and polarization-related genes was determined through real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and protein expression was analyzed via western blotting. Macrophage polarization markers were analyzed using flow cytometry. Multiomics approaches, including RNA transcriptome sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, and 16S ribosomal RNA microbiota analysis, were also used. Mice with dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis were used to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs.ResultsPreconditioning MSCs with hypoxia (1% O₂) and serum deprivation significantly enhanced autophagy and upregulated ATG5 expression. Adenovirus-mediated ATG5 overexpression in MSCs (MSCs-ATG5) enhanced their autophagic activity and antioxidant capacity, upregulated HMOX-1, SOD2, and CAT expression, and increased glutathione peroxidase and catalase enzymatic activity, while enhancing cell proliferation, without altering surface marker expression. Further, MSCs-ATG5 significantly promoted M2 macrophage polarization and regulated oxidative stress-related signaling pathways. Additionally, MSCs-ATG5-based therapy markedly ameliorated colitis disease signs in mice. Transcriptome analysis revealed that MSCs-ATG5 suppressed the IL-17/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway. This treatment also regulated levels of the anti-inflammatory metabolite prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) in colon tissues. Finally, MSCs-ATG5 increased the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., Oscillospirales), thereby alleviating intestinal microbiota dysbiosis.ConclusionAdenovirus-mediated ATG5 overexpression enhances the autophagic activity, immunomodulatory functions, and antioxidant capacity of MSCs. MSCs-ATG5 can alleviate colitis by inhibiting the IL-17/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway, enhancing secretion of the anti-inflammatory metabolite PGD2, and increasing the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria. Our findings support the potential clinical efficacy of MSCs-ATG5-based therapies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05008-2.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Oxidative stress, Autophagy-related gene 5, Inflammatory bowel disease, Prostaglandin D2, Butyrate-producing bacteria

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have potent immunomodulatory capacity, low immunogenicity, homing, and paracrine properties, and they are associated with abundant sources; thus, they have considerable potential for applications in immunotherapy and tissue repair [1]. These cells have been extensively investigated and exploited in diverse disease contexts, including autoimmune, cardiovascular, neurological, digestive, and orthopedic diseases [2–6]. However, the clinical translation of MSCs is still hindered by multiple critical challenges, including the lack of standardized preparation protocols, limited in vivo survival and persistence, unoptimized administration strategies, and inter-individual variability in therapeutic efficacy [7]. Therefore, strategies aimed at enhancing the in vivo survival and homing efficiency of MSCs are pivotal for boosting their therapeutic efficacy in clinical settings.

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) comprises a group of idiopathic, nonspecific inflammatory disorders of the intestinal tract, primarily ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). Its pathogenesis is predominantly driven by complex interplay among genetic factors, environmental triggers, intestinal microbiota dysbiosis, and immune dysregulation [5]. Currently, no curative therapy is available for IBD. MSCs have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality and have been extensively investigated in preclinical studies. However, their therapeutic efficacy is directly contingent on the intestinal oxidative stress microenvironment, which modulates their homing capacity and survival [8]. This microenvironment exhibits elevated local concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS), directly inducing MSCs apoptosis or functional impairment [9]. Additionally, the hypoxic intestinal milieu constrains MSCs energy metabolism, further compromising their in vivo survival [10]. Thus, enhancing MSC adaptation to the intestinal inflammatory microenvironment is a promising strategy for improving the efficacy of MSC-based therapies for IBD.

MSC preconditioning modulates their key physiological properties, including autophagy, apoptosis, viability, survival, and homing capacities [11]. Autophagy is a pivotal process required for maintaining cellular homeostasis, and it plays a central role in regulating the survival, differentiation, immunomodulatory functions, and senescence-resistance of MSCs [12]. Autophagy inhibits D-galactose-induced MSC senescence via the ROS/JNK/p38 signaling pathway [13]. Three-dimensional-cultured MSCs exhibit enhanced cell viability, which is primarily attributed to augmented autophagy induction and modulated ROS levels [14]. Notably, under hypoxic oxidative stress conditions, autophagy preserves MSC stemness by eliminating damaged cellular components, promoting osteogenic differentiation, and enhancing MSC survival via the AMPK/mTOR pathway [15]. This highlights the fact that autophagy regulates multiple biological functions of these cells via distinct signaling cascades, underscoring its pivotal role in maintaining MSC functionality.

Autophagy-related gene 5 (ATG5), a key component of the autophagic machinery, regulates the formation of autophagosomes, a hallmark of autophagy [16]. ATG5 modulates MSC differentiation and mediates chemosensitivity [17]. The discordance between ATG5 mRNA and protein levels in MSCs under stress reflects the multilevel regulation of autophagy, indicating that post-translational modification of ATG5 serves as a more direct indicator of autophagic activity. Given that genetic modification strategies hold promise for enhancing the cellular functions of MSCs, we employed an adenoviral vector-mediated approach to overexpress ATG5 in MSCs to improve their autophagic activity and therapeutic efficacy. Our findings demonstrate that ATG5 is a key responsive gene under oxidative stress conditions. Specifically, ATG5-overexpressing mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs-ATG5) exhibited enhanced cell viability and optimized biological properties via the modulation of oxidative stress-related signaling pathways, as well as superior therapeutic efficacy in a mouse model of IBD.

Methods

Cell culture

Umbilical cord MSCs of Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) grade were provided by Allcare Biomedical Development Co., Ltd (Qingdao, China). The MSCs were cultured in MSC-specific medium (Sanly-health Biotech, Beijing, China) with 5% CO2 at 37℃. MSCs were passaged until the fifth generation for subsequent experiments.

The rat intestinal epithelial cell line NO.6 (IEC-6) was purchased from iCell Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 and P815 cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). IEC-6 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (high glucose, 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) and 0.1U/mL insulin (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA). RAW264.7 and P815cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (high glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco).

Adenovirus construction

An adenoviral vector harboring ATG5 (Ad ATG5, transcript: NM_004849) and its control vector (Ad GFP) were constructed by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The target gene ATG5 was inserted into the vector plasmid. After enzymatic digestion, target gene amplification, and plasmid extraction, the plasmid harboring the ATG5 gene was obtained. This plasmid was co-transfected with the packaging plasmid carrying the adenoviral genome into host cells to prepare the adenoviral particles.

Detection of MSC surface markers

The surface markers of MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5 were quantified using flow cytometry. MSCs (1 × 106) were incubated with human-specific immunofluorescent antibodies specific to CD90, CD105, CD73, CD11b, CD34, and CD45 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The incubation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was performed using a CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Dye670 assay

MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5 in good growth condition were harvested. Cells (1 × 10⁶/type) were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in 0.5 mL PBS. Separately, 10 µM Dye670 (Cell Proliferation Dye eFluor™ 670, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was diluted in 0.5 mL PBS. The cell suspension was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with the dye solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Labeling was quenched by adding five volumes of complete medium, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min. Cells were washed three times with complete medium. The stained cells were seeded in 6-well plates. Cells were collected at 0, 24, and 48 h post-seeding for flow cytometric analysis (Beckman Coulter). Cell generation and proliferation indexes were analyzed by Modfit LT 5 software (Verity Software House, Topsham, ME, USA).

Luciferase T assay

T cells isolated from peripheral blood were transduced with a lentiviral expression system to chromosomally integrate the luciferase gene, generating Luci-T cells. These Luci-T cells were then co-cultured with MSCs-GFP or MSCs-ATG5 according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Sanly-health Biotech). After 24 h of co-culture, luciferase activity was quantified using a luminometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, USA), and the inhibition rate of MSCs on T cells was calculated based on relative luminescence values.

Macrophage phagocytosis assay

RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. A subset of these cells was co-cultured separately with MSCs-GFP or MSCs-ATG5 cells for 24 h. Well-adherent RAW264.7 cells were harvested, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and resuspended in 1 mL of fresh medium. FITC-labeled ovalbumin (OVA) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to a final concentration of 100 µg/mL, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The phagocytosis reaction was terminated by adding cold PBS. Following centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 400 µL PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter).

M1/M2 polarization of RAW264.7 cells

MSCs-GFP or MSCs-ATG5 cells were co-cultured with RAW264.7 cells in tissue culture plate inserts, with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) added at 1 µg/mL; MSC GFP/ATG5 cells were seeded in the upper chamber at 5 × 10⁴ cells/cm², whereas RAW264.7 cells were plated in the lower chamber at 6 × 10⁴ cells/cm². After 24 h of co-culture, RAW264.7 cells from one well were lysed with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) for subsequent real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and remaining cells were stained for surface marker CD86 (M1 macrophage marker) and intracellular marker CD206 (M2 macrophage marker), the latter requiring prior fixation/permeabilization, followed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter).

Antioxidant capacity of MSC

MSCs were transfected with Ad GFP and Ad ATG5. Antioxidant capacity of MSC includes antioxidant enzymes glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), catalase (CAT), and hydroxyl free radicals-scavenging ability. Cells or cultural supernatant were collected and performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). All assays involved measurement with a microplate reader (Molecular Devices) using standard curves.

Mouse colitis model and MSC therapy

Animal studies were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Care and the Use of Laboratory Animals. This study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (approval number: AHQU-MAL20221118). Specific pathogen-free male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Shandong Pengyue Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). The mice were acclimated for 7 days with free access to food and water. An acute colitis mouse model was established by adding 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) to the drinking water for the mice to drink freely. The DSS model was continuously established for 6 days, and then, the DSS solution was replaced with water. GMP-grade UC-MSCs were transfected with Ad-GFP or Ad-ATG5 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. The mice were randomly divided into four groups: negative control (NC, n = 5), DSS + 1×PBS (DSS + PBS, n = 5), DSS + MSCs-Ad-GFP (DSS+MSCs-GFP, n = 8), and DSS + MSCs-Ad-ATG5 (DSS+MSCs-ATG5, n = 8). Each mouse was injected with 1 × 106 MSCs-GFP/ATG5 mix with PBS via the tail vein on days 3 and 5. Mice in the DSS + PBS group were injected with the same dose of PBS. During the experiment, the weight of each mouse was measured daily, fecal characteristics were observed, and the presence of occult blood in the feces was tested.

The anesthesia protocol for sample collection involved intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital solution. Euthanasia was performed via inhalation of an excessive amount of CO2. The work has been reported in line with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Colon tissue specimens were harvested at the end of the study period and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Following fixation, samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin staining. The specimens were observed under an inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and photographed.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse colon tissue or cell lysates using TRIzol reagent. The RNA concentration was measured at A260 nm and 280 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices). One microgram of RNA was used for reverse transcription, which was performed using a TransScript® One-step cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen, Beijing, China). A SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix kit (TransGen) was used for qRT-PCR. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were measured by an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 RT-PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The total volume of qRT-PCR reactions was 20 µL. The primers were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). β-Actin was used as the internal reference gene. All primer sequences used for tissues or cells are listed in Table 1. The reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2− ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from cells (1 × 106) using RIPA lysis buffer (Meilunbio, Dalian, China) supplemented with 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (Meilunbio). Protein concentration was quantified using BCA assay (Solarbio). Protein (20 µg) was separated via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies (β-actin, ABclonal, Wuhan, China; ATG5, HIF-1α, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA; LC3B, zenbio, Chengdu, China; ATG16L1, p65, p-p65, Selleck; SQSTM1/p62, IκBα, p-IκBα, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4℃ overnight. Next, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-Bio, Beijing, China) and visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence solution (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Protein bands were imaged with a Protein Simple Jess system (Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Serum samples or cell culture supernatant were collected for detecting the content of cytokines and prostaglandin D2 (PGD2). Sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Elabscience), which includes coating, blocking, incubation, signal development, and quantification. The optical density value was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Cytokine concentration analysis

The cytokines in mouse serum and RAW264.7 cell culture supernatants were quantified using the Luminex assay. The commercial kits were purchased from BioRad (Hercules, CA, USA) and performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

Colon tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Colon tissues were incubated with primary antibodies (Occludin, ZO-1, F4/80, CD206, and PTGDS, Servicebio) at 4℃ overnight, and then tissues were incubated with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio) for 1 h (room temperature, dark). Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica). The mean pixel intensity was analyzed by Saiviewer software (Servicebio).

Immunohistochemistry

Colon tissues were embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and then sections were blocked with serum. After blocking, tissues were incubated with primary antibody COX-2 (Servicebio) at 4℃ overnight. After washing with PBS, tissues were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAB development and hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were captured using a microscope (Leica). Staining intensity was analyzed using a H-score system (Servicebio).

RNA sequencing

MSCs-GFP, MSCs-ATG5, and colon samples (NC, DSS + PBS, DSS + MSCs-GFP, and DSS + MSCs-ATG5 groups) were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80℃, with three replicates for each group. Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA integrity and concentration were verified using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A cDNA library was constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7530) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB, E7500). cDNA libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq sequencing platform and performed by Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China). The adapters and reads from the raw reads of each sample were trimmed to obtain clean reads. Gene expression levels were estimated as fragments per kilobase of exons per million fragments mapped. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were based on Padj<0.05 and a log2|fold-change|>1 as the criteria used to establish significance.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis of colon tissues

The same colon tissues were selected for an untargeted metabolomic analysis. Samples were uniformly collected from the same anatomical region to minimize biological variability. The selected tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80℃. The samples were then thawed at 4℃ to prepare for extraction. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies). The first and second spectra were obtained using a TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Finally, raw data were processed with XCMS software (v3.18) for peak alignment and statistical identification of significantly altered metabolites. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd.

16S ribosomal RNA analysis

Fetal contents (NC, DSS + PBS, DSS + MSCs-GFP, DSS + MSCs-ATG5 groups) were collected under sterile conditions and stored at − 80℃, and every group contained three biological replicates. 16S ribosomal RNA analysis was performed by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Microbial DNA was extracted from the fecal samples. DNA pyrosequencing of the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S ribosomal RNA gene was performed using an Illumina MiSeq platform.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

MSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104/cm2. MSCs were cultured for 24–48 h. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Yeasen) was added to each well and incubated at 37℃ in dark for 2 h. The optical density value was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Co-culture assay

Co-culture experiments were conducted using polycarbonate membrane (0.45 μm) cell embedding dishes (JET, Guangzhou, China). P815 or RAW264.7 cells were seeded in the lower chamber. MSCs-GFP or MSCs-ATG5 were seeded into the apical chamber. After co-culture for 24 h, the cells were collected for subsequent experiments.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments, unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-tests for two groups or one-way analysis of variance for multiple groups. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism V10.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Hypoxia and serum-deprivation induce autophagy of UC-MSCs

UC-MSCs were cultured under hypoxic (1% oxygen) and serum-deprivation (H/SD) conditions. Figure 1A shows the autophagosome formation under H/SD conditions at 24 h. Subsequently, we examined the expression levels of classic autophagy-related genes, including ATG3, ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, and Beclin-1, via qRT-PCR, following the incubation of MSCs under H/SD conditions for 12, 24, and 36 h. H/SD conditions significantly enhanced the expression of ATG5, ATG7, and Beclin-1 compared with that under normoxic conditions (Fig. 1B). Moreover, based on western blot analysis, the protein expression of HIF-1α, ATG16L1, ATG5, and LC3 Ⅱ/Ⅰ was upregulated under H/SD conditions both in 12 and 24 h. Upregulation of P62 expression at 12 h followed by its downregulation at 24 h indicates unimpeded autophagic flux under H/SD conditions (Fig. 1C-D).

Biological characteristics of MSCs transfected with Ad-ATG5

A schematic diagram of the adenoviral vector harboring ATG5 is shown in Figure S1A. Ad-GFP and Ad-ATG5 adenoviral particles were transfected into UC-MSCs at MOIs of 100 and 200, respectively. The transfection efficiency reached 78.7% at a MOI of 100 and 92.8% at a MOI of 200 (Figure S1B, C). GFP fluorescence was observed via fluorescence microscopy following the transfection of MSCs with Ad-GFP or Ad-ATG5 (Fig. 2B). qRT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed that ATG5 was overexpressed in MSCs-ATG5 at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2C, D). The expression of MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5 surface markers, such as CD90, CD105, CD73, CD11b, CD34, and CD45, was detected by performing flow cytometry. Adenovirus-mediated ATG5 transfection did not alter the cellular morphology or expression of MSC surface markers (Fig. 2A, G). However, the cell viability of MSCs-ATG5 was significantly higher than that of MSCs-GFP at 24 and 48 h post-transfection both under normoxic and H/SD conditions (Fig. 2D, E). Similarly, Dye670 staining analysis demonstrated that MSCs-ATG5 exhibited a significantly higher proliferation index compared to MSCs-GFP (Fig. 2F).

MSCs-ATG5 promotes the M2 polarization of macrophages

In this part, T cells and macrophages were used as effector cells to validate the immunomodulatory capacity of MSCs. The luciferase-T assay demonstrated that MSCs-ATG5 possess significantly enhanced efficacy in suppressing T-cell proliferation compared to MSCs-GFP (Fig. 3A). RAW264.7 cells are commonly used as models of macrophage polarization. Therefore, MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5 were each co-cultured with RAW264.7 cells for 24 h. Phagocytic capacity was detected via flow cytometry. After co-culture for 24 h, both MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5 significantly enhanced the phagocytic capacity of RAW264.7 cells, but no significant difference was observed in the phagocytosis-enhancing effect between MSCs-ATG5 and MSCs-GFP (Fig. 3B). MSCs-ATG5 had a stronger enhancing effect on the proliferation of RAW264.7 cells than MSCs-GFP (Fig. 3C). The M1 macrophage marker CD86 and M2 macrophage marker CD206 were detected using flow cytometry. After co-culturing RAW264.7 cells with MSCs, the M2 polarization ability of RAW264.7 cells was significantly enhanced, and MSCs-ATG5 had a stronger enhancing effect on the M2 polarization (CD206+) of RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 3D). After co-culture with MSCs, M1/M2 macrophage markers were detected using qRT-PCR. MSCs-ATG5 inhibited the expression of some M1 markers, such as Tnfα and Nos2, and promoted the expression of M2 markers, such as Ym1, CD206, and Arg1 (Fig. 3E, F). Furthermore, LPS was used to induce inflammation in macrophages. LPS significantly increased the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD86+) (Fig. 3G). After co-culturing LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells with MSCs, MSCs-ATG5 have a stronger ability to inhibit M1 macrophages and can promote the transformation of macrophages into M2 macrophages (Fig. 3G). qRT-PCR results showed that LPS significantly promoted the expression of M1 macrophage markers, such as Tnfα and Nos2, and inhibited the expression of M2 macrophage markers, such as Ym1, CD206, and Arg1 (Fig. 3H, I). The results of qRT-PCR were consistent with those of flow cytometry after co-culture with MSCs-ATG5. Both types of MSCs (MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5) suppressed the expression of M1 macrophage markers (Tnfα and Nos2) while upregulating the expression of M2 macrophage markers (Ym1 and Arg-1). Compared with the MSCs-GFP group, the MSCs-ATG5 group exhibited no significant difference in the expression of M1 markers, but significantly enhanced the expression of M2 markers. LPS markedly enhanced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, by RAW264.7 cells. Compared with MSCs-GFP, MSCs-ATG5 exhibited enhanced inhibitory capacity against the secretion of these cytokines (Fig. 3J, K).

ATG5 regulates the oxidative stress signaling pathway in MSCs

To further elucidate the mechanism by which ATG5 modulates the biological properties of MSCs, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on the MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5, with three biological replicates per group. Based on the DEGs identified between the two groups, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the signaling pathways and functional discrepancies between MSCs-GFP and MSCs-ATG5. Based on the significantly altered metabolic and signal transduction pathways associated with the DEGs (MSCs-ATG5 vs. MSCs-GFP), the p53 signaling pathway, cellular senescence, and ferroptosis were predominantly enriched (Fig. 4B). Notably, these signaling pathways are associated with oxidative stress. By screening the DEGs and mapping them to these specific signaling pathways, the key DEGs could be summarized as follows. In the cellular senescence signaling pathway, ATG5 overexpression in MSCs significantly increased PIK3R3, CDKN1A, IGFBP3, and MDM2 expression. ATG5 overexpression upregulates the expression of autophagy- and antioxidative stress-associated genes in MSCs

We investigated the effects of ATG5 overexpression on autophagy and the antioxidant capacity of MSCs under normoxic and H/SD conditions. Under normoxic conditions, compared to that in the MSCs-GFP group, ATG5 overexpression in MSCs significantly upregulated the expression of autophagy-related genes (ATG3, ATG7, ATG12, and Beclin-1) and antioxidant-related genes (HMOX-1, NRF-2, Bax, SOD2, and CAT) (Fig. 4C, D). After 12 h of incubation with MSCs under H/SD conditions, ATG5 overexpression upregulated the expression of autophagy- (ATG3, ATG7, ATG12, and Beclin-1) and antioxidant-related (HMOX-1, SOD2, and CAT) genes (Fig. 4E, F). Meanwhile, NRF2 expression was downregulated in MSCs-ATG5 cells under H/SD. Subsequently, we tested the antioxidant enzymes and antioxidant capacity of MSCs. Under H/SD conditions, the secretion of antioxidant enzymes, such as GSH-Px and CAT, by MSCs was inhibited, and the hydroxyl free radicals-scavenging ability of MSCs was suppressed. Notably, ATG5 overexpression in MSCs increased the intracellular levels of antioxidant enzymes and enhanced their hydroxyl radical-scavenging activities (Fig. 4G–J).

MSCs-ATG5 attenuate DSS-induced colitis in mice

Figure 5A presents a flow diagram illustrating the application of MSCs to a DSS-induced model of colitis. Daily body weight data for each group during the experimental period are shown in Fig. 5B. The DSS model group showed a significant decrease in body weight, whereas MSC treatment alleviated this body weight loss. The alleviation of weight loss in the MSCs-ATG5 treatment group was significantly different from that observed in the model group (Fig. 5B). Representative photographs of the colons from each group are shown in Fig. 5C. The colon lengths of mice in the DSS + MSCs-ATG5 group were significantly different from that of mice in the DSS group (Fig. 5D). Based on the colon staining results, the severity of intestinal mucosal injury was evaluated using the Disease Activity Index (DAI). As shown in Fig. 5E, the DSS + MSCs-ATG5 group had significantly lower DAI scores than the DSS + PBS group. Additionally, MSC treatment effectively restored the structural integrity of the colonic villi and reduced the infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, MSCs treatment reduced the serum levels of pro-inflammatory factors. Notably, MSCs-ATG5 exhibited the potent capacity to downregulate the production of pro-inflammatory factors (including IL-1β, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, G-CSF, and IL-17 A) while increasing the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors, such as IL-10 and IL-4 (Fig. 5G). Additionally, MSCs-ATG5 treatment upregulated expression of the intestinal epithelial tight junction proteins occludin and ZO-1 (Fig. 5H, J, and K). Notably, immunofluorescence revealed upregulation of CD206 + macrophages in colon tissues of MSCs-ATG5 treatment groups (Fig. 5I).

MSC-ATG5 treatment suppresses IL-17/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathways in mice with DSS-induced colitis

RNA-seq analysis was performed on colon tissues from the aforementioned mouse models. Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that, compared to results from the normal group, the upregulated genes in the DSS model group were primarily enriched in classical inflammation-related signaling pathways. These pathways included the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and pathways associated with IBD (Figure S2D). Subsequently, we analyzed DEGs between the MSCs-ATG5 and DSS model groups. Figure 6A, B present the volcano plot and heatmap of DEGs compared between the ATG5 and DSS groups. Intriguingly, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the downregulated genes in the MSCs-ATG5 group were significantly enriched in the IL-17 and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 6C). In DSS-induced mouse colitis, the NF-κB signaling pathway was significantly activated, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of p65 protein, degradation of IκBα protein, and upregulation of IL-17 signaling pathway proteins. Treatment with MSCs-ATG5 effectively reduced p65 phosphorylation, enhanced IκBα protein stability, and inhibited the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 6F-I). Next, we conducted co-culture experiments of IEC-6 and MSCs in vitro. The H/SD conditions were used to simulate the oxidative stress environment in the body. qRT-PCR was used to detect the above-mentioned IL-17 and NF-κB signaling pathway genes. The results indicated that MSCs-ATG5 could inhibit the expression of genes, including Card14, Mmp13, and Cxcl3 (Fig. 6J-N). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed that MSCs-ATG5 regulate multiple biological processes in colon cells, such as the immune response, immune system process, B cell-mediated immunity, and inflammatory response (Figure S2E).

MSCs-ATG5 regulates the anti-inflammatory metabolite PGD2 in colon tissue

Inflammation-related metabolites in ulcerative colitis are key factors that regulate its pathological progression. We conducted untargeted metabolomic sequencing of the colon tissues of mice in the MSCs-ATG5 and DSS model groups. The volcano plot shows the differential metabolites identified through screening using positive/negative ion models (MSCs-ATG5 vs. DSS, Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1, P < 0.05). The specific differential metabolites based on the positive/negative ion models are illustrated in a butterfly diagram. The butterfly graphs show the upregulated and downregulated metabolites in the ATG5 group, along with an analysis of the difference ratios. We selected PGD2 in the negative ion mode for subsequent experiments (Fig. 7A). Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and PGD2 share a close upstream–downstream relationship via the prostaglandin synthesis pathway in vivo. Therefore, we detected the expression of PGD2 synthase (PTGDS) in colon tissues using immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7B, C), and the results demonstrated that MSCs-ATG5 treatment significantly increased PTGDS levels. Subsequently, we examined COX-2 expression in colon tissues, revealing that the MSCs-ATG5 group exhibited a trend of upregulated COX-2 expression compared to that in the other groups (Fig. 7D, E). To further investigate how MSCs-ATG5 regulate the prostaglandin PGD2, we quantified the levels of this protein in mouse serum, which showed an increase in the MSCs-ATG5 group (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, we measured PGD2 secretion by MSCs and MSCs-ATG5 under normoxic and H/SD conditions. MSCs secreted low levels of PGD2 under basal conditions, whereas ATG5 overexpression significantly increased PGD2 secretion (Figure S3D). Notably, the PGD2-secretion capacity decreased with prolonged exposure to H/SD conditions (Figure S3E). After incubation under H/SD conditions, the levels of PGD2 in both the culture supernatant (Figure S3F) and intracellular compartment (Figure S3G) of MSCs-ATG5 were significantly higher than those in MSCs-GFP. PGD2 is primarily secreted by mast cells and macrophages. Therefore, we co-cultured the mast cell line P815 and the macrophage cell line RAW264.7 with MSCs-GFP or MSCs-ATG5. MSCs-ATG5 significantly promoted PGD2 secretion by both P815 and RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7G, H). What’s more, PGD2 promotes the proliferation of IEC-6 cells (Fig. 7I). PGD2 exhibits inhibitory effects on the NF-κB and IL-17 signaling pathways in IEC-6 cells under H/SD conditions, and this inhibition is reversed by a PGD2 antagonist (PGD2-IN-1) (Fig. 7J-N).

Through visualization of the LEfSe data, the figure shows the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores of the significantly different species between the MSCs-ATG5 and DSS groups. The relative abundances of probiotic taxa, such as Prevotellaceae, Allobaculum, and Oscillospirales in the microbiota were significantly higher after MSCs-ATG5 therapy than after DSS-PBS treatment (Fig. 8B). Oscillospirales comprises butyric acid-producing bacteria. The STAMP difference analysis method was used to compare species abundances between the MSCs-ATG5 and DSS groups. Through a KEGG functional analysis of the intestinal microbiota, MSCs-ATG5 therapy significantly enhanced the metabolic functional adaptability of the intestinal microbiota to environmental changes, including replication and repair, cell growth, and drug resistance (Figure S4C). Discussion

The therapeutic efficacy of MSCs is governed by their homing efficiency and adaptability to the pathological microenvironments of tissues [18]. Intrinsic microenvironmental stressors, including oxidative stress, hypoxia, and nutrient deprivation, collectively impair MSC survival and engraftment [19–21]. Although local injection can increase the local MSC density, cells often undergo apoptosis at the injection site due to hypoxia and nutrient deficiency [22]. Multiple strategies including preconditioning, genetic engineering, and targeted delivery systems have been established to enhance the MSC survival rate after transplantation [23, 24]. Hypoxia is an effective preconditioning strategy for MSCs, to promote cell engraftment and angiogenesis through SDF-1 and VEGF secretion [25]. The present study demonstrates that hypoxia combined with serum deprivation triggers autophagy and upregulates ATG5 expression in umbilical cord-derived MSCs. These stress conditions impair the antioxidant capacity of MSCs, as reflected by the reduced levels of antioxidant enzymes and compromised hydroxyl radical-scavenging ability. Our results further revealed that H/SD conditions significantly upregulate the expression of several autophagy-related genes, including ATG5, ATG7, and Beclin-1. Western blot analysis confirmed that ATG5 protein levels were significantly increased. Based on these findings, ATG5 emerged as a stress-sensitive gene in MSCs exposed to oxidative stress. Gene knockout of Atg5 in mouse bone marrow-derived MSCs affected their survival and proliferation (unpublished data). These findings indicate that the Atg5 gene is critically involved in the proliferation of MSCs and essential for their survival. Thus, approaches involving ATG5 modification and autophagy-mediated preconditioning to optimize MSC survival and homing efficiency are critical for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-based interventions.

Autophagy is a critical determinant of MSC fate [12]. The survival or death of MSCs is governed by the dynamic balance of autophagy, as inhibiting excessive autophagy protects cells from autophagic cell death [26], whereas activating autophagy enhances the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-derived exosomes in the SLE inflammatory microenvironment [27]. Cen et al. further confirmed that autophagy activation in MSCs mediates CD4⁺ T cell migration and differentiation through the secretion of CXCL8 and TGF-β1 [28]. ATG5 is a key component of the autophagic machinery and is primarily involved in autophagosome formation and maturation. Additionally, it performs autophagy-independent functions in various diseases [29], contributing to the maintenance of cellular homeostasis and physiological processes. For example, ATG5 regulates eosinophil hematopoiesis and effector functions in both mice and humans [30], whereas Kim et al. reported that Atg5-mediated elevations in basal autophagic activity in naked mole-rat cells inhibits p53/Rb-induced apoptosis [31]. ATG5 helps reduce the inflammatory response in mouse embryonic stem cells and regulates differentiation [32]. ATG5-mediated autophagy in renal tubules specifically suppresses the NF-κB signaling pathway [33]. Interestingly, Atg5-deficient adipose-derived MSCs protect against nonalcoholic fatty liver disease by promoting hepatocyte growth factor secretion [34]. Further, ATG5-modified MSCs may have enhanced therapeutic efficacy in a mouse model of DSS-induced colitis, and the underlying mechanisms were further explored.

Our results showed that ATG5 overexpression in MSCs had no effect on surface marker expression or cell morphology but promoted cell proliferation. ATG5 function is not directly associated with the regulatory pathways of MSC phenotype. After co-culture with RAW264.7 cells, MSCs-ATG5 exhibited robust immunomodulatory capacity, characterized by enhanced RAW264.7 cell proliferation and polarization toward the M2 phenotype. RNA-seq analysis further indicated that ATG5 regulates the p53 pathway, cellular senescence, and enhance MSC resistance to oxidative stress via upregulation of the ferroptosis-inhibitor gene SLC7A11. Furthermore, MSCs-ATG5 demonstrated enhanced autophagy induction and antioxidant gene expression under both normoxic and H/SD conditions, as well as increased antioxidant enzyme activity and hydroxyl-radical scavenging ability.

The in vivo therapeutic potential of MSCs-ATG5 was also assessed using a mouse model of DSS-induced acute ulcerative colitis. We focused on the acute-phase mechanisms of intestinal inflammation. Compared with those in the model group, MSCs-ATG5 remarkably restored body weights and colon lengths, reduced DAI scores and inflammatory factor levels, and improved the intestinal barrier integrity in mice with colitis. These results demonstrate that this ATG5 modification is critical for augmenting the therapeutic efficacy of MSC transplantation for the treatment of inflammatory diseases. To elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of MSCs-ATG5 on colitis, we performed multi-omics sequencing of colon tissues and 16 S rRNA sequencing of the intestinal microbiota. In mice with DSS-induced colitis, the expression of genes involved in inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., the IL-17 and TNF pathways) was upregulated. Moreover, compared to that in the NC group, the DSS group displayed increased expression of genes associated with IL-17- and TNF-mediated inflammatory signaling. Of note, MSCs-ATG5 treatment significantly downregulated the expression of IL-17 pathway-related genes and suppressed NF-κB signaling pathway activation compared with those in the DSS group. IL-17 is a hallmark cytokine produced by T helper 17 (Th17) cells that induces the expression of neutrophil chemoattractants (such as CXCL1, CXCL8/IL-8) in intestinal epithelial cells, leading to the infiltration of neutrophils and exacerbation of mucosal damage [35, 36]. IL-17 activates downstream NF-κB, MAPK (p38), and PI3K–AKT pathways, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response [37–39]. MSCs-ATG5 exerted their colitis-alleviating effect by inhibiting the IL-17 and NF-κB signaling pathways.

Metabolomic analyses can reveal metabolic perturbations and identify metabolic biomarkers of IBD, thereby facilitating the establishment of early risk-prediction models and the development of metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies [40]. Our results showed elevated levels of the metabolite PGD2 in colon tissues from the MSCs-ATG5 group. First identified by Hamberg in 1973, PGD2 is synthesized from arachidonic acid via COX-dependent pathways and is catalyzed by PTGDS [41]. Given its well-documented anti-inflammatory properties, we focused on the role of PGD2 in MSCs-ATG5 therapy. Specifically, PGD2 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by binding to the D prostanoid receptor (DP), and its mechanism involves inhibition of the migration and activation of neutrophils, basophils, dendritic cells, and T cells [42, 43].

PGD2 is primarily secreted by mast cells and macrophages [44, 45]. Iwanaga et al. demonstrated that mast cell-derived PGD2 inhibits colitis and colitis-associated colon cancer (CAC), whereas mast cell-specific PTGDS deficiency exacerbates colitis and accelerates CAC progression [46]. Notably, PGD2 functions as an early anti-inflammatory signal in colitis by suppressing granulocyte infiltration into the colonic mucosa [47]. Macrophages are the primary source of PGD2 in the adipose tissue, and this metabolite promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [45]. Our results indicate that MSCs-ATG5 exhibit an enhanced capacity to regulate PGD2 secretion. Immunohistochemical staining for COX2 and PTGDS in colon tissues demonstrated that the MSCs-ATG5 treatment group had stronger PGD2-synthesis capacity, which was accompanied by elevated serum PGD2 concentrations. Using two major PGD2-secreting cell types, the mast cell lines P815 and RAW264.7, we found that MSCs-ATG5 stimulate PGD2 secretion in both cell lines based on co-culture assays. Collectively, these findings suggest that MSCs-ATG5 regulate PGD2 secretion by mast cells and macrophages in vivo.

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota plays a central role in IBD pathogenesis and progression. The most prominent alteration among individuals with IBD is a reduction in bacteria with anti-inflammatory properties or the capacity to produce short-chain fatty acids [48]. The potential pathways and molecular mechanisms by which MSCs regulate gut microbiota dysbiosis are primarily manifested through: paracrine signaling (including secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, induction of regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, and modulation of macrophage polarization), regulation of host-derived metabolites, and secretion of exosomes [49–52]. Through 16S rRNA intestinal microbiota sequencing analysis, we reveled that MSCs-ATG5 therapy changes the composition of probiotics by significantly increasing the abundance of Prevotellaceae, Allobaculum, and Oscillospirales. (including the butyric acid bacteria Oscillospira in Oscillospirales) [53, 54]. The butyric acid in the colon of IBD patients are significantly reduced [55]. Several studies have shown that butyrate has beneficial effects on intestinal immune and epithelial barrier functions [56]. Research has also indicated that the addition of butyrate significantly alleviates DSS-induced colonic mucosal inflammation by constraining neutrophil functions [57]. Liang et al. found that butyrate facilitates M2 macrophage polarization and promotes the formation of goblet cells in colitis, as well as mucosal recovery [58]. This is consistent with our results that butyrate promotes the polarization of M2 macrophages. The effect of butyrate on M2 macrophages exhibited a synergistic relationship with that of MSCs-ATG5 in enhancing M2 macrophage polarization. Consequently, our findings provide multiple lines of evidence supporting the efficacy of MSCs-ATG5-based therapy, with particular emphasis on its regulatory effect on macrophages and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Conclusion

ATG5 is a stress-sensitive gene that regulates MSC biological functions. Adenovirus-mediated ATG5 overexpression significantly enhanced autophagic activity, immunomodulatory functions, and antioxidant capacity in MSCs. Specifically, ATG5-engineered MSCs (MSCs-ATG5) exerted a potent therapeutic effect on colitis through multiple synergistic mechanisms, including inhibition of the IL-17/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway, upregulation of the secretion of PGD2—an anti-inflammatory metabolite—and promotion of the abundance of butyrate-producing gut bacteria. This study provides a potential clinical efficacy of MSCs-ATG5-based therapies on IBD.
