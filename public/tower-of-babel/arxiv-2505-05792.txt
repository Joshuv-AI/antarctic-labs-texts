On the Stability Barrier of Hermite Type Discretizations of Advection Equations
Xianyi Zeng

Abstract
. In this paper we establish a stability barrier of a class of high-order Hermite-type discretization of 1D advection equations underlying the hybrid-variable (HV) and active flux (AF) methods. These methods seek numerical approximations to both cell-averages and nodal solutions and evolves them in time simultaneously. It was shown in earlier work that the HV methods are supraconvergent, providing that the discretization uses more unknowns in the upwind direction than the downwind one, similar to the “upwind condition” of classical finite-difference schemes. Although it is well known that the stencil of finite-difference methods could not be too biased towards the upwind direction for stability consideration, known as “stability barrier”, such a barrier has not been established for Hermite-type methods. In this work, we first show by numerical evidence that a similar barrier exists for HV methods and make a conjecture on the sharp bound on the stencil. Next, we prove the existence of stability barrier by showing that the semi-discretized HV methods are unstable given a stencil sufficiently biased towards the upwind direction. Tighter barriers are then proved using combinatorical tools, and finally we extend the analysis to studying other Hermite-type methods built on approximating nodal solutions and derivatives, such as those widely used in Hermite WENO methods.

Introduction

In this work we establish stability barriers of Hermite-type discretizations of linear advection equations, a prototype problem for general hyperbolic conservation laws.
Hyperbolic conservation laws are underlying many systems such as those related to fluid mechanics, cellular dynamics in tumor growth models, and population migrations.
While most practical methods focus on second-order accuracy, largely due to the existence of shock waves, recent interests in multi-scale problems make higher-order methods attractive as they tend to resolve small structures better in regions away from discontinuities.

In general, higher-order methods either demand a larger stencil to update the solution at a local node or cell, which makes parallel computation more difficult, or they insert more degrees of freedom inside one mesh cell, which results in more unknowns to achieve the same order of accuracy.
Examples of the first kind include the high-order finite difference methods
[
23
,
15
,
6
]
and k-exact finite volume schemes
[
5
,
4
,
28
]
, whereas the latter is exemplified by discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
10
,
7
]
.
There is a vast volume of literature in any of these topics and our lists are by no means complete.
Recently there have been growing interests in methods utilizing multiple moments (such as cell-average and nodal values, or nodal values and derivatives, or all of them), which seek a balance between using a too-large stencil and too many unknowns in each geometric cell.
This work focuses on methods that use two moments, particularly the active flux methods
[
3
,
1
,
13
]
and the hybrid-variable methods developed by the author
[
33
,
34
,
18
,
35
]
, which find numerical approximations to both cell-averages and nodal values; methods using nodal values and derivatives like the Hermite WENO methods
[
8
,
27
]
and multi-moment methods
[
29
,
26
]
will also be discussed after presenting the main result.
It should be noted that methods involving more moments have been proposed in the literature, such as
[
17
,
2
,
9
]
; we shall leave the analysis of these methods in future work.

To handle discontinuities, these methods combine a high-order discretization of the equation and a non-linear adaptive dection of discontinuities and stability enhancements near these locations.
For example, HV methods utilize a residual-consistent artificial viscosity in compressible flow computations
[
35
]
, the AF methods use the classical idea of flux limiting and special local interpolation to reduce undershoots or overshoots
[
3
,
13
]
, and both Hermite WENO methods and multi-moment schemes cited before adopt WENO-type limiters to build a high-order accurate flux as a convex combination of lower-order ones.
In this work we avoid the discussion of nonlinear stability enhancements and concentrate solely on the underlying high-order discretization that is typically linear for linear problems.
The rationale is that almost all aforementioned methods reduce to their underlying high-order ones when the solution is sufficiently smooth; thus the linear stability of this high-order scheme is necessary for successful application of the method to solve more complicated problems.
As the underlying linear schemes are identical for active flux and hybrid-variable methods, we shall refer to it as the HV discretizations for simplicity, as most of the notations are adopted from the author’s previous work
[
34
]
.

To this end, the main contribution of this paper is shedding lights on the condition under which an optimal HV discretization gives rise to a linearly stable scheme; and our analysis is conducted in the context of method of lines as one can always pair a stable semi-discretized method with appropriate time-integrator (explicit or implicit) to construct a lienarly stable fully-discretized scheme.
Note that a similar question has been studied long ago to the single-moment finite-difference methods (FDM), which states that FDMs with optimal accuracy for an advection equation is linearly stable if and only if
r\leq l\leq r+2
, where

,

Note that

It is usually adopted that

This inequality is known as the

of FDM to solve linear advection equations; and our purpose is to show a similar barrier exists to HV discretizations.

Extension of existing analysis of stability barrier from FDMs to HVs is highly challenging.
In a series of work of Iserles and coworkers, the theory of order stars is developed
[

,
19

20

21

Later, Després

However, neither technique seems to extend to Hermite-type methods as discussed briefly in

To this end, our strategy is to center around the classical Fourier analysis (or von Neumann stability analysis) and use heavily combinatoric tools to study the characteristic trigonometric function that determines the stability of the methods.
In particular, after reviewing the coefficients and charateristic functions of the HV discretizations in

Preliminaries

Let us consider the Cauchy problem for the advection equation:

(2.1)

u_{t}+u_{x}=0\;,

on the domain
[0,\,1]
with periodic boundary condition
u(0,t)=u(1,t)
; and we seek numerical approximations with respect to a uniform grid
x_{j}=jh
, where
h=1/N>0
is the grid size.
The discretization of
eq.

2.1
in space by the hybrid-variable (HV) method concerns approximations to both the nodal values
u_{j}(t)\approx u_{j}^{\ast}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}u(x_{j},t)
and cell averages
\overline{u}_{{{j}+1/2}}(t)\approx\overline{u}^{\ast}_{{{j}+1/2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}u(x,t)dx
.
It is understood that
u_{j}=u_{j+N}
and
\overline{u}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\overline{u}_{{{j+N}+1/2}}
for all integer
j
.
Here the asteroid denotes exact values.
An HV scheme is determined by the discrete differential operator (DDO)
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
, which is defined by (suppressing the dependence on
t
):

(2.2)

[\mathcal{D}_{x}u]_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=-l}^{r-1}\alpha_{k}\overline{u}_{{{j+k}+1/2}}+\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=-l^{\prime}}^{r^{\prime}}\beta_{k}u_{j+k}\;.

The construction of
[\mathcal{D}_{x}u]_{j}\approx u_{x}(x_{j})
uses
l
cells and
l^{\prime}
nodes to the left of
x_{j}
, and
r
cells and
r^{\prime}
nodes to the right of
x_{j}
; the quadruple
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})
is thus called the stencil of the DDO
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
.
It is natural to assume that the stencil utilizes a continuous set of entities, thus
l^{\prime}\leq l\leq l^{\prime}+1
and
r^{\prime}\leq r\leq r^{\prime}+1
.
An equivalent way to denote such
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
is the pair of indices
(L,R)
, where
L=l+l^{\prime}
and
R=r+r^{\prime}
; one can determine the four-integer index by
l^{\prime}=\left\lfloor{L/2}\right\rfloor
,
l=L-l^{\prime}
,
r^{\prime}=\left\lfloor{R/2}\right\rfloor
, and
r=R-r^{\prime}
.

The DDO
eq.

2.2
is
p
-th order accurate if for sufficiently smooth
u
, one has:

[\mathcal{D}_{x}u^{\ast}]_{j}=u_{x}(x_{j})+c_{p}\frac{d^{p+1}u(x_{j})}{dx^{p+1}}h^{p}+O(h^{p+1})

for some
c_{p}\neq 0
.
In previous work
[
34
]
it is shown that given a continuous stencil
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})
there exists a unique
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
that has the optimal order
p=l+r+l^{\prime}+r^{\prime}=L+R
.
We shall only consider these optimally accurate DDO in the present work.
Once
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
is chosen, the semi-discretization of
eq.

2.1
is thus given by for all
1\leq j\leq N
:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\overline{u}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{h}\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)=0\;,\\
\\
u_{j}^{\prime}+[\mathcal{D}_{x}u]_{j}=0\;.\end{array}\right.

By the von Neumann analysis, much of the property of the numerical method can be obtained by studying the eigenvalues of this linear system of ordinary differential equations.
Particularly, one can show that (for example, following
[
34
, Section 4]
) all eigenvalues of the ODE system
eq.

2.3
lie on the set of eigenvalues (denoted
\mathcal{S}
) of the
2\times 2
matrix:

(2.4)

\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{i\theta}-1\\
G(\theta)&H(\theta)\end{bmatrix}\;,\quad\textrm{ where }G(\theta)=\sum_{k=-l}^{r-1}\alpha_{k}e^{ik\theta}\;,\ \textrm{ and }\ H(\theta)=\sum_{k=-l^{\prime}}^{r^{\prime}}\beta_{k}e^{ik\theta}\;,

with
\theta
runs from
0
to
2\pi
.
The characteristic polynomial of this matrix is given by:

(2.5)

\lambda^{2}-H(\theta)\lambda-(e^{i\theta}-1)G(\theta)=0\;,

and the set
\mathcal{S}
is thus defined as:

(2.6)

\mathcal{S}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,\lambda^{2}-H(\theta)\lambda-F(\theta)=0\ \textrm{ for some }\ 0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\},

where
F(\theta)=(e^{i\theta}-1)G(\theta)
.
Physically,
\theta=\kappa h
where
\kappa
represents the wave number.

It was proved that given sufficiently smooth initial data, the semi-discretized solution converges to the actual one pointwise at
(p+1)
-th order of accuracy if: (1) the DDO
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
is
p
-th order, (2) the stencil is biased towards the upwind direction, or
L>R
, and (3) the ODE system is stable (to be made precise later).
Here the first condition is known as
supraconvergence
, as the order of the method is higher than that of the local truncation error.
The upwind-biased condition, despite its similarity to that of finite-difference method, is not due to stability considerations but a necessary condition for one of the two roots to
eq.

2.5
decays exponentially fast to zero as
h\to 0
or
\theta\to 0
.
This paper focuses on the third condition and investigates the stability given an upwind biased stencil (
L>R
).

When saying the linear ODE system
eq.

2.3
is stable, we mean all its eigenvalues have negative real parts except at most a simple one at zero.
Note that these eigenvalues are precisely
-\lambda(\theta)
, where
\lambda
is a root of
eq.

2.5
; and it is easy to compute when
\theta=0
, the two roots of

2.5
are
0
and
H(0)=\sum_{k=-l^{\prime}}^{r^{\prime}}\beta_{k}>0

The two conditions

is contained in the open right complex plane

(2.7)

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,\lambda^{2}-H(\theta)\lambda-F(\theta)=0\ \textrm{ for some }\ 0<\theta<2\pi\}.

Our analysis is based on the following sufficient and necessary condition for

Lemma 2.1
.

The set

is contained in

if and only if for all

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H>0\;,

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H\,\mathrm{Re}(\overline{H}F)+(\mathrm{Im}F)^{2}<0\;.

Here

Proof.

Let the two roots of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H=x_{1}+x_{2}\;,

It is easy to see that

Remark 2.2

The seemingly simple proof does not tell how the conditions

3.
Barriers of stable HV methods

In this section, we establish some barrier results for stable semi-discretized methods.
The main result is given in
theorem

3.1
, which confirms that given any
R
, an HV method with stencil
(L,R)
cannot be stable for large
L
.
Tighter barriers in special cases are given later in
theorem

3.2
and
theorem

3.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
be a DDO with stencil
(L,R)
and optimal accuracy, then if
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)>0
for all
0<\theta\leq\pi
, there is:

(3.1)

L-R<\min(2R+7,\,9+\sqrt{21R+49})\;.

This theorem indicates that if
eq.

3.1
is violated, the corresponding semi-discretized HV method is unstable.
Note that in
eq.

3.1
, the linear bound is tighter than the square-root one when
R\leq 9
.
The proof is based on analyzing whether the
\beta
-coefficients satisfy the boundedness condition on coefficients of non-negative trigonometric polynomials, as given in
section

3.1
.

While this theorem confirms that given a downwind stencil
R
, the HV method cannot be stable if the upwind stencil
L
is too large; the bound is relatively loose, in the view of
table

2.1
.
Analysing HV methods with small stencil bias (i.e.,
L-R
) is more relevant for practical purposes, as they tend to simplify inter-processor communications in parallel computations.
These methods, however, are much more difficult to analyse; and in what follows we present some results by investigating the sign of
\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)
.
For this purpose, we develop a combinatorics tool to compute
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)
in

Theorem 3.2
.

The HV method is unstable if
4\leq L-R\leq 7
.

The proof of this theorem is given in

3.3

Unfortunately, effort of carrying out the calculation along this line may not pay off for larger

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m,t,t+m,t)
and let

Then if
m\equiv 1\mod 4

\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)>0
for sufficiently large

\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)<0

3.1.
Proof of
theorem

3.1

Consider an arbitrary trigonometric polynomial:

(3.2)

C(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\cos(k\theta)\;,\quad c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\;,

then a simple bound on the coefficients are given in

3.4

[
16
]

Lemma 3.4
.

If

3.2
is non-negative for
0\leq\theta\leq\pi

\left|c_{k}\right|<2c_{0}
for all
1\leq k\leq m
.

Proof.

By orthogonality one has:

c_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}C(\theta)d\theta\;;\quad c_{k}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}C(\theta)\cos(k\theta)d\theta\;,\ 1\leq k\leq m\;.

Thus if
C(\theta)\geq 0
, one has
c_{0}\geq 0
and for all
1\leq k\leq m

\left|c_{k}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|C(\theta)\right|\left|\cos(k\theta)\right|d\theta<\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}C(\theta)d\theta=2c_{0}\;.

Note that the strict inequality is due to the fact that
C(\theta)
is continuous and equality requires
C(\theta)=0
for all
\cos(k\theta)\neq\pm 1
.
∎

Writing:

(3.3)

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{l^{\prime}}h_{k}\cos(k\theta)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{r^{\prime}}(\beta_{k}+\beta_{-k})\cos(k\theta)+\sum_{k=r^{\prime}+1}^{l^{\prime}}\beta_{-k}\cos(k\theta)\;,

where the

2.9d

The idea is to show that if

is sufficiently large, there will be some

such that
\left|h_{k}\right|\geq 2h_{0}
, hence by
lemma

3.4

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)
cannot be non-negative.

For this purpose, we shall distinguish four different cases: (1)
L=2t+2m,R=2t
, (2)
L=2t+2m+1,R=2t
, (3)
L=2t+2m+1,R=2t+1
, and (4)
L=2t+2m+2,R=2t+1
, where
t
and
m
are non-negative integers such that
L>R
.
And we assume that
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)\geq 0
for all
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
.

Case 1:
L=2t+2m
,
R=2t
, where
t\geq 0
and
m\geq 1
.

In this case, the four-index stencil is
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m,t,t+m,t)
and one has:

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=4\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}=4\left(\frac{1}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{t+m}\right)\;;

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k}\left[\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t-k)!}\right)^{2}\right]\;,\quad 1\leq k\leq t\;;

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k}\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}\;,\quad t+1\leq k\leq t+m\;.

It is easy to see that all
h
’s are positive, and for all
1\leq k\leq t
there is
(t+m)!t!<(t+m+k)!(t-k)!
; hence it is necessary for all
1\leq k\leq t+m
:

(3.4)

\frac{2}{k}\left[\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}-1\right]<h_{k}<2h_{0}=8\left(\frac{1}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{t+m}\right)\;,

or equivalently:

(3.5)

2\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{m-k}{t+1}\right)+\cdots\ln\left(1+\frac{m-k}{t+k}\right)\right]<\ln\left(1+\frac{4k}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4k}{t+m}\right)\;.

A bound on
m
can already be obtained by setting
k=1
, in which case
eq.

3.5
gives:

\displaystyle\ 2\ln\left(1+\frac{m-1}{t+1}\right)<\ln\left(1+\frac{4}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4}{t+m}\right)<\ln\left(1+\frac{4m}{t+1}\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\ \frac{(m-1)^{2}}{2(m+1)}<t+1\Leftrightarrow m-3+\frac{4}{m+1}<2t+2\Rightarrow m\leq 2t+4\;.

Note that the bound can be improved to
m\leq 2t+3
using the fact that
1/x
is convex, hence
\frac{4}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4}{t+m}\leq\frac{2m}{t+1}+\frac{2m}{t+m}
and one must have:

\left(1+\frac{m-1}{t+1}\right)^{2}<1+\frac{2m}{t+1}+\frac{2m}{t+m}\;;

letting
m=2t+4
one has
\frac{1}{t+m}<\frac{1}{3t+3}
and the preceding inequality gives:

\left(3+\frac{1}{t+1}\right)^{2}<1+\frac{2(2t+4)}{t+1}+\frac{2(2t+4)}{3t+3}=\frac{19}{3}+\frac{16/3}{t+1}<7+\frac{6}{t+1}\;,

which is clearly a contradiction.
Thus one has
m\leq 2t+3
.

To get a better bound, we want to pick a
k
such that the left hand side of
eq.

3.4
is approximately the largest.
For this purpose, we pick
k=\left\lfloor{m/2}\right\rfloor
and have
k\leq m-k
, hence:

\displaystyle\ 2\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)+\cdots\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)\right]\leq 2\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{m-k}{t+1}\right)+\cdots\ln\left(1+\frac{m-k}{t+k}\right)\right]

(3.7)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\ \ln\left(1+\frac{4k}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4k}{t+m}\right)\leq\ln\left(1+\frac{4k}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4k}{t+2k+1}\right)\;.

As
\ln(1+x)
is concave, the left hand side satisfies:

2\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)+\cdots\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)\right]\geq k\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)+k\ln\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)\;;

and the right hand side clearly has the estimate:

\frac{4k}{t+1}+\cdots+\frac{4k}{t+2k+1}\leq(4k^{2}+2k)\left(\frac{1}{t+1}+\frac{1}{t+k}\right)\;.

Thus
eq.

3.7
leads to:

(3.8)

\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)^{k}\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)^{k}<1+\frac{4k^{2}+2k}{t+1}+\frac{4k^{2}+2k}{t+k}\;.

Because
m\leq 2t+3
, there is
k\leq t+1
.
It is elemetary to show if
x\leq 1
,
(1+x)^{\frac{1}{x}}\geq 2
; setting
x=\frac{k}{t+1}
and
x=\frac{k}{t+k}
one gets respectively
\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)^{k}\geq 2^{\frac{k^{2}}{t+1}}
and
\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)^{k}\geq 2^{\frac{k^{2}}{t+k}}
.
Denoting
\gamma=\frac{k^{2}}{t+1}+\frac{k^{2}}{t+k}
, one thus has from
eq.

3.8
:

2^{\gamma}\leq\left(1+\frac{k}{t+1}\right)^{k}\left(1+\frac{k}{t+k}\right)^{k}\leq 1+\frac{4k^{2}+2k}{t+1}+\frac{4k^{2}+2k}{t+k}\leq 1+6\gamma\;.

It follows that
\gamma<5
and:

\frac{2k^{2}}{t+k}\leq\gamma<5\quad\Rightarrow\quad k<\frac{5}{4}+\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}t+\frac{25}{16}}\;.

Using
L-R=2m\leq 4k+2
and
R=2t
, the preceding inequality gives:

L-R\leq 7+\sqrt{20R+25}<7+\sqrt{21R+49}\;.

Combining with
L-R=2m\leq 4t+6=2R+6
, we obtain
L-R\leq\min(2R+6,9+\sqrt{21R+49})
.

The other three cases are handled similarly, and we omit the details here and leave them in
appendix

B
.
To this end, we finish the proof of
theorem

3.1
.

3.2.
Combinatorics and ReH

In this section we establish some combinatorics tools that help with proving
theorem

3.2
and
theorem

3.3
.
Let us begin with the simpler case
(L,R)=(2t+2m,2t)
or
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m,t,t+m,t)
as before and have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=

\displaystyle\ 4\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{2}{k}\left[\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t-k)!}\right)^{2}\right]\cos(k\theta)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\ +\sum_{k=t+1}^{t+m}\frac{2}{k}\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}\cos(k\theta)\;.

The key is to define the polynomial
P_{t,m}(x)=(x+t+1)^{(m)}
, where
(x)^{(m)}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+m-1)
is the rising factorial, then for all
1\leq k\leq t
:

\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t-k)!}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t+m-k)!}\right)^{2}Q_{t,m}(k)\;,

where
Q_{t,m}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}P^{2}_{t,m}(x)-P^{2}_{t,m}(-x)
.
Noting that
P_{t,m}(t+j)=0
,
1\leq j\leq m
, one immediately obtains for
t+1\leq k\leq t+m
:

\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+k)!}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t+m-k)!}\right)^{2}P_{t,m}^{2}(k)=\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m+k)!(t+m-k)!}\right)^{2}Q_{t,m}(k)\;.

Thus
eq.

3.9
can be re-written as:

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=4\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}+\sum_{k=1}^{t+m}\frac{2Q_{t,m}(k)}{k}\left(\frac{(t+m)!t!}{(t+m-k)!(t+m+k)!}\right)^{2}\cos(k\theta)\;.

To move on, we introduce the following notation: Let
P(x)
be a polynomial in
x
, then we write:

(3.10)

P(x)=[P]_{0}+[P]_{1}x+\cdots+[P]_{n}x^{n}\;;

that is,
[P]_{k}
is the coefficient of
x^{k}
,
k\geq 0
in
P
and
n
is the degree.
It is clear that one can define
[P]_{k}=0
for all
k>n
so that the notation holds for all
k\geq 0
.
For example:

(3.11)

[P_{t,m}]_{0}=(t+1)\cdots(t+m)=\frac{(t+m)!}{t!}\;,\quad[P_{t,m}]_{1}=\frac{(t+m)!}{t!}\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}\;.

Noting that:

\frac{Q_{t,m}(x)}{x}=\frac{P_{t,m}(x)-P_{t,m}(-x)}{x}\cdot\left[P_{t,m}(x)+P_{t,m}(-x)\right]

one has
Q_{t,m}(x)/x
is an even polynomial with constant term:

\left[\frac{Q_{t,m}(x)}{x}\right]_{0}=4[P_{t,m}]_{1}[P_{t,m}]_{0}=4\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}\left(\frac{(t+m)!}{t!}\right)^{2}\;.

Let us write:

(3.12)

\frac{Q_{t,m}(x)}{x}=\left(\frac{(t+m)!}{t!}\right)^{2}\left[q_{t,m;0}+q_{t,m;1}x^{2}+\cdots+q_{t,m;m-1}x^{2(m-1)}\right]\;,

then
q_{t,m;0}=4\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}
and one has:

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=q_{t,m;0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{t+m}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}q_{t,m;j}k^{2j}\right)\left(\frac{(t+m)!(t+m)!}{(t+m-k)!(t+m+k)!}\right)^{2}\cos(k\theta)\;.

For convenience, we define a function
C_{m,n}(\theta)
with two indices as follows:

(3.13)

C_{m,n}(\theta)=\sum_{k}\binom{m}{n+k}\binom{m}{n-k}\cos(k\theta)\;,

where the summation is taken over all integers and we adopt the convention that
\binom{m}{n}=0
for all
n<0
or
n>m
.
Then the real part of
H(\theta)
has the following formulation:

(3.14)

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}q_{t,m;j}\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m,t+m}(\theta)\;.

This equation plays a critical role in the proof of
theorem

3.2
, for which we’ll need the following results that are proved in
appendix

C
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
m\geq n\geq 0
, there is:

(3.15)

C_{m,n}(\theta)=\sum_{k}\binom{m}{m-k}\binom{2k}{2n}\sin^{2(m-k)}\frac{\theta}{2}\cos^{2(k-n)}\frac{\theta}{2}\;.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
m,n\geq 1
, there is:

(3.16)

C^{\prime\prime}_{m,n}(\theta)=m^{2}C_{m-1,n-1}(\theta)-n^{2}C_{m,n}(\theta)\;.

Note that a direct consequence of
lemma

3.5
is:

(3.17)

C_{m,n}(\pi)=\binom{m}{n}\;.

Remark 3.7
.

When
m=1
,
eq.

3.14
reduces to
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=4\zeta_{0}^{t+1,t}\binom{2t+2}{t+1}^{-2}C_{2t+2,t+1}(\theta)
, which is non-negative by
lemma

3.5
.
This actually verifies
eq.

2.8a
for methods with stencil
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+1,t,t+1,t)
.
It is not a stability proof, though, as we are still lacking
eq.

2.8b
that is much more complicated.

In the rest of this section we show that the same strategy can be used to study the other stencils.
When
(L,R)=(2t+2m+1,2t+1)
or
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m+1,t+1,t+m,t)
, one has:

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=2(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+1,t+1}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})+\sum_{k=1}^{t+m}\frac{\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m+k}\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m-k}}{\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m}^{2}}\frac{(t+1)!t!}{(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}\frac{2\check{Q}_{t,m}(k)}{k}\cos(k\theta)\;,

where
\check{Q}_{t,m}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}P_{t+1,m}(x)P_{t,m}(x)-P_{t+1,m}(-x)P_{t,m}(-x)
and
\check{Q}_{t,m}(x)/x
is an even polynomial of degree
2(m-1)
, whose constant term is given by:

\left[\frac{\check{Q}_{t,m}(x)}{x}\right]_{0}=2[P_{t+1,m}]_{0}[P_{t,m}]_{1}+2[P_{t+1,m}]_{1}[P_{t,m}]_{0}=\frac{2(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}{(t+1)!t!}(\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m+1,t+1})\;.

Writing:

(3.18)

\frac{\check{Q}_{t,m}(x)}{x}=\frac{(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}{(t+1)!t!}\left[\check{q}_{t,m;0}+\check{q}_{t,m;1}x^{2}+\cdots+\check{q}_{t,m;m-1}x^{2(m-1)}\right]\;,

where
\check{q}_{t,m;0}=2(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+1,t+1}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})
, one has the next representation of
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)
:

(3.19)

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\check{q}_{t,m;j}\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m+1,t+m}(\theta)\;.

Next consider
(L,R)=(2t+2m+1,2t)
or
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m+1,t,t+m,t)
, then:

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=2(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+1,t+1}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})+\sum_{k=1}^{t+m}\frac{\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m+k}\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m-k}}{\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m}^{2}}\frac{t!t!}{(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}\frac{2\hat{Q}_{t,m}(k)}{k}\cos(k\theta)\;,

where
\hat{Q}_{t,m}(x)=P_{t,m+1}(x)P_{t,m}(x)-P_{t,m+1}(-x)P_{t,m}(-x)
.
It follows that
\hat{Q}_{t,m}(x)/x
is an even polynomial of degree
2m
with constant term
\frac{2(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}{t!t!}(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+1,t}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})
.
Writing:

(3.20)

\frac{\hat{Q}_{t,m}(x)}{x}=\frac{(t+m+1)!(t+m)!}{t!t!}\left[\hat{q}_{t,m;0}+\hat{q}_{t,m;1}x^{2}+\cdots+\hat{q}_{t,m;m}x^{2m}\right]\;,

we obtain the following formula of
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)
:

(3.21)

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=\binom{2t+2m+1}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\hat{q}_{t,m;j}\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m+1,t+m}(\theta)\;.

Lastly consider
(L,R)=(2t+2m+2,2t)
or
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m+2,t+1,t+m,t)
, then:

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=2(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+2,t+1}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})+\sum_{k=1}^{t+m}\frac{\binom{2t+2m+2}{t+m+k}\binom{2t+2m+2}{t+m-k}}{\binom{2t+2m+2}{t+m}^{2}}\frac{(t+1)!t!}{(t+m+2)!(t+m)!}\frac{2\tilde{Q}_{t,m}(k)}{k}\cos(k\theta)\;,

where
\tilde{Q}_{t,m}(x)=P_{t+1,m+1}(x)P_{t,m}(x)-P_{t+1,m+1}(-x)P_{t,m}(-x)
.
Then
\tilde{Q}_{t,m}(x)/x
is an even polynomial of degree
2m
with constant term
\frac{2(t+m+2)!(t+m)!}{(t+1)!t!}(\zeta_{0}^{t+m+2,t+1}+\zeta_{0}^{t+m,t})
.
Defining

(3.22)

\frac{\tilde{Q}_{t,m}(x)}{x}=\frac{(t+m+2)!(t+m)!}{(t+1)!t!}\left[\tilde{q}_{t,m;0}+\tilde{q}_{t,m;1}x^{2}+\cdots+\tilde{q}_{t,m;m}x^{2m}\right]\;,

we have the next form of
\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)
:

(3.23)

\mathrm{Re}H(\theta)=\binom{2t+2m+2}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\tilde{q}_{t,m;j}\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m+2,t+m}(\theta)\;.

Proof of

3.3

Let
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(t+m,t,t+m,t)
where
m\geq 1
, then
\mathrm{Re}H
is given by
eq.

3.14
.
As we’re interested in
\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)
at fixed
m
as
t\to\infty
, we shall expand each term of
eq.

3.14
in
1/t
.
Particularly, we shall need such estimates for
q_{t,m;j}
,
0\leq j\leq m-1
and
\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m,t+m}(\pi)
.
For notational simplicity, we denote
O(1/t)
by
\varepsilon
.

First we estimate coefficients
q_{t,m;j}
, defined for each
0\leq j\leq m-1
:

\displaystyle q_{t,m;j}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t!}{(t+m)!}\right)^{2}[Q_{t,m}(x)]_{2j+1}=\left(\frac{t!}{(t+m)!}\right)^{2}[P_{t,m}^{2}(x)-P_{t,m}^{2}(-x)]_{2j+1}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{t!}{(t+m)!}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=\max(0,2j+1-m)}^{\min(m,2j+1)}[P_{t,m}]_{k}[P_{t,m}]_{2j+1-k}\;.

Because
P_{t,m}(x)=(x+t+1)(x+t+2)\cdots(x+t+m)
, there is:

[P_{t,m}]_{k}=\frac{(t+m)!}{t!}\left[\binom{m}{k}\frac{1}{t^{k}}+O(\frac{1}{t^{k+1}})\right]=\binom{m}{k}t^{m-k}(1+\varepsilon)\;,\ 0\leq k\leq m\;;

therefore:

[P_{t,m}]_{k}[P_{t,m}]_{2j+1-k}=\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{2j+1-k}t^{2m-2j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\;.

Hence we obtain an estimate for
q_{t,m;j}
as:

(3.24)

q_{t,m;j}=2\left(\frac{t!}{(t+m)!}\right)^{2}\left[\sum_{k}\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{2j+1-k}\right]t^{2m-2j-1}(1+\varepsilon)=2\binom{2m}{2j+1}t^{-2j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\;.

Next we consider
\frac{d^{2j}}{d\theta^{2j}}C_{2t+2m,t+m}(\theta)
.
To this end, let us define the index set:

(3.25)

D_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\{(d_{1},\cdots,d_{q}):\,0\leq d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{q}\leq p\}\;,

and the following
Z
-functions:

(3.26)

Z_{p,q}(x)=\sum_{D_{p,q}}(x-d_{1})^{2}(x-d_{2})^{2}\cdots(x-d_{q})^{2}\;.

In what follows we write
n=t+m
for simplicity (so
C_{2t+2m,t+m}(\theta)
is written
C_{2n,n}(\theta)
) and because all
Z_{p,q}(\bullet)
will take argument
n
, we shall just write
Z_{p,q}=Z_{p,q}(n)
.
By convention, we denote
Z_{p,0}=1
for
p\geq 0
and
Z_{p,q}=0
if
p<0
or
q<0
.
A key step is to use induction to show that for
0\leq j\leq n
:

(3.27)

\frac{d^{2j}C_{2n,n}}{d\theta^{2j}}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j+k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{j-k,k}C_{2n-j+k,n-j+k}\;.

When
j=0
,
eq.

3.27
reduces to:

C_{2n,n}=Z_{0,0}C_{2n,n}\;,

which is true for all
n
.
Now suppose
eq.

3.27
holds for
j
, where
0\leq j\leq n-1
, we then consider the case with
j+1
.
By
lemma

3.6
and the inductive assumption, there is:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2j+2}C_{2n,n}}{d\theta^{2j+2}}=

\displaystyle\ \frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j+k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{j-k,k}C_{2n-j+k,n-j+k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j+k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{j-k,k}\left[(2n-j+k)^{2}C_{2n-j-1+k,n-j-1+k}-(n-j+k)^{2}C_{2n-j+k,n-j+k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j-1+k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{j-k,k}C_{2n-j-1+k,n-j-1+k}+

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{j+1}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j-1+k)!}\right)^{2}(n-j-1+k)^{2}Z_{j+1-k,k-1}C_{2n-j-1+k,n-j-1+k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=0}^{j+1}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(2n-j-1+k)!}\right)^{2}\left[Z_{j-k,k}+(n-j-1+k)^{2}Z_{j+1-k,k-1}\right]C_{2n-j-1+k,n-j-1+k}\;.

To complete the induction, we need to show
Z_{j+1-k,k}=Z_{j-k,k}+(n-j-1+k)^{2}Z_{j+1-k,k-1}
, which is not difficult:

\displaystyle Z_{j+1-k,k}=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{D_{j+1-k,k}}(n-d_{1})^{2}\cdots(n-d_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{D_{j-k,k}}(n-d_{1})^{2}\cdots(n-d_{k})^{2}+\sum_{D_{j+1-k,k-1}}(n-d_{1})^{2}\cdots(n-d_{k-1})^{2}(n-(j+1-k))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ Z_{j-k,k}+(n-j-1+k)^{2}Z_{j+1-k,k-1}\;.

One last step before estimating
\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)
is the following estimate for
Z_{j-k,k}
,
0\leq k\leq j\leq m-1
, which are not difficult to obtain:

(3.28)

Z_{j-k,k}=\sum_{D_{j-k,k}}(t+m-d_{1})^{2}\cdots(t+m-d_{k})^{2}=\binom{j}{k}t^{2k}(1+\varepsilon)\;.

At last, combining
eq.

3.14
,
eq.

3.17
and
eq.

3.27
, and using
eq.

3.24
and
eq.

3.28
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}q_{t,m;j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{(2t+2m)!}{(2t+2m-j+k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{j-k,k}\binom{2t+2m-j+k}{t+m-j+k}

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}q_{t,m;j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\left(\frac{(2t+2m)!}{(2t+2m-k)!}\right)^{2}Z_{k,j-k}\binom{2t+2m-k}{t+m-k}

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}(t+m)!(2t+2m)!}{(2t+2m-k)!(t+m-k)!}\sum_{j=k}^{m-1}q_{t,m;j}Z_{k,j-k}

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}2(t+m)!(2t+2m)!t^{-2k-1}(1+\varepsilon)}{(2t+2m-k)!(t+m-k)!}\left[\sum_{j=k}^{m-1}\binom{2m}{2j+1}\binom{j}{j-k}\right]\;.

For each
0\leq k\leq m-1
, we have:

\frac{(t+m)!(2t+2m)!}{(2t+2m-k)!(t+m-k)!}=(2t)^{k}t^{k}(1+\varepsilon)=2^{k}t^{2k}(1+\varepsilon)\;,

and it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}2^{k+1}t^{-1}(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{j=k}^{m-1}\binom{2m}{2j+1}\binom{j}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{t}\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{2m}{2j+1}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-2)^{k}\binom{j}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{t}\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{2m}{2j+1}=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}(1+\varepsilon)\frac{2}{it}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{2m}{2j+1}i^{2j+1}

\displaystyle=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}(1+\varepsilon)\frac{2}{it}\frac{(1+i)^{2m}-(1-i)^{2m}}{2}=\binom{2t+2m}{t+m}^{-1}\frac{2^{m+1}}{t}\sin\frac{m\pi}{2}(1+\varepsilon)\;.

Here the carrying over of
(1+\varepsilon)
along the equalities is justified by the fact that
m
is fixed and all summations are independent of
t
; and the last equality indicates that when
t
is large (
\varepsilon\ll 1
), the sign of
\mathrm{Re}H(\pi)
is the same as
\sin\frac{m\pi}{2}
given that
m
is odd, which proves
theorem

3.3
.

4.
Relation to other Hermite type methods

The hybrird-variable discretization builds on Hermite interpolations.
To see the relation, let us consider in this section a stencil such that
l=l^{\prime}
and
r=r^{\prime}
(so
(l,r,l^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(l,r,l,r)
).
Let
U(x)=\int_{0}^{x}u(y)dy
be an anti-derivative of
u(x)
, then one can interprete
\overline{u}_{{{j}+1/2}}
and
u_{j}
as approximations to nodal value and derivative of
U
:

\overline{u}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{1}{h}(U(x_{j+1})-U(x_{j}))\;,\quad u_{j}=U_{x}(x_{j})\;.

Using the primitives,
eq.

2.2
can be written as:

[\mathcal{D}_{x}u]_{j}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\sum_{k=-l}^{r-1}\alpha_{k}(U_{j+k+1}-U_{j+k})+\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=-l}^{r}\beta_{k}DU_{j+k}\;,

where
U_{j}\approx U(x_{j})
and
DU_{j}\approx U_{x}(x_{j})
; and
[\mathcal{D}_{x}u]
is
p
-th order if and only if for sufficiently smooth
U(x)
:

(4.1)

U_{xx}(x_{j})=u_{x}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\sum_{k=-l}^{r}\Delta\alpha_{k}U(x_{j+k})+\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=-l}^{r}\beta_{k}U^{\prime}(x_{j+k})+O(h^{p})\;,

where
\Delta\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}
, see also
eq.

A.7
.
It follows that
\Delta\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}

5.
Conclusions

In this paper we established results on the stability barriers of a class of high-order Hermite-type discretizations of linear advection equations that are underlying the recently developed active flux methods and hybrid-variable methods.
In particular, we proved that the stencil of the discretization cannot be biased too much towards the upwind direction for stability consideration.
Despite the similarity of the result as its counterpart for finite-difference schemes, existing analytic tools for the latter do not extend to the current case; to this end we developed combinatoric tools to study the stability of these Hermite-type methods.
Then the analysis is extended to other Hermite-type methods such as those approximating nodal values and derivatives, which has been used in the construction of Hermite WENO schemes.
Future work includes proving the sharp barrier for stability of these Hermite-type methods, which is conjectured in the current work, and studying Hermite discretizations that involve more than two moments of the solutions.

Appendix A
Existing strategies for studying stability barrier of FDMs

In this appendix we review two strategies that have been successfully used in establishing the stability barrier of explicit finite difference methods for advection equations, namely order stars and error functions.
We shall also discuss why they do not easily apply to the analysis of HV discretizations.

A.1.
Order stars

A first proof of the stability barrier of finite difference methods (FDM) was done using the theory of order stars
[
24
,
25
,
22
]
.
Let the Cauchy problem
eq.

2.1
be discretized by a finite difference method (FDM) in space:

(A.1)

u_{j}^{\prime}+\mathcal{D}_{x}u_{j}=0\;,\quad\mathcal{D}_{x}u_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=-l}^{r}a_{k}u_{j+k}\;,

then the conventional stability analysis requires:

(A.2)

\mathcal{S}_{{\textrm{fdm}}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\left\{\sum_{k=-l}^{r}a_{k}e^{ik\theta}:\,0<\theta<2\pi\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{+}\;.

The theory of order stars takes a different approach and consider the set:

(A.3)

\mathcal{O}_{{\textrm{fdm}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,-\pi\leq\mathrm{Im}z\leq\pi,\ \mathrm{Re}(\lambda-z)<0\,,\ \lambda=\sum_{k=-l}^{r}a_{k}e^{kz}\right\}\;.

On the other hand, supposing

Intuitively, it means that the origin is adjoined by

of

A.2.
Integral form of the error function

A second proof of the stability barrier of FDMs by Després
[
12
]
studies the approximation error of
\mathcal{D}_{x}u_{j}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
in
eq.

A.1
have the optimal order of accuracy
p=l+r
, one has:

i\theta=\sum_{k=-l}^{r}a_{k}e^{ik\theta}+O(\theta^{p+1})\;,

and by writing
e^{-i\theta}=1+z
:

(1+z)^{r}\ln(1+z)=P(z)+O(z^{p+1})\,,\quad P(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}-\sum_{k=-l}^{r}a_{k}(1+z)^{r-k}.

As
P(z)
has degree
p
, it is the
p
-th Taylor polynomial of
S(z)=(1+z)^{r}\ln(1+z)
and by the Taylor’s theorem:

(A.6)

S(z)=P(z)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{p}S^{(p+1)}(tz)z^{p+1}}{p!}dt=P(z)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-1)^{l}(1-t)^{p}r!l!z^{p+1}}{p!(1+tz)^{l+1}}dt\;,

where
S^{(p+1)}(z)
is the
(p+1)
-th derivative of
S(z)
.
Using the method of contradiction, Després shows by elementary calculus that the real part of the integral term in
eq.

A.6
cannot be positive for all
\theta
if
l\geq r+3
, which is required by the stability.

In contrast, the same strategy does not apply to hybrid-variable discretizations, mainly due to a lacking of a general theory of algebraic approximation to non-polynomials (Taylor series produce polynomial approximations to such functions).
Specifically, one can show that an optimally accurate method defined by
eq.

2.2
gives:

i\theta=\frac{1}{i\theta}F(\theta)+H(\theta)+O(\theta^{p+1})\;,

where
G
and
