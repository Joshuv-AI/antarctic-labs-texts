Gut microbiota analysis of adrenal injury induced by chronic intermittent hypoxia in mice
Authors: Qingshi Chen, Yaopeng Guo, Jiayi Lin, Siying Wu, Chaowei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGrowing evidence indicates that alterations within the gut microbiota are linked to the onset and progression of diverse diseases. However, the contribution of the gut microbiota to adrenal injury associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) remains incompletely understood. In the present work, a chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) mouse model was developed to characterize the shifts in gut microbial composition induced by CIH and to provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying adrenal injury associated with OSA.MethodsA CIH mouse model was established, and fecal pellets and intestinal contents were collected for 16 S rDNA amplicon sequencing to evaluate gut microbial diversity and overall community structure. Functional prediction and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were then conducted using PICRUSt2 to identify disrupted metabolic pathways.ResultsCIH exposure induced marked adrenal injury. 16 S rDNA sequencing showed that, compared with controls, CIH increased α-diversity and produced a clear separation in β-diversity, accompanied by a decreased relative abundance of Akkermansia and significant increases in Desulfovibrio, Lactobacillus, and Lachnospiraceae. KEGG functional prediction further indicated enrichment of pathways associated with Ribosome, Genetic Information Processing, and Metabolism of Cofactors and Vitamins, whereas Transporter-related pathways were downregulated. Collectively, these functional alterations may be closely associated with adrenal injury caused by CIH and suggest a potential link to aldosterone dysregulation.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our findings suggest that CIH exposure is associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and adrenal injury in mice, and further indicate a potential link between microbial alterations and aldosterone dysregulation in the context of OSA. These results expand current understanding of the possible gut–adrenal interaction under intermittent hypoxic stress and provide a basis for future mechanistic studies.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea, Chronic intermittent hypoxia, Adrenal injury, Gut microbiota, KEGG pathway analysis

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep related breathing disorder marked by repeated upper airway collapse, which results in chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) [1]. Intermittent hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation trigger oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and sympathetic activation, thereby driving widespread organ dysfunction [2]. If left untreated over the long term, OSA can lead to diverse health consequences, including renal and pulmonary injury [3, 4], metabolic dysregulation [5], adipose tissue dysfunction [6], and cardiovascular disease [7], ultimately imposing a substantial burden on patients’ quality of life. Notably, OSA can accelerate NAFLD progression to steatohepatitis and fibrosis [8], in one recent cohort, all patients with severe fatty liver disease had coexisting moderate-to-severe OSA. Emerging evidence also implicates OSA in adrenal gland injury. Intermittent hypoxia can activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), driving up aldosterone levels [9]. Consistently, patients with hypertension associated with OSA exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of primary aldosteronism (PA) compared to those without OSA [10]. This bidirectional OSA–PA interplay may create a vicious cycle, as aldosterone excess promotes fluid retention and pharyngeal edema that worsen OSA severity [11]. These recent insights underscore the multi-organ impact of OSA and its intimate links to metabolic dysfunction and endocrine pathology, highlighting an urgent need for integrated research and therapy. However, the mechanisms underlying OSA-associated adrenal injury remain incompletely understood, and the specific signaling pathways involved require further investigation.

The human gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a central regulator of host physiology, with effects that extend far beyond the intestinal tract [12]. Through coordinated immune, metabolic, and neuroendocrine signaling, gut microbes interact with distant organs and help sustain systemic homeostasis [13]. Accumulating evidence associates gut microbial imbalance with cardiovascular disease [14], as well as with metabolic dysfunction such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [15]. The gut microbiome also helps shape immune homeostasis, and its disturbance may amplify maladaptive inflammatory programs that contribute to autoimmune and other chronic inflammatory conditions [16]. Moreover, the microbiota–gut–brain axis provides a mechanistic link between gut microbes and neuroendocrine regulation, as microbial signals can modulate stress responses and influence hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity [17]. Notably, recent Mendelian randomisation evidence suggests that specific gut bacteria may causally impair adrenal function [18]. However, the specific pathways and mechanisms by which gut microbial alterations contribute to adrenal injury triggered by CIH remain not fully elucidated.

Here, we developed an experimental mouse model of adrenal injury caused by CIH to investigate how CIH perturbs the gut microbiota and alters adrenal pathology. Intestinal contents and fecal samples were collected from mice exposed to CIH and normoxic controls for 16 S rDNA gene sequencing to characterize shifts in the gut microbial community associated with CIH. Microbial community composition was compared between groups to identify taxa affected by intermittent hypoxia, and overall community changes were assessed using analyses of alpha diversity and beta diversity. We then applied PICRUSt2 to infer the functional potential of the microbial community. This work reveals gut dysbiosis associated with CIH and its potential functional consequences, suggesting a possible interaction between intestinal ecological disruption and adrenal injury under hypoxic stress. Overall, to our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize gut microbiota alterations and their putative functional implications in mice exposed to CIH with adrenal injury, which may provide new therapeutic targets for the clinical management of primary aldosteronism associated with OSA.

Materials and methods

Animal

In this work, we employed male C57BL/6J mice obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. This study was conducted in full accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals issued by the Institute for Laboratory Animal Resources of the Life Sciences Committee of the National Research Council of the United States. This research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. 2025-FYFELL-619). All mice can drink water and eat freely.

CIH protocol

The CIH paradigm was developed according to the protocol we previously reported [19]. In short, the mice in the CIH group were housed in specially constructed hypoxic chambers. This chamber is linked to a programmable gas delivery system that allows pure nitrogen to be introduced, lowering the O2 concentration to 6% within 60 s. After that, the control system enables swift replenishment of oxygen, thereby restoring the concentration to 21% O2 in less than 60 s. Each CIH episode consisted of a 2-minute cycle that was repeated 30 times per hour over an 8-hour period each day, and this regimen was maintained for a total of 8 weeks. For mouse in the control group, ambient indoor air was continuously delivered into the chamber for the duration of the experimental period. Following eight consecutive weeks of CIH exposure and induction of isoflurane anesthesia, euthanasia of all mice was performed by trained personnel using the cervical dislocation technique. Finally, adrenal tissues were isolated on ice, and fecal pellets along with intestinal contents were collected.

HE staining

Adrenal tissues were harvested and rinsed in 0.9% saline, immersion-fixed with 4% formaldehyde, dehydrated and paraffin-embedded. Five-micrometer sections were cut and subjected to HE staining. Morphological changes were subsequently evaluated under a light microscope.

Genomic DNA isolation and 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from individual samples with the CretMag™ Power Soil DNA Kit (Cretaceous, China). DNA integrity was confirmed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Specifically, the 16 S rRNA gene V4 region (primers 515 F/806R) and the eukaryotic 18 S SSU rRNA gene (primers 0817 F/1196R) were amplified in triplicate 50 µL reactions per specimen. The triplicate PCR products were pooled into a single composite, and run on a 2% agarose gel to verify the anticipated amplicon size, and then purified from the gel using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA). The purified amplicons were measured on a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS® Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and subsequently run on an MGI high-throughput sequencing platform.

16 S marker gene data analysis

The sequencing reads generated in this study were processed using the QIIME 2 bioinformatics platform. Quality filtering, denoising, and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference were performed with the DADA2 algorithm (Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2). Diversity metrics were calculated based on unweighted UniFrac distances, including α-diversity and β-diversity, and were displayed using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied to determine taxa showing differential abundance across groups, using a logarithmic linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold of 3.0. In addition, to explore the functional impact of CIH on the mouse adrenal gland, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway functions were predicted using PICRUSt2, and between-group differences were assessed according to variations in predicted functional abundances [20]. Data visualization included bar plots for microbial composition and community structure, as well as PCoA plots.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS (version 27.0.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). For data that met assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, group differences were assessed with Student’s t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test) was applied. A P value < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.

Results

CIH-Induced Adrenal Injury

Representative HE staining revealed clear morphological differences between the two groups. As shown in Fig. 1, the adrenal architecture in the control group was well preserved, with clear cellular boundaries. In contrast, the adrenal glands from the CIH-exposed group exhibited marked structural disorganization, characterized by cellular edema, cell enlargement, and a pale cytoplasm.

Effects of CIH on gut microbiota diversity

The gut microbiota was analyzed by 16 S rDNA amplicon sequencing. To visualize community structure, an ASV rank–abundance curve was constructed using ASV identifiers along the x-axis and relative abundance along the y-axis. The curve showed a broad and smooth horizontal distribution, suggesting good evenness and richness in both groups (Fig. 2A). As shown in the Venn diagram, a total of 513 ASVs were detected, of which 443 were shared between the control and CIH groups. Additionally, 39 and 31 ASVs were uniquely identified in the control and CIH groups, respectively (Fig. 2B). Regarding α-diversity, the CIH group exhibited upward trends in ACE, observed ASVs, Chao1, and Shannon indices, but none of the between-group differences reached statistical significance (Fig. 3). Collectively, these results indicate that CIH exposure did not markedly alter overall microbial diversity, yet it may have shifted community composition, as further supported by β-diversity and taxonomic analyses. Consistently, unweighted UniFrac–based PCoA demonstrated a distinct divergence in bacterial community structures between the two groups, with distinct clustering of control and CIH samples (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that CIH substantially reshapes the gut microbial community structure in mice and may consequently contribute to adrenal injury.

Impact of CIH on the gut microbial community composition in mice

To investigate how CIH alters murine enteric microflora, we compared the structural distribution of taxa across the experimental cohorts at various phylogenetic tiers, including phylum, class, family, and genus (Fig. 5).

Comparison of gut microbial community composition between the two groups. Taxonomic profiles are shown at the (A) phylum, (B) class, (C) family, and (D) genus levels

At the phylum level, the CIH group exhibited a higher relative abundance of Bacteroidota, whereas Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota were less abundant than in the control group (Fig. 5A). At the class level, the relative abundances of Bacteroidia and Clostridia showed higher proportional representation in the CIH group, while Bacilli and Verrucomicrobiae were decreased (Fig. 5B). When examined at the family rank, Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae were enriched in the CIH group, whereas Erysipelotrichaceae was reduced (Fig. 5C). At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Desulfovibrio comprised a larger fraction of the community in the CIH group, but lower abundances of Allobaculum and Akkermansia compared with controls (Fig. 5D).

In addition, the heatmap revealed a distinct genus-level clustering pattern that segregated the two groups, indicating marked differences in the intestinal microbial profiles of control and CIH mice (Fig. 6). Under CIH exposure, several dominant taxa displayed distinct abundance patterns.

Comparison of gut microbial communities

LEfSe was used to pinpoint group-discriminatory bacterial taxa, using an LDA threshold of > 3.0 to visualize differential biomarkers across the CIH and control groups (Fig. 7A). The CIH group showed pronounced enrichment across multiple taxonomic ranks, including increased abundances at the genus level of Klebsiella, Ligilactobacillus, and Odoribacter, as well as an elevated abundance of Tannerellaceae within the family rank. In contrast, the control group was enriched for Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobiota at the phylum level; Enterobacterales, Verrucomicrobiales, and Erysipelotrichales at the order rank; Enterobacteriaceae, Akkermansiaceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae at the family rank; and Akkermansia and Ileibacterium at the genus level. The cladogram further highlighted distinct phylogenetic signatures that discriminated the two groups (Fig. 7B).

KEGG functional profiling of the gut microbiota

To explore functional shifts in the gut microbiota, KEGG pathway profiles were inferred using PICRUSt2. At KEGG Level 2, mice exposed to CIH exhibited a significant increase in pathways related to genetic information processing and the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, whereas pathways associated with signaling and cellular processes, as well as amino acid metabolism, were reduced (Fig. 8A). At KEGG Level 3, the heatmap further illustrated specific functional alterations in response to CIH, revealing an enrichment of ribosomal functions and a reduction in pathways linked to transporters. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that in a CIH mouse model, CIH exposure not only induces adrenal injury but is also accompanied by substantial structural shifts in the gut microbial community. 16 S rDNA sequencing revealed a substantial reduction in overall microbial diversity, characterized by the loss of health-associated taxa and the expansion of potentially pathogenic genera that may be detrimental to the adrenal gland. As far as we are aware, this represents the first investigation to examine the potential contribution of the gut microbiota to adrenal injury under CIH conditions, thereby providing a more comprehensive insight into the mechanisms underlying adrenal damage in an OSA model. Taken together, these findings advance our understanding of how gut dysbiosis induced by CIH contributes to the development of hyperaldosteronism associated with OSA.

OSA is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with wide-ranging pathophysiological impacts. CIH and sleep fragmentation in this condition provoke excessive oxidative stress and a cascade of inflammatory responses [21]. This pro-inflammatory and hypoxic milieu triggers neuroendocrine disturbances such as heightened sympathetic nervous activity and dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [22]. Notably, sympathetic overactivation in OSA stimulates the RAAS and results in elevated circulating angiotensin II and aldosterone levels [23]. Sympathetic excitation and activation of the RAAS in OSA may increase aldosterone levels and, through sustained adrenal stimulation in concert with local inflammatory signaling and redox imbalance, may thereby facilitate the onset and worsening of primary aldosteronism, although further mechanistic evidence is needed. Emerging clinical and experimental evidence indicates a bidirectional link between OSA and primary aldosteronism via this mechanism [24]. Patients with sleep apnea display a disproportionately high prevalence of primary aldosteronism and higher aldosterone levels independent of obesity or blood pressure, which suggests that the sleep disorder causes adrenal overstimulation or injury [25]. Reciprocally, treating hyperaldosteronism using mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists or adrenalectomy can alleviate the severity of sleep apnea and underscores the crosstalk between the adrenal glands and the disorder [26]. Animal models further demonstrate that activation of the RAAS induced by intermittent hypoxia causes aldosterone-mediated tissue injury that can be attenuated by mineralocorticoid blockade [27]. Taken together, inflammation, oxidative stress, and system dysregulation induced by OSA may injure adrenal glands and predispose individuals to primary aldosteronism. However, the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Recent studies implicate gut microbiota dysbiosis as a novel mediator in the systemic effects of the disorder by showing that hypoxia related to sleep apnea can disrupt gut flora and amplify inflammation [28]. Therefore, exploring the impact of CIH on adrenal function from a gut microbiome perspective is of critical significance to elucidate how OSA might precipitate primary aldosteronism.

Emerging evidence has identified the gut microbiota as a key player in a wide spectrum of diseases involving cardiometabolic, inflammatory, neurologic, endocrine, and neoplastic disorders. Numerous microbiome studies over the past few years have elucidated significant host-microbe associations and causal contributions to disease. For example, targeted modulation of the microbiota can improve outcomes in metabolic syndrome and obesity. A recent randomized trial demonstrated that dietary resistant starch supplementation induced weight loss and enhanced insulin sensitivity in humans. These effects were linked to microbiota shifts including the enrichment of Bifidobacterium species and were causally validated in gnotobiotic mice [29]. Regarding autoimmune inflammation, perturbing the microbiome can alter disease trajectories. This was illustrated when transferring the gut microbiota of a healthy donor to patients with new-onset type 1 diabetes halted the progression of islet autoimmunity in a controlled trial [30]. The axis connecting the gut and the brain represents another paradigm of microbiome influence where interventions in animal models have demonstrated that introducing specific microbes or metabolites can modulate neuroinflammatory and behavioral outcomes. For instance, a Clostridium butyricum probiotic improved motor function and reduced microglial activation in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease [31]. Similarly, long-term treatment with Bifidobacteria in APP/PS1 transgenic mice decreased amyloid plaques and neuroinflammation [32]. Together, these studies underscore that the microbiome is intimately linked to disease pathogenesis across organ systems.

Beyond these potential gut–adrenal interactions, another plausible mechanism linking CIH-induced gut dysbiosis to adrenal injury may involve activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway. AhR is a multisignal sensor that responds to both endogenous and exogenous ligands and has been implicated in hypertension, RAAS-related signaling, and CIH-associated organ injury [33]. Notably, indole-like metabolites derived from gut bacteria, including Lactobacillus, can function as endogenous AhR ligands [34]. Under CIH conditions, alterations in microbial composition may disturb metabolite-dependent AhR signaling, thereby contributing to adrenal oxidative stress, inflammation, and endocrine dysregulation [35]. Although this mechanism is biologically plausible, AhR activation was not directly evaluated in the present study and therefore warrants further investigation in future mechanistic studies.

It is increasingly recognized that gut dysbiosis can influence the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and adrenal hormone homeostasis. Recent evidence from a Mendelian randomization analysis utilizing genome-wide association data indicates that specific gut microbial genera may causally impact adrenal function along with the activity of distinct adrenal cortical zones [18]. Mechanistic studies support a bidirectional gut–adrenal axis. On one hand, pro-inflammatory gut microbiota alterations can exacerbate adrenal stress responses. For instance, microbiota-driven activation of Toll-like receptor 4 signaling in diabetic rodents has been shown to heighten the production of adrenal glucocorticoids such as cortisol [36]. Conversely, certain microbes can modulate adrenal hormones directly as demonstrated by a recent study that isolated a bacterial des-like enzyme capable of converting cortisol to androgens. Colonization with this cortisol-degrading bacterium protected mice against excessive cortisol levels and depressive-like behaviors [37]. Gut microbiota may also influence adrenal physiology through systemic immune-metabolic interactions involving microbial metabolites like SCFA. These metabolites can affect immune cell trafficking and cytokine profiles to potentially mitigate autoimmune-mediated adrenal damage or alter feedback sensitivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis [17]. However, few studies have directly linked alterations in the gut microbiota to hyperaldosteronism associated with OSA. Therefore, we employed a mouse model subjected to CIH to investigate how shifts in the gut microbial community may drive adrenal injury induced by CIH.

In this study, 16 S rDNA amplicon sequencing was employed to characterize alterations in the gut microbiota of mice exposed to CIH. Notably, the CIH group exhibited pronounced microbial shifts, which may contribute to adrenal injury and the development and progression of hyperaldosteronism. Alterations in the intestinal microbial community were evident across several taxonomic ranks in the group subjected to CIH. Analysis at the class and family levels revealed that CIH elevated Bacteroidia and Clostridia while concurrently reducing Bacilli and Verrucomicrobiae. These shifts favor obligate anaerobes with pathogenic potential and coincide with a decline in certain lineages producing SCFA [38]. For instance, Lachnospiraceae increased under hypoxic conditions and includes members that promote inflammation. In contrast, Erysipelotrichaceae levels decreased despite the importance of these SCFA producers for gut barrier integrity. At the genus level, CIH increased the abundance of Desulfovibrio as well as the lactate-producing bacteria Lactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus while depleting Allobaculum and Akkermansia. Similarly, patients with OSA often show increased lactate-producing Lactobacillales and bacteria producing lipopolysaccharides with a concurrent reduction in mucin-protective commensals [39]. The mucin-degrading bacterium Desulfovibrio is enriched by CIH and proliferates in OSA to undermine the gut barrier and promote endotoxemia [40]. Conversely, Akkermansia normally supports the mucosal barrier, so its depletion under hypoxic conditions may exacerbate gut permeability and inflammation [41]. An excess of pro-inflammatory microbes coupled with the loss of beneficial ones contributes to a leaky gut and chronic systemic inflammation. The resulting endotoxemia and surge in cytokines can disrupt endocrine homeostasis and impair adrenal function [36]. Moreover, gut dysbiosis can hyperactivate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis to elevate cortisol levels and potentially overtax the adrenal glands [42]. Thus, the gut microbiome imbalance induced by CIH may be a key mediator by which the intermittent hypoxia of OSA leads to adrenal injury.

To further elucidate how the gut microbiota contributes to CIH-induced adrenal injury, we utilized PICRUSt2 to infer functional differences in microbial communities between the control and CIH groups. The results demonstrated that CIH markedly altered the functional profile of the gut microbiome. In our model, we observed elevated pathways for cofactor, vitamin, and amino acid metabolism. This suggests an increase in the microbial generation of metabolites that may exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation. For instance, enhanced bacterial metabolism of choline to TMAO could contribute to hypertension by amplifying endothelial dysfunction and angiotensin II receptor signaling [43]. Additionally, gut dysbiosis related to OSA is characterized by the loss of commensals that produce SCFA and the concurrent expansion of opportunistic pathogens. This imbalance provokes intestinal inflammation and systemic oxidative stress [38]. Consequently, this pro-inflammatory milieu can damage adrenal tissue as lipid peroxidation and local inflammation induced by CIH have been shown to cause cellular injury in the adrenal gland [44]. Hypoxia within the adrenal zona glomerulosa cells enhances the mobilization of cholesterol esters for steroidogenesis and thereby increases aldosterone output [45]. Furthermore, the enrichment of bacterial two-component systems under CIH suggests an expansion of stress-responsive pathobionts with heightened virulence. This likely increases the release of lipopolysaccharide endotoxins, which drives macrophage recruitment and heightened proinflammatory cytokine output in the adrenal gland while disrupting normal aldosterone regulatory feedback [46]. These results also mirror findings in patients with primary aldosteronism who display distinct metabolic pathway alterations including disrupted nucleotide metabolism and ABC transporters [47]. Thus, the functional changes induced by CIH likely contribute to the adrenal oxidative injury and hyperaldosteronism observed in OSA.

While this study offers novel insights into the influence of CIH on gut microbiota composition and its association with adrenal gland injury in mice, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the reliance on 16 S rDNA sequencing without complementary metagenomic or metabolomic profiling restricted taxonomic resolution and precluded the acquisition of direct information regarding microbial functional activity. Although this approach captures dominant bacterial groups, it fails to identify differences at the strain level or detect community members other than bacteria that might influence adrenal physiology. Second, although H&E staining revealed adrenal histopathological abnormalities, circulating hormones related to the adrenal gland or RAAS, including aldosterone, renin, and corticosterone, were not assessed, nor were molecular markers of adrenal injury examined. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence suggesting a potential link between CIH-induced gut dysbiosis and adrenal injury, rather than definitive proof of adrenal endocrine dysfunction, RAAS activation, or aldosteronism. Third, although we observed concurrent adrenal injury and gut microbiota dysbiosis after CIH exposure, no microbiota-targeted functional validation experiments, such as fecal microbiota transplantation, bacterial reconstitution, antibiotic depletion, or probiotic intervention, were performed. Therefore, the current data do not establish a causal role for the altered gut microbiota in CIH-induced adrenal injury.

Conclusion

Overall, our study demonstrates that the intestinal microbiota exhibits a pronounced compositional shift in a mouse model of adrenal injury induced by OSA. Collectively, these findings suggest that the shifts in microbiota associated with CIH may contribute to the underlying mechanisms of adrenal injury. Importantly, our results highlight the potential of targeting the gut microbiota as a therapeutic avenue and provide a rationale for future clinical efforts to mitigate primary aldosteronism associated with OSA through microbiota modulation.
