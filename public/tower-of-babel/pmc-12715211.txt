Rhodomyrtus tomentosa-incorporated silicone inhibits planktonic growth and surface adhesion of Staphylococcus spp. and Candida albicans in simulated vaginal fluid
Authors: Supakit Paosen, Wiphawadi Phromphithak, Mareena Daus, Siwaporn Bilhman, Ratchaneewan Siri, Supayang P. Voravuthikunchai, Sakkarin Lethongkam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Menstrual cups are increasingly used for menstrual hygiene. However, their use may alter the vaginal microenvironment and increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections caused by Staphylococcus spp. and Candida albicans. The current study aimed to develop an antimicrobial menstrual cup by incorporating silicone with Rhodomyrtus tomentosa (RT) leaf extract, a medicinal plant recognized for its antimicrobial properties. RT-loaded silicone was fabricated using an internal mixer and compression molding at 160 °C for 3 min. Surface roughness and wettability remained unchanged after incorporation. RT-loaded silicone exhibited pronounced anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion against single- and dual-species cultures of Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and C. albicans. The loaded silicone significantly inhibited bacterial growth as early as 1 h after incubation (p < 0.05), with a reduction of > 5 log CFU/mL of pathogens after 12 h. Similar inhibitory activity was observed in experiments conducted in simulated vaginal fluid. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed the strong antimicrobial properties of the RT-loaded material. No cytotoxic effects were detected on fibroblast cells. Importantly, the antimicrobial activity was retained after 12 boiling cycles, indicating good stability. These findings demonstrate the potential of RT-loaded silicone as a promising material for developing safer menstrual cups with enhanced antimicrobial protection.

Introduction

Menstrual hygiene management remains a challenge for girls and women, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where access to or affordability of period products is limited1,2. A variety of feminine hygiene products are available on the market, including tampons and pads. However, these are disposable and designed for single use only. In addition to their high costs, such sanitary products have a major environmental impact, as they are composed of polyethylene polymers, non-biodegradable plastics that generate large amounts of waste requiring several hundred years to decompose3,4. Menstrual cups have emerged as a sustainable alternative for collecting menstrual flow. Typically made of medical-grade silicone, these cups can be reused after proper washing to remove debris and microorganisms. Consequently, menstrual cups are considered environmentally friendly products that generate substantially less waste5.

Several studies have reported that flexible cups are generally well accepted after some initial discomfort. Volunteers using menstrual cups reported high satisfaction due to their comfort, dryness, and reduced odor and leakage6–8. A previous study found that menstrual cups cause leakage 0.5 times less often and need to be changed approximately 2.8 times less frequently than conventional menstrual products such as pads9. Moreover, most participants in previous studies preferred menstrual cups over other sanitary products and expressed their intention to continue using and recommending them after study completion10,11.

The human vaginal microbiota plays a critical role in maintaining genital health by protecting against potential pathogens. Disruption of this microbial balance can lead to vaginal infections, which are often characterized by inflammation, erythema, and purulent, yellowish discharge with a foul odor. The microbial etiology of these infections commonly involves Candida species, Staphylococcus species, Group B streptococci, and normal enteric flora12. Candida species are commensal components of this microbiota, colonizing mucosal surfaces at multiple body sites, including the vagina. Under certain conditions, they can transition to a pathogenic state, causing infections that range from superficial mucosal lesions to life-threatening systemic disease13. Vaginal candidiasis is particularly common in women of childbearing age, with C. albicans representing the predominant pathogen, accounting for approximately 80–90% of the disease14.

Similarly, Staphylococcus species are important members of the vaginal microbiota, contributing to host defense against primary pathogens. However, fluctuations in vaginal pH during the menstrual cycle can trigger pathogenic behavior. S. epidermidis, generally a harmless commensal, can become pathogenic through biofilm formation. In contrast, S. aureus is a more opportunistic vaginal colonizer, capable of inducing vaginal dysbiosis, aerobic vaginitis, and, in rare cases, life-threatening menstrual toxic shock syndrome15.

Anaerobic vaginitis, which is more prevalent, is primarily associated with the overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella vaginalis. In contrast, aerobic vaginitis is dominated by aerobic pathogens, including Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, and is typically characterized by a more pronounced inflammatory response, elevated vaginal pH, and epithelial erosions or ulcerations16,17. In addition, it is concerning that a high resistance rate of 65.71% has been reported among bacteria isolated from patients with aerobic vaginitis18. The composition of tampons, such as carboxymethylcellulose, favors bacterial colonization19. Therefore, it is postulated that the accumulation of menstrual blood in the cup may pose the greatest risk to promote bacterial growth. An antimicrobial menstrual cup incorporating bioactive compounds from natural sources to inhibit growth and prevent adhesion is an intriguing alternative strategy for preventing menstrual cup‑associated toxic shock syndrome.

Rhodomyrtus tomentosa is a native plant species in Southeast Asia and it has been used as a traditional medicine in several countries including Malaysia, Vietnam, and Thailand. The leaf extract of R. tomentosa has demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and their virulence factors. It has been shown to inhibit the adhesion, invasion, and intracellular survival of methicillin-resistant S. aureus in human keratinocytes20. Additionally, the extract inhibits microbial growth and the ability of S. aureus, Streptococcus mutans, and C. albicans to adhere to plastic surfaces and buccal cells21. Moreover, R. tomentosa leaf extract has also been studied for use in various applications, such as anti-acne serum22, bovine mastitis prevention23, immune stimulation for fish diet24, psoriasis treatment25 and as a food biocontrol agent26.

The potent antibacterial activity of R. tomentosa is primarily attributed to rhodomyrtone, a bioactive compound isolated from the leaf extract. This compound has been shown to exhibit strong antimicrobial effects against Gram-positive bacteria and fungi, comparable to vancomycin antibiotics27,28. The mechanisms of action of the pure compound have been demonstrated to exert novel and unique effects to kill bacteria, as the compound can trap membrane proteins in vesicles and increase fluidity, resulting in cell death29.

The current study aims to develop a novel antimicrobial menstrual cup by loading a medical-grade silicone with R. tomentosa leaf extract and assessing its antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Candida albicans, representative microorganisms of the female reproductive tract. The antimicrobial effects were confirmed using scanning electron. Antibacterial stability of the cup was maintained after multiple washings. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of the antimicrobial-loaded cup was investigated according to the international standard of biocompatibility testing methods for medical devices. The R. tomentosa leaf extract-incorporated menstrual cup demonstrated significant antimicrobial effects, inhibiting growth and adhesion of both single and dual-species cultures of all the tested microorganisms, with no observed cytotoxic effects on fibroblast cells. This approach not only provided broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacterial and fungal pathogens but also leveraged the reusability of menstrual cups, which produced substantially less waste, compared with disposable products such as pads and tampons. By using a natural, plant-derived compound instead of synthetic additives, the study combined safety, antimicrobial efficacy, and environmental sustainability, highlighting both the innovation and practical significance of this work in advancing safer and more eco-friendly menstrual hygiene solutions.

Materials and methods

Materials and culture media

Medical grade silicone and silicone curing agent were supplied by Neo Plastomer, Bangkok, Thailand. Animal cell culture media, fetal bovine serum, and antibiotics were purchased from Gibco Laboratories (Grand Island, NY, USA). A microtiter plate and cell culture flask were obtained from SPL Life Science (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany).

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The antimicrobial activity of the loaded material was evaluated against microbial reference strains, including Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 35984, and Candida albicans ATCC 90028. All strains were stored at − 80 °C until use. Bacterial strains were thawed and cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA), while C. albicans were cultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 3–5 colonies were picked and suspended in tryptic soy broth for bacteria or Sabouraud dextrose broth for C. albicans, and incubated for 4–6 h at 37 °C. The microbial inoculum was then adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standards in 0.85% saline for subsequent antimicrobial experiments.

Preparation of Rhodomyrtus tomentosa ethanolic leaf extract

Rhodomyrtus tomentosa leaves were collected in Songkhla province, Thailand (6°55’31.0"N 100°19’09.0"E) in March 2021. The permission was obtained from Natural Product Research Center of Excellence, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand. The plant was identified by Mr. Jarensak Sea Wai. A classified reference voucher specimen (NPRC0057) was deposited at the Faculty of Traditional Thai Medicine, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand. The leaves were washed with water to remove dirt and dried in an oven for 48 h. The dried leaves were ground into powder using an electrical blender and then extracted with 95% ethanol for 7 days. The extract was evaporated using a rotary evaporator until completely dry and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for further use.

Preparation of silicone loaded with R. tomentosa ethanolic leaf extract

The medical-grade silicone rubber was initially placed in an internal mixer (Chareon tut MX75-TQ, Thailand) and allowed to rest for 1 min at 60 °C. The mixing speed was held at 60 rpm. R. tomentosa extract was added and mixed for an additional 3 min. After that, a silicone curing agent was added to the mixer and left for mixing for 5 min. The extract-incorporated silicone was re-mixed using two-roll mills, and a compression molding process was then performed at 160 °C for 3 min.

Physical characterization by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of R. tomentosa leaf extract, unloaded silicone, and RT-loaded silicone were recorded with Nicolet iS5 (Thermo Scientific, USA). A total of 64 scans were accumulated for each spectrum, including the background, within the frequency range of 4000 –500 cm− 1.

Physical characterization

Surface topology and roughness of representative areas of the extract-loaded silicone were determined using the non-contact mode of atomic force microscopy (AFM) (easyScan 2, Nanosurf, Switzerland). Changes in hydrophobic properties of the surfaces were determined by measuring the water contact angle (OCA-15EC, Dataphysics).

Anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion

The antimicrobial activity of the newly developed antimicrobial menstrual cup was investigated against single and dual-species cultures of representative microorganisms of the female reproductive tract including S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and C. albicans. Bacterial and fungal strains were cultured on TSA and SDA, respectively, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Following incubation, microbial colonies from the agar plates were suspended in a culture broth medium and incubated for an additional 4–6 h in a shaker incubator until they reached the mid-logarithmic phase. The microbial solutions were then adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standards in physiological saline. Small pieces (1 × 1 cm) of antimicrobial-loaded silicone and unloaded silicone were placed into separate wells of a 24 well-plate. A hundred microliter of bacterial cells at a concentration of 106 CFU/mL in Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) was then added to each silicone piece. After incubation at 37 °C for 1, 3, 6, 8, and 12 h, microbial suspension on the materials of each incubation period was diluted and plated on TSA to determine planktonic growth. To determine antimicrobial adhesion, the materials were removed from the cultures and gently washed twice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent cells. The material was then soaked in phosphate buffer saline for diluting, and the diluted solution was dropped on TSA for bacterial adhesion enumeration.

Inhibitory effects of R. tomentosa-loaded silicone against polymicrobial cultures

Polymicrobial inhibition assay was performed according to the method previously described30. Overnight cultures of S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and C. albicans were diluted with 0.85% sodium chloride solution to 0.5 McFarland standards. The suspended inoculum of each microbial species was combined at a ratio of 1:1. Dual-species cultures of bacterial cells were grown in MHB, while co-cultures of bacteria and fungi were conducted in RPMI 1640 medium. The anti-planktonic growth and anti-adhesion effects of the antimicrobial-loaded silicone were assessed using the methods described above.

Antimicrobial activity in simulated vaginal fluid containing red blood cells

To mimic the vaginal environment during menstruation, simulated vaginal fluid was prepared by mixing sodium chloride, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide, bovine serum albumin, lactic acid, acetic acid, glycerol, urea, and glucose at appropriate concentrations as mentioned in a previous study31. The solution was then adjusted to pH 7.0, and human red blood cells were added to the prepared solution. Anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion were performed as described above by replacing the microbial culture medium with the simulated vaginal fluid containing red blood cells. The antimicrobial activity was investigated after 12 h incubation. To confirm the pronounced antimicrobial effects of the material, the R. tomentosa-loaded silicone was visualized under scanning electron microscope (SEM) after coincubation with both single and dual-species cultures of microorganisms.

Antibacterial stability after washing by boiling

Since menstrual cups are reusable period products, cleaning instructions to remove mense and contaminated microorganisms have been recommended for TSS prevention. Boiling the cup for five minutes before initial use and after each menstrual cycle is a well-known standard disinfecting method. Therefore, to investigate antibacterial stability after washing, R. tomentosa-loaded silicone was boiled for 5 min and allowed to cool for 20 min. The process was repeated for 12 times. After each boiling cycle, the cups were investigated for anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion as mentioned earlier.

Biocompatibility testing

The cytotoxicity of the antimicrobial-loaded silicone was evaluated using an indirect assay with Transwell microplates and L929 mouse fibroblast cells, following a modified version of the ISO 10993-5:2009 standard for biocompatibility testing. Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well Transwell plates and incubated for 24 h until reaching 80–90% confluence. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and the silicone materials were placed in the Transwell inserts. This setup allowed the cells to be exposed to extracts released from the materials without direct contact, which is an appropriate method for screening the cytotoxicity of novel biomaterials. The treatment was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction assay. The MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 4 h. The solution was then removed, and the wells were allowed to dry before dissolving the formazan crystals in DMSO. Optical density was measured at 570 nm to determine cell viability.

Statistical analysis

The results were obtained from three independent experiments performed in triplicate. The presented results were means ± standard deviation (SD). A paired Student’s t-test was used to compare the tests and controls. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

FTIR characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed to characterize the surface chemistry and determine the changes in the chemical composition of the R. tomentosa-loaded material, compared with unloaded control. Figure 1A clearly shows the broad spectrum at 3323 cm− 1, corresponding to the O-H stretching signal. In addition, FTIR of leaf extract also showed the C = C stretching in the aromatic ring (2933 cm− 1), and the C = O stretching (1620 cm− 1) indicated present phenolic compounds in the ethanolic extract. The FTIR of unloaded silicone (Fig. 1B) displayed characteristic peaks for asymmetric CH3 stretching in Si-CH3 (2962 cm− 1), asymmetric deformation in Si-CH3 (1258 cm− 1), Si-O-Si stretching (1070 cm− 1) and CH3 rocking and Si-C stretching in Si-CH3 (785 cm− 1)32. However, RT-loaded silicone showed unclear interaction of these two materials due to the large different amount of starting materials in the FTIR spectrum (Fig. 1C).

Surface characterization

The changes in surface roughness of the loaded and unloaded silicone were observed under AFM. Rhodomyrtus tomentosa-loaded silicone had lower surface roughness than unloaded materials as the arithmetical mean height (Sa) was 41.74 nm while it was 92.76 nm for the unloaded control (Fig. 2A). However, the changes were not much different since the surface wettability measured by contact angle was almost the same. Hydrophobic properties with a relatively high contact angle of 121.10 ± 1.42° and 117.68 ± 0.67° were observed for unloaded and R. tomentosa-loaded material, respectively (Fig. 2B). The changes in surface properties were evaluated after incubating the materials in simulated vaginal fluid for 12 h. Both materials exhibited a slight increase in hydrophobicity, as indicated by a decrease in water contact angle from 121.10 ± 1.42° to 83.29 ± 0.61° for the unloaded silicone and from 117.68 ± 0.67° to 108.23 ± 1.58° for the R. tomentosa-loaded material.

Anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion

Antimicrobial activities of the novel antimicrobial menstrual cup were investigated by measuring the ability to inhibit planktonic growth and microbial adhesion to the cup surface against single and dual-species cultures of representative microorganisms of the female reproductive tract including S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and Candida albicans. The antimicrobial effects were determined after incubation for 1, 3, 6, 8, and 12 h (Fig. 3). The R. tomentosa-loaded menstrual cup exhibited excellent anti-planktonic growth against all the tested microorganisms by significantly inhibiting the growth of single-species cultures of S. aureus and S. epidermidis and dual-species cultures of S. aureus/S. epidermidis and S. aureus/C. albicans since 1 h after incubation (p < 0.05). The highest antimicrobial effects were observed after incubation for 12 h as the planktonic growth of both single-species cultures of the tested bacteria and dual-species cultures were reduced by more than 7 log CFU/mL, compared with the unloaded control. Meanwhile, RT-loaded silicone could significantly inhibit the growth of C. albicans after incubation for 6 h (p < 0.05). A reduction of 5.86 log CFU/mL of the tested fungi was observed after incubation for 12 h. Similar results were observed for antimicrobial adhesion (Fig. 4). A significant reduction of bacterial adhesion of both single-species and dual-species cultures was recorded since the first hour of incubation while it took 6 h to significantly inhibit fungal adhesion to the loaded-material surfaces (p < 0.05). After incubation for 12 h, approximately 6 log CFU/mL of all tested microorganisms was detected for RT-loaded silicone, compared with unloaded silicone.

Antimicrobial activity in simulated vaginal fluid containing red blood cells

To mimic the biological conditions during menstruation, antimicrobial efficacies were further investigated in simulated vaginal fluid containing red blood cells. Following 12 h incubation, the antimicrobial-loaded silicone significantly inhibited planktonic growth and microbial adhesion of all tested microorganisms (Fig. 6A). Planktonic growth of S. aureus and S. epidermidis was decreased by 1.54 and 3.74 log CFU/mL while a reduction of about 2 log CFU/mL was observed for coculture of dual-species microorganisms with RT-loaded silicon. The adhesion of all tested pathogens, including single- and dual-species cultures, was inhibited by approximately 2–3 CFU/mL, compared with unloaded control (Fig. 6B).

Antibacterial stability after washing

As the menstrual cup is a reusable product, it has to be washed by boiling after use. To investigate whether antimicrobial activity remained after the washing process, the cups were boiled for 12 cycles while anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion against S. aureus were determined after boiling for 1, 3, 6, 8, and 12 cycles (Fig. 7). Following coincubation the silicone with microorganisms for 12 h, excellent antibacterial effects were observed after boiling for 1 and 3 cycles by inhibiting S. aureus growth and adhesion by approximately 7 log CFU/mL, compared with unloaded control. Gradually increased bacterial growth and adhesion to RT-loaded silicone were observed after boiling for 6, 8, and 12 cycles. These findings suggest that the antibacterial activity of the antimicrobial-loaded silicone was decreased with each wash. This reduction in antibacterial activity may be attributed to the heat, which could accelerate the release of R. tomentosa extract from the cup. However, even after 12 boiling cycles, the novel antimicrobial-loaded silicone exhibited a significant reduction in both planktonic growth and adhesion of S. aureus (p < 0.05).

Biocompatibility testing

Cytotoxic effects of RT-loaded silicone were determined on L929 fibroblast cells. Extracted solutions were collected through Transwell microplates, and cell viability was assessed using an assay for cell metabolic activity. Following incubation for 24 h, the extract exhibited non-cytotoxicity on the tested cells, with a cell viability of approximately 100%, compared with untreated cells (Fig. 8). The percentage cell viability was similar to the unloaded silicone.

Discussion

Only a few approaches are available for managing menstruation, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Single-use pads remain the most common option because of their ease of use and wide availability. However, prolonged use of pads can cause discomfort and may promote bacterial growth, thereby increasing the risk of infections. Menstrual cups have been proposed for menstrual hygiene management owing to their reusability, which not only reduces living costs for women and girls but also supports environmental sustainability. However, the use of menstrual cups to collect menstrual flow may cause menstrual TSS, a severe complication that can result in multiorgan failure33. Moreover, fluctuations in hormonal levels during the menstrual cycle can promote the opportunistic pathogenicity of commensal microorganisms. Although the hydrophobic nature of silicone does not favor microbial adhesion or growth, the accumulation of menstrual blood in the cup provides a potential medium for microorganisms.

Therefore, the present study aimed to develop antimicrobial menstrual cups by incorporating a natural extract, R. tomentosa, traditionally used as medicine in Southeast Asian countries. To produce antimicrobial biomaterials, a common technique is the coating method, in which materials are immersed in an antimicrobial solution. This approach is favored for its simplicity and time efficiency. Other strategies include using hydrogels or intermediate polymers to retain antimicrobial agents on material surfaces30,34, as well as directly diffusing antimicrobial compounds into the substrates35. However, these methods are more suitable for disposable products. Thus, incorporating antimicrobial compounds into materials by mixing prior to compression is the most appropriate method for developing reusable antimicrobial materials. FTIR analysis showed no difference in peaks between unloaded and RT-loaded silicone. A distinct peak of R. tomentosa extract was not detected in the loaded sample. Both surface roughness and hydrophobicity of RT-loaded silicone remained nearly identical to those of the unloaded control. These results suggest that the loading procedure did not alter surface properties. The reduction in surface wettability after incubation in simulated vagina fluid was likely attributed to the adsorption of components present in the simulated fluid, such as amino acids, glycerol, glucose, and red blood cells. These molecules could adhere to the material surface, altering its chemical composition and topography. Previous studies have reported that several factors, including amino acids and proteins present in biological fluids, can lead to changes in the hydrophobic properties of material surfaces36. Coating techniques can alter surface topology, as compound aggregation on surfaces increases roughness34,37. It has been reported that smooth surfaces have stronger adhesion at the contact area because of higher friction than rougher surfaces38,39. Conversely, rough surfaces provide a greater area for microbial attachment40. When comparing roughness at different magnitudes, low to intermediate average roughness combined with high concentrations of antimicrobial agents showed pronounced inhibition of bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation41. Moreover, the high roughness of the material surface can also shield microorganisms from removal by shear forces42.

The antimicrobial efficacy of Rhodomyrtus tomentosa-loaded silicone was investigated by assessing its ability to inhibit planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion of representative microorganisms found in the female reproductive tract including S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and C. albicans. Synergistic interactions of S. aureus and C. albicans were reported in several studies. An increase in S. aureus proliferation, biofilm formation, pathogenicity, and drug resistance was observed in dual-species cultures of S. aureus and C. albicans43. During coinfection, S. aureus can adhere to C. albicans promoting bacterial dissemination through immune cells44. Moreover, prostaglandin E2 produced by C. albicans can stimulate S. aureus growth and biofilm formation in dual cultures of S. aureus and C. albicans biofilms45. Accordingly, antimicrobial activity was observed against both single- and dual-species cultures. Rhodomyrtus tomentosa-loaded silicone demonstrated significant anti-planktonic growth and antimicrobial adhesion effects against all tested microorganisms in both single- and dual-species cultures.

Antimicrobial efficacy of RT-loaded silicone was evaluated at incubation times of 1, 3, 6, 8, and 12 h. The time points were selected to reflect the typical duration of menstrual cup use, which generally ranges from 3 to 6 h. Shorter time points were included to assess the onset and early kinetics of antimicrobial activity, while longer time points were chosen to evaluate sustained efficacy over the period the cup would realistically be worn. The strong antimicrobial activity of the material may be attributed to the slow release of antimicrobial compounds from the loaded silicone, leading to gradual microbial killing. Notably, even after 1 h of incubation, an approximately 99% reduction (2 log CFU/mL) of the tested bacteria was observed, compared with unloaded materials. Longer incubation was required to eliminate fungal cells, which are eukaryotic microorganisms with more complex structures than bacteria. The antimicrobial effect of the extract is linked to rhodomyrtone46, a bioactive compound that kills bacteria by targeting the cell membrane, causing leakage of adenosine triphosphate and cytoplasmic proteins, and ultimately inducing cell death47,48. The mechanisms of rhodomyrtone have been identified as novel antibacterial strategies: it can transiently bind to phospholipid head groups, distort lipid packing, and generate protein-trapping membrane vesicles29. Additionally, phenolic-rich extracts of R. tomentosa have been reported to alleviate high-fat diet–induced gut dysbiosis by promoting the growth of Lactobacillus species49. Thus, it is postulated that the developed cup containing R. tomentosa extract may also support the growth of vaginal flora. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the effects of the R. tomentosa-loaded cup on vaginal microbiota.

To investigate the antimicrobial effects in a more relevant environment, antimicrobial activity was further investigated in simulated vaginal fluid containing red blood cells. Several factors in the vaginal environment may limit the efficacy of drugs and antimicrobial compounds. It has been reported that the delivery of prophylactic contraceptive compounds can be affected the osmolarity and pH of the surrounding vaginal fluid50. The mixture of fluids in the vagina may include cervical mucus and semen which can impact drug retention and release51. Therefore, to investigate whether the antimicrobial activity was affected by the vaginal environment, simulated vaginal fluid was prepared as previously described52. The results demonstrated that even under conditions closely reflecting the microenvironment during menstruation, the novel antimicrobial menstrual silicone remained effective.

Antibacterial stability was evaluated to determine whether R. tomentosa-loaded silicone maintains its activity after boiling, the most effective method for eliminating residues and microorganisms, as recommended by multiple guidelines. However, other washing methods, such as cleaning with cold water followed by warm water or using soap followed by tap water, should also be included in future studies. The results suggested that RT-loaded silicone may lose some of its active agent during the boiling process. Nevertheless, even after twelve boiling cycles, the antimicrobial-loaded material remained active, inhibiting S. aureus growth and adhesion by more than 90%, compared with the unloaded control.

Biocompatibility testing is an important process to evaluate the safety of novel biomaterials. The cytotoxicity was assessed on fibroblast cells following the guidelines for in vitro biocompatibility testing of medical devices (10993-5:2009). Fibroblast cells are a type of connective tissue cell that play a crucial role in maintaining the structure and function of various tissues throughout the body, including the vagina. As expected, the results demonstrated that RT-loaded silicone was safe, with cell viability at approximately 100%, compared with both unloaded silicone and untreated cells. Moreover, cytotoxicity of rhodomyrtone, the bioactive compound isolated from R. tomentosa has been reported. There were no toxic effects of the compound in every tested model including human erythrocytes, Galleria mellonella larvae, zebrafish embryo, and mouse model53. Accordingly, it was confirmed that R. tomentosa is safe to apply in consumer products intended for human use.

Conclusions

In conclusion, these promising findings suggest that the novel antimicrobial silicone loaded with R. tomentosa leaf extract is effective against all tested pathogens under various culture conditions, with stable activity and proven safety. It may serve as an alternative menstrual product for managing menstruation, with the potential to reduce the risk of aerobic vaginitis and candidiasis associated with menstrual cup use.
