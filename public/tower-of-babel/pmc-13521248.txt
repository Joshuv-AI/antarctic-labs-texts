CHRONIC ACTINOMYCES NEUII ENDOPHTHALMITIS POSTCATARACT SURGERY TREATED WITH INTRAVENOUS AND INTRAMUSCULAR ANTIBIOTICS AND INTRAOCULAR LENS EXPLANTATION: CASE REPORT AND REVIEW OF THE LITERATURE
Authors: Carol Tadrous, Nadine Cheffi, Rishi B. Gupta, Jonathan B. Angel, Bernard R. Hurley, Chloe C. Gottlieb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic postoperative endophthalmitis is a rare complication of cataract surgery. This case of recalcitrant Actinomyces neuii chronic postoperative endophthalmitis was successfully treated with prolonged IV and intramuscular antibiotics alongside intraocular lens explantation. This report highlights the importance of interdisciplinary management and systemic antibiotic therapy in persistent chronic postoperative endophthalmitis caused by unusual pathogens.

Endophthalmitis is a serious and dreaded but rare complication of cataract surgery. It results from the introduction of foreign organisms during or after the procedure, resulting in an infectious inflammation of intraocular fluids. Chronic postoperative endophthalmitis (CPE) is defined by symptom onset greater than 6 weeks after surgery. Unlike acute presentations, CPE is often caused by indolent organisms capable of persisting within the capsular bag and forming biofilm on intraocular lens (IOL) surfaces. In this paper, we are reporting a recalcitrant case of CPE secondary to the rare pathogen, Actinomyces neuii (A. neuii), which was comanaged by ophthalmology and infectious disease, using a combination of intravitreal, IV, intramuscular antibiotics, and intraocular lens (IOL) explantation. We report a microbiologically confirmed case of CPE caused by A. neuii requiring prolonged systemic penicillin therapy and IOL explantation, with objective confirmation of therapeutic intravitreal penicillin levels.

Case

A 77-year-old man underwent routine and uncomplicated cataract surgery with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation in the left eye in July 2016. He had uncomplicated cataract surgery in the right eye 1 month ago. He had no past ocular history, and his medical history was significant for controlled hypertension. Three months after his surgery, he presented with reduced vision, eye redness, pain, and he was found to have anterior uveitis in his left eye. He was treated with topical corticosteroids with some resolution. He then developed an epiretinal membrane (ERM) and underwent a routine pars plana vitrectomy with membrane peel in April 2017.

In August 2017, 4 months after the vitrectomy was performed, he was found to have recurrent anterior uveitis and cystoid macular edema in the left eye. He was treated with topical corticosteroids and topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs without resolution of the uveitis or the macular edema. He then underwent a vitreous tap and was empirically treated with intravitreal injections of ceftazidime 2.25 mg/0.1 mL and vancomycin 1 mg/0.1 mL, in addition to topical moxifloxacin. The vitreous culture was positive for A. neuii. He had resolution of uveitis but unfortunately presented 3 months later in December 2017 with a recurrence of uveitis. A repeat vitreous tap was obtained, and although negative on preliminary reporting, it ultimately grew A. neuii after 1 week. He had repeat intravitreal injections of ceftazidime and vancomycin.

Between December 2017 and December 2019, he had multiple rebounds of anterior uveitis and cystoid macular edema (Figure 1B) with fluctuating vision. Each episode was treated with topical corticosteroids with some response to treatment each time. He developed elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) because of steroid response and was started on IOP lowering eyedrops.

Findings

In December 2019, he was referred to the uveitis service at a tertiary care center. On examination at that time, vision in the affected left eye was hand motion. Slit lamp examination revealed granulomatous keratic precipitates, 3+ anterior chamber cells, and 2+ flare. His IOL implant was found to be in good position, without any opacity in the capsular bag. There was note of 1+ vitreous haze. He had cystoid macular edema with a secondary ERM (Figure 2B).

OCT of the right eye (A) and left eye (B), January 2020. Left eye macula showing cystoid macular edema with a secondary epiretinal membrane.

He received weekly intravitreal ceftazidime injections for 4 weeks and oral moxifloxacin. A targeted uveitis workup was obtained and was negative for syphilis, tuberculosis, sarcoid investigations, and HLA-B27. His complete blood count, electrolytes, liver, and kidney function testing were within normal limits. Ultrasound biomicroscopy was obtained and was found to be noncontributory. He was referred to a vitreoretinal surgeon for diagnostic vitrectomy. The diagnostic vitrectomy culture was positive for A. neuii. On cytologic assessment, there was no evidence of a lymphoproliferative process.

Owing to the chronic course of his disease and lack of sustained response to treatment, a decision was made to refer him to an infectious disease consultant. The infectious disease physician recommended a loading course of intravitreal ampicillin, followed by an extended 6-month course of IV penicillin G in addition to considering removal of the IOL to potentially cure the infection. A peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) line was placed for administration of IV penicillin G, four million units every 4 hours for a total dose of 24 million units per day. Subsequently, he underwent a second diagnostic vitrectomy this time combined with IOL explantation. At the time of lensectomy, vitreous sampling obtained 4 hours after IV dosing demonstrated a penicillin G concentration of 3.5 µg/mL. The cultures of the vitreous and lens were both negative for A. neuii.

One month after PICC line insertion and treatment with IV penicillin G (February 2020), the patient could no longer tolerate the PICC line because of interference with activities of daily living, more difficult control of his hypertension, and fatigue. The PICC line was removed, and the patient was observed closely. He unfortunately developed a mild recurrence of anterior uveitis with examination findings of trace cell and no flare. A repeat vitreous tap was obtained, which was negative for A. neuii. This uveitic flare was therefore deemed to be postoperative inflammation and resolved with a short course of topical corticosteroids.

In May 2020, 3 months after cessation of topical steroids and systemic antibiotics, he developed an additional recurrence of uveitis. The infectious disease specialist started intramuscular (IM) benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units every 2 weeks for a 6-month course as an alternative to IV antibiotics requiring PICC line insertion.

One year later, he maintained quiescence of uveitis and underwent a secondary iris-fixated IOL implant. He had a routine postoperative course. Approximately 2 years later in November of 2022, the most recently documented visit, his final best corrected VA was 20/500. He had no evidence of intraocular inflammation. His reduced vision is attributed to his marked macular thinning secondary to chronic macular edema, as presented in Figure 3B.

Discussion

Actinomyces species are Gram-positive, nonspore forming, mostly anaerobic bacilli that reside in human mucosal tissue, such as oral mucosa and the gastrointestinal tract, and have been implicated in systemic granulomatous infections.1 Actinomyces spp. are typically highly susceptible to beta-lactam antibiotics, particularly penicillin G, which remains the treatment of choice for systemic actinomycosis.1

A PubMed literature search (keywords “Actinomyces” and “endophthalmitis”) yielded 12 cases of this pathogen causing endophthalmitis. We have outlined the details of this literature review in Table 1, with related and comparative data pertaining to A. species, type of endophthalmitis, clinical presentation, treatments, initial VA, and final VA.2–10 The previously reported cases demonstrate considerable heterogeneity in both clinical presentation and management strategies, where chronic postoperative presentations have demonstrated a more indolent and refractory course. Raman et al8 reported a case of A. neuii CPE six weeks after cataract surgery, treated with oral and topical antibiotics followed by vitrectomy, ultimately achieving VA of 20/60. Roussel et al10 described multiple cases of Actinomyces-associated postoperative endophthalmitis, including one chronic case due to Actinomyces israelii that required repeated vitrectomies and intraocular lens removal after recurrence of inflammation, yet visual outcome remained limited. These cases illustrate the challenges inherent to chronic presentations, in which inflammation may temporarily respond to therapy but subsequently recur.

A notable finding across the literature is the relative short duration of systemic antibiotic therapy. Although some reports describe brief courses of oral or IV antibiotics (e.g., Roussel et al10 administered a 5-day course of IV penicillin followed by oral therapy in one case), prolonged systemic treatment extending several months was not reported. Furthermore, explicit involvement of infectious disease specialists was not routinely documented. Management decisions in previous reports seem to have been driven primarily by ophthalmologic intervention, focusing on intravitreal antibiotics and surgical approaches.

We report on a case of A. neuii CPE, which produced a diagnostic challenge, because of the indolent and initially low-grade recurring inflammatory activity and proved resistant to local antibiotic therapy. This case highlights the importance of consulting with infectious disease specialists with recommendation for IOL explantation, because their principle for infections involving prosthetic materials is definitive source control. The infection was refractory to the most common treatments previously described in the literature, including repeated intravitreal injections, vitrectomies, oral, and intravitreal antibiotics. Our patient required three injections of intravitreal ampicillin 5 mg/0.1 mL, a 4-week course of IV penicillin G, four million units every 4 hours, by a 6-month course of biweekly IM benzathine penicillin G, 2.4 million unit injections (14 total injections), in addition to IOL explantation, to achieve complete resolution of his A. neuii endophthalmitis. The initiation of loading dose intravitreal antibiotics, IOL explantation, and IV antibiotics was accomplished within one week.

The Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS) did not demonstrate benefit of adjunctive systemic antibiotics in acute postoperative infections; however, chronic biofilm-associated infections were not represented in that cohort. Therefore, those findings cannot be directly extrapolated to indolent CPE. Given the ongoing debate regarding systemic antibiotic penetration into the eye, we quantified the intravitreal concentration of penicillin G at the time of lensectomy. A vitreous sample obtained 4 hours after IV administration demonstrated a penicillin G concentration of 3.5 µg/mL. Published minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for A. species range from 0.063 to 2 µg/mL6, indicating that therapeutic levels exceeding the upper MIC threshold were achieved intraocularly.

To the best of our knowledge, objective confirmation of therapeutic intravitreal beta-lactam concentrations in chronic postoperative endophthalmitis has not previously been reported. This finding supports the biological plausibility of prolonged systemic penicillin therapy in indolent Actinomyces infections and provides pharmacokinetic evidence complementing surgical source control.

Chronic postoperative endophthalmitis is frequently associated with sequestration of organisms within the capsular bag and biofilm formation on intraocular lens surfaces. Indolent organisms such as Actinomyces are particularly capable of forming adherent colonies protected from host immune response and intravitreal antibiotic exposure. Chronic infections are frequently misdiagnosed as sterile uveitis, leading to prolonged corticosteroid exposure and delayed source control. Previous management algorithms for CPE have emphasized capsular bag removal and IOL explantation when inflammation recurs despite intravitreal therapy. Although IOL removal has been reported in refractory Actinomyces cases, such as those described by Roussel et al,10 it has generally been performed after multiple relapses and without standardized prolonged systemic antimicrobial therapy.

In our patient, definitive source control through IOL explantation was coordinated with extended systemic penicillin therapy. The combined surgical and pharmacologic strategy resulted in durable quiescence, supporting a multimodal approach in recalcitrant chronic cases.

In this case, the diagnosis and management were primarily guided by microbiologic confirmation rather than imaging features. Multimodal imaging such as anterior segment photography or fluorescein angiography may further characterize chronic postoperative endophthalmitis; however, such imaging was not consistently available in this prolonged case, reflecting real-world diagnostic challenges in indolent presentations.

Overall, this case highlights several practical considerations: persistent or recurrent postoperative inflammation should raise suspicion for CPE; repeated recurrence despite intravitreal therapy warrants reconsideration of the treatment strategy; early interdisciplinary collaboration with infectious disease specialists may assist in tailoring systemic antimicrobial therapy; and IOL explantation should be considered when inflammation persists, particularly with indolent organisms, for definitive eradication.

This report is the first to highlight the use of intravitreal and IV/IM antibiotics along with IOL explantation to achieve resolution of chronic postoperative exogenous endophthalmitis secondary to A. neuii.

Conclusion

Chronic postoperative endophthalmitis due to A. neuii may present with recurrent inflammation despite repeated intravitreal therapy. Our patient required intravitreal antibiotics, an extended course of IV and IM antibiotics and IOL explantation, demonstrating that systemic penicillin can achieve therapeutic intravitreal concentrations and that durable eradication may require both prolonged organism-directed systemic therapy and immediate source control. This case highlights that persistent postoperative inflammation should prompt early microbiologic evaluation and consideration of multimodal management in indolent infections.
