Limitations of PIV Biomarker Analysis Related to Chemoradiotherapy and Unadjusted Confounders
Authors: Hongbo Gao, Wencai Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pan‐immune‐inflammation value (PIV) derived from four blood cell counts warrants cautious interpretation in rectal‐cancer immunotherapy, as CRT cannot be disentangled from anti‐PD‐1 effects within the small n = 16 subgroup.

Dear Editor,

We read Wang et al.'s work on pan‐immune‐inflammation value (PIV) in rectal cancer with great interest [1], yet several critical confounding and methodological gaps require clarification (Figure 1).

The pan‐immune‐inflammation value (PIV) derived from four blood cell counts warrants cautious interpretation in rectal cancer immunotherapy, as CRT cannot be disentangled from anti‐PD‐1 effects within the small n = 16 subgroup.

First, chemotherapy and radiotherapy are well‐known confounders that remodel neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts to alter PIV. The primary two cohorts strictly excluded all patients with prior chemo/radiotherapy, limiting conclusions solely to treatment‐naïve cases, while most locally advanced rectal patients receive neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Only 16 patients received anti‐PD‐1 plus CRT for immunotherapy prediction; as all 16 patients in this immunotherapy cohort received CRT combined with anti‐PD‐1 therapy, CRT exposure cannot be statistically adjusted as a covariate within this subgroup. Instead, the core interpretive limitation is that the study cannot distinguish whether PIV specifically predicts response to PD‐1 blockade or response to the combined neoadjuvant CRT plus anti‐PD‐1 regimen. The paper barely discusses this major confounding bias of CRT, greatly narrowing clinical generalizability.

Second, other unadjusted confounders weaken reliability: chronic inflammatory comorbidities and MSI/MMR status were not incorporated into Cox/logistic regressions. MSI status drives distinct tumor immune landscapes and immunotherapy responses, which may confound the PIV‐immune correlation observed. Besides, the study only used one‐time preoperative blood draws without standardizing sampling conditions, introducing transient measurement bias from acute stress or mild infection. Notably, extensive multivariable adjustment for these factors may not be statistically feasible in the small immunotherapy cohort (n = 16), and these confounders should be acknowledged as limitations to support more cautious interpretation of findings in this subgroup.

Third, the authors claimed PIV outperforms NLR/SII but lacked head‐to‐head multivariate comparisons to validate its superiority. When linking low PIV to abundant CD8+ T cells and mature TLS, tumor burden covariates were not adjusted, possibly overstating direct associations.

We recommend that the confounding effect of CRT on PIV and immunotherapy response be explicitly discussed as a key limitation, with cautious interpretation of PIV's predictive value restricted to the combined neoadjuvant CRT plus anti‐PD‐1 regimen rather than PD‐1 blockade alone (Figure 1). We also suggest integrating MSI status and chronic inflammatory comorbidities into regression models where statistically feasible (e.g., in the larger treatment‐naïve cohorts), and supplementing head‐to‐head multivariate comparisons with traditional inflammatory markers to strengthen the clinical value of this promising biomarker.
