Targeting prostaglandin catabolism via topical nanotherapy rescues vision in ischemic optic neuropathy
Authors: Shweta Modgil, RuSiou Hsu, Mohammad Ali Shariati, Juan A Oses-Prieto, Al Burlingame, Yaping Joyce Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic optic neuropathy is a leading cause of acute vision loss and currently lacks effective therapy. Here, we identify 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH), the key enzyme responsible for prostaglandin degradation, as a metabolic checkpoint in ischemic optic neurodegeneration. Analysis of human ischemic optic nerve tissue and a murine model of optic nerve ischemia reveals marked upregulation of retinal 15-PGDH, suggesting dysregulated prostaglandin homeostasis after ischemic injury. To therapeutically target this pathway, we develop a nano-micellar formulation of the hydrophobic 15-PGDH inhibitor SW033291 (SW@NM) that enables efficient topical ocular delivery and retinal penetration. Topical administration of SW@NM suppresses retinal 15-PGDH activity, restores PGE2 levels, preserves retinal ganglion cells and optic nerve axons, and significantly improves visual function after ischemic injury. Mechanistically, 15-PGDH inhibition suppresses ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation and preserves mitochondrial integrity, thereby mitigating ischemia-induced neurodegeneration. Collectively, these findings establish 15-PGDH–mediated prostaglandin catabolism as a therapeutic target in ischemic optic neuropathy and demonstrate a clinically translatable nanotherapeutic strategy for protecting the central nervous system from ischemic neurodegeneration.Keywords: 15-PGDH, Nano-micelle, Neuroprotection, Optic neuropathy, SW033291, AION

Introduction

Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a bioactive lipid mediator traditionally viewed as a pro-inflammatory molecule but now increasingly recognized as a context-dependent regulator of tissue repair and regeneration [1,2]. [3,4] In models of ischemic injury, PGE2 signaling promotes tissue preservation and neuroprotection [5,6]. However, direct therapeutic administration of PGE2 has failed to translate clinically due to its rapid degradation and short half-life [7].

15-Hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH), the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for PGE2 catabolism, functions as a metabolic checkpoint controlling endogenous PGE2 bioavailability. Inhibition of 15-PGDH represents a strategy to amplify physiologic PGE2 signaling without exogenous prostaglandin administration. The small-molecule inhibitor SW033291 (SW) is a high-affinity 15-PGDH inhibitor with favorable pharmacokinetics and demonstrated ability to elevate tissue PGE2 levels in vivo [8,9]. Systemic SW treatment has shown regenerative benefits across multiple organ systems [10], including hematopoietic recovery [8], muscle rejuvenation [11], peripheral nerve regeneration [12]. Despite these promising findings, the role of 15-PGDH inhibition in central nervous system (CNS) ischemia remains insufficiently explored, particularly in the retina. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of SW limits its clinical applicability, especially for localized ocular delivery [9].

Ischemic optic neuropathy, specifically non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (hereby refered as AION), represents a common acute ischemic injury of the optic nerve and serves as a clinically relevant model of CNS ischemia. AION affects predominantly individuals over 50 years of age, with an annual incidence of approximately 10.2 per 100 000 in the United States [13,14] increasing substantially in high-risk populations such as patients with diabetes [15]. Additional risk factors include hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, sleep apnea, disc-at-risk anatomy, and cataract surgery [16]. Recent reports have also described an association between semaglutide [17] use and increased risk of AION, raising important public health concerns as GLP-1 receptor agonists become widely prescribed for diabetes and obesity management. With aging populations and increasing metabolic disease prevalence, the burden of AION and related ischemic optic neuropathies is expected to rise.

AION typically presents as sudden, painless vision loss that is often irreversible. The fellow eye becomes affected in approximately 15% of patients within five years, with a lifetime risk of ∼21% [18,19]. Since its first description in 1966, extensive research is focused on the topic, but no effective treatment exists. Aspirin remains controversial [20,21], and other interventions—including corticosteroids, anti-VEGF agents, brimonidine, erythropoietin, endothelin antagonists, and stem cell–based approaches—have yielded limited or inconsistent benefit [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Effective treatment for AION is one of the most important unmet needs in neuro-ophthalmology.

Given the emerging regenerative and cytoprotective roles of PGE2, we hypothesized that 15-PGDH functions as a metabolic brake in ischemic neurodegeneration and that its inhibition could enhance endogenous pro-survival signaling. To overcome the solubility limitations of SW and enable clinically feasible ocular delivery, we engineered a nano-micellar formulation using the amphiphilic polymer DSPE-PEG2000 to encapsulate SW (SW@NM). Recent advances in nanotechnology, particularly in drug delivery systems, offer new opportunities for improving drug delivery and enhancing targeted ocular therapies. Nano-micelle–based topical therapies have demonstrated clinical translatability in ophthalmology, as exemplified by FDA-approved micellar formulations such as Azasite® and Cequa® [27,28]. Topical delivery offers substantial advantages, including non-invasiveness, compartmental accessibility, reduced systemic exposure, and suitability for chronic administration.

Using a murine model of anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, we investigated whether topical nano-micellar inhibition of 15-PGDH could preserve retinal ganglion cells, modulate ferroptosis-associated pathways, and improve visual outcomes. By positioning the retina as a tractable CNS ischemia model, this study explores 15-PGDH as a metabolic checkpoint in neurodegeneration and proposes nano-formulated 15-PGDH inhibition as a broadly applicable therapeutic strategy for ischemic CNS disorders beyond the eye.

Materials and Methods

Material

SW033291 was obtained from ApexBio Technology, #A8709.1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N- [methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000) and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N [FITC (polyethylene glycol)-2000](DSPE-PEG2000-FITC) were obtained from Broadpharm, USA. Tetrahydrofuran was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and all other reagents were analytic grade.

Preparation of SW033291-loaded nanomicelles (SW@NM)

To prepare the SW033291-loaded nanomicelles (SW@NM), the SW033291 was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF) at a concentration of 25 mg/mL. 10 mg DSPE-PEG2000 was dissolved in 2mL THF in a 20 mL glass vial. 40 μl of prepared SW033291 solution was added to the DSPE-PEG2000 and stirred for 3 h. Organic solvent was allowed to evaporate inside a fume hood for overnight. Next day, the vial was kept in 60 °C oven for 6 h. Then 3 mL water were added to the glass vial, and the glass vial was sonicated until the nanomicelles were fully redisperse to form a yellow color transparent solution. For FITC tagged SW@NM (SW@NM- FITC), DSPE-PEG2000-FITC was used in place of DSPE-PEG2000, rest protocol remain same.

Preparation of FITC-loaded nanomicelles (FITC@NM)

To prepare the FITC-loaded nanomicelles (FITC@NM), FITC was dissolved in THF at a concentration of 25 mg/mL. 10 mg DSPE-PEG2000 was dissolved in 2 mL THF in a 20 mL glass vial. 40 μl of FITC-THF solution was added to the DSPE-PEG2000 solution. Rest steps were similar as for SW@NM as mentioned above. The release of FITC (as a model drug SW033291) from FITC@NMs was performed with dialysis membrane tubes (molecular weight cut-off 3 KDa). First, the nanomicelle solution was dispersed in PBS. Then, nanomicelle solution was transferred to each dialysis membrane tube. The tubes were put in a container filled with artificial tears and stirred at 37 °C for 12 h. At 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9 and 12 h time points, three tubes were collected and the residual FITC in each tube was measured using the UV-vis method for analysis. The released FITC was calculated using the initial fed FITC minus the residual FITC in each tube.

Characterization of the nanomicelles

Morphology and size characterizations of the nanomicelles were conducted using low-acceleration-voltage Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7700, Hitachi). 5 μL of each micelle solution was dropped onto a carbon film-supported copper grid. After the samples were half-air dried, 1 % (wt/vol) uranyl acetate was used to stain the samples for 5–10 min. The excess staining reagent was removed and air-dried before TEM observation. The zeta potential and size distribution of the nanomicelles were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Nano-ZS, Malvern).

Stability studies

The physical stability of SW@NM solution (water solution) was evaluated after storage for 3 weeks. The particle size was analyzed after different storage time (1, 4, 7 days) at 4 °C).

Cell viability assay

5∗106 SHSY5Y neuroblastoma cells were grown in 24 well culture plates and differentiated into neurons as previously described [29]. The cells at passage 5 to 7, were treated with different concentration of SW@NM added to media. 24 h post-treatment the media is replaced with serum-free media and incubated for 3 h. After incubation, MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and kept again the incubator for 3 h to form purple formazan crystals. 100 μl of the HCL is added into each well and incubated to solubilize purple crystals. The spectrophotometrical absorbance of the samples at 570 nm is recorded. For calculations, the average absorbance values of wells without cells are subtracted from the absorbance values of wells with cells. Then, the percentage of cell viability is calculated as: % Viability = Mean OD sample/Mean OD control (wells without drug) × 100.

Animals and human tissue

The human tissue specimen used in this study was obtained through the Stanford Autopsy Program under an autopsy consent that includes provisions permitting the use of tissue specimens for research purposes, including immunohistochemistry (IHC). Adult male wild-type C57BL/J6 mice were procured from Charles River Laboratories, Inc. (Wilmington, MA, USA). All procedures except for behavioral tests were performed under sedition. Animals were anesthetized using a cocktail of Ketamine 100 mg/kg (Hospira, Inc., Lake Forest, IL, USA) and xylazine 10 mg/kg (Bedford Laboratories, Bedford, OH, USA). The pupils of anesthetized mice were dilated with 1% tropicamide (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA) and 2.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride eye drops (Akorn, Inc., Lake Forest, IL, USA). Custom-made contact lenses achieved lubrication of the eyes for prolonged imaging.

Induction of AION using photochemical thrombosis

AION was induced by photochemical thrombosis as previously standardized in the lab [30,31]. Briefly, 50 μL of rose bengal (1.25 mM) was injected retro-orbitally and the optic disc was exposed to laser light (400 μm diameter spot size, 50 mW, 1 s duration, 15 spots) using frequency-doubled 532 nm Nd:YAG laser (PASCAL, OptiMedica, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

In vitro cell uptake assay and in-vivo evaluation of drug delivery

5 × 106 SHSY5Y cells grown per well in 24 well plates were subjected to different concentrations of SW@NM-FITC for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed with 1XPBS and fresh media was added. Cells were imaged to determine the FITC fluorescence. For assessment of in vivo drug delivery, 5 μL of NM@SW-FITC or 5 μL of PBS was applied topically on adult control mice eyes. The mice were sacrificed at 0.5, 1, and 4 h after instillation. The eyes were sectioned and screened for FITC fluorescence as an indicator of the presence of SW@NM.

Quantitation of SW033291 in mouse retina by LC-MS/MS

Individual analyte and internal standard primary stock solutions (10 mM) were prepared in DMSO. SW033291 stock solution was serially diluted with acetonitrile/water (1:1 v/v) buffer to obtain a series of standard working solutions (1–4000 ng/mL). Calibration curves were prepared by spiking 5 μL of each of the standard working solutions into 25 μL of homogenization solution (acetone/water 1:1, 0.005% BHT) followed by the addition of 5 μL internal standard solution of (Z)-4-hydroxytamoxifen (4OHT) (800 ng/mL).

The retina was harvested, and snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen. It was combined with a 200 μL homogenization buffer, sonicated and kept frozen at −80 °C until analysis. Before LC-MS/MS, samples were vortexed, sonicated for 2 min, and centrifuged for 10 min at 10 000 rpm at 4 °C. 5 μL internal standard solution (800 ng/mL) was added to 25 μL aliquot of supernatant followed by vortexing. 150 μL ice cold solution of 1% acetic acid in methanol was added to the sample, vortexed, and then samples were placed at −20 °C for an hour to facilitate protein precipitation. After centrifugation, 20 μL of supernatant was transferred to a new vial, diluted with 80 μL methanol/water 1:1, 0.1% formic acid, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

All analyses were carried out by positive electrospray LC-MS/MS using a Waters Acquity I-class UPLC system with Waters Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (RRID:SCR_018 510). Chromatographic conditions: an Acquity UPLC® BEH C18 2.1 × 50 mm 1.7 μm particle size column (Waters Corp., part number 186002352) was operated at 40 °C at a flow rate 0.25 mL/min. Mobile phases consisted of A: 0.2% formic in water and B: 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile. Elution profile: initial hold at 45% B for 1 min, followed by a linear gradient of 45%–65% B in 2 min, then linear gradient of 65%–95% B in 3 min and hold at 95% B for 2 min, equilibrate back to 45% B; total run time was 10 min. Injection volume was 10 μL. Quantification was done using selected reaction monitoring (SRM) and an internal standard approach with Target Lynx quantification software (Waters Corp.).

Western blot assay of 15-PGDH

After extraction and quantification, equal amount of protein was subjected to electrophoresis on an SDS-PAGE, then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane and blocked in 5% nonfat dried milk. The membrane was overnight incubated with primary antibody (Table S1) at 4 °C, washed next day and incubated with appropriate secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. The bands were detected by ECL-based chemiluminescence, quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software and normalized to the β-actin.

ELISA of PGE2

Retinae were harvested, rinsed in ice-cold PBS containing indomethacin (5.6 μg/mL), and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Retina was homogenized in 0.1 mL of ice-cold PBS with indomethacin. The suspension was sonicated for 3 min using cycles of 20 s of sonication with 20 s of cooling, followed by centrifugation for 10 min at 12 000 rpm. The PGE2 level of the supernatant was measured using a PGE2 ELISA Kit as per the manufacturer's protocol (Cayman Chemical, #514010) and normalized to protein concentration measured by BCA assay.

Serial OCT examination

Spectral-domain OCT Spectralis HRA + OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, GmbH, Germany) was used to assess changes of retinal thickness at different time points. To measure the ganglion cell complex (GCC), we performed circular scan (12°, also known as the retinal nerve fiber layer or “RNFL” scan) around optic nerve head , and images were manually segmented on the Spectralis to quantify GCC thickness while masked.

Optomotor response evaluation in mice

Visual acuity in mice was measured using optometry (Cerebral Mechanics. Inc., Lethbridge, Alberta, Canada) as previously described [32].

Electroretinography

Animals were overnight dark-adapted and were anesthetized the next day before recording. Pupils dilated with 1% tropicamide ophthalmic solution (Akorn, Inc., Lake Forest, IL), followed by application of 1–2 drops of proparacaine hydrochloride (0.5% ophthalmic solution, Akorn, Inc.). A layer of Optixcare gel was applied to the cornea making a thin layer between the cornea and stimulators. PhNR was recorded using Celeris ERG system (Diagnosys, Lowel, MA) as per in-built celeris protocol.

Pattern ERG (PERG) was recorded at 100% contrast using Miami PERG system (Jorvec) as previously described by Li et al., 2020 [33]. Positive electrode was inserted between the eyes, negative between the ears and the ground electrode was placed subcutaneously in the tail. Eyes were dilated and visual stimuli were presented at each eye independently from 10 cm distance and consisted of contrast-reversal of gratings (0.052 cycles/deg, 85% contrast) generated on two light-emitting diode (LED) tablets (154 × 154 cm square field, 800 cd/m2 mean luminance) alternating at slightly different frequencies around 1 Hz (OD, 1.016 Hz; OS, 1.008 Hz. Two recordings per animal with 100 sweeps and 300 sweeps were recorded and averaged to get the final measurements. PERG P1–N2 amplitude were used to determine the functional activity of RGCs.

RGC cell count and Axonal Count

Animals were sacrificed, and eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h and thereafter washed with 1XPBS. The eye was dissected along the limbus, and the anterior portion containing the lens and vitreous was discarded. The eyecup with the retina was flattened by making radial cuts. The wholemount was blocked with blocking buffer (10% goat serum, 5% BSA, 0.5% tritonX-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Overnight incubation at 4 °C in primary antibody (anti-Brn3a; # sc-8429, Santa Cruz) following washing (1X PBS-0.1% Tween 20) and incubation with secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse) for 2 h at room temperature (RT) in the dark was done. The retina was washed with PBS and mounted in Vectashield mounting medium.

For retinal whole mounts, images were photographed from 3 different regions from each retinal quadrant using a fluoview3000 fluorescent microscope (Olympus, Ltd., USA). RGCs were counted in Image J using the RGC plug-in as described earlier [34]. The experimenter was blinded to the treatment group and retinal region during the counting of total numbers of Brn3a+ cells. The mean number of RGCs/image in central retina was calculated from a mean count at each of the center and middle regions of interest (ROI) while for peripheral RGC count, mean of periphery ROIs was considered.

For optic nerve axon count, optic nerve was fixed in PFA, washed and transverse semi-thin epoxy (1 μm) sections of ON were cut on an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were stained with 1% PPD in methanol: isopropanol (1:1). Four sections of each ON were imaged at 60X lens using a fluoview3000 fluorescent microscope (Olympus, Ltd., USA). Two areas of 21.4 μm × 29.1 μm were cropped from the center of each image, and the surviving axons within the designated areas were counted manually as described earlier [33].

Immunofluorescence

For Immunofluorescence studies, eyes were fixed in PFA, washed in PBS followed by incubation in 15% and 30% sucrose solution and were cryosectioned (8 μm). For staining, retinal sections were equilibrated to room temperature, hydrated in PBS, permeabilized in 0.1% Triton x-100 in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. The non-specific blocking with buffer (5% bovine serum albumin (g/ml), 0.1% Tween-20, 10% normal goat/donkey serum) for 1 h at room temperature was followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight (Table S1) at 4 °C. The following day, retinal sections were washed for 3 × 5 min in PBS and incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h followed by washing and mounting (Vector Laboratories).

Seahorse assay

Mitochondrial function was assessed in a 24 well plate set up using Seahorse XFe24 Islet Capture FluxPak (#103518–100) in Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer. Briefly, retinal wholemounts were prepared from fresh eyes enucleated after 24 h of SW@NM treatment. 1 mm radial punches (3 punches/eye around ONH) were prepared using a biopsy puncher. The punch were transferred using forceps on the Cell Tak (Corning, #354240) coated mesh insert with ganglion cell layer side touching the mesh. The mesh insert containing retinal punch is loaded into islet capture plate following protocol of Jiang et al., 2021 [35]. For the mitochondrial function test using the Mito stress test kit (Agilent, #103010–100) the protocol developed by Shetty et al., 2021 [36] was followed.

Proteomics of retinal tissue

Retinal tissues were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C. Proteins were extracted using 8 M Guanidine Hydrochloride and quantified by BCA assay. Tryptic digestion was followed by TMTPro™-18 plex labeling (ThermoFisher Scientific) per manufacturer's instructions with minor modifications. High-pH Fractionation and LC-MS/MS Analysis was done.

Peak lists were generated using in-house software (PAVA) [37] and searched against the mouse SwissProt database using Protein Prospector [38]. For detailed protocol and analysis see supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. For multiple comparisons, one- or two-way analysis of variance as appropriate, with Tukey's posthoc test was applied using GraphPad Prism 9. Data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test if it failed the equal variance test. The cut-off for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Elevated 15-PGDH expression in the inner retina post AION

15-PGDH has gained attention in recent years as a negative modulator of prostaglandins. To date however, there are no available reports of spatial expression of 15-PGDH in the retina. We performed immunostaining with an anti-PGDH antibody on human paraffin-embedded retina from a healthy control and a patient premortem clinically evaluated (YJL) and confirmed to have the diagnosis of nonarteritic AION. In the control human retina, 15-PGDH expression was low or absent, whereas robust expression was observed in the AION human retina (Fig. 1A and B). Remarkably, 15-PGDH expression is spatially restricted to the inner plexiform layer (IPL), RGC layer and nerve fiber layer (NFL); together referred to as Ganglion Cell Complex (GCC).

(C) Representative images from mouse retinal sections stained for 15-PGDH (D) The fluorescence intensity was measured using imageJ software and quantified (n = 4 mice/group) (E) Immunoblotting for 15-PGDH in whole retinal extract 1-day post AION (n = 3/group repeated twice). To investigate the expression of 15-PGDH in acute AION, we induced AION in mice using well established photochemical thrombosis model [30,39]. Immunostaining of the post-AION day 1 retina showed upregulation of 15-PGDH in the RGC-NFL layers (Fig. 1C and D) with no involvement of outer nuclear layers (Fig. S1A), phenotyping the AION patient (Fig. 1A). Western blotting of whole retina extract showed elevated 15-PGDH expression (Fig. 1E and F), confirming results of the immunostaining. We performed fundus imaging and optical coherence tomography (OCT) to monitor retinal and optic nerve structure. At 1-day post-AION, there was optic disc swelling and GCC thickening compared with the contralateral eyes (Fig. 1G and H). Robust increase in 15-PGDH and astrocyte reactivity was seen around the ONH (Fig. 1I) which may account for optic disc swelling to some extent. These results show that 15-PGDH is upregulated in the inner retina in response to optic nerve ischemia, raising the possibility of 15-PGDH antagonism as a novel therapeutic approach in AION.

SW@NM enhanced drug solubility and effectively reached the retina

We hypothesized that post ischemic elevation of 15-PGDH in the inner retina drives RGC death and that inhibition of 15-PGDH can promote neuronal survival and functional recovery. SW033291 is a highly hydrophobic molecule known to inhibit 15-PGDH. We developed a new nano-micellar formulation for SW033291 (SW@NMs) with enhanced solubility for topical targeted delivery. SW033291 was encapsulated in nano-micelles using the 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000), a widely used pharmaceutical and medical material with amphipathic nature (Fig. 2A). SW@NM was soluble at various concentrations of SW033291 (Fig. S1B).

The nanoparticles were characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS) which showed zeta (ζ) potentials of −22.95 ± 3.0 mV for SW@NMs indicating long term stability of particles (Fig. 2B). Particle size was confirmed by transmission electron microscopy showing range between 60 and 120 nm (Fig. 2C and D). Further, SW@NMs was stable at 4 °C with uniform size for up to 3 weeks (Fig. 2E). Another aspect for topical delivery is the balance between the drug release from nanocarriers and rapid clearance is vital. If the drug is released too rapidly, toxicity may appear, however, slow drug release may compromise the therapeutic efficacy because of low drug concentration. To observe the release pattern from nanomicelles, we encapsulated fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) inside the nanomicelle (FITC@NM), Fig. S1C), which allows direct visualization of FITC release from nanomicelles. As shown in Figs. 2F 22% of FITC was released from FITC@NM in the first 20 min after topical application, and 58% FITC was released by 1 h. Most of the drug was released (more than 90%) from the nanocarriers within 8 h and plateauing thereafter. These all characteristics of SW@NM made it a good candidate for topical delivery.

To investigate cellular uptake in vitro, we incubated human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y neurons with varying concentrations of SW encapsulated in nanomicelles and tagged with FITC (SW@NW-FITC) (Fig. S1C). Assessment of drug uptake (Fig. S1D) revealed a dose-dependent increase in uptake, with maximum uptake observed at 15 μg/mL or higher concentrations (Fig. S1E and S1F). To visualize in-vivo penetration of nanomicelle into the retina after topical application, SW@NM-FITC was applied to cornea of mice and eyes were collected at different time points to assess fluorescence in the ocular compartment. We found that after topical application, SW@NM-FITC rapidly permeated the corneal epithelia and reached the retinae within 0.5 h. There was no fluorescence signal in the non-treated control eyes (Fig. 2G and H). The fluorescence gradually decreased over time but persisting at low levels for more than 4 h (Fig. S1G). The accumulation and retention of SW@NM-FITC in the posterior segment of the retina indicates the efficacy of nanomicelles as a delivery carrier of SW. Interestingly, the FITC signal was observed not only in the corneal epithelium, but also in conjunctiva and choroid (Fig. S1H). Considering the long distance of the corneal approach to the fundus, we speculated that SW@NM can enter the eye through the conjunctiva-choroid-retina pathway.

To confirm that the nanomicelles reaching the retina can effectively release the drug, we collected retinal tissue 0.5 h after SW@NM topical application and performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS). LC/MS confirmed the presence of SW in the mice retinae treated with SW@NM, whereas SW was undetectable in the eyes of untreated mice (Fig. 2I). Time-dependent collection of mouse retinae after SW@NM administration and LC/MS measurement revealed that the drug was detected in the retina as early as 30 min (Fig. 2J) and gradually began to decline at 1 h but still detectable until 4 h post-administration (Fig. S1I). Importantly, no cytotoxicity was observed in vitro in SH-SY5Y neurons at concentration up to 26.4 μg/mL (Fig. 2K), indicating that SW@NMs is safe for neurons. Together these results confirm that nanomicelles enable efficient delivery and sustained release of SW033291 to the retina without toxicity, providing further support for its use for ocular therapy.

SW@NM inhibited retinal 15-PGDH and sustained PGE2 levels after AION

As shown in section 3.1. , 15-PGDH increases one day after AION (Fig. 1C and D). To assess the impact of topical application of SW@NM, we performed immunoblotting of whole retinal extract collected at day 1 post-AION. Vehicle-treated AION retinae exhibited significantly elevated 15-PGDH levels compared to naïve control. Retinae treated with SW@NM showed a trend toward reduced 15-PGDH levels relative to injury group; however the reduction did not reach a statistical significance compared to injury group. Furthermore, no significant difference was observed between the treatment and control suggesting a partial normalization of 15-PGDH pointing to the effective delivery and functional impact of SW@NM on 15-PGDH inhibition at the posterior pole (Fig. 3A and B).

We also investigated whether 15-PGDH inhibition by SW@NMs leads to elevated retinal PGE2 levels – expected outcome of 15-PGDH antagonism. In untreated AION retinae, there was lower PGE2 levels compared with naïve retinae (p = 0.02 vs control) due to post-ischemic upregulation of 15-PGDH. In contrast, SW@NM-treatment led to significant increase in retinal PGE2 levels (p = 0.003 vs AION-veh) by inhibiting PGE2 degradation (Fig. 3C). The Western blot data was further corroborated by immunostaining of the retina after AION, where eyes treated with SW@NM showed low levels of 15-PGDH (p = 0.33 vs control), in contrast to untreated AION eyes, which exhibited marked increase in 15-PGDH (p = 0.04 vs control) (Fig. 3D and E). Interestingly, the increase in 15-PGDH and corresponding decrease in PGE2 levels were only seen in the acute phase (1 day post-AION), and their levels were back to baseline by day 7 after AION (Fig. S2A–2C). This suggest that the benefit of SW@NM is likely greatest in the acute phase. The gene expression of PGE2 receptors (EP1, EP2, EP3 and EP4) was not altered in the retinae after AION or with SW@NM treatment suggesting neuroprotection by PGE2 is not dependent on canonical pathways activated via EP receptors (Fig. S2D). Together, these findings demonstrate that topical SW@NM treatment in acute AION effectively antagonizes 15-PGDH induction in the post-ischemic retina, which sustained a persistently elevated retinal PGE2 level by preventing its degradation.

SW@NM resolved optic disc edema acutely and preserved retinal thickness chronically

To ask whether 15-PGDH inhibition can rescue RGCs after experimental AION, animals were treated with topical SW@NM or vehicle (nano-micelle without SW) once per day for 21 days (Fig. 3F). We performed weekly serial OCT imaging to monitor optic disc edema and thickness of the peripapillary ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness. At longer time point (days 14 and 21), SW@NM-treated eyes exhibited significant preservation of the GCC thickness (66.2 ± 1.44 μm) compared with the vehicle-treated eyes (52.6 ± 52.6 μm, p = 0.04 at day 21) (Fig. 3G and H).

A close look at the data revealed that on day 1, both SW@NM- and vehicle-treated eyes exhibited expected optic disc edema and peripapillary GCC thickening due to AION [40], but these were significantly reduced in the SW@NM-treated eyes (Fig. 3H). This suggests that SW@NM treatment dampened early post-ischemic inflammation. At day 7, the GCC measurements showed resolution of edema in the SW@NM- and vehicle-treated eyes and pseudonormalization of the GCC thickness. Decreased inflammation and edema by day 7 can also be a plausible reason why 15-PGDH levels revert back to normal by day 7 as discussed before (Fig. S2A).

SW@NM rescued RGC soma and axons led to preservation of visual function

To assess the effect of topical therapy on visual outcomes, we performed visual function measurements using 3 well-established methods: optomotor response (OMR), a measurement of visual acuity; photopic negative response (PhNR) and pattern electroretinogram (PERG); a reflection of RGC function. AION eyes treated with SW@NM exhibited significantly improved OKR at day 21 compared with vehicle-treated AION eyes (p = 0.0001), consistent with improved visual acuity (Fig. 4A and B). Similarly, SW@NM treated mice had significantly better PhNR (p = 0.008) and pattern ERG responses (p = 0.008), indicating improved RGC and retinal function, respectively (Fig. 4C and D).

Morphometric analysis of the retina and optic nerves to assess the histologic impact of SW@NM treatment reported preservation of RGC soma and axon. Quantification of whole mount retina immunostained for Brn3a, a marker for RGCs, showed that SW@NM-treated AION eyes exhibited significantly higher RGC density compared with vehicle-treated AION eyes (p = 0.008) (Fig. 4E and F). Likewise, quantification of the optic nerve axonal density using paraphenylenediamine (PPD) stain revealed significant preservation of RGC axons with SW@NM treatment (p = 0.012) (Fig. 4G and H). Astrogliosis was also reduced at the optic nerve head and retinae after treatment with SW@NM (Fig. S2E). Overall, SW@NM treatment after AION was neuroprotective corresponding to significantly improved visual and retinal function described above.

Effectiveness of delayed treatment after AION or increasing dose frequency/day

AION model demonstrates prominent optic nerve swelling and disc edema. Severe swelling and distortion of the peripapillary retina on Day 1 after murine AION induction is reported [41,42]. In rat model, Bernstein et al. 2003 [43] reported that tissue edema in the intrascleral optic nerve was evident beginning after 1 day, ON edema decreased 3 days after induction and resolved by day 5. While in another rat study prominent optic nerve swelling was seen 2 days after induction [44], supporting the presence of substantial acute edema within the first 1–2 days following injury. Thus, the available literature indicates the edema rises rapidly within the first 24 h and is essentially at its maximum by 1–2 days [45], while the reported precise peak varies depending on the species, photothrombotic protocol, laser intensity/duration, and severity of ischemic injury. In our experimental cohort, the edema observed on Day 1 had largely resolved by Day 7, we therefore selected this early time point to evaluate the therapeutic effect of SW@NM during the acute phase of AION, when ischemic injury and edema are readily detectable and potentially modifiable. Unchecked upregulation of 15-PGDH after ischemia can impact outcome of topical SW@NM. To assess whether topical SW@NM offers therapeutic benefit when applied at different time points after injury, we induced AION and administered the first topical treatment at 0.5, 6, 12, or 24 h after onset of ischemia, followed by once per day topical treatment for total of 21 days. OCT imaging at day 21 revealed relatively improved GCC thickness in all SW@NM-treated AION group compared with vehicle-treated AION eyes. However, only AION eyes that started treatment at 0.5 h post-injury showed significant preservation of GCC thickness (p = 0.04) (Fig. 5A). In all SW@NM-treated AION eyes, regardless of timing of the first dose, visual function as measured by OMR was significantly improved (Fig. 5B). Morphometric analysis showed that RGC density was significantly better in SW@NM-treated AION eyes at all time points compared with vehicle-treated AION eyes (p = 0.05). Despite the variability of some of the measurements, the overall data suggest that SW@NM conferred neuroprotective effect even when the first dose was delayed up to 24 h after AION (Fig. 5C).

To assess whether increasing the frequency of drug administration can improve outcome, we treated animals three times a day instead of once per day for 21 days. The rationale is that SW@NM may have relatively short half-life given our data that the drug reaches the retina rapidly but at the same time the level decreases within hours. Surprisingly, we found that increasing the frequency of drug administration did not further improve outcome. Eyes treated once per day or three time per day had similarly improved retinal thickness measurements on OCT, OMR and PERG results (Fig. S3 A-C). This suggest that either sufficient drug effect remains in the retina even at lower levels or that this treatment antagonizes 15-PGDH and normalize PGE2 levels sufficiently to improve outcome.

SW@NM treatment restored mitochondrial function after AION

Unmyelinated RGC axons depend on high density of mitochondria to fuel their high metabolic needs. To investigate the impact of SW@NM treatment on retinal mitochondrial function, we performed Seahorse Mito-Stress assay to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in the mouse retinae 1 day post-AION, when maximal optic disc edema and axoplasm stasis occur (Fig. 1G). We applied topical SW@NM or vehicle on the cornea within 30 min of AION induction. On day 1 after AION, we rapidly dissected whole mount retinae and collected multiple 1 mm retinal punches within distance of approx. 3 mm of the optic nerve head, which were used to assess retinal mitochondrial function (Fig. 5D). The Seahorse data collectively indicate that AION impairs mitochondrial respiratory function in the retina. The mitochondria have both lower routine respiratory activity and, more importantly, reduced ability to increase respiration when additional energetic demand is imposed. SW@NM treatment shifted these parameters toward control levels. Notably, maximal respiration was significantly increased following SW@NM treatment, whereas the increases in basal respiration and spare respiratory capacity did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5F–H). Spare respiratory capacity (SRC) is calculated as the difference between maximal and basal respiration and represents the mitochondrial reserve capacity available to respond to increased energetic demand. AION significantly reduced SRC in AION-Veh retinae (∗p < 0.05). This reduction indicates that AION compromises the ability of retinal mitochondria to increase oxidative metabolism beyond their basal activity, suggesting reduced metabolic flexibility and vulnerability to additional energetic stress. SW@NM treatment improved SRC indicating a numerical/partial restoration toward control levels; although, this increase was not statistically significant compared with AION-Veh retinae. Improved mitochondrial respiratory capacity may support the high energetic demands of retinal ganglion cells, including maintenance of ion homeostasis, membrane potential, axonal transport, and cellular survival following injury. This may result from SW@NM–mediated reduction of edema, which restores axoplasmic flow, promoting mitochondrial quality control and energy delivery to stressed neurons. These findings suggest that SW@NM treatment and 15-PGDH inhibition in acute AION improves RGC survival and visual function through improving mitochondrial function and intracellular energy production.

Untargeted retinal proteomics revealed ferroptosis as the top pathway changed in AION and reversed by SW@NM treatment

Based on the results above, day 1 post AION seems to be ideal therapeutic window for treatment. To uncover the key signaling pathways involved in the SW@NM-mediated neuroprotection, we performed untargeted proteomics analysis on retinal tissue. Compared with naive retinae, AION retinae (vehicle treated) showed 59 significantly altered proteins (50 increased and 9 decreased) with p < 0.05, ≥Log 2 (fold change) (Fig. 6A). While SW@NM-treated AION retinae had a different profile, with 38 significantly altered proteins (33 increased, 5 decreased) (Fig. 6B). Comparing the protein profile of AION group with the SW@NM group, out of 59 proteins that were altered in AION group, 21 proteins were reversed with SW@NM treatment (17 downregulated and 3 upregulated by SW@NM). One eye exhibited protein expression pattern that was almost like that of the naive eyes (see heatmap, Fig. 6C). STRING analysis of the 21 proteins that were reversed by SW@NM treatment revealed top protein-protein interaction networks (Fig. 6D). Further, pathway analysis using Wiki pathway database showed that the top pathways activated in AION that were reversed by SW@NM treatment include iron homeostasis and complement activation, among others (Fig. 6E).

Imbalance in iron homoeostasis can lead to ferroptosis, a type of cell death characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of toxic lipid peroxidation products. SW033291 has been shown to improve tissue regeneration by inhibiting ferroptosis. To explore whether 15-PGDH inhibition using SW@NM protects retinal neurons from ferroptosis-mediated cell death, we performed Western blot and immunohistochemical assessments of retinal ferroptosis-related proteins. Compared to vehicle, SW@NM-treated AION retinae revealed significantly improved protein levels of key ferroptosis driver transferrin receptor (TFR1, p = 0.03) and acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4) (p = 0.07). Also, antioxidant glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPx-4), which was downregulated in vehicle-treated AION eyes (p = 0.04), was restored with SW@NM treatment (Fig. 6F and G). Immunostaining of retinal ferroptosis markers TFR1 and ACSL4 confirmed that SW@NM treatment led to dampening of key proteins that activate ferroptosis (Fig. 6H–J). Together, these findings provide strong evidence that SW@NM, through 15-PGDH inhibition, improved level of antioxidant and ameliorates AION-induced ferroptosis and toxic lipid-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in preservation of RGCs and improved visual function.

Discussion

We generated a topical formulation of SW033291 and showed that inhibition of retinal 15-PGDH using SW@NM is an effective way to restore vision in animal model of AION. 15-PGDH antagonism functionally augments retinal PGE2 level by preventing its degradation, leading to improved tissue metabolic function, decrease inflammation salvaging RGC soma and axons thereby improved visual and retinal function. This mechanism of action by SW@NM is consistent with the benefit of SW033291 described in earlier studies [46,47].

PGE2 is historically known as a pro-inflammation mediator, but we now know that it possess context-dependent anti-inflammatory properties. During inflammation, PGE2 can trigger lipid mediator class switching toward anti-inflammatory lipid mediators, such as lipoxins and resolvins—collectively known as specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs)—which aid in the resolution of inflammation [48,49]. At low doses, aspirin acetylate cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) remains catalytically active in cells which then favors the synthesis of endogenous lipoxin mimics instead for prostaglandins [50]. Lipoxins mediated reduced inflammation may account for some of aspirin's beneficial effects in AION [51]. Additionally, PGE2 also inhibits neutrophil infiltration and formation of neutrophil extracellular trap during inflammation [52]. Although the precise mechanism of PGE2-mediated neuroprotection in human AION remains unclear, the elevated levels of 15-PGDH observed in human and murine AION suggest that PGE2 downregulation may contribute to increased neuroinflammation and gliosis in acute AION. Whether PGE2 promotes inflammation resolution by recruiting SPMs in our study remains to be determined. However, the downregulation of inflammation-related markers in proteomics and reduced glial reactivity around the optic nerve head in the SW@NM-treated group support 15-PGDH–PGE2–mediated inflammation regulation as a plausible mechanism.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation [53]. ROS species generated after ischemia releases free iron which catalyze the Fenton's reaction propagating formation of cytotoxic lipid peroxides [54]. Poly unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are more labile to peroxidation and retina being enriched in PUFAs is more vulnerable to ischemia induced ferroptosis. Further, metabolically active iron in the cell is processed in the mitochondria [55] which risks oxidative damage to mitochondria under iron overload. Ferroptosis [56,57] and mitochondrial dysfunction [58,59] are linked to retinal ischemic neuropathies. AION patients often progress within days to weeks of symptom onset, leading to more vision loss. Although the mechanism for this is unknown, one model of AION disease progression can involve exacerbation of the anterior optic nerve milieu due to propagation of toxic lipids that spread from axon fascicle to another, taking out more axons and sabotaging axon-glia-vascular niche. Further, our data suggest that ischemia leads to dysregulation of iron metabolism and depletion of antioxidants, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of ferroptosis. Our study showed that AION triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, which is restored by SW@NM treatment. Recent reports have highlighted the potential of 15-PGDH inhibition in promoting mitochondrial function recovery [11,60] and inhibiting ferroptosis [61,62]. Clinical data from our previous study shows increased flavoprotein fluorescence imaging at the optic disc, which corresponds with increase mitochondrial oxidative stress in AION patients [63]. From current data it can be speculated that a likely mechanism by which 15-PGDH inhibition salvages RGC soma and axons is mitochondrial rejuvenation which prevent ferroptosis by restoring the antioxidant repertoire (Fig. 7). PGE2–lipid mediator class-switching–SPM axis represents a proposed downstream mechanism based on previously published literature rather than a mechanism directly demonstrated by our experiments. In the present study, we demonstrated that AION is associated with increased 15-PGDH expression and that SW@NM treatment inhibits 15-PGDH and increases retinal PGE2 levels. Our hypothesis that increased PGE2 following 15-PGDH inhibition may promote a lipid-mediator class switch toward pro-resolving mediators is supported by previous mechanistic studies. Levy et al. demonstrated that PGE2 can induce a temporal switch in lipid mediator biosynthesis from predominantly leukotriene/5-lipoxygenase products toward lipoxin A4 (LXA4), a specialized pro-resolving mediator, during the resolution phase of inflammation [64]. Subsequent reviews have further established that PGE2 can initiate lipid-mediator class switching by inducing relevant lipoxygenases and promoting the production of SPMs, including lipoxins, resolvins, protectins, and maresins [65].

Schematic of proposed mechanisms of SW033291-mediated neuroprotection through inhibition of 15-PGDH.

This proposed mechanism is also biologically relevant to the retina. Previous studies have demonstrated that SPMs, including LXA4, resolvins, and neuroprotectin D1 (NPD1/PD1), are present in ocular tissues and participate in maintaining inflammatory homeostasis [66]. In particular, LXA4 has been reported to be synthesized in the retina and to exert anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, including modulation of microglial activity and protection against retinal degeneration [67,68].

Therefore, based on (i) our finding that 15-PGDH inhibition by SW@NM increases PGE2, (ii) established evidence that PGE2 can initiate lipid-mediator class switching toward SPM production, and (iii) the documented pro-resolving and neuroprotective functions of SPMs in the retina, we propose that the 15-PGDH–PGE2 axis may promote a pro-resolving lipid mediator environment following AION (Fig. 7). This could contribute to resolution of inflammation and thereby support RGC and axonal preservation.

There are several limitations of our study. First, although we show that the drug reaches retina and the posterior pole, we do not know for certain that drug reached the optic nerve axons specifically, the primary site of injury in AION. However, as RGC soma and axons are interconnected and axonal transport from soma to axons is rapid, we believe that drug once in RGC move to the axons latching on this transport. Alternatively, given there is functional and structural benefits of SW@NM treatment, a retina-centric model of drug action could be that SW@NM primarily confers the neuroprotection at the retina, which then secondarily protects the axons. Second, while our data suggest that SW@NM acts via improving mitochondrial function, we do not know how this occurs. A possible mechanism may be by reducing inflammation at the optic nerve head, which then improves mitochondrial axonal transport by diminishing axostasis. Comprehensive studies to confirm the benefit of SW@NM treatment in enhancing mitochondrial function can expand the therapeutic potential of 15-PGDH inhibition. Third, our study is limited to the small eye of rodent, and further studies are needed to measure the stability, safety, and efficacy of the topical formulation in a larger eye like that of humans, such as in pig or non-human primate. Previous clinical trials have shown that recruitment of acute AION patients soon after onset is possible. However, how these animal studies can be translated into clinical practice still needs to be determined.

Taken together, our data provides important proof-of-concept for the use of SW@NM as a novel topical therapy for acute AION that can be explored for future human clinical trial. Further, harnessing the regenerative potential of PGE2 may have broader applications for vision restoration beyond the optic nerve, including in other ischemic or degenerative conditions. The inhibition of 15-PGDH offers important path forward in the treatment of AION and possibly other optic neuropathies.
