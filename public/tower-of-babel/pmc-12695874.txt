RBP2 promotes gastric cancer cell migration by acting as a competitive endogenous RNA to upregulate SOX4 via sponging miR-212-3p
Authors: Liu Jilan, Wang Haocheng, Sun Kaiyue, Li Xize, Zeng Jiping
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigration is a critical driver in the initiation and progression of gastric cancer. However, the involvement of RNA–RNA interactions in this process remains poorly understood. Previous work from our group and others has established RBP2 (RB-Binding Protein 2) and SOX4 (SRY-related high-mobility-group box 4) as key regulators of tumor development, influencing processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and migration. Here, we demonstrate that RBP2 regulates SOX4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels via a miRNA- and 3′UTR-dependent mechanism. Integrated bioinformatics and experimental analyses revealed downregulation of miR-212-3p and concomitant overexpression of RBP2 and SOX4 during gastric carcinogenesis. Importantly, miR-212-3p was identified as a shared upstream regulator of both RBP2 and SOX4. Knockdown of RBP2 consistently reduced SOX4 expression, supporting its role as a upstream regulator. Furthermore, we showed that the 3′UTR of RBP2 promotes gastric cancer cell migration by enhancing the expression of both RBP2 and SOX4. Collectively, our findings reveal the oncogenic function of the RBP2 3′UTR and delineate a novel RBP2–miR-212-3p–SOX4 regulatory axis, providing mechanistic insights with potential diagnostic and therapeutic relevance for gastric cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27544-2.Keywords: RBP2, miR-212-3p, SOX4, ceRNA, Gastric cancer progression

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks among the most common malignancies worldwide and remains the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths1. Although molecular biology has advanced considerably, a major challenge in GC treatment lies in translating these discoveries into effective clinical therapies2. Furthermore, the majority of gastric cancer patients show limited response to immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy, underscoring the urgent need to develop and optimize combination treatment strategies3. The inherent heterogeneity of gastric cancer further complicates the development of targeted therapies4. In light of these challenges, continued research is essential to identify effective strategies that can improve patient outcomes5.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs, approximately 19–25 nucleotides in length, that play pivotal roles in tumorigenesis and cancer progression by regulating the expression of target genes6. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated miRNA expression is closely associated with various tumor-related pathological processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis7. As research progresses, the involvement of miRNAs in tumor initiation, progression, diagnosis, and treatment has become increasingly clear8. Notably, the expression profiles of specific miRNAs in tumor tissues or body fluids (such as blood) show considerable promise as biomarkers for early tumor diagnosis and prognostic assessment9. Certain miRNAs have also been linked to the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents, opening new avenues for personalized treatment10. Moreover, modulating miRNA expression to inhibit tumor growth or enhance therapeutic efficacy has emerged as a promising strategy in oncology, encompassing anti-miRNA therapies, miRNA replacement therapies, and the integration of miRNAs with immunotherapy11–13. Therefore, leveraging the ability of specific miRNAs to simultaneously target multiple oncogenes holds substantial potential for developing broad anticancer strategies, which may become integral to precision cancer medicine14.

Among small non-coding RNAs, miR-212 has attracted increasing attention for its role in tumorigenesis, progression, and treatment, with studies in gastric cancer highlighting its clinical relevance15. For instance, in high-grade serous ovarian cancer, miR-212-3p suppresses tumor progression by directly targeting MAP3K316. In bladder cancer, miR-212-3p inhibits cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis by targeting nuclear factor IA17. In triple-negative breast cancer, miR-212-5p suppresses epithelial–mesenchymal transition by targeting Prrx218. Furthermore, miR-212 expression can be modulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), thereby promoting carcinogenesis and disease progression19–22. In gastric cancer, miR-212 expression levels significantly correlate with chemotherapy resistance, lymph node metastasis, and patient survival, suggesting its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker23. Current evidence indicates that miR-212 represents a promising target for cancer diagnosis and therapy, although its precise mechanisms of action require further elucidation24.

Through integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, our research group has further confirmed that downregulation of miR-212 in human gastric cancer tissues leads to aberrant expression of multiple oncogenes—including RBP2 and SOX4—contributing to gastric cancer development. Bioinformatics analysis has implicated several signaling pathways, such as TGF-β, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK. Our studies have demonstrated that miR-212 targets the demethylase RBP2 (RB-binding protein 2), which specifically removes tri- or di-methyl groups from histone H3K4 and plays a key regulatory role in the development of gastric and liver cancers25,26. SOX4 (SRY-box transcription factor 4) is a critical oncogene involved in various developmental processes—such as stem cell differentiation and progenitor cell development—and influences multiple tumor-related signaling pathways, including PI3K and TGF-β27. In several cancer types, including gastric cancer, SOX4 has been reported to be negatively regulated by miR-212 and implicated in tumor proliferation and progression28–31, which aligns with existing evidence. Therefore, a key objective of this study is to investigate the mechanism by which miR-212 directly binds to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of RBP2 and SOX4, thereby inhibiting their expression and forming an RBP2–miR-212–SOX4 regulatory axis that influences gastric cancer progression.

Results

The RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 axis could modulate gastric carcinogenesis

Our previous studies demonstrated that miR-212-3p acts as a post-transcriptional regulator of RBP2, suppressing the proliferation of gastric cancer cells. To further investigate the biological and clinical relevance of miR-212-3p in gastric carcinoma, we collected 20 pairs of paraffin-embedded gastric cancer and matched adjacent normal tissues from the Department of Pathology at Qilu Hospital, Shandong University, in compliance with institutional ethical guidelines. Total RNA was extracted from these samples, and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis showed significant downregulation of miR-212-3p in tumor tissues compared with matched normal controls (Fig. 1A). Notably, the decreased expression of miR-212-3p was not correlated with patient gender, age, TNM stage, or histological grade (P > 0.05; Table 1). In line with these findings, analysis of the GSE108307 dataset revealed consistent suppression of miR-212-3p in Helicobacter pylori-infected AGS gastric cancer cells (Fig. 1B), supporting its potential tumor-suppressive role in gastric carcinogenesis.

To explore the regulatory mechanisms of miR-212-3p in gastric cancer, we used the bioinformatics tools StarBase v3.0 and TargetScan 7.2, which identified 377 putative target genes of miR-212-3p (Fig. 1C). Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these target genes were significantly associated with multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, especially those involved in gastric cancer progression, such as the TGF-β and PI3K-Akt pathways (Fig. 1D). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed using the STRING database and Cytoscape (confidence score > 0.7), leading to the identification of 100 hub genes, including RBP2 and SOX4 (Fig. 1E). Both RBP2 and SOX4 are well-established mediators of gastric carcinogenesis and are functionally linked to key cancer-related pathways identified in the KEGG analysis. Importantly, previous studies have shown that RBP2 and SOX4 can be regulated by specific miRNA networks. Collectively, the integration of clinical tissue data and bioinformatic predictions supports the presence of an RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 regulatory axis in gastric carcinogenesis.

RBP2 and SOX4 were co-overexpressed in gastric carcinoma and might have potential clinical applications

We analyzed RBP2 and SOX4 expression in gastric cancer and adjacent normal tissues using data from the TCGA database. The results showed significant overexpression of both RBP2 and SOX4 in gastric cancer tissues compared with matched normal samples (Fig. 2A, C). A similar upregulation pattern was observed in non‑matched comparisons between gastric cancer and normal tissues (Fig. 2B, D). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed elevated protein expression of RBP2 and SOX4 in gastric cancer tissues (Fig. 2E). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for both genes, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.856 for RBP2 and 0.969 for SOX4, indicating high diagnostic accuracy (Fig. 2F, G). Moreover, high expression levels of RBP2 and SOX4 were associated with significantly worse overall survival in gastric cancer patients (Fig. 2H, I). Notably, analysis of TCGA data revealed a strong positive correlation between RBP2 and SOX4 expression (Fig. 2J). These findings suggest that RBP2 and SOX4 may serve as valuable diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in gastric cancer, and further investigation into the regulatory mechanisms driving their coordinated overexpression could provide important clinical insights.

miR-212-3p directly bond to the 3’UTR of RBP2 and SOX4 in a sequence-specific manner and thereby coordinately regulated the expression of both genes

Bioinformatic analysis using miRNA target prediction databases identified conserved binding sites within the 3′UTR of RBP2 and SOX4 that are complementary to the seed region of miR‑212‑3p (Fig. 3A). To experimentally validate these interactions, we conducted gain‑of‑function assays by transfecting miR‑212‑3p mimics into AGS and BGC‑823 gastric cancer cell lines. qRT‑PCR confirmed efficient overexpression of miR‑212‑3p (Fig. 3B). Ectopic expression of miR‑212‑3p led to significant downregulation of both RBP2 and SOX4 at the mRNA (Fig. 3C, D) and protein levels (Fig. 3E, F). Conversely, inhibition of miR‑212‑3p (Fig. 3G) using specific inhibitors markedly increased the expression of both targets at the mRNA (Fig. 3H) and protein levels (Fig. 3I, J), confirming that miR‑212‑3p acts as an endogenous negative regulator.

RBP2 and SOX4.

To validate direct binding specificity, we generated luciferase reporter constructs containing either wild-type or mutated versions of the predicted miR‑212‑3p binding sites within the 3′UTR of RBP2 and SOX4 (Fig. 4A). Co‑transfection with miR‑212‑3p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity from the wild‑type reporters (*P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001 versus control; Fig. 4B, C). In contrast, mutation of the seed‑pairing sequences completely abrogated this repression (P > 0.05; Fig. 4D, E). These results confirm that miR‑212‑3p directly and specifically binds to canonical target motifs in the 3′UTR of both RBP2 and SOX4.

The RBP2 3′UTR Acts as a ceRNA for miR-212-3p to promote gastric cancer migration

To investigate the functional relationships within the RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 axis, we performed targeted silencing of RBP2 using specific siRNAs. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed a codependent expression pattern wherein SOX4 levels decreased following RBP2 knockdown, while the epithelial marker E-cadherin was significantly upregulated (Fig. 5A–C). Together with our earlier findings, these results indicate that both RBP2 and SOX4 are negatively regulated by miR-212-3p, supporting the existence of the RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 axis. The miR-212-3p-associated co-expression of RBP2 and SOX4 likely contributes to gastric cancer cell migration.

To elucidate the competitive binding mechanism, we transfected AGS and BGC-823 cells with an RBP2 3′UTR plasmid that acts as a molecular sponge for miR-212-3p. While miR-212-3p expression remained unchanged (Fig. 5D), both mRNA (Fig. 5E, F) and protein (Fig. 5G, H) levels of RBP2 and SOX4 were significantly restored. A rescue experiment was conducted to verify that the ceRNA activity of RBP2 specifically depends on its interaction with miR-212-3p. As shown in Fig. 5I and J, co-transfection of the wild-type RBP2 3′UTR plasmid markedly increased SOX4 expression in both AGS and BGC-823 cells compared to controls treated with empty vector and miR-212-3p mimics. Importantly, mutation of the miR-212-3p binding site in the RBP2 3′UTR abolished this restorative effect, with SOX4 expression remaining at levels comparable to the negative control. These results provide definitive genetic evidence that the ceRNA function of RBP2 is strictly dependent on the miR-212-3p binding site within its 3′UTR.

Functional validation using wound healing assays further showed that cells transfected with the RBP2 3′UTR exhibited enhanced migratory capacity (Figs. 5K, L), mechanistically linking miR-212-3p sequestration to promoted cell motility. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RBP2 regulates SOX4 expression by serving as a molecular sponge for miR-212-3p through its 3′UTR, thereby facilitating migration in gastric cancer cells.

Discussion

The present study elucidates a novel molecular mechanism in GC progression, demonstrating that the histone demethylase RBP2 functions not only at the epigenetic level but also as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA). Specifically, the RBP2 3’UTR sequesters miR-212-3p, thereby alleviating the miRNA’s repression of the oncogenic transcription factor SOX4 and promoting GC cell migration (Fig. 6). This work significantly advances the field by integrating these previously disconnected players—RBP2, miR-212-3p, and SOX4—into a functional ceRNA axis, moving beyond isolated reports of miRNA-target interactions.

The summary map of the research.

Our findings introduce several layers of novelty. We first identified the 3’UTR of RBP2 itself as a potent oncogenic driver, which functions as a miRNA sponge. This role is in direct contrast to the protein’s previously described canonical function in chromatin remodeling mediated via the TGF-β and PI3K/Akt pathways32,33. This expands the functional repertoire of protein-coding genes and challenges the traditional dichotomy between coding and non-coding RNAs, providing evidence for the “cross-regulation of histone-modifying factors” hypothesis34. Second, while SOX4 is known to drive metastasis in pancreatic and lung cancers through EMT and PI3K/Akt signaling35,36, our discovery of its ceRNA-mediated upregulation via the RBP2/miR-212-3p axis adds a crucial post-transcriptional layer to its control in GC. The robustness of this axis was confirmed by rescue experiments, where exogenous RBP2 3’UTR overexpression reversed miR-212-3p–mediated suppression of SOX4.

The coordinated dysregulation within this axis suggests its potential utility in biomarker development. RBP2 and SOX4 demonstrated promising diagnostic accuracy in our cohort37. However, their clinical applicability remains to be established. Future studies should explore the ratio of (RBP2 + SOX4) to miR-212-3p in liquid biopsies (e.g., serum or plasma) as a potential multi-analyte signature. Such an approach may improve sensitivity and specificity for early detection, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring—consistent with the vision of earlier ceRNA studies38. Nevertheless, rigorous validation in large, multi-center prospective cohorts is essential before any clinical translation can be considered.

Therapeutically, restoring the tumor-suppressive miR-212-3p39 represents a promising strategy for simultaneously suppressing two key oncogenes (RBP2 and SOX4), potentially offering a synergistic effect superior to single-target approaches40. However, the realistic prospects for therapeutic delivery hinge on overcoming major challenges, including ensuring in vivo stability, achieving tumor-specific delivery, and minimizing off-target effects. Recent advancements in nanoparticle-based carriers and chemical modifications provide a feasible path forward. A promising clinical application could involve combining a miR-212-3p mimic with immune checkpoint inhibitors, given the established interplay between oncogenic pathways and immunosuppressive microenvironments41,42, which may help overcome the limited efficacy of monotherapy in GC.

This study has some limitations. It should be noted that the statistical power of this study, especially for subgroup analyses, is limited by the relatively small cohort size. Consequently, while trends were observed, we did not detect a statistically significant correlation between miR-212-3p expression and certain pathological characteristics such as TNM stage or histological grade. Future investigations with larger, multi-center prospective cohorts are warranted to confirm and extend our findings. Nonetheless, the prognostic significance of the key molecules in our proposed axis (RBP2 and SOX4) was robustly validated in a large independent database, strengthening the clinical relevance of our conclusions.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses, was constrained by the relatively small cohort size. As a result, although certain trends were observed, we did not detect statistically significant correlations between miR-212-3p expression and specific clinicopathological features such as TNM stage or histological grade. In addition, the single-center origin of the clinical samples may limit the generalizability of the findings.Future investigations involving larger, multi-center prospective cohorts are necessary to validate the prognostic and diagnostic relevance of the RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 axis across diverse populations. Incorporating patient-derived xenografts or organoid models could also help strengthen the physiological relevance of the functional insights reported here.

In summary, our work delineates a novel RBP2/miR-212-3p/SOX4 ceRNA network that integrates epigenetic regulation with post-transcriptional control to drive GC migration. By integrating bioinformatics, clinical data, and functional assays, we propose a tripartite model that could bridge miRNA biology and oncogenic signaling. These findings may advance the ceRNA field43, offer actionable biomarkers, and pave the way for miRNA-based combination therapies in gastric cancer. Future work should focus on validating this axis in patient-derived xenografts and larger multi-center cohorts to fully realize its translational medical potential.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The GSE108307 dataset was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) by searching with the keywords “miRNA expression”, “Helicobacter pylori”, and “gastric epithelial cells”. Gene expression and clinical data for gastric cancer and matched normal tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)44(https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga).A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for the 377 predicted target genes using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database45(version 11.5), with subsequent network visualization and top 100 gene selection performed through Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) [46]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis47-49for these prioritized genes was performed with the Metascape platform50. The prognostic value of RBP2 and SOX4 expression in gastric cancer was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter tool51(https://kmplot.com/analysis/) with log-rank testing for survival differences. Gene expression correlation between RBP2 and SOX4 in gastric adenocarcinoma was investigated using the GEPIA2 web server52 (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), incorporating TCGA stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) cohort data.

Clinical samples

Gastric cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected from paraffin-embedded samples of patients (n = 20) at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. The gender, age, and miR-212-3p expression levels of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong University School of Basic Medical Sciences (Approval No. ECSBMSSDU2018-1–012).

Cell culture

The human gastric cancer cell lines AGS and BGC-823 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) and the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), respectively. AGS cells were maintained in F-12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and BGC-823 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone) with 10% FBS. Both cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Transfection

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 5 μL of 50 nM RNA per well, including miR-212-3p mimics, miR-212-3p inhibitor, miRNA negative control (NC), and corresponding control RNAs (RiboBio). Transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 Reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‑PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed using either TaqMan Universal Master Mix (Life Technologies) or SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara), with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. The PCR primers for RBP2 expression were: sense: 5'-GCTGCTGCAGCCAAAG TTG-3', and antisense: 5'-AGCATCTGCTAACTGGTC-3'; SOX4: sense, 5'-CCCAGCAAGAAGGCGAGTTA-3', and antisense, 5'-CATCGGCCA AATTCGTCACC-3'; β-actin: sense, 5'-GAAGTGTGACGTGGACATCC-3', and antisense, 5'-CCGATCCACACGGAGTACTT-3'. MiR-212 expression was normalized to U6 expression. The expression fold change was calculated relative to the control using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Following treatment, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime) for 30 min on ice, with intermittent vortexing every 5 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing total protein was separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed three times with TBST between incubations. The primary antibody against RBP2 was obtained from Abcam, while antibodies targeting SOX4, E-cadherin, and β-actin were sourced from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Protein bands were detected using an ECL Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (Millipore), and expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Luciferase reporter assay

To validate direct binding of miR-212-3p to the 3′-UTR of SOX4 and RBP2, AGS and BGC-823 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well and transfected with miR-212-3p mimics. Cells were co-transfected with either wild-type (WT) or mutant (Mut) pMiR-Report plasmids (Yike Biotechnology), along with a TK control plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000. After 72 h, luciferase activity was measured with a Glomax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega). Transfection efficiency was normalized based on Renilla luciferase activity.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was assessed using a wound healing (scratch) assay. Briefly, 3 × 105 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, a uniform scratch was introduced in the cell monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the cells were transfected with either the RBP2 3′-UTR plasmid or an empty vector control using Lipofectamine 2000. Wound closure was monitored and photographed at 24 and 48 h post-transfection, and migration was quantified based on the remaining scratch area.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. All the performed statistical analyses are described in each figure legend. Statistical P-values were obtained by application of the appropriate statistical tests using the GraphPad Prism 8. For all tests, P < 0.05 was considered significant (ns: not significant, *P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001).
