Early total triiodothyronine, illness severity, and in-hospital mortality in critically ill adults
Authors: Shaowen Zhong, Yao Yu, Quan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow T3 is common in critical illness, but whether it adds prognostic information beyond overall illness severity remains uncertain. We performed a retrospective cohort study of adults in the first ICU stay recorded in MIMIC-IV who underwent total triiodothyronine measurement within 48 h of ICU admission and had no documented thyroid disease. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Of 201 included patients, 65 (32.3%) had low T3 (< 60 ng/dL). Compared with patients with normal T3, those with low T3 had higher SOFA scores, more frequent mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use, and higher in-hospital mortality (24.6% vs. 8.1%, P = 0.003). Low T3 was associated with mortality in the unadjusted model (OR 3.71, 95% CI 1.61–8.56) and after adjustment for age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.17–6.81), but the association was attenuated after additional adjustment for SOFA score (OR 1.37, 95% CI 0.51–3.68). Cox regression showed a similar pattern (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.42–2.67). Introduction

Altered thyroid hormone metabolism is common in critical illness. The characteristic biochemical pattern of non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), or euthyroid sick syndrome, is a reduction in circulating T3 without a compensatory increase in thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)1–3. In ICU populations, this pattern has been interpreted as an adaptive response to systemic stress, a marker of illness severity, or a sign of impaired recovery1,4.

In clinical practice, low T3 in a critically ill patient without established thyroid disease can be difficult to interpret. The key question is not whether this pattern occurs, but what it means at the bedside: whether it reflects primary thyroid dysfunction, a transient illness-related change, or a finding that contributes information beyond conventional severity assessment.

Previous studies have linked low T3 or NTIS to adverse outcomes in sepsis, cardiovascular disease, and a range of ICU populations4–8. However, adjustment for disease severity has been inconsistent. Some smaller observational studies reported an apparently independent association with mortality, whereas larger or more comprehensively adjusted analyses suggested substantial attenuation after illness severity was taken into account6,8.

We therefore used the MIMIC-IV database to examine early total T3 levels and in-hospital mortality among adults during the first ICU stay after exclusion of documented thyroid disease. Our aim was to determine whether the association between low T3 and mortality persisted after adjustment for established severity measures, particularly the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score9.

Early organ support was also more frequent in the low T3 group. Mechanical ventilation within 24 h was present in 44.6% of the low T3 group versus 25.0% of the normal T3 group (P = 0.006), and vasopressor use occurred in 30.8% versus 16.9%, respectively (P = 0.029). Albumin was lower, whereas creatinine and bilirubin were higher in the low T3 group, consistent with greater acute physiological disturbance.

Low T3 and in-hospital mortality

Overall in-hospital mortality was 13.4% (27/201). Mortality was significantly higher in the low T3 group than in the normal T3 group (24.6% vs. 8.1%, P = 0.003). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed lower hospital survival probability in the low T3 group (Fig. 2; log-rank P = 0.025).

Regression analyses

As shown in Table 2, low T3 was significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in the crude logistic model (OR 3.71, 95% CI 1.61–8.56, P = 0.002). The association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and CCI (OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.17–6.81, P = 0.021). After additional adjustment for SOFA score, the association was markedly weakened and no longer statistically significant (OR 1.37, 95% CI 0.51–3.68, P = 0.537).

In a complete-case sensitivity analysis that further included albumin and lactate, the association remained non-significant (OR 2.08, 95% CI 0.33–13.03, P = 0.434; n = 60, events = 17). The adjusted Cox model yielded similar findings (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.42–2.67, P = 0.905).

When total T3 was analyzed as a continuous variable, each 10 ng/dL decrease was not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality (OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.92–1.38, P = 0.255). As shown in Fig. 3, restricted cubic spline analysis showed neither a significant overall association (P = 0.230) nor significant nonlinearity (P = 0.239). Missing data

Missingness was 17.4% for TSH, 45.3% for FT4, 52.7% for albumin, 47.8% for lactate, 38.8% for bilirubin, and 1.5% for platelet count. Because albumin and lactate were missing in a substantial proportion of patients, models including these variables were interpreted as sensitivity analyses rather than primary models. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, low T3 was associated with a heavier comorbidity burden, higher SOFA scores, greater use of early organ support, and higher crude in-hospital mortality. Early low T3 therefore marked patients with greater acute illness severity rather than an isolated biochemical abnormality.

After SOFA adjustment, the association between low T3 and in-hospital mortality was markedly weakened. The same pattern was seen in the continuous T3 model, restricted cubic spline analysis, and Cox sensitivity analysis. These findings support the interpretation that early total T3 largely reflects the burden of acute organ dysfunction rather than providing a robust independent prognostic signal.

From an endocrine perspective, these findings argue against interpreting isolated low total T3 during early ICU admission as primary thyroid failure. They also do not support thyroid hormone replacement on the basis of low T3 alone. A low total T3 result is better understood as part of the endocrine-metabolic response to severe illness and should be interpreted alongside organ failure burden, hemodynamic support, and the overall clinical trajectory10.

Several mechanisms may contribute to this pattern. Inflammatory signaling can suppress peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 through reduced deiodinase type 1 activity and increased deiodinase type 3 activity, thereby lowering biologically active T31,2,11,12. Persistent systemic stress may also alter hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis regulation1,4. In addition, critical illness is often accompanied by hypoalbuminemia, capillary leak, and changes in thyroid hormone binding proteins, which can influence total T3 measurements. Low T3 may therefore reflect a broader state of altered energy handling and multiorgan stress rather than a discrete thyroid disorder13.

Our findings fit with a mixed literature. Several ICU and sepsis studies reported that lower T3 levels were associated with adverse outcomes5,14–18, whereas larger or more comprehensively adjusted analyses have shown that NTIS tracks with poor outcomes without remaining independently predictive after severity adjustment6,8. In this setting, low T3 remains clinically informative because it identifies patients with greater illness severity, but its incremental prognostic value beyond standard severity assessment appears limited.

This study has several limitations. First, because only patients who underwent early T3 testing were included, testing-selection bias and indication bias are likely. Second, although we incorporated SOFA and early organ support variables, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, thyroid disease exclusion relied on diagnosis codes. We could not fully account for thyroid-related medications or ICU therapies that may alter thyroid function tests, including levothyroxine, antithyroid drugs, amiodarone, glucocorticoids, dopamine, and recent iodinated contrast exposure. Fourth, albumin and lactate had substantial missingness, which limited the complete-case sensitivity analyses. Fifth, we examined total T3 rather than FT3 or reverse T3, so the findings reflect a clinically available marker rather than the full spectrum of thyroid hormone physiology. Finally, as a single-center database analysis, generalizability should be confirmed in other cohorts.

In summary, early low total T3 in the ICU appears to function primarily as a marker of acute illness severity rather than as a stand-alone prognostic signal.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, version 3.1, which contains de-identified electronic health record data from patients admitted to the emergency department or intensive care units at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 202219–21. Use of MIMIC-IV was approved by the institutional review boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center19,20. The database contains de-identified data, and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the responsible institutional review boards. All analyses were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The author responsible for data access completed the required human-subjects research training and data use agreement.

Study population

Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older from the first ICU stay who had total T3 measured within 48 h after ICU admission. Patients with documented thyroid disease, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and thyroiditis, were excluded using ICD-9/10 diagnosis codes. Patients without ascertainable in-hospital outcomes were also excluded.

Of 94,458 ICU stays in MIMIC-IV v3.1, 65,366 were adult first ICU stays. Among these, 446 had total T3 measured within 48 h after ICU admission. After exclusion of 245 patients with documented thyroid disease, 201 patients remained in the final analytic cohort.

The clinical rationale for ordering total T3 testing was not available in MIMIC-IV; therefore, this cohort should be interpreted as a tested ICU population rather than an unselected ICU population.

Variables and definitions

Total T3 was defined as the first available value within 48 h after ICU admission. TSH and free T4 (FT4) were extracted as the first available values within the same 48-hour window. To characterize early baseline physiology while reducing potential information leakage from later ICU events, albumin, lactate, creatinine, bilirubin, platelet count, and glucose were defined using the first available values within 24 h after ICU admission.

Illness severity was assessed using the SOFA score within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Early organ support variables included mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use within the first 24 h. Comorbidity burden was represented by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Low T3 was defined a priori as total T3 < 60 ng/dL, corresponding to the lower boundary of a commonly used clinical reference range and a threshold used in prior critical care studies5,14,22.

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. In time-to-event analyses, ICU admission served as time zero, in-hospital death was treated as the event, and discharge alive was treated as censoring.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median [interquartile range] and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as n (%) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to supplement baseline comparisons.

The primary analysis used logistic regression because the main endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and CCI. Model 3 further adjusted for SOFA score. Because only 27 in-hospital deaths occurred, we used a parsimonious severity-adjusted model centered on SOFA score to reduce overfitting. Covariates were selected a priori according to clinical relevance, event number, and data availability in MIMIC-IV. Mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use were reported descriptively but were not included in the primary model because they overlap with acute illness severity and would further reduce model stability given the limited number of events. Because albumin and lactate had substantial missingness, an additional model including these variables was treated as a complete-case sensitivity analysis. Cox proportional hazards regression was used as a supportive time-to-event sensitivity analysis; because discharge alive may represent informative censoring, the Cox model was interpreted cautiously. Continuous T3 was evaluated per 10 ng/dL decrease. Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to explore potential nonlinearity between total T3 and mortality, with knots at 42, 67, and 108 ng/dL and a reference value of 80 ng/dL. Missing data were not imputed; each model used complete cases for the variables included in that model. Analyses were performed in Python 3.12.10 using pandas 2.3.3, NumPy 2.4.4, SciPy 1.17.1, statsmodels 0.14.6, patsy 1.0.2, lifelines 0.30.3, and matplotlib 3.10.8. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
