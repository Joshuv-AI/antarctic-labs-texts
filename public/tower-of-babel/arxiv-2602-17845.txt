A Refinement in Čech Cohomology of Coron’s Necessary Condition
Bryce Christopherson, Farhad Jafari

Abstract
Coron established a homological obstruction to continuous feedback stabilization of nonlinear control systems x ˙ = f ⁡ ( x , u ) \dot{x}=f(x,u) with f ∈ C ⁡ ( Ω , ℝ n ) f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n}) and f ⁡ ( 0 , 0 ) = 0 f(0,0)=0 , showing that local asymptotic stabilizability implies the induced homomorphism f ∗ f_{*} satisfies f ∗ ​ ( H n − 1 ​ ( Σ ϵ ) ) = H n − 1 ​ ( S n − 1 ) f_{*}\big(H_{n-1}(\Sigma_{\epsilon})\big)=H_{n-1}(S^{n-1}) , where Σ ϵ = { ( x , u ) ∈ Ω : f ( x , u ) ≠ 0 , ∥ x ∥ < ϵ , ∥ u ∥ < ϵ } \Sigma_{\epsilon}=\{(x,u)\in\Omega:f(x,u)\neq 0,\enskip\|x\|<\epsilon,\enskip\|u\|<\epsilon\} . In this paper, we refine Coron’s necessary condition using Čech cohomology and the Vietoris–Begle mapping theorem. Specifically, we prove that the closed version of Σ ϵ \Sigma_{\epsilon} , i.e. Σ ¯ ϵ = { ( x , u ) ∈ Ω : f ( x , u ) ≠ 0 , ∥ x ∥ ≤ ϵ , ∥ u ∥ ≤ ϵ } \overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}=\{(x,u)\in\Omega:f(x,u)\neq 0,\enskip\|x\|\leq\epsilon,\enskip\|u\|\leq\epsilon\} , must be a Čech cohomology ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -sphere and that the restriction f | Σ ¯ ϵ : Σ ¯ ϵ → f ⁡ ( Σ ¯ ϵ ) f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}}:\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}) induces an isomorphism on the Čech cohomology groups of Σ ¯ ϵ \overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon} in all degrees. This strengthens Coron’s condition from a constraint on the top class to a full cohomological rigidity statement.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider autonomous control systems of the form
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
a neighborhood of the origin. We are interested in
stabilizing
these systems. That is (as in, e.g.,
(
11

), we are looking at the following property.

Definition 1

(Local Asymptotic Stabilizability - Feedback Laws)
.

Given a control system
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
, we say the system is locally asymptotically stabilizable by means of feedback laws if there exists a neighborhood of the origin
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\textrm{proj}_{1}(\Omega)
and a feedback
u\colon\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\textrm{proj}_{2}(\Omega)
satisfying
u(0)=0
that renders the origin a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium
(
6

of the closed-loop system
\dot{x}=f\big(x,u(x)\big)
.

When we reference local asymptotic stability throughout the paper as defined in
Definition

1

always
assume that the feedback

satisfies the following additional criterion:

Criterion 1

(Uniqueness Criterion - Feedback Stabilization)

The state-feedback controller
u(x)
is such that
f\big(x,u(x)\big)
is continuous and
\dot{x}=f\big(x,u(x)\big)
has a unique solution
x(t)
for all

at all
x_{0}
in a neighborhood of the origin.

There is some disagreement as to whether
1

Definition

1

On the other, it is also possible to define various more general notions of solutions (e.g. Filippov and Krasovskii) which allow for non-unique trajectories and still exhibit convergence in the desired fashion. All the same, it is necessary for us and we will adopt it here.

A variety of conditions describing whether a system is locally asymptotically stabilizable by means of continuous feedback laws have been derived. As a nowhere-near-comprehensive listing, see, e.g., control Lyapunov techniques

Definition 2

(Sections, Local Sections)

Given topological spaces

Coron’s Condition

Theorem

2
provides a complete characterization of stabilizability, but is not always practically useful in that it is often difficult to verify that such a local section does or does not exist. Accordingly, ’no-go’ theorems ruling out stabilizability are still quite attractive. The most well known of these is Brockett’s theorem, which requires that the vector field
f
in a control system
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
be such that
f(\mathcal{O})
be a neighborhood of the origin in the codomain whenever
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\Omega
is a neighborhood of the origin in the domain. In 1989, Coron strengthened this condition to a homological one, revealing a necessary relation between the existence of stabilizing feedback laws and a condition regarding a singular homology group associated to the system and the homomorphism on homology induced by
f
. Namely, for
\epsilon\in(0,\infty]
, let

\Sigma_{\epsilon}:=\{(x,u)\in\Omega:f(x,u)\neq 0,\enskip\|x\|<\epsilon,\enskip\|u\|<\epsilon\},

that is,
\Sigma_{\epsilon}=\big(\left(\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\times\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)\right)\cap\Omega\big)\setminus f^{-1}(0)
. Let
H_{q}(M)
denote the
q
-th (unreduced) singular homology group with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
for a given topological space
M
, and let
f_{*}
be the induced homomorphism from
H_{n-1}(\Sigma_{\epsilon})
into
H_{n-1}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\big)
. Then, Coron’s necessary condition may be stated as follows:

Theorem 3

(Coron’s Condition)
.

If a control system
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
is locally asymptotically stabilizable by means of continuous stationary feedback laws, then for all
\epsilon>0
it is necessary that

f_{*}\left(H_{n-1}(\Sigma_{\epsilon})\right)\cong H_{n-1}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\big).

Since
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
deformation retracts to the
n-1
sphere
S^{n-1}
, and since

H_{k}(S^{n-1})=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&k\neq 0,n-1\\
\mathbb{Z},&k=0,n-1\end{matrix}\right.

Coron’s condition simply says that it is necessary to have
f_{*}\left(H_{n-1}(\Sigma_{\epsilon})\right)=\mathbb{Z}
.

Coron demonstrated that the condition of
Theorem

3
is necessary but not sufficient with the following example:

Example 1

(Insufficiency of
Theorem

3

10
)
.

Consider the system on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

\dot{x}=f(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{3}^{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})&x_{3}^{2}(x_{2}-x_{3})&u\end{bmatrix}^{T}

This system satisfies Coron’s necessary condition (
Theorem

3
) but is not stabilizable via a continuous feedback.

This raises a natural question: is Coron’s top-degree homology condition the whole story? The example above suggests either that stabilization cannot be characterized in a purely (co)homological way (which we view as a likely possibility) or, if it can, that a stronger (co)homological condition is necessary. After some preliminaries in the next section, we will prove a refinement of this condition which is stronger than
Theorem

3
.

3
Reframing in Čech Cohomology

Throughout this section and the remainder of the paper, all Čech cohomology groups are taken with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
, unless explicitly stated otherwise. Early in the development of homological theories for compact metric spaces, Vietoris proved a landmark result known as the
Vietoris theorem
or
Vietoris-Begle mapping theorem

24
;
23
. This result, concerning the so-called
Vietoris homology groups
of such a space, showed that continuous mappings whose fibers have the homology of points induce isomorphisms in homology. Specifically, letting
\widetilde{VH}_{k}
denote the
k^{th}
reduced Vietoris homology group of a compact metric space, the theorem is as follows:

Theorem 4

(Vietoris-Begle Mapping
28
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces and let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a continuous surjection such that
\widetilde{VH}_{k}\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)=\widetilde{VH}_{k}\big(\{*\})\big)=0
for
0\leq k\leq N-1
and all
y\in Y
. Then, the induced homomorphism
f_{*}:\widetilde{VH}_{k}(X)\rightarrow\widetilde{VH}_{k}(Y)
is an isomorphism for
k\leq N-1
and a surjection for
k=N
.

The condition that
\widetilde{VH}_{k}\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)=\widetilde{VH}_{k}\big(\{*\})\big)
for
0\leq k\leq N-1
and all
y\in Y
is sometimes stated instead as ’
f
has
(N-1)
-acyclic fibers
’ (wherein the
(N-1)
modifying prefix is omitted when the condition holds for all
r\geq 0
). This is also commonly applied to other homology and cohomology theories as well, so we opt to avoid this terminology here to avoid any confusion regarding which homology or cohomology theory is intended.

Later, Vietoris homology would eventually fall out of fashion and give rise to Čech homology and cohomology (Dowker gives a wonderful treatment of the relation between Vietoris and Čech homology/cohomology and the strengths of the latter/deficiencies of the former
12
). With these more modern tools in hand, Begle would go on to produce the following generalization:

Theorem 5

(Begle
16
;
7
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be paracompact spaces, let
G
be an abelian group, and let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a continuous closed surjection. If
\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(f^{-1}(y);G)=\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\{*\};G)
for each
0\leq k<N
, then the induced homomorphism
f^{*}:\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(Y;G)\rightarrow\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(X;G)
is an isomorphism for
0\leq k\leq N-1
and a surjection for
k=N
.

We will use Begle’s theorem to produce a necessary condition for stabilizability that is similar to, but properly stronger than, Coron’s original one. The reasoning for this may seem opaque: One might wonder why, exactly, is Čech cohomology a ’better’ setting than singular homology? While we do not pretend to have a fully satisfying answer for this, an intuitive justification is that Čech cohomology enjoys a continuity property with respect to shrinking neighborhoods of compact sets that singular homology does not. Specifically, if
K
is compact, then

\check{H}^{k}(K)=\underset{K\subset U}{\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}}\check{H}^{k}(U)

where the limit is taken over open neighborhoods
U
of
K
(we state this more precisely in
Lemma

7
). For instance, in singular homology, a compact set may admit a neighborhood basis of contractible open sets while still possessing nontrivial singular homology, while the same cannot happen in Čech cohomology. Consequently, singular homology is not as flexible a device to use to when employing arguments that rely on understanding a set through arbitrarily small open neighborhoods of it.

But notice, this is precisely the context we are in! Since our interest lies in local asymptotic stabilizability and, hence, in the behavior of the vector field in arbitrarily small neighborhoods of fibers above the equilibrium (that is, in sets like
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
), Čech cohomology is a ’nicer’ context to work in. This does not mean, necessarily, that it is the ’correct’ context for studying stabilization, but instead that it is a
easier
setting to characterize
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
.

4
The Strong Version of Coron’s Necessary Condition

To begin, we will recall two standard results, which are well known.

Recall first that, we say a subspace
X
of a topological space
Y
is a
retract
(resp. neighborhood retract) of
Y
if there is a continuous map
r:Y\rightarrow X
(resp. open set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq Y
and continuous map
r:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow X
) called a
retraction
(resp.
neighborhood retraction
) such that
r|_{X}=\textrm{id}_{X}
. Likewise, given a class
\mathcal{C}
of topological spaces that is closed under taking subsets and homeomorphisms, we say that a space
X
is an
absolute retract
(resp.
absolute neighborhood retract
) for
\mathcal{C}
if
X\in\mathcal{C}
and, whenever
X
is a closed subset of
Y\in C
, then
X
is a retract (resp. neighborhood retract) of
Y
. Finally, if
X
is a subspace of a topological space
Y
, we say that
X
is a
deformation retract
of
Y
if there is a retraction
r:Y\rightarrow X
such that
\iota\circ r
, where
\iota:X\rightarrow Y
is the inclusion, is homotopic to the identity on
Y
(i.e. if there exists a continuous function
h:Y\times[0,1]\rightarrow Y
called a
deformation retraction
such that
h(y,0)=r(y)
and
h(y,1)=y
). If, in addition, there exists for all
t\in[0,1]
a retract
r_{t}:Y\rightarrow X
such that
(\iota\circ r_{t})(y)=h(y,t)
, we say that
X
is a
strong deformation retract
of
Y
and that
h
is a
strong deformation retraction
.

Borsuk proved the following result relating compact absolute neighborhood retracts for the class of metric spaces to strong deformation retracts of their open neighborhoods in the metric spaces containing them.

Lemma 6

(
2
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space, let
A\subseteq X
be a compact absolute neighborhood retract, and suppose
A
is a retract of an open neighborhood
U\subset X
. Then,
A
is a strong deformation retract of
U
.

Succinctly,
Lemma

6
tells us that if a compact absolute neighborhood retract retracts to an open neighborhood, then there is a retract that is homotopic to the identity. Set-theoretically, this says that if
A
is a compact subset of a metric space such that any metric superspace
Y\supseteq A
has a neighborhood
Y\supseteq\mathcal{O}\supseteq A
for which there is a continuous map
r:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow A
satisfying
r|_{A}=\textrm{id}_{A}

and
that at least one of these neighborhoods is open, then
r
may be chosen so that
r\circ\iota
is homotopic to the identity on this open neighborhood, where
\iota
is the inclusion.

We will also need a result, which tells us that the Čech cohomology groups of a compact subset of a compact metric space can be computed in terms of a limit of their open supersets.

Lemma 7

(
(
4
, Ch. 1, Sec. 7)
)
.

For
K
a compact metric space and
F\subseteq K
compact, then for all
k\geq 0
,

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(F)=\underset{F\subset U\subset K\textrm{ open}}{\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}}\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(U).

That is, for compact metric spaces, the Čech cohomology of a compact subset is completely determined by the Čech cohomology of arbitrarily small open neighborhoods of it. This will serve a key role in the next lemma.

Now, with that in hand, we are ready to show that stabilizable systems induce mappings with the Čech cohomology of a point. The following result is the ’core’ of the entire argument; the main result (
Theorem

9
) is effectively a direct consequence of it and an application of Begle’s theorem (
Theorem

5
).

Lemma 8
.

Suppose the control system
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
is locally asymptotically stabilizable at the origin by continuous stationary feedback laws. Then, there is a compact neighborhood
K\subset\Omega
of the origin such that for every
y\in f(K)
and every
k\geq 0
,

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}\big(f^{-1}(y)\cap K\big)=\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}\big(\{*\}\big).

Proof.

By
Theorem

2
, there is some compact neighborhood
V
of
(0,0)\in\Omega
and continuous function
\alpha:f(V)\rightarrow V
(note: since
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
is stabilizable, Brockett’s condition yields that
f(V)
is a neighborhood of the origin) with components
\alpha(x)=\big(\alpha_{1}(x),\alpha_{2}(x)\big)
such that
\dot{x}=\alpha_{1}^{-1}(x)
has the origin as a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium and
f\circ\alpha=\textrm{id}_{f(V)}
. In particular, this implies
\alpha
is injective, since whenever
\alpha(y_{1})=\alpha(y_{2})
, we have
y_{1}=f\big(\alpha(y_{1})\big)=f\big(\alpha(y_{2})\big)=y_{2}
.

Fix a small ball
B
around the origin so that
\overline{B}\subseteq V
and choose a compact neighborhood of the origin
K\subseteq\Omega
such that
f(K)\subseteq B
(which is always possible by the continuity of
f
and the condition that
f(0,0)=0
; i.e. by continuity, there is a neighborhood
U
of the origin such that
f(U)\subseteq B
, so we can take
K
to be a closed ball centered at the origin contained in
U
). Define
g:K\rightarrow\alpha(\overline{B})
via
g(x,u):=\alpha\big(f(x,u)\big)
(notice, since
f(K)\subseteq B\subseteq f(V)
,
g
is well-defined). Observe that if
\alpha(y)\in\alpha(\overline{B})
, then
g(\alpha(y))=\alpha(y)
(since
f\circ\alpha
is the identity). Moreover, observe that
g
collapses the fibers
(f|_{K})^{-1}(y)=f^{-1}(y)\cap K
to a point, as
g\big(f^{-1}(y)\cap K\big)=\alpha\big(f\big(f^{-1}(y)\cap K\big)\big)=\alpha(y)
. Thus, the fiber of
g
over
\alpha(y)
is exactly the same as the fiber of
f|_{K}
over
y
; i.e.,
g^{-1}\big(\alpha(y)\big)=f^{-1}(y)\cap K
.

Now, fixing
y\in f(K)
, we will construct a neighborhood basis of contractible open sets for the fiber
f^{-1}(y)\cap K
. Using
Lemma

7
, this yields our desired result. To begin, note that since
y\in B\subset\mathrm{int}(\overline{B})
(as
f(K)\subset B
by construction), we have
\alpha(y)\in\mathrm{int}\big(\alpha(\overline{B})\big)
. Since
\overline{B}
is compact and
\alpha:\overline{B}\rightarrow\alpha\big(\overline{B}\big)
is continuous and injective, and since
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is Hausdorff, the restriction
\tilde{\alpha}:\overline{B}\rightarrow\alpha\big(\overline{B}\big)
is a homeomorphism onto its image. Choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
\overline{\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(y)}\subset B
. Define

A_{\epsilon}(y):=\alpha\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}\big),\qquad W_{\epsilon}(y):=\alpha\big(\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(y)\big)\subset\alpha(\overline{B}).

First, we show
W_{\epsilon}(y)
is open in
\alpha(\overline{B})
. Since
\tilde{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism and
\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(y)
is open in
\overline{B}
(with the subspace topology), it follows that
W_{\epsilon}(y)=\tilde{\alpha}\big(\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(y)\big)
is open in
\alpha(\overline{B})
.

Now, we show
A_{\epsilon}(y)
is homeomorphic to a closed
n
–ball. Because
\tilde{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism,
A_{\epsilon}(y)=\tilde{\alpha}\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}\big)
is homeomorphic to
\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}
, which is a closed
n
–ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence
A_{\epsilon}(y)
is compact and contractible, and therefore is a compact absolute neighborhood retract.

Next, we construct a retraction
r_{\epsilon}:W_{\epsilon}(y)\rightarrow A_{\epsilon}(y)
. Define the radial projection
p_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(y)\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}
by

p_{\epsilon}(z)=\begin{cases}z,&\|z-y\|\leq\epsilon,\\
y+\epsilon\frac{z-y}{\|z-y\|},&\epsilon<\|z-y\|<2\epsilon.\end{cases}

This map is continuous and satisfies
p_{\epsilon}(z)=z
for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}
. Defining

r_{\epsilon}:=\tilde{\alpha}\circ p_{\epsilon}\circ\tilde{\alpha}^{-1}:W_{\epsilon}(y)\rightarrow A_{\epsilon}(y).

and noting that
\tilde{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism, it is straightforward to see that
r_{\epsilon}
is continuous. Moreover, if
w\in A_{\epsilon}(y)
, then
w=\tilde{\alpha}(z)
for some
z\in\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(y)}
, and hence
r_{\epsilon}(w)=\tilde{\alpha}\big(p_{\epsilon}(z)\big)=\tilde{\alpha}(z)=w
. Thus
r_{\epsilon}
is a retraction of
W_{\epsilon}(y)
onto
A_{\epsilon}(y)
.

Penultimately, we construct a neighborhood basis of open sets for the fiber
(f|_{K})^{-1}(y)
. Define

U_{\epsilon}(y):=g^{-1}\big(W_{\epsilon}(y)\big).

Since
g
is continuous and
W_{\epsilon}(y)
is open in
\alpha(\overline{B})
, the set
U_{\epsilon}(y)
is open in
K
. Furthermore,

\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}U_{\epsilon}(y)=g^{-1}\left(\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}W_{\epsilon}(y)\right)=g^{-1}\big(\alpha(y)\big)=f^{-1}(y)\cap K,

so
\{U_{\epsilon}(y)\}_{\epsilon>0}
forms a neighborhood basis of open sets for the fiber
(f|_{K})^{-1}(y)=f^{-1}(y)\cap K
.

Finally, we show
U_{\epsilon}(y)
is contractible. Define

\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}:=r_{\epsilon}\circ g\big|_{U_{\epsilon}(y)}:U_{\epsilon}(y)\rightarrow A_{\epsilon}(y).

Because
r_{\epsilon}
is a retraction and
g|_{A_{\epsilon}(y)}=\mathrm{id}
, it follows that
\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}
restricts to the identity on
A_{\epsilon}(y)
. Hence
A_{\epsilon}(y)
is a retract of the open set
U_{\epsilon}(y)
. Since
A_{\epsilon}(y)
is a compact absolute neighborhood retract,
Lemma

6
implies that
A_{\epsilon}(y)
is a strong deformation retract of
U_{\epsilon}(y)
. Therefore
U_{\epsilon}(y)
is contractible. Consequently, for all
k\geq 0
,
\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}\big(U_{\epsilon}(y)\big)\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\{*\})
. Since the sets
U_{\epsilon}(y)
form a cofinal system of open
neighborhoods of the compact set
f^{-1}(y)\cap K
in
K
, the continuity property of Čech
cohomology (
Lemma

7
) yields

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}\big(f^{-1}(y)\cap K\big)=\underset{f^{-1}(y)\cap K\subset U\subset K\text{ open}}{\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}}\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(U)=\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}\big(U_{\epsilon}(y)\big)\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\{*\}).

∎

From this, we may use the Vietoris-Begle mapping theorem to great effect. The product of this is the main result of the paper. Note that here we use the closed version of
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
, unlike the open analogue Coron uses in
Theorem

3
. That is, we use

\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}:=\Big(\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)}\times\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)}\big)\cap\Omega\Big)\setminus f^{-1}(0).

Theorem 9

(Coron’s Condition-Strong Version)
.

Suppose
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f(0,0)=0
is locally asymptotically stabilizable by continuous stationary feedback. Then, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, the set
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is a Čech cohomology
(n-1)
–sphere (with integer coefficients). Moreover, for all
k\geq 0
, the restriction
f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}}:\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
induces an isomorphism

(f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}})^{*}:\check{H}^{k}\big(f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\big)\rightarrow\check{H}^{k}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}).

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small that
\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)}\times\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)}\subset\Omega
, so that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is a compact subset of
\Omega
. By Brockett’s necessary condition for continuous stationary stabilization, for every neighborhood
W
of
(0,0)
the set
f(W)
contains a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small there exists
\rho>0
such that
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(0)\subset f\left((\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)}\times\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)})\cap\Omega\right)
. Since
f\big(W\setminus f^{-1}(0)\big)\supset f(W)\setminus\{0\}
, it follows that
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(0)\setminus\{0\}\subset f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
. Thus,
f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is a punctured neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore deformation retracts onto
S^{n-1}
.

By
Lemma

8
, after shrinking if necessary there exists a compact neighborhood
U\subset\Omega
of
(0,0)
such that
f|_{U}:U\rightarrow f(U)
is a continuous surjection and every fiber
f^{-1}(y)\cap U
is Čech-acyclic. Fix
\epsilon>0
so small that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}\subset U
. Then,
f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}}:\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is a continuous surjection between compact metric spaces, hence a closed map. Moreover, for each
y\in f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\subset f(U)
,
\big(f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}}\big)^{-1}(y)=f^{-1}(y)\cap\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is Čech-acyclic by
Lemma

8
. Therefore
Theorem

5
applies and yields that
f^{*}:\check{H}^{k}\big(f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\big)\rightarrow\check{H}^{k}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is an isomorphism for all
k\geq 0
. Since
f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is a punctured neighborhood of the origin, it deformation retracts onto
S^{n-1}
, and hence
\check{H}^{k}\big(f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\big)\cong\check{H}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for all
k
. This proves that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
has the Čech cohomology of
S^{n-1}
and that
f^{*}:\check{H}^{k}\big(f(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\big)\rightarrow\check{H}^{k}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is an isomorphism for all
k
.
∎

It’s important to note that this doesn’t mean that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
must be all that much like a sphere. For example, the Poincaré homology
3
-sphere has the same Čech cohomology as
S^{3}
but nontrivial homotopy groups, so it is not homeomorphic to
S^{3}
. Indeed, while Theorem
9
does say that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
must be connected, it need not even be the case that
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is simply connected.

Example 2
.

As in
Example

1
, the system on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
given by

\dot{x}=f(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{3}^{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})&x_{3}^{2}(x_{2}-x_{3})&u\end{bmatrix}^{T}

satisfies Coron’s necessary condition (
Theorem

3
) but is not stabilizable via a continuous feedback. Coron used this to demonstrates that
Theorem

3
is not sufficient
10
. However, it also serves as a nice example showing that
Theorem

9
is necessary:
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
is not a Čech cohomology
2
–sphere (with integer coefficients), which implies that the system violates
Theorem

9
and hence cannot be stabilized by a continuous stationary feedback. To see that the strong condition of
Theorem

9
fails, we show that
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
has nontrivial first cohomology, which is impossible for a Čech cohomology
2
–sphere.

To do so, first observe that
(x_{3},u)\neq(0,0)
for every
(x,u)\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
. Indeed, if
x_{3}=0
and
u=0
, then
f(x,u)=(0,0,0)
, so
(x,u)\in f^{-1}(0)
, contradicting
(x,u)\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
. Hence the map

\pi:\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow S^{1},\qquad\pi(x,u):=\frac{(x_{3},u)}{\sqrt{x_{3}^{2}+u^{2}}}

is well-defined and continuous. Now, fix
\rho\in(0,\epsilon/4)
and consider the loop

\gamma:[0,2\pi]\rightarrow\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon},\qquad\gamma(t):=\big((0,0,\rho\cos t),\ \rho\sin t\big).

We check that
\gamma(t)\in\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
for all
t
: clearly
\|\gamma(t)\|<\epsilon
by choice of
\rho
. Also,
f(\gamma(t))=\bigl(0,\ -(\rho\cos t)^{3},\ \rho\sin t\bigr)
, which is never zero because
(\rho\cos t,\rho\sin t)\neq(0,0)
. Therefore,
\gamma
avoids
f^{-1}(0)
and lies in
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
. Finally,

(\pi\circ\gamma)(t)=\frac{(\rho\cos t,\rho\sin t)}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}\cos^{2}t+\rho^{2}\sin^{2}t}}=(\cos t,\sin t),

which has winding number
1
in
S^{1}
. Thus
(\pi\circ\gamma)_{*}:H_{1}(S^{1})\rightarrow H_{1}(S^{1})
is the identity on
\mathbb{Z}
, and in particular
\pi_{*}:H_{1}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\rightarrow H_{1}(S^{1})
is surjective. Equivalently,
\pi^{*}:H^{1}(S^{1})\rightarrow H^{1}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})
is injective, so
H^{1}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\neq 0
. Since
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is a compact metric space, the same conclusion holds for Čech cohomology: Since
\pi\circ\gamma:S^{1}\rightarrow S^{1}
has degree
1
, the induced map
(\pi\circ\gamma)^{*}:\check{H}^{1}(S^{1})\rightarrow\check{H}^{1}(S^{1})
is the identity. Thus
\gamma^{*}\circ\pi^{*}
is the identity on
\check{H}^{1}(S^{1})
, so
\pi^{*}
is injective and
\check{H}^{1}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\neq 0
. But a Čech cohomology
2
–sphere has a trivial first Čech cohomology group, so
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
is emphatically
not
a Čech cohomology
2
-sphere. Therefore, the strong necessary condition of
Theorem

9
fails for this system, even though Coron’s condition holds.

Example 3
.

The condition of
Theorem

9
is not sufficient. To see this, it is enough to look at the system
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
with
f(x,u)=x
, which is certainly not locally asymptotically stabilizable. However,

\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}=\left(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\times\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)\right)\setminus f^{-1}(0)=\left(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\setminus\{0\}\right)\times\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0),

so, via the contractibility of
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)
,

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\setminus\{0\})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(S^{n-1}).

Worse still,
f|_{\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}}
is just the projection
\pi:\left(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\setminus\{0\}\right)\times\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(0)\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)\setminus\{0\}
, which induces an isomorphism in Čech cohomology in all degrees. So, the conditions of
Theorem

9
all hold, yet the system fails miserably to be stabilizable by feedback.

5
Conclusion

We have shown that Coron’s classical homological obstruction to feedback stabilization can be strengthened by using Čech cohomology instead and applying Begle’s theorem. While Coron’s condition requires surjectivity of the induced map on the top singular homology group of
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
, our refinement demonstrates that the entire Čech cohomology of the closed analogue
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
must coincide with that of the sphere
S^{n-1}
. This, like Brockett’s original necessary condition, gives further credence to the intuitive sense one gets that a control system must be able to ’see’ in all directions anywhere sufficiently near an equilibrium point for stabilization to be possible.

This strengthened condition imposes significant additional topological constraints on
\overline{\Sigma}_{\epsilon}
and, with it, on the form a system must have if it is to be stabilizable. Several directions for future work following this theme suggest themselves: For one, it would be interesting to find if there are additional conditions that, if imposed, render this cohomology-sphere necessary condition a sufficient one instead. Likewise, producing any homological or cohomological obstructions that are stronger yet still might be of great value. In light of the fact that mappings satisfying Brockett’s necessary condition satisfy a local analogue to the characteristic property of quotient maps
(
9
, Section 4.1)
and the fact that Smale provided an analogue of the Vietoris mapping theorem in homotopy
25
which was extended by Calcut et. al. and applies directly to quotient maps
7
, it would also be interesting to know if there is an analogue to
Theorem

9
in homotopy.

Overall, this work demonstrates that the topology underlying feedback stabilization is rich and that Coron’s original condition captures a genuinely deep property of stabilizable systems. As we have shown here, Čech cohomology provides a robust context for expressing some of these constraints stabilization requires, but the search for a complete homological or cohomological characterization of stabilizability (if such a thing is even possible) remains ongoing.
