“Back to the Future”: Testing the “Individual Problems and Strengths” Scale With a Stepwise Confirmatory Factor Analysis Approach
Authors: Rune Zahl‐Olsen, Nicolay Gausel, Åshild Tellefsen Håland, Terje Tilden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRoutine Outcome Monitoring systems have been developed to monitor how clients' lives change over the course of therapy. However, for such systems to be effective, they must possess sufficient construct validity and scale‐reliability to warrant their use. In this study, using a sample of 841 Norwegian clients, we performed the first independent test of the construct validity and scale‐reliability of the “Individual Problems and Strengths” (IPS) scale, a subsection of the “Systemic Therapy Inventory of Change.” We used a stepwise Confirmatory Factor Analysis approach specifically designed to test the construct validity of the measurement scales. We found satisfactory support for the construct validity of the original “8‐factor” version of the IPS; however, its scale reliability received poor to mixed support. Furthermore, we failed to find support for all other previously or recently suggested factorial alternatives to the original 8‐factor model. Our analysis leads us to conclude that the developers of the IPS should return “back to the future” by refining their original 8‐factor structure of the IPS, while disregarding other proposed versions.Trial RegistrationThe data stems from an RCT study registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01873742) as well as from a prior pilot study (Tilden et al. 2015).

Introduction

Although psychotherapy is effective for a large proportion of clients (Sexton et al. 2013), not all benefit from treatment. In fact, some clients' symptoms even get worse (Ogles 2013). To better understand and explain why some clients benefit while others do not, quantitative systems consisting of standardized questionnaires—known as Routine Outcome Monitoring systems (ROM)—have been developed in recent years (Lambert 2010; Ogles 2013; Tilden and Wampold 2017). These measurement systems tap into various aspects of clients' lives, and when completed regularly, the resulting mean scores allow both therapists and clients to monitor progress throughout the course of therapy (Duncan et al. 2004; Zahl‐Olsen and Oanes 2017).

One such popular system is the “Systemic Therapy Inventory of Change” (STIC) developed by Pinsof et al. (2009). The STIC addresses therapeutic change through a battery of six large thematic sets of questionnaires. One of these, the “Individual Problems and Strengths” (IPS) theme, taps into eight typical aspects of distress and everyday difficulties that clients individually experience during therapy. These aspects include, for example, clients' degree of Flexibility/resilience in changing plans and coping with changes; Open expression or the ability to express and share feelings; Life functioning, such as managing daily work and household tasks; and managing Negative affect or coping with negative emotional states. As the IPS is considered clinically important, this thematic component of the STIC has seen increasing application in psychotherapy, particularly within couple and family therapy settings (Tilden and Wampold 2017).

IPS: Is It Validated and Reliable?

When implementing a ROM system, researchers must ensure that the system demonstrates construct validity—measuring what it intends to measure—and internal consistency, where scale items function as reliable indicators of that construct (i.e., its scale‐reliability). One system consistently argued to be valid and reliable is the “Individual Problems and Strengths” (IPS) measurement scale (He et al. 2019, 2024; Pinsof et al. 2009, 2015, 2024; Zinbarg et al. 2018).

The IPS was originally developed by Pinsof et al. (2009) as an 8‐factor structure (i.e., having eight different but related themes). Its initial construct validity and scale‐reliability were tested in the Chicago area using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) on 188 outpatient clients. Although the sample size was on the lower side of acceptability, the CFA yielded a good fit of data for this 8‐factor structure with a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) of 0.06 and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.94. However, subsequent scale‐reliability tests using Cronbach (1951) alpha on each of the eight factors yielded mixed results with reliability ranging from low (α = 0.54) to good (α = 0.89), suggesting that items within certain factors do not consistently measure the same underlying construct.

Some years later, the same team (Pinsof et al. 2015) aimed to re‐validate the “8‐factor” IPS structure, this time with greater statistical power by increasing their sample to 581 clients. Despite the larger sample, the CFA did not perform well. The fit indices revealed a poor representation of the data (RMSEA = 0.117; CFI = 0.59), failing to validate the 8‐factor construct. As in the 2009 study, the reliability tests using Cronbach's alpha demonstrated a range from low (α = 0.45) to good (α = 0.85) scale‐reliability, again indicating that certain items did not describe the constructs effectively.

Following the failure to validate the 8‐factor structure of the IPS, Pinsof et al. (in the same study) proposed a new factorial structure. This time, they introduced a uniform, “1‐factor” model suggesting that all IPS items represent the same overall construct—essentially same psychological experience of IPS. By this argument, any IPS item would convey the same meaning as any other item, ultimately turning the IPS into a very large, non‐nuanced measurement scale. This stands in stark contrast to their 2009 paper arguing for the 8‐factor IPS as a nuanced measurement scale. However, when they tested this “1‐factor” solution with CFA, it failed the test of construct validity (RMSEA = 0.099; CFI = 0.71).

As the “1‐factor” structure did not stand the test of construct validation, Pinsof et al. (2015, p. 468) proposed a “higher order factor structure.” In this CFA model, all items were loaded onto the previously “failed to be supported” 8‐factor solution, which in turn served as eight independent indicators for the equally unsupported 1‐factor solution. This unusual and complex approach—combining two failed models without a theoretical explanation of why it was needed—yielded only a borderline fit (RMSEA = 0.069; CFI = 0.86). Nevertheless, Pinsof et al. (2015) interpreted this as validation for their original 2009 8‐factor IPS, despite that model's failing to validate in the first phase of their threefold 2015 analyses.

Three years later, in 2018, the same research team, now led by Zinbarg (Zinbarg et al. 2018), addressed the IPS and its relationship with other ROMs using data from 476 outpatient clients in the Chicago area. Although they ran multiple CFAs, including one dedicated to the 8‐factor IPS model, they did not provide the fit‐indices. Instead, they generically stated that the IPS items “load on eight factors” (p. 737) and reported the means, standard deviations, and reliabilities (α ranging from 0.66–0.87) for those factors. Following this, they extracted only two of the eight sub‐scales from the IPS (using a total of nine items) for further analysis. Then they ran a CFA on these two sub‐scales, subsequently collapsing them to run another CFA on a “1‐factor” version of the two sub‐scales. Fit indices were provided for these two CFA‐solutions of extracted parts of the IPS. In response to why they did not do this with the crucial 8‐factor version of the IPS, footnote 2 (p. 747) notes that the missing fit indices of the 8‐factor CFA were reserved for a forthcoming study. However, the result of this study has not yet been published. In our view, it is unclear why they decided to suppress the CFA fit indices from the important “8‐factor” solution of the IPS but reported such results from two other factorial solutions using nine items extracted from the IPS.

The following year (2019), the same team, now led by He et al. (2019), revisited the IPS. While the original sample consisted of 1099 participants, 516 were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 583 participants. In this study, 1 He et al. (2019) stated that “The factor structures, concurrent validity, convergent and discriminant validity, and reliabilities of the scores of the STIC—Initial and Intersession Scales and Sub‐scales—have been tested and supported on three outpatient clinical samples and a nationally representative normative sample” (p. 1108) citing the 2009, 2015, and 2018 studies previously discussed. However, this statement appears incorrect, and we are uncertain why He et al. concluded in this manner.

In their 2019 study, they bypassed CFA analyses of the IPS; instead, they simply averaged the score from all items in the IPS, turning it into the same unidimensional “1‐factor” solution which the same team had reported as having very poor fit 4 years earlier (2015), subsequently reporting its reliability level as “good” (p. 1110). 2 Ignoring their own acknowledgement of the flawed quality of a unidimensional IPS, they proceeded to correlate this “1‐factor” model with three other commonly used measures, interpreting higher correlations as evidence for the “validity of the IPS change scores” (p. 1112). However, demonstrating that a flawed unidimensional construct correlates with other measures is not a demonstration of validity; it is merely a demonstration of correlation. Interestingly, in this 2019 paper, the authors abandoned their unidimensional IPS in Tables 4 and 5 (p. 1115), choosing instead to correlate each of the eight IPS factors against the other three commonly used measures. It is unclear why they treated the IPS as unidimensional in the first section of the paper while abandoning it in favor of the 8‐factor structure, all without providing CFA evidence for either the 1‐factor or the 8‐factor model.

Five years later, in 2024, He et al. re‐examined IPS (among other STIC intersession scales). As in 2019, He et al. collapsed the IPS into the “1‐factor” unidimensional solution, while simultaneously using an 8‐factor version for the correlations presented in Table 2 without providing confirmatory factorial evidence for either solution. Furthermore, as in 2019, they repeated the incorrect claim that “the validity and the reliability of the STIC—Initial and Intersession Scales—have been supported” (p. 462), citing the same 2009, 2015, and 2018 studies used in their 2019 study. 3 This vacillation between various versions of the IPS without any supporting factorial analyses suggests a premise that the IPS is a therapeutic measurement tool that is so inherently valid that it requires no further empirical validation, regardless of its application or format.

Taken together, we view the IPS as an important approach to measuring and tracking changes as clients undertake therapeutic assistance. However, before promoting or recommending the IPS, one needs to be sure that it is indeed valid and reliable. To date, the construct validity and scale‐reliability of the IPS have been tested twice—in 2009 and 2015—and only by researchers within the same team. Only one of these validation attempts succeeded, and that was with the 8‐factor structure originally proposed by Pinsof et al. (2009), but it relied on a very small sample. A second attempt by Pinsof et al. (2015), using a suitable sample size, failed. Despite this failure, Pinsof et al. proposed other factorial versions of the IPS that also failed. Consequently, subsequent papers on the IPS from this team have not tested its construct validity; instead, they have incorrectly claimed that the IPS is valid and reliable (He et al. 2019, 2024). Consequently, we believe that independent tests of the IPS, its construct validity, and scale reliability are needed. Hence, this was the first independent test of IPS.

The Current Study

In this study, we aimed to replicate the factorial structure of the IPS, as originally proposed by its developer (Pinsof et al. 2009), along with its scale reliability. Specifically, we first tested Pinsof et al.'s (2009) hypothesis that the IPS represents an “8‐factor” solution. Second, we tested the “1‐factor” unidimensional structure suggested by Pinsof et al. (2015). Third, we examined the alternative “higher‐order factor structure” also proposed by Pinsof et al. (2015).

Stepwise Construct Validation Procedure

To test the various factorial structures associated with the IPS, we followed the stepwise approach to construct validity developed by researchers (Gausel et al. 2012, 2016, 2018; Pardede et al. 2021). This approach recommends a four‐step procedure where the preferred hypothesized factorial structure—in our case, the original 8‐factor structure of the IPS—serves as a point of reference. All alternative factorial solutions are compared against the preferred hypothesized model and then with each other. The best‐fitting model (which should ideally be the hypothesized one) is the one that would “win.” However, should the hypothesized model “lose” (i.e., fail to achieve the best fit to the data), further exploration of the data is needed. Preferably, Exploratory Factor Analyses (EFA) can be used to establish a new factorial structure, which is then developed into a revised, hypothesized model to be tested with the same procedure used by Gausel et al. (for a discussion and practical example of this approach, see Pardede et al. 2021).

Our aim in testing these factors is primarily motivated by the fact that the developers promote the IPS as a validated and reliable measurement tool—a claim that is contradicted by their own analyses. Nevertheless, the IPS is increasingly deployed in clinical settings. Therapists often use the 8‐factor solution to calculate eight different mean values to monitor specific changes or the 1‐factor solution to calculate an overall mean value for monitoring therapeutic change. Naturally, should we fail to confirm the construct validity of the IPS, therapists must be informed that the tool which they use to interpret and track changes in clients' lives throughout the therapy is flawed. As this directly influences client well‐being, it is of great importance for therapists to know whether the IPS is truly valid and reliable.

Methods

This study's data were collected in Norway, where couple and family therapy treatment is offered to the general public through a stepped‐care model. The initial level of care, which requires no referral, is provided by two agencies. Outpatient agencies represent the second level of care, while an inpatient facility represents the third and final level of care; both require a referral. As data are derived from standard clinical practice, no inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied other than the criteria used by each site to accept patients for treatment. The data were collected from clients of over 40 therapists at all three levels of care through online questionnaires. 4

Ethics and Consent

This study utilized data from a questionnaire common to both a multi‐site Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) investigating the effects of online feedback in therapy, registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01873742), and a prior pilot study (Tilden et al. 2015). Its protocol was approved by the Modum Bad Ombudsman for Data Protection and the Regional Ethics Committee for Medical Research with human subjects (2009/927/REK Sør‐øst C: The Pilot study was approved Nov. 13, 2009, and the RCT study was approved Jun. 13, 2016). For participants under the age of 16, consent was obtained from parents or guardians. All ethical recommendations were strictly followed.

Participants, Data, and Procedure

The RCT and pilot studies recruited 841 clients (51.8% women; mean age: 40 years; age range: 12–72) through ordinary clinical practice from March 2010 to April 2016. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The procedural approach for collecting data in therapy is described in more detail by Zahl‐Olsen et al. (2021b). We reused the data from these two studies for this analysis, with the current data originating from responses to the initial questionnaire. If readers wish to perform the same or other analyses, data is available for download (Zahl‐Olsen et al. 2021a).

Flexibility/resilience was measured using three items—“I generally find it easy to overcome difficulties,” “When what I'm trying doesn't work out, I can change my approach or my plans,” and “When I get upset, I find healthy ways to make myself feel better”—(α = 0.67) on a scale ranging from 1 (1st item: “very easy,” 2nd and 3rd items: “strongly disagree”) to 5 (1st item: “very hard,” 2nd and 3rd items: “completely disagree”). Life functioning was measured using two items—“Performing work/school/household tasks” and “Managing day‐to‐day life”—(α = 0.78) on a scale ranging from 1 (“really bad”) to 5 (“really good”). Open expression was measured using two items—“I can openly express my feelings” and “I can speak up for myself when the situation calls for it”—(α = 0.68) on a scale ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). Self‐acceptance was measured using two items—“I can be myself in every situation” and “I am comfortable with who I am”—(α = 0.71) on a scale ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). Disinhibition was measured using three items—“Thought about seriously harming or killing someone,” “Had fits of rage you could not control,” and “Had urges or impulses that you could not control”—(α = 0.53) on a scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“all the time”). Negative affect was measured using six items—“Had thoughts or images over and over again that you could not get rid of,” “Felt tense or anxious,” “Felt sad most of the day,” “Thought about ending your life,” “Felt hopeless about the future,” and “Not enjoyed things as much as you used to”—(α = 0.86) on a scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“all the time”). Self‐misunderstanding was measured using two items—“I don't understand why I do the things I do” and “It's tough for me to know what I'm feeling”—(α = 0.66) on a scale ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). Substance abuse was measured using two items—“Drank too much alcohol” and “Used illegal drugs/misused prescribed medication”—(α = 0.20) on a scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“all the time”). 6

Statistical Analysis

We used IBM AMOS 25 to test our hypothesis via CFA with Full Maximum Likelihood Estimation. 7 Following the stepwise “construct validity” approach (p. 945 recommended by Gausel et al. (2012, 2016, 2018), we first tested the preferred “8‐factor” model. In the second step, we tested a “1‐factor” model and compared its fit against the “8‐factor” model. Third, we tested a “higher order factor structure,” again comparing its fit to the “8‐factor” model. In the final step, we compared the “8‐factor” model against other plausible, data‐driven alternatives. 8

In line with Gausel et al's recommendations, we allowed latent factors to correlate, but restricted items from cross‐loading onto any of the factors, and error terms from correlating.

Results

Step 1: Validating the “8‐factor” IPS Construct

As a first step, we tested the “8‐factor” version of the IPS as originally proposed by Pinsof et al. (2009). Despite a significant chi‐square, χ 2 (181) = 605,994, p <0.001, χ 2/df = 3.35, the “8‐factor” model fit the data well as indicated by the other fit‐indices: IFI = 0.936, CFI = 0.935, RMSEA = 0.053 [0.048−0.058], AIC = 793.994. As seen in Figure 1, all factor loadings were significant (all p values < 0.001), ranging from standardized λ = 0.30–0.86, with most >0.55 indicating that factors were well defined by their respective items (Gausel et al. 2012, 2016, 2018). The correlations among the eight different factors ranged from low (r = 0.06, p = 0.398) to high (r = 0.89, p < 0.001) with more than half of the items producing an explained variance of around 50%, an ideal level for a CFA (Kline 2016).

The 8‐factor Model.

Step 2: Validating the “1‐Factor” IPS Construct, and then Comparing it to the “8‐Factor” Construct

In the second step, we tested the “1‐factor” version of the IPS widely used and promoted by Pinsof et al. In this version, all items are collapsed into one big unidimensional IPS construct, the mean of which is treated as representative of the overall IPS and its eight underlying factors (Pinsof et al. 2015; Zinbarg et al. 2018; He et al. 2019, 2024). As such, we allowed all items to load onto a 1‐factor, grand omnibus IPS. The significant chi‐square—χ 2 (209) = 1949,931, p < 0.001—along with a high χ 2/df ratio of 9.33, low IFI (0.736), low CFI (0.734), indicated that this “1‐factor” model fit the data poorly. This was further supported by a high RMSEA of 0.100 (90% CI [0.096–0.104]) and AIC of 2081.93. Additionally, only half of the items had factor loadings > λ 0.55, and only three items in the undifferentiated model reached the suggested 50% level of explained variance (Kline 2016).

Even though the fit indices clearly indicated that the “1‐factor” model fit the data poorly, we continued to follow Gausel et al.'s (2012, 2016, 2018) recommendations by comparing it against the “8‐factor” model. We found that the “8‐factor” model fit the data significantly better than the “1‐factor” model: ∆ χ 2 (28) = 1343.937, p < 0.001. Moreover, the difference in AIC was substantial ∆ (AIC = 1287.937), demonstrating that the “8‐factor” model was favored over the “1‐factor” model. 9

Step 3: Validating the “Higher Order Factor Structure” of IPS, Followed by a Comparison of Its “8‐Factor” Construct

Pinsof et al. (2015) tested the so‐called “higher order factor structure,” where they allowed individual factors such as “IPS Negative Affect and Open Expression” to load onto “a single second‐order general factor like IPS” (p. 470). They emphasized that this analysis was “theoretically and methodologically important because finding support for a higher order model (…) demonstrates that each of the scale's group factors links to a single higher order factor that underlies the scale and its factors” (p. 470). Consequently, we decided to test the “higher order factor structure” by allowing each of the eight factors to load independently onto a “higher‐order” IPS‐factor. This model approached a borderline acceptable fit of data, χ 2 (201) = 818,833, p < 0.001, χ 2/df = 4.07, IFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.906, RMSEA = 0.060 (90% CI [0.056–0.065]), AIC = 966.833.

However, when we compared this “higher order” model against the original “8‐factor” model, the “8‐factor” model fit the data significantly better than the “higher order” model; ∆ χ 2 (20) = 212.839, p < 0.001, with a large difference in AIC; ∆ AIC = 212.839. Hence, the original “8‐factor” model fit substantially better than the “higher order factor structure.”

Step 4: Comparing the “8‐Factor” Version of IPS Against the Other Plausible Data‐Driven Alternatives

Following the final step recommended by Gausel et al. (2012, 2016, 2018), we compared the “8‐factor” version of IPS against other meaningful theory‐ or data‐driven alternatives, in this case, data‐driven ones. Based on the four correlations in Figure 1 were r ≥ 0.70, we identified five models as plausible data‐driven alternatives to the 8‐factor model. These alternatives were not specified solely on statistical magnitude; they also reflected conceptual proximity between constructs (e.g., overlapping self‐evaluative processes and interpersonal expressiveness), making them theoretically interpretable structural alternatives within the CFA framework.

When we tested the “8‐factor” model against these five data‐driven models, it consistently outperformed all of them. First, it provided a better fit than a model collapsing “open expression” with “self‐acceptance,” ∆ χ 2 (7) = 149.44, p < 0.001 and ∆ AIC = 135.440. Second, it yielded superior fit indices than a model collapsing “flexibility/resilience” with “self‐acceptance,” ∆ χ 2 (7) = 27.761, p < 0.001 and ∆ AIC = 13.761. Third, it fit better than a model collapsing “flexibility/resilience” with “self‐misunderstanding,” ∆ χ 2 (7) = 75.735, p < 0.001 and ∆ AIC = 61.735. Fourth, it outperformed a model collapsing “self‐misunderstanding” with “self‐acceptance.” ∆ χ 2 (7) = 95.713, p < 0.001 and ∆ AIC = 81.713. Finally, it showed a significantly better fit than a model, in which, all four aforementioned factors were collapsed, ∆ χ 2 (18) = 301.710, p < 0.001, and ∆ AIC = 260.71.

As these alternative models were informed by latent correlations observed in the present sample, they represent data‐informed structural comparisons conducted within a constrained CFA framework. While high latent correlations in strictly specified CFAs (with zero cross‐loadings) can sometimes reflect modeling constraints rather than true conceptual overlap, the alternative models were explicitly specified and evaluated within the same confirmatory model‐comparison logic. As with all model comparisons conducted within a single sample, independent replication remains essential.

Discussion

In recent years, psychotherapy research has focused on the best ways to monitor client progress throughout therapy using ROM systems. Naturally, it is important for therapists and researchers to be reassured that the ROM administered represents the clients' experiences as accurately as possible. One such suggested ROM is the STIC, which includes a clinically important subsection, the IPS, originally developed by Pinsof et al. (2009).

It is claimed that the IPS is a valid and reliable measurement tool, most recently by He et al. (2024). However, the IPS has received robust scale‐validatory support only once (Pinsof et al. 2009), in a study with a small sample, an 8‐factor solution, and mixed reliability results. Furthermore, the IPS has only been tested in the Chicago area, in English, by the same, somewhat expanded team that first proposed it in 2009. Subsequent attempts (e.g., 2015) by the Pinsof team to validate updated versions have resulted in disappointing results and failed to meet established minimum standards for validation and reliability. Ignoring these failures, the developing team continues to recommend the IPS as a validated and reliable measurement tool to be used in clinical practice (e.g., He et al. 2024). Consequently, there are ample grounds for an independent group of researchers—familiar with both the tool and the use of ROM in therapy—to test the construct validity and reliability of the IPS in a different cultural and linguistic context. In this study, we employed Gausel et al.'s (2012, 2016, 2018) stepwise CFA approach for construct validation. Our first step was to test the fit of the original “8‐factor” model as suggested by Pinsof et al. (2009). This step was important because if it failed to fit well it would be pointless to compare it to other models (Gausel et al. 2012, 2016, 2018). In support of our hypothesis, the original “8‐factor” version of the IPS (Pinsof et al. 2009) represented the data well. In fact, whichever way we tried to modify the combination of factors in different steps of the Gausel et al. CFA approach, the “8‐factor” model always came out as the superior factorial solution. Thus, we provide the first independent validation of the original “8‐factor” model as developed by Pinsof et al. (2009).

Following the successful scale validation of the 8‐factor structure of the IPS, we tested the proposed “1‐factor” model (Pinsof et al. 2009, 2015), and found that it represented the data very poorly and was inferior to the 8‐factor model across all indicators. This finding contradicts Pinsof et al.'s (2009, 2015) argument that all IPS items are equally representative of the overall construct; it also challenges the common therapeutic practice of using an overall mean to track therapeutic change (He et al. 2019, 2024; Oanes et al. 2015; Spanier 1988).

While the “higher order factor structure” (Pinsof et al. 2015), achieved borderline acceptable fit, it performed significantly worse than our “8‐factor” model. Hence, it remained an inferior alternative to the better‐fitting “8‐factor” model. This finding contradicts Pinsof et al.'s (2015) argument that the IPS is best represented by a “higher order factor structure.” In our view, the IPS is best represented by the original 8‐factor structure proposed in 2009; it outperformed all other versions, including the data‐driven alternatives demonstrated in step 4 of our analyses.

Regarding scale‐reliability, of the eight IPS factors, only one (i.e., Negative Affect) achieved a Cronbach's α > 0.80. While two factors—Life functioning and Self‐acceptance—demonstrated acceptable reliability (>0.70), the remaining five ranged from low to very low. Specifically, Flexibility/resilience, Open expression, and Self‐misunderstanding barely exceeded 0.60; Disinhibition scored just above 0.50; and Substance abuse was below 0.20. Consequently, most of these reliability results contradict the claims made by He et al. (2019, 2024), that the IPS is a reliable measurement scale. A measurement model yielding low to moderate reliability levels clearly indicates a need for further development of the scale (Schmitt 1996).

Our findings regarding the lower reliability of the IPS scale resonate with the initial assessments by Pinsof et al. (2009), who acknowledged that their scale's low reliability constituted “a major methodological concern” (p. 151). To overcome this shortcoming, they aimed “to increase the reliability of the subscales with low alphas” (p. 151) in future studies. Six years later, Pinsof et al. (2015) still struggled with low reliabilities, reiterating that the issue was “concerning” (p. 478). This “concern” appears to have vanished into thin air in more recent years as the same developing team now posits the IPS as a reliable scale (He et al. 2019, 2024; Pinsof et al. 2024). A measurement scale weak in reliability does not become reliable simply with the passage of time or through repetitive claims of validity. Hence, we encourage the developers to temper their enthusiasm for the measurement scale until it has reached commonly agreed‐upon levels for acceptable scale reliability.

Possible Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

This study tested the IPS following Gausel et al.'s (2012, 2016, 2018) comparative evaluation of competing factorial structures within a stepwise CFA framework. Future research could further examine the relative contributions of general and sub‐scale‐specific variance using bifactor modeling. This approach would be particularly valuable in contexts where both total and subscale scores are interpreted, providing complementary insight into whether observed variance reflects a general factor or domain‐specific constructs.

The data for this study were collected over a 6‐year period (2010–2016), which may not reflect current trends in clinical contexts or societal developments. However, because a factorial structure represents the core organization of a measurement model, it is expected to be relatively stable over time, particularly for instruments like the IPS that assess enduring psychological constructs and track change in clients throughout therapy. While changes in therapeutic context may influence levels of reported symptoms or experiences, they are less likely to substantially alter the underlying measurement structure. Nevertheless, future research could benefit from replicating these findings using more recent samples.

The comprehensive STIC system includes several themes besides the IPS baked into it (Pinsof et al. 2009). Therefore, we cannot comment on the construct validity or scale reliability of the remaining five subthemes, but only encourage their future testing. Similarly, we are unable to evaluate the construct validity or scale reliability of other ROM systems, such as the Systemic Clinical Outcome and Routine Evaluation (Carr and Stratton 2017). Nevertheless, we encourage both developers and independent researchers to rigorously test these systems. Ultimately, our focus is client welfare and providing the best possible care during therapy. This demands measurement tools that can be trusted to measure what they are supposed to measure (construct validity) with items that best describe what is measured (scale‐reliability).

This study's generalizability to other populations is somewhat limited by the use of a sample from a single Scandinavian country, which is characterized by a relatively homogeneous population, high standards of living, and high levels of educational attainment. Participation in the assessment was voluntary, which may have introduced some degree of self‐selection bias, potentially favoring individuals who were more motivated or engaged. Nevertheless, this study evaluated and supported the original 8‐factor model in the Norwegian clinical context and language. These findings contribute to the broader applicability of the IPS by extending its empirical support beyond the US to Northern Europe.

Finally, this study did not examine measurement invariance across multiple demographic sub‐groups. While such analyses could offer a more nuanced understanding of the model, our primary aim was to replicate and evaluate the factorial structure of the IPS within a stepwise CFA framework (Gausel et al. 2012, 2016, 2018), rather than to assess invariance across demographic levels. Therefore, future research should consider examining the stability of the IPS across various clinically relevant subgroups.

Conclusion

Following the construct validity approach, proposed by Gausel et al. (2012, 2016, 2018), our study lends the first independent support to the construct validity of the original “8‐factor” solution of the IPS as originally developed by Pinsof et al. (2009). Our findings suggest that this version is superior to alternative data‐driven models. We encourage the IPS developing team to reflect on our findings, noting that this independent test provides renewed support for the original 8‐factor structure both cross‐culturally (Norwegian culture) and cross‐linguistically (Norwegian language). We encourage the team to view this as a major step forward in validating the original “8‐factor” solution.

These findings should, however, be interpreted in light of an important distinction between factorial validity and scale‐reliability. Although the original “8‐factor” solution demonstrated acceptable structural coherence at the latent level, our positivity comes with a fair amount of soberness. The scale‐reliability of the original factors within the IPS must be improved. All IPS subscales demonstrated a reliability score varying between questionable and poor, except one: “negative affect” which demonstrated a good score. Such a low level of reliability across the measurement tool raises substantial concerns regarding the interpretability and clinical utility of several observed IPS subscale scores. For instance, low reliability increases the risk that observed score differences reflect measurement error rather than meaningful therapeutic change. While the present findings support the theoretical organization of the original 8‐factor model, we ask the developing team to reflect on whether current evidence sufficiently supports their claims that the IPS is a reliable measurement tool; given that it does not achieve acceptable levels of reliability, has undergone only minimal construct validation, and even then, only in the 8‐factor version. While we appreciate the team's eagerness to implement this measurement tool, the IPS remains clearly under development. All but the original 8‐factor structure—which in turn suffers from unreliability—showed a very poor fit of data. This is evident not only in our current study but also in the developers' own studies (Pinsof et al. 2009, 2015).

In terms of clinically consequential use: If therapists misunderstand their clients' therapeutic start‐point and therapeutic progress due to a structurally flawed and unreliable measurement scale, the therapist (and client) may be misled into thinking that therapeutic strengths, difficulties, or therapeutic progress are real, while in fact, it is due to measurement error rather than meaningful clinical change. In our view, several IPS factors (i.e., subscales) require further psychometric refinement before the measurement tool can be considered sufficiently robust for widespread clinical application.

Importantly, our findings suggest that refinement efforts should build upon the original 8‐factor structure. After all, the underlying 8‐factor structure is coherent and demonstrated a clear superiority to any other factorial alternative, both the alternatives suggested by the developers and other data‐driven alternatives. This highlights the original 8‐factor solution as the preferred solution for further scale development. As such, we call upon the developers to “go back to the future,” by exercising substantial caution regarding the current clinical interpretation and practical application of IPS until further development and subsequent research clarify construct validity and improve scale reliability.

We examined item‐level response distributions, including skewness and kurtosis. Overall, the items demonstrated acceptable distributional characteristics. However, a small subset of clinically relevant, low base‐rate risk items showed pronounced floor effects. The observed floor effects occurred in clinically important low base‐rate items, where skewed response distributions are theoretically expected. These included: IPS 08 (“Thought about ending your life”; skewness = −1.94, kurtosis = 2.98), IPS_11 (“Thought about seriously harming or killing someone”; skewness = −5.02, kurtosis = 27.25), IPS 13 (“Had urges or impulses that you could not control”; skewness = −1.38), IPS_14 (“Drank too much alcohol”; skewness = −1.75, kurtosis = 2.28), and IPS 15 (“Used illegal drugs/misused prescribed medication”; skewness = −6.76, kurtosis = 55.48). These distributional patterns are consistent with the low prevalence of such behaviors. Given the limited number of affected items and their theoretical relevance, the observed deviations were not considered likely to meaningfully influence the CFA results. Importantly, all competing models included the same set of items; thus, any potential impact of non‐normality would be constant across models and unlikely to bias model comparisons.

Across 841 participants and 22 IPS items, 117 item responses were missing out of a total of 18,502 possible responses (= 0.63%). Missingness was dispersed across items and participants, with no indication of systematic non‐response. Given the very low level of missingness (<1%), the impact on parameter estimates, model fit, and reliability coefficients is negligible. However, to handle the missing data, we implemented Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML), which uses all available information under the assumption of Missing at Random (MAR).

Model comparisons were conducted using both chi‐square difference testing and differences in information criteria (AIC). Where models were nested (e.g., specified through parameter constraints such as factor collapsing), chi‐square difference tests were used. In all comparisons, relative model fit was also evaluated using standard fit indices and differences in AIC.

To account for possible clustering, we investigated a demographically meaningful subsample defined by gender, given that the data were collected in a family therapy context, in which, gender is clinically relevant. The relative model comparison pattern was consistent with that observed in the full sample. The 8‐factor model fit well with females (χ2 (181) = 407,65, p <0.001, χ2/df = 2,55, IFI = 0.935, CFI = 0.933, RMSEA = 0.054, AIC = 595,65) as well as males (χ2 (181) = 416,22, p <0.001, χ2/df = 2,30, IFI = 0.927, CFI = 0.925, RMSEA = 0.057, AIC = 604,22). Conversely, the 1‐factor model fit the data poorly for females (χ2 (209) = 1217,98, p <0.001, χ2/df = 5,83, IFI = 0.640, CFI = 0.702, RMSEA = 0.105, AIC = 1349,98) as well as males (χ2 (209) = 994,54, p <0.001, χ2/df = 4,76, IFI = 0.754, CFI = 0.750, RMSEA = 0.096, AIC = 1126,54). These results closely mirror the results of the full sample.
