Paper BOAT Bruhat Order and Alcovic Tilings
Federico Castillo, Damian de la Fuente, Nicolas Libedinsky, David Plaza

Abstract
We derive a formula for computing the size of lower Bruhat intervals for elements in the dominant cone of an affine Weyl group of type A A . This enumeration problem is reduced to counting lattice points in certain polyhedra. Our main tool is a decomposition—or tiling—of each interval into smaller, combinatorially tractable pieces, which we call paper boats. We also conjecture a generalization of this formula to all affine Weyl groups, restricted to elements in the lowest two-sided Kazhdan–Lusztig cell, which contains almost all of the elements.



A note on the title

The title of this paper reflects two aspects of this work, though the connection between them emerged unexpectedly.
Initially, we referred to the sets that we introduce as “paper boats” due to their visual resemblance in certain cases to actual paper boats (see the green objects in
Figure

1(a)
).
Subsequently, we observed that this name could also serve as an acronym for “Bruhat Order and Alcovic Tilings”, which is the central theme of the paper (see
Figures

1(a)
and
1(b)
).

Another interesting aspect of this work is the conjecture (partially established in this paper) that the resulting formulas, when restricted to some specific sets, are polynomials.
This is a scenario that exceeds expectations in both simplicity and elegance.
To elaborate briefly, let
\Pi^{+}
denote the fundamental parallelepiped spanned by the fundamental coweights.
In (
2.15

\mathfrak{B}
can be tiled using the set
\{\Pi^{+}+\lambda\mid\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}\}

This tessellation is illustrated in

\Pi^{+}

(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
.
The aforementioned polynomiality phenomenon asserts that, for a fixed alcove
x\subset\Pi^{+}
, the size of the Bruhat interval
[\text{id},x+\lambda]
is a polynomial in the coordinates of
\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
expressed in the basis of fundamental coweights.

Three observations are worth highlighting.
First, within each tile
\Pi^{+}+\lambda
there is exactly one element of
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
, so this polynomiality phenomenon extends that of

[
7
]
.
Second, the emergence of polynomiality from this tiling provides yet another instance of the deep connection between alcove geometry (via the Bruhat order) and Euclidean geometry.
Finally, and in alignment with the previous observation, we have verified computationally—quite unexpectedly—that all polynomials that arise in this context, even when extended to the full cell
\mathfrak{C}
, are
geometric polynomials
.
That is, they are linear combinations (with coefficients that do not depend on
\lambda
) of the volumes of the faces of the permutohedron
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
, which is defined as the convex hull of the
W_{\mathrm{f}}
-orbit of
\lambda

This was proven for
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
in
[
7
]
, and this observation strongly suggests that the tessellation using
\Pi^{+}
introduced here is the natural and correct framework for this study.
Let us briefly explain the
main contributions of this paper.

Bruhat order criterion in alcovic terms

Elements in
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
have nice algebraic properties that arbitrary elements do not share; see
Lemma

2.5
.
As a consequence
[
7
, Eq. (3.3)]
, their lower Bruhat intervals can essentially be reduced to ideals in the dominance order.
The latter is significantly more accessible than the former, which simplifies matters considerably.
To extend our results beyond
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
, we require a criterion for determining whether
x\leq y
, not merely as abstract elements of the affine Weyl group, but specifically as alcoves.
We establish a criterion for the dominant region in type
A
: comparing alcoves reduces to comparing their corresponding vertices with respect to the dominance order.
More precisely we have the following proposition.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
be an affine Weyl group of type
A
,
V
the set of vertices of the fundamental alcove, and

x,y\in\mathfrak{B}
.
Then,

x\leq y\qquad\mbox{if and only if}\qquad x(v)\leq y(v),\ \forall\ v\in V,

where the left-hand relation denotes the (strong) Bruhat order and the right-hand relation denotes the dominance order.

This is the content of
Proposition

3.1
.
The criterion for the entire group was originally mentioned by Schremmer in
[
19
, §3]
.
We warmly thank him for sharing a sketch of the proof, which we have expanded into the detailed version presented in

Section

6
.

Tiling lower intervals

To describe our next result, we first introduce some notation.
For
w\in W_{\mathrm{aff}}
, where
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
is an affine Weyl group, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{w}
the corresponding alcove.
Let
(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
denote the set of dominant coweights.
We define

\mathcal{F}=\{a\in W_{\mathrm{aff}}\mid\mathcal{A}_{a}\subset-\Pi^{+}\},

and for
\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
and
a\in\mathcal{F}
, we let
\theta_{a}(\lambda)
be the unique element in
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
satisfying

\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{a}(\lambda)}=\mathcal{A}_{aw_{0}}+\lambda
.

The region
\mathfrak{B}
(resp.
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
) consists of elements in
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
of the form
\theta_{a}(\lambda)
(resp.
\theta_{\mathrm{id}}(\lambda)
).
Consider the Bruhat intervals of the form

\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda):=[\mathrm{id},\theta_{a}(\lambda)]=\{x\in W_{\mathrm{aff}}\mid x\leq\theta_{a}(\lambda)\}.

The following definition introduces the central combinatorial object of this paper.

Definition 1.2
.

The
Paper Boat
associated with
\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
and
a\in\mathcal{F}
is defined as

PB_{a}(\lambda):=\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)\setminus\bigcup_{\mu\lessdot\lambda}\mathcal{I}_{a}(\mu),

(1.1)

where
\mu\lessdot\lambda
denotes a covering relation in the poset
(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
ordered by dominance.

A key observation is that lower intervals for elements in
\mathfrak{B}
are invariant under the left action of
W_{\mathrm{f}}
. This allows us to focus on their dominant part. The fundamental building blocks of our tiling are the Paper Boats.
This structure is illustrated in
Figures

1(a)
and
1(b)
.

The next theorem establishes that Paper Boats provide a tessellation of lower Bruhat intervals for elements in
\mathfrak{B}
.
For the rest of the Introduction, all results stated will be for the affine Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
of type
A_{n}
.

Theorem A
.

Let
\lambda
be a dominant coweight, and let
a\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then we have the following decomposition:

\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)=\bigsqcup_{\mu\leq\lambda}PB_{a}(\mu),

(1.2)

where the union runs over all
\mu\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
satisfying
\mu\leq\lambda
in the dominance order.

In our previous work, we established a tiling
[
7
, Theorem A]
for lower Bruhat intervals
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{id}}(\lambda)
(
i.e.
for elements in
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}
).
This tiling led to a formula for the size of these intervals due to two key properties.
First, among all intervals there is only one type of tile, consisting of the alcoves of the finite Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{f}}
.
Second, for a given
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{id}}(\lambda)
, the number of tiles in the corresponding tiling corresponds to the number of lattice points in the permutohedron
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
.

The tiling presented in
Theorem

A
, this time for general elements in
\mathfrak{B}
, retains similar properties.
The first difference is that now there is more than one type of tile.
In
Definition

3.11
we introduce a finite partition
\mathcal{Z}
of the dominant coweights.
The following theorem shows that, among all possible tilings, this new tiling features a finite number of distinct types of tiles.

Theorem B
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{F}
and
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
If
\lambda,\mu\in Z
, then the dominant alcoves in
PB_{a}(\lambda)
are a translation of the dominant alcoves in
PB_{a}(\mu)
.
In particular,
|PB_{a}(\lambda)|=|PB_{a}(\mu)|.

It is worth noting that the definition of Paper Boats can be generalized to all affine Weyl groups and to all elements of
\mathfrak{C}
.
We conjecture that there is a tiling as in
Theorem

A
with a property as in
Theorem

B
, at this level of generality (see Conjectures
2.12
and
2.13
).

Formulas for sizes of intervals.

Combining
Theorem

A
and
Theorem

B
, we derive the following theorem and our main counting formula.
For a dominant coweight
\lambda
, let
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
be the component of
\Lambda^{\vee}
(modulo the coroot lattice) containing
\lambda
.
We let
\mathfrak{I}_{\lambda}
be the dominant lattice points (within
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
) in the permutohedron
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
, see
Equation

2.33
.

Theorem C
.

Let
\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
and let
a\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then

|\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)|=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}c_{a}(Z)|\mathfrak{I}_{\lambda}\cap Z|,

where
c_{a}(Z)
is
|PB_{a}(\mu)|
for any
\mu\in Z
.

As we mentioned above, the first difference between our previous tiling for elements in
\mathord{\text{\textgoth{A}}}

[
7
]
and our new tiling for elements in
\mathfrak{B}
(
1.2
), is that we now have to deal with the sizes of Paper Boats
c_{a}(Z)
.
For example in
Figure

1(a)
(resp.
Figure

1(b)
), the blue tile at the bottom, for instance, has size five (resp. three).
The second difference is that, after a reinterpretation of this formula as a
weighted lattice point count
(see (
5.4
)), the number of tiles, of the same type, is equal to the number of lattice points in slices of permutohedra; whereas in
[
7
]
the relevant polytopes where always permutohedra.

Sizes of Paper Boats:

This is an intriguing problem even in type
\widetilde{A}_{n}
.
In
Proposition

4.1
we obtain a closed formula for the size of Paper Boats
c_{a}(Z)
, when
Z
is sufficiently deep inside the dominant chamber; their size is equal to
(n+1)!^{2}
, which does not depend on
a
.
This is particularly striking given that the shapes of Paper Boats can vary dramatically.
See the green regions highlighted in both
Figures

1(a)
and
1(b)
for type
\widetilde{A}_{2}
and
Figure

3
for
\widetilde{A}_{3}
.
More generally, we conjecture that, for any type, the size of Paper Boats associated with coweights sufficiently deep inside the dominant chamber equals
|W_{\mathrm{f}}|^{2}
.

Counting lattice points:

In
Section

5
, we see each
\mathfrak{I}_{\lambda}\cap Z
as the lattice points in convex polytopes.
These lattice points are depicted in
Figure

1(c)
, where each color represents a different zone
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
One technical difficulty is that we have to consider polytopes whose vertices are not lattice points (
Lemma

5.7
) but rather rational points.
Using general results about counting lattice points in polyhedra
[
1
]
, we prove that the number of lattice points in a rational polytope agrees with a quasi-polynomial, leading to the following theorem.

Theorem D
.

Let
\lambda\in(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}
and
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
its coordinates in the basis of fundamental coweights.
For a fixed
a\in\mathcal{F}
the function
q_{a}:\mathbb{N}^{n}\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\mapsto|\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)|
,
agrees with a
quasi-polynomial
of degree
n
on the subset
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}^{n}
where each coordinate is at least two.
Moreover, the top homogeneous component is a polynomial and it is independent of
a
.

A quasi-polynomial on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of degree
d
is a function that agrees with a polynomial function of degree
d
on cosets of a subgroup
H
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of finite index, see
Definition

5.9
.
The most basic example is the floor function
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

The quasi-polynomial
q_{a}
is obtained as a linear combination of quasi-polynomials, and the coefficients in this combination are the sizes of different Paper Boats.
But
Theorem

D
is not the end of the story.
After extensive computations, we have consistently found that
q_{a}
is actually a polynomial.
This is quite unexpected as the quasi-polynomials composing
q_{a}
are definitely not polynomials in general.
It suggests the presence of algebraic relations between the sizes
c_{a}(Z)
to be discovered.

Furthermore, in every case our computations show that the polynomial is also geometric.
To be more precise, the polynomials are rational combinations of volume polynomials from faces of permutohedra.

Conjecture 1.3
.

For any affine Weyl group the map
q_{a}
is a geometric polynomial.

We remark that the polinomiality and the geometricity parts of this conjecture are both difficult.
Heuristically the polinomiality part suggests that
q_{a}
is as simple as one could reasonably expect, while the geometricity part indicates that, among the full set of polynomials, these are exceptionally well-behaved and hint to a deeper connection with convex geometry.
In
Theorem

4.8
, we verify this conjecture for type
\widetilde{A}_{3}
, and we also show it in type
\widetilde{A}_{n}
for the top homogeneous part of
q_{a}
in
Theorem

5.13
.

We further conjecture an extension of
Conjecture

1.3
to lower intervals coming from elements in
\mathfrak{C}
(rather than
\mathfrak{B}
).
See
Conjecture

4.10
for more details.
In this general form, it holds for types
\widetilde{A}_{1}
(trivially),
\widetilde{A}_{2}
(see
[
13
]
) and
\widetilde{B}_{2}
(see
[
2
]
).
We have not found a counterexample in types
\widetilde{A}_{3}
and
\widetilde{B}_{3}
.

We also introduce a parametrization of the lowest two-sided Kazhdan-Lusztig cell and define the Paper Boat, our main object of study.

,

.

2  The Paper Boat

In this section, we introduce the Paper Boat associated with any element in the lowest two-sided Kazhdan-Lusztig cell, which is the central object of study in this paper. To achieve this, we begin by recalling key facts about affine Weyl groups. Additionally, we present a parametrization for the elements within the lowest two-sided Kazhdan-Lusztig cell and some of its properties.

2.1
Affine Weyl groups

Let
\Phi
be an irreducible root system of rank
n
, and let
E
be the Euclidean space spanned by
\Phi
, with inner product
\langle-,-\rangle:E\times E\to\mathbb{R}
.
We denote by
\mathbf{0}
the origin of
E
.
Let
{\Phi^{+}}
be a choice of positive roots and
\Delta\subset\Phi^{+}
the simple root basis.
We denote by
\rho
the half-sum of the positive roots.
Let
{\alpha^{\vee}=2\alpha/\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}
be the coroot corresponding to a root
{\alpha\in\Phi}
.
The set
\Phi^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}\mid\alpha\in\Phi\}
is also a root system.
The fundamental weights
\varpi_{\alpha}
and fundamental coweights
\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}
are defined by the equations
{\langle\varpi_{\alpha},\beta^{\vee}\rangle=\langle\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee},\beta\rangle}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
.
Both sets form a basis of
E
.
We denote by
\displaystyle\Lambda^{\vee}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{Z}\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}
the set of coweights.
We define

C^{+}=\{x\in E\mid\langle x,\alpha\rangle\geq 0,\,\mbox{ for all }\alpha\in\Delta\}.

We refer to
{(\Lambda^{\vee})^{+}}\coloneqq\Lambda^{\vee}\cap C^{+}
as the set of dominant coweights.

We denote by
\leq
the dominance order on
\Lambda^{\vee}
, that is,
{\mu\leq\lambda}
if the coordinates of
{\lambda-\mu}
in the simple coroot basis
\{\alpha^{\vee}\}_{\alpha\in\Delta}
are non-negative.

Remark 2.1
.

In this paper we use the symbol
\leq
for different orders: for the standard ordering on real numbers, the Bruhat order on elements of the affine Weyl group, and for the dominance order on elements of the (co)weight lattice.
Since they are all on different objects, from context it should be clear which one we are using.

For
\alpha\in\Phi
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we define the hyperplane

H_{\alpha,k}=\{\lambda\in E\mid\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle=k\}.

We denote by
s_{\alpha,k}
the affine reflection along the hyperplane
H_{\alpha,k}
.
We write
H_{\alpha}=H_{\alpha,0}
and
s_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha,0}
.
The affine Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
of
\Phi
is the group of affine transformations of
E
generated by
s_{\alpha,k}
, for
\alpha\in\Phi
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

An alcove is a connected component of the space
E-\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi,k\in\mathbb{Z}}H_{\alpha,k}.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of all alcoves.
We define the fundamental alcove
\mathcal{A_{\text{id}}}\in\mathcal{A}
by

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}=\{x\in E\mid-1<\langle x,\alpha\rangle<0,\ \forall\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\}.

The walls of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}
are the hyperplanes
\{H_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\}
, together with
H_{\widetilde{\alpha},-1}
, where
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\Phi
is the highest root.
We set
{s_{0}=s_{\widetilde{\alpha},-1}}
and
{S=\{s_{0},s_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\}}
.
Then the pair
{(W_{\mathrm{aff}},S)}
is a Coxeter system, with length function
\ell
and
Bruhat order
\leq
.

The action of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
on
E
gives a simply transitive action on
\mathcal{A}
.
Thus, we have a bijection
W_{\mathrm{aff}}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
given by
w\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{w}=w(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}})
.
We define the vertices of an alcove
\mathcal{A}_{w}
to be the vertices of its closure
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{w}}
, and denote this set by
V(w)
.
For
X\subset W_{\mathrm{aff}}
, we write

\mathcal{A}(X)=\bigcup_{x\in X}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{x}}.

(2.1)

If
Y\subset E
is such that
Y=\mathcal{A}(X)
, we write
\mathcal{A}^{-1}(Y)=X
.

We have a geometric interpretation of the length function.
Concretely,

\ell(w)=\#\{H=H_{\alpha,k}\mid H\mbox{ separates }\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}\mbox{ and }\mathcal{A}_{w}\}.

(2.2)

The (finite) Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{f}}
is the parabolic subgroup of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
generated by the set
S_{\mathrm{f}}=\{s_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\}
.
We denote by
w_{0}
the longest element of
W_{\mathrm{f}}
.
The affine Weyl group can also be realized as a semi-direct product:
{W_{\mathrm{aff}}\cong\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\vee}\rtimes W_{\mathrm{f}}}
, where the elements of
\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\vee}
act on
E
as translations.
We define
W_{\mathrm{aff}}^{+}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(C^{+})
and
W_{\mathrm{aff}}^{-}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(C^{-})
, where
C^{-}=-C^{+}
.
We have

W_{\mathrm{aff}}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W_{\mathrm{f}}}wW_{\mathrm{aff}}^{+}.

(2.3)

Consider the extended affine Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{e}}=\Lambda^{\vee}\rtimes W_{\mathrm{f}}
and the subgroup

\Omega=\{\sigma\in W_{\mathrm{e}}\mid\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}\}.

(2.4)

For
\sigma\in\Omega
and
w\in W_{\mathrm{aff}}
we write
w_{\sigma}\coloneqq\sigma w\sigma^{-1}
. We have
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{w})=\mathcal{A}_{w_{\sigma}}
. For
X\subset W_{\mathrm{aff}}
we write
X_{\sigma}=\sigma X\sigma^{-1}\subset W_{\mathrm{aff}}
.
Each
\sigma\in\Omega
permutes the walls of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}
and thus conjugation by
\sigma
permutes the simple reflections
S
of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
,
i.e.
,
S_{\sigma}=S
.
Note that
(W_{\mathrm{f}})_{\sigma}\cong W_{\mathrm{f}}
is the maximal parabolic subgroup of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
corresponding to
(S_{\mathrm{f}})_{\sigma}=S\setminus\{(s_{0})_{\sigma}\}
, whose longest element is
(w_{0})_{\sigma}
.
In fact, all maximal parabolic subgroups of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
arise in this way.

It is well-known
[
4
, Prop VI.2.3.6]
that the set
\{-\sigma(\mathbf{0})\mid\sigma\in\Omega\}
forms a complete system of representatives of
\Lambda^{\vee}/\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\vee}
.
Hence, for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{\vee}
, there is a unique
\sigma_{\lambda}\in\Omega
such that

\lambda\equiv-\sigma_{\lambda}(\mathbf{0})\hskip-5.69046pt\pmod{\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\vee}}.

(2.5)

Note that if
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\vee}
, then
\sigma_{\lambda}=\mathrm{id}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The group
\Omega
can be seen as a subgroup of the automorphism group of the Dynkin diagram of
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
.
Indeed, if
\sigma\in\Omega
,
by definition
\sigma
is an isometry satisfying
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{id}}
.
Therefore, it permutes the generators
S
of the Coxeter system
(W_{\mathrm{aff}},S)
while respecting its defining relations.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
I,J\subset S
, we define

Lemma 3.10

Let

On the other hand, (

Proposition 4.1

(4.4)

Consider

To prove the proposition, it is enough to prove that

We first show that

By definition of

2.

(recall

3.12

1.

On the one hand, since

\langle t_{\mu}w(-\varpi_{i}),\beta\rangle\geq 2

and all

On the other hand, since

In other words, there exists

.
Therefore, every vertex

(4.5)

), this implies that
\eta_{\alpha,v}\neq 0
(recall that

for all

\beta\neq\alpha

\alpha\in\Delta_{v}
.

\eta_{\alpha,v}
there exists some

, so that

\langle v_{j}+\kappa_{v},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle=0.

(4.6)

A combination of (
4.3
) and (

) yields
\langle v_{j}+\kappa_{v},\alpha\rangle\leq 0
.
Recall that
v_{j}=w(-\varpi_{j})-v(-\varpi_{j})
.
We have
2\leq\langle t_{\mu}w(-\varpi_{j}),\alpha\rangle=\langle v_{j}+\kappa_{v},\alpha\rangle+\langle t_{\mu-\kappa_{v}}v(-\varpi_{j}),\alpha\rangle\leq 0
.

This contradiction proves
1
.

Applying

3.1
, we see that

and will derive a contradiction.
By part

and

3.1
, we get

\mathbf{0}\leq\kappa_{v}+v_{i}-\alpha,\qquad\forall\,i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}

(4.7)

in the dominance order.
In particular,
i=0
yields
\alpha\leq\kappa_{v}
, so
-\eta_{\alpha,v}\geq 1
, and in particular
\eta_{\alpha,v}\neq 0,

thus
\alpha\in\Delta_{v}
. As before (
4.6
) holds for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
By pairing (
4.7
) for
i=j
with
\varpi_{\alpha}

.
This contradiction proves

We have shown that
\phi
is well defined.

The injectivity of the map
\phi
follows directly from the definition of semidirect product.
Let us now prove that
\phi
is surjective.
Let
t_{\mu-\gamma}v\in PB_{a}^{+}(\lambda)
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\Phi
and

.
We must show that
\gamma=\kappa_{v}
.

We begin by noticing that, since
t_{\mu-\gamma}v\leq t_{\mu}w
,

implies that
-v_{i}\leq\gamma
for all
0\leq i\leq n
in the dominance order.
Therefore,
(-v_{0})\vee\cdots\vee(-v_{n})=\kappa_{v}\leq\gamma
.

\Delta_{v}=\operatorname{Supp}(\gamma)

\operatorname{Supp}(\gamma)=\{\alpha\in\Delta\mid\langle\gamma,\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle\neq 0\}
.

\langle\gamma,\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle\leq\langle\kappa_{v},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle=-\eta_{\alpha,v}
for all
\alpha\in\Delta_{v}
.

1
.

0\leq\kappa_{v}\leq\gamma
we have
\Delta_{v}\subset\operatorname{Supp}(\gamma)
.

\alpha\in\operatorname{Supp}(\gamma)\setminus\Delta_{v}
.

we have

\langle\gamma-\alpha+v_{i},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle=\langle\gamma,\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle-1+\langle v_{i},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle\geq 1-1+\langle v_{i},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle\geq 0,

(4.8)

\gamma\geq 0

4.3
) implies that
0\leq\gamma+v_{i}
for all

\beta\in\Delta

\langle\gamma-\alpha+v_{i},\varpi_{\beta}\rangle=\langle\gamma+v_{i},\varpi_{\beta}\rangle\geq 0

(4.9)

\mathbf{0}\leq\gamma-\alpha+v_{i}
for all

.

3.1
implies that
t_{\mu-\gamma}v\leq t_{\mu-\alpha}w

\langle\gamma,\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle>-\eta_{\alpha,v}

\langle\gamma+v_{i},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle>-\eta_{\alpha,v}+\langle v_{i},\varpi_{\alpha}\rangle\geq-\eta_{\alpha,v}+\eta_{\alpha,v}=0.

We have proved that

4.2
Paper boats in terms of lower intervals

In this section we use the principle of inclusion-exclusion to obtain a formula for the size of a Paper Boat in terms of lower Bruhat intervals.
Recall the weight poset
\Lambda
and its join (
3.7
) and meet (
3.6
) operators.
For
J\subset\Phi
, we define
\displaystyle\vee_{J}=\bigvee_{\alpha\in J}\alpha
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{F}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
.
Then

c_{a}(\lambda)=\sum_{J\subset\Phi^{+}(\lambda)}(-1)^{|J|}\,\left|\,\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\vee_{J})\,\right|.

(4.11)

In the proof of
Proposition

4.3
, we will make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
,
a\in\mathcal{F}
and
\emptyset\neq J\subset\Phi^{+}(\lambda)
.
Then,

\bigcap_{\alpha\in J}\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\alpha)=\mathcal{I}_{a}\left({\bigwedge_{\alpha\in J}}(\lambda-\alpha)\right)=\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\vee_{J}).

(4.12)

Proof.

We note that by Equations (
3.7
), (
3.6
) and the fact that
\wedge^{+}
is the restriction of
\wedge
to the dominant weights, we have

{\bigwedge_{\alpha\in J}}^{+}\left(\lambda-\alpha\right)=\bigwedge_{\alpha\in J}\left(\lambda-\alpha\right)=\lambda-\vee_{J}.

(4.13)

Therefore,
\bigwedge_{\alpha\in J}\left(\lambda-\alpha\right)=\lambda-\vee_{J}
is a dominant weight, so that (
4.12
) makes sense.
The first equality in
Equation

4.12
is a direct application of
Lemma

3.7
, whereas the second equality follows from (
4.13
).
∎

Proof of
Proposition

4.3
.

Note that

\displaystyle-\left|\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}(\lambda)}\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\alpha)\right|\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subset\Phi^{+}(\lambda)}(-1)^{|J|}\left|\bigcap_{\alpha\in J}\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\alpha)\right|\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subset\Phi^{+}(\lambda)}(-1)^{|J|}\left|\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda-\vee_{J})\right|,

(4.14)

where the first equality follows from the inclusion-exclusion principle and the second one is implied by
Lemma

4.4
.
By the definition of
c_{a}(\lambda)
, (
4.14
) implies (
4.11
).

It follows that we can define

u=\min\{1\leq b\leq k\mid i_{b}\neq j_{b}\}.

(5.9)

Let
\mathbf{B}=(b_{ij})
.
We split
\mathbf{B}
in blocks as follows

\mathbf{B}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}X_{11}&X_{12}&X_{13}\\
\hline\cr X_{21}&b_{uu}&X_{23}\\
\hline\cr X_{31}&X_{32}&X_{33}\\
\end{array}\right],

(5.10)

where
X_{11}
and
X_{33}
are square matrices of size
u-1
and
k-u
, respectively.
We notice that
b_{uu}=-1
or
b_{uu}=0
.

We first treat the case when
b_{uu}=0
.
Let us assume that this zero was located above the diagonal in
\mathbf{A}_{n}
.
In this case, we have that
X_{12}
,
X_{13}
and
X_{23}
are the zero matrix.
Therefore,
\det(B)=0
.
The case when the zero is located under the diagonal in
\mathbf{A}_{n}
is analogous.

We now suppose that
b_{uu}=-1
.
Let us assume that this
-1
was located above the diagonal in
\mathbf{A}_{n}
.
The case when the
-1
is located below the diagonal is entirely analogous and is left to the reader.
As before, we have that
X_{12}
,
X_{13}
and
X_{23}
are the zero matrix.
It follows that

|\det(\mathbf{B})|=|\det(X_{11})|\cdot|\det(X_{33})|.

(5.11)

Note that splitting
\mathbf{A}_{n}
as in (
5.10
), we see that
X_{11}
(resp.
X_{33}
) is a submatrix of
\mathbf{A}_{u-1}
(resp.
\mathbf{A}_{n-u}
).
We can apply our inductive hypothesis to
X_{11}
and
X_{33}
to obtain partitions
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r})
and
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s})
such that:

1.

\nu
is a partition of
0\leq c\leq u-1
;

2.

\eta
is a partition of
0\leq d\leq n-u
;

3.

r\leq u-c
and
s\leq n-u+1-d
;

4.

|\det(X_{11})|=(\nu_{1}+1)(\nu_{2}+1)\cdots(\nu_{r}+1)
and
|\det(X_{33})|=(\eta_{1}+1)(\eta_{2}+1)\cdots(\eta_{s}+1)
.

Let
\mu
be the partition of
m\coloneqq c+d
that is obtained by reordering in decreasing order the composition
(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s})
.
We notice that

.
On the other hand,
\mu
has

parts and

.
Thus by (

),

satisfies (

).
This proves the first claim in the proposition.

To see the second claim, we first notice that

(\mu_{1}+1)+(\mu_{2}+1)+\cdots+(\mu_{\ell}+1)=m+\ell\leq n+1.

By the integrality of the multinomial coefficients we have that
(\mu_{1}+1)!(\mu_{2}+1)!\cdots(\mu_{\ell}+1)!
divides
(m+\ell)!
, which in turns divides
(n+1)!
.
Since clearly

divides
(\mu_{1}+1)!(\mu_{2}+1)!\cdots(\mu_{\ell}+1)!
, the proposition follows.
∎

.

Let

be a dominant weight.

5.2
Quasipolynomiality

Definition 5.9
.

A function
q:\mathbb{N}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a
quasi-polynomial
of degree
d
if there exists a lattice (a discrete subgroup)
\Gamma
of rank
n
such that
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
cosets
g_{1}+\mathbb{Z}^{n},\dots,g_{M}+\mathbb{Z}^{n}
together with polynomial functions
p_{1},\dots,p_{M}
all of degree
d
, such that
q(\gamma)=p_{k}(\gamma)
whenever
\gamma\in g_{k}+\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Quasi-polynomials appear in many different contexts, see
[
24
]
for a survey.
If
q
is a quasi-polynomial then its generating function has the following rather simple description

\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}q(\mathbf{n})X^{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{p(X)}{(1-X^{\mathbf{b}_{1}})\dots(1-X^{\mathbf{b}_{k}})},

(5.13)

where
p(X)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
.

Example 5.10
.

Let
\mathsf{P}=[0,1/2]
be a 1-dimensional polytope.
The function
q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}
defined as
q(n)=|nP\cap\mathbb{Z}|
is the quasi-polynomial given by
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
.
This function has two different polynomial expressions in the cosets
2\mathbb{Z}
and
1+2\mathbb{Z}
.
In this case the generating function is

\sum_{n\geq 0}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1)X^{n}=\frac{1+X}{(1-X^{2})^{2}}.

The main technical result we use is
[
15
, Theorem 7]
, which we restate here in a slightly different form.

Theorem 5.11

(McMullen)
.

Let
E
be a real vector space of dimension
n
,
\Gamma
a lattice, and
\mathsf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{P}_{k}
be polytopes in
E
with vertices in
\Gamma\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
.
Then we have that the function
q:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{Z}
defined as

q(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})=|\left(\lambda_{1}\mathsf{P}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}\mathsf{P}_{k}\right)\cap\Gamma|,

(5.14)

is a quasi-polynomial of degree at most
n
over the subgroup
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}N_{i}\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
where

N_{i}:=\min\left\{m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\penalty\ :\penalty\ m_{i}\mathsf{P}_{i}\text{ has vertices in }\Gamma\right\}

(5.15)

Furthermore, let
q^{\mathsf{top}}
be the top homogeneous component of
q
.
Then
q^{\mathsf{top}}
is a polynomial, and it satisfies the following relation

\relvol\left(\lambda_{1}\mathsf{P}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}\mathsf{P}_{k}\right)=q^{\mathsf{top}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}).

(5.16)

The left-hand side of
eq.

5.16
refers to the
relative volume
of a polytope with integral vertices but perhaps contained in a linear subspace
L
.
The relative volume is a measure on
L
normalized so that the fundamental parallelepiped of the lattice
\Gamma\cap L
, in
L
, has relative volume equal to one.
A priori,
q^{\mathsf{top}}
is merely a quasi-polynomial.
It is a classical result that the relative volume of a Minkowski sum is a polynomial, as in
eq.

5.16
(cf.
[
8
, Chapter 4]
).
The last part on the homogeneous top component can be easily deduced from the integral version
[
17
, Theorem A.3]
.
This in particular implies that if
\sum\lambda_{i}\mathsf{P}_{i}
is not full dimensional, then the quasi-polynomial has degree strictly lower than
n
.
The statement in
[
15
]
uses a different definition of
N_{i}
, and we chose this one (which is a multiple of the original) for simplicity.

We now have all the key ingredients to prove the next proposition.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{i}\in\left\{0,1,2\right\}^{n}
and write
\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{N}_{\geq i_{j}}
.
The function
q_{\boldsymbol{i}}:\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\to\mathbb{Z}
given by

q_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})=|\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}\Phi+\lambda\right)|,\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\varpi_{i},

is a quasi-polynomial in the coordinates
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, that is, when both
\lambda
and
\lambda-\varpi(\boldsymbol{i})
are dominant weights.
Furthermore,
q^{\mathsf{top}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})=\relvol\left(\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu\coloneqq\lambda-\varpi(\boldsymbol{i})
.
We have
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\varpi_{i}
, with
\mu_{j}=\lambda_{j}-i_{j}
.
Note that
Lemma

5.7
gives

\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)-\lambda=\big(\mathsf{P}^{+}(\mu)\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}\big)-\mu.

(5.17)

Therefore,

\big(\mathsf{P}^{+}(\mu)\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}\big)-\mu=\Big[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathsf{P}^{+}(\varpi_{i})\Big)\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big]-\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\big[\big(\mathsf{P}^{+}(\varpi_{i})\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}\big)-\varpi_{i}\big],

(5.18)

where the first equality follows from
Lemma

5.6
, while the second one is easily derived from the definition of
V_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
On the other hand, by
eq.

5.17
, we get

q_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})=\big|\left(\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)-\lambda\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}\Phi\big|=\big|\left[\big(\mathsf{P}^{+}(\mu)\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}\big)-\mu\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}\Phi\big|.

(5.19)

Since
\mathsf{P}^{+}(\varpi_{i})\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}
is a face of
\mathsf{P}^{+}(\varpi_{i})
(cf.
Lemma

5.7
), the dilation
(n+1)!\mathsf{P}^{+}(\varpi_{i})\cap V_{\boldsymbol{i}}
has vertices in
\mathbb{Z}\Phi
, by
Corollary

5.5
.
Therefore, substituting (
5.18
) in (
5.19
) and applying
Theorem

5.11
, we get that the function
q_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
is a quasi-polynomial in the variables
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
, and that its top homogeneous component is

\relvol\left(\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)-\lambda\right)=\relvol\left(\mathsf{P}^{+}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\lambda)\right).

After performing the change of variables from
\mu_{i}
to
\lambda_{i}
, we get that
q_{\boldsymbol{i}}
is also quasipolynomial in these variables, and its top homogeneous component remains unaltered as
\mu_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}
differ only by a constant.
∎

Now we come to the crux of the section.
Recall that we proved
[
7
, Theorem A]
that the size of the lower Bruhat interval
\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)
for

is equal to
(n+1)!|\mathsf{P}(\lambda)\cap(\mathbb{Z}\Phi+\lambda)|
, which agrees with a polynomial function on
\lambda
.
The following theorem establishes a weaker result, concluding only quasi-polynomiality.
However, it offers greater generality, as it holds for general

and for weights deep enough in the dominant region.
Let
\boldsymbol{2}\coloneqq(2,\ldots,2)\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
.
In the notation of
Proposition

5.12
, recall that
\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{2}}
denotes the
n
-tuples of integers
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with all
\lambda_{i}\geq 2
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let

.
The function
q_{a}:\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
given by

q_{a}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\big|\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)\big|,\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\varpi_{i},

is a quasi-polynomial in
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, that is, when
\lambda\in Z(\boldsymbol{2})
.
Furthermore, it is of degree
n
and its top homogeneous component is given by

q^{\mathsf{top}}_{a}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})=(n+1)!\relvol\left(\mathsf{P}(\lambda)\right).

Proof.

We recall Equation (
5.4

|\mathcal{I}_{a}(\lambda)|=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\left\{0,1,2\right\}^{n}}c_{a}(\boldsymbol{i})|\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{+}(\lambda)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}\Phi+\lambda\right)|.

Since
\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{2}}\subset\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
for all
\boldsymbol{i}
, each

is non-empty.

Proposition

5.12
implies that the right-hand side agrees with a quasi-polynomial in

and so the left-hand side does too.
Note that

As the top homogeneous component is contributed by polytopes of maximal dimension, we get

q^{\mathsf{top}}_{a}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=c_{a}(\boldsymbol{2})|\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{2}}^{+}(\lambda)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}\Phi+\lambda\right)|=(n+1)!^{2}\relvol(\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{2}}^{+}(\lambda)),

(5.20)

where the second equality follows from Propositions
5.12
and
4.1
(see

).

The polytope
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
from

\mathsf{P}^{+}(\lambda)
.

.
∎

6  A criterion for Bruhat order in affine type A

The main goal of this section is to establish the criterion for the Bruhat order presented in
Proposition

3.1
. To do so, we first prove a generalized version of the criterion applicable to the entire affine Weyl group. Subsequently, we demonstrate that this generalized criterion simplifies significantly when comparing two elements within the dominant chamber.

In this section, we use the realization of a root system of type
A
introduced at the beginning of
Section

3
.
We recall that the vertices of the fundamental alcove are
V(\mathrm{id})=\{\mathbf{0}=\varpi_{0},-\varpi_{1},\ldots,-\varpi_{n}\}
.

6.1
Bruhat order in terms of dominance order

6.1.1
The group
S_{n+1}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\prime}

Let
S_{n+1}
be the symmetric group on
n+1
letters. It acts on the left on
\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
by permutation of coordinates,
i.e.
if
w\in S_{n+1}
and
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1},
we have

w\cdot(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})=(\mu_{w^{-1}(1)},\ldots,\mu_{w^{-1}(n+1)})
.
Let us define (
Lemma

6.1
will clarify the reasoning behind our choice of notation)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}\displaystyle

\displaystyle:=\{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}\mid 0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu_{i}\leq n\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}\ |\ \sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu_{i}=0\}\subset\Lambda^{\prime}.

For
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, define
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}:=(0,\ldots,0,1,-1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}\Phi^{\prime}
, where the
1
appears in the
i
-th position.
Similarly, we define
\varpi^{\prime}_{i}:=(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
, where the first
i
entries are equal to
1
, and the remaining
n+1-i
entries are equal to
0
.
We note that
\varpi^{\prime}_{i}\in\Lambda^{\prime}
for
0\leq i\leq n
.

Define the partial order
\preceq
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
as follows:
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})\preceq(\mu^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{\prime}_{n+1})
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu^{\prime}_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu^{\prime}_{i},\qquad\text{ for all }m=1,\ldots,n+1.

(6.1)

We say
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1})
is

if
\mu_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n+1}
. We denote by
\mu_{\text{dom}}
the unique dominant element in the

-orbit of
\mu
.
We notice that
\mu_{\text{dom}}
satisfies

for all

.

For
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, let
s_{i}\in S_{n+1}
be the transposition exchanging
i
and
i+1
.
It is well known that the map
h:S_{n+1}\to W_{\mathrm{f}}
given by
h(s_{i})=s_{\alpha_{i}}
for all
