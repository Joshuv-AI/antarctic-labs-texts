Association of age at menarche and post-pubertal body mass index: A comparative study among students from government and private schools in Bhubaneswar, Odisha
Authors: Snigdha Singh, Ayesha Das, Ipsa Mohapatra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Age at menarche (AAM) is a critical marker of pubertal timing and has been associated with various health outcomes, including Body Mass Index (BMI) in adolescence and adulthood which is an early predictor of cardiovascular risk in later life. Differences in socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, and lifestyle factors between government and private school students may influence this association. To assess the relationship between AAM and post-pubertal BMI among students from a government and a private school in Bhubaneswar, Odisha.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 180 late adolescent girls (16–19 years) (90 each from a government and a private school) in Bhubaneswar between February 2024 and October 2024. Multivariate linear regression model was built to assess the association between AAM and post-pubertal BMI in both the type of schools and strength of association between the two were compared.RESULTS:Among late adolescent girls in the government and private school, early age at puberty contributed to higher post-pubertal BMI which is a potential risk factor for CVD in later life. On comparing the relationship of exposure and the outcome by the type of school, association was stronger among the private school students (β coeff. = -1.37, 95%CI: -2.59, -0.16) in comparison to government school students (β coeff. = -0.66, 95%CI: -2.57, -0.14).CONCLUSION:AAM significantly influences post-pubertal BMI, with early menarche associated with higher BMI. Relationship is more pronounced among private school students, potentially reflecting socioeconomic and lifestyle differences.

Introduction

Menarche, defined as the first menstrual period in a woman’s life, is a landmark of pubertal development in girls.[12] Although menarche occurs quite late in the chain of pubertal events, normally following breast development and peak height velocity, puberty, it is a well-validated indicator and an easily remembered event when compared to other events in the process of female sexual maturity.[23]

The mean age at menarche (AAM) varies depending on the context and is recognized as a sensitive predictor of a number of population parameters, including as socioeconomic status, nutritional status, geography, and environmental factors.[456] These deviations from the normal age range, whether early or delayed, can have significant health implications. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a growing global health concern due to high morbidity and mortality.[7] In 2025, premature CVD deaths have been estimated to be 7.8 million unless preventive measures to decline the trajectory are adopted.[8] AAM is a critical marker of pubertal timing and existing literature show that it has been associated with various health outcomes, including body mass index (BMI) in adolescence and adulthood which is an early predictor of CVD risk in later life potentially due to hormonal, behavioral, and lifestyle factors that shape post-pubertal growth trajectories. An early puberty attainment might be due to factors like maternal nutrition during pregnancy, maternal BMI, early life nutrition and weight, pre-pubertal BMI, genetics, etc.[91011] Menarche causes hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis stimulation, leading to increased estrogen production taht influences fat deposition, particularly around the hips and abdomen, contributing to differences in BMI post-menarche.[3101112] Adolescents with higher post-menarche BMI have shown greater prevalence of obesity-related conditions.[1213] Early menarche has been linked to prolonged menstrual cycles and irregularities, which are further compounded by sedentary behaviors and poor physical activity levels.[1415]

Bhubaneswar, the capital city of Odisha, exemplifies a diverse educational, socioeconomic, and cultural setting which presents a unique scenario to study the interaction between AAM and BMI, with significant disparities between students in private and government schools. Factors such as maternal health and nutrition, individual’s dietary habit, and physical activity, can vary significantly between these different populations, influencing both the pubertal timing and subsequent health outcomes.[9101112] This study aims to explore the association between AAM and post-pubertal BMI (PP-BMI) among late adolescent girls (16-19 years) and compare the strength of association between a government and a private school in Bhubaneswar, Odisha. The evidence is expected to furnish valuable insight into the interplay of biological and social determinants of adolescent health, contributing to development of interventions for health promotion in this population.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among late adolescent, school-going girls in Bhubaneswar, Odisha.

Study participants and sampling

The present study was done among 180 adolescent girls (16–19 years) who had attained menarche and consented to participate, from two schools in Bhubaneswar, Odisha- one private and one government school, representing different socioeconomic strata. Between February 2024 and October 2024, 90 girls from each school, from class 8 to 12 were enrolled for the study, using convineint sampling method. Government schools usually cater to students from lower socio-economic background, while private schools are attended by those from middle-to-higher income groups. This selection enabled exploration of socio-economic influence on AAM, nutritional status, dietary habits, and access to health education, and provides an opportunity to study AAM and post-pubertal BMI, a rise in which is an early indicator of CVD risk later in life among the population undergoing rapid urbanization. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the 2 schools were convineintly selected from the administrative zone (North zone) of the city in which the place of study is, i.e., Kalinga Institute of Medical Sciences (KIMS), capturing diverse demographic and environmental factors [Figure 1].

Data collection tools and technique

A team of 3 researchers, 6 medical interns and a female social worker was formed for data collection at the schools. After obtaining the consent of the school principal and parents of the students consenting, information on household socio-demography and other covariates were collected using questionnaires administered by the study team. Each interview with anthropometric measurement took 20-25 min.

The outcome variable in study was an anthropometric risk factor- PPBMI. Height and weight were measured twice using calibrated instruments to calculate PP-BMI of the participants. Height was measured in centimeters (cm) using standardized and calibrated stadiometer, while weight was measured in kilograms (kg) using standardized adult weighing machine with zero error correction to 0.05. Average of the two readings was considered for the analysis.

The AAM of the adolescent girls which is a distinct event of puberty among females, is the exposure of interest. Data collection was carried out during school hours, ensuring minimal disruption to the academic schedule, after necessary permission from the Principal. Data was self-reported and collected using semi-structured questionnaire. The questionnaire used to interview the participant had the following sections: (1) Socio-demographic information, collected to study the characteristics of the students and also categorize students based on their socio-economic status, school type and other relevant parameters. (2) Medical, reproductive and menstrual history including the data on AAM and menstrual pattern. (3) General information regarding the student including the diet intake, physical activity

The physical activity and healthy diet intake were taken as proxy indicator of pre-pubertal characteristics of the participants as the timing of attaining puberty is recent and the lifestyle variables or factors are considered to have not changed in this short span of time which is approximately 2.5-3 years.

Physical activity was assessed using the WHO STEPS instrument, where physical activity was discussed on basis of work, travel and recreation and data was categorized into 3 groups vigorous, moderate, or sedentary activity.[16]

Higher fiber intake has been associated with a more favorable metabolic profile, which may impact the timing of puberty.[17] Thus, for the study purpose, frequency of fruit and green leafy vegetables intake over past 3 months from interview, was taken as suitable indicator of healthy diet. Data were available individually for ‘fruits’ and ‘green leafy vegetables’ as 1 = Twice per day or more, 2 = Once a day, 3 = Thrice a week or more, 4 = Once a week, 5 = Twice per month, 6 = Once per month, 7 = Sometimes, 8 = Never, which was then categorized into ‘Daily, weekly and rare/never intake’, according to intake frequency [Table 1]. Daily and weekly intake of fruit and/or green leafy vegetable was considered as ‘Healthy diet intake’ while the rest was not.

While obtaining the written consent from the parents for their child to participate in this study, a section of questionnaire was sent to them for acquiring the following details, if they approved their child’s participation: history of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) this child in her childhood, menstrual history of the mother including her AAM, current BMI and maternal nutrition supplementation (MNS) while pregnant with this child who is participating in the study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA-17. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize demographic characteristics, AAM, and BMI. Independent t-tests and Chi-square tests were used to compare outcomes between government and private school students.

A multivariate linear regression model was built to assess the association between AAM and PP-BMI in both the type of schools and the strength of association between the two were compared, adjusting for potential confounders such as dietary intake and socio-economic status. A forward approach was adopted to build the model wherein, the potential confounders were identified and added to model if it altered the crude association between the exposure and the outcome under study. For the analysis, “Age at PP-BMI measurement” was taken a-priori due to its biological relevance and potential to confound the relationship between AAM and PP-BMI. Adjusting for it ensures that the observed association will reflect the independent contribution of exposure, accounting for PP-BMI variation attributable to age-related growth and development in the adolescents.

Ethical considerations

Data collection was done after approval from the institute’s ethics committee (KIIT/KIMS/IEC/1514/2024) and school principals. Informed consent from the participants and their parents was obtained before the administration of the questionnaire. Confidentiality of participant’s data was maintained throughout the study.

Results

Over the study period, data were collected for 180 students from one private and one government school of Bhubaneswar. Table 2 depicts the baseline characteristics of the students by the type of school. The mean age at PP-BMI measurement was 16.5 ± 1.5 years in government school girls while it was 16.1 ± 1.3 years in private school (P = 0.05). History of EBF was prevalent among the government school adolescent girls (52.28%; P = 0.03) while healthy diet intake was common in private school adolescent girls (84.4%; P = 0.04). The mean PP-BMI was significantly higher among private school students (23.4 ± 3.6 kg/m²) compared to government school (21.1 ± 2.9 kg/m²) (P = 0.02). Maternal supplementation during pregnancy was reported to be higher among the mothers of participants from the government school (66.7%; P = 0.04).

Discussion

As per literature reviewed, this study is one of the first in Eastern India to assess the association of AAM and PP-BMI among the late adolescent girls with statistically adequate data in urban setting of Bhubaneswar. It provides type of school-specific data (private and government) for the population to get unique pattern that might not be apparent when combining it.

The analysis revealed a significant association between AAM and PP-BMI at late adolesence, with earlier menarche associated with higher BMI in the post puberty period. This relationship persisted even after adjusting for potential confounding factors, including age at PP-BMI measurement, history of being exclusively breastfed, MNS, and healthy diet, thus supporting the study hypothesis.

Indian women usually attain menarche during 11.4–14.8 years.[1920] This study reports the mean AAM to be 13.2 ± 0.04 years among government school girls and 12.9 ± 0.12 years among private school girls which stands slightly in contrast with the study by Gupta A et al.[21] which revealed that government school girls attained menarche at an age of 12.39 ± 0.063 years, while in private school it was 12.69 ± 0.057 years (P = 0.05) while another study from Northern India reports AAM to be 15.2 years.[12] This variation from current study might be attributed to the difference in the altitude and geography causing a delay in menarche attainment among those at higher altitude as climate evidently influences pubertal timing.[20]

The mean PP-BMI of the adolescent girls in government schools was 21 ± 2.9 kg/m², while in private schools, was 23.4 ± 3.6 kg/m². In a similar study done among adolescent girls of Medinipur, West Bengal, mean PP-BMI was 19.86 (1.42) kg/m² which is lower than the current study finding.[22] This difference might be due to the participant’s characteristics in present study where their lifestyle and diet are influenced by urbanization while the West Bengal study was done in a small town where resources might be limited.

The AAM has been declining globally, raising concerns about its potential health implications, one such concern being its relationship with PP-BMI that serves as a critical indicator of metabolic health, obesity risk, and future non-communicable diseases. The current study found strong evidence for an association between AAM and PP-BMI, irrespective of school type. This might be attributed to the fact that puberty has an immediate effect on body composition, considering the short time period between the attainment of menarche and late adolescence. Studies indicate that body fat distribution and overall nutritional status during adolescence significantly impact cardiovascular health in adult life.[132324] The results lead to a similar conclusion of a study from central India highlighting that girls with higher BMI attained menarche earlier than their counterparts with normal and lower BMI, which could be a precursor to long-term metabolic risks, like CVDs.[2425] In support, research has shown that females with early menarche are dissatisfied with their body image that is physical health in comparison to those with late menarche.[2627] However, in contrast, a study from Rajasthan shows that later age at puberty is associated with CVD which differs from present study findings.[20] This might be due to the pre-pubertal nutrition and BMI status of the girls in the study where lower BMI often indicating undernutrition, leads to delayed puberty and the associated metabolic dysfunction predisposes to CVD risk.

The study findings bridges the gap in existing evidence regarding the association between AAM and PP-BMI among students in different school settings. By identifying the associations, the study provides insights into long-term health implications of early or late menarche, aiding in the development of targeted interventions for adolescent health.

Limitation and recommendation

This was a cross-sectional study where the pre-pubertal characteristics of adolescent girls like BMI, diet, physical activity, etc., could not be accounted for and lack of adjustment for which might have a residual confounding effect on the association. A longitudinal study following a birth or a very young cohort can provide information on these pre-pubertal variables. However, the current analysis includes the PP-BMI, diet, and physical activity of the participants as proxy indicators of the pre-pubertal characteristics, as the timing of attaining puberty is recent and the lifestyle variables or factors are considered to have not changed in this short span of time which is approximately 2.5-3 years.

The information regarding the AAM was self-reported which might have influenced the association found with information bias. Rather the growth and physiological data points of the adolescents collected by following the cohort prospectively from birth or time nearer to population/geography-specific puberty time would have been appropriate. The advantage of collecting data closer to the age of attaining puberty decreases the scope of underestimating the association under study. The anthropometric measurements were carried out manually that might have led to measurement error. However, to prevent this bias, measurement of height and weight was done twice using calibrated devices and standardized procedure. Study involves participants from urban setting who might have specific characteristic in terms of lifestyle, diet, and environment, having an effect on pubertal timing and PP-BMI. Hence, the findings of this study may not be applicable to rural Indian population and different age group.

Relationship is more pronounced among private school students, potentially reflecting socioeconomic and lifestyle differences. Study findings emphasize the need for monitoring BMI trends and promoting physical activity in adolescent girls to mitigate potential CVD risks. Tailored interventions are needed to address weight management in this population based on pubertal timing. Further research is warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and to evaluate the efficacy of targeted approaches in diverse geographic settings.

Conclusion

AAM significantly influences post-pubertal BMI, with early menarche associated with higher BMI. Maternal factors like maternal nutrition supplementation during pregnancy and participants having history of EBF during early life were found to be influencing the association of AAM with PP-BMI.
