Noise-robust reward machine induction via probabilistic modeling and genetic local search
Authors: Zhengwei Zhu, Zhixuan Chen, Chenyang Zhu, Mingjie Li, Weina Zhang, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReinforcement learning (RL) in partially observable and noisy environments remains a central challenge because of the difficulty in inferring latent structures, managing label uncertainty, and ensuring robust policy optimization. Reward Machines (RMs) offer a structured formalism for representing non-Markovian rewards through symbolic automata, but existing methods exhibit fragility under stochastic observations. To address this limitation, we introduce Probabilistic Induction with Genetic Local Search (PI-GLS), a unified framework that integrates probabilistic perception, inductive logic programming, and evolutionary optimization. PI-GLS achieves noise-robust RM induction by combining Bayesian inference for uncertain labels, sampling-based symbolic abstraction, and genetic local refinement of automaton structures. In addition, we propose a belief-aware reward shaping strategy that leverages distributions over RM states to guide policy learning under uncertainty. Extensive experiments on benchmark domains show that PI-GLS substantially improves convergence efficiency, robustness to sensor noise, and interpretability of learned reward models, achieving performance on par with manually engineered RMs even under severe noise. These results demonstrate the scalability and effectiveness of PI-GLS for autonomous decision-making in real-world scenarios where stochastic feedback and partial observability are unavoidable.Keywords: Reinforcement learning, Reward machines, Partial observability, Noisy labels, Genetic local search, Reward shapingSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a foundational paradigm in machine learning in which agents learn to maximize cumulative rewards through sequential interaction with their environments1. Landmark achievements such as AlphaGo’s victory over world champions in the game of Go2 and the integration of RL into large-scale language models have demonstrated its effectiveness for complex decision-making tasks. Despite these advances, the deployment of RL in real-world domains remains difficult due to three persistent challenges: limited cross-task generalization, where policies trained in one environment often fail to transfer to new tasks3; fragility under partial observability and noisy perception, as modeled by partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs)4,5; and lack of interpretability in learned policies, which hinders adoption in safety-critical applications6.

Reward Machines (RMs) provide a structured approach to specifying non-Markovian rewards by encoding high-level propositional events as finite-state automata. This abstraction decomposes complex tasks into interpretable subgoals, thereby enhancing both expressivity and transparency7,8. By transforming non-Markovian rewards into an equivalent Markovian representation, RMs allow standard RL algorithms to be applied while preserving task-level structure. Recent work has investigated automated RM induction through hierarchical synthesis and inductive logic programming9,10. However, these approaches generally assume access to perfect propositional labels. In practice, sensor noise and environmental uncertainty make such assumptions unrealistic, and consequently existing RM induction techniques are highly sensitive to noisy or incomplete observations, which limits their applicability in partially observable settings11.

To illustrate these challenges, consider the continuous-state environment depicted in Fig. 1. The agent, shown as a blue figure, must navigate sequentially to targets labeled A, B, C, and D, operate coffee machines, and avoid noisy disturbances represented by red stars. The environment includes dynamic elements such as buttons that may be open or closed and coffee cups that may be empty or full. Because the agent cannot fully observe the environment, it must rely on partial perception to form an observation memory and incrementally update its policy. Effective performance requires integrating temporal dependencies, adapting to stochastic perturbations, and strategically choosing actions under uncertainty. This scenario highlights how Reward Machine-based policy generation within a partially observable reinforcement learning framework supports robust decision-making in uncertain environments. In our setting, the agent operates under partial observability and cannot access the true environment state. Instead, at each time step, it receives a noisy observation produced by a probabilistic perception module, which provides probabilistic estimates over atomic propositions rather than ground-truth symbolic labels. This naturally leads to a POMDP formulation.

Illustration of an agent operating in a noisy office environment with dynamic elements and partial observability.

The limitations of existing RM induction methods under noisy and partially observable conditions motivate the need for a more robust framework. To this end, we propose Probabilistic Reward Machine Induction with Genetic Local Search (PI-GLS), a framework designed to enable noise-robust RM learning. PI-GLS integrates probabilistic modeling with evolutionary optimization to enhance both robustness and search efficiency. The framework consists of three main components. First, a noise-aware induction mechanism leverages inductive logic programming (ILASP) in combination with genetic local search to infer reward structures from noisy trajectories12. Second, a belief-aware reward shaping module employs probabilistic confidence over RM states to guide policy learning in the presence of uncertainty. Third, a dynamic alternating optimization process interleaves policy updates with RM structure refinement, ensuring the co-evolution of agent behavior and symbolic task abstraction. Importantly, when current RMs fail to explain observed trajectories, PI-GLS adaptively generates new candidate RMs during training, thereby expanding the hypothesis space without sacrificing computational efficiency. Building upon the foundation of probabilistic reward machine induction, which handles label uncertainty via Bayesian inference and ILASP, we propose to address its limitations in search efficiency and robustness through evolutionary optimization13. In this work, we extend the probabilistic reward machine induction framework introduced by PROB-IRM. While PROB-IRM laid the foundation for probabilistic modeling in reward machine induction, our approach, PI-GLS, enhances search efficiency and robustness by introducing genetic local search techniques and a dynamic alternating optimization strategy. Unlike denoising preprocessing methods14, PI-GLS remains agnostic to specific noise models while maintaining compatibility with external denoisers. By unifying probabilistic perception, symbolic induction, and evolutionary refinement, our framework addresses the shortcomings of existing methods and provides a scalable solution for RL under partial observability and uncertain feedback.

Related works

Reinforcement learning under partial observability and uncertain feedback

Reinforcement learning is conventionally modeled as Markov decision processes (MDPs) in fully observable environments, where classical algorithms such as Q-learning, SARSA, and policy gradient methods are widely applied15,16. However, practical tasks often involve incomplete state information, which necessitates the formulation of partially observable MDPs17. Solving POMDPs is computationally demanding: finding optimal policies is PSPACE-complete even under known models18, while determining optimal memoryless strategies remains NP-hard19. Theoretical challenges also arise in parameter estimation, which is as difficult as solving the noisy parity problem in hidden Markov models20.

To address these issues, several methods have been introduced. Point-based value iteration approximates solutions in large belief spaces4, and Bayesian filtering techniques, such as particle filters and Gaussian processes, estimate posterior distributions of latent states21. Advances in spectral methods yield polynomial sample complexity guarantees for POMDP learning22, while posterior-sampling algorithms improve efficiency by assuming model separability or reliable belief-state estimation23. Exploration under partial observability has also been studied: Jin et al. proposed sample-efficient algorithms when hidden states are bounded by observed states24, and Golowich et al. presented quasi-polynomial time planning algorithms under the γ-observability condition25.

Deep RL approaches extend these ideas by leveraging recurrent neural networks and attention mechanisms to capture dependencies across temporal sequences. This has shown significant success in games, robotics, and natural language processing5,26. Further advances integrate Markov Chain Monte Carlo-based parameter inference and domain randomization to enhance generalization, comparing Transformer- and LSTM-based policies in real-world maintenance applications27. To benchmark partially observable RL, Shao et al. introduced Mask Atari, which evaluates memory-based methods under controlled occlusions28.

Nonetheless, RNN-based solutions often suffer from limited ability to preserve long-term dependencies, leading to degraded performance and unstable policy learning29,30. These limitations highlight the need for explicit memory representations to maintain historical context. Our work addresses this gap by employing Reward Automata as structured memory to capture non-Markovian dependencies and improve strategy synthesis.

Reward machine learning

Reward Machines extend the expressiveness of RL by encoding non-Markovian rewards through finite automata defined over event sequences. Originally proposed by Toro Icarte et al., RMs enable decomposing tasks into automaton-guided subgoals, thereby simplifying policy construction7. Subsequent research has focused on automated RM induction from data. Toro Icarte and Zhan formulated RM structure inference as a discrete optimization problem on execution traces31, while Hasanbeig et al. applied program synthesis to derive automaton transitions from expert demonstrations32. Furelos et al. advanced inductive logic programming (ILP)-based induction to support hierarchical abstractions10,33. SAT-based formulations have also been developed for RM inference, enforcing structural constraints to improve interpretability34,35.

RM learning methods are commonly grouped into two categories. Prediction-based approaches estimate future reward labels conditioned on current automaton states and inputs31, whereas minimal-state methods aim to derive the smallest automaton consistent with the reward semantics34, often supported by ILP-driven biases for interpretability10. Structural regularization further enhances these procedures by discouraging unnecessary complexity35.

Recent research has begun to address uncertainty in RM induction. Verginis et al. proposed preprocessing noisy labels before induction14, while Dohmen introduced probabilistic RMs that model stochastic transitions and rewards36. Corazza et al. extended these ideas by incorporating probabilistic output labeling35, and Li et al. examined how noisy reward functions affect policy learning, proposing belief update strategies to improve robustness37,38.

PROB-IRM introduces probabilistic reward machine induction by explicitly modeling uncertainty in propositional observations and integrating this uncertainty into an ILASP-based induction pipeline13. In this approach, noisy propositions are treated probabilistically rather than as deterministic labels, enabling reward machine synthesis from stochastic or error-prone feedback. However, the search over automaton structures still relies heavily on the underlying ILASP optimization dynamics, which can become inefficient or unstable as the hypothesis space grows or when trajectories are highly ambiguous. Additionally, the induced structures may be sensitive to local optima, and repeated calls to the symbolic solver can dominate runtime in complex tasks.

Despite these advances, existing approaches face three major challenges: (1) difficulty in preserving long-term dependencies under partial observability, (2) reliance on restrictive assumptions for latent dynamics estimation, and (3) lack of robust methods for inducing compact and semantically accurate automata from noisy or sparse feedback. These limitations underscore the need for frameworks that jointly model uncertainty in observations and reward induction.

Building on probabilistic ILASP-based RM induction as exemplified by PROB-IRM, PI-GLS targets the remaining bottlenecks in search efficiency and robustness when learning from long, noisy, and partially informative trajectories. Specifically, PI-GLS introduces a genetic local search refinement module to explore structurally meaningful neighborhoods of ILASP-derived candidates, reducing sensitivity to local optima and improving the quality of induced automata under severe ambiguity. Furthermore, PI-GLS adopts a dynamic alternating optimization loop that interleaves policy improvement with structure refinement, so that newly collected trajectories continuously stress-test and update the symbolic abstraction, improving both convergence behavior and robustness. Unlike denoising preprocessing methods14, PI-GLS remains agnostic to specific noise models while maintaining compatibility with external denoisers. By unifying probabilistic perception, symbolic induction, and evolutionary refinement, our framework addresses the shortcomings of existing methods and provides a scalable solution for RL under partial observability and uncertain feedback.

Framework description

Problem formulation

In RL, agents interact with an environment to maximize cumulative rewards. When rewards are sparse and depend on temporally extended, non-Markovian conditions, standard MDPs become insufficient. To address this, RMs have been introduced as a structured formalism for reward specification. An RM models reward functions as finite-state automata, where transitions are triggered by high-level events. This abstraction decomposes complex tasks into interpretable subgoals, enabling temporal reasoning and modular policy learning.

Despite their expressiveness, most RM-based approaches assume that proposition sets are deterministically observed. In practice, this assumption is often violated due to sensor noise and limited observability, both prevalent in real-world robotics. For example, in the BUTTONWORLD domain, an agent must complete sequential tasks (e.g., visiting a coffee machine before entering an office). Under noisy conditions, incorrect event detection corrupts the event sequence and hinders the learning of reliable reward representations.

This probabilistic formulation introduces two fundamental challenges. The first is label inconsistency, where variations in noise characteristics across environments or sensor configurations can produce inconsistent observations even when the underlying atomic proposition remains unchanged. Such inconsistencies undermine symbolic induction methods that rely on clean and deterministic supervision. The second challenge is the combinatorial explosion of the hypothesis space, since jointly inferring latent event sequences and structural abstractions from noisy trajectories leads to exponential growth in possible hypotheses as trajectory length and structural complexity increase, rendering direct optimization intractable.

In the setting of partially observable RL with sparse, non-Markovian rewards and probabilistic noise on event traces, the central objective is to induce RMs that capture temporal dependencies despite uncertainty, preserve compactness and interpretability, and enhance both policy efficiency and robustness. Achieving this goal requires integrating probabilistic modeling, as formalized in Equation (6), with symbolic reasoning to operate effectively under noisy supervision while simultaneously controlling the complexity of the search space. The complete framework that addresses these challenges is presented in the following sections.

PI-GLS framework

PI-GLS integrates probabilistic reasoning, symbolic induction, and evolutionary computation for task decomposition and policy optimization in noisy, partially observable environments. It builds upon and extends probabilistic RM induction frameworks through the introduction of a Genetic Local Search (GLS) module and a dynamic alternating optimization process, aiming to enhance robustness and search efficiency. In this paper, the IRM baseline corresponds exactly to the probabilistic RM induction framework introduced in13, without the GLS refinement or the dynamic alternating optimization strategy. Figure 2 summarizes the framework.

The PI-GLS framework integrates probabilistic perception, symbolic reasoning, and evolutionary policy optimization within an interactive loop. Noisy trajectories are denoised via Bayesian inference, abstracted into RMs through inductive logic programming, and used to guide policy search via hybrid GA-LS optimization.

Specifically, the framework begins with a probabilistic observation model that handles sensor noise. Noisy labels are refined via Bayesian posterior estimation to produce reliable trajectory samples. To infer high-level task structure, PI-GLS employs the ILASP framework for inductive logic programming, synthesizing RMs that encode task semantics. A noise-aware penalty mechanism follows, balancing robustness to noisy inputs with structural simplicity, thereby preventing overfitting while preserving interpretability.

For policy optimization, PI-GLS introduces a hybrid genetic local search (GA-LS) that maintains a diverse strategy population. Each candidate is evaluated by expected return under belief states induced by the RM. Genetic operators ensure global exploration, while local fine-tuning accelerates convergence toward high-quality policies.

The system operates in a closed-loop paradigm: the agent interacts with the environment under the current RM and policy, triggers model updates when prediction deviations exceed a threshold, and re-optimizes strategies accordingly. This iterative perception-modeling-optimization cycle enables adaptation to complex dynamics.

Experiments

Experimental setup and configurations

Environment configuration

Experiments are conducted in the OfficeButtonWorld environment, an extended variant of OfficeWorld that introduces interactive elements such as buttons that can be open or closed, coffee machines, and designated target locations labeled A, B, C, and D. The agent must navigate to specific targets, operate machines, and manipulate buttons while accounting for red stars that represent environmental noise. Due to partial observability and stochastic perturbations, the agent relies on self-perception to construct observation memory and guide decision-making. Four tasks are defined to progressively evaluate PI-GLS. In COFFEE, the agent must reach and operate a coffee machine. In COFFEEMAIL, coffee must be delivered to a designated location. In BUTTON, the task involves pressing a coffee machine button. Finally, in ALLTARGETS, the agent is required to visit all of the locations A, B, C, and D in any order while completing the necessary interactions. These tasks were chosen for their increasing structural complexity and established use in prior RM-based studies, enabling systematic evaluation and direct comparison. To account for layout variability and stochastic noise, experiments are repeated under multiple randomized configurations.

Noise configuration

Sensor noise is characterized in terms of acquisition efficiency and detection accuracy. Two dimensions of noise are considered. First, the noise target specifies the subset of sensors affected. Two cases are evaluated: only the first event-correlation sensor required for task completion (noise-first), and all sensors (noise-all). Second, the noise level reflects the joint sensitivity and specificity of a sensor, referred to collectively as sensor confidence. For simplicity, sensitivity and specificity are set to identical values. Although sensor confidence can be controlled directly, the observable effect is constrained by the prior probability of each proposition being detected. To ensure clarity, noise level is defined as the posterior probability that a disturbed label is correctly identified. Three levels of noise are evaluated: 0.9 (mild), 0.7 (moderate), and 0.5 (severe), with a noise-free baseline defined by a posterior probability of 1.0.

Policy learning

The agent policy is trained using the RM-based reinforcement learning algorithm described in Section 3.3. For the DQN baseline, we adopt a standard implementation that treats the partially observable environment as if it were fully observable: its input is the current noisy observation vector, without any recurrent memory. This provides a strong memoryless lower-bound benchmark. The Q-function is represented in tabular form, indexed by RM belief vectors discretized via a binning procedure in which belief values are truncated to a fixed number of decimal places to group similar values equivalently. Exploration follows an ε-greedy strategy: ε is initialized at 1.0 (fully random exploration), decreases linearly to 0.1 over the first 2000 steps, and remains fixed thereafter. All experiments employ the proposed probabilistic reward modeling framework, including both fixed-RM and learning-RM settings, with a discount factor of γ =0.99.

Comparison with state-of-the-art methods

We compare PI-GLS with several baselines in both clean and noisy environments: the standard Deep Q-Network (DQN)5, Hierarchical Reward Machines (HRM)33 as a state-of-the-art RM-based method, and our own framework, denoted as PI-GLS. We additionally include FSC, a finite-state controller learning method based on stochastic gradient descent for partially observable environments. FSC directly optimizes a fixed-size controller without performing symbolic structure induction or reward machine learning, thereby serving as a representative policy-gradient-style baseline for POMDP control41. For fair comparison, the controller size of FSC is aligned with the reward machine state size used in PI-GLS. IRM implements the probabilistic reward machine induction framework introduced in prior work. It does not include the GLS module or the dynamic alternating optimization strategy, and therefore represents the direct probabilistic predecessor of PI-GLS. As shown in Fig. 3 and summarized in Table 3, PI-GLS consistently outperforms all baselines in terms of final reward, convergence speed, and robustness to noise.

This behavior demonstrates its ability to adapt under noisy conditions and highlights the effectiveness of its inductive bias in guiding reward optimization.

Building upon the probabilistic induction framework adopted in IRM, our contribution lies in coupling it with belief-aware reward shaping and evolutionary refinement. Ablation studies

Reward shaping

We evaluate the contribution of reward shaping (RS) by comparing performance with and without this component. Specifically, the experiments in Fig. 5 were repeated after removing reward shaping, and the resulting performance was compared with the original results. The findings indicate that reward shaping significantly reduces the number of training episodes required to reach high performance across all noise levels and improves the final policy quality. Moreover, under the evaluated noisy setting with posterior accuracy 0.9, reward shaping consistently improves both convergence efficiency and final performance. Reward shaping is already part of the underlying probabilistic RM induction framework implemented in IRM. In this subsection, we evaluate its individual contribution and its interaction with the GLS extension.

Ablation study of key model components. IRM corresponds to PI-GLS without the GLS module, and IRM without reward shaping corresponds to removing both GLS and reward shaping.

The advantage of this mechanism stems from two complementary effects. At the policy-learning level, intermediate rewards improve the efficiency of Q-value estimation in sparse-reward scenarios. At the RM-learning level, reward shaping accelerates the elimination of incorrect reward models by guiding exploration towards regions of the state space that are poorly explained by or contradictory to the current candidate RM, thereby generating trajectories that expose its flaws. This dual effect enhances both policy optimization and RM induction. In high-noise environments in particular, reward shaping enables the agent to produce higher-quality training samples under the ε-greedy exploration strategy, making it an effective tool for learning in complex and uncertain domains.

Computational and stability analysis of GA-LS

Compared to plain ILASP, the GA-LS module introduces overhead proportional to the population size and number of generations. In our experiments, this overhead was offset by a reduction of up to 30% in the number of ILASP calls required for convergence, as GA-LS efficiently refines initial hypotheses. Regarding convergence stability across task complexities, GA-LS consistently improved upon the initial ILASP solution in all tasks (COFFEE to ALLTARGETS). The monotonic elitism guarantee (Theorem 1) ensures stable convergence without regression. Notably, in the most complex task (ALLTARGETS), GA-LS provided the most significant relative improvement, demonstrating its enhanced utility where the pure inductive baseline struggles.

These results confirm that GLS enhances ILASP-derived reward machines by exploring structurally similar candidates, preserving elite solutions, and correcting misclassifications, while simultaneously reducing unnecessary structural complexity. As a result, reinforcement learning agents benefit from more accurate and robust symbolic task models, achieving higher rewards and maintaining stability even under partial observability and noisy observations.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

We conducted a systematic investigation of the sensitivity of two key hyperparameters, α and λ, on classification accuracy across three benchmark environments: COFFEEMAIL, BUTTON, and VISITABCD. The results, summarized in Fig. 6, provide insights into optimal configurations for these parameters.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis across three environments: (a) influence of α in balancing fitting accuracy and structural complexity, (b) influence of λ in balancing accuracy and model parsimony.

Effect of α and β

The parameters α and β in the reward machine induction objective (Eq. 11) control the trade-off between fitting accuracy and model complexity. α weights the penalty for label inconsistencies , while β weights the structural complexity penalty . We analyze their relative effect by fixing β =1.0 and varying α. As shown in Fig. 6a, performance improved as α increased from 0.1 to 0.5, with the best rewards at α = 0.5. For α > 0.5, performance plateaued or declined, indicating that over-penalizing inconsistencies leads to overly complex models without further benefit.

Effect of λ

The parameter λ in the genetic algorithm evaluation function (Eq. 12) balances accuracy and parsimony when scoring candidate reward machines, thereby guiding the exploration of the RM hypothesis space. As depicted in Fig. 6b, the optimal setting was λ = 0.1. Values that are too small (e.g., 0.01) overemphasize simplicity, causing premature convergence to suboptimal structures. Values that are too large (e.g., 0.2 or 0.5) overemphasize accuracy, leading to unnecessarily complex automata that hinder policy learning. A moderate λ achieves an effective trade-off for structural search in partially observable environments.

Framework implications

The observed trends align closely with the mechanisms embedded in our framework. Within ILASP, accurate value estimation weighted by α provides more reliable guidance for symbolic rule extraction, ensuring that the induced logical structures reflect stable state-value information. In the Genetic Local Search module, the entropy coefficient λ directly influences the diversity of candidate policies explored during evolutionary search, thereby preventing premature convergence. For Reward Shaping, both α and λ impact how additional rewards are propagated through the learning signal: a well-calibrated value loss weight stabilizes shaped returns, while a moderate entropy bonus ensures sufficient exploration of alternative shaped trajectories.

Taken together, the results suggest that setting α around 0.5 and λ around 0.1 provides a principled configuration that balances value-based stability, exploratory diversity, and structured reward design, thereby strengthening the synergy among the components of our proposed method.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presented PI-GLS, a probabilistic reward machine induction framework that integrates symbolic reasoning with genetic local optimization for reinforcement learning in noisy and partially observable environments. By combining probabilistic label inference, inductive logic programming, and belief-aware reward shaping, PI-GLS establishes a principled connection between low-level noisy perception and high-level task abstraction. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed framework outperforms traditional RM-based and deep RL baselines in terms of robustness to noise, convergence speed, and interpretability.

Several avenues for future research emerge from this work. First, our current assumption of known sensor noise parameters can be relaxed. The probabilistic framework of PI-GLS (Fig. 2) provides a foundation for online noise estimation, for instance by treating these parameters as latent variables within a hierarchical Bayesian model, which would enable adaptation to non-stationary environments. Second, to further push the boundaries of robustness under extreme uncertainty, integrating advanced robust optimization techniques—such as evolutionary algorithms designed for large-scale robust multi-objective optimization—into the structure search process could yield policies that are not only high-performing but also guaranteed to be stable across a wider distribution of noise perturbations. Third, incorporating deep representation learning into symbolic induction may enhance scalability to high-dimensional environments. Fourth, extending PI-GLS to multi-agent reinforcement learning could provide new insights into coordination under uncertainty. Advancing these directions will further strengthen the role of probabilistic reward machines as a foundation for bridging symbolic reasoning and reinforcement learning, thereby contributing to trustworthy decision-making in complex real-world systems.
