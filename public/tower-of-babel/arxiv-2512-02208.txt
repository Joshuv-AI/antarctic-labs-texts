Projective limits of probabilistic symmetries and their applications to random graph limits
Pim van der Hoorn, Huck Stepanyants, Dmitri Krioukov

Abstract
We couple projective limits of probability measures to direct limits of their symmetry groups. We show that the direct limit group is the group of symmetries of the projective limit probability measure. If projective systems of probability measures represent point processes in increasingly larger finite regions of the same infinite space, then we show that under some additional niceness and consistency assumptions, an extension of the direct limit group is the symmetry group of the projective limit point process in the whole infinite space. The application of these results to random graph limits provides “shortest paths” to graphons and graphexes as it recovers these random graph limits as trivial corollaries. Another application example encompasses a broad class of limits of random graphs with bounded average degrees. This class includes a representative collection of paradigmatic random graph models that have attracted significant research attention in diverse areas of science. Our approach thus provides a general unified framework to study limits of very different types of random graphs.



1
Introduction

Projective limits
[
39
,
15
,
13
]
are a very general and powerful concept applicable to a wide variety of categories—sets, topological spaces, measurable spaces, or probability measures. Projective limits of probability measures can be seen as a generalization of the Kolmogorov extension theorem
[
33
]
, which establishes the conditions for the existence of a stochastic process as a limit of a family of finite-dimensional distributions. A classic example is the Dirichlet process, extensively studied and used in statistics and machine learning
[
23
,
42
,
17
,
37
]
. The projective limit in this case is the limit of Dirichlet distributions
[
42
]
, used in the latent Dirichlet allocation
[
6
]
, for instance.

Direct limits
[
39
,
15
,
13
]
are as general and powerful, as they are dual to projective limits
[
39
,
48
]
. In fact, projective limits are also known as
inverse
limits, to emphasize the duality. This duality boils down to inverting the direction of all the arrows in the commutative diagrams in the definitions of the limits. A classic example of this duality is the Pontryagin duality between direct limits of locally compact abelian groups and the inverse limits of their Pontryagin-dual groups, which are the groups of continuous homomorphisms from the original group to the circle group
[
40
]
. Yet the objects to which projective and direct limits apply do not have to belong to the same category.

Here, we couple projective limits of probability measures to direct limits of groups with respect to which these measures are invariant. Our first result in Section
3
states that under certain natural conditions, the projective limit of probability measures is invariant with respect to the direct limit of groups of symmetries of these measures. That is to say that probabilistic symmetries are preserved in the limit, if things are right. This result is very general, and the closest previous results to this one, at least in spirit, appear to be
[
1
,
32
,
41
,
12
,
42
]
.

However, in general, projective and direct limits deal with product spaces that are not always terribly useful in applications. Things change drastically if spaces that these limits deal with embed into each other, i.e., if a smaller space is a subspace of a bigger space. And this is exactly the settings we narrow down our next results to, motivated by applications.

We consider projective systems of probability measures that represent point processes in increasingly larger finite chunks of the same infinite space. Our second result in Section
3
states that the projective limit of these measures in this case is a point process in the whole infinite space.

Finally, in our last result in Section
3
, we couple the projective systems of these point processes with the direct system of their symmetry groups, and show that the limit process in the whole infinite space is invariant with respect to not only the direct limit group, but also suitable larger groups.

These results, especially the last one, are strongly motivated by applications that we present in Section
4
. These applications deal with projective limits of random graphs
[
45
]
. We represent random graphs as point processes in which points are graph edges. If an edge connects vertices labeled by
x
and
y
, where
x
and
y
are elements of some label space
\mathbb{L}
, then this edge is a random point
\{x,y\}\in\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
, so the random graph is a point process in
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
. A finite graph of size
n
has a finite label space
\mathbb{L}_{n}
, a bigger graph of size
m>n
has a bigger
\mathbb{L}_{m}
, while an infinite graph needs an infinite
\mathbb{L}_{\infty}
. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

It is now just a matter of choice what these label spaces and their symmetry groups are. Whatever they are, our last theorem in Section
3
tells us what the symmetry group in the limit is. We show in Section
4
that if one makes the most conventional choice of integer labels
\mathbb{L}_{n}=[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, Fig.
1
(a), and takes the symmetry group to be the symmetric group of permutations of labels
[n]
, then in the limit we get random graphs with labels in
\mathbb{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{N}
invariant with respect to the infinite symmetric group of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
. These graphs have a nice representation in terms of graphons
[
38
,
21
,
27
]
. If one chooses instead the labels to be reals
\mathbb{L}_{n}=[0,n]
, Fig.
1
(b), and takes the symmetry group to the group of measure-preserving transformations of
[0,n]
, then in the limit we get random graphs with labels in
\mathbb{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
invariant with respect to measure-preserving transformations of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. These graphs have a nice representation in terms of graphexes
[
47
,
11
,
28
]
. In a way, these observations, based on applications of our coupling of projective and direct limits, provide “shortest paths” to graphons and graphexes.

Unfortunately, graphons are limits of dense graphs, graphexes are limits of sparse but not ultrasparse graphs, while most real-world networks are ultrasparse
[
7
,
4
]
. Here, by
ultrasparse
we mean that the (expected) average degree is bounded in the limit, while by
dense
we mean that it grows linearly with
n
. For a variety of reasons, it has been a major struggle to come up with useful general limits for ultrasparse graphs. The most relevant and powerful notion of graph limits applicable to ultrasparse graphs and explored well in the past, is perhaps the Benjamini-Schramm limit, also known as local convergence
[
5
,
2
,
24
]
. It characterizes well the local structure of the graph in the limit, yet it does not provide any prescription on how to sample a graph from its limit.

Our last example in Section
4
presents a simple combination of label spaces and their symmetry groups that can easily produce limits of ultrasparse graphs. This combination is actually the simplest out of the three examples presented in Section
4
. The label spaces
\mathbb{L}_{n}
are concentric balls of volume
n
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, Fig.
1
(c), while the symmetry group is the group of rotations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
around the center of the balls, so in the limit we get rotationally invariant graphs in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This example includes a collection of impactful ultrasparse random graph models studied extensively in diverse domains—(soft) random geometric graphs
[
43
,
44
]
, inhomogeneous random graphs
[
9
,
20
,
25
]
and their geometric versions
[
16
,
35
,
19
]
, as well as causal sets in quantum gravity
[
10
,
34
,
46
]
. The key difference between this example and the other two above is that there is currently no—nice or ugly—representation result for these rotationally invariant random graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Obtaining such a result would put these ultrasparse graph limits on par with graphons and graphexes.

Forgoing this representational challenge, our approach that couples projective and direct limits, provides a flexible unified framework for studying limits of random graphs, be they dense, sparse, or ultrasparse.

2
Background information and definitions

2.1
Point processes

We begin with a brief recollection of the definition of point processes suitable for our needs
[
36
]
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a Polish space, with topology
T
and denote by
\mathcal{X}:=\sigma(T)
the associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra,
and let
M(\mathbb{X})
to be the set of locally finite simple counting measures on
\mathbb{X}
.

We now endow
M(\mathbb{X})
with a measurable structure. For this we define for every
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
\xi\in M(\mathbb{X})
the evaluation map
\epsilon_{X}:\xi\mapsto\xi(X)
. We then let
\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}
denote the smallest topology such that the maps
\epsilon_{X}
are continuous. The associated
\sigma
-algebra is denoted by
\mathcal{M}:=\sigma(\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon})
. This is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra that makes the evaluation maps
\epsilon_{X}
measurable. We note that the described construction is the standard way to endow the space of locally finite simple counting measures on
\mathbb{X}
with a topology and measurable structure.

Now that we have a measurable space
(M(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{M})
, we define a
point process
as a random element of
M(\mathbb{X})
. We identify such a point process with its corresponding probability measure
\mu
on
(M(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{M})
, and say that
\mu
is
a point process on
\mathbb{X}
.

2.2
Projective limits

Here we will briefly discuss the notions of projective limits of topological spaces and of measures on them.

2.4
Limits of probabilistic symmetries

Having defined the notion of projective limits of probability measures and direct limits of groups, we now move forward to our main goal by defining the idea of probabilistic symmetry in the limit.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
be a measurable space and
\Phi
a group acting on
\mathbb{X}
. We say that
\Phi

acts measurably on
\mathbb{X}
if
\varphi^{-1}A\in\mathcal{X}
holds for all
\varphi\in\Phi
and
A\in\mathcal{X}
. This definition of measurable action is different from the standard one, see for example
[
29
, Chapter 1]
or
[
30
, Chapter 7]
. This requires a measurable structure on the group as well, which we do not need.

Definition 2.8

(Probabilistic symmetry)
.

A probability measure
\mu
on measurable space
\mathbb{X}
is
invariant with respect to a group
\Phi
that acts measurably on
\mathbb{X}
if

\mu(A)=\mu(\varphi^{-1}A),\quad\forall\varphi\in\Phi\text{ and }A\in\mathcal{X}.

The goal of this section is to define projective systems of invariant probability measures and their limits. This requires us to couple a projective system of topological spaces with a directed system of groups acting on them.

Definition 2.9

(Compatible system of topological spaces and groups)
.

A projective system of topological spaces
\langle\mathbb{X}_{n},T_{n},\pi_{mn}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
and a directed system of groups
\langle\Phi_{n},\eta_{nm}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
acting measurably on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
are
compatible
if

3
Main results: existence and symmetry of projective limits of point processes

Our main results are about projective limits of point processes and their invariance. Specifically, we identify the symmetry group with respect to which the projective limit of a point process is invariant.

Yet we start with a general result, showing that projective limits of probability measures that are invariant with respect to a compatible direct system of groups, is invariant with respect to the direct limit of these groups.

Theorem 3.1

(Direct limit of compatible groups preserves invariance)
.

Let
\langle\mathbb{X}_{n},T_{n},\pi_{mn}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
and
\langle\Phi_{n},\eta_{nm}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
be a compatible projective system of topological spaces and direct system of groups acting on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
. Furthermore, let
\langle\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{N}},T_{\mathbb{N}},\pi_{n}\rangle
and
\langle\Phi_{\mathbb{N}},\eta_{n}\rangle
denote, respectively, their projective and direct limits. Then the group
\Phi_{\mathbb{N}}
acts on
(\mathbb{X}_{\mathbb{N}},T_{\mathbb{N}})
compatibly with respect to
\pi_{n}
, i.e., the diagram (
8
) commutes.
Moreover, if
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a projective system of probability measures on
(\mathbb{X}_{n},\sigma(T_{n}))
that are invariant with respect to
\Phi_{n}
, then the projective limit
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}=\underleftarrow{\mathrm{lim}}\,\mu_{n}
is invariant with respect to
\Phi_{\mathbb{N}}=\underrightarrow{\mathrm{lim}}\,\Phi_{n}
.

Consider now a projective system
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
that are invariant under a direct system of groups. Then it follows from Theorem
3.1
that the projective limit of these point processes
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
, which is a probability measure on the projective limit of the
\langle M(\mathbb{X}_{n}),\sigma(T_{n}),\pi_{mn}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
, is invariant under the direct limit of the groups. This is nice, but the main problem with this observation is that according to Theorem
3.1
, the projective limit
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
is a general probability measure on a projective limit space, while we actually want
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
to be a point process on a given topological space. For this to be the case, we need to impose some additional conditions that would allow us to show that the projective limit of
\langle M(\mathbb{X}_{n}),\sigma(T_{n}),\pi_{mn}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
is a space of locally finite measures
M(\mathbb{X}_{\infty})
on some space
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}
.

To do so, let
(\mathbb{X},T)
be a Polish space and
\mathcal{X}
the associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Next, consider an exhausting sequence
(\mathbb{X}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of non-decreasing open sets, i.e.,
\mathbb{X}_{n}\in T
,
\mathbb{X}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{X}_{m}
for any
n\leq m
, and
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{X}_{n}=\mathbb{X}
. Then each
\mathbb{X}_{n}
with the induced subset topology is again a Polish space and we denote its Borel
\sigma
-algebra by
\mathcal{X}_{n}
. In particular, we can now consider sequences
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
.

To define the projective structure we denote by
\iota_{nm}:\mathbb{X}_{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}_{m}
and
\iota_{n}:\mathbb{X}_{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
the canonical inclusions, and note that these are continuous. Then we can define the projections
\pi_{mn}:M(\mathbb{X}_{m})\to M(\mathbb{X}_{n})
by

\pi_{mn}\xi=\xi\circ\iota_{nm},

(9)

where ‘
\circ
’ denotes the standard composition of functions. Similarly, we define the projections
\pi_{n}:M(\mathbb{X})\to M(\mathbb{X}_{n})
by

\pi_{n}\xi=\xi\circ\iota_{n}.

(10)

We now take a projective system of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
and show that their projective limit is a point process on
\mathbb{X}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Projective limits of point processes exist)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
and
(\mathbb{X}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be as above and let
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a projective system of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
with respect to
\pi_{mn}
defined in (
9
). Then the projective limit
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}=\underleftarrow{\mathrm{lim}}\,\mu_{n}
exists and corresponds to a probability measure on
M(\mathbb{X})
, i.e., a point process on
\mathbb{X}
, which is the unique probability measure satisfying
\pi_{n}\ast\mu_{\mathbb{N}}=\mu_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, where
\pi_{n}
is defined in (
10
).

Having established the existence of projective limits of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, we now turn to their symmetries. To start, we extend the group action from
\mathbb{X}_{n}
to
M(\mathbb{X}_{n})
. Let
\Phi_{n}
be a group that acts measurably on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
. Then the group action on a measure
\xi\in M(\mathbb{X}_{n})
is given by

\varphi_{n}\xi(A):=\xi(\varphi_{n}^{-1}A),

where
\varphi_{n}\in\Phi_{n}
and
A\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
. We note that this action is measurable as well.

Combining Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
now implies that a projective system of point processes
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that is invariant under a direct system of compatible groups
\langle\Phi_{n},\eta_{nm}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
acting on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
will yield a projective limit point process
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}

on
\mathbb{X}
that is invariant under the direct limit
\Phi_{\mathbb{N}}
of the groups.

However, this result is not sufficiently strong for some applications, in particular those that we will consider in the next section. For such applications, we want to go beyond the direct limit group because this group turns out to be not the full limit group with respect to which we want to show invariance of our limit point process. The direct limit group is instead a dense finitary subgroup of this full group. To illustrate what we mean, let
\Phi_{n}
be the group of permutation on
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. They form a direct system whose direct limit
\Phi_{\mathbb{N}}
is the group of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
that only change a finite number of elements
[
31
, Chapter 6]
. However, we really want to show the invariance of our limit measure with respect to the group
\Phi_{\infty}
of all permutations on
\mathbb{N}
, and this is where our definition of a compatible direct pre-limit group comes into play.

Yet for technical reasons, we need one more restriction on the connection between a projective limit probability measure
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
on
M(\mathbb{X})
and its pre-limit symmetry group
\Phi_{\infty}
.

Definition 3.1

(Finite characteristic)
.

Let
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a directed system of groups, and
\Phi_{\infty}
be a direct pre-limit. The invariance of a probability measure
\mu
on
M(\mathbb{X})
with respect to
\Phi_{\infty}
has a
finite characteristic
if there exists a family of subsets
\Psi_{n}\subseteq\Phi_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, such that the invariance of
\mu
with respect to
\Phi_{\infty}
holds if and only if

\mu(A)=\mu((\eta_{n}\varphi_{n})^{-1}A),\quad\forall n\geq 1,\,\varphi_{n}\in\Psi_{n},\text{ and }A\in\mathcal{X}.

With this last definition in place, we are ready to formulate our last main result.

Theorem 3.3

(Projective limits of invariant point processes are invariant)
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a projective system of point processes on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
with respect to
\pi_{mn}
, as in Theorem
3.2
, and let
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}=\underleftarrow{\mathrm{lim}}\,\mu_{n}
be its projective limit. Let
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a compatible directed system of groups acting on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, and let
\Phi_{\infty}
be a direct pre-limit group acting on
\mathbb{X}
, whose invariance has a finite characteristic with respect to
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
. If
\mu_{n}
is invariant with respect to
\Phi_{n}
for every
n\geq 1
, then
\mu_{\mathbb{N}}
is invariant with respect to
\Phi_{\infty}
.

Although the requirements on the limit group
\Phi_{\infty}
seem rather restrictive, we will provide a variety of relevant examples in the next section where they hold.

4
Applications to random graphs

In this section, we showcase the implications of our results by applying them to random graphs. We show how specific choices of spaces
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{X}_{n}
yield limits of different types of random graphs. In the first two examples, we recover the known limits of dense graphs and sparse graphs known as
graphons

[
38
,
21
,
27
]
and
graphexes

[
47
,
11
,
28
]
, which are invariant with respect to permutations of integer and real labels of vertices, respectively. The third example presents a completely new limit of ultrasparse graphs that are invariant under rotations, where vertices are labeled by their positions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.1
Random graphs as point processes

Random graphs can be viewed as point processes in spaces
\mathbb{X}:=\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
, where
\mathbb{L}
is the space of vertex labels, e.g.,
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{N}
in the simplest case. Points in such a point process are graph edges.

Indeed, let
(\mathbb{L},T)
be a Polish space and
\mathcal{L}
its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Then
\mathbb{X}:=\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
with the product topology is again Polish. Denote by
M_{s}(\mathbb{X})
the simple
symmetric
counting measures on
\mathbb{X}
, i.e., the counting measures
\xi
that satisfy
\xi=\xi\sigma^{-1}
, where
\sigma(x,y)=(y,x)
. Any undirected (random) graph
G
with vertex set
V\subset\mathbb{L}
and edge set
E\subset V\times V
has an associated (random) measure
\xi^{G}\in M_{s}(\mathbb{X})
given by

\xi^{G}=\sum_{(x,y)\in E}\delta_{(x,y)}.

Conversely, any (random) symmetric measure
\xi=\sum_{i}\delta_{e_{i}}
, where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{X}
, has an associated (random) undirected graph with the edge set
E=\cup_{i}e_{i}
. Therefore, we will identify the space of symmetric measures
M_{s}(\mathbb{X})
on space
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
with the space of undirected graphs with vertex labels in
\mathbb{L}
. Technically, in order to have
\xi^{G}
be locally finite we need that for any bounded set
B\subseteq\mathbb{L}
the set of vertices in
B
is finite, i.e.,
|B\cap V|<\infty
. This will be true for all the examples considered in this section, so we choose to skip the additional technical definitions here.

If graph
G
is random, and so is its associated measure
\xi^{G}
, we will denote by
\mu
, as before,
\xi^{G}
’s probability measure on
M_{s}(\mathbb{X})
, and refer to this
\mu
as a random graph on
\mathbb{L}
. We will also say that
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a projective system of random graphs with respect to some projections
\pi_{mn}
, if
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a projective system of symmetric point processes.

4.2

Here we show how our general results in Section
3
apply to general projective limits of random graphs.

To set up projective systems and limits of random graphs, let
(\mathbb{L}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exhausting sequence of non-decreasing open sets of the label space, i.e.,
\mathbb{L}_{n}\in T

With these conventions, we are now in specific settings of Section

(Projective limits of random graphs exist)

To apply Theorem

. Moreover, any directed system

of groups, where

for any
\varphi_{n},\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\in\Phi_{n}
.
To minimize clutter, we will slightly abuse the notation below by not distinguishing between the group acting on a label space and the same group acting on the space of random graphs on this label space, as they are related as above. We will similarly abuse the notation for homomorphisms
\eta_{nm}
as well.

With these notations, this corollary follows immediately from Theorem

Corollary 4.2

(Projective limits of invariant random graphs are invariant)

Let

4.3
Specific examples of projective graph limits

Here we apply Corollaries
4.1
and
4.2
to three specific random graph limits, which are still quite general from the point of view of random graphs. The first two examples recover known powerful notions of limits of dense and sparse random graphs, while the third one reveals a new notion of graph limits applicable to a wide class of ultrasparse graphs, which were found useful in practical applications.

To describe each example properly, we will have to:

1.

specify:

(a)

the label spaces
\mathbb{L}_{n}
and
\mathbb{L}
;

(b)

the symmetry groups
\Phi_{n}
and
\Phi_{\infty}
, as well as their actions on
\mathbb{L}_{n}
and
\mathbb{L}
, which extend to the product spaces as discussed in Section

;

(c)

the group homomorphisms
\eta_{nm}:\Phi_{n}\to\Phi_{m}
and
\eta_{n}:\Phi_{n}\to\Phi_{\infty}
;

2.

and verify that:

(a)

the space
\mathbb{L}
is Polish and
(\mathbb{L}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a exhausting non-decreasing sequence of open sets;

(b)

the system
\langle\Phi_{n},\eta_{nm}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
forms a compatible directed system of groups;

(c)

is a compatible direct pre-limit, i.e., diagrams (

) and (

) commute; and

(d)

the invariance with respect to
\Phi_{\infty}
is of finite characteristic.

We will refer to the numbering above in our specific examples below.

4.3.1
Graphons as projective limits of random graphs on
\mathbb{N}
, invariant under permutations

1.

(a)

We let
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{L}_{n}=[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and endow each of these with the standard counting topology.

(b)

We take
\Phi_{n}
to be the finite symmetric group of permutations on
[n]
, and
\Phi_{\infty}
the infinite symmetric group of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
.

(c)

We let
\eta_{nm}:\Phi_{n}\to\Phi_{m}
and
\eta_{n}:\Phi_{n}\to\Phi_{\infty}
be the canonical embeddings.

2.

(a)

Since we deal with with the standard counting topology,
\mathbb{L}
is Polish as required. Moreover, we observe that each
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is indeed an open subset of
\mathbb{L}
, and clearly
(\mathbb{L}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an exhausting non-decreasing sequence.

(b)

Because we work with the canonical embeddings, it is clear that
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
together with
\eta_{nm}
forms a directed system of groups. In addition, since

denotes a permutation of

that leaves

fixed and permutes the other elements the same as it did in

, it follows that the conditions of point 1 of Lemma

are satisfied. Hence, the system
\langle\Phi_{n},\eta_{nm}\rangle_{\mathbb{N}}
is a compatible directed system of groups.

(c)

Similar to the previous point, the use of the canonical embeddings ensure that diagram (
5
) commutes. The fact that
\eta_{n}\varphi_{n}
as permutation on
\mathbb{N}
leaves
\{n+1,n+2,\dots\}
fixed while permuting the others in the same way as it did in
[n]
, implies that the conditions of point 2 of Lemma
4.1
are satisfied. This then implies that diagram (

) commutes.

(d)

It is known
[
21
]
that a measure on
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is invariant under permutations of
\mathbb{N}
if it is invariant under any permutation

for all

in this case.

Therefore, the application of Corollaries

and

that are invariant under finite permutations is a random graph on
\mathbb{N}
that is invariant under infinite permutations. Furthermore, by evoking the classical representation result by Aldous and Hoover
[
3

, this limit random graph is a probabilistic mixture of

is a symmetric measurable function known as a

of i.i.d. points sampled from

, and connecting vertices
i
and

independently with probability

.

We summarize these observations in the following corollary:

Corollary 4.3

(Graphons as projective limits)
.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a projective sequence of random graphs with labels in

While the previous two examples stayed in the domain of known results for dense and sparse graphs, this last example moves us to the new domain of projective limits of ultrasparse graphs.

(c)

(d)

This follows immediately from the fact that all groups are the same.

Therefore, the application of Corollaries

and

Unfortunately, in contrast with the previous two examples, we lack any representation results for these graphs. However, since their symmetry group is small and does not increase with

, it is clear that the class of such graphs is extremely vast. In particular, it includes graphs that can be obtained by first sampling a rotationally invariant point process in

This subclass of rotationally invariant graphs is also very broad. It includes many random graph models that attracted significant research attention in different domains. First, the most basic examples of rotationally invariant point processes in

More importantly, by selecting different classes of functions

•

If

•

If

or distributions
[

•

If

[
16

, i.e., random hyperbolic graphs
[
35
,
19
]
.

•

If

In all these examples, if the point process is of a finite rate, and if
