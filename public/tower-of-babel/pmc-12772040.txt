Estimated pulse wave velocity and incident cognitive impairment risk in stroke patients: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Tao Xia, Xiaoling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArterial stiffness is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV), as a surrogate marker of arterial stiffness, may be associated with cognitive decline through vascular pathways. Yet studies examining the relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment in stroke patients remain limited.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected between 2011 and 2020, we included 2,003 stroke patients. ePWV was calculated using age and blood pressure, and cognitive impairment was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and word recall tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the association between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk, with nonlinear relationships evaluated through restricted cubic spline analysis. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves, decision curve analysis, and calibration plots, along with subgroup analyses.ResultsAfter adjusting for confounders, each standard deviation increase in ePWV was associated with a 26.3% increased risk of cognitive impairment (HR = 1.263, 95% CI: 1.154–1.383). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association (P nonlinear = 0.010). The highest ePWV quartile (Q4: 12.1–17.7 m/s) showed significantly elevated risk compared to the lowest quartile (Q1: 5.7–9.3 m/s) (HR = 2.828, 95% CI: 1.909–4.189), demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship. The predictive model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.812) and positive net benefit at threshold probabilities 0.1–0.8. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in males (HR = 1.333, 95% CI: 1.168–1.522) and patients with diabetes (HR = 1.426, 95% CI: 1.038–1.959).ConclusionePWV demonstrates a significant nonlinear positive association with cognitive impairment risk in stroke patients, suggesting its potential as a marker for cognitive impairment risk assessment and offering new perspectives for early prediction and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05392-2.

Introduction

Stroke remains a significant global public health challenge with an increasing disease burden worldwide [1]. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease Study, stroke ranks as the third leading cause of death and disability globally, with an age-standardized mortality rate of 87.4/100,000 (95% CI: 79.5–94.4) [2]. In China, the number of stroke patients reached 17.8 million in 2020, with an age-standardized prevalence of 2.6% (95% CI: 2.6%−2.6%), and incidence and mortality rates of 505.2/100,000 person-years (95% CI: 488.5–522.0) and 343.4/100,000 person-years (95% CI: 329.6–357.2.6.2), respectively [3]. Post-stroke Cognitive Impairment (PSCI), defined as cognitive decline occurring within 6 months following stroke that affects one or more cognitive domains, represents an underrecognized yet significant complication, affecting 20–80% of stroke survivors, with this wide variation attributed to differences in study populations, diagnostic criteria, assessment tools, and timing of evaluation post-stroke, impacting both quality of life and rehabilitation outcomes while imposing substantial socioeconomic burden [4, 5]. Therefore, identifying simple and effective predictors of cognitive impairment is crucial for improving post-stroke patient management.

Arterial stiffness has been established as a critical indicator for cardiovascular risk assessment, with carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) recognized as the gold standard measurement [6]. Studies have demonstrated that cfPWV independently predicts cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality risk [7, 8]. However, cfPWV measurement requires specialized equipment and trained personnel, limiting its application in large-scale population studies and primary healthcare settings [9]. In recent years, estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV), calculated from clinical parameters such as age and blood pressure, has emerged as a practical alternative to cfPWV. The Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease study revealed that subjects in the highest quartile of ePWV demonstrated significantly increased stroke risk compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.57–3.58) [10]. Previous research has demonstrated positive linear associations between ePWV and cardiovascular disease risk, with higher ePWV values consistently predicting increased cardiovascular events. Similarly, emerging evidence suggests a positive association between arterial stiffness and cognitive decline [11]. Arterial stiffness may influence cognitive decline through its effects on cerebral perfusion and vascular endothelial function [12]. However, systematic research on the predictive value of ePWV for cognitive impairment in stroke patients remains limited.

Based on these considerations, this study utilizes longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database to investigate the relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment in stroke patients. Through restricted cubic spline analysis, we explore potential non-linear associations and evaluate the predictive value of ePWV for post-stroke cognitive impairment, aiming to provide new insights for cognitive assessment and early intervention in stroke patients.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese residents aged 45 years and above. CHARLS employed a multistage stratified probability proportional sampling strategy, initially enrolling 17,708 participants from 28 provinces in the 2011–2012 baseline survey, with follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 [13]. This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 45 years; (2) confirmed history of stroke based on self-reported physician diagnosis during CHARLS interviews, regardless of time since stroke onset; (3) complete blood pressure measurements and cognitive assessments. Exclusion criteria included: (1) missing key variables; (2) abnormal blood pressure values (systolic blood pressure < 60 or >260 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure < 30 or >160 mmHg) [14, 15]; (3) cognitive impairment at baseline defined as total cognitive score < 10 using the MMSE-based cognitive assessment; (4) follow-up time < 1 year or missing cognitive assessments during follow-up. A total of 2,003 stroke patients were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure S1).

Assessment of ePWV and cognitive impairment

Blood pressure was measured using an Omron HEM-7200 monitor. Participants were required to rest quietly for 5 min before three seated measurements were taken at 45-second intervals. The average of these measurements was used for subsequent analyses. The ePWV was calculated using age and blood pressure data according to the following formula [16, 17]:

ePWV = 9.587 − 0.402 × age + 4.560 × 10 − 3 × age2 − 2.621 × 10 − 5 × age2 × MBP + 3.176 × 10 − 3 × age × MBP − 1.832 × 10 − 2 × MBP.

where MBP (Mean Blood Pressure) = DBP + 0.4 × (SBP - DBP), SBP and DBP represent systolic and diastolic blood pressure, respectively.

Cognitive function was assessed using a structured cognitive battery adapted from the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in Chinese version [18]. Cognitive function assessment comprised two domains (total score 0–21): Executive Function (score range 0–11), including temporal orientation (year, month, day, season, week; 1 point each), serial subtraction (five consecutive subtractions; 1 point each), and figure copying (1 point); and Episodic Memory (score range 0–10), evaluated through immediate and delayed word recall tests, with scores averaged [19, 20]. Cognitive impairment was defined as a total score < 10 [20]. All cognitive assessments were conducted by standardized trained investigators.

Covariates

Based on previously identified factors affecting cognitive impairment [21], we collected covariates including age (continuous variable), body mass index (BMI), gender, education level (primary and below/junior high school/high school and above), marital status, residential area, retirement status, current smoking status (yes vs. no), current alcohol consumption (yes vs. no), physical activity, and history of diabetes and heart disease.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive analyses, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Participants were divided into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on ePWV values. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables across quartiles, while chi-square tests were used for categorical variables.

Overfitting was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 indicating significant model overfitting (Table S1). Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves to estimate the cumulative incidence of cognitive impairment across ePWV quartiles, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. We then employed stratified Cox proportional hazards regression models to assess the association between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk, with stratification for residence, retirement status, and physical activity to address potential proportional hazards assumption violations, calculating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for demographic characteristics (age, gender, BMI); and Model 3 further adjusted for all variables in Model 2 plus socioeconomic factors (marital status, residence, retirement status, and education level), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity), and comorbidities (diabetes, heart disease). To comprehensively evaluate the association pattern, ePWV was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as quartiles.

To explore potential non-linear associations between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed using five knots (5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles). Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the heterogeneity of associations across different population characteristics, including age (< 65/≥65 years), gender (male/female), BMI (underweight: <18.5 kg/m², normal weight: 18.5–24.9 kg/m², overweight/obese: ≥25.0 kg/m²) based on WHO criteria for Asian populations to facilitate clinical interpretation and identify potential threshold effects [22, 23], marital status (married/others), residential area (rural/urban), retirement status (yes/no), lifestyle factors (smoking [yes/no], alcohol consumption [yes/no]), and comorbidities (diabetes [yes/no], heart disease [yes/no]). Likelihood ratio tests were used to assess potential interaction effects.

The predictive performance of the fully adjusted model (Model 3) was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calculating the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CI. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical utility across different risk thresholds. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions), and calibration curves were plotted [24].

We performed multiple sensitivity analyses to verify the stability of our results. To address potential subclinical cognitive decline, we conducted analysis excluding participants with baseline cognitive scores in the lowest tertile (10–12). We further incorporated hypertension, sleep duration, and depression score (CESD-10) as additional adjusted covariates based on Model 3 to examine the robustness of the results. Additionally, a competing risk model was also used to determine the risk of ePWV for cognitive impairment after considering the competitive risk of death.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), with two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Based on ePWV quartiles [Q1: 5.7–9.3 m/s, Q2: 9.3–10.6 m/s, Q3: 10.6–12.1 m/s, Q4: 12.1–17.7 m/s], we divided 2,003 stroke patients into four groups (Q1-Q4) for analysis. Characteristics of participants showed significant differences in demographic features and health status across groups (Table 1). Key findings included substantial age differences across quartiles, with Q1 patients averaging 54.27 ± 5.08 years compared to 78.84 ± 6.60 years in Q4 (P < 0.001). This wide age distribution reflects the strong correlation between age and ePWV and was addressed through multivariable adjustment in subsequent analyses. Gender distribution showed a higher proportion of females in lower ePWV quartiles (56.1% in Q1 vs. 41.4% in Q4, P < 0.001). Cognitive scores demonstrated a clear gradient, decreasing from 10.51 ± 4.12 points in Q1 to 7.95 ± 4.14 points in Q4 (P < 0.001), with cognitive impairment prevalence increasing from 41.4% to 66.2% across quartiles. Blood pressure parameters showed expected increases with higher ePWV levels.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curve results showed significant differences in cognitive impairment incidence among the four groups during follow-up (Log-rank P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1). The survival curves demonstrated a clear hierarchy, with Q4 showing the steepest decline in cognitive function-free survival, followed by Q2, Q3, and Q1 respectively. Some curve crossing was observed in the early follow-up period, likely reflecting the complex interplay between age and ePWV effects. During the 5-year follow-up period, the cumulative survival rate of Q4 group patients was significantly lower than the other three groups, decreasing from 1.00 at baseline to 0.153 (95% CI: 0.116–0.202). In comparison, the cumulative survival rate decreased from 1.00 to 0.291 (95% CI: 0.232–0.365) in Q1 group, to 0.247 (95% CI: 0.194–0.314) in Q2 group, and to 0.336 (95% CI: 0.279–0.404) in Q3 group. This suggests that higher ePWV levels may be an early predictor of cognitive impairment occurrence.

Association between ePWV and cognitive impairment

Among all 2,003 study participants, 989 cognitive impairment events occurred. Study results showed a significant association between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk (Table 2). For each standard deviation increase in ePWV (SD = 2.1 m/s), the risk of cognitive impairment increased by 26.3% in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) (HR = 1.263, 95% CI: 1.154–1.383, P < 0.001). Quartile analysis showed that compared with Q1 group, Q4 group had significantly higher risk of cognitive impairment (HR = 2.828, 95% CI: 1.909–4.189, P < 0.001). Furthermore, nonlinear association analysis showed a significant nonlinear relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk (P overall < 0.001, P nonlinear = 0.010) (Fig. 2). The relationship showed a steeper increase in risk above approximately 11 m/s, suggesting a potential threshold effect.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis results showed consistent positive correlations between ePWV and post-stroke cognitive impairment, but the predictive strength showed significant heterogeneity across different subgroups. When ePWV was analyzed as a continuous variable, factors such as BMI, marital status, retirement status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and diabetes showed significant interactions (P for interaction < 0.05) (Figure S2). The increase in cognitive impairment risk was more pronounced among males (HR = 1.333, 95% CI: 1.168–1.522), individuals with normal weight (HR = 1.345, 95% CI: 1.172–1.543), and patients with diabetes (HR = 1.426, 95% CI: 1.038–1.959) and heart disease (HR = 1.314, 95% CI: 1.072–1.611). In the quartile analysis of ePWV (Fig. 3), multiple subgroups including BMI, marital status, retirement status, smoking, and alcohol consumption showed significant interactions (P for interaction < 0.05), while other subgroups showed no significant interactions, but overall Q4 group had higher cognitive impairment risk compared to Q1 group. For example, BMI stratification showed highest risk in the normal weight population Q4 group (HR = 4.122, 95% CI: 2.230–7.621, P < 0.001), non-smoker Q4 group (HR = 2.985, 95% CI: 1.934–4.606, P < 0.001) and non-drinker Q4 group (HR = 2.791, 95% CI: 1.778–4.381, P < 0.001) all showed significantly elevated risk. These results indicate significant population heterogeneity in the association between ePWV and cognitive impairment, a finding that has important implications for clinical risk stratification assessment and personalized prevention strategy development.

Model performance

The predictive model demonstrated good performance (Fig. 4). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed the model’s area under the curve (AUC) was 0.812 (95% CI: 0.793–0.830, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). At the optimal cutoff point of 0.216, the model showed sensitivity of 69.97% and specificity of 77.61%, with positive and negative predictive values of 75.30% and 72.60%, respectively, and a Youden index of 0.476 (Table S2). Decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that the model showed clinical net benefit across most threshold probabilities, with optimal performance within a risk threshold range of 0.1 to 0.8 (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the calibration curve showed good consistency between predicted probabilities and actual observed outcomes (Fig. 4C), further confirming the model’s clinical application value.

Sensitivity analysis

The exclusion of participants with potentially subclinical cognitive decline did not attenuate the association; rather, the effect size was slightly enhanced, confirming the robustness of our findings (Table S3-4). Despite the inclusion of additional potential confounders, the association between ePWV and cognitive function remained robust and statistically significant, indicating that the observed relationship is independent of these factor (Table S5). The competing risk analysis yielded consistent results) (Table S6), with subdistribution hazard ratios of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.144–1.618), 1.515 (95% CI: 1.239–1.854), and 3.078 (95% CI: 2.42–3.916) for Q2, Q3, and Q4 versus Q1, respectively (all P < 0.001. Similar findings were observed when ePWV was treated as a continuous variable, confirming the robustness of our findings after accounting for competing mortality.

Discussion

This study, utilizing data from the CHARLS database, represents the first systematic investigation of the relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment in stroke patients. Our findings demonstrate a significant positive association between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk, exhibiting a clear dose-response pattern with important predictive value.

After comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors, we found that each standard deviation increase in ePWV was associated with a 26.3% increased risk of cognitive impairment in stroke patients. This finding is consistent with previous research on ePWV and cognitive impairment. The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) demonstrated that, after adjusting for age, blood pressure, and other factors, ePWV was significantly associated with both cognitive impairment without dementia (OR = 2.761) and dementia (OR = 6.344) [25]. Additionally, the Northern Manhattan Study confirmed significant associations between ePWV and cognitive impairment (b=−0.100, 95% CI: −0.120, −0.080) and its temporal changes (b=−0.063, 95% CI: −0.082, −0.045) [21]. These findings collectively support the crucial role of arterial stiffness in age-related cognitive decline and provide a theoretical foundation for our observed associations in stroke patients. However, the magnitude of associations varies across studies, which may be attributed to differences in study populations, follow-up duration, cognitive assessment methods, and baseline cardiovascular risk profiles.

Our analysis revealed a nonlinear rather than purely dose-dependent relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk. The highest quartile (Q4) showed significantly elevated risk compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) (HR = 2.828, 95% CI: 1.909–4.189), with the most pronounced risk increase occurring above the threshold of approximately 11 m/s. This finding parallels results from the Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease study, which reported increased stroke risk in the highest ePWV quartile (HR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.57–3.58) [10]. A study in rural Chinese elderly populations found that each 1 m/s increase in ePWV was associated with increased risks of dementia (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.30–1.75) and Alzheimer’s disease (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.33–1.89) [26]. The higher risk ratios observed in stroke patients may reflect the unique interaction between arterial stiffness and post-stroke cognitive impairment, where impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation may amplify the adverse effects of arterial stiffness on cerebral perfusion [27]. These findings not only confirm a dose-dependent relationship between ePWV and cognitive impairment but also support ePWV as a potential marker for assessing cognitive impairment risk in stroke patients.

The impact of stroke and elevated ePWV on cognitive impairment involves complex pathophysiological mechanisms. Given that ePWV is calculated from age and blood pressure, the observed association with cognitive outcomes is partially expected due to the known relationships between these variables and cognitive impairment. However, our findings suggest effects beyond these inherent correlations. At the neuronal level, acute stroke causes neuronal death and synaptic dysfunction, while the chronic phase leads to gliosis and persistent inflammation [28–30]. At the vascular level, stroke severely disrupts cerebral autoregulation, increasing brain sensitivity to blood pressure fluctuations [31]. Elevated ePWV may reflect underlying arterial stiffness that affects cerebral blood flow regulation through increased pulse pressure, endothelial damage, and inflammatory responses [32]. The nonlinear relationship we observed suggests potential threshold effects, where vascular compromise becomes particularly detrimental above certain ePWV levels. Research has demonstrated that arterial stiffness significantly correlates with white matter lesions, with each standard deviation increase in arterial stiffness associated with a 7% increase in white matter lesion volume [33, 34]. In stroke patients, these two pathological processes interact synergistically, with impaired cerebrovascular regulation amplifying the adverse effects of arterial stiffness, while continued vascular stress and inflammation accelerate neurodegeneration [35]. This vicious cycle not only exacerbates endothelial dysfunction but also promotes β-amyloid deposition and neuronal mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to accelerated cognitive decline [36, 37]. It is important to acknowledge that our study lacks direct biological measurements to validate these proposed mechanisms. The mechanistic pathways discussed above are largely inferential based on existing literature, and alternative explanations for the observed associations cannot be ruled out. Future studies incorporating biomarker analyses, neuroimaging, and molecular-level investigations are needed to elucidate the specific biological processes linking ePWV to cognitive outcomes in stroke patients.

Our subgroup analysis provided important clinical implications. The association between ePWV and cognitive impairment showed significant heterogeneity across subgroups, with stronger associations observed in elderly (≥ 65 years), male, and normal-weight populations. The stronger association in the elderly group may reflect the interaction between age-related vascular deterioration and post-stroke vascular remodeling [38, 39]. The enhanced association in males may be related to gender-specific vascular protective mechanisms [40]. These findings align with our nonlinear results, suggesting that certain populations may be particularly susceptible to threshold effects of elevated ePWV.

Our predictive model demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.812), though direct comparisons with existing clinical prediction models for post-stroke cognitive impairment are limited due to the relative novelty of this research area. Future studies should compare ePWV-based models with traditional risk factors and establish its incremental predictive value.

Our predictive model demonstrated moderate discriminative ability (AUC = 0.812), though several important caveats warrant discussion. Given that ePWV calculation incorporates age as a key component, this predictive performance may be substantially driven by age-related cognitive decline rather than ePWV-specific vascular effects. The clinical utility of ePWV-based prediction remains uncertain, as we have not demonstrated meaningful incremental value beyond simpler age-based models. The added complexity of ePWV calculation may not justify its use in clinical practice without evidence of superior predictive performance compared to readily available risk factors. Future studies should rigorously compare ePWV-based models with traditional risk factors, establish incremental predictive value, and validate clinical utility before recommending implementation in practice.

Our study has several strengths. It is based on a large population cohort with adequate sample size and extended follow-up duration. Additionally, our study provides evidence for the ePWV-cognitive impairment relationship in stroke patients, contributing to this emerging research area.

However, several important limitations exist. The cognitive assessment tools, while standardized, may not capture subtle cognitive changes or domain-specific impairments. We also lacked neuroimaging data to directly verify the relationship between ePWV and brain structural changes. Moreover, the inherent correlation between ePWV components (age, blood pressure) and cognitive outcomes may partially explain the observed associations. Potential recall bias may exist in the collection of baseline covariates, particularly regarding stroke history and lifestyle factors, as these were based on self-reported data. Additionally, insufficient biological markers such as inflammatory indicators limited our ability to explore potential mechanisms in depth. Our predictive model has not undergone external validation in independent cohorts, and therefore should be applied with caution in clinical practice until further validation studies are conducted. Furthermore, our subgroup analyses were conducted without multiple testing correction, which increases the risk of Type I error. These analyses were primarily exploratory in nature, and results should be interpreted with caution, requiring future dedicated studies for validation. Several additional methodological limitations warrant discussion. Survival bias may influence our findings, as patients with severe vascular pathology might have died before developing observable cognitive impairment, potentially leading to underestimation of the true risk in high ePWV groups. We lacked detailed information on stroke severity and subtypes (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic, large vessel vs. small vessel), which are important determinants of cognitive outcomes and potential confounders. Finally, our findings are based on a Chinese population, and generalizability to other ethnic groups and healthcare settings remains uncertain, requiring validation in diverse populations. While our longitudinal design provides evidence for temporal associations between ePWV and cognitive decline, we cannot definitively establish causal relationships due to potential unmeasured confounding factors and the observational nature of the study. The possibility of reverse causation cannot be entirely excluded, as subclinical cognitive changes might influence vascular health through complex pathways including medication adherence, lifestyle factors, and stress responses.

Conclusion

This study shous a significant nonlinear association between ePWV and cognitive impairment risk in stroke patients. Each standard deviation increase in ePWV was associated with a 26.3% increased risk of cognitive impairment, with the highest quartile showing nearly threefold increased risk. The predictive model demonstrated good discriminative ability and clinical utility. These findings suggest that ePWV may serve as a useful tool for cognitive risk assessment in stroke patients, particularly in males and those with diabetes.
