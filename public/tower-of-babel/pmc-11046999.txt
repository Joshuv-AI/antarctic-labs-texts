Evaluation of patients' suitability for a treatment break from bisphosphonates
Authors: Annie Noor Gheasuddin
Published: 2023

Bisphosphonates have been widely used in the treatment of osteoporosis, with data demonstrating efficacy in fracture risk reduction over 3–5 years of treatment. Although bisphosphonates are generally safe and well tolerated, there is a well-documented relationship with adverse effects such as osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femoral fractures, atrial fibrillation, oesophageal cancer and renal impairment after prolonged use. Due to this dose–response relationship and that bisphosphonates accumulate in bone with persistent antifracture efficacy after therapy is stopped, it is reasonable to consider a drug holiday.

SMART improvement aim: To increase the number of patients taking a drug holiday from bisphosphonates where appropriate (ie patients not meeting the exclusion criteria). This quality improvement project (QIP) was carried out at a GP practice in Coventry (Clay Lane Medical Practice).

The search was for all patients currently at the practice who have been started on bisphosphonate therapy in the past 12 months. This returned a total of 61 patients. From these, the search was for patients who met the exclusion criteria (ie ≥7.5 mg prednisolone, fragility fractures during bisphosphonate treatment, T score ≤–2.5 after 3–5 years of treatment). This returned a total of 23 patients. These patients were divided into low, medium and high risk, according to the Derbyshire Joint Area Prescribing Committee (JAPC) guidelines.

13 patients were found to be at low risk, four at moderate risk and six at high risk.

Low-risk patients can stop treatment. They require reassessment using FRAX/DEXA if they suffer a further fracture or their risk factors alter. They should continue to take calcium and vitamin D supplements during the treatment break. Moderate-risk patients require assessment of bone mineral density for consideration of a treatment break with a DEXA. Patients with a femoral neck T score ≥–2.0 have a low risk of vertebral fracture and are unlikely to benefit from continued treatment. These patients should discontinue therapy, restarting when and if indications for therapy are met. High-risk patients should continue therapy with a bisphosphonate if no contraindications or intolerance for a further 5 years.

The patients at the practice who have been flagged up as low risk will be contacted by the community pharmacist, a medication review will be conducted, and the patient will be commenced on a treatment break if appropriate. Furthermore, the patients who fall into the moderate-risk category will also be contacted and a DEXA scan will be arranged to decide whether a treatment break is suitable. A practice-specific template based on the Derbyshire JAPC guidelines will be created for the clinicians at the practice. This will make it simpler to advise the clinicians about appropriate management and facilitate decision-making. To ensure that no patients are missed, the audit should be reviewed by the community pharmacist annually. This should be done by flagging all patients at the practice who have been commenced on bisphosphonate treatment in the past 12 months, applying the exclusion criteria and then sorting into low, moderate and high risk.
