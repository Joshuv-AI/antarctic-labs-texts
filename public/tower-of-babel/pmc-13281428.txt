The influence of adolescent diet on the occurrence of late-onset multiple sclerosis: a population-based case–control study
Authors: Naghmeh Abbasi Kasbi, Nasim Rezaeimanesh, Sanaz Khodadadi, Sahar Nikkhah Bahrami, Kosar Kohandel, Sajjad Ghane Ezabadi, Faezeh Khodaie, Amir Hossein Sahraian, Melika Arab Bafrani, Sharareh Eskandarieh, Mohammad Ali Sahraian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe role of diet in multiple sclerosis (MS) development is still a matter of debate and its impact on MS course is not well understood. We tried to investigate the possible role of adolescent diet in late-onset multiple sclerosis (LOMS) odds.MethodsLOMS patients were obtained from the National MS Registry of Iran. Controls were sex and age matched with no history of neurological disorders. We assessed dietary factors using a questionnaire based on multinational studies. Food consumption levels were classified into low, medium, and high for each item. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate diet's impact on LOMS odds.ResultsWe included 83 LOMS cases and 207 matched healthy controls. The mean age for LOMS patients was 61.14, compared to 61.51 years for controls. The results showed that higher consumption of dairy as in the third tertile associated with 79% decline in LOMS odds (AOR: 0.21; 95%CI: 0.09–0.47). Higher seafood consumption, in the third tertile was associated with a decrease in the odds of LOMS (AOR: 0.32; 95%CI: 0.14–0.72). Furthermore, increased nut consumption, in the third tertile (AOR: 0.37; 95%CI: 0.18–0.77), decreased the odds of LOMS. Additionally, higher consumption of fruits (AOR: 0.22; 95%CI: 0.07–0.63) and vegetables (AOR: 0.26; 95%CI: 0.12–0.55) was linked to a reduced odds of LOMS.ConclusionThis research highlights the advantageous impact of dairy products, seafood, nuts, fruits, and vegetables in lowering the LOMS odds. Hence, advocating for nutrition role in development of LOMS could represent a preventive measure for people susceptible to MS.Keywords: Late-Onset, Multiple Sclerosis, Odds, Diet, Nutrition

Introduction

Late-onset multiple sclerosis (LOMS) characterized by the occurrence of MS beyond the age of 50, affects less than 10% of the MS population [33]. Over the last 60 years, the occurrence of MS has more than doubled. This rise encompasses individuals aged 50 and above as well. In comparison to adult-onset type of MS, LOMS is often accompanying with more unfavorable disease outcomes and a faster progression of disability [20, 27]. Research indicates that factors such as older age at presentation (characteristic of LOMS) correlates with a more rapid progression of disability, and disease severity [15, 28]. Hence, the identification of risk factors associated with this particular type of MS is advantageous for the purpose of prevention.

The specific mechanism responsible for the development of MS are still unclear. However, various environmental factors and genetic components are considered to contribute to the development of MS. Variables including smoking, inadequate vitamin D levels, obesity, and unhealthy diets have been involved in the pathogenesis of MS [31]. Pursuing a well-balanced life by incorporating routine exercise and a nutritious diet has been demonstrated to reduce inflammation and potentially lessen the speed of MS progression [43].

Some research has demonstrated that patients with MS had less healthy diets than the general population [21, 22]. Several studies have been performed to investigate the correlation between dietary patterns and MS risk, revealing some evidence of a potential association. The results suggested that increased intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins, besides restrictions on convenience foods, sugar, and saturated fatty acid, can confer favorable effects on account of their inherent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory features. Conversely, diets characterized by high intake of animal fat or saturated fat, while concurrently low polyunsaturated fat and inclusive of high fat dairy products, have shown an elevated risk of developing MS [8, 29]. Research investigations have revealed a correlation between higher rates of MS and individuals who consume excessive amounts of saturated fats and exhibit insufficient amounts of vitamin D. Recent studies have also demonstrated that individuals who regularly consume a significant quantity of fish and integrate omega-3 supplements into their dietary intake have a reduced odds of developing MS [4, 13]. These compelling findings highlight the significant influence of nutritional pattern considerations in the chance of developing MS [31, 37]

While potential correlations exist between the onset of MS and dietary habits, research on investigating the relationship between LOMS and dietary components is insufficient. The exact contribution of dietary factors to the development of LOMS still remains uncertain. Therefore, the intention of this inquiry is to assess the potential correlation among dietary factors during adolescence period of life and the odds of LOMS occurrence.

Materials and methods

Study design

This research was involved a population-based case-control study in Tehran from November 2022 to July 2023. We employed a combination of in-person and remote data collection methods to investigate the potential correlation between the dietary factors, and the odds of LOMS occurrence.

Participants

The study population comprised individuals aged 50 years and above who reside in any of the 22 districts within the Tehran. To identify cases, we enrolled all definite LOMS patients according to the 2017 McDonald criteria [44] and registered in the national MS registry of Iran (NMSRI) [7, 14]. Controls, healthy individuals with no history of MS, were randomly selected from the similar cases’ source of population. The selection process involved sex and age-matched randomization. We excluded individuals cognitive impairment or any neurological disorder other than MS" from both groups. Overall, we contacted with 83 registered cases and 207 matched controls for further investigation.

Matching Ratio: We employed a 1:2 matching ratio (83 cases to 207 controls), which is appropriate for case-control studies to enhance statistical power.

Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interview with random selected controls. This approach ensured that controls were truly representative of the general population of Tehran.

Data collection

To gather relevant data, we retrieved clinical characteristics of LOMS from the NMSRI. Additionally, we utilized an internationally established questionnaire called EnvIMS-Q, specifically conducted for multinational case-control studies examining environmental risk factors for MS [36]. This questionnaire focuses on the evaluating the risk factors during adolescence (13–19 years) on MS risk. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this questionnaire has been published previously [40]. To supplement our findings, we conducted telephone interviews among cases led by two proficient interviewers. Furthermore, five interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews with the general population located in various areas of Tehran, who served as the control group. The same questionnaire, EnvIMS-Q, was also employed. Nutritional epidemiology notes that while recall bias is a concern in case-control studies, the use of validated instruments and careful study design can yield meaningful results.

Exposure assessment

The participants were asked to answer the study questionnaire about the main epidemiological items including demographic questions such as age, sex, marital status, paternal and maternal ethnicity (Fars, Kurd, Lur, Turk, Mazani, Gilak, Baloch, Turkmen, and Arab), the highest level of education, and their past dietary history.

Data on the average frequency and amount (based on special portion sizes) of consumption for each food item per day, week, or month during the age of 13 to 19 were collected from participants. To address recall bias, participants were asked to consult their parents or family members if they were uncertain about their food consumption.

We classified and reported the EnvIMS-Q foods items as mentioned below:

Dairy group: Yogurt, milk, cheese, and doogh (a traditional Iranian yogurt-based drink).

Statistical analysis

Mean (standard deviation) and count (percentage) were employed to define quantitative and qualitative data, respectively. Independent samples t-test or chi2 tests were used to compare quantitative and qualitative variables between two groups, respectively. All food groups’ intakes were converted to units per week, except seafoods and fast foods, which were reported as units per month. In order to reach low, medium, and high levels of intake, the mean consumption of each food group was divided into three subgroups. Food groups were tertiled using the visual binning method in SPSS software and by determining two cut-points for each food group. In this method, the dietary intakes of each group are divided into equal categories based on the intake levels observed in the study sample. In our study, dietary intakes were divided into three groups, allowing the intakes to be classified as low, medium, and high. This approach facilitates comparison between the study groups and helps in calculating the odds ratio. The median intake of each food group in each tertile, plus the number of cases and controls in each tertile, were reported in the relevant tables. Binary logistic regression models were applied to investigate the role of diet in the odds of LOMS. Crude and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were also used to check the effect size of independent variables on dependent variables. Adjustment was established for age, gender, parent’s ethnicity as well as educational level. The analyses were completed by Stata software version 14 and at a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

All participant were thoroughly informed about their role in the study and the purpose of this research, and all their probable questions about the study were answered. Besides, they were required to give verbal consent being informed that their personal information is kept secure. Those who were unwilling to take part in our study were excluded. The protocol of this research was approved by the institutional review boards (IRB) at the ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences with the code IR.TUMS.SINAHOSPITAL.REC.1404.088.

Higher intake of dairy in the third tertile against the first tertile correlated with a 77% decrease (OR: 0.23; 95%CI: 0.11–0.50) and 79% decrease (AOR: 0.21; 95%CI: 0.09–0.47) in the odds of LOMS in unadjusted model and adjusted regression model with the P-value for trend of < 0.01, respectively (Table 2).

In terms of meat group intake, the crude OR for LOMS was 0.28 (OR: 0.28; 95%CI: 0.13–0.58) in the third tertile. However, following regression modeling for confounders, the third (AOR: 0.31; 95%CI: 0.14–0.66) tertile of meat consumers showed reduced odds of LOMS compared to the first tertile (Table 3).

Sub-analysis of meat sub-groups showed that red meat, poultry, and egg intake did not correlate with LOMS odds (P > 0.05). However, the increased amount of seafood’s intake in the third tertile versus the first tertile, led to significant decrease in the odds of LOMS in both unadjusted (OR: 0.26; 95%CI: 0.12–0.55) and adjusted regression (AOR: 0.32; 95%CI: 0.14–0.72) models (Table 3).

Additionally, in terms of nut consumption, higher intakes in the second and third tertiles lead to lower odds of LOMS in unadjusted regression model and in the third tertile of adjusted model. In the adjusted model the OR and 95% CI were as follows: AOR: 0.37; 95%CI: 0.18–0.77 in the third tertile (Table 3).

However, consumption of fat sources was not associated with the odds of LOMS (P-value > 0.05) (Table 4).

Higher intake of fruits in the third tertile was related to reduced odds of LOMS in the unadjusted (OR: 0.22; 95%CI: 0.08–0.62) and adjusted regression (AOR: 0.22; 95%CI: 0.07–0.63) models (P-value < 0.01). A higher intake of vegetables led to a significant reduction in LOMS odds (unadjusted model: OR: 0.35; 95%CI: 0.17–0.73; adjusted model: AOR: 0.26; 95%CI: 0.12–0.55) (P-value < 0.01) (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study examined how varied dietary intakes in adolescence (13–19 years old) may affect the odds of developing LOMS in adulthood for the first time. This investigation demonstrated that consumption of more fruits, vegetables, dairy products, meat (which was linked to increased intake of seafood and nut subgroups), and dairy products may provide protection against LOMS. These findings suggested that healthy dietary patterns may have beneficial effects on MS prevention.

The female-to-male ratio of 1.5:1 in our LOMS sample merits is lower than the typical 2.5:1 ratio observed in classic adult-onset MS. However, this finding aligns closely with previous LOMS studies, including a recent meta-analysis reporting a pooled ratio of 1.8:1. This observation underscores that LOMS may have distinct epidemiological characteristics warranting dedicated study rather than extrapolation from adult-onset MS data [33].

The impact of diet on the odds of MS has been explored by various studies. A case–control study evaluated the diet-related habits of PPMS cases throughout adolescence and discovered that eating more dairy, seafood, chicken, fruit, and vegetables was connected to a decreased chance of MS in contrast to the overall population, which aligns with the outcomes of the present study [37].

Numerous factors, such as socioeconomic status influence dietary preferences. Increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, and seafood was mostly seen in people with higher levels of education [18, 30]. The control group in the present investigation represented more advanced educational level compared to those with LOMS. This disparity in education level may have contributed to a decrease in the consumption of certain foods, which in turn could have led to the development of LOMS.

The result of our research exhibited that consumption of dairy was connected with a decrease in the odds of LOMS which is consistent with previous case–control studies that showed a lower odds of MS with higher dairy consumption [2, 37]. The components of dairy products, such as proteins, calcium, vitamin A, and fatty acids could have a protective impact on MS development. Abdollahpour et al. showed that consuming more cheese and yogurt throughout adolescence was linked to a considerably decreased incidence of MS [2]. Similarly, it was found that yogurt consumption at any age during childhood and early adolescence was associated with reduced risk of MS after 18 years old, while no such association was observed with butter, cheese, or milk consumption [10]. One possible mechanism for this correlation is the role of vitamin A that can change the balance between two types of immune cells, regulatory T lymphocytes (Treg) and T helper 17 lymphocytes (Th17), which are associated with the inflammation. Patients tend to have more pro-inflammatory Th17 lymphocytes and fewer anti-inflammatory Treg lymphocytes. Vitamin A can be transformed into retinoic acid, a substance that can modulate the genes of specific proteins, including Forkhead box P3 (FoxP3). FoxP3 plays a crucial role in the formation and activation of Treg lymphocytes. Therefore, vitamin A can help in maintaining the balance between Th17 and Treg lymphocytes through increasing the expression of FoxP3 [39].

Different studies may have different results depending on the types of dairy products they include. One of the main elements influencing the odds of MS, as extensively demonstrated by research, is vitamin D deficiency [17]. While vitamin D can be primarily sourced from sunlight, its availability through food sources is rather limited. Among these sources, dairy products hold a significant importance. Our research showed that a higher intake of dairy group may have a protective effect against LOMS [31]. However, a study by Arganoff et al. discovered that consuming milk fats raised the chance of MS [3]. Our present study did not include high-fat dairy products, such as cream or butter, in the dairy group.

The present study has also demonstrated that vegetable and fruit consumption during adolescence was linked to a lower chance of LOMS onset. This result aligns with previous research on MS [35, 37]. Consumption of vegetables and fruits, which is frequent in vegetarian and lacto-vegetarian diets, may reduce the incidence of MS [19]. Certain components of vegetables and fruits, including vegetable protein, dietary fiber, vitamin C, thiamin, vitamin B2, calcium, and potassium, significantly reduced the chance of MS [16]. Vegetables and fruits may protect against MS by modifying the immune response to myelin, altering the intestinal microbiota, and providing antioxidants. The intestinal microbiota controls the balance between Th17 and Treg immune cells and makes anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) to affect the immune response to myelin and gut-brain communication. Diet can change the microbiota in the gut. For instance, the ratio of Prevotella to Bacteroides, two major groups of bacteria in the gut, goes up in non-Western foods that are high in fiber and plant-derived polysaccharides. According to research, the bacterial genera Bacteroides and Prevotella, which can produce SCFA were significantly fewer in MS patients in comparison to healthy individuals [6, 12].

In the pathogenesis of MS, inflammation and oxidative stress have been identified as influential factors [34]. Due to decreased antioxidant levels in MS patients, supplementation of antioxidants in animal models decreases EAE (experimental allergic encephalomyelitis) and reduces the severity and duration of attacks [46]. Extensive investigations have revealed the potential of dietary antioxidants to decrease the incidence of MS [1]. Notably, vegetables and fruits are rich sources of antioxidants like vitamin C, E, and carotenoids, which may contribute to a lower risk of LOMS [42]. Decreased risk of MS development indicated an association with higher fruits and vegetable-based diet throughout adolescence, based on a case–control study involving 547 individuals with MS [2].

According to some studies, seafood consumption, particularly during adolescence or before the onset of MS, may have a protective influence on MS susceptibility. In our study, we discovered that consuming seafood during adolescence reduced odds of MS by roughly 77 percent. Eating fish/seafood or fish oil supplement at least once a week regularly 12 months before MS symptoms or index date was associated with a 44% decreased chance of MS than infrequently or never consuming fish products [24]. Taking a minimum of 0.5 portions of fish each week throughout adolescence decreased the incidence of MS by around 23%, according to a meta-analysis of six case–control studies. Meanwhile, some research failed to find a meaningful correlation between fish consumption and MS risk [9, 38, 41]. The heterogeneity of the results could be attributable to the number of participants, range of ages, quantity of seafood consumed, the location of the country of origin, as well as the amount of sunlight received. Our results are supported by evidence suggesting that Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), a lipid type present in seafood, can affect the inflammation associated with MS by inhibiting MMPs, which are enzymes T-cells in invading the CNS [26, 45]. This function of omega-3 PUFAs in MS was reinforced by a study, demonstrated a relationship between risk of MS and genetic polymorphisms in the FADS2 gene, which impact the availability or metabolism of omega-3 PUFAs [24].

Our slightly meaningful results in subgroups of nuts are consistent with previous studies [3, 19]. Nuts have also omega-3 PUFA that can help prevent inflammation and damage to the myelin sheath. These fatty acids can block the release of harmful substances like interleukin IL-1, IL-2, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interferon-gamma (INF-γ) that cause inflammation. They can also protect the myelin sheath by reducing the activity and growth of T-helper lymphocyte 1 (Th-1), a type of immune cell that attacks the myelin [11, 25, 32]. Alhaj et al. findings support anti-inflammatory effects of polyunsaturated and monounsaturated with MS odds [5].

The findings of this investigation align with the fundamentals of the Mediterranean diet (MD), which highlights the use of herbal derived food foods like fruits, cereals, vegetables, tree nuts, legumes, and seeds together with olive oil as the main source of additional fats. Furthermore, the diet promotes a diet composed of dairy products, fish, and seafood while recommending a lower intake of red meat and other meat groups [23]. According to a case–control investigation involving 142 controls and 70 patients with MS, MD could reduce the incidence of MS. They assessed the MD intake one year prior to diagnosis for the case subjects and similarly, one year before the interview for the control participants [2, 41].

The impact of diet on LOMS is emphasized in this study. Although this study has successfully demonstrated that diets in adolescence may affect the odds of developing LOMS, it has a possible limitation in terms of recall bias, owing to the retrospective nature of case–control studies. To address this issue, participants were asked to consult their parents or family members if they were uncertain about their food consumption. Additionally, we did not sub-analyze distinct dietary items. We made an effort to reduce it to some extent with a large sample size and by matching two groups in terms of age and gender.

A potential limitation of this study is the use of different data collection modes for cases (telephone interviews) and controls (face-to-face interviews), which could introduce differential measurement error. However, several measures were implemented to minimize this risk: both modes used the identical validated questionnaire, interviewers underwent standardized training, and pilot testing confirmed comparable comprehension across modes.

While this study provides novel evidence on the association between adolescent diet and LOMS odds, we acknowledge that the lack of comparison with other MS onset types (pediatric and classic adult-onset) limits our ability to draw conclusions about the role of diet in determining the age at MS onset. Future studies with a broader inclusion of all MS subtypes and appropriate analytical models (e.g., survival analysis) are warranted to investigate whether dietary factors influence not only the odds but also the timing of MS onset.

The most significant limitation of this study is the potential for substantial recall bias due to the extremely long recall period. While this recall bias likely affects both cases and controls (potentially leading to non-differential misclassification and bias toward the null). Another limitation of this study may be the lack of follow‑up on the participants’ dietary patterns in the subsequent years of their live.

Future research is required to validate our findings and investigate the potential intermediate mechanism.

Conclusion

To conclude, this research has reported for the first time that adolescent’s dietary factors potentially influence the odds of LOMS occurrence. The results suggest that including dairy, seafood, nuts, fruits, and vegetables in the diet may reduce the odds of LOMS. Hence, advocating for a dietary pattern rich in these food subgroups could serve as a preventive measure for people susceptible to LOMS.
