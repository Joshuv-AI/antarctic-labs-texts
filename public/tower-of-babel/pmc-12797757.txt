Stress hyperglycemia ratio and physical frailty in HFpEF
Authors: Pasquale Mone, Klara Komici, Germano Guerra, Tommaso Dazzetti, Urna Kansakar, Gianluca Gennarelli, Antonio Rainone, Gaetano Macina, Marco Di Mauro, Guido Iaccarino, Gianluca Testa, Gaetano Santulli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress hyperglycemia, reflected by the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), is increasingly recognized as a marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Stress-induced hyperglycemia arises from acute metabolic and inflammatory stress responses and may signify impaired glycemic resilience. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) commonly coexists with metabolic abnormalities such as hyperglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes, while physical frailty—frequent in older adults—is mechanistically linked to both dysglycemia and HFpEF. In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between SHR and physical performance in frail older adults with HFpEF.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study enrolling consecutive frail adults aged > 65 years with a confirmed diagnosis of HFpEF and Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) score < 26. Frailty was defined by ≥ 3 of 5 Fried criteria (low physical activity, unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, and slowness). SHR was calculated as the ratio of admission plasma glucose (mmol/L) to estimated chronic glucose derived from HbA1c (%). Participants were stratified into two groups: SHR ≤ 1 and SHR > 1. Physical function was assessed by gait speed (m/s).ResultsOf 295 screened individuals, 204 met inclusion criteria and completed the study. Patients with SHR > 1 demonstrated significantly reduced physical performance compared with those with SHR ≤ 1 (mean gait speed 0.65 ± 0.20 m/s vs. 0.72 ± 0.20 m/s, p = 0.0004).ConclusionsA higher SHR was independently associated with poorer physical function in frail older adults with HFpEF. These findings suggest that stress-related dysglycemia may contribute to functional decline in this population, highlighting the potential utility of SHR as a metabolic marker of frailty severity and cardiovascular vulnerability.Graphical abstract

Background

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) has emerged as an important biomarker for predicting adverse cardiovascular outcomes in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [1, 2]. Stress hyperglycemia, reflected by elevated SHR, represents a potentially transient metabolic response to acute physiological stressors, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurohormonal activation [3, 4]. While traditionally studied in acute cardiovascular settings, such as myocardial infarction or critical illness, emerging evidence suggests that SHR may also have relevance in chronic cardiovascular conditions. In particular, elevated SHR has been associated with worse outcomes in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a form of chronic heart failure that is increasingly recognized as a major public health challenge [5]. HFpEF is characterized by diastolic dysfunction, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, and a high burden of comorbidities, including hyperglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes, hypertension, and obesity [6–9]. These comorbid conditions interact synergistically to exacerbate cardiovascular stress, promote systemic inflammation, and impair metabolic homeostasis, creating a milieu that favors functional decline and frailty.

HFpEF predominantly affects older adults, a population particularly susceptible to physical impairment and adverse outcomes [10–12]. Physical frailty, defined by decreased strength, endurance, and physiologic reserve, is common in this population and closely linked to dysglycemia and metabolic dysregulation [13, 14]. Mechanistic studies indicate that hyperglycemia contributes to the development of sarcopenia and muscle dysfunction via pathways involving oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, impaired protein turnover, and mitochondrial dysfunction [15, 16]. Consequently, glycemic alterations may serve as early markers of vulnerability, identifying older adults at risk for accelerated functional decline [17, 18]. Nonetheless, the relationship between SHR—a dynamic measure integrating acute and chronic glycemic burden—and physical performance in frail older adults with HFpEF remains poorly understood.

Understanding this relationship has important clinical implications. If SHR is shown to correlate with reduced physical function, it could serve as a simple, readily available biomarker to identify patients at highest risk of frailty progression and functional deterioration. Such knowledge would enable clinicians to implement targeted interventions, including glycemic optimization, exercise programs, and multidisciplinary management strategies, to preserve mobility and independence in this vulnerable population. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that elevated SHR is associated with worse physical performance in frail older adults with HFpEF and designed a prospective observational study to investigate the role of SHR in determining physical function, independent of traditional comorbidities and demographic factors.

Methods

We designed a prospective observational study to enroll consecutive frail older adults with a previous diagnosis of chronic HFpEF admitted from March 2021 to March 2022 at Azienda Sanitaria Locale Avellino as outpatients. We included patients with the following criteria: age > 65 years, confirmed diagnoses of frailty, HFpEF, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score < 26. We excluded all patients who did not match with the aforementioned criteria. We enrolled each patient (or legally authorized representative) after signed an informed consent form. We performed the study according to the ethical standards in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical committee Campania Nord approved the protocol.

We measured each patient's glycemia, glycated hemoglobin, creatinine, and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) at admission. We diagnosed SHR as the ratio of the glucose value (expressed in mmol/L) recorded upon hospital admission and the chronic glycemic burden in the prior 8–12 weeks estimated from HbA1c (expressed in %) [19]. Based on our previous study, we selected 1.0 as a cut-off for SHR [2]. Of note, SHR is not currently used in chronic conditions. We decided to apply to our patients, considering the clinical phenotype. We diagnosed HFpEF according to 2021 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines and its 2023 focused update [20, 21]. We maintained the cut-off of 26 for the MoCA test consistent with previous papers [22, 23]. We applied a physical frailty diagnosis with at least three points out of five Fried criteria: low physical activity level, weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, and slowness [24, 25]. We used the 5-m gait speed test to evaluate physical function [26–28], evaluating whether SHR was ≤ or > 1.

Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD and compared between groups using the Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and log-transformed when included in multivariate regression models to meet normality assumptions. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Physical function was specifically analyzed with the Student’s t-test, and all results were further adjusted for potential confounders using multivariate regression analyses.

Results

We initially screened a total of 295 patients for eligibility. Of these, 70 patients were excluded because they did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria, and an additional 21 individuals declined to provide clinical information or to participate in the study, leaving 204 patients who were ultimately enrolled and completed all study assessments (Fig. 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. To explore the relationship between stress hyperglycemia and physical performance, participants were stratified into two groups based on SHR values: ≤ 1 and > 1. Comparisons of physical function revealed that patients in the SHR > 1 group had significantly lower gait speed compared with those in the SHR ≤ 1 group (0.65 ± 0.2 m/sec vs. 0.72 ± 0.2 m/sec; p = 0.0004), indicating a clear impairment in physical performance among patients with elevated SHR (Fig. 2). These findings were further validated through multivariate regression analysis to account for potential confounders, including sex, body mass index, renal function, and comorbidities (Table 2). Interestingly, while most comorbid conditions did not significantly influence the relationship between SHR and gait speed, age emerged as a strong and independent predictor of reduced physical performance (p < 0.0001), underscoring the impact of aging on functional decline in this population. Discussion

HFpEF is a prevalent and clinically challenging cardiovascular syndrome, often under-recognized in older adults, and characterized by clinical symptoms, elevated BNP or NT-pro-BNP, and echocardiographic evidence of diastolic dysfunction [21, 29–32]. Its frequent coexistence with multiple comorbidities, including metabolic alterations, further exacerbates the risk of frailty and functional decline [11, 33]. Managing these comorbidities is therefore essential to preserve physical function and mitigate frailty progression [26, 34, 35]. The Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR) has emerged as a sensitive marker of cardiovascular risk beyond the presence of diabetes [36–38]. In our study, we observed that an SHR > 1 was associated with significantly reduced gait speed, indicating worse physical function in frail older adults with HFpEF. This finding suggests that glycemic dysregulation, even in the absence of diagnosed diabetes, may contribute to physical impairment through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and muscle catabolism. By identifying patients with elevated SHR, clinicians may be able to recognize those at higher risk of functional decline, offering a potential avenue for early intervention and targeted management in this vulnerable population.

SHR primarily reflects the acute severity of illness and hitherto has not been considered as a chronically modifiable marker [39, 40]. We conceptualized SHR as a continuous metabolic marker reflecting the balance between acute and chronic glycemic states. While higher SHR values are typically interpreted as stress-related hyperglycemia, very low SHR values may instead indicate chronic hyperglycemia with a blunted acute response, both of which can adversely affect physical performance. When expressed as a spline curve, the relationship between SHR and gait speed exhibited a U-shaped pattern, with reduced physical function observed at both extremes of SHR and an optimal range around 1.0. This pattern suggests that both acute stress hyperglycemia and chronic glycemic dysregulation may contribute to functional impairment in frail older adults with HFpEF. In light of these observations, our results are innovative because highlight that SHR may serve in chronic patients with HFpEF as well. We speculate that SHR is an important marker in frail older adults with HFpEF. In this context, SHR should be evaluated in frail HFpEF patients to find out those at a higher risk of physical decline. This attention could reduce the risk of disability and/or dysautonomia of each person.

SHR reflects the relative increase of acute glucose over an individual’s baseline glycemia, capturing stress-induced metabolic dysregulation. In frail older adults, this may interact with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and sarcopenia, which are key drivers of physical decline. Elevated SHR could thus reflect both impaired metabolic resilience and heightened vulnerability to stressors, explaining its association with reduced physical function in this population.

Our study is not exempt from limitations. It was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations and healthcare settings. The observational design precludes establishing causality between SHR and physical performance, and the relatively limited sample size of participants may reduce the power to detect smaller effect sizes or interactions with less common comorbidities. Assessments were performed at a single time point without longitudinal follow-up, preventing evaluation of temporal changes in SHR or physical function. The SHR cut-off of 1.0 was derived from studies in acute settings and may not perfectly apply to frail older adults with chronic HFpEF. Additionally, the study included only participants with cognitive impairment (MoCA < 26), and physical function was assessed primarily via gait speed without other frailty or muscle strength measures. Finally, the study population was regionally and ethnically homogeneous, which may limit the applicability of the findings to more diverse populations.
