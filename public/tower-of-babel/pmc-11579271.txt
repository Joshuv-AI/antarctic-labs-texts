Predicting survival, neurotoxicity and response in B-cell lymphoma patients treated with CAR-T therapy using an imaging features-based model
Authors: Blanca Ferrer-Lores, Alfonso Ortiz-Algarra, Alfonso Picó-Peris, Alejandra Estepa-Fernández, Fuensanta Bellvís-Bataller, Glen J. Weiss, Almudena Fuster-Matanzo, Juan Pedro Fernández, Ana Jimenez-Pastor, Rafael Hernani, Ana Saus-Carreres, Ana Benzaquen, Laura Ventura, José Luis Piñana, Ana Belén Teruel, Alicia Serrano-Alcalá, Rosa Dosdá, Pablo Sopena-Novales, Aitana Balaguer-Rosello, Manuel Guerreiro, Jaime Sanz, Luis Martí-Bonmatí, María José Terol, Ángel Alberich-Bayarri

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis multicentre retrospective observational study aims to develop imaging-based prognostic and predictive models for relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell lymphoma patients undergoing CAR-T therapy by integrating clinical data and imaging features. Specifically, our aim was to predict 3- and 6-month treatment response, overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and the occurrence of the immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS).ResultsSixty-five patients of R/R B-cell lymphoma treated with CAR-T cells in two centres were included. Pre-infusion [18F]FDG PET/CT scans and clinical data were systematically collected, and imaging features, including kurtosis, entropy, maximum diameter, standardized uptake value (SUV) related features (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVstd, SUVmedian, SUVp25, SUVp75), total metabolic tumour volume (MTVtotal), and total lesion glycolysis (TLGtotal), were extracted using the Quibim platform. The median age was 62 (range 21–76) years and the median follow-up for survivors was 10.47 (range 0.20–45.80) months. A logistic regression model accurately predicted neurotoxicity (AUC: 0.830), and Cox proportional-hazards models for CAR-T response at 3 and 6 months demonstrated high accuracy (AUC: 0.754 and 0.818, respectively). Median predicted OS after CAR-T therapy was 4.73 months for high MTVtotal and 37.55 months for low MTVtotal. Median predicted PFS was 2.73 months for high MTVtotal and 11.83 months for low MTVtotal. For all outcomes, predictive models, combining imaging features and clinical variables, showed improved accuracy compared to models using only clinical variables or imaging features alone.ConclusionThis study successfully integrates imaging features and clinical variables to predict outcomes in R/R B-cell lymphoma patients undergoing CAR-T. Notably, the identified MTVtotal cut-off effectively stratifies patients, as evidenced by significant differences in OS and PFS. Additionally, the predictive models for neurotoxicity and CAR-T response show promising accuracy. This comprehensive approach holds promise for risk stratification and personalized treatment strategies which may become a helpful tool for optimizing CAR-T outcomes in R/R lymphoma patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-024-01172-9.

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy is a form of adoptive immunotherapy where, autologous T-lymphocytes are genetically engineered to express the fusion protein CAR that will recognize the tumor-associated antigen, allowing immune-mediated attack of the tumor cells [1]. CAR-T therapies have exhibited notable success, with complete remission rates ranging from 40 to 54% in patients with aggressive relapsed or refractory (R/R) B-cell malignancies. However, despite these advancements, a significant proportion of patients progress or relapse after CAR-T infusion [2–5]. This highlights the need for an earlier patient’s risk stratification of CAR-T failure to customize patient treatment plans after CAR-T infusion.

Additionally, the adoption of CAR-T therapies has underlined the importance of addressing associated morbidities, most notably cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [6]. CRS, characterized by a systemic inflammatory response resulting from cytokine release [7–9], and ICANS, marked by a spectrum of neurological symptoms, significantly impacting the overall tolerability of CAR-T treatment [10, 11]. Consequently, the development of predictive non-invasive biomarkers capable of anticipating the risk of CAR-T-associated morbidities development and advancing treatment outcomes is essential for refining patient management strategies and optimizing therapeutic results.

In this context, predictive models based on features extracted from real-world imaging examinations offer a compelling avenue for enhancing the precision of outcome predictions in CAR-T-treated B-cell lymphoma patients. The standard-of-care imaging technique for evaluating CAR-T cell therapy is Flourine18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography ([18F]FDG PET/CT) [12–15]. From PET images, several quantitative imaging features can be extracted, such as PET parameters and radiomics features. Standard PET parameters such as maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), metabolic tumour volume (MTV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) have already proven to have prognostic significance in various malignancies and have the potential to contribute to the predictive models for CAR-T therapy outcomes [16–20]. Nevertheless, these parameters cannot capture the metabolic heterogeneity of lesions, a factor that has shown promising results in improving the accuracy and performance of prognostic and predictive models. In this regard, radiomics involves the extraction of quantitative features from medical images—such as MRI, CT, or PET scans-allowing to capture spatial heterogeneity through simple and higher-order statistical parameters, providing a comprehensive characterization of tumour aggressiveness and microenvironment [21–23]. Integrating imaging features with clinical data allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the tumour landscape.

In this study, our objective was to develop novel imaging-based prognostic and predictive models that integrate clinical data and imaging features to better stratify R/R B-cell lymphoma patients receiving CAR-T cell therapy. For that, we retrospectively assessed the ability of advanced imaging features and clinical data to predict 3- and 6-month treatment response, overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and the occurrence of neurotoxicity, specifically ICANS.

Methods

Study design and eligibility criteria

Patients diagnosed with R/R diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) or high-grade B-cell lymphoma (HGBCL) treated with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells at Valencia Clinic University Hospital (Centre 1) and La Fe University Hospital (Centre 2) from 2019 to 2023 (data cut-off date: May 2023) were included in this multicentre retrospective observational real-world study. The inclusion criteria of the study were the following: (i) CAR-T treated R/R DLBCL de novo or transformed from an indolent lymphoma and PMBCL based on World Health Organization (WHO) 2016 criteria; (ii) to have a [18F]FDG PET/CT scan acquired before CAR-T cell infusion with enough diagnostic quality; (iii) provided written consent for participating into the study. Patients with (i) low-quality PET/CT images affected by image artifacts; (ii) absence of clinical data such as age, sex, and stage, or (iii) complete response (CR) in pre-lymphodepletion [18F]FDG PET/CT scan, were excluded.

Clinical data

Clinical and demographic data were collected at pre-lymphodepletion (LD), before CAR-T cell therapy. The following clinical data were collected: sex, age, type of B-lymphoma, cell-of-origin classification according to Hans algorithm, total number of previous lines of received treatment, consolidation therapy, International Prognostic Index (IPI) score, Ann Arbor stage, Easter Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation-specific Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI), presence of bulky disease, ratio of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), ferritin levels, C-reactive protein levels, lymphocyte levels, reception of bridge therapy pre-LD, type of CAR-T therapy, the development of ICANS, maximum grade of ICANS developed, presence of CRS, and the maximum grade of CRS developed.

The American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) criteria were adopted to grade cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity [24, 25]. The LDH ratio was calculated by dividing the LDH levels by the upper limit of normal (ULN), which was 480 IU/L for Centre 1 and 220 IU/L for Centre 2.

[18F]FDG PET/CT image acquisition

[18F]FDG PET/CT scans before CAR-T cell infusion were collected and analysed, following the EANM procedure guidelines for tumour imaging [26], approximately 60 min (range 55–75 min) after the injection of [18F]FDG. Serum glucose levels of all patients were confirmed to be on the recommended range between 7 and 8.3 mmol/L (126–150 mg/dL) after fasting for at least 6 h. PET/CT scans were performed from the base of the skull to the midthigh with 2–3 min per bed position. All patients were scanned in the supine position with arms elevated above the head. Full descriptions of [18F]FDG PET/CT acquisition protocols used by all participating centres are provided in the supplementary material (Table S1).

Image pre-processing and extraction of imaging features

PET/CT images were uploaded to the QP-Insights platform (Quibim SL, Valencia, Spain) for their analysis, involving the segmentation of the lesions and the extraction of quantitative imaging features. All the cases were reviewed by an imaging technician with more than 5 years of experience to ensure that the images met the quality standards. A nuclear medicine physician with more than 20 years of experience supervised the quality control and reviewed the lesion segmentation tasks. Imaging characteristics such as the correct alignment between PET and CT component and the absence of artefacts were reviewed. The presence in the imaging metadata of important parameters for the analysis of the imaging features, such as the patient weight, was also verified at this stage.

Lesion identification and segmentation was performed based on the lesion location described by the local nuclear physician in their reports. Volume of interest (VOI) was manually delineated for all visible lesions in the PET/CT imaging exams. PET and CT series were fused to perform the segmentation. All visible lesions with a SUV higher than the reference tissues (liver and mediastinum) were volumetrically delineated on the PET component, using the CT series as anatomical reference. Segmentation was performed on the transversal plane and the lesions were delineated in order of appearance following craneo-caudal and right-left directions. All nodal and extranodal lesions were individually segmented with a unique identifier. However, conglomerate lymph node masses were segmented as one lesion when it was not possible to individualize the lymph nodes anatomically. Finally, a 2D ROI was drawn on the liver for SUV normalization.

Following this, the following imaging features were extracted from the segmented lesions using QP-Insights (Quibim SL, Valencia, Spain): kurtosis, entropy, max-diameter, SUV-related parameters (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVstd, SUVmedian, SUVp25, SUVp75), MTVtotal and TLGtotal. The mean SUV of the 2D ROI drawn on the liver was calculated to perform a voxel wise normalization. This normalization allowed to remove possible biases in SUV calculation due to radiotracer injection and patient variability, hence ensuring the validity of a direct SUV comparison across timepoints. The MTV was calculated as the volume of voxels with SUVs higher than the threshold of 41% × SUVmax [27]. Imaging features were calculated for the dominant lesion (i.e., the lesion with the largest volume) and for all lesions (total). For calculating imaging features at the patient level, MTVtotal and TLGtotal were obtained by summing the individual MTV and TLG values of all lesions. SUVmean were calculated as the average of SUV values across all lesions, and similarly applied to other SUV-related statistics (SUVmax, SUVstd, SUVmedian, SUVp25, SUVp75). Max-diameter was taken from the largest lesion. Texture values (kurtosis and entropy) were recalculated by treating all lesions as a single combined lesion for analysis purposes. Only imaging features calculated at the patient level, rather than the dominant lesion, were included in the predictive models, as they provide a more comprehensive representation of the patient as a whole.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis proposed in this study was carried out in two phases, the first of which was descriptive and exploratory, while the second was inferential. The mean (with standard deviation) and median (with minimum and maximum values) were reported for all numerical variables, while the absolute and relative frequencies were calculated for categorical variables. As a complement, histograms and box-and-whisker plots were used to evaluate the distribution of the variables. In addition, a Spearman correlation matrix was used to evaluate the association between the numerical variables.

Different regression models were used in the analysis of the variables of interest. To evaluate the predictive capacity of the independent variables for neurotoxicity measured by ICANS (presence of neurotoxicity vs absence of neurotoxicity), 3 different models were created: a first model with only clinical predictors, a second model with only imaging predictors, and finally a combined model with clinical and imaging predictors. Similarly, to predict the response prediction at 3 and 6 months after CAR-T (complete response vs. other response), 6 logistic regression models were created, 3 of them for the prediction at 3-month response and the other 3 for the prediction at 6-month response. For the variable selection process of the models, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to control multicollinearity. The predictors that presented a VIF greater than 5 were eliminated from the model in a stepwise semi-automatic approach. Finally, models with best overall fit based on Akaike information criterion and with tenfold repeated cross-validation were selected.

On the other hand, univariate and multivariate analyses were carried out using Cox proportional-hazards models to predict OS and PFS, considering the time of the initial CAR-T cell infusion defined as the starting point. As in the previous logistic regression models, 3 types of models were created for each of the dependent survival variables (imaging predictors, clinical predictors, and combination of imaging and clinical predictors). Kaplan–Meier curves were conducted according to MTVtotal values.

Clinical variables that were not registered for most of the patients such as lymphocyte levels or maximum grade of ICANS, were excluded for the generation of the predictive models.

In all hypothesis testing, a p-value < 0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed with the freely distributed software: jamovi v.2.3.26 and R v.4.2.2.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 103 consecutive patients with R/R B-cell lymphoma were treated with CAR-T therapy in Centre 1 and Centre 2 during the inclusion period (Fig. 1). Among them, 33 patients were excluded as due to the lack of availability of [18F]FDG PET/CT scan acquired before CAR-T cell infusion. Therefore, 70 patients were eligible for inclusion as they had a [18F]FDG PET/CT scan acquired before CAR-T cell infusion that passed the quality check. After the segmentation of the lesions in pre-lymphodepletion [18F]FDG PET/CT, 5 patients were excluded for not having any identifiable lesion, as they had achieved complete remission (CR) with bridging therapy. Among these 5 patients with CR, 2 remain alive at the time of the last follow-up, with OS periods of 547 and 829 days from the CAR-T infusion. At three-months after CAR-T cell therapy, 55 patients (84.6%) remained alive. Among them, 27 patients (49.1%) achieved a complete response (CR) (lost follow-up, n = 8 patients). At six months after CAR-T treatment, 47 (72.3%) patients remained alive (lost follow-up, n = 15 patients), with 22 of them (46.8%) having achieved a complete response. Among all the patients (n = 65), 40 (61.5%) experienced progressive disease (PD). After CAR-T treatment follow-up period of 43.10 months, the whole cohort exhibited a median PFS of 6.07 months (range 0.77–43.10) and a median OS of 11.27 months (range 0.20–43.10). In terms of toxicity, 56 patients (86.2%) experienced any grade of CRS (n = 2 for grade ≥ 3, 3.1%), and 31 patients (47.7%) experienced ICANS at any grade (n = 6 for grade ≥ 3, 9.2%).

CAR-T response prediction

Logistic regression models were used to predict CAR-T response at 3 and at 6 months. Three PET parameters (MTVtotal, TLGtotal, and SUVmax), and one radiomics feature (entropy) were selected for assessing CAR-T therapy response at 3 and 6 months. These parameters were selected based on their significant weight in the assessment process. The results indicated an AUC of 0.646, a 20.0% of specificity and 81.5% of sensitivity for the 3-month assessment. Survival analysis

After CAR-T therapy, and during the 43.10 months of follow-up, 34 patients died (52.3%). The median OS for death patients was 7.27 (0.2–38.07 months). Most of these deaths, 27 patients (79.41%), were attributed to progressive disease.

For survival analyses, a cut-off MTVtotal value of 236 mL that maximized the Log-Rank statistic was selected to divide patients into high MTVtotal (n = 26) and low MTVtotal (n = 39) categories. Patients that were still alive at the last follow-up were censored at the last follow-up (n = 31). Patients with low MTVtotal had superior OS (HR: 0.35, 95% CI [0.18–0.69], p = 0.002) and PFS (HR: 0.45, 95% CI [0.23–0.84], p = 0.013).

Median overall survival after CAR-T therapy was 4.73 (95% CI [3.16–NA]) months and 37.55 (95% CI [13.87–NA]) months for the high (n = 26) and low (n = 39) MTVtotal, respectively (Fig. 3). In multivariate analyses, a model including only imaging features such as MTVtotal, SUVmax, TLGtotal, and entropy was able to successfully predict OS (p = 0.002). If the model includes only clinical variables: ICANS, the type of CAR-T, the presence of bulky disease at time of LD, whether patients have received bridge therapy, the presence of cytokine release syndrome (CRS), LDHLD ratio, and C-reactive proteinLD (CRPLD), the prediction of OS was also achieved (p = 0.001). Discussion

This retrospective multicentric study integrates imaging features and clinical variables to predict outcomes in R/R B-cell lymphoma patients undergoing CAR-T. Notably, only a few studies, mainly based on patients recruited in a single centre, have identified radiomics features based on PET scans that correlate with prognosis prediction of CAR-T treated patients [28–32]. In our work, the combination of clinical variables with imaging features, provided a promising valuable insight into the prognostic landscape, offering a non-invasive mean of patient risk stratification.

Early identification of non-responders to CAR-T treatment is essential for allowing timely adjustments in patient management. In this context, the development of regression models offers the best balance between predictive capability and control of overfitting, particularly in the absence of a partition validation strategy. Whitin this study, logistic regression models developed for predicting CAR-T treatment response at 3 and 6 months, demonstrated high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The inclusion of clinical variables alongside PET-derived parameters and entropy, enhanced the precision of response prediction. This suggests that a combination of imaging and clinical variables can effectively predict treatment response at different time points of the patient after cell therapy.

In R/R B-cell lymphoma, prior experiences in the determination of the MTV or total TLG by applying several algorithms provided a significant prognostic value in patients with non-Hodgkin lymphomas treated with chemotherapy as compared to standard visual or semi quantitative assessment evaluation [33–36]. In this sense, higher tumour burden in aggressive lymphomas before initiating first-line chemotherapy was significantly associated with shorter PFS and OS. Similar experience has been reported after CAR-T cell treatment in patients with R/R diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [17, 19, 28, 37–39]. In this study, an identified cut-off MTVtotal value of 236 mL effectively distinguished CAR-T patients into high and low MTVtotal categories, revealing significant differences in OS and PFS. The case-study presented in Fig. 6 could be used as an example of the clinical relevance of incorporating MTV assessment into risk stratification protocols for patients undergoing CAR-T therapy. The median MTVtotal of this patient (589.28 mL) was an indicative of the high tumour burden and surpassed the established cut-off MTVtotal value, therefore classifying the patient as high risk. Despite an initial partial response, disease progression occurred 91 days post-treatment, and the patient passed away 5 days later, resulting in a PFS and OS of 3.03 months and 3.2 months post-treatment, respectively. As described in the results section, patients classified as high MTVtotal had a median PFS of 2.73 [95% 1.5–9.03%] months and a median OS of 4.73 (95% CI [3.16–NA]) months. The correlation between high MTVtotal and shorter PFS and OS aligns with the findings of the developed model. Another pivotal aspect of CAR-T therapy is the development of immune-related side effects such as CRS and ICANS [6]. Different studies have reported the association of several clinical biomarkers with severe CRS and/or ICANS, including elevated tumour burden, LDH, serum CRP and ferritin as general inflammatory markers [10, 40–43]. However, the individual predictive power of these clinical biomarkers is still limited, and the development of novel biomarkers is strongly needed. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first model published, combining imaging features with clinical variables, to early predict the appearance of neurotoxicity in patients treated with CAR-T therapy. Probably, tumoral lymphoma mass is predictive of both CAR T-cell therapy response and neurotoxicity, as CAR expansion could be related to tumoral lymphoma mass. The inclusion of quantitative imaging features from the lesions in the models slightly improves the accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity of neurotoxicity prediction. It should be noted that the same imaging parameters are already captured for response, PFS, and OS prediction and can be readily included into the prediction of treatment-related toxicity.

In this work, patients were recruited from two different centres, ensuring a broader representation of the patient population and enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Models were validated using tenfold repeated cross-validation, which supports the reliability of our predictive models.

Despite of the promising results, this study has some limitations. First, while the number of patients aligns with that of other published studies, this research is still constrained by the low number of subjects with [18F]FDG PET/CT available due to the novelty and complexity of the therapy, as well as the challenges in collecting imaging exams from centres other than centres 1 and 2. Prospective studies with a larger number of sites, larger cohorts, extended follow-up, and validation in other independent datasets are essential to establish the generalizability and robustness of the developed models, and to minimize potential bias in disease progression or treatment outcomes. It is also worth to mention that [18F]FDG PET/CT has some limitations, including a non-insignificant frequency of false positives associated with inflammatory activity. This can lead to misleading interpretations of disease status, given the absence of clear criteria for pseudo progression feature [38, 44, 45]. Additionally, the optimal timing for disease response evaluation using [18F]FDG PET/CT after CAR-T cell infusion remains to be determined.

Regarding the segmentation process, it should be noted that test–retest studies would be needed to check that segmentation performed by more than one reader is consistent. The assistance of artificial intelligence models would be beneficial to automate these segmentations, improving efficiency. Additionally, the methodology used for extracting imaging features from multiple lesions in a single patient might has inherent limitations, particularly in capturing complex patterns of intra-patient heterogeneity. Recent studies, such as Cavinato et al.[46], have proposed more complex methods to address these challenges by developing radiomics-based inter-lesion relation networks. Further refinement and validation in feature extraction strategies are needed to better account for the interplay between multiple lesions.

Finally, although the study integrates clinical and imaging variables, other factors such as genetic markers may further enhance predictive accuracy. Future research should explore the incorporation of additional biomarkers to refine predictive models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the integration and analysis of imaging features with clinical variables and CAR-T monitoring data in this retrospective multicentric study shows promising results for optimizing CAR-T outcomes in R/R lymphoma patients. These developed predictive models would help improve prognostic stratification at baseline before CAR-T cells infusion and to identify patients who might benefit from early interventions, such as therapies that stimulates CAR T-cells expansion, subsequent CAR T-cells infusions, or early referral to allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Furthermore, the findings underscore the potential of a comprehensive approach for patient risk stratification, offering potential to improve personalized treatment strategies.
